Harnessing chimeric antigen receptor macrophages against solid tumors
Authors: Mengru Wang, Zhen Qin, Xiu‐Wu Bian, Yu Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMacrophages are prevalent in multiple tumors and exhibit diverse and potent functional activities. Therapeutic reprogramming of macrophage phenotypes represents a promising strategy for cancer immunotherapy. Engineering chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) to endow macrophages with anti‐tumor capacities demonstrated encouraging efficacy, particularly in enhancing tumor‐targeted phagocytosis. Furthermore, CAR macrophages (CAR‐Ms) orchestrate adaptive immunity through secreting pro‐inflammatory cytokines and presenting tumor antigens, thereby activating cytotoxic T lymphocyte responses. These multifaceted properties establish CAR‐Ms as potent immunotherapeutic agents against therapy‐refractory solid malignancies. Herein, we delineate the design principles, recent research advances, and rational combination strategies of CAR‐Ms, with particular emphasis on emerging clinical evidence from ongoing CAR‐M trials. We also explore potential applications of CAR‐Ms in non‐tumorous diseases and forecast future trends based on CAR‐T therapy evolution. CAR‐M development, combined with emerging technologies, will generate new perspectives for advancing cancer immunotherapy.

INTRODUCTION

The advent of immunotherapy has revolutionized oncology, with chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)‐T cell therapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) representing pivotal advances. Both modalities require robust T cell infiltration and function, limiting their efficacy against T cell‐deficient solid tumors [1, 2]. Currently, seven CAR‐T cell products listed on fda.gov have been approved for treating hematologic malignancies, significantly improving overall survival in large B‐cell lymphoma and progression‐free survival of multiple myeloma [3]. However, clinical translation of CAR‐T therapies remains challenging for solid tumors. Meanwhile, ICIs are approved as primary or adjuvant treatments for multiple cancers but demonstrate limited efficacy in solid tumors with low T‐cell infiltration [4].

Immunotherapies targeting alternative immune infiltrates are rapidly advancing. Tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs), typically abundant in immunologically “cold” solid tumors [5, 6, 7], exhibit a density positively correlated with tumor malignancy and poor prognosis [8]. During early tumorigenesis, TAMs intrinsically exert anti‐tumor functions, including phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and pro‐inflammatory cytokine secretion to remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME). However, as tumors progress, TAMs fuel tumor growth, metastasis, and immunosuppression [9]. Diverse TAM‐targeted strategies have been developed [10]. These strategies aim at suppressing TAM recruitment or survival [C‐C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2)/C‐C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2) axis and colony stimulating factor 1 (CSF1)/colony stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) axis targeting inhibitors) [11, 12], reprogramming TAMs towards anti‐tumor states [anti‐CD40 antibody and indoleamine 2,3‐dioxygenase (IDO) inhibitor] [13, 14], or enhancing phagocytosis (anti‐CD47 antibody) [15] of TAMs. Despite preclinical success, corresponding Phase I/II trials of these regimens frequently terminated due to suboptimal efficacy and toxicity [16].

CAR macrophages (CAR‐Ms) represent an emerging therapeutic paradigm [17]. These cells specifically recognize tumor‐associated antigens (TAAs) or tumor‐specific antigens (TSAs), executing potent tumor clearance via phagocytosis [18]. Their phagocytic potency can be augmented by integrating phagocytosis‐related signaling domains [e.g., Fc receptor gamma chain (FcRγ) or multiple EGF‐like domains 10 (Megf10)] [18] or co‐stimulatory domains into the CAR intracellular region [e.g., tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 9 (4‐1BB) or CD28] [19], or through interferon gamma (IFN‐γ)‐mediated polarization [20]. Preclinical studies and early clinical data indicate that, compared to CAR‐T cells, CAR‐Ms exhibit distinct advantages in phagocytic capacity, antigen presentation, and pro‐inflammatory niche formation (Figure 1) [21, 22]. These features position CAR‐Ms as promising immunotherapeutic agents for T cell‐refractory solid tumors.

This review synthesized recent advances in CAR‐M research, encompassing design principles, preclinical progress, clinical trial developments, and rational combination strategies. We also explored potential non‐oncological applications and highlighted prospects of next‐generation CAR‐M technologies to guide future CAR‐M research.

CAR‐M DESIGN PRINCIPLES

CAR structure

CARs are modular transmembrane proteins designed to recognize specific tumor antigens and elicit anti‐tumor responses in CAR‐equipped immune cells [23]. A canonical CAR comprises four domains [24]: an extracellular ligand‐binding domain for recognizing tumor antigens; a spacer domain that provides structural flexibility and facilitates antigen‐binding accessibility; a transmembrane domain for anchoring the CAR to the cellular membrane; and an intracellular domain that triggers anti‐tumor signaling. CAR architecture has undergone five generations of iterative refinement (Figure 2). This developmental trajectory, originally established in CAR‐T cell models [25], remains relevant to CAR‐M engineering. First‐generation CARs contain only a CD3 zeta chain (CD3ζ) signaling domain without costimulatory elements [26], resulting in limited persistence and transient activity. Second‐generation CARs include one costimulatory domain (e.g., CD28 or 4‐1BB) [27, 28], whereas third‐generation CARs integrate two such domains [e.g., CD28/4‐1BB, inducible T cell costimulator (ICOS)/tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 4 (OX40)] [29], enhancing signal transduction and cellular persistence. Although effective in hematologic malignancies, these constructs remain vulnerable to the immunosuppressive TME in solid tumors. Fourth‐generation CARs are engineered to secrete immunomodulatory cytokines [e.g., interleukin 2 (IL‐2), interleukin 15 (IL‐15)] for TME modulation [30]. Fifth‐generation CARs incorporate multiple strategies to optimize safety and efficacy, including Janus kinase ‐ signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK‐STAT) pathway integration [31], and enable universal “off‐the‐shelf” applications through gene‐editing technologies [32] (Figure 2A).

Evolution of CAR architectures for CAR‐T cells and CAR‐Ms. (A) Progression of CAR‐T cells. First‐generation CARs contain only a CD3ζ signaling domain without costimulatory elements; second‐generation CARs include one costimulatory domain; third‐generation CARs integrate two costimulatory domains; fourth‐generation CARs are engineered to secrete immunomodulatory cytokines for TME modulation; and fifth‐generation CARs incorporate multiple strategies to optimize safety and efficacy, including JAK‐STAT pathway integration. (B) Progression of CAR‐Ms. First‐generation CARs consist of a single intracellular domain that triggers phagocytosis, such as CD3ζ, FcRγ, or Megf10; second‐generation CARs incorporate two intracellular domains: a phagocytosis‐related domain coupled with either a costimulatory domain, an additional phagocytic domain, or a cytokine inducer. Currently reported CAR‐Ms are predominantly first‐ or second‐generation constructs, featuring structural designs distinct from those of CAR‐T cells (Figure 2B). First, the principles for selecting intracellular domains differ. Effective CAR‐T cell activation requires co‐stimulatory domains (e.g., CD28 or 4‐1BB) combined with CD3ζ [33]. In contrast, CAR‐M phagocytosis is activated by a single intracellular domain containing an immunoreceptor tyrosine‐based activation motif (ITAM) [18], with co‐stimulatory domains providing supplementary signaling when necessary. CD3ζ, FcRγ, and Megf10 constitute the primary intracellular domains for CAR‐M activation [34, 35, 36]. Antigen recognition induces ITAM phosphorylation, activating downstream phagocytic signaling [37]. This single‐domain design characterizes first‐generation CAR‐Ms. Second, CAR‐Ms' functional extension beyond phagocytosis. CAR‐Ms can be engineered to remodel TME and enhance T cell immunity, whereas CAR‐T cells primarily eliminate targets through cytotoxic mechanisms. For example, integrating CD3ζ with toll‐like receptor 4 intracellular toll/IL‐1R (TIR) domain enables both phagocytic activity and pro‐inflammatory cytokine secretion, establishing an immunostimulatory niche [38]. This dual‐domain configuration defines second‐generation CAR‐Ms. Additionally, CAR‐Ms equipped with the CD147 intracellular domain stimulate matrix metalloproteinase secretion to degrade tumor extracellular matrix [39], thereby enhancing immune cell infiltration and therapeutic efficacy [40].

Autologous PBMCs constitute the primary macrophage source in current CAR‐M trials [47]. According to the clinical trial NCT04660929, the entire manufacturing process—from PBMC isolation to final CAR‐M product—requires approximately 21 days, comparable to autologous CAR‐T cell production timelines [22, 48]. Considering the limited in vitro expansion capacity of PBMC‐derived monocytes/macrophages, recombinant human granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor filgrastim pretreatment enhances monocyte yield via colony‐stimulating factor 3 receptor (CSF3R) targeting, ensuring final yields ≥5 × 10⁹ cells per patient [22]. Adenoviral and lentiviral vectors or particles serve as primary CAR delivery systems for PBMC‐derived macrophages [17, 43, 44, 49, 50]. The modified adenoviral vector Ad5.F35 achieves high CAR transduction efficiency (∼88.5%) while conferring pro‐inflammatory properties [17, 22]. However, viral packaging constraints limit the delivery of large or multiple transgenes. Alternative gene editing techniques, such as clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) and CRISPR‐associated protein 9 (Cas9) or zinc finger nucleases, also face significant challenges when applied to primary PBMCs or their derived macrophages [51].

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), human embryonic stem cells, and multipotent hematopoietic stem cells represent alternative cell sources, offering superior gene editing efficiency and proliferative capacity [52, 53]. CRISPR‐Cas9‐mediated genome editing technology enables stable CAR construct integration into PSCs, yielding CAR‐Ms with persistent receptor expression [19]. Targeted editing further enhances anti‐tumor efficacy through knockout of immunoregulatory genes [e.g., aconitate decarboxylase 1 (ACOD1) [41] or sialic acid binding immunoglobulin‐like lectin‐5/10 (Siglec‐5/10) [42]], thereby potentiating CAR‐M‐mediated pro‐inflammatory cytokine secretion and phagocytosis. Furthermore, approximately 6,000 macrophages can differentiate from a single PSC within three weeks [20]. The CAR‐M complete generation protocol—encompassing CAR delivery (via non‐viral transfection or viral transduction approaches) and PSC differentiation—generally spans 20‐28 days [20, 42, 45].

Cryopreservation compatibility without functional impairment may enable “off‐the‐shelf” CAR‐PSC banking [45]. While promising for cost‐effectiveness, genetic stability concerns and allogeneic immunogenicity risks observed in CAR‐T [32] or CAR‐Natural Killer (CAR‐NK) cell [54] studies necessitate rigorous evaluation for CAR‐Ms.

In situ reprogramming represents a third strategy, utilizing nanoparticles to deliver CAR constructs directly to TAMs [35, 55, 56]. This approach bypasses the in vitro cell culture and the CAR delivery process. However, the efficiency and long‐term safety of in situ CAR‐M generation require further investigations.

ADVANCES IN CAR‐M PRECLINICAL STUDY FOR TUMOR THERAPY

Given the prevalent infiltration of macrophages into immunologically “cold” tumors characterized by low T cell infiltration, deficient antigen presentation, or absent immune activation [57], CAR‐M therapy represents a promising therapeutic strategy for these malignancies. Since Morrissey et al. first demonstrated the feasibility of applying CAR technology to macrophages [18], preclinical CAR‐M studies have advanced rapidly across diverse tumor models, as summarized in Table 3. The most frequently used targeted antigens are TAAs, such as human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) in breast cancer and mesothelin (MSLN) in ovarian cancer. Following antigen recognition, CAR‐Ms exert multifaceted anti‐tumor effects through: (1) direct elimination of tumor cells via phagocytosis and cytotoxicity [17]; (2) cross‐presentation of tumor antigens to T cells post‐phagocytic uptake [58]; and (3) creation of an immune‐supportive niche through secreting pro‐inflammatory cytokines. These cytokines, such as IL‐12, tumor necrosis factor beta (TNF‐β), and IFN‐γ, activate T and NK cells while programming TAMs from a pro‐tumor to an anti‐tumor phenotype [22, 59]. CAR‐M therapies have demonstrated promising anti‐tumor efficacy in xenograft models of ovarian cancer, breast cancer, glioblastoma, pancreatic cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, gastric cancer, melanoma, neuroblastoma, and renal cell carcinoma (Table 3). Efficacy validation was conducted at the animal or cellular level in these models.

However, CAR‐M efficacy is potentially constrained under systemic administration or when targeting advanced‐stage malignancies [45]. Current understanding of CAR‐M persistence in peripheral blood and systemic distribution remains limited. In a Phase I clinical trial targeting HER2+ solid tumors (NCT04660929), transient CAR‐M presence was detected in human peripheral blood after intravenous infusion of 5 × 109 cells, with maximal circulating CAR‐M detection of 48 hours or less [22]. A complementary preclinical study utilizing fluorescence‐labeled CAR‐Ms revealed primary accumulation in the liver, spleen, tumor and lung tissues. Residual signals persisted in the liver and spleen for up to 7 days post‐infusion, indicating organ‐specific retention [58]. Additional investigations are warranted to monitor long‐term CAR‐M status post‐adoptive transfer.

PROGRESS IN CAR‐M CLINICAL TRIALS

Pioneering clinical trials of macrophage infusion for cancer therapy originated in the late 1980s [74], wherein Andreesen et al. administered IFN‐γ‐activated autologous macrophages to patients. Although no significant efficacy was observed, this and subsequent trials established the safety profile of adoptive macrophage transfer [74, 75, 76, 77], supporting the feasibility of CAR‐M clinical investigations. As of May 31st, 2025, six CAR‐M clinical trials are registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, comprising one observational study and five interventional studies (Table 4). All CAR‐M products target HER2‐ or MSLN‐overexpressing solid tumors in patients with recurrence following standard therapy. Administration routes include intravenous or intraperitoneal delivery, with the total dosages varying from 3 × 107 to 1 × 1010 cells per patient. Two trials (NCT04660929 and NCT06562647) combine CAR‐M with anti‐programmed cell death 1 (PD‐1) therapy. Primary endpoints evaluate safety and tolerability, while secondary endpoints assess efficacy and pharmacokinetics.

Updated results from NCT03608618 and NCT04660929 demonstrate the favorable feasibility and safety of MCY‐M11 [78] and CT‐0508 [22]. MCY‐M11 was well‐tolerated in 11 treated patients, with most reported treatment‐related adverse events being Grades 1‐2. One patient experienced Grade 2 pericarditis, fever, and transient neutropenia, clinically assessed as related severe adverse events. CT‐0508 exhibited no dose‐limiting toxicity or neurotoxicity in 14 patients. Grade 1‐2 cytokine release syndrome (CRS) occurred in 64.3% of patients, with all cases resolving spontaneously within 1‐4 days. Two trials demonstrated therapeutic activity. Stable disease (SD) was achieved in 36.4% of MCY‐M11‐treated patients and 28.6% of CT‐0508‐treated patients. Recent assessments indicate superior CT‐0508 efficacy in HER2 immunohistochemistry (IHC) 3+ tumors compared to HER2 IHC 2+ tumors. Notably, tumor burden reduction was 20% in patients with breast cancer compared to 14% in those with salivary gland cancer. Additionally, 62% of patients showed a transient but measurable decrease in circulating tumor deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) levels, suggesting a potential treatment response.

Preliminary findings suggest that CT‐0508 CAR‐M therapy possesses advantages over conventional CAR‐T therapy [22]. First, CT‐0508 exerts multifunctional anti‐tumor activity. Bulk RNA‐seq analysis of tumor biopsies revealed CT‐0508‐mediated upregulation of genes involved in antigen presentation, adaptive immunity, and TME remodeling. Increased cytotoxic T cell infiltration in TME and peripheral blood indicates T cell activation. Second, a transient peripheral circulation (≤48 h) of CAR‐Ms was found prior to tissue migration, potentially reducing CRS associated with prolonged systemic exposure. Furthermore, CAR‐M therapy circumvents lymphodepletion preconditioning required for CAR‐T cell infusion, avoiding associated immunosuppression, infectious risks, and cytopenia. Finally, biodistribution analysis of CT‐0508 demonstrates metastasis‐targeting potential. Significant lesion reduction was observed in liver (80%) and lymph node (50%) metastases, demonstrating substantial therapeutic potential against advanced metastatic diseases.

Although CT‐0508 was detectable in 92% of tumor biopsies, intratumoral infiltration density remains low. Further research is warranted to elucidate intratumoral distribution dynamics and persistence. To address limited persistence, CAR‐monocytes—exhibiting longer half‐life and enhanced accessibility—are being explored in an ongoing trial (NCT06254807) [79]. Future studies should establish optimal dosing regimens (dosage, timing, and administration routes) for maximal efficacy. Given clinical treatment paradigms, evaluating CAR‐M combination therapies is essential.

CAR‐M COMBINATION STRATEGIES WITH OTHER CANCER THERAPEUTICS

CAR‐M therapy combines effectively with surgery, chemotherapy, CAR‐T therapy, ICI, and anti‐CD47 therapy in several tumor models, demonstrating enhanced anti‐tumor efficacy (Figure 4). Treatment modalities that directly eliminate tumor cells—including chemotherapy, radiation, or molecularly targeted agents—induce immunogenic cell death and TAA release [80, 81], thereby potentiating CAR‐M‐mediated phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and pro‐inflammatory cytokine secretion. Furthermore, combining CAR‐Ms with other immunotherapies augments intercellular immune crosstalk, generating enhanced anti‐tumor responses.

Combination with chemotherapy

Chemotherapy‐induced immunogenic cell death releases TAAs or TSAs, recruiting macrophages to phagocytose cell debris or residual tumor cells, thereby accelerating tumor burden reduction [82]. Combining CAR‐Ms with oxaliplatin (a platinum‐based agent) significantly reduces gastric carcinoma growth and extends mouse survival [36]. Compared to CAR‐M monotherapy, this combination elevates pro‐inflammatory cytokines [interleukin 6 (IL‐6) and IFN‐γ], promoting anti‐tumor polarization of CAR‐Ms. This supports the potential roles of chemotherapy in reshaping the CAR‐M phenotype. However, specific chemotherapeutic agents may induce pro‐tumoral TAM polarization [83, 84]. To mitigate direct adverse effects, sequential administration of CAR‐Ms post‐chemotherapy represents a viable strategy.​

Combination with surgery

Most malignant tumors exhibit invasive growth associated with post‐surgical tumor recurrence. Evidence indicates that intra‐cavitary injection of CAR construct‐laden nanoporters effectively reduces glioblastoma relapse in murine models [35]. Monocyte‐derived macrophages or resident microglia surrounding the cavity internalize the nanoparticles and express CARs, thereby eliminating residual glioma cells. However, murine glioblastoma possesses distinct immunological and anatomical features compared to other solid tumors and human glioblastoma [85, 86]. Translating this approach thus requires assessment of post‐resection macrophage abundance and CAR delivery efficiency in relevant tumor types.

Neoplastic cells secrete chemokines (e.g., CCL2, CSF‐1) to recruit peripheral macrophages into TME prior to intervention [11, 12]. Tumor‐mediated macrophage recruitment constitutes the primary infiltration mechanism in solid malignancies [5]. Consequently, investigating CAR‐Ms as a neoadjuvant therapy to reduce pre‐surgical tumor burden warrants further exploration.

Combination with CAR‐T therapy

CAR‐M antigen presentation and secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines potentiate T cell immunity. Preliminary in vitro data indicated efficient tumor cell elimination upon co‐culturing of CAR‐Ms with CAR‐T cells [63]. CAR‐T‐secreted inflammatory factors induced upregulated costimulatory ligands (CD80 and CD86) on CAR‐Ms. These ligands reciprocally enhance CAR‐T cell activation and functional fitness. Further studies should determine whether CAR‐Ms can degrade extracellular matrix to augment CAR‐T cell infiltration and overall anti‐tumor efficacy.

Combination with ICIs

Anti‐PD‐1/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD‐L1) therapies alleviate T cell exhaustion and enhance T cell activation. PD‐1 expression on TAMs is associated with phagocytic inhibition and anti‐inflammatory cytokine secretion [87]. Preclinically, combining CAR‐Ms with anti‐PD‐1 therapy reduces tumor burden and prolongs survival in ovarian carcinoma models [41, 59]. Clinical potential of this combination is being assessed in two CAR‐M clinical trials (NCT04660929, NCT06562647). Notably, in NCT04660929, CAR‐M infusion increased both exhausted and activated T‐cell subsets, particularly in patients achieving SD. The findings support the use of ICI combination strategies to enhance efficacy.

Combination with anti‐CD47 therapy

Phagocytosis checkpoints inhibit tumor cell clearance, transmitting “don't eat me” signals or counteracting “eat me” signals [88]. CD47—highly expressed across tumors—binds to the signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) receptor on TAMs [89], representing a key inhibitory checkpoint. Anti‐CD47 therapy reverses macrophage phagocytic inhibition, thereby augmenting CAR‐M phagocytosis [15]. Combining CAR‐Ms with anti‐CD47 therapy demonstrates efficacy in animal models of glioblastoma, ovarian and pancreatic cancer, Burkitt's lymphoma, and melanoma [20, 35, 41, 49].

The predominant clinical toxicity of anti‐CD47 therapy is anemia, resulting from the “on‐target, off‐tumor” effect due to CD47 expression on erythrocytes [90]. Innovative antibody engineering strategies—including Fc domain optimization [91], bispecific antibodies [92], and TME‐responsive constructs [93]—have demonstrated better safety profiles by mitigating anemia. However, combining CAR‐M therapy with these advanced anti‐CD47 antibodies requires further investigation.

APPLICATIONS OF CAR‐Ms IN NON‐TUMOROUS DISEASES

Beyond oncology, CAR‐engineered immune cells demonstrate therapeutic potential for non‐tumorous diseases, including autoimmune disorders, infections, fibrotic diseases, and senescence‐related conditions [94]. These disorders share a common characteristic: pathogenic cells or pathogens express targetable antigens that can be recognized by CAR‐engineered immune cells. CAR‐T cells pioneered this approach, demonstrating efficacy in these disease models [95]. Notably, CAR‐engineered immune cells encounter fewer barriers in non‐tumorous diseases than in malignancies. First, pathogenic cells in non‐tumorous diseases exhibit minimal proliferative capacity with low burden, enabling therapeutic benefits even without complete eradication. Second, disease‐associated antigens display relative stability and limited mutational heterogeneity. Third, the absence of an immunosuppressive TME reduces suppression of CAR‐engineered immune cells. Building on CAR‐T experience, CAR‐Ms have been explored for the application of infectious, fibrotic, neurodegenerative, and inflammatory diseases (Table 5).

Applications in infections

Staphylococcus aureus and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS‐CoV‐2) resist conventional macrophage phagocytosis. CAR‐Ms targeting Staphylococcus aureus surface protein A [97, 98] and the SARS‐COV‐2 spike protein [96] enhance phagocytic efficiency and accelerate pathogen clearance.

Applications in fibrosis‐related diseases

Tissue fibrosis—a pathological hallmark of chronic diseases—stems from hyperactivated fibrogenic cells overproducing collagen. These cells overexpress markers like fibroblast activation protein (FAP) [102], urokinase‐type plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) [99], or dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4) in disease‐specific contexts [104]. CAR‐M‐mediated phagocytosis of these cells alleviates liver fibrosis [99], cardiac fibrosis [101 , 103], myocardial ischemia‐reperfusion injury [102], and scarring [104].

Applications in neurodegenerative diseases

Alzheimer's disease (AD) features amyloid beta (Aβ) deposition. CAR‐Ms targeting Aβ potentially slow AD progression [106]. Macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (M‐CSF)‐secreting anti‐Aβ CAR‐Ms achieved significant Aβ clearance in vivo [100], supporting their therapeutic potential for AD.

Applications in inflammatory diseases

Macrophages exhibit functional dichotomy: pro‐inflammatory subtypes enhance pathogen clearance, while anti‐inflammatory subtypes promote tissue repair [107]. In inflammatory diseases, suppressed anti‐inflammatory macrophages hinder recovery [108]. Interleukin 4 (IL‐4) induces anti‐inflammatory macrophages [109]. To enhance tissue repair, CAR‐Ms were engineered with an intracellular IL‐4 receptor alpha (IL‐4Rα) domain fused to an extracellular tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐binding domain. TNF recognition triggers IL‐4 anti‐inflammatory signaling, conferring a reparative phenotype [105]. These CAR‐Ms ameliorate inflammation and promote tissue repair in mouse models of renal/hepatic ischemia‐reperfusion injury and adriamycin nephrosis.

Challenges in non‐tumorous diseases

Despite promising potential, key challenges persist: First, many disease‐associated antigens lack tumor‐antigen specificity, increasing “on‐target, off‐tumor” toxicity risks. Incorporating synthetic logic gates or safety switches [110] into the CAR‐Ms may mitigate this effect (discussed in Section 7). Second, CAR‐M delivery strategies for non‐tumorous diseases need optimization. For anatomically restricted pathologies (e.g., liver fibrosis), regional administration (e.g., hepatic artery infusion) enhances local accumulation versus systemic delivery. Third, similar to tumors, certain diseases (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus [111], sepsis [112], rheumatoid arthritis [113]) feature immunosuppressive cytokines that impair phagocytosis. CAR‐M designs require phagocytosis‐preserving strategies. Fourth, extended CAR‐M residence risks chronic inflammation and tissue damage (e.g., microglia‐like activation in AD exacerbating neuronal injury [114, 115]). Rigorous evaluation of tissue distribution profiles and development of inducible suicide gene systems are essential.

NEXT‐GENERATION CAR‐Ms

Current CAR‐M optimization remains nascent compared to CAR‐T, with most reported CAR‐Ms being either first‐generation (single phagocytic intracellular domain) or second‐generation (dual functional domains that enhance phagocytosis and pro‐inflammatory factor production). CAR‐Ms intrinsically migrate into solid tumors and exert multifaceted anti‐tumor functions. However, the heterogeneous tumor antigen expression and immunosuppressive TME limit efficacy. Strategic optimization prioritizing specificity, fitness, efficacy, and safety is warranted (Figure 5).

Specificity

“On‐target, off‐tumor” toxicity concerns persist due to the low TAA expression in normal tissues [116]. Logic gates enhance the specificity of target antigen recognition [117, 118, 119]. AND gate requires dual antigen recognition. For instance, pancreatic cancer cells co‐express carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) [120] and mesothelin [121]; CAR‐Ms can be engineered to selectively eliminate dual‐antigen‐expressing malignant cells while sparing normal cells expressing either antigen individually [122]. IF‐THEN gate utilizes the synthetic notch (SynNotch) system to induce CAR expression upon tissue‐specific antigen detection [123]. In glioblastoma therapy, SynNotch receptor activation upon recognition of the brain‐enriched antigen brevican induces expression of CARs targeting ephrin type A receptor 2 (EphA2) or interleukin 13 receptor subunit alpha2 (IL13Rα2), enabling brain‐specific elimination of glioblastoma cells while avoiding toxicity to normal extra‐cerebral tissues expressing these antigens [124]. NOT gate inhibits cytotoxicity upon healthy tissue antigen recognition. For instance, CEA is a well‐characterized TAA overexpressed in multiple epithelial malignancies while retaining basal expression in normal epithelia [125]. To prevent off‐target toxicity to non‐malignant epithelial cells co‐expressing CEA and trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (TROP2) [126], NOT‐gated CAR‐T cells have been engineered to deactivate cytotoxicity upon recognizing TROP2, thus preserving epithelial integrity [127]. Low immunogenicity may be addressed by single‐chain variable fragment (scFv) affinity maturation [110] or OR gate targeting multiple TAAs [128]. Co‐localization‐dependent protein switch (Co‐LOCKR) integrates AND, NOT, and OR logic gates for complex multi‐antigen recognition [129], while its impacts on CAR‐M anti‐tumor efficacy require further evaluation.

Fitness

Effective intratumoral CAR‐M function requires adaptation to immunosuppressive TME [6]. Engineered CARs recognizing immunosuppressive molecules counteract TME effects. For example, TGF‐β is a key immunosuppressive cytokine in TME [130]. CARs incorporating a scFv derived from the extracellular domain of TGF‐β receptor and an IL‐2/15 signaling intracellular domain enable TGF‐β sensing and pro‐inflammatory cytokine secretion, enhancing fitness in TGF‐β‐overexpressing prostate cancer [131]. Alternatively, gene editing improves TME adaptation. Hypoxia‐induced lactate sensed by G protein‐coupled receptor 132 (Gpr132) promotes macrophage pro‐tumor polarization [132, 133]. Gpr132 knockout enhances TME fitness and suppresses breast cancer [69], suggesting its utility for TME‐resistant CAR‐Ms. Further CAR‐M‐TME interaction studies remain essential for CAR‐M design.

Efficacy

The intracellular domain critically regulates CAR immune cell activation, rendering its modification essential for enhancing CAR‐M efficacy. Tandem use of two phagocytosis‐related intracellular domains (FcRγ and CD19 cytoplasmic domain that recruits the phosphoinositide 3‐kinase p85 regulatory subunit [18]) effectively triggers CAR‐M phagocytosis, while concatenated functional domains (e.g., CD3ζ‐TIR [67] or CD147 [70]) augment anti‐tumor effects. Although multi‐domain CAR‐Ms (3‐4 domains) exist [19, 38, 45, 56], extended CAR fragments complicate transduction and may compromise domain‐specific activation. Combinatorial signaling motif libraries coupled with machine learning optimize domain selection [134]. Beyond optimizing intracellular domains, gene editing further enhances CAR‐M function. Knockout of phagocytosis inhibitors (Siglec5/10 [42]) and anti‐inflammatory mediators (ACOD1 [41]) potentiates efficacy, while cytokine overexpression (e.g., M‐CSF for prolonged persistence [100]) improves self‐sustenance. Administration of extrinsic cytokines like IFN‐γ further bolsters CAR‐M anti‐tumor polarization [135] and enhances efficacy [136]. Combining CAR‐Ms with monoclonal antibodies synergizes via macrophage‐mediated antibody‐dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) [137] or antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) [138], enabling direct tumor elimination coupled with amplified phagocytic or cytotoxic activity.

Safety

CAR‐T therapy exhibits three primary toxicities: CRS, neurotoxicity, and “on‐target, off‐tumor” toxicity [110]. Autologous CAR‐M therapy demonstrates improved CRS and neurotoxicity [22, 77], but shares “on‐target, off‐tumor” risks from TAA expression in normal cells. Current mitigation strategies encompass drug‐inducible suicide systems [e.g., inducible caspase 9 (iCasp9) [139], truncated epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFRt) [140], herpes simplex virus thymidine kinase (HSV‐tk) [141]] for controlled CAR‐cell elimination and CAR expression. The latter includes protease‐off switches where protease inhibitors prevent degron cleavage to induce CAR degradation [142, 143]. Physical on‐switches could utilize focused ultrasound to promote heat shock‐induced CAR expression [144]. Allogeneic CAR‐M therapy requires evaluation of host‐versus‐graft reaction (HvG), mediated by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecular recognition [145]. Strategies to reduce immunogenicity include: MHC [146]/class II major histocompatibility complex transactivator (CIITA) [146]/beta‐2‐microglobulin (B2M) [147] knockout and human leukocyte antigen‐E (HLA‐E) [148]/CD47 overexpression [149]. Additionally, PSC‐derived CAR‐Ms necessitate tumorigenicity assessment due to genetic instability risks.

LIMITATIONS AND PERSPECTIVE

Despite their therapeutic potential for tumors and non‐neoplastic diseases, CAR‐Ms face critical limitations: (1) Deficient longitudinal tracking of adoptively transferred CAR‐M biodistribution and functionality necessitates integrating advanced in vivo monitoring (e.g., stable isotopic or fluorescent labeling with spatiotemporal multi‐omics) to delineate persistence and safety; (2) Suboptimal CAR delivery efficiency for PBMC‐derived and in situ‐reprogrammed CAR‐Ms requires high‐efficacy viral/non‐viral vectors; (3) TME immunosuppression compromising efficacy may be mitigated via intracellular domain engineering or preemptive gene editing in hPSC‐derived CAR‐Ms; (4) Cost or production constraints from autologous manufacturing demand universal allogeneic platforms and automated bioprocessing systems for scalable deployment.

CONCLUSIONS

Preclinical and clinical data substantiate CAR‐M therapeutic potential in oncology and non‐neoplastic diseases. While gene editing and combination strategies enhance safety/efficacy, and next‐generation CAR‐Ms evolve from CAR‐T paradigms, critical unknowns persist: infusion‐triggered biodistribution patterns; liquid biopsy biomarker identification for dynamic efficacy monitoring and dose optimization; and harnessing CAR‐M–immune cell interactions to create immunostimulatory TME. Emerging technologies—spatiotemporal omics, spectral tracing, multiphoton imaging, and radiolabeling—can delineate CAR‐M migration, persistence, and tissue adaptation. Defining these parameters will enable biomarker development for post‐infusion monitoring and response prediction. NCT04660929 data reveal CAR‐Ms exert dual T‐cell effects (activation/exhaustion), though mechanisms remain elusive. Single‐cell/spatial omics assists in elucidating CAR‐M–immune cell crosstalk and post‐infusion TME remodeling, guiding next‐generation CAR designs or combination therapies. Furthermore, artificial intelligence (AI)‐driven modeling of tumor‐specific pathogenesis enables optimized CAR‐M design and patient stratification, while synthetic immunology advances will improve scalable manufacturing.
