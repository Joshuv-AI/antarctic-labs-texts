From breaking rules to making rules in materials science
Authors: Bharat Jalan
Published: 2025

When I was a child, my father ran a business in India making cast-iron products. I often found myself in and around the casting workshop, where molten iron was poured into molds and transformed into everyday objects. To me, it looked almost magical: raw, glowing liquid turning solid and strong. That was my first lesson that matter could be reshaped—that structure was not fixed but could be made.

Years later, as a student of metallurgical and materials engineering at the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) Madras, I learned the formal mantra: structure and bonding control properties. It was a powerful truth, but it sparked a bigger question in me: Who controls structure and bonding? My professors would point to thermodynamics as the ultimate authority. Sometimes, I would joke that thermodynamics is the “God” of our field—deciding what bonds can form and which structures are stable.

But here’s the thing: I was never comfortable with that answer. There must be a way to control bond and structure and bonding on demand. I wanted to decide what bonds form, what structures emerge, and, ultimately, what properties can be created—on demand.

That desire is what drew me to molecular beam epitaxy (MBE), an approach in which thermodynamics is not absolute. In MBE, thermodynamics has to negotiate with surface kinetics, with strain, with substrate chemistry, even with external fields. This is where science becomes art. By leaning into that negotiation, we can stabilize “impossible” phases, create freestanding membranes, or engineer properties.

Along the way, I have also learned that discovery rarely comes by following the neatest path. Defects and impurities can alter intrinsic properties, and they do, but in synthesis science, the cleanest crystals often emerge through the messiest routes. Embracing disorder, rather than avoiding it, can be the very strategy that leads to order at the atomic scale. This is especially true in synthesis science and processing.

Today, with the rise of oxide membranes, we have an even more powerful tool. By stacking and twisting these membranes, we are no longer bound to the symmetries and bonds that nature dictates—we can design entirely new ones including quasicrystals. We can tailor not only structure but also bonding itself, effectively writing our own rulebook for matter.

Not long ago, a living legend of oxide MBE, Darrell Schlom, professor of Materials Science at Cornell University reminded me: “Break the rules to discover new properties.” It resonated deeply, but as I reflect on where the field is heading, I realize that the challenge before us is even greater. We are no longer just breaking rules—we are making them. With membranes and epitaxial design, we are learning to program matter, to decide which rules of bonding and structure should apply, and to create materials that nature never intended.

For me, discovery has never been about obedience (i.e., “follow the rules”). It has always been about creativity—moving from breaking rules thoughtfully to making rules deliberately, with the hope that in doing so, we uncover properties that no one thought possible.
