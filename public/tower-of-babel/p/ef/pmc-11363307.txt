Evolving dual-trait EPSP synthase variants using a synthetic yeast selection system
Authors: Kevin B. Reed, Wantae Kim, Hongyuan Lu, Clayton T. Larue, Shirley Guo, Sierra M. Brooks, Michael R. Montez, James W. Wagner, Y. Jessie Zhang, Hal S. Alper
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceGlyphosate is a herbicide that selectively inhibits the enzyme 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase (EPSPS) of the plant shikimate pathway. Mutations within EPSPS enable glyphosate resistance and thus protective capacity to modified crops. However, these mutations decrease basal enzymatic efficiency and can hinder plant growth. In this work, we develop a synthetic yeast selection system that closely simulates growth trends seen in plants and utilize a dual-trait selection strategy to enable the rapid evolution of novel EPSPS variants that confer both improved enzyme properties and glyphosate resistance. Transfection into plants validates this dual phenotype and demonstrates a superior mutant enzyme without imparting growth deficiencies.

5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase (EPSPS) catalyzes the reaction of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) and shikimate-3-phosphate (S3P) to generate phosphate and 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate (EPSP). This enzyme is well studied owing to both the central role it plays in aromatic amino acid biosynthesis, among other essential secondary metabolites such as folates and quinones, and its propensity to be inhibited by a common herbicide, glyphosate. Glyphosate’s effectiveness against many plant species to inhibit the binding of PEP to EPSPS, its simple synthesis route, and overall low cost has made it the most used herbicide in history (1–3). First used as a broad-spectrum, nonspecific herbicide, glyphosate was on the market for 20 y before glyphosate-tolerant crops were developed. The discovery of a bacterial EPSPS enzyme from Agrobacterium sp. strain CP4 with high glyphosate resistance enabled transgenic crops starting in the 1990s (4). Since then, detailed structural and biochemical studies have led to the discovery and rational design of additional glyphosate-resistant mutants (5–8). Herbicide-tolerant transgenic crops enable better control of weeds and therefore have been widely adopted in many areas, including the United States, where 90% of the soybeans, corn, and cotton is grown with this trait (9). In addition, the identification of a native, mutant EPSPS can take advantage of new gene editing technologies like CRISPR/Cas, TILLING, and others without employing traditional biotechnology transgenes (10, 11).

To date, only a small set of mutations are currently known to confer glyphosate resistance to native plant EPSPS and related homologs. However, these resistance-conferring mutations also reduce the net activity of this essential enzyme and thus lead to reduced plant growth under native expression (12–14). In enzymes of plant origin and similar homologs (termed Class I EPSPS enzymes), it is well established that glyphosate insensitivity comes at a high detriment to the catalytic efficiency. Commonly evaluated mutations such as G101A, T102I, and P106S all lead to varying levels of improved glyphosate tolerance in Zea mays (Z. mays) and other species, but are not commercially viable as single mutants due to either hindered enzymatic efficiency for G101A and T102I or insufficient glyphosate tolerance for P106S (4). The double mutant T102I and P106S (denoted as TIPS), was one of the earliest commercially viable glyphosate-resistant EPSPS mutants of plant origin to be discovered (15). TIPS is the only Class I enzyme that is essentially insensitive to glyphosate while still maintaining a reasonable Km value for PEP. Even still, the turnover number (kcat) is significantly reduced in TIPS and manifests as a growth deficit in plants (16, 17). In a more recent report, a glyphosate-intolerant version of the Z. mays EPSPS with kinetics similar to that of bacterial Agrobacterium sp. strain CP4 gene was evolved by starting from the G101A mutant and discovering at least 17 stacked mutations in varying locations on the protein through multiple evolutionary rounds (5). Such a high number of mutations can be challenging to constitute in plants using gene editing techniques. Mutations that improve the catalytic efficiency of TIPS with fewer residue changes can establish a commercially viable glyphosate-resistant EPSPS variant.

To address the need for identifying novel EPSPS variants, a more rapid screening system than in planta or in vitro experiments is required. However, the subtle growth deficits caused by known mutants in plants are not distinguishable in Escherichia coli (E. coli) or other microbial model systems. As an example, the mutant G101A displayed very similar growth to wild-type EPSPS in an E. coli model even though this variant has only ~1% of the catalytic efficiency of the wild-type enzyme (7) and shows a growth deficiency in plants. Here, we demonstrate the use of a simple eukaryotic model, Saccharomyces cerevisiae (S. cerevisiae), as a more applicable host system for evolution of EPSPS variants. Yeast have already been used to study certain herbicide interactions, including herbicides targeting acetyl-CoA carboxylase and other metabolizing enzymes (18, 19). Moreover, the high similarities in aromatic amino acid biosynthesis between yeast and plants can help lead to new targets for glyphosate tolerance (20). To establish this system, we create a synthetic strain of yeast that is dependent upon the heterologous expression of EPSPS for growth. Next, we utilize a directed evolution strategy to select for both glyphosate resistance and improved catalytic properties (i.e., kcat/Km) simultaneously through iterative selection strategies. Finally, we demonstrate only 1 to 2 additional mutations in the background of the TIPS mutant can greatly improve catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) while maintaining high glyphosate insensitivity. In doing so, this work evaluates the importance of synthetic selection strategies on the trajectory of enzyme evolution for dual-trait optimization.

Results and Discussion

Creating an S. cerevisiae Selection Host Strain Dependent on EPSPS Expression.

Previous research has primarily focused on E. coli as a chassis for selecting improved EPSP synthases (5, 6, 21). Here, we sought to develop S. cerevisiae as a model eukaryotic system for glyphosate resistance, thus requiring synthetically addicting this cell to the expression and activity of EPSPS in an effort to create a growth-based selection scheme. Yeast have been reported to be resistant to glyphosate in prior work (22). Validating this finding, we saw that wild-type S. cerevisiae BY4741 is relatively insensitive to high quantities of glyphosate and only showed slight growth inhibition at 5 and 10 mM concentrations, thus suggesting that the native EPSP synthase subunit of ScARO1 is not inhibited strongly by the compound (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

In order to create a selection scheme, it was necessary to remove the native function of EPSPS and complement a mutant version. In plants, EPSPS is a stand-alone enzyme and the fifth step in the pathway from DAHP to aromatic amino acids (Fig. 1A). However, in yeast, ARO1 is pentafunctional and the EPSPS function is in the middle of this protein, thus the elimination of function using previously developed approaches (23) such as truncations were not feasible (Fig. 1). Eliminating ARO1 EPSPS function was accomplished through testing a series of alanine mutations and screening on media lacking aromatic amino acids. To guide mutation selection, a homology model for yeast ARO1p was generated using the structurally similar pentafunctional AROM complex from Chaetomium thermophilum as a template (Fig. 1B). Using this model, structural data from similar EPSPS enzymes were assessed to identify mutations that were likely to disrupt only the EPSPS function while keeping the other four enzyme functions intact (24, 25). Through this analysis, mutations at residues D731 and C853 as well as a triple mutant composed of residues K425, S426, and R430 were evaluated. This analysis suggested that the single mutants each disrupt a purported vital residue in the reaction mechanism and the triple mutant acted to disrupt the entire active site.

Each of the three identified aro1 mutants was expressed from a plasmid (p415-PTEF-PRM9t) in the deletion strain, S. cerevisiae Δaro1::KanMX (Fig. 1C). The resulting strains were then plated on solid media with and without aromatic amino acids to test for growth complementation. The yeast minimal media utilized contained yeast nitrogen base which provides precursors to other molecules such as folates that are required for adequate growth and are also produced through the shikimate pathway. Both D731A and the triple alanine substitution mutant did not grow on media lacking aromatic amino acids (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), whereas C853A retained its native activity and thus was not a viable option. Having identified a putative EPSPS-null allele, the same strains were transformed with a plasmid containing wild-type EPSPS from Z. mays (ZmEPSPS) to see whether the aromatic amino acid synthesis capacity can be revived through complementation. Expression of aro1-D731A effectively abolishes only ScEPSPS function and the growth can be reconstituted by complementing ZmEPSPS. This final strain, containing the knockout of native aro1 function and replacement with p415-PTEF-aro1(D731A)-PRM9t, was grown in liquid media with and without heterologous ZmEPSPS expressed to validate the desired growth-based selection strategy (Fig. 1D) and was thus used for all future experiments in this work. This EPSPS-dependent yeast (denoted as sKR-024) serves as a synthetic yeast model chassis to study known mutants and identify high functional variants.

Characterizing the Synthetic EPSPS Mutation Screening System.

After developing the ZmEPSPS-dependent strain, we next sought to confirm whether this complementation could elicit and recapitulate glyphosate sensitivity and plant responses in S. cerevisiae. To do so, strain sKR-024 was grown in varying glyphosate concentrations to determine sensitivity. This strain indeed displayed a growth deficit in glyphosate compared to WT BY4741, but over time was able to eventually show growth even at high glyphosate concentrations (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). This escape phenotype is likely due to the high expression of ZmEPSPS achieved here with the strong TEF1 promoter and parallels previous literature that observed improved glyphosate resistance in some weeds which have developed high expression of wild-type EPSPS (26). This observation suggests that this synthetic yeast system can recapitulate accurate growth trends from plants.

To determine whether this system could further corroborate observations from previous literature, we generated and complemented the well-studied EPSPS mutants T102I, P106S, and the combined double mutant TIPS (T102I, P106S) into this yeast system. These mutations are reported to result in varying degrees of glyphosate resistance (15). We confirmed that experimental growth data in yeast generally matched the literature trends related to glyphosate tolerance for these EPSPS variants with P106S<T102I<TIPS (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C).

Next, we sought to demonstrate that this difference in tolerance led to a selectable phenotype. To do so, a mock selection for glyphosate resistance was achieved by transforming wild-type EPSPS into sKR-024 containing an RFP cassette and TIPS into sKR-024 without an RFP cassette to enable flow cytometry tracking of population distributions. A 50:50 starting inoculum of each strain was cultured in yeast minimal media containing 0, 2.5, 5, 7.5, and 10 mM glyphosate. Yeast containing wild-type EPSPS (RFP fluorescence around 104 RFU) begin to decrease in count rapidly after the addition of glyphosate in a titratable manner with glyphosate (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). On the other hand, the nonfluorescent strain containing the TIPS variant shows consistent cell count across multiple levels of glyphosate as predicted by its significantly higher Ki for glyphosate.

A final observation from this initial mock selection experiment was the consistently lower cell count of TIPS relative to wild-type EPSPS in the absence of glyphosate, thus suggestive of a lower overall fitness with TIPS. Previous literature has reported that mutants resulting in higher glyphosate tolerance are negatively impacting in Km and/or kcat (5, 15). In plant models, this phenotypically leads to a significant growth deficit in EPSPS mutant–expressing plants when glyphosate is not present (16, 17). To further explore this phenotype (and enable a more powerful dual-selection strategy), we hypothesized that lower expression of the complemented EPSPS enzyme could further exacerbate this deficit and lead to stronger selection pressures. To do so, a synthetic promoter series was constructed consisting of (in order of reported expression level) TEF1p, CYC1p, STE5p, and synthetic minimal yeast promoters Core1p and Core4p (27). In doing so, we find that weaker expression of EPSPS and TIPS does correlate to a lower specific growth rate, longer lag times, and often lower final OD values (Fig. 2 A and B). However, the reduction in growth rate is much greater for TIPS than for EPSPS when the expression strength is weakened (Fig. 2A). Thus, this weaker expression exploits the kinetic deficiency of TIPS and provides a window of selection for identifying EPSPS variants stemming from a TIPS background (and other backgrounds). As synthetic promoter Core1p generated the greatest difference in growth rates between EPSPS and TIPS (~50% reduction in specific growth rate), it was chosen for all future selection experiments. To evaluate strain stability and rule out the possibility of the generation of spontaneous glyphosate resistance, we next grew strain sKR-024 with wild-type EPSPS expressed under Core1p weak promoter in 1 mM glyphosate across a 10-d period with 48 replicates. Complete inhibition of growth was observed across all 48 replicates, suggesting high strain stability (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B).

Having established low copy number and weak promoter expression of EPSPS variants and confirmed high strain stability, we sought to recharacterize phenotypes under field conditions that are used in planta. Fig. 2 C and D thus show the observed growth characteristics of our synthetic yeast model with expression of wild-type EPSPS, P106S, and T102I/P106S (TIPS) in either 0.5 mM or 0 mM glyphosate. It has been reported previously that under standard glyphosate foliar application, 0.3 mM or more of glyphosate (gly) can accumulate in a plant’s root nodules and meristems (28, 29). In yeast minimal media (0 mM gly), both EPSPS and P106S grow very similarly, supporting the fact that they have similar kinetic properties. However, TIPS grows at a significant deficit, with an approximately 42% reduction in specific growth rate, as seen in plant hosts. In 0.5 mM gly, on the other hand, EPSPS is completely inhibited, showing no growth, while P106S shows slightly higher growth than EPSPS after 80 h, supporting its fivefold higher Ki value for glyphosate. TIPS, which is highly insensitive to glyphosate, grows well in the presence of glyphosate (~2× higher specific growth rate than P106S), yet still shows less than WT overall growth in 0 mM gly, owing to low catalytic efficiency. These trends support reported literature of mutants P106S and TIPS grown in various plant hosts (16, 17). As a result, our synthetic yeast model system closely resembles in planta observations and thus can be used to select for dual-trait EPSPS variants aimed at both enzymatic efficiency and glyphosate resistance.

Flexible Selections for the Directed Evolution of EPSP Synthases Using a Synthetic Yeast System.

With a selection host in place, we next sought to evaluate various directed evolution strategies to generate novel variants of corn EPSPS. Mutagenesis libraries were generated using both EPSPS and TIPS variant as background scaffolds. These mutagenic pools were placed under selection separately to observe their independent outcomes toward the goal(s) of improved glyphosate-resistance and/or improved overall catalytic activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and D). Different selection regimes were evaluated for their mutational and phenotypic outcomes, leading to the final hypothesis and confirmation that through flexible selection criteria (i.e., varying the number of subcultures in nonglyphosate media vs. glyphosate media), it is possible to bias the evolutionary outcomes.

As a first pass, mutagenesis libraries from both EPSPS and TIPS background were grown in increasing amounts of glyphosate, starting with 0 mM and ending with 5 mM (SI Appendix, Figs. S5 and S6). For EPSPS, library pools were simply grown in the presence of increasing levels of glyphosate hypothesizing that wild-type EPSPS already confers high growth in nonglyphosate and thus only improved tolerance was necessary. In the case of TIPS, we hypothesized that oscillating between glyphosate and nonglyphosate conditions could prioritize mutations that confer improved growth in nonglyphosate media while maintaining a relatively high glyphosate tolerance already seen in this variant. Multiple, parallel pools from these backgrounds were placed in separate flasks under these selection criteria to observe mutational outcomes (SI Appendix, Figs. S7 and S8).

For the EPSPS library pools, a few functional mutations were identified including A188T, which is in an alpha helix close to P106S and located in a position that may play a significant role in the conformational change that occurs when both substrates, PEP and S3P, are bound. However, mutations with the well-studied proline to serine mutation (P106S) dominated the EPSPS background, thus showcasing the strength of our synthetic yeast model for selection of glyphosate-resistant mutants. For the TIPS mutagenesis pools, a few new mutations appeared in this initial first pass, yet the selection outcomes were primarily dominated by wild-type TIPS. As a result, we next hypothesized that a better set of outgrowth criteria would balance the selection poles, since growth in nonglyphosate seemingly has a lower selective pressure between EPSPS and TIPS than glyphosate-containing conditions.

To achieve a better balance, we next experimented with multiple outgrowths in non-glyphosate-containing media, ending with 1 to 2 outgrowths. To do so, one of the cultures was a fresh library, TIPS(med), with no exposure to any strong selections, one culture was the same library that had a single outgrowth in 2.5 mM glyphosate, and the last was the outcome of the oscillating selection ending on 2.5 mM glyphosate from SI Appendix, Fig. S6 (schematically depicted in SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Through this experiment, we sought to evaluate the hypothesis that using various starting points would lead to variations in the outcomes and inform a better-directed evolution campaign for dual-trait evolution. Several interesting observations emerge from the outcomes of these selection strategies (SI Appendix, Figs. S10–S12). First, it was possible to revert the TIPS mutant back to wild-type EPSPS by selecting in 0 mM for 4 subcultures followed by plating on 0 mM glyphosate agar plates. This shows the utility of this selection pole for improved basal enzymatic traits and activity. Second, it was only necessary to have a single outgrowth on high levels of glyphosate in either liquid media or agar to completely eliminate any variants with low glyphosate tolerance, validating the strength of this selection pole in comparison to the nonglyphosate selection pole. More subtle titrations with weaker content (such as 0.25 mM) could be tested in the future to further improve this selection. Third, it was observed that the resulting mutants varied depending on the exact selection pressure and evolutionary trajectory, suggesting that each approach generates slightly different traits in glyphosate and nonglyphosate media. With this proof of principle established that balancing the selection poles is important to deliver tailored protein traits, we proceeded with a more informed directed evolution campaign strategy.

A final directed evolution campaign was then pursued with the same library mutagenesis pools starting from either EPSPS or TIPS genetic background using the more optimal selection strategy shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S13. The first stage selects only for high-functioning EPSPS variants that confer strong growth characteristics and the second stage subcultures into increasing levels of glyphosate to provide a graded selection pressure to unearth glyphosate tolerant mutants. The top hits from each stage of these selections are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S13. Depending on the selection pressure applied, different outcomes were observed. For example, from the EPSPS background, mutants P106S V125L and P106A A113V emerged from the high glyphosate selections, whereas single mutations such as V332A and A188T emerged from lower glyphosate selections. Interestingly, significant improvements in EPSPS under nonglyphosate conditions were not observed, although a few mutants such as T368A and E225D Y234F were isolated after multiple outgrowths in nonglyphosate media. Presumably, selection in nonglyphosate could identify variants stemming from wild-type EPSPS with better enzymatic efficiency, although these mutations were not observed. For the TIPS background library pools, a large range of mutational outcomes were observed. Under nonglyphosate selection, TIPS was either reverted to variants containing only P106S or entirely back to wild-type EPSPS, along with a few other single mutations such as V377I. As expected, when the pools were moved back into glyphosate-containing media, all resulting variants contained the TIPS mutation plus a few other additional mutations. Of note were mutants TIPS V66L M104L and TIPS P126S M217L K296R, which were highly dominant in their respective pools and were not observed in the first pass unbalanced oscillating selection.

By employing such a bespoke dual-trait selection strategy that balanced the two selection poles, novel mutations were identified that were isolated in greater abundance than the starting points, EPSPS and TIPS. Interestingly, single mutations that have been previously reported to have high glyphosate insensitivity but extremely low catalytic efficiency, such as G101A and T102I, were not observed from any of the selection pools, further validating the strength of our devised selection strategy to enable dual-traits phenotypes.

In Vivo Characterization of Novel Mutants from Varied Selection Criteria.

Select mutants isolated from the final directed evolution campaign with the improved selection strategy were further evaluated in assorted glyphosate concentrations to determine growth characteristics. Mutants isolated from an EPSPS background were evaluated in media containing 0 mM and 0.25 mM glyphosate with the latter being the concentration at which wild-type EPSPS cannot grow after 100 h. Mutants isolated from a TIPS background were evaluated in media containing 0 mM and 5 mM glyphosate, since the goal for this campaign was to maintain high glyphosate tolerance while enhancing catalytic efficiency. These results are shown in Fig. 3 as a phenotypic landscape. The nonglyphosate monoselection highlighted in blue represents mutations resulting from five subcultures in 0 mM glyphosate. The low glyphosate dual selection highlighted in light green represents mutants resulting from an additional subculture into 0.5 mM glyphosate. Last, the high glyphosate dual selection highlighted in dark green represents mutations isolated after subcultures in increasing levels of glyphosate, up to 10 mM.

(B) TIPS evolution entry point mutation pools. These results demonstrate that flexible selection criteria can generate tailored enzymes. For example, pools grown solely in yeast minimal media without glyphosate give phenotypes that tend to concentrate in the lower right quadrant of the phenotypic landscape where normal enzyme activity and growth characteristics dominate. For EPSPS, this resulted in the isolation of wild-type EPSPS along with a few other mutations with similar growth characteristics to EPSPS (Fig. 3A). For TIPS, simple reversions of the T102I or even complete reversion back to EPSPS was seen in selections that overly biased improved catalytic efficiency (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, the single subculture in 0.5 mM glyphosate, representing a low glyphosate dual selection, resulted in new mutants that did not dominate in the 0 mM glyphosate monoselection but have moderate glyphosate tolerance, such as A118T and V332A from the EPSPS background and TIPS P126L and TIPS I177V from the TIPS background. Last, selection in higher concentrations of glyphosate (2.5, 5, and 10 mM) resulted primarily in mutants that retain high glyphosate tolerance and also high growth in nonglyphosate, such as TIPS V66L M104L and TIPS P126S M217L K296R from the TIPS background.

The top performing mutant, TIPS P126S M217L K296R and single mutant TIPS P126S were further evaluated more rigorously to determine specific growth rates in comparison to EPSPS and TIPS. TIPS P126S M217L K296R confers high glyphosate tolerance (even higher than TIPS) and retains normal growth characteristics in the synthetic yeast host, similar to that of EPSPS, with only a slightly extended lag time. To determine the underlying mutations here that led to the highest improvement in growth phenotype, we next generated each combination of mutant TIPS P126S M217L K296R (SI Appendix, Fig. S14) and found that M217L does not confer any improved growth traits and may even be slightly deleterious in some conditions. Moreover, both P126S and K296R as single mutants improved growth in nonglyphosate media compared to TIPS and the additive effect of both P126S and K296R can further improve growth phenotype to nearly wild-type growth levels, representing over a 100% increase in specific growth rate (SI Appendix, Fig. S15).

Next, we determined the portability of mutants P126S and K296R to other glyphosate-resistant mutant scaffolds including G101A (SI Appendix, Fig. S16), T102I (SI Appendix, Fig. S17), and P106S (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). We find that these mutations are capable of conferring improved growth in both 0 mM and 5 mM glyphosate for all known glyphosate-resistant single mutant backgrounds. Of note, we observe significant improvements to both G101A and T102I in 0 mM, two mutants that independently have abysmally low catalytic efficiencies and strong growth defects. For example, G101A PSKR was observed to confer a specific growth rate over fourfold higher than G101A in 0 mM gly, and roughly 1.2-fold higher in 5 mM gly. Furthermore, mutations P126S and K296R (PSKR), in certain combinations, led to improved growth in 5 mM glyphosate for certain variants. For example, P106S with double mutant PSKR is capable of observable growth in 5 mM glyphosate after roughly 80 h, whereas P106S on its own exhibits no growth in high levels of glyphosate.

Last, we evaluated mutant TIPS PSKR at varying levels of glyphosate, from mild to extreme. TIPS PSKR performs extremely well in every condition of glyphosate, ranging from 0.05 mM to 5 mM, and as shown previously, performs very similarly to wild-type EPSPS in 0 mM glyphosate (Fig. 4). TIPS PSKR is thus extremely robust and confers a highly similar growth rate across any condition, further supporting its potential use in planta.

In Vitro Characterization and Crystallization of TIPS P126S, K296R.

We sought to characterize the highest-performing protein variant, TIPS PSKR, in vitro to further understand the kinetic and structural basis of improvement. As shown in Table 1, the enzymatic efficiency of protein variant TIPS PSKR is significantly higher, roughly 2.5-fold, than the evolutionary starting point TIPS, with a roughly 50% increase in kcat. Enzymatic activity, including inhibitory constant Ki, for EPSPS and TIPS closely corroborate values determined by previous reports (5). Amino acid replacements P126S and K296R have beneficial effects on their own to both Km and kcat and are additive when combined, similar to the observations of the in vivo growth characterization. Furthermore, the high Ki is maintained for each of the variants, showing similar or slightly higher values of around 800 μM compared to the starting point, TIPS. These findings confirm the phenotype endowed by the dual-trait selection pressure employed during the directed evolution campaigns.

To understand how P126S and K296R mutations improve the enzyme activity, all Z. mays EPSPS variants (Wild type, TIPS, TIPS P126S, TIPS K296R, TIPS P126S K296R) were investigated by X-ray crystallography. The crystal structure of ZmEPSPS has not yet been reported in the literature, only deduced in silico by similarity based on the recently reported structure of Arabidopsis thaliana EPSPS (30). Each of the five variants was cocrystallized with shikimate-3-phosphate (S3P) and glyphosate to capture the state close to the active reaction center. The crystals diffracted, and the final structures were refined with resolutions extending to ~2 Å for all five variants (SI Appendix, Table S1). With the low symmetry space group (P1) for the protein, it was only possible to scale two of the variants (TIPS K296R and TIPS P126S K296R) to completeness of less than 90% due to the limitation of crystal orientation (SI Appendix, Table S1). However, all ligand densities were very clear in the structures (SI Appendix, Fig. S19A). This analysis showcased that Z. mays EPSPS preserves an identical configuration to that of other reported EPSP synthases (30–32). As observed previously, the domains are highly dynamic and close upon ligand association (24, 33). For each of the mutants, the locations and identities of the mutated residues were clearly distinguishable from the high-resolution structures (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S19 B and C), yet the overall folding of the protein was nearly identical. The structure of TIPS variant is also consistent with the previous observation that TIPS mutation endows glyphosate resistance to the enzyme by shifting the location of Gly101 (15).

Crystal structure of ZmEPSPS TIPS (PSKR) with mutations highlighted. The original TIPS mutations are circled (dotted yellow). Complete X-ray crystallography data collection and refinement statistics are provided in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Unlike TIPS mutation sites, P126S and K296R locate ~14 Å and ~26 Å away from the active site, thus making it impossible to directly contribute to protein–ligand interactions (Fig. 5). Nevertheless, both mutations likely stabilize the surface energy of protein and serve to allosterically improve the enzyme activity, though improvements in turnover and other explanations cannot be ruled out. For example, P126 is a nonpolar residue located at a sharp beta-turn at the protein surface of EPSPS. A small polar residue like serine replaces proline, relieving the torsion tension of such a tight turn while being further stabilized by the solvation effect (Fig. 5). On the other side of the protein surface, K296 is a positively charged residue that another positively charged residue, arginine, can replace. Although the interaction around K296 (or R296) is not well defined due to structure flexibility, substitution with arginine may provide better surface stability due to increased electrostatic interactions or hydrogen bonding (Fig. 5).

In Planta Characterization of EPSPS Variants.

Finally, we characterized the EPSPS variants in a plant system to determine whether any of the observed improvements showed biological improvements in planta. We turned to maize as a model system to test the functionality of the EPSPS variants because of detailed previous characterization of glyphosate tolerance in maize and a suitable transgenic plant generation protocol that relies on glyphosate. We generated plant transformation vectors in which we used the native maize EPSPS locus, that is, the native gene promoter elements, the native introns and exons and the native 3′ untranslated region, and introduced mutations that corresponded to each of the selected amino acid changes. We used the native locus as a means to mimic as closely as possible how the selected EPSPS variants would perform if the variants were introduced directly in the genome without the help of strong expression elements. The following variants were generated: TIPS (as a positive control); TIPS P126S M217L K296R; TIPS P126S K296R; T102I P126S K296R; P106S P126S K296R; and P126S K296R. The last three mutants were selected to test the P126S and K296R mutants with only a partial TIPS background or without TIPS in the background. Maize cells were transformed with all six of these EPSPS variants.

The transformation protocol utilizes glyphosate as a part of the process to select developing plantlets. Therefore, in order for a developing plantlet to survive, the plantlet must be expressing the EPSPS transgene, and the EPSPS variant must be capable of providing tolerance to glyphosate while maintaining a reasonable catalytic efficiency because the native genomic EPSPS will be nonfunctional due to the glyphosate in the selection media. Previous work has shown that the addition of a second wild type copy of the maize EPSPS as a transgene is not sufficient to provide glyphosate tolerance capable of surviving the glyphosate selection during transformation. This glyphosate selection process during transformation therefore provides an early indication of the enzymatic capabilities of the EPSPS variants before testing of plants in greenhouses by quantifying the number of successfully regenerated plants by transformation of EPSPS variants.

The TIPS P126S M217L K296R and TIPS P126S K296R EPSPS outperformed the other variants and successfully regenerated plants with TIPS P126S K296R performing similar to the TIPS positive control (Fig. 6A). The variants in which partial or missing TIPS backgrounds were tested did not produce as many regenerated plants suggesting the full TIPS background was needed for the performance of the new variants. The regenerated plants were screened to identify molecularly clean transformed plants (mostly to eliminate plants in which multiple copies of the transgene were found) and further screened for plant health (a measure of their ability to continue to grow following transplanting to greenhouse media). Following this screen to determine advanced plants, only the TIPS control and the TIPS P126S M217L K296R and TIPS P126S K296R EPSPS variants produced more than one plant, further demonstrating the leading EPSPS variants (Fig. 6A).

Maize plants expressing selected EPSPS variants demonstrated tolerance to glyphosate. (A) Wild type maize plants were transformed with the indicated variants and with TIPS as a positive control. Plants were regenerated on media which included glyphosate to select only those plants that were transformed with the indicated EPSPS variant and demonstrated glyphosate tolerance. Following regeneration of plants, the plants were allowed to recover and were screened for molecular quality and plant health. The resulting advanced plants were moved to the greenhouse for seed production and surplus plants were spray challenged with glyphosate. (B) Only two EPSPS variants resulted in enough plants for a glyphosate spray challenge, the TIPS positive control and TIPS P126S/K296R. Plants were challenged with a glyphosate spray and injury observed 1 wk following treatment. Three of the 10 TIPS control plants were injured by the glyphosate spray while none of the TIPS P126S/K296R expressing plants showed visual injury. (C) Plants were assayed in a hybrid background in the following generation in a typical greenhouse spray assay and monitored for glyphosate injury using visual injury ratings (plant stunting, chlorosis, necrosis, malformation, and death) at 18 d posttreatment.

The surviving plants were moved to a greenhouse setting for production of seeds, where only the TIPS control and TIPS P126S M217L K296R and TIPS P126S K296R EPSPS variant expressing plants produced seed. However, the TIPS control and TIPS P126S K296R EPSPS variant expressing plants had produced an excess of surviving plants. Therefore, these extra plants were subjected to a traditional glyphosate spray similar to what would be done in a field situation to further assay the potential glyphosate tolerance. The ratio of plants showing, or lacking glyphosate injury is used as an indicator of glyphosate tolerance. In these assays, the TIPS P126S K296R EPSPS variant expressing plants outperformed the control TIPS plants indicating very robust glyphosate tolerance. (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S20). Therefore, we conclude that the TIPS P126S M217L K296R and TIPS P126S K296R EPSPS variants are catalytically active enzymes capable of providing glyphosate tolerance in maize plants, and, furthermore, the TIPS P126S K296R EPSPS variant was the lead variant tested.

To further characterize the TIPS P126S M217L K296R and TIPS P126S K296R EPSPS variants in planta, these plants were assayed in a hybrid background in the following generation in a typical greenhouse spray assay (Fig. 6C and SI Appendix, Fig. S21). In this assay, both variants provided excellent tolerance to an equivalent 1× field dose of glyphosate. Interestingly, the TIPS P126S M217L K296R variant also provided full tolerance at the 2× field rate suggesting robust tolerance. Both the TIPS P126S K296R and the TIPS control plants showed minor injury at the 2× field rate. This testing confirms that both TIPS P126S M217L K296R and TIPS P126S K296R EPSPS variants can provide robust tolerance in greenhouse testing designed to mimic field conditions.

Conclusions

Selection systems are critical for determining the outcomes of directed evolution campaigns. In this work, we develop a synthetic yeast host to characterize and evolve novel EPSP synthase variants. By evaluating various selection pressures and the outcomes of multiple mutagenesis pools, we then find that our synthetic yeast host can enable the selection of EPSPS variants with tunable dual traits through outgrowth in media lacking or containing glyphosate. Using these flexible selections, we show that it is possible to isolate variants with specific phenotypes simply by altering the outgrowth conditions and varying the number of subcultures. This resulted in the isolation of variants with properties ranging from low glyphosate tolerance or low enzymatic efficiency to variants with combined high glyphosate tolerance and high enzymatic efficiency. By balancing the selection pressure through dual-trait outgrowth stages, we evolve the well-studied double mutant TIPS to grow better in a wide range of glyphosate exposure levels with only a single round of evolution. In doing so, we characterize the mutational and phenotypic landscape of evolutionary outcomes for this protein. With the most promising protein variants, we show that simple changes of 1 to 2 amino acid exchanges can boost enzymatic efficiency in vitro multiple fold while maintaining robust glyphosate tolerance. Given the findings here, more targeted directed evolution campaigns, such as to the loop region near residues 125 to 130 that closes upon binding of S3P, and additional mutagenesis rounds would likely yield fruitful outcomes with improved phenotypes. We expect this synthetic yeast host to be useful for rapidly prototyping novel EPSPS variants that can be further studied in planta. Altogether, this study highlights the potential for dynamic selection criteria to tune proteins toward a specific dual-trait phenotype of interest and for yeast models as a general synthetic tool to study simple herbicide mechanisms.

Materials and Methods

Strains and Media.

Yeast plasmids were propagated in E. coli DH10B. S. cerevisiae strain BY4741 was obtained from the European S. cerevisiae Archive for Functional Analysis (EUROSCARF, Y00000). Yeast genomic DNA was extracted using the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega). Yeast were routinely grown in YPD, YSC, and YMM. YMM was used for selections and characterization of EPSPS mutants and consists of 1× YNB [6.7 g L−1 of Yeast Nitrogen Base without amino acids (YNB, Difco)], 1× glucose (20 g/L), 1× histidine, and 1× methionine. Solid media contained 2.0% agar. Yeast and bacterial strains were stored at −80 °C in 20% glycerol in 2 mL Cryogenic Storage Vials (Fisherbrand).

Transformations.

E. coli transformations were routinely performed via electroporation with a BioRad Genepulser Xcell at 2.5 kV in 2 mm Electroporation Cuvettes (Bioexpress). Typically, 100 ng of cloning product either from Gibson assembly or ligation were added to 50 µL of competent cells. Transformations were rapidly recovered in 500 µL of SOC media. Recovered cultures were then plated or streaked on LB agar plates containing 100 µg mL−1 ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The next day, individual colonies were picked into 4 mL of LB media containing 100 µg mL−1 ampicillin and grown overnight at 30 °C. The following day, plasmids were then isolated (GeneJET Plasmid Miniprep Kit, Thermo Scientific) and confirmed via Sanger sequencing.

Yeast transformations were routinely performed using the Frozen EZ Yeast Transformation II Kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In general, 25 µL of chemically competent S. cerevisiae BY4741 strains were transformed with 100-500 ng of plasmid DNA. Transformations were incubated at 30 °C for 1-2 h and then plated on selective media, either YSC-Ura, YSC-Leu, and YSC-Ura-Leu for 2 d at 30 °C. Individual colonies were then picked at random into 1-2 mL of appropriate selective media and grown 2 d at 30 °C with 225 RPM orbital shaking.

Plasmid and Strain Construction.

Oligonucleotides were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (SI Appendix, Table S1). PCR was performed using Q5 Hot Start polymerases (NEB). PCRs and other DNA products were cleaned using a NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (Macherey Nagel). Vectors were digested using various restriction enzymes as noted (NEB) and prepared for ligation using Antarctic phosphatase (NEB). The low copy CEN/ARS vector backbone from a modified Mumberg collection p415-PTEF1-PRM9t and p416-PTEF1-CYC1t with strong TEF1 promoter were used for all initial experiments unless otherwise noted (34, 35). ScARO1 was PCR amplified from S. cerevisiae BY4741 genomic DNA and cloned into plasmid p415-PTEF1-PRM9t via the Gibson method for overexpression in S. cerevisiae (36). ZmEPSPS was codon optimized for S. cerevisiae, synthesized as a gBlock (IDT), and cloned initially into plasmid p416-PTEF1-CYC1t. These plasmids are referred to as p415-TEF-ARO1mut (S. cerevisiae endogenous gene) and p416-TEF-EPSPS (Z. mays homolog) throughout this work. The TEF1 promoter was selected for these vectors because it is reported to be a strong promoter that exhibits moderately more consistent activity than other very strong promoters across different carbon sources and concentrations of glucose (37). Promoters for the promoter series were either synthesized via oligonucleotide synthesis (IDT) for short core promoters Core1p and Core4p or amplified from the S. cerevisiae genome for STE5p and CYC1p (27). The resulting plasmids are referred to as p416-Core1p (or other promoter)-EPSPS (low copy, CEN/ARS) and p415-TEF-ARO1mut (low copy, CEN/ARS) throughout this work.

Mutagenesis Methods and Library Construction for Mutants of ARO1 and EPSPS.

ScARO1 point mutants were generated using the Agilent QuikChange Multi Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit. ZmEPSPS was amplified using Agilent Mutazyme II to introduce mutations into the coding sequence (CDS) to generate mutagenesis pools. Two mutation frequencies were targeted–approximately 1 to 3 and 3 to 5 mutations per CDS length, denoted as low and medium frequency. The resulting PCR product pool was cloned into p416-Core1p through either Gibson assembly or ligation cloning with T4 DNA Ligase and transformed into E. coli DH10B, where approximately 8 × 105 to 4 × 106 variants per library were harvested depending on the mutagenesis pool for transformation into yeast (35, 36). DNA was isolated and sequenced from 10 individual colonies and an average of 1 to 3 mutations per CDS for low frequency and 2 to 5 mutations per CDS for medium frequency was achieved for each library, respectively; limited wild type template sequence was observed. The low copy plasmid p416-Core1p was selected as the recipient vector for this experiment to minimize the potential impact of plasmid copy number variation and to ensure maximized growth differentiation when comparing mutant EPSPS sequences (38). After DNA isolation from E. coli, the library was transformed into S. cerevisiae strain sKR-024 using a high-efficiency lithium acetate protocol (39), yielding libraries of approximately 3 × 105 to 2 × 106 variants, depending on cloning and transformation efficiency.

Flow Cytometry.

Yeast cultures were either started directly from a random colony or from freezer stock and picked in triplicate into appropriate selective media, either YSC-Ura, YSC-Leu, and YSC-Ura-Leu. Cultures were grown for 48 h at 30 °C to saturation phase. Fully grown yeast cultures were then diluted back to a cell density of 0.01 (optical density measured at 600 nm [OD600]) and allowed to grow for 48 h at 30 °C in a 96 deep-well plate shaker. Fluorescence was then analyzed via flow cytometry (BD Accuri C6 Flow Cytometer, BD Biosciences) at an excitation of 588 nm and emission of 633 nm for mKate2 RFP. Yeast populations of interest were then gated according to relative size and complexity using a logarithmic plot of side scatter (SSC) and forward scatter (FSC) and spectra were generated outlining RFP fluorescence intensity vs. cell count. FlowJo software was used to analyze all flow cytometry data.

Directed Evolution Workflow.

Yeast library pools were started from freezer stock and moved into yeast minimal media with or without glyphosate depending on the selection criteria applied. In general, library pools were diluted back to an OD600 of 0.01 and allowed to grow until saturation. Depending on mutants present in each pool this typically ranged from 48 to 96 h. Once saturation phase was reached, a portion of the pools were freezer stocked in 20% glycerol and then subcultured into the next set of media, either containing or lacking glyphosate. Isolation of mutants was then performed by selecting random colonies on agar plates, either containing or lacking glyphosate as indicated in workflow diagrams. Unless otherwise indicated, final selections were all isolated on agar plates lacking glyphosate. EPSPS library ORFS were isolated from random colonies that survived on glyphosate selection on plates using a modified colony PCR method with a boiling step containing NaOH and Triton X-100. PCRs were confirmed via gel electrophoresis, cleaned then subjected to Sanger sequencing. Sequenced PCR products were subsequently reamplified and transformed into a clean strain of S. cerevisiae sKR-024 for confirmation of phenotype in biological triplicate.

In Vivo Characterization of EPSPS Variants and Growth Rate Analysis.

EPSPS variants were recloned into a clean p416-Core1p backbone and strain sKR-024 was transformed with selected plasmids as described above. Colonies were then picked in triplicate into YSC-Ura-Leu and outgrown for 2 d at 30 °C. Cultures were then diluted back to an OD600 of 0.001 in yeast minimal media (YMM) containing varying amounts of glyphosate as indicated. Growth rate measurements were then obtained using an Infinite M200 PRO microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland) with incubation at 30 °C for 72 to 120 h depending on growth rate of selected variants. The specific growth rate, represented by the symbol μ, was calculated as the slope of the tangent line at the point of inflection on the sigmoid curve. This curve was obtained by fitting the data points of the natural logarithm of absorbance at 600 nm plotted over time.

Statistical Analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism’s built-in functions. One-way ANOVA was used to determine statistical differences between group means. Pairs of group means were compared Tukey’s post hoc test. Number of biological replicates and significance levels are highlighted as appropriate.

In Vitro Characterization of EPSPS Mutants.

Activity was determined by quantifying release of Pi, coupled to a reaction with MESG, catalyzed by purine-nucleoside phosphorylase using the EnzChek Phosphate Assay Kit from Invitrogen, first published in 1992 (40). 95 µL of master mix was added to each well and 5 µL of the varied substrate dilutions were multichanneled into the plate to start the reaction simultaneously for all conditions. Absorbance measurements at 360 nm were taken every 10 s for 3 min to capture the initial reaction rate of each condition. Varying levels of substrate were added to generate the resulting reaction rate curves. Each condition was performed in triplicate. Ki was determined as previously described (41). Data were fit to the Michaelis–Menten kinetics equation and were also validated through GraphPad Prism’s built-in function for each variant.

Protein Expression and Purification.

For protein expression of EPSPS and its variants, E. coli BL21 (DE3) was used as the expression host. Electrocompetent cells were transformed with the corresponding constructed plasmids, pET28a(+) with selected EPSPS variants (wild-type, TIPS, TIPS P126S, TIPS K296R, TIPS P126S/K296R. A single colony of an E. coli BL21 (DE3) strain harboring one of the constructed plasmids was inoculated into 2 mL of lysogeny broth (LB) medium with 50 µg/mL kanamycin and grown overnight at 37 °C/225 rpm. The overnight-grown culture (using 100 µL) was scaled up with 2,000-fold dilution in a 500-mL triple baffled shake flask and grown to a cell density of 0.8 (optical density [OD600]) at 37 °C/225 rpm. Protein expression was induced by adding 0.2 mM of isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and cells were cultured for 24 h at 20 °C/225 rpm. The induced cell culture was harvested by centrifugation at 4,000 g and 4 °C for 20 min. Cell pellets were then resuspended in 25 mL of Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) pH 7.0 buffer containing 10 mM imidazole, 1 g/L of lysozyme and 5 µL of Pierce™ Universal Nuclease (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), followed by mixing on a rocker for 30 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, cells were lysed by sonication and the resulting cell lysate was centrifuged at 14,000 g and 4 °C for 20 min to obtain the supernatant that contains soluble proteins. Target proteins from the supernatant were purified by HisPur™ Ni-NTA Resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to the manufacturer’s instruction.

The eluate was dialyzed with 3C protease added to the dialysis cassette, into the appropriate buffer followed by size-exclusion fast protein liquid chromatography. All EPSP synthase variants were stored in 20 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, and 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol. The protein concentration was then determined by using the Coomassie Plus Bradford Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the Infinite M200 PRO microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland) to measure the absorbance of assay mixtures. The presence and purity of the purified proteins were assessed by sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis.

Protein Crystallization.

To identify crystallization conditions of Z. mays EPSP synthase variants, 20 mg/mL purified wild-type EPSP synthase sample was preincubated with 5 mM shikimate-3-phosphate and 5 mM glyphosate for 3 h at 4 °C, and then screened with sparse-matrix screening using Phoenix robotic system (Art Robinson). The initial crystallization hit was identified with rod-shaped crystals appearing after incubating screening plates at 25 °C for 3 d. The crystallization conditions for all EPSP synthase variants were optimized as 0.1 M MES, 25% PEG 8000, pH 6.5, set up as sitting-drop vapor diffusion. All sitting drops were set up by mixing 1.33 μL of reservoir solution and 0.67 μL of 20 mg/mL protein sample.

X-ray Diffraction Data Collection, Data Processing, and Model Refinement.

Individual EPSP synthase crystals were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen after being cryoprotected with 30% (v/v) glycerol. X-ray diffraction data were collected at the 23-ID-B beamline in Advanced Photon Source (Lemont, IL). The X-ray diffraction patterns for all EPSP synthase crystals were processed to ~2 Å resolution using HKL2000 (42). The wild-type EPSP synthase structure was solved by molecular replacement with EPSP synthase from E. coli as the initial search model (PDB code 1G6T). The molecular replacement solution for wild-type EPSP synthase was iteratively built and refined using Coot (43) and Phenix (44) refinement packages. Crystal structures of other variants were solved by molecular replacement with wild-type EPSP synthase structure and iteratively refined. Procheck and MolProbity evaluated the quality of the finalized EPSP synthase structures. The final diffraction data collection and structural determination statistics are shown in SI Appendix, Table S1.

In Planta Testing.

Maize mature seed embryo explants were transformed with selected EPSPS variants using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation and transgenic plants were selected on glyphosate containing selection media as previously described (45). The complete DNA and final amino acid sequences for the transformed variants can be found in the following GenBank records (OR582967, OR582968, OR582969, OR582970, OR582971, OR582972). To mimic the native expression patterns of EPSPS in plants as closely as possible, the transformation vectors only used the native maize promoter, 3′ untranslated region (UTR) and preserved the introns and exons of the native EPSPS. See SI Appendix, Table S5 for detailed information of the transformation construct. Plants containing only single copies of the selected transgene were selected via molecular analysis as previously described (46). Plants that were successfully transformed with only a single copy of the selected EPSPS variants were transplanted to greenhouse growth media and allowed to grow to maturity under standard greenhouse conditions for seed collection. A subset of extra plants from two of the EPSPS variants were selected and sprayed at approximately the four-leaf stage with a commercial formulation of glyphosate, Roundup® PowerMax3 at 840 g active ingredient/ha. These sprayed plants were returned to the greenhouse and glyphosate injury (plant stunting and leaf yellowing) was observed at 1 wk following spray applications.

Additional plant screening was completed in a hybrid background by following the transformed plants to the next generation. These plants were grown in the greenhouse and plants containing a single copy of the transgene (hemizygous plants) from two independent transformation events for each of the two variants that were advanced were selected in a spray test at the third leaf stage using a commercial formulation of glyphosate, Roundup® PowerMax3, at two spray rates: 1,120 g active ingredient/ha (1× the field rate) and 2,240 g active ingredient/ha (2× the field rate). An untreated control without glyphosate was also included. Nontransformed control plants and TIPS positive control plants were sprayed as controls. Photographs of representative plants were taken at 12 d posttreatment and visual injury ratings (plant stunting, chlorosis, necrosis, malformation, and death) were taken at 18 d posttreatment.
