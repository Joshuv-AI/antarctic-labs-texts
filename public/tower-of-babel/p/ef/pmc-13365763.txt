Neurodevelopmental impairment in infants with necrotizing enterocolitis: a comprehensive review of mechanisms, outcomes, and emerging strategies
Authors: Michele Bosisio, Miguel A Garcia, Augusto Zani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNecrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a severe gastrointestinal condition predominantly affecting preterm infants and is characterized by intestinal inflammation, ischemia, and, in advanced cases, bowel necrosis. Although advances in neonatal care have improved survival, infants with NEC remain at substantially increased risk of long-term neurodevelopmental impairment (NDI), including cognitive, motor, sensory, and behavioral deficits. Clinical severity, surgical NEC, and comorbidities, including intraventricular hemorrhage and metabolic instability, further exacerbate neurodevelopmental risk. Neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies have consistently revealed structural and microstructural brain abnormalities that provide early prognostic markers. While prematurity contributes to baseline vulnerability, growing evidence indicates that NEC-specific factors, such as systemic inflammation, disruption of the gut-brain axis, and immune-mediated injury, play a central role in the pathogenesis of brain injury. Preclinical research targeting inflammation, oxidative stress, microbiome modulation, and high-mobility group box 1-Toll-like receptor 4 (HMGB1-TLR4) signaling has demonstrated promising neuroprotective effects, underscoring the need for translational strategies. This review comprehensively examines the literature on NDI in infants with NEC to compare the clinical outcomes of this population of babies with those of their preterm peers, evaluate the underlying mechanisms of brain injury associated with NEC, and discuss emerging preventative and therapeutic strategies to address this morbidity.

Introduction

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is one of the most common and severe surgical intestinal emergencies in infants, affecting primarily premature babies.1 2 Despite significant advances in neonatal care over the last decades, NEC remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality in infants worldwide.1 3 Mortality rates remain high, ranging from 15% to 30% overall, and are proportional to the extent of the intestinal damage, with babies with pancolitis experiencing mortality rates greater than 50%.4 Severe inflammation leading to intestinal perforation and requiring surgical intervention is mostly reported in very low birth weight (VLBW) infants and is associated with lower survival rates.5 Infants who survive NEC are also commonly affected by both short-term and long-term sequelae that involve not only the gastrointestinal function due to the severe bowel inflammation sometimes leading to intestinal failure but also injury to other organs such as the lungs and brain due to systemic inflammatory reaction syndrome.4 5 Approximately 40% of NEC survivors develop neurodevelopmental impairment (NDI).6 Although NDI was historically attributed to the vulnerability of the premature brain, several studies demonstrated that neurological outcomes in babies with NEC are worse than in gestational age-matched infants.1 6 7 Since the first studies reporting neurodevelopmental outcomes of babies with NEC before the 1990s,8 awareness of and research in this aspect of NEC-related morbidity have considerably increased.6 In this review, we comprehensively examine the literature on NDI in infants with NEC to compare the clinical outcomes of this population of babies with those of their preterm peers, evaluate the underlying mechanisms of brain injury associated with NEC, and discuss emerging preventative and therapeutic strategies to address this morbidity.

Clinical manifestations and comparison with preterm controls

Neurodevelopmental and imaging assessment

Definitions of NDI vary considerably across studies, contributing to the wide range of reported neurological outcomes in infants with NEC. A systematic review of the literature estimated that approximately 40% of NEC survivors develop NDI, defined as a significant deviation or loss of neurodevelopmental function resulting in below-average performance on neurological, cognitive, or developmental assessment tools.6 This definition included motor deficits such as cerebral palsy (abnormal muscle tone and impaired control of movement or posture), sensory deficits such as vision (decreased functional vision or blindness) and hearing impairment (requiring hearing aids), and neurobehavioral and/or educational issues. Among the standardized developmental assessment tools used to define NDI, the Bayley Scale of Infant Development (BSID-II/III) is the most frequently used. Using this method, scores <70 (i.e., >2 standard deviations below the mean) on the Mental Development Index (MDI) or Psychomotor Development Index (PDI) reflect significant NDI.9 10 Using these assessment tools, preterm infants with NEC exhibited lower BSID-III scores in cognitive, language, and motor function at 18 months of corrected gestational age compared with preterm controls, a finding also confirmed at 36 months.3 7 11 A meta-analysis showed that cerebral palsy occurs in about 17% of survivors with NEC compared with 7% of matched preterm controls.6 Furthermore, NEC is associated with a threefold increase in visual and hearing impairment.6 12 Notably, these sensory deficits do not appear to result from primary ophthalmologic or otologic pathology, as survivors with NEC do not have higher rates of retinopathy of prematurity or middle-ear disease.6 13 Conversely, these impairments likely reflect central nervous system injury, supported by findings of prolonged conduction times and altered myelination patterns at brainstem auditory evoked response testing.6 14 In several cohorts, NEC remained an independent predictor of cognitive delay even after adjusting for gestational age, sepsis, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, and postnatal steroid exposure.15 In these patients, the severity of systemic inflammation and bowel necrosis plays a crucial role in the development of NDI: babies with NEC who require surgical intervention are the most vulnerable (with higher rates of cerebral palsy and poorer verbal and non-verbal performance), but medically treated patients with NEC also showed a higher risk of NDI compared with preterm control.16 17

From a neuroimaging standpoint, head ultrasonography (US) has been used as the primary tool for infants with NEC, followed by brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) that is usually performed at term-equivalent age (~40 weeks of corrected gestational age).1820 Conversely, brain computed tomography (CT) scans have no role in this population due to the low diagnostic yield and radiation exposure. Head US studies are typically performed serially during Neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission and have consistently shown structural abnormalities in survivors with NEC,6 whereby several cohorts reported a higher incidence of ventriculomegaly, intraventricular hemorrhage, and cystic periventricular leukomalacia, particularly in surgically managed babies with NEC compared with preterm controls.62123 MRI provides higher sensitivity for detecting white matter injury (WMI) and impaired brain growth. MRI studies performed at term-equivalent age demonstrated that infants with NEC have reduced brain volume and biparietal diameter, delayed cortical folding, and enlarged extra-axial spaces and ventricles.6 10 24 25 Recent MRI series performed at 36-40 weeks of corrected gestational age confirmed that WMI is highly prevalent in surgical NEC, with lesions identified in half of infants, and cerebellar abnormalities such as hemorrhage, siderosis, or reduced volume in nearly one-third.2628 Using diffusion tensor imaging, a non-invasive MRI technique that maps the brain white matter, studies demonstrated delayed white matter maturation in survivors with NEC, with increased mean diffusivity in the splenium of the corpus callosum and the corticospinal tracts, and decreased white matter integrity across multiple tracts, indicating poorly organized neuronal connectivity.11 24 These imaging findings confirm the disruption of oligodendrocyte maturation that has been documented in the experimental studies described below. Overall, neuroimaging and functional studies were found to be predictors of low BSID-III motor and language scores, severe motor delay, and cerebral palsy at 2 years of age, independent of other neonatal factors.11 28

Patient-related risk factors

Current evidence indicates that infants with NEC who develop brain injury tend to be born at a lower gestational age, highlighting the vulnerability of the most premature population.2931 This is because the third trimester is a crucial period for cortical growth, synaptogenesis, and white matter maturation. Nonetheless, while historically the neurological deficits observed in NEC survivors were attributed only to prematurity, there is evidence that prematurity alone accounts only for a small portion of the risk of developing NDI in preterm babies with NEC.6 Sex and birth weight represent additional important determinants of risk. Within NEC cohorts, higher rates of NDI have been reported in male infants and in those with lower birth weight.30 31 Furthermore, lower 1-minute and 5-minute Apgar scores have been associated with subsequent NDI in patients with NEC.30 The risk of NDI is further amplified when NEC occurs in the context of additional neonatal comorbidities, such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia and periventricular hemorrhagic infarction.29 32

Disease-related risk factors

Among disease-related risk factors, the severity of the systemic inflammatory response plays a central role in driving NDI in infants with NEC. Elevated inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein, proinflammatory cytokines, procalcitonin, and immature-to-total neutrophil ratio have been associated with an increased risk of NDI in affected infants.17 29 33 These findings support the concept that inflammation itself is a key mediator of brain injury in NEC and are supportive of the potential activation of the gut-brain inflammatory cascade. Accordingly, patients with more severe NEC, such as those who require surgery, exhibit higher levels of inflammatory markers and tend to demonstrate more frequent WMI.31 Comparisons between NEC and spontaneous intestinal perforation (SIP) further reinforce the pathogenic role of inflammation. Although SIP may also require surgical management, it is typically characterized by minimal systemic inflammation and is associated with lower rates of cognitive, motor, and sensory impairment compared with infants with NEC not only at 18 and 36 months but also long term.3436

Treatment-related risk factors

The intensity and duration of treatment and the need for surgical management also influence neurodevelopmental outcomes in infants with NEC. Hemodynamic and pharmacologic support may further contribute to the underlying risk. Exposure to vasopressors, particularly dopamine, has emerged as an independent predictor of adverse neurodevelopment.23 30 Similarly, prolonged use of parenteral nutrition or mechanical ventilation and greater exposure to postnatal steroids have all been associated with higher rates of WMI and subsequent NDI in patients with NEC.29 31 The need for surgical management itself represents an important treatment-related risk factor. Across multiple cohorts, infants undergoing operative treatment consistently demonstrate higher rates of cerebral palsy, poorer cognitive performance, and increased risk of head growth failure and microcephaly compared with medically managed patients with NEC.6 9 16 23 Surgery has to be considered not as a risk factor in itself, rather as an indirect marker of the severity of the intestinal disease. In fact, emerging laboratory and clinical data suggest that leaving necrotic bowel in situ may worsen systemic inflammation and brain injury.63740 Accordingly, delayed transfer and late laparotomy independently predict NDI, highlighting that prolonged uncontrolled inflammation accelerates neurological injury.293141 The Necrotizing Enterocolitis Surgery Trial (NEST) evaluated laparotomy versus drainage in infants with NEC with the primary endpoint of NDI at 2 years of age.40 This study confirmed that in infants with NEC, the rate of survival without NDI was lower in infants who underwent laparotomy than in those who were initially treated with an intraperitoneal drain.40 This underlines the concept that leaving a segment of necrotic bowel in situ is detrimental for the infant’s brain and that performing an exploratory laparotomy is critical. A recent study reported that patients who developed hyponatremia (serum sodium <130 mEq/L) or thrombocytopenia (platelet count <50×10⁹/L) at any point between NEC diagnosis and intestinal resection had a higher risk of subsequent NDI.30 This study concluded that an early laparotomy and temporary closure may provide neuroprotection by allowing better cerebral perfusion and easy access for a second bowel assessment 24-48 hours later.30 In patients with NEC, there are several iatrogenic exposures that may be associated with the development of NDI. Prolonged postoperative ileus has been associated with a higher incidence of WMI.31 Blood transfusions have also been associated with lower cognitive, motor, and language scores in infants with NEC.31 42 Cumulative exposure to sedatives such as fentanyl and benzodiazepines has been associated with altered cerebellar and hippocampal development in preterm infants, but there is no study reporting this association in infants with NEC.43 44 Similarly, the use of metronidazole has been associated with a risk of neurotoxicity in older patients, but its long-term neurologic effects in neonates remain unclear. A multicenter retrospective study in VLBW infants found no differences in BSID-III scores, seizure incidence, or NDI between metronidazole-exposed and unexposed infants.45

Complication-related risk factors

Acute kidney injury has been associated with higher rates of WMI and ventricular dilatation in infants with NEC.26 41 NDI is also more frequent in infants with surgical NEC who subsequently develop intestinal failure (IF), a condition that itself increases the risk of neurologic impairment. IF confers a substantial neurodevelopmental burden, likely reflecting the combined impact of multiple surgical procedures, cholestasis, and recurrent sepsis.37 46 47 Finally, the management of NEC babies often includes prolonged hospitalization, which contributes to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes through environmental deprivation and reduced opportunities for early stimulation.3 31

Long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes in children with NEC

Evidence from multiple cohorts shows that both medically and surgically treated infants with NEC are associated with higher rates of long-term NDI, with the greatest burden among infants with high disease severity.10 12 16 48 49 School-age assessments indicate that deficits extend beyond motor, sensory, and global cognitive performance and include specific impairments in reading, language processing, memory, and adaptive behavior, resulting in a higher need for specialized educational services.36 5052 Long-term follow-up studies confirmed that infants with surgical NEC represent the group at the highest risk for adverse outcomes, with lower IQ scores and greater impairments across multiple developmental domains compared with medically treated patients with NEC.26 49 Multiple epidemiologic studies also reported higher rates of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorder, and intellectual disability in children who had NEC compared with matched controls.6 16 53 54 Nonetheless, it is important to underline that a high proportion of children who recover from NEC achieve overall acceptable long-term functioning, with subjective well-being comparable to peers without NEC.5557

Mechanism of NEC-associated NDI

Macroscopic and cellular phenotypes in experimental NEC brain

The pathophysiology of NEC brain injury is complex and remains elusive (figure 1). To understand the mechanisms behind intestinal damage and associated organ injuries, several models have been employed in neonatal rats, mice, and piglets, and more recently in organoid systems.58 59 Experimental studies in neonatal mouse and piglet models of NEC reported macroscopic brain changes, characterized by cerebral edema, decreased cortical thickness, decreased brain weight, and increased brain:body weight ratio.60 61 Additionally, impaired myelination was reported in the periventricular regions and hippocampus of mice with NEC,62 a finding that coincides with clinical observations of WMI in infants with NEC that is proportional to the degree of intestinal injury.63 At a cellular level, the brain of neonatal mice with NEC had increased activation of astrocytes and microglia in the hippocampus, cerebral cortex, and thalamus, and a reduction in oligodendrocyte precursor maturation contributing to impaired myelination.61 62 64 However, the specific effect of NEC on neurons remains to be further explored. In one study, mice with experimental NEC exhibited reduced levels of mature neurons in the cerebral cortex, thalamus, and hippocampus61, whereas another study observed no difference in neuron density in the hippocampus of NEC mice versus controls, but rather observed impaired myelination.62

Animal models of NEC have additionally shown indications of a damaged blood-brain barrier (BBB). One study using the preterm piglet model of NEC demonstrated a reduction in the coverage of perivascular astrocyte end-feet (a measure of BBB integrity regulation) in the hippocampus in addition to various alterations in various adherens junction and tight junction proteins including occludin, ZO-1, and VE-cadherin, in the striatum and hippocampus.60 65 These findings are further supported by another study using the mouse model of NEC which observed increased Evans Blue Dye accumulation (an indication of deteriorated BBB integrity) and similarly observed reduced occludin expression in the brains of mouse pups with NEC.66 At a molecular level, the brain of rodent and piglet models showed upregulated expression of hypoxia, neuroinflammation, and downregulated expression of markers pertaining to protection against oxidative stress.61 62 67 In particular, mouse pups with NEC had accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the periventricular regions and hippocampus, areas that also have increased microglial activation and reduced myelination.62 Interestingly, expression of inflammatory markers, such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor alpha, in the brain positively correlated with their levels in the NEC gut.61 67 Evidence from human brain autopsy samples remains limited, but periventricular brain sections obtained from autopsy samples of premature babies with NEC display higher levels of microglial activation than controls without NEC as shown by increased expression of ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1, a microglia marker.62 Additionally, brain section autopsy samples from 36-week-old infants with NEC display increased numbers of CD4+ T cells versus age-matched controls.59

Molecular mechanisms behind NEC brain injury

There is strong evidence supporting the notion of NEC-associated NDI being driven by a combination of both gut-derived immune cells and proinflammatory mediators being released from necrotic portions of the bowel and subsequently infiltrating the neonatal brain.59 62 As outlined by Dr Hackam’s group in the ‘cross-switching hypothesis’, the development of NEC is driven by exaggerated Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling, an innate immune system receptor that plays a critical role during intestinal development.62 68 The hypothesis describes how the normally developmental role of intestinal TLR4 ‘switches’ to that of a proinflammatory mediator in the context of prematurity, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of NEC.69 During normal intestinal development, TLR4 contributes to intestinal development by regulating the differentiation of goblet cells.68 It has been shown that TLR4 deficiency on the intestinal epithelium leads to increased differentiation of intestinal stem cells into goblet cells via reduced Notch signaling; hence, its expression is normally increased in the developing intestine.68 69 In homeostatic conditions, this does not present an issue as the expression of TLR4 in the intestine decreases by the time of full gestation.70 71 However, in cases of premature birth where the expression of TLR4 has not yet decreased, an exaggerated immune response occurs on colonization of the intestine by external microbes, resulting in severe inflammation, release of danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), and thereby contributing to the inflammatory phenotype observed in NEC.72 73 Among the DAMPs released from dying enterocytes, a key role is played by high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), a non-histone nuclear protein, which has been shown to play various roles in the intestine both intracellularly and extracellularly.74 75 Intracellularly, HMGB1 acts to promote cell survival by protecting autophagy-related proteins during inflammation,74 whereas extracellularly, HMGB1 secreted into intestinal mucus causes bacteria to aggregate, thereby limiting their invasion into deeper layers of intestinal tissue.75 However, there is also experimental evidence that implicates HMGB1 in the pathophysiology of NEC brain injury as blocking HMGB1 either by knock down its expression in intestinal epithelial cells or by using anti-HMGB1 antibodies was protective against microglia activation in neonatal mice.62 The deterioration in BBB integrity during NEC, as demonstrated in the piglet and mouse models of NEC, may facilitate the translocation of liberated HMGB1 into the brain, thus contributing to the progression of NEC brain injury.60 66

Infiltrating CD4+ T cells have also been implicated in driving NEC-associated brain injury as they have been shown to accumulate in the brain of neonatal mice with NEC, whereas preventing their influx into the brain of these mice also protected against brain injury.59 Additionally, the release of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) from these infiltrating cells is essential as IFN-γ addition to brain organoids results in reduced myelin and increased expression of proinflammatory markers, whereas T cells with knocked down IFN-γ are unable to recapitulate the same NEC brain injury in mice when reintroduced to naïve mice lacking mature T cells.59 76 These alterations in the cellular and molecular landscape not only provide further insight into the pathogenesis of NEC brain injury but also offer new targets for future therapeutics and preventative strategies.

Clinical strategies and future perspectives

There are no specific therapies for NEC-associated brain injury in the clinical setting, where management mainly relies on the standard treatment of NEC (i.e., bowel rest and broad-spectrum antibiotics, resection of necrotic intestinal tissue) and management of neurological sequelae.77 78 Here, we report strategies that have been implemented in preterm infants and might benefit NEC babies, as well as novel therapies that are still in a preclinical phase.

Clinical strategies to prevent brain injury in preterm infants

High-dose erythropoietin (EPO) has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in preclinical models of neonatal brain injury. However, in a multicenter randomized trial, EPO did not reduce death or severe NDI at 2 years compared with placebo.79 A meta-analysis suggested that EPO may modestly reduce the risk of NDI in preterm infants, but no effect has been observed on cerebral palsy, visual or hearing deficits, or IVH, indicating that further trials are needed to define optimal dosing and timing.80 Although some of the preterm babies enrolled in these studies had NEC, the effects of EPO administration on the prevention of NDI in this specific population remain unclear.

Therapeutic controlled hypothermia is a well-established intervention for term infants with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy that improves survival and long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes. Due to safety concerns, its use in preterm infants remains limited. Preliminary studies in preterm infants with NEC explored mild whole-body cooling (35-35.5°C for 48 hours) as a therapeutic strategy for the intestine with possible indirect neuroprotective effects on the brain.81 82

Antioxidant and anti-inflammatory strategies

Given the importance of oxidative stress in the pathogenesis of NEC brain injury, a therapeutic approach is to reduce ROS accumulation and increase the expression of genes associated with antioxidant effects in the neonatal brain. Antioxidant therapy with N-acetylcysteine was reported to reduce microglial activation and ROS accumulation, increase myelination in periventricular and hippocampal regions, and offer long-term neuroprotection in a mouse model of NEC.62 Building on the protective effects of breastfeeding against NEC, administration of human milk oligosaccharides 6′-sialyllactose and 2′-fucosyllactose showed reduction of ROS accumulation in the hippocampus and midbrain of both mice and piglet models of NEC.77 83 Hyperbaric oxygen therapy in a rat model of NEC also demonstrated an increase in superoxide dismutase 3 gene expression in the hippocampus,84 thus warranting further research into these antioxidant therapeutic approaches.

Microbiome-based therapies

It is well documented that gut dysbiosis plays a key role in the development of NEC, as many affected infants have decreased gut microbiota diversity and an abundance of Gram-negative bacteria.8587 As such, modulation of the gut microbiome with the use of probiotics has been explored as a possible therapeutic strategy for infants with NEC. A study assessing the administration of Limosilactobacillus reuteri (L. reuteri) biofilm (a Gram-positive bacterium with antimicrobial properties) in a piglet model of NEC reported reductions in microglial activation but did not observe differences in myelination.88 Additionally, L. reuteri was also shown to prevent myelin loss during NEC, while also reducing anxiety-like behaviors and microglial activation in a rat model of NEC.89 Finally, another study assessing administration of butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid produced by Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes gut microbes, observed reductions in microglial activation and increased numbers of oligodendrocytes in the cerebellum, hippocampus, and cortex of mouse neonates with NEC.90 91

Targeting HMGB1-TLR4 signaling

The interaction of liberated HMGB1 with TLR4 on brain immune cells remains a pertinent contributor to NEC-associated brain injury. As such, methods of inhibiting this signaling cascade have been explored. Administration of glycyrrhizin, a HMGB1 inhibitor and bioactive compound found in liquorice, was found to reduce the expression of proinflammatory factors in the brains of mouse pups with NEC, while also reducing microglial activation in an in vitro cell culture system.66 92 Another study assessing N-acetylcysteine administration in a rat model of NEC displayed reductions of brain and ileum levels of TLR4, thereby reducing HMGB1-TLR4 signaling.62 93

While there are many prospective therapeutic routes currently being explored that show promise in mitigating NEC-associated brain injury, none have yet reached clinical translation, underscoring the need for rigorous pharmacokinetic, safety, and efficacy studies to translate such approaches from bench to bedside.

Concluding remarks

This review highlights the growing literature on NDI in NEC and confirms that great strides have been made over the last decades to understand this clinical phenotype and more recently the molecular and cellular changes that occur in the brain of babies with NEC. The risk of NDI in NEC goes beyond the vulnerabilities of prematurity alone, underscoring the central role of NEC-specific inflammatory and systemic insults in driving brain injury. While some risk factors are non-modifiable, careful assessment of clinical and pathological contributors can help identify infants at highest risk of WMI and consequent NDI. Recognizing this vulnerability highlights the importance of structured, longitudinal neurodevelopmental follow-up, beginning in the neonatal period and extending to school age, to allow early detection and timely intervention. Optimizing outcomes for NEC survivors requires balancing immediate survival with long-term neurological health. Multidisciplinary collaboration, parent education, and proactive neurodevelopmental monitoring are essential to improve quality of life and support families in making informed decisions. Ultimately, basic and translational research efforts over the last decade have helped understand some of the mechanisms underlying NEC-related brain injury and have proposed potential new targets for diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy. Further studies are needed to elucidate the pathogenesis of NDI in NEC, identify biomarkers of brain injury, and test new therapeutic strategies that could be used in the clinical setting.
