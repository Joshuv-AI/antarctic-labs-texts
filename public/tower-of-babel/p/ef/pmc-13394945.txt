Pleiotropic Roles of FBXO11 in Tumorigenesis: Implications for Targeted Therapy
Authors: Yuqi Zhang, Changyi Fan, Shiheng Chen, Tianyue Lu, Yuanyuan Li, Feng Du, Shanshan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTF‐box protein 11 (FBXO11), a critical component of the F‐box protein family, serves as the substrate recognition subunit of the Skp1‐Cul1‐F‐box (SCF) E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, orchestrates the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of a diverse array of substrates, thereby regulating various physiological and pathological processes. Emerging evidence reveals that FBXO11 is aberrantly expressed in multiple tumor types. Predominantly, FBXO11 functions as a potent tumor suppressor, and its downregulation is strongly correlated with tumor initiation, aggressive progression, and poor prognoses. Mechanistically, FBXO11 deficiency facilitates tumor development and metastasis by deregulating the cell cycle progression, enhancing cell migration and invasion, and driving epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT). However, the broad substrate spectrum of FBXO11 dictates its context‐dependent roles in cancer biology, imparting significant challenges to its direct therapeutic targeting, as systemic modulation may yield unpredictable off‐target or paradoxical effects. This review provides a comprehensive overview of current understanding of FBXO11 in oncology. By critically appraising its substrate diversity and functional versatility, we aim to re‐evaluate the clinical translation of FBXO11, proposing that it be regarded not merely as a straightforward therapeutic target, but rather as a prognostic biomarker and a context‐specific vulnerability that necessitates precision medicine strategies for effective clinical intervention.

Introduction

The ubiquitin‐proteasome system (UPS) is a pivotal intracellular proteolytic pathway that regulates cell proliferation, transcription, and apoptosis by mediating the degradation of over 80% of cellular proteins [1]. E3 ubiquitin ligases, which determine substrate specificity, are categorized into three superfamilies: Ring‐type, HECT‐type, and RBR‐type [2]. The Cullin‐Ring ligase (CRL) family represents a prominent E3 subfamily, encompassing eight members (CRL1‐CRL9) [3]. CRL complexes require neddylation modification for catalytic activation, a post‐translational event essential for proteolytic function [4]. Composed of a Cullin‐1 scaffold, adaptor proteins, substrate receptor subunits, and a RING finger domain, CRL E3s form a stable catalytic platform where the RING domain facilitates E2 enzyme recruitment. The SCF complex, a prototypical CRL1 subclass, employs at least 69 distinct F‐box substrate receptors to precisely modulate cellular processes such as cell cycle progression, DNA replication, transcriptional regulation, and apoptosis [5]. The F‐box protein, as an essential component of E3 ubiquitin ligases, is a key subunit for substrate‐specific recognition of the SCF complex. Based on C‐terminal domain architecture, F‐box proteins are classified into three subfamilies: FBXW (containing WD40 repeat domains), FBXL (containing leucine‐rich repeats), and FBXO (containing other uncharacterized domains) [6].

FBXO11 as a substrate receptor within the SCF complex and belongs to the FBXO subfamily [7]. Recently, FBXO11 has been found to play a critical role in the development of many tumors. It is also involved in the pathology of non‐tumorigenic diseases such as vitiligo, secretory otitis media, and neurological disorders, and plays a key role in physiological activities such as melanocyte apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, cell migration, B‐cell differentiation, and epithelial development [8].

In this review, we summarize recent advances regarding FBXO11, with a particular focus on its structural characteristics and functional roles in tumor progression. We further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying its upstream regulators and downstream targets. By integrating these regulatory networks we aim to provide a theoretical foundation and novel perspectives for future development of targeted therapeutics, optimization of clinical cancer treatments, and discovery of prognostic biomarkers.

Basic Information and Structure of FBXO11

The FBXO11, also known as FBX11 or UBR6, is located at chromosome 2q16.3 and encodes an F‐box protein that serves as a critical component of the ubiquitin‐proteasome system. As a constituent of the SCF complex, FBXO11 is responsible for substrate recognition and the subsequent ubiquitination that targets specific proteins for degradation. Functionally indispensable, FBXO11 exhibits extensive sequence conservation across multiple species, indicating that its amino acid sequence is under strong purifying selection and is essential for conserved biological processes (Figure 1A). Three alternatively spliced isoforms of FBXO11 have been identified; these variants displayed amino acid sequence divergence exclusively within the region spanning residues 21 to 95, while the remaining sequences are strictly conserved across all isoforms (Figure 1B). Despite their differences in the variable region, all three isoforms shared a conserved core architecture comprising an F‐box domain, CASH repeat domains, and a zinc‐finger domain (Figure 1C). Furthermore, the tertiary structures of these three alternatively spliced isoforms were predicted using Alpha‐Fold (Figure 1D), and FBXO11 was mainly localized on the cell nucleus.

The SCFFBXO11 Complex and Its Substrates

FBXO11, an F‐box protein composed of 927 amino acids, contains a proline‐rich domain and F‐box domain at the N‐terminal end, and three central carbohydrate‐binding protein and glycolysis (CASH) domains and a zinc finger domain at the C‐terminal end. The CASH domain is responsible for substrate recognition, and the F‐box domain mediates binding to SKP1, ensuring the integrity of the SCF complex [7]. The SCFFBXO11 complex functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, working in concert with ubiquitin‐activating (E1) and ubiquitin‐conjugating (E2) enzymes to mediate protein ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation [9].

FBXO11 may recognize substrates phosphorylated at specific residues via its CASH domain (Figure 2). It plays essential roles in regulating cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and the PI3K/AKT and EMT signaling pathways. By differentially targeting multiple substrates (Table 1) including Snail [10, 11], ZEB1 [12, 13, 14], and p53 [15, 16], FBXO11 exhibited context dependent functions as either a tumor suppressor or potential oncogene in human cancers.

The Regulation of FBXO11 Expression

Noncoding RNAs Regulation of FBXO11

Evolutionarily conserved miRNAs govern gene expression and cellular homeostasis, while most function as tumor suppressors; certain onco‐miRNAs promote malignancy [17, 18]. miR‐21, a canonical onco‐miRNA frequently upregulated in breast, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers, suppressed multiple tumor suppressive targets including PTEN and PDCD4 [18], and accelerated tumorigenesis by downregulating FBXO11 in melanoma, prostate cancer, and glioblastoma. Conversely, miR‐621, miR‐181a‐5p, miR‐197‐3p, and miR‐26a acted as tumor suppressors by directly repressing FBXO11 expression. In breast cancer, miR‐621 antagonized FBXO11‐mediated inhibition of p53, restoring p53 activity and enhancing chemosensitivity [19]. In glioblastoma, miR‐181a‐5p was downregulated in drug resistant cells; its restoration reduced FBXO11 expression, promoted apoptosis, and sensitized cells to temozolomide [20]. In cigarette smoke exposed (CSE) bronchial epithelial cells, the circRBMS1/miR‐197‐3p axis regulated FBXO11, which mediated TGF‐β/Smad2 signaling and modulated apoptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress [21]. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), miR‐26a was downregulated and directly suppressed FBXO11, whose overexpression correlated with aggressive clinicopathological features and poor survival [22, 23]. Ectopic miR‐26a or FBXO11 depletion inhibited HCC proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas FBXO11 restoration reversed these effects [24, 25]. Collectively, these miRNAs converge on FBXO11 to control EMT, p53 function, drug responsiveness, and inflammatory pathways, highlighting the miRNA‐FBXO11 regulatory network as a promising therapeutic target in human cancers.

Epigenetic Regulation of FBXO11

Transcriptional Regulation of FBXO11

Transcriptional regulation of FBXO11 plays a critical role in several physiological processes. In erythropoiesis, the histone marker H3K27me3 and PRC2 mediate transcriptional repression and affect hematopoietic development. In contrast, FBXO11 can target BAHD1 to deregulate PRC2‐mediated repression, and BAHD1 recruits transcriptional co‐repressors as a reader of H3K27me3. FBXO11 mediated the degradation of BAHD1, which is used to deregulate PRC2 repression, promote erythropoiesis‐associated gene expression, and facilitate erythropoiesis maturation. In the absence of FBXO11, erythroid development was retarded and related gene expression was reduced [26]. FBXO11 proteomic screen revealed that FBXO11 was a binding chaperone for CIITA, and the half‐life of CIITA was regulated primarily through the FBXO11‐dependent ubiquitin‐proteasome system. By downregulating CIITA, FBXO11 expression attenuated MHC‐II promoter activity, transcription, and surface presentation. The expression of FBXO11 was negatively correlated with MHC‐II and was associated with the prognosis of cancer patients. FBXO11 was a key regulator that determined the level of MHC‐II, and its expression may serve as a biomarker for cancer [27]. In conclusion, FBXO11 maintains the normal physiological function of cells through transcriptional regulation in different pathways.

Regulation of Cell Proliferation and Cell Cycle

FBXO11 exhibits context‐dependent functions in cell proliferation and cell cycle progression and is critically implicated in malignancies such as glioblastoma. In glioblastoma tissues and cells, FBXO11 was significantly downregulated, and its overexpression suppressed proliferation and induced cell cycle arrest in U251MG and A172 cells. Mechanistically, FBXO11 reduced Cdc25A expression, thereby inhibiting PKM2/c‐MYC dependent aerobic glycolysis and restraining tumor growth. Conversely, enforced Cdc25A expression abolishes the inhibitory effects of FBXO11 on proliferation and glycolysis, and overexpression of FBXO11 suppressed tumorigenesis in vivo [28]. Notably, FBXO11 does not act uniformly: in HCC, knockdown of FBXO11 inhibited proliferation and colony formation, and it also suppressed leukemic transformation in myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). Collectively, FBXO11 modulates cell proliferation and the cell cycle via protein and metabolic regulation in a context‐dependent manner, providing novel insights into tumorigenesis.

FBXO11 Regulation at Posttranslational Level

Phosphorylation Modification

Phosphorylation modification of FBXO11 is significant in several physiological and pathological processes. In prostate cancer, NDR1 phosphorylates β‐catenin at Ser33/37 to facilitate its interaction with FBXO11, which promotes cytoplasmic β‐catenin ubiquitination and degradation. The NDR1‐FBXO11 complex also targeted JNK2 for ubiquitination, suppressing β‐catenin nuclear translocation and thereby inhibiting EMT [29]. As a bona fide E3 ubiquitin ligase for Snai1/2, FBXO11 destabilized Snail independently of its basal phosphorylation, reducing cell invasion. This process is further modulated by protein kinase D1 (PKD1) mediated Snail Ser11 phosphorylation, establishing the PKD1‐FBXO11‐Snail axis that represses Snail‐driven EMT, tumor initiating capacity, and metastasis in breast cancer [10, 11]. Additionally, FBXO11 mediated ubiquitin‐dependent degradation of BCL‐6; monoallelic inactivation of FBXO11 in diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) increases BCL‐6 stability, contributing to lymphomagenesis by dysregulating BCL‐6 homeostasis [30].

Neddylation Modification

FBXO11 as a key regulator of neddylation and ubiquitination in tumor suppression, immunity, and metabolism. FBXO11 mediated site specific neddylation of p53 at Lys320/321, inhibiting p53 transcriptional activity without altering its stability or ubiquitination [16]. In antiviral immunity, FBXO11 promoted K63‐linked ubiquitination of TRAF3 in a NEDD8‐dependent manner to regulate type I IFN signaling. During lipid metabolism reprogramming [31], FBXO11 induced ubiquitin‐dependent degradation of hnRNPA2B1, whereas CAND1 blocked FBXO11‐SCF complex assembly to drive lipogenesis. The CAND1‐SCFFBXO11‐hnRNPA2B1 axis thus promoted tumor progression via metabolic and proteolytic remodeling [32].

Genetic Alterations of FBXO11

The regulatory mechanism of FBXO11 mutation is complex and plays an important role in diseases such as myeloid malignancies and B‐cell lymphomas. In myeloid malignancies, loss of FBXO11 dysregulated transcriptional programs governing leukocyte proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. In MDS, FBXO11 deletion conferred cytokine‐independent growth and promoted progression to secondary acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [33], establishing FBXO11 as a critical tumor suppressor and therapeutic target in myeloid neoplasms [34, 35]. In DLBCL, FBXO11 was frequently deleted or mutated, impairing BCL‐6 ubiquitination and degradation. Stabilized BCL‐6 enhanced lymphoma cell proliferation and survival. Reconstitution of FBXO11 restored BCL‐6 turnover, suppressed proliferation, and diminished tumorigenicity, highlighting the FBXO11‐BCL‐6 axis as a key driver of lymphomagenesis [36].

The Expression of FBXO11 in Human Cancer

To evaluate the clinical relevance and prognostic potential of FBXO11 across human cancers, we performed comprehensive pan‐cancer expression analyses using publicly available datasets, including GEPIA (Figure 3A), UALCAN (Figure 3B), and TIMER (Figure 3C). Overall, the mRNA expression of FBXO11 was significantly upregulated in various human tumor tissues compared to normal tissues. However, we observed unexpected discrepancies in the expression trends reported by different databases for the same cancer type. For example, in Colon Adenocarcinoma (COAD), GEPIA indicated low expression of FBXO11 relative to normal tissues, whereas UALCAN and TIMER indicated high expression. Conversely, in Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma (PAAD) and Thymoma (THYM), GEPIA showed high expression, while UALCAN and TIMER showed low expression. These inconsistencies may be attributed to variations in detection and data processing methodologies across platforms. Specifically, GEPIA integrates both TCGA adjacent normal tissues and GTEx normal tissues (FPKM normalized), whereas UALCAN and TIMER rely exclusively on TCGA adjacent normal tissues (TPM and RSEM normalized, respectively). The inclusion of distinct GTEx normal tissues in GEPIA, compared to the limited adjacent normal samples in UALCAN and TIMER, inevitably leads to variations in statistical power and differential expression outcomes. Beyond primary tumor tissues, expression patterns of FBXO11 also show tissue‐specific trends, with notably high levels in certain lung cancer (A‐549, Calu‐3) and hematopoietic malignancy (Raji, Ramos) cell lines, while more moderate to low expression is seen in gastrointestinal cancer (HT‐1080, PC‐3) and central nervous system cancer (U‐87MG) cell lines (Figure 3D). Collectively, these bioinformatics‐derived data from independent patient cohorts demonstrate a strong clinical correlation between aberrant FBXO11 expression and poor prognosis, underscoring its potential as a pan‐cancer prognostic biomarker. However, it must be emphasized that these pan‐cancer expression profiles represent clinical correlations and do not inherently establish causal functional mechanisms. Theses correlative findings serve strictly to identify prognostic potential, whereas the specific functional mechanisms of FBXO11 are exclusively substantiated by subsequent empirical in vitro and in vivo experimental evidence.

Clinical Significance of FBXO11 in Human Cancer

Although the bioinformatics analyses established the clinical correlation between FBXO11 expression and patient prognosis, they do not provide causal evidence for its functional roles in tumorigenesis. To further elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, we systematically reviewed published literature concerning in vitro and in vivo functional experiments of FBXO11 across various cancer types.

Prostate Cancer (PCa)

FBXO11 serves as a critical tumor suppressor in prostate cancer through different regulatory mechanisms. Functioning as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, it mediates the ubiquitin‐dependent proteasomal degradation of oncogenic substrates, including β‐catenin and BCL6, thereby inhibiting EMT and metastasis. Mechanistically, in vitro and in vivo studies using PC3 and DU145 cell lines and nude mouse xenograft models demonstrated that NDR1‐induced phosphorylation facilitates the binding of FBXO11 to β‐catenin, promoting its cytoplasmic degradation and preventing nuclear translocation(Figure 4A) [29]. Conversely, the expression of FBXO11 is frequently attenuated in PCa via epigenetic and post‐transcriptional silencing. Specifically, its promoter is epigenetically silenced by H3K27me3 enrichment at its promoter in high‐grade PCa [37], while experimental validations in DU145 cells and DU145 xenograft mouse models show that miR‐21 directly targets and represses FBXO11 to abrogate its pro‐apoptotic and anti‐tumor effects [38]. Collectively, the functional loss of FBXO11 driven by either impaired substrate recognition or transcriptional/post‐transcriptional silencing promotes malignant progression, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic target and biomarker for PCa intervention.

Gastric Cancer (GC)

Studies have shown that the expression of FBXO11 is significantly upregulated in GC compared with paraneoplastic tissues, and its high expression is closely associated with tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and advanced TNM stage. Functionally, in vitro studies in MGC‐803 and SGC7901 cell lines demonstrated that FBXO11 could accelerate GC metastasis by promoting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion during the EMT process. Mechanistically, FBXO11 plays an oncogenic role by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which provides a new direction for targeted therapy of GC [39]. Furthermore, functional validations utilizing LINC01436 knockdown in BGC823 cells and overexpression in AGS cells revealed that the LINC01436/miR‐585 axis upregulates FBXO11 to drive tumor cell proliferation and invasiveness, ultimately contributing to the poor prognosis of patients with GC (Figure 4A) [40]. This regulatory mechanism is associated with increased tumor cell proliferation, metastatic ability, and poor prognosis of GC patients [40]. The above studies have demonstrated the multiple pro‐oncogenic mechanisms of FBXO11 in GC development, underscoring its significant clinical translational value as a therapeutic target.

Lung Cancer (LC)

LC, particularly non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) which accounts for 85% of cases, exhibits the highest global incidence and mortality, imposing substantial socioeconomic burdens. FBXO11 functions as a critical tumor suppressor in NSCLC via multiple mechanisms. Through in vitro experiments in A549 and H1299 cells, FBXO11 was shown to directly interact with the nuclear EMT regulator ZEB1, targeting the CZF structural domain for K48‐linked polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation in a concentration‐dependent manner, thereby suppressing EMT, cell migration, and invasion. These tumor suppressive effects were further validated in vivo [14]. Clinically, the FBXO11‐ZEB1 axis correlates with favorable prognosis, as FBXO11 overexpression restrains EMT and predicts better patient outcomes. Additionally, in vitro validations in A549 and SPC‐A1 cells demonstrated that miR‐324‐5p directly targets FBXO11 via its 3′‐UTR to reduce its expression, inducing cisplatin resistance. This resistance mechanism was further confirmed in vivo using patient derived xenograft (PDX) models. Clinically, low FBXO11 and high miR‐324‐5p levels correlate with poor survival in chemotherapy‐treated patients [41]. Furthermore, rare FBXO11‐ALK fusion variants are identified in lung adenocarcinoma, with partial responsiveness to crizotinib, warranting further therapeutic investigations (Figure 4A) [42].

Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC)

HCC is characterized by extensive metabolic reprogramming, with elevated de novo lipogenesis as a key feature [43, 44]. In HCC, FBXO11 exhibits context‐dependent dual roles. Metabolically, FBXO11 can interact with the RRM2 domain of hnRNPA2B1 via its zinc‐finger domain, inducing K48‐ and K27‐linked ubiquitination and subsequent degradation in Hep3B and Huh7 cells, thereby modulating the expression of lipid synthesis‐related genes including FASN, ACC1, and ACLY, and suppressing lipid accumulation. Its tumor‐suppressive function is negatively regulated by the upstream factor Cullin‐associated Neddylation Dissociative Protein 1 (CAND1), which disrupts the FBXO11‐CUL1 association and causes SCF complex dissociation [32]. Restoring FBXO11 efficacy in vivo via CAND1 knockdown significantly delayed tumor formation, metastasis, and lipid accumulation. Clinically, FBXO11 downregulation correlates with aggressive HCC features and poor prognosis. Conversely, FBXO11 also demonstrates oncogenic properties in HCC. In vitro functional assays revealed that FBXO11 knockdown significantly attenuates HCC cell proliferation, colony formation, and migration. Mechanistically, FBXO11 is negatively targeted by miR‐26a; miR‐26a overexpression phenocopied FBXO11 depletion, while FBXO11 restoration reversed this tumor suppression (Figure 4A) [25]. Notably, FBXO11 exhibits divergent prognostic values across malignancies, predicting favorable outcomes in lung cancer but adverse prognosis in breast cancer, underscoring its complex, tissue‐specific roles in tumorigenesis [10, 19].

Pancreatic Cancer (PDAC)

PDAC is a malignant tumor with a poor prognosis, FBXO11 exerts oncogenic roles in PDAC by antagonizing SAM domain‐containing protein 1 (SAMD1) in PaTu8988t and BxPC3 cells. While SAMD1 normally represses EMT‐associated genes such as CDH2 to restrain PDAC cell migration, FBXO11 antagonizes this function by binding to SAMD1 and impairing its chromatin occupancy, ultimately promoting EMT and contributing to disease progression [45]. Moreover, FBXO11 is highly expressed in PDAC; ultrasound‐targeted microbubble destruction (UTMD)‐mediated FBXO11 silencing suppresses PDAC cell proliferation, EMT, metastasis and induces apoptosis in vitro and in vivo, partly by abrogating TP53 ubiquitination and degradation [46]. Additionally, in vitro studies in MIAPaCa‐2 and PANC‐1 cells demonstrated that ERK3 stabilizes the EMT driver Snail by blocking FBXO11‐mediated Snail ubiquitination and degradation. This pro‐oncogenic effect was further validated in vivo using PDX mouse models, which confirmed the acceleration of PDAC progression (Figure 4B) [47]. Collectively, these findings establish FBXO11 as a critical oncoprotein operating via multiple regulatory axes in pancreatic cancer pathogenesis.

Glioblastoma (GBM)

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most prevalent primary malignant brain tumor, characterized by high recurrence, chemoresistance, and poor prognosis, with aerobic glycolysis serving as a key therapeutic target [20, 48]. FBXO11 is downregulated in GBM tissues and U251‐MG and A172 cells, and its overexpression markedly suppresses cell proliferation, induces cell cycle arrest, and attenuates aerobic glycolysis. Mechanistically, FBXO11 mediates the ubiquitin‐dependent degradation of the cell cycle phosphatase Cdc25A, thereby reducing PKM2 dephosphorylation at Ser37, inhibiting PKM2/β‐catenin interaction, and inactivating β‐catenin/c‐MYC signaling. This consequently decreases the expression of glycolytic genes including GLUT1 and LDHA, impairing glycolytic activity. Restoration of Cdc25A reverses the glycolytic inhibition mediated by FBXO11 (Figure 4B). In vivo, FBXO11 exerts tumor‐suppressive effects, which were further validated using xenograft mouse models via ubiquitinating and degrading Cdc25A, blocking glycolytic metabolism and GBM progression, highlighting it as a promising molecular target for GBM treatment [28].

Breast Cancer (BRCA)

BRCA remains a leading malignancy in women worldwide. Within this context, FBXO11 exhibits multifaceted and context‐dependent functions in BRCA, with critical roles in chemosensitivity, invasion, immune regulation, and prognosis. Regarding chemosensitivity, in vitro studies utilizing BRCA cell lines (MCF‐7, MDA‐MB‐231) demonstrated that miR‐621 directly targets FBXO11. Mechanistically, FBXO11 suppresses p53 activity by promoting its neddylation; thus, miR‐621‐mediated inhibition of FBXO11 restores p53 function and amplifies chemotherapy‐induced apoptosis. These findings were further validated in vivo using xenograft mouse models, where upregulation of miR‐621 enhances the response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (paclitaxel/carboplatin, PTX/CBP) by targeting FBXO11 (Figure 4B) [19, 49, 50]. FBXO11 drives invasiveness in a phenotype‐dependent manner. In vitro assays in MCF‐7 cells revealed that FBXO11 promotes non‐EMT collective invasion via repressing the p53/p21 axis, while in triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC), the AMPK‐FBXO11‐Snail axis triggers Snail degradation to restrain stemness and migration. In vivo metastasis models further confirmed that activation of this axis inhibits tumor invasion and chemoresistance [10, 51, 52]. Moreover, FBXO11 plays a pivotal role in immune evasion which negatively regulates MHCII expression by targeting the transcriptional activator CIITA. Clinically, a phenotype of characterized low FBXO11 combined with high CIITA predicts favorable survival, especially in early‐stage patients. Among its multiple substrates, BLIMP1 may partially mediate FBXO11 effects, whereas the FBXO11‐CIITA‐MHCII axis dominates prognostic stratification, supporting its utility as a prognostic biomarker and a context‐specific therapeutic target in BRCA [27].

Hematological Malignancies

FBXO11 exerts pivotal tumor‐suppressive roles across hematological malignancies, utilizing distinct substrate networks to regulate both lymphoid and myeloid pathogenesis. In lymphoid disorders, the FBXO11‐BCL‐6 axis is a core pathogenic driver. In DLBCL, FBXO11 loss‐of‐function mutations stabilize the oncogenic transcription factor BCL‐6, driving lymphomagenesis a process further reinforced by co‐chaperone AIP [53, 54], highlighting this axis as a core therapeutic target. Compared with DLBCL, Burkitt's lymphoma (BL) exhibits a higher frequency of FBXO11 mutations, which are loss‐of‐function alterations impairing BCL‐6 and Snail degradation. In vivo models demonstrate that FBXO11 deletion accelerates MYC‐driven lymphomagenesis independent of proliferation and synergizes with p53 mutations, supporting combined MYC/BCL‐6 targeting for BL therapy(Figure 4B) [7, 55]. In germinal‐center (GC) reactions, FBXO11 maintains BCL‐6 homeostasis during B‐cell maturation, and its inactivation induces lymphoid hyperplasia with gene dosage‐dependent effects, predisposing individuals to lymphoproliferative disease [30, 55, 56, 57, 58]. Additionally, EBV‐associated lymphomagenesis introduces a distinct regulatory mechanism, in EBV‐positive DLBCL, the viral antigen EBNA3C directly interacts with FBXO11 to enhance SCFFBXO11 mediated BCL6 ubiquitination and degradation, promoting viral‐mediated cell proliferation and transformation (Figure 4B) [59, 60]. Beyond the lymphoid lineage, FBXO11 acts as a potent tumor suppressor in myeloid malignancies. In vitro studies in MDS‐L cells demonstrated that FBXO11 loss drives MDS transformation into AML by disrupting RNA metabolism pathways and causing EZH2 aberrant splicing [34]. In established AML, FBXO11 interacts with the mitochondrial protease LONP1, mediating K63‐linked ubiquitination to promote LONP1 mitochondrial localization, thereby regulating cellular respiration. In vivo, FBXO11 loss synergizes with AML1/ETO and KRAS G12D mutations to induce aggressive leukemia [61]. Collectively, these findings delineate FBXO11's broad yet lineage‐specific tumor‐suppressive functions targeting BCL‐6/Snail in lymphoma and EZH2/LONP1 in myeloid malignancies underscoring its dual significance as a mechanistic driver and a therapeutic target in hematological cancers.

Emerging Roles of FBXO11 in Additional Human Cancer

Beyond the aforementioned malignancies, FBXO11 exhibits complex roles in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and ovarian cancer (OC). In RCC, particularly clear cell RCC (ccRCC), is a prevalent urinary malignancy with high metastatic potential and poor prognosis [62]. Bioinformatic and immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses revealed that FBXO11 is markedly upregulated in RCC, especially metastatic ccRCC, and its high expression serves as an independent adverse prognostic indicator. A prognostic chart integrating FBXO11 expression enhances the accuracy of overall survival prediction in ccRCC patients [63]. Mechanistically, lncRNA DRAIC directly binds hnRNPA2B1, disrupting its interaction with FBXO11 and preventing FBXO11‐mediated ubiquitination and degradation of hnRNPA2B1 in Caki‐1 and 769‐P cells. Stabilized hnRNPA2B1 then inhibits ccRCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by downregulating IGF1R, counteracting FBXO11's oncogenic drive [62, 64]. Conversely, in OC, FBXO11 is transcriptionally repressed by ZNF217. FBXO11 acts as a tumor suppressor by promoting K48‐linked KIF2C degradation to inhibit proliferation and migration in OVCAR3 and CAOV3 cells, which was validated in vivo by suppressed xenograft growth and peritoneal metastasis [65]. Collectively, FBXO11 acts as an oncoprotein in RCC but a tumor suppressor in OC and myeloid malignancies, underscoring its diverse functionalities.

The Role of FBXO11 in Tumor Chemotherapy Sensitivity and Prognosis

FBXO11 plays a crucial and context‐dependent role in tumor chemosensitivity, progression, and patient prognosis. In BRCA, it inhibits p53 transcriptional activity by promoting its neddylation, reducing chemosensitivity and inducing drug resistance [19]. In LUAD, it regulates sensitivity to cisplatin and crizotinib, suggesting that targeting FBXO11 may be a potential strategy to overcome drug resistance [66, 67]. Mechanistically, FBXO11 exerts distinct effects through the ubiquitination of diverse substrates: it acts as an oncogenic factor in PDAC by ubiquitinating SAMD1 to abrogate its tumor‐suppressive function [45], and inhibits the p53/p21 pathway in non‐EMT‐like cells to promote tumorigenesis [15]. In contrast, it functions as a tumor suppressor in DLBCL by degrading BCL6 to inhibit proliferation and induce apoptosis [36]. Additionally, FBXO11 participates in cell cycle and DNA damage repair via regulating the CRL4Cdt2 complex [68, 69], and its expression is modulated by miR‐26a in HCC and miR‐585 in GC to affect tumor progression [40]. Clinically, the prognostic value of FBXO11 is strictly tumor‐specific; high expression predicts poor prognosis in BRCA [19, 46], while low expression is associated with RCC progression [63]. In conclusion, FBXO11 functions as a molecular double‐edged sword in oncology, exerting either oncogenic or tumor‐suppressive effects depending on the cellular context and substrate specificity. Consequently, strategies targeting FBXO11 require careful evaluation and patient stratification, rather than being universally regarded as a therapeutic target.

Conclusion and Future Perspectives

FBXO11, as the substrate recognition subunit of the SCF E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, orchestrates the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of diverse downstream targets, occupying a central position in tumor biology. Accumulated evidence delineates a dualistic functional landscape that defies simplistic therapeutic categorization.

In most malignancies examined, FBXO11 functions as a bona fide tumor suppressor. Its downregulation or loss consistently correlates with tumor initiation, aggressive progression, and adverse clinical outcomes. Mechanistically, FBXO11 deficiency drives oncogenesis through deregulated cell cycle control, enhanced migratory and invasive capacity, and facilitation of EMT. Representative examples include FBXO11 mediated BCL‐6 degradation in lymphoma [53, 54, 55], its suppression of invasive phenotypes in GBM [28], miR‐26a driven FBXO11 silencing in HCC [25], and the LINC01436/miR‐585 axis that extinguishes FBXO11 expression in GC [40]. Additionally, FBXO11 modulates chemosensitivity in LC and apoptotic signaling via p53 interaction in BRCA [19], collectively reinforcing its broad tumor‐suppressive repertoire. However, the breadth of FBXO11's substrate spectrum precludes a uniformly suppressive role. In PDAC, FBXO11 mediated degradation of SMAD1 promotes disease progression [45], in BRCA and PCa, elevated FBXO11 expression correlates with poor prognosis [10, 29, 38, 51, 52]. This duality underscores a fundamental principle: the biological consequence of FBXO11 activity is dictated not by its expression level alone, but by the combinatorial landscape of available substrates, upstream regulators, and tumor‐specific molecular circuitry.

These observations carry profound therapeutic implications. While pharmacological modulation of FBXO11 is conceptually attractive, particularly inhibiting its oncogenic degradation in specific contexts, the context‐dependent nature of its function poses substantial challenges. Systemic manipulation risks unpredictable off‐target effects given the diversity of its substrate repertoire across tissues. We therefore propose that FBXO11's greatest near‐term clinical utility resides in its potential as a prognostic biomarker; its expression patterns carry tumor‐specific prognostic significance and could inform risk stratification and treatment decisions. Context‐specific therapeutic targeting may be feasible only where oncogenic substrates are clearly defined and patient stratification is rigorously implemented.

Several critical gaps warrant immediate attention to fully elucidate the therapeutic potential of FBXO11. First and foremost, systematic proteomic and interactome mapping across diverse tumor types is essential to define context‐specific substrate networks. Parallel efforts must comprehensively elucidate upstream regulatory layers including transcriptional control, non‐coding RNA modulation, and post‐translational modifications to identify actionable therapeutic nodes. Beyond cell‐intrinsic mechanisms, the interplay between FBXO11 and the tumor immune microenvironment remains largely unexplored; investigating its role in immune evasion could uncover novel combinatorial immunotherapy strategies. To mitigate the risks associated with systemic modulation, the development of tumor‐selective delivery systems, such as antibody‐drug conjugates or nanoparticle platforms, is imperative. Ultimately, large‐scale, multi‐center clinical studies are required to validate the prognostic value of FBXO11 by rigorously correlating its expression with therapeutic responses and patient survival outcomes.

In summary, FBXO11 occupies a unique nexus between ubiquitin‐dependent proteolysis and tumor biology. Its functional duality demands a paradigm shift from viewing FBXO11 as a binary therapeutic target to embracing it as a context‐specific vulnerability requiring precision medicine strategies. Only through a nuanced, tumor‐context‐aware approach can its full clinical potential be realized.
