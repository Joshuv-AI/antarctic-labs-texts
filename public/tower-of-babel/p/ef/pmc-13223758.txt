Association of a mild or an important decline in left ventricular ejection fraction with ventricular tachyarrhythmias, sudden cardiac death, and all-cause death in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction: a report from the CHART-2 Study
Authors: Tomohiro Ito, Takashi Noda, Kotaro Nochioka, Takashi Shiroto, Nobuhiko Yamamoto, Hiroyuki Sato, Hideka Hayashi, Takahiko Chiba, Makoto Nakano, Hiroyuki Takahama, Jun Takahashi, Satoshi Miyata, Hiroaki Shimokawa, Satoshi Yasuda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsA decline in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is often observed in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and is associated with adverse outcomes. Although baseline LVEF is crucial for assessing the risk of sudden cardiac death in patients with HF, little is known about the relationship between the decline in LVEF and lethal arrhythmic events among patients with HFpEF.Methods and resultsWe retrospectively analysed data from the CHART-2 Study. A total of 1453 patients with HF and LVEF ≥ 50% at registration (73 years, 39% female) were included and categorized into three groups based on their LVEF at the prespecified 1-year follow-up; 1316 with LVEF ≥ 50%, 120 with LVEF 36–50%, and 17 with LVEF ≤ 35%. The primary endpoint was a composite of ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, and sudden cardiac death. All-cause death was also evaluated. During a median follow-up of 7.9 years, 79 (5.4%) patients experienced a composite event. Patients with LVEF < 50% at 1 year had a significantly higher incidence of the composite event (11.7 vs. 4.8%, P < 0.001), and all-cause death (62.8% vs. 51.8%, P = 0.006), compared to those with LVEF ≥ 50%. Importantly, a decline in LVEF to <50% was independently associated with an increased risk of the composite event (adjusted hazard ratio 1.99, 95% CI 1.04–3.79, P = 0.04).ConclusionIn patients with HFpEF, even a mild decline in LVEF was associated with a higher risk of ventricular tachyarrhythmia, sudden cardiac death, and all-cause death. Introduction

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) accounts for nearly 50% of all heart failure (HF) cases, and its prevalence continues to increase as the dominant form of HF.1 These patients face significant complications, including disease progression and a substantial rate of sudden death, although the exact risk of sudden cardiac death (SCD) remains poorly defined.2,3

Baseline left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is crucial in assessing the risk of ventricular tachyarrhythmias (VAs) and SCD in patients with HF. Patients with LVEF ≤ 35% are at significantly greater risk of these events, and implantable cardioverter–defibrillator (ICD) therapy is indicated in such patients.4,5 In contrast, the risk among patients with HF and a higher LVEF was relatively lower. A recent large-population study reported that the incidence of VAs was 0.3 and 0.7 events per 100 person-years in patients with an LVEF of ≥50% compared to those with an LVEF between 41 and 49%, respectively.6 Therefore, the baseline LVEF provides a stratified risk assessment for patients with HF. However, LVEF may change over time, thereby influencing these outcomes. In patients with HFpEF, LVEF may decline by 5.5% over 5 years, and mortality increases by 7% per 5% decline in LVEF.7 Furthermore, in large-scale cohort studies, the transition from HFpEF to heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction has been associated with a marked increase in all-cause mortality and an increased likelihood of hospitalization for HF.8,9 Despite these insights, many aspects of the temporal decline in LVEF and its impact on the risk of VAs and SCD remain unclear.

To address these gaps, we investigated the risk of VAs and SCD in patients with HFpEF, focusing on LVEF changes, using data from the CHART-2 (Chronic Heart Failure Analysis and Registry in the Tohoku District 2) Study.10 This large-scale, multicentre, longitudinal cohort study provided critical insights into HF progression, particularly in patients with preserved LVEF.

Methods

Study designs

This study was a post hoc analysis of the CHART-2 Study, a large-scale multicentre prospective observational cohort study conducted in Japan, in which 10 219 consecutive outpatients with chronic HF were enrolled at 24 affiliated hospitals between October 2006 and March 2010. The study protocol specified annual collection of all patient data, with all clinical events during the observation period reviewed by an independent data monitoring committee. Detailed information regarding this study has been described previously.10,11

Of the 10 219 patients enrolled in the CHART-2 Study, we initially selected 1910 patients with HFpEF who had no history of sustained ventricular tachycardia or ICD implantation at registration. According to the ESC Guidelines, HFpEF was defined if they had (i) symptomatic heart failure (HF) with LVEF ≥ 50%, (ii) B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) over 35 pg/mL or N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide over 125 pg/mL, and (iii) relevant structural heart disease (left ventricular mass index > 95 g in females and >115 g in males or left atrial dilation > 40 mm) and/or diastolic abnormality (E/A ratio < 0.75 or >1.5, or deceleration time of E-wave < 140 ms).12 Patients with cardiac sarcoidosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, or amyloidosis were also excluded. Subsequently, LVEF data were available for 1453 patients at the prespecified 1-year follow-up. They were divided into three groups based on LVEF at 1 year; 1316 with LVEF remained ≥50%, 120 with LVEF mildly declined to 36–50%, and 17 patients with LVEF declined to ≤35% (Figure 1).

Data collection

Data collected at registration and during the specified annual follow-up over a 10-year period were included in the analysis (see Supplementary material online, Table S1). LVEF was assessed using either the Simpson method or the Teichholz method by echocardiography. Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT) was defined as three or more consecutive premature ventricular beats lasting up to 30 s. NSVT data were obtained from 24 h Holter electrocardiograms (ECGs) or prior ECG data, including 12-lead ECGs, and continuous monitoring during hospitalization. Certified cardiologists further evaluated the diagnoses of NSVT.

Definitions and clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes including the first occurrence of a composite of VAs and SCD, with VAs including ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation and all-cause death were collected. Ventricular tachycardia (VT) was defined as tachycardia lasting >30 s or haemodynamically unstable tachycardia, and ventricular fibrillation (VF) was defined as a polymorphic ventricular tachyarrhythmia with an RR interval < 200 ms. SCD was defined as instantaneous, unexpected death, or death within an hour of symptom onset not related to circulatory failure.13

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as means ± standard deviations or as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical data are presented as frequencies (percentages). Clinical variables were compared using the Student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

In the primary analysis, we compared cumulative incidence rates of the composite outcome of VAs and SCD, or all-cause death between the groups, with follow-up starting from the time point of the LVEF assessment at the 1-year follow-up. The incidence rates were compared using the Gray's test. Kaplan–Meier analyses stratified by presence of ischaemic aetiology were also performed. To address time bias in the LVEF classification, Kaplan–Meier analyses were performed for additional periods. Based on the initial cohort of 1910 patients with HFpEF at registration, patients were reclassified into three groups based on their LVEF at the 3- or 5-year follow-up, using the same methods as in the primary analysis. After excluding patients diagnosed with cardiac sarcoidosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, or cardiac amyloidosis, prognostic outcomes were examined.

A multivariable model with Fine–Gray subdistribution hazards was adjusted for variables showed significance in the univariate analysis. To identify the predictors of a decline in LVEF to <50% in the following year, we used baseline variables and LVEF values at the 1-year follow-up, followed by univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses. The association between LVEF at the 1-year follow-up, treated as a continuous variable, and the risk of VAs and SCD or all-cause death was assessed using a restricted cubic spline model with four knots. The reference point was set at an LVEF of 50%, with hazard ratios scaled to 1.0.

In each analysis, all-cause mortality was considered a competing risk factor. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0). A two-sided P-value of <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tohoku University Graduate School of Medicine (reference no. 2021-1-634).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 1453 patients (mean age; 72.5 ± 10.4 years, females; 39%) were included in the primary analysis. Approximately 80% of patients were classified as New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II or higher. Atrial fibrillation (AF) was observed in 805 patients (55%). The mean heart rate at echocardiography was 68 ± 14 beats per minute. Patients were divided into three groups based on their LVEF at the 1-year follow-up: 1316 (90.6%) patients with LVEF remained ≥50%, 120 (8.3%) with LVEF mildly declined to 36–50%, and 17 (1.2%) patients with LVEF declined to ≤35%, and as shown in Table 1. The mean change in LVEF in a year was −1.1 ± 9.4%.

Compared to LVEF ≥ 50%, LVEF 36–50% group exhibited a lower prevalence of females and a higher prevalence of prior myocardial infarction (MI). Only 1 patient experienced a newly diagnosed myocardial infarction before the 1-year follow-up, while 3 did in the LVEF ≥ 50% group. Additionally, this group had larger left ventricular diastolic diameter and systolic diameters. AF was fewer in LVEF 36–50% group. There were no significant differences in medication use between these groups. In contrast, LVEF ≤ 35% group showed similar prevalence of females, numerically higher prevalence of prior MI, including 1 patient experienced a newly diagnosed myocardial infarction compared to LVEF ≥ 50% group. The baseline characteristics associated with an LVEF decline to <50% at the 1-year follow-up included, prior myocardial infarction, lower baseline LVEF, and increased left ventricular diastolic and systolic diameters (see Supplementary material online, Table S2). Notably, only prior myocardial infarction was significantly associated with a decline in LVEF of >10% within 1 year.

Ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death in patients with declined LVEF

We compared the clinical outcomes between the 3 groups. During a median follow-up period of 7.9 years [interquartile range (IQR) 3.8–10.9], the composite outcome of VAs and SCD occurred in 79 (5.4%) patients and 768 (52.9%) died. Composite of VAs and SCD was observed in 63 patients (4.8%; 0.6 events per 100 patient-years) in LVEF ≥ 50% group, 15 (12.5%; 1.9 events per 100 patient-years) in LVEF 36–50% group, and 1 (5.9%; 1.0 events per 100 patient-years) in LVEF ≤ 35% group (Figure 2A). Compared to the LVEF ≥ 50% group, the incidence of the composite of VAs and SCD was significantly higher in both the LVEF 36–50% group (P < 0.001) and the combined LVEF < 50% group (P < 0.001). Stratified analysis by presence of ischaemic aetiology demonstrated consistent trends across both groups (see Supplementary Figure). Among patients with LVEF 36–50% at 1 year, the event rate for VAs and SCD was 13.2% (10/76) in those with ischaemic aetiology and 11.4% (5/44) in those without, suggesting a comparable risk between ischaemic and non-ischaemic aetiologies in this LVEF group. All-cause death was observed in 682 patients (51.8%; 7.0 events per 100 patient-years) in LVEF ≥ 50% group, 76 (63.3%; 9.5 events per 100 patient-years) patients in LVEF 36–50% group, and 10 (58.8%; 9.8 events per 100 patient-years) in LVEF ≤ 35% group (Figure 2B). Similar to the composite outcome of VAs and SCD, all-cause death was also significantly higher in patients with LVEF 36–50% (P < 0.001) and in those with LVEF < 50% (P = 0.008), compared to those with LVEF ≥ 50%. Examining each event individually, VAs were observed in 22 (1.7%) patients in LVEF ≥ 50% group, 10 (8.3%) patients in LVEF 36–50% group, and none of the patients in LVEF ≤ 35% group (Figure 2C). SCD occurred in 44 (3.3%) patients in LVEF ≥ 50%, 7 (5.3%) patients in LVEF 36–50% group and, 1 (5.9%) in LVEF ≤ 35% group (Figure 2D). Patients with LVEF 36–50% experienced significant higher incidence of VAs (P < 0.001) and numerically higher incidence of SCD (P = 0.15) than those with LVEF ≥ 50%.

Landmark analysis of LVEF decline at 3- and 5-year follow-up

A landmark analysis at the 3- and 5-year follow-up was also performed to account for the timing of the decline in LVEF. At the 3-year follow-up, 1157 patients were included, with 1016 showing LVEF ≥ 50%, 116 showing a mild decline in LVEF to 36–50%, and 25 showing LVEF declined to ≤35%. During a median follow-up period of 7.4 years (IQR 3.8–9.2), those with LVEF 36–50% had a significantly higher incidence of VAs and SCD compared to those with LVEF ≥ 50% (12.9% vs. 3.4%, P < 0.001, Figure 3A). At the 5-year follow-up, 880 patients were included, with 20 had LVEF ≤ 35%, 98 had LVEF 36–50%, and 762 with LVEF remained LVEF ≥ 50%. During a median follow-up period of 6.2 years (IQR 3.7–7.5), those with LVEF 36–50% had a significantly higher incidence of VAs and SCD compared to those with LVEF ≥ 50%, similarly. (7.1% vs. 2.4%; P = 0.01, Figure 3B).

Clinical significancy of mild decline in LVEF in patients with HFpEF

A decline in LVEF from ≥50% to <50% was significantly associated with an increased predictor of the composite outcome of VAs and SCD [adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) 1.99, 95% CI 1.04–3.79; P = 0.04], with covariables those were significant (P < 0.05) in the univariable analysis: prior MI, history of NSVT, anaemia and log-transformed BNP (Table 2). Moreover, a decline in LVEF from ≥50% to 36–50% was even associated with VAs and SCD (aHR 1.94, 95% CI 1.02–3.71, P = 0.04), with covariables above. In Model 2, LVEF at the 1-year follow-up was also an independent predictor for VAs and SCD (aHR, 0.97, 95% CI 0.95–0.99; P = 0.03), along with the same covariates. When adjusted with clinically important variables as age, gender, NYHA class, prior MI, prior hospitalization for HF, anaemia, eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, AF, NSVT, log-transformed BNP, and medications, a decline in LVEF and LVEF at the 1-year follow-up were also presented as independent risk factors. Impact of LVEF decline on the risk of VAs and SCD

The effect of a decline in LVEF < 50% on VAs and SCD was further examined. When LVEF at the 1-year follow-up was considered a continuous variable, the risk for VAs and SCD gradually increased as LVEF decreased (Figure 4A). The analysis for all-cause death showed similar trend (Figure 4B).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to reveal that even a mild or an important decline in LVEF at 1-year follow-up among patients with HFpEF is associated with higher incidences of subsequent VAs and SCD. This finding is consistent with the landmark analyses of LVEF decline at the 3- and 5-year follow-up, supporting that a decline in LVEF is associated with an increased risk of these events whenever it occurs.

Mild decline in LVEF and its association with lethal arrhythmic risk in HFpEF patients

Previous landmark studies have identified HF patients with LVEF ≤ 35% as a key high-risk group for SCD.4,5 Most of these studies relied on baseline LVEF data to provide projections for long-term outcomes at an initial point in time. These projections do not account for dynamic changes in LVEF, which can alter a patient's risk profile. While baseline LVEF assessment is sufficient to identify patients at high absolute risk, longitudinal evaluations are essential for detecting emerging risk in those with declining LVEF. Given the progressive nature of ventricular remodelling, this study focused on patients with preserved LVEF. A decline in LVEF among baseline HFpEF patients was associated with an increased risk of lethal arrhythmic events, with this elevated risk evident within 1 year. Notably, within the 36–50% range, the risk increased as LVEF approached the critical threshold of 35%. Our focus was on those with LVEF declines to 36–50%, where guidelines remain unclear, and the results may indicate that prognostic risk in this LVEF range was comparable regardless of ischaemic aetiology. This allowed us to explore whether modest decline in LVEF from preserved LVEF carry emerging arrhythmic risk. Recent guidelines and reviews have emphasized the limitations of relying solely on NYHA class and LVEF ≤ 35% for SCD risk stratification.14,15 Additional risk factors, such as syncope, late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac MRI, inducible sustained monomorphic VT, and pathogenic mutations in specific genes, highlight the presence of high-risk individuals even among those with mildly declined LVEF. Importantly, most assessments are based on a single LVEF value, overlooking its evolution over time. Our findings reveal that early decline in LVEF from preserved levels is associated with increased arrhythmic risk, reinforcing the concept that risk develops dynamically. This trajectory is particularly relevant in HFpEF patients, in whom progressive decline may go unrecognized. Serial LVEF evaluation could improve risk stratification and inform timely intervention before severe dysfunction develops (Table 2).

Possible mechanism for the increased risk associated with a decline in LVEF

Reduced LVEF, as a key manifestation of systolic dysfunction, is a well-recognized risk factor for ventricular arrhythmias due to increased arrhythmic substrates, ion channel remodelling, and abnormal autonomic nervous system activity.16,17 Similarly, diastolic dysfunction has been associated with an elevated risk of arrhythmias, with greater severity potentially correlating with higher risk.18,19 A decline in LVEF among patients with HFpEF may trigger similar pathophysiological changes, thereby heightening the risk of lethal arrhythmic events. In this study, several baseline characteristics were identified as potential contributors to LVEF decline, but only prior myocardial infarction was significantly associated with an LVEF decline over 10% within a year. These findings suggest that ischaemic events and the gradual progression of HF play critical roles in LVEF decline, contributing to the formation of arrhythmic substrates. Advancements in imaging technologies, such as cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and echocardiography, have improved the detailed evaluation of anatomical and functional abnormalities.20–22 These imaging techniques offer valuable insights into the subtle changes associated with mild LVEF decline in HFpEF patients, shedding light on the mechanisms linked to an increased risk of lethal arrhythmias.

Clinical implication: importance of re-assessment and targeted approach for patients at increased risk

Only a limited number of clinical features associated with lethal arrhythmic events have been identified in HFpEF patients.23,24 This challenge arises from the diverse characteristics of the HFpEF population, which complicate the identification of baseline risk factors applicable to all individuals. In our study, we focused on patients who exhibited a higher risk of these events by utilizing the clinical data collected over the following year. These individuals may represent a distinct subset of the broader HFpEF population. While we did not specifically analyse the timing between the onset of heart failure and the decline in LVEF, it is essential to pay closer attention to patients who experience a decline in LVEF, regardless of when this decline occurs. Regular re-assessment of LVEF in patients with HFpEF could facilitate early identification and intervention for those at an increased risk of lethal arrhythmias.

Study limitations

This study had several limitations. First, because the number of patients with LVEF ≤ 35% at 1 year was small, the statistical power to detect significant associations in this group was limited. Secondly, we lacked information on late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac MRI and a history of syncope, which are important predictors of ventricular arrhythmic events. Furthermore, detailed classification of VT (e.g. presence of syncope, monomorphic vs. polymorphic) was unavailable. The assessment of NSVT was based on intermittent Holter ECG monitoring and inpatient monitoring, which may have led to misclassification, potentially resulting in some subjects being incorrectly categorized as free of NSVT. Continuous monitoring methods, such as implantable loop recorders, could offer a more comprehensive evaluation. Thirdly, we did not collect detailed echocardiographic data on left ventricular diastolic function, such as E/e′, or right ventricular function to further classify HFpEF. A substantial proportion of patients lacked echocardiographic data at 1 year, limiting more detailed functional assessment (see Supplentary material online, Table S4). Fourthly, the doses of beta-blockers were generally lower than guideline-recommended targets, which may have limited their protective effect against VAs and SCD. Fourth, the timing of HFpEF diagnosis and LVEF decline varied among patients, which could have influenced the results. Finally, the cohort was enrolled between 2006 and 2010, prior to the introduction of newer therapies such as SGLT2 inhibitors, which may limit the applicability of our findings to contemporary practice.

Conclusion

In patients with HFpEF, even a mild LVEF decline, which typically does not warrant consideration for ICD, is significantly associated with a higher risk of VT, VF, or SCD. Regular clinical assessments to track changes in LVEF are crucial for the management of these patients.
