Sex-specific considerations in migraine therapy
Authors: Eloisa Rubio-Beltran, Antoinette MaassenVanDenBrink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Migraine is a highly prevalent and disabling neurovascular disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of moderate to severe headache, often accompanied by nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia. A prominent feature of migraine is its disproportionate burden on women, as evidenced by a higher prevalence, frequency and disability, relative to men. These differences are largely attributed to the modulatory effects of sex hormones on migraine pathophysiology. While the cyclic fluctuations of estrogen across reproductive milestones are known to significantly influence migraine onset and severity, far less is known about how sex hormones shape therapeutic response. Currently, the role of hormonal status in determining treatment efficacy remains poorly studied and represents a critical gap in the field. This narrative review synthesizes the current knowledge on the potential mechanisms underlying the higher prevalence in females. It further discusses the sex-specific responses to pharmacological therapies, as well as hormone-based interventions such as contraceptives and hormone replacement therapy. Special attention is given to female-specific migraine phenotypes, such as menstrual migraine, and to therapeutic implications across the female reproductive lifespan. Finally, we identify major gaps in current research and underscore the urgent need for sex- and gender-stratified approaches to optimize treatment and advance precision medicine in migraine care.

Introduction

Migraine is a highly prevalent and disabling neurovascular disorder characterized by unilateral, pulsatile headache attacks of moderate to severe intensity lasting 4–72 h, and commonly accompanied by photophobia, phonophobia, and/or nausea and vomiting. In approximately 30% of patients, attacks are preceded by focal neurological symptoms, most commonly visual or sensory disturbances. Accordingly, migraine is classified into migraine with aura (MA) and migraine without aura (MO) [1]. It is estimated that 1.2 billion people worldwide live with migraine, corresponding to a global, all-age prevalence of 18% [2,3]. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2021, headache disorders rank among the leading causes of years lived with disability (YLD), negatively affecting the quality of life of patients, and having a significant impact in the global economy [3]. Migraine therefore represents a major public health issue, affecting both individuals and society.

There is a marked gender disparity in migraine prevalence, with approximately 75% of migraine patients being women [4,5]. The one-year prevalence of migraine is 18% in women and 6% in men, while the lifetime incidence is 43% and 18%, respectively [4,6]. The differences in prevalence vary with age, with rates being similar in prepubescent children, followed by a marked increase in females after menarche, peaking during the reproductive years and then decreasing after menopause [4]. Interestingly, the prevalence of migraine in transgender women on gender affirming therapy is similar to that observed in cisgender women [7].

Besides being more prevalent in women, migraine is the leading cause of YLD in females of reproductive age [8]. Women with migraine not only experience greater disability, but also longer-lasting, more intense and frequent headache attacks, that are more often accompanied by symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, photo- and phonophobia [5,[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Female patients also have a higher risk of chronification, as well as an increased risk of cardiovascular events, including cardiovascular mortality [[12], [13], [14]].

Current research suggests that there is a significant hormonal component in migraine pathophysiology. However, the exact mechanisms underlying this gender disparity and its implications for treatment have not yet been fully elucidated. Unfortunately, most clinical trials and therapeutic guidelines for migraine do not explicitly address sex or gender as a biological variable. Consequently, a critical gap exists in optimizing migraine therapies based on hormonal status. The present narrative review aims to synthesize current evidence on the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms contributing to the marked female predominance of migraine, as well as therapeutic considerations across the female lifespan, taking into account both efficacy and safety. In doing so, we aim to integrate pathophysiological insights with clinical management considerations, addressing a gap in the literature, which often treats these aspects separately. Furthermore, current challenges and potential directions for future research toward the development of sex- and gender-informed, personalized approaches to migraine therapy are discussed.

This narrative review used a targeted search strategy to identify literature on sex-specific considerations in migraine treatment. The search strategy and inclusion criteria were defined a priori. PubMed and Scopus were searched from inception to October 7th, 2025, using terms as “migraine,” “sex differences,” “hormones,” “menstrual migraine,” “pregnancy,” “menopause,” and treatment-related keywords (e.g., acute and prophylactic therapy). Eligible studies included original preclinical and clinical articles, key guidelines, and selected high-quality reviews published in English and available in full text. In this review, individuals assigned female at birth are referred to as “women” or “females” and those assigned male at birth as “men” or “males”, while acknowledging that contemporary definitions of sex and gender extend beyond a binary framework and include individuals who identify as intersex, transgender, genderfluid, or genderqueer. Although such non-binary definitions are largely absent from the migraine literature, they have been incorporated as comprehensively as possible in the present review. Using this framework, we sought to identify areas of scientific consensus regarding sex- and gender-related differences in migraine research.

Migraine across the female lifespan

The course of migraine in women is profoundly influenced by the reproductive milestones, which shape the hormonal environment across the lifespan (see Fig. 1). Consistent with this observation, approximately 60% of patients report that their migraine headache attacks are triggered by menses [15]. The International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD) therefore recognizes menstrual migraine (MM), defined as migraine attacks occurring on day 1 ± 2 of menstruation in at least two out of three menstrual cycles [1]. MM is further categorized into pure MM (PMM), in which attacks occur exclusively around menstruation, and menstrually related migraine (MRM), in which attacks predominantly occur in the perimenstrual window but may also arise at other times in the cycle [1]. It is estimated that approximately <1–8% of females with migraine have PMM and 13% have MRM, although reported rates vary depending on study design and diagnostic criteria [[16], [17], [18]]. Perimenstrual attacks are typically more severe than non-perimenstrual attacks, they are more often without aura, longer-lasting, more disabling, less responsive to treatment, more likely to recur, and associated with increased triptan use [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. During pregnancy, patients often report a reduction in the frequency of migraine headache attacks. However, patients with MM experience higher headache intensity during the first trimester and the postpartum period [25]. Moreover, the phenotype of migraine may change; for example, a retrospective study showed that 70% of women who reported MA during pregnancy had no prior history of aura [26]. Prospective studies have also shown that women with MA are less likely to present a reduction in migraine headache attacks during gestation than women with MO [27,28].

In the perimenopausal period, the prevalence of migraine is estimated to be 16–29% [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34]], with 8–13% of patients reporting new migraine onset [28,35]. Conflicting data exist regarding the clinical presentation of migraine during this period, as the frequency of migraine episodes has been reported to remain either unchanged, decreased, or increased [29,30,35,36]. Studies have suggested that while the frequency of MA does not change with menopause, patients with MO may experience an increase in migraine headache attacks during the perimenopausal state, followed by a decrease after physiological menopause [29,36]. Conversely, women with surgically-induced menopause without estrogen add-back therapy generally report more frequent and/or disabling headaches [33,35,37,38]. Taken together, these observations highlight the crucial role of hormonal fluctuations in shaping migraine patterns and underscore the importance of incorporating reproductive stage and hormonal status into the clinical evaluation and management of migraine in women.

Pathophysiology of migraine, role of sex hormones

Migraine is a complex, multiphasic neurovascular disorder. Neuroimaging studies have increasingly demonstrated widespread structural and functional alterations in brain regions involved in pain processing and modulation among individuals with migraine, with growing evidence that these changes exhibit sex-specific patterns [39,40].

The pronounced female predominance of migraine, together with the variation in attack frequency across reproductive milestones, suggests that sex hormones are involved in the pathophysiology of migraine [41]. Accordingly, studies have shown that during the late luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, the decline in plasma estrogen concentration correlates with the onset of migraine attacks, with patients exhibiting a more rapid decline in estrogen levels when compared with controls [[42], [43], [44], [45], [46]]. In contrast, phases of the menstrual cycle characterized by elevated estrogen levels are associated with a reduction in MO attack frequency, supporting a protective role of estrogen [41,45]. Notably, women with MA have higher plasma estrogen levels compared with those with MO, and phases of the menstrual cycle with rising estrogen levels have been associated with an increased frequency of aura attacks, similar to what is observed during pregnancy [28,47]. Consistent with these findings, preclinical studies have shown that estrogen modulates the threshold for cortical spreading depression, the neurobiological correlate of MA [28]. Hormonal influences in men remain less clearly defined. Some studies report lower progesterone levels and higher estrogen-to-progesterone ratios in men with migraine compared to healthy controls, although results have been inconsistent [[48], [49], [50], [51]]. Nonetheless, evidence suggests that fluctuations in the balance between testosterone and estrogen over the lifespan may contribute to migraine susceptibility in men.

The headache phase of migraine is associated with activation of the trigeminovascular system (TVS), followed by the release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) from sensory fibers, leading to sensitization of nociceptive pathways [52]. Notably, preclinical studies have shown increased expression of CGRP and its receptor in the female TVS, along with widespread distribution of estrogen, progesterone, and androgen receptors throughout its components [53]. These findings suggest that sex hormones can directly modulate CGRP release and trigeminovascular function. In line with this, CGRP administration has been shown to produce more robust and longer-lasting migraine-like responses in female compared to male rodents [54]. Animal studies have also demonstrated that the decline in ovarian steroid hormones correlates with increased sensitization of the TVS [55], further indicating that its activity is regulated by hormonal fluctuations.

Observations in patients are consistent with preclinical data. CGRP levels are higher in women than in men and fluctuate across reproductive milestones [[56], [57], [58], [59]]. During pregnancy, CGRP levels rise substantially, reaching peak concentrations toward term and returning to baseline after delivery [57]. Certainly, many patients experience a reduction in migraine frequency during pregnancy and the puerperium. This apparent paradox may be explained by a distinction between local cranial CGRP release, which is implicated in migraine attacks, and the increased systemic CGRP levels observed in pregnancy, which are involved in physiological hemodynamic adaptations [60]. Alternatively, sustained elevation of CGRP during pregnancy may induce receptor desensitization, thereby reducing migraine susceptibility. Conversely, in postmenopausal women, decreased levels of CGRP in plasma have been reported [58]. A recent crossover study measured CGRP concentrations in tear fluid and plasma among women with regular menstrual cycles, those using combined oral contraceptives (COCs), and postmenopausal women, comparing each group with migraine-free controls. CGRP levels were elevated in women with migraine and regular menstrual cycles compared with controls, whereas no differences were observed between migraine and non-migraine groups among women using COCs or those who were postmenopausal [59]. Similar results were observed with PACAP, a neuropeptide that has also been shown to play a relevant role in migraine pathophysiology [61]. Interestingly, studies evaluating capsaicin-induced changes in dermal blood flow, a readout of CGRP release from cephalic and extracephalic regions, have shown that healthy female subjects exhibit increased responses during menstruation compared to other phases of their cycle, whereas patients with MRM exhibit no cyclic changes, suggesting reduced trigeminovascular cyclicity in women with migraine [44,62].

In summary, clinical and preclinical evidence indicates that sex hormones, particularly estrogens, play a central role in modulating the activity of the TVS, thus contributing to the marked female predominance and cyclical patterns of migraine. The presence of sex hormone receptors throughout the TVS provides mechanistic support for these observations.

Sex-specific considerations in treatment

Pharmacological management remains a primary component of migraine care; however, most available medications have not been systematically developed or evaluated with respect to sex- and gender-dependent pharmacokinetic differences, female-specific migraine subtypes or hormone-related responses. Personalized treatment approaches should consider biological sex, gender, hormonal status, reproductive plans, and individual risk profiles (see Fig. 2). For instance, men and women differ in body composition, with men generally having greater body weight, muscle mass, and total body water, whereas women tend to have a higher percentage of body fat, a difference that diminishes with age. These physiological variations, together with age-related reductions in muscle mass and fluid compartments, can significantly influence drug distribution and pharmacokinetics [63]. Comorbidities such as obesity can further modify these responses [64]. Notably, obese women show more than a two-fold increased risk of migraine, likely due to increased estrogen production in adipose tissue [[65], [66], [67], [68], [69]], adding to the complexity of clinical management.Fig. 2Sex- and gender-specific considerations in migraine pharmacotherapy.

Currently, the management of MM generally follows the same acute and preventive strategies used for other migraine types, with specific short-term preventive strategies or hormone therapy implemented in women [70]. Nevertheless, treatment guidelines remain inconsistent. Moreover, as the field evolves, there is increasing recognition that sex and gender are distinct constructs. In transgender individuals, gender affirming therapy may result in new-onset or worsened migraine headache attacks [7]. Thus, the hormonal status of gender-diverse patients should also be considered in clinical assessment and management.

Sex-specific considerations are especially important in migraine prophylaxis because preventive therapies are often long-term and frequently involve medications with long half-lives. Since migraine disproportionately affects women of childbearing age, treatment choices must incorporate reproductive plans, the possibility of unintentional pregnancy, and the need for appropriate contraceptive counseling [71]. Ideally, women planning pregnancy should discontinue preventive medications at least five plasma half-lives of the drug(s) they use before conception, which may last up to several months. As this is rarely feasible in practice, all women of reproductive potential receiving preventive therapy should be considered at ongoing risk of pregnancy. In this context, non-pharmacological strategies, such as lifestyle modifications, should be prioritized [71]. Moreover, although pregnancy often improves migraine for many women, attacks may persist or worsen in a substantial proportion, requiring both pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic interventions [[25], [26], [27], [28],72]. Medication use during this period must balance maternal symptom relief with fetal safety, as drugs may cross the placenta or indirectly impact fetal development depending on the stage of gestation, drug potency, and dosage [73]. Similar precautions apply during breastfeeding, where maternal treatment must be balanced against potential infant exposure [74]. Although most drugs have lower concentrations in breast milk than maternal plasma, minimal exposure may still pose risks, particularly for preterm or medically fragile infants. Timing of administration and selection of safer agents are key strategies, and in some cases, discontinuation of breastfeeding may need to be considered to prioritize maternal health. Building on these considerations, this section will explore acute and preventive migraine therapies, highlighting how sex-, gender-, and hormone-related factors can guide individualized treatment selection.

Acute, non-migraine specific treatments

Acetaminophen (paracetamol) and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), including ibuprofen, naproxen, and mefenamic acid, are widely used for the acute treatment of mild-to-moderate migraine headache attacks [75]. When prescribing NSAIDs, it is important to account for potential sex-related differences in pharmacokinetics. For instance, acetylsalicylic acid is absorbed, metabolized, and cleared more rapidly in women than in men [76]. Nonetheless, a retrospective study of 61 migraine patients reported no sex-related differences in the clinical efficacy of acetylsalicylic acid for migraine relief, although the authors noted that plasma concentrations were a possible predictor of response [77]. Similarly, in a human experimental pain study evaluating ibuprofen 800 mg, the weight-adjusted volume of distribution was approximately twofold higher in women; however, no association was observed between plasma ibuprofen concentrations and analgesic response [78]. Interestingly, studies have reported that acetylsalicylic acid use in men is more strongly associated with upper gastrointestinal and peptic ulcer bleeding, while women who take low-dose aspirin regularly, particularly those older than 70 years, have an increased risk of peptic ulcer disease [79,80]. Collectively, these findings underscore that sex-related pharmacokinetic differences may influence not only drug efficacy but also tolerability.

In the management of MM, NSAIDs are particularly relevant, as prostaglandin levels increase during menstruation and contribute to nociceptive signaling [81]. In line with this, in a small clinical trial, mefenamic acid (500 mg three times daily) achieved significantly higher 2-h pain-free rates than placebo [82]. Additionally, two randomized, placebo-controlled trials demonstrated that a fixed-dose combination of naproxen 500 mg and sumatriptan 85 mg was superior to placebo for both 2-h and sustained headache-free outcomes in the treatment of perimenstrual attacks [83].

During pregnancy, the use of NSAIDs is generally avoided during the first and second trimesters, as first-trimester exposure has been reported to be possibly or inconclusively associated with miscarriage and congenital malformations [84,85], although other studies have not found an increased risk [86]. Use during the third trimester is contraindicated due to the risk of premature closure of the ductus arteriosus [[87], [88], [89]]. Acetaminophen has long been considered safe throughout pregnancy, but recent studies have raised concerns regarding increased miscarriage risk and potential associations with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, asthma, and testicular maldescent in males following prolonged in-utero exposure (>28 days) [[90], [91], [92], [93], [94]]. It is worth noting that the European Medicines Agency reviewed two of these studies and concluded that current evidence remains insufficient to establish a causal relationship between prenatal exposure and later neurodevelopmental outcomes [95].

During lactation, acetaminophen, ibuprofen, and diclofenac are generally regarded as safe, whereas evidence for naproxen and indomethacin is more limited [87]. Acetylsalicylic acid should not be used as a first-line agent due to the risk of neonatal hemolysis and bleeding, although a recent study indicated that infant exposure is minimal when using low doses [96]. Ibuprofen is considered the preferred NSAID during breastfeeding because of its low relative infant dose and established pediatric safety profile. In contrast, NSAIDs with longer half-lives such as naproxen, may accumulate in the infant with prolonged maternal use and should be used cautiously [97].

Acute, migraine-specific treatments

Triptans

Triptans are considered the gold standard for the acute treatment of migraine. Studies have shown that women are more likely to use migraine-specific treatments, particularly triptans [5]. Although they are generally effective in both men and women, evidence indicates that their efficacy and tolerability may differ between sexes. A recent meta-analysis examining sex-specific outcomes reported no significant differences in initial response rates; however, female patients exhibited a higher risk of headache recurrence at 24 and 48 h following triptan administration. Interestingly, women also had an increased risk of adverse events and greater drug exposure [98]. These clinical findings are supported by pharmacokinetic studies showing higher plasma concentrations and lower clearance rates of several triptans in women compared with men [[99], [100], [101]].

In the context of MM, evidence indicates that triptans are effective therapeutic options [70,[102], [103], [104], [105], [106], [107], [108], [109], [110], [111], [112], [113], [114], [115], [116]]. Nevertheless, perimenstrual migraine attacks are associated with increased triptan use, intake of higher triptan doses, and higher recurrence rates compared with non-perimenstrual attacks [19,20,24,117].

During pregnancy, triptans may be considered for the treatment of more severe migraine attacks. A 2015 meta-analysis that included 4208 infants born to women who used triptans during pregnancy (predominantly sumatriptan) and 1,466,994 infants of pregnant women with migraine who did not use triptans, found no increased risk of spontaneous abortion, major congenital malformations, or preterm birth among triptan users compared with migraine controls. However, the rate of spontaneous abortion was significantly higher when triptan-exposed pregnancies were compared with healthy, non-migraine controls [118]. A more recent meta-analysis evaluating acute treatment during pregnancy similarly reported that triptan exposure was not associated with increased risks of fetal malformations, low birth weight, or preterm birth when compared with the general population [119]. Nevertheless, not all triptans should be considered equally safe during pregnancy. Sumatriptan, the most hydrophilic agent in this class, has limited placental transfer, with only approximately 15% of the administered dose reaching the fetal compartment over 4 h, whereas the other, more lipophilic triptans may cross the placenta more readily [120,121]. Summarizing, as most data are available on sumatriptan, and because of its hydrophilic properties, sumatriptan seems the preferred triptan to use during pregnancy.

During breastfeeding, available data remain limited; therefore, caution is generally advised [87]. Nonetheless, the relative infant dose of several triptans, particularly sumatriptan and eletriptan, is very low, suggesting that use during lactation poses minimal risk to the infant [87,122,123].

Gepants

No studies have evaluated differences in the pharmacokinetics of gepants. Regarding efficacy, a recent subgroup subpopulation analysis of available published data from FDA reviews reported enhanced efficacy in women compared with men for achieving pain freedom and freedom from the most bothersome symptom 2 h after administration [124]. Nonetheless, it should be noted that, due to the lower prevalence of migraine in males, men often represent only 10%–14% of participants in clinical trials. As a result, these trials are not designed or adequately powered to evaluate sex-specific treatment differences. In line with this, a recent secondary analysis of pooled data from randomized clinical trials of ubrogepant reported similar rates of pain freedom between males and females, as well as similar rates of freedom from the most bothersome symptom 2 h after administration [125]. These findings underscore the need for clinical trials designed and adequately powered to detect sex- and gender-related differences in treatment response.

Data on the efficacy and safety of gepants in MM, pregnancy and lactation are limited. A post-hoc analysis of a phase 3, multicenter, 52-week, open-label long-term safety extension trial reported comparable efficacy for the treatment of perimenstrual and non-perimenstrual headache attacks [126]. In pregnancy, gepants are not recommended, due to the lack of safety data. For breastfeeding, an open-label, phase 1 study evaluated the safety, plasma and milk pharmacokinetics, excretion in breast milk, and the relative infant dose of ubrogepant following a single oral dose, and found gepants may be used with caution, and strategies such as withholding breastfeeding for 8–12 h after administration are advised [75].

Short-term prophylactic treatment

Given the predictable timing and increased severity of perimenstrual migraine attacks, several studies have investigated short-term perimenstrual prophylaxis. This strategy is particularly relevant for women with highly regular menstrual cycles or those using oral contraceptives, where the period of greatest susceptibility can be anticipated. It may also serve as an adjunct to standard prophylaxis in women with MM who do not benefit from current treatments or cannot use hormonal therapies. However, its effectiveness may be limited in women with irregular cycles. Additionally, this approach carries a potential risk of medication overuse headache (MOH) or migraine chronification, highlighting the importance of considering long-term preventive therapy in appropriate patients.

Clinical trials have evaluated the efficacy of naproxen [127], frovatriptan [[128], [129], [130]], naratriptan [115,131], and zolmitriptan [106] administered for 5–14 days around the onset of menstruation. Overall, the results are favorable, with the strongest evidence supporting frovatriptan (2.5 mg twice daily), followed by zolmitriptan (2.5 mg three times daily) and naratriptan (2.5 mg twice daily) [81].

Due to their lower MOH risk, gepants may offer an alternative to NSAIDs and triptans for short-term prophylaxis. Preclinical studies have shown that continuous exposure to gepants does not induce orofacial sensitization [132], and long-term, open-label data indicate that rimegepant use is not associated with MOH [133]. A recent case series evaluated short-term prophylaxis with rimegepant and found that it prevented perimenstrual migraine attacks in most patients. However, sustained reduction in monthly migraine days was observed in only one patient, while three patients experienced delayed migraine onset 3–7 days after menstruation, indicating a “temporal displacement” rather than complete prevention [134]. Currently, a Phase 3, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study is ongoing to evaluate the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of ubrogepant for the short-term preventive treatment of MM (NCT06417775).

Hormonal treatment

When short-term prophylaxis is ineffective, hormonal treatment with a continuous regimen of combined hormonal contraceptives or progestin-only contraceptives can be considered in MO [75]. Nonetheless, evidence supporting the use of hormonal treatments in MM is limited, as most available studies are small, open-label, non-comparative, and methodologically heterogeneous. Overall, COCs show variable effects on migraine frequency: approximately 25% of patients report worsening, 25% improvement, and 50% no change. These responses appear to be independent of the specific formulation or estrogen dose [135].

The hormone-free interval in standard COC regimens is a well-recognized trigger of estrogen-withdrawal headache, affecting up to 70% of users [136]. As a result, strategies that shorten or eliminate this interval have been evaluated. Extended-cycle or continuous COC regimens reduce estrogen fluctuations and have been associated with decreased headache burden compared with traditional 21/7 schedules [137,138]. Similarly, a 24/4 regimen has demonstrated greater reductions in attack intensity and duration than the 21/7 regimen in women with PMM [139].

Progestin-only pills, which suppress ovulation and maintain relatively stable estradiol concentrations, may offer a theoretically favorable option for MM. This option is particularly preferred in women with MA. A meta-analysis demonstrated that desogestrel 75 μg/day significantly, though modestly, reduced the frequency of migraine headache attacks, headache intensity and duration, and the use of analgesics and triptans, while also improving headache-related quality of life [140].

In perimenopause, hormonal fluctuations often aggravate migraine, therefore, hormone replacement therapy (HRT) may provide benefit. However, research on the impact of HRT is limited, and the findings are not consistent. In postmenopausal women, data from the Women’s Health Study, which included 1909 women who had experienced migraine in the previous year, showed that those currently using HRT were more likely to report migraine than those who had never used it. This pattern was similar for both estrogen-only and combined HRT, and no clear differences were observed based on how the therapy was given (oral vs. transdermal), the estrogen dose, the type of progestogen, or whether it was taken continuously or cyclically [141]. A separate cross-sectional study of over 6000 postmenopausal women also found that current HRT use was associated with a higher likelihood of headache attacks, regardless of whether the treatment was local or systemic [142]. On the other hand, a smaller clinical study of 120 women attending a headache clinic, most participants (64.1%) reported that their migraine headache attacks improved or completely resolved with HRT, while a smaller proportion reported no change or worsening symptoms [143]. Despite these mixed findings, HRT is still often recommended for perimenopausal women with migraine. This is likely because migraine worsening during this period often occurs alongside vasomotor symptoms, so in practice, treatment is usually aimed at managing both conditions together.

Safety considerations are central when selecting hormonal therapies. Estrogen-containing contraceptives increase the risk of ischemic stroke, particularly in women with MA [13,14]. Accordingly, consensus guidelines from the European Headache Federation and the European Society of Contraception recommend avoiding combined hormonal contraception in MA and favor non-hormonal or progestin-only options. The same strategy is preferred in women with MO who have additional cardiovascular risk factors, such as smoking, arterial hypertension or previous history of a vascular event [144]. These recommendations align with the WHO Medical Eligibility Criteria, which emphasize the elevated stroke risk associated with combined estrogen–progestin methods in women with MA [144].

An additional limitation of current estrogen-based therapies is their broad activity across multiple estrogen receptor subtypes, which may contribute to both variable therapeutic effects and off-target adverse events [145,146]. Overall, while hormonal approaches may benefit a subset of individuals, their use is constrained by limited evidence, heterogeneous responses, and the potential for significant adverse effects, particularly with estrogen-containing therapies. For these reasons, hormonal strategies are typically reserved for women with predictable attacks who have not benefited adequately from standard acute or preventive treatments. Future research should focus on delineating the specific estrogen receptor pathways involved in migraine pathophysiology and identifying receptor-selective targets that could reduce migraine burden while minimizing systemic side effects. Such an approach may enable the development of more precise and safer hormonal interventions.

Prophylactic, non-migraine specific treatment

Before the advent of migraine-targeted therapies, non–migraine-specific agents served as the primary preventive options and continue to play an important role in clinical practice. Beta-blockers are among the most commonly prescribed drugs for migraine prophylaxis, with propranolol being the most widely available worldwide [147,148]. While propranolol is generally considered effective, safe, and well-tolerated, its blood pressure-lowering effects and non-selective properties can lead to a range of adverse events. Its use may also be limited in patients over 60 years of age or those with low baseline blood pressure [148,149]. Sex differences in propranolol pharmacokinetics have been reported, with women exhibiting higher plasma concentrations and lower clearance rates than men [[150], [151], [152]]. Interestingly, a recent retrospective study found that propranolol had greater efficacy in men with migraine compared to women, despite the higher drug exposure observed in the latter [153]. Across reproductive milestones, propranolol is considered relatively safe during the last two trimesters of pregnancy, and during lactation; however, it should be tapered 2–3 days prior to delivery due to an increased risk of neonatal respiratory depression, bradycardia, and hypoglycemia [72,87,123,154].

OnabotulinumtoxinA has not been studied with respect to sex- or gender-specific pharmacokinetics. In MM, a recent retrospective study evaluated its efficacy during perimenstrual and non-perimenstrual headache (migraine) days, finding a significant reduction in non-perimenstrual (migraine) headache days after 3 months of treatment, but no significant improvement in perimenstrual attacks [155]. Due to its high molecular weight and local administration, onabotulinumtoxinA is not expected to enter systemic circulation, making it a potential option for pregnant and lactating patients [156]. Supporting this, a 29-year retrospective review of onabotulinumtoxinA injections found no significant increase in fetal malformations compared with the general population [156], and additional studies have similarly reported no adverse effects on pregnancy outcomes [157].

Antidepressants, including amitriptyline, venlafaxine, paroxetine, and escitalopram play an important role in migraine management, particularly in women with comorbid depression, anxiety, or perimenopausal symptoms. Their use requires consideration of sex-specific pharmacokinetic differences, as women often exhibit higher plasma concentrations that can influence both dosing and tolerability [158]. Amitriptyline is often preferred in women of childbearing age due to its short half-life and relatively favorable safety profile in the first two trimesters of pregnancy [154]. Doses below 50 mg are generally considered safer during pregnancy, although a retrospective study reported an increased risk of preeclampsia in women with depression [159]. When used during pregnancy, it should be tapered 3–4 weeks before delivery to minimize the risk of neonatal withdrawal symptoms, hyperexcitability, and feeding difficulties. During lactation, amitriptyline is considered compatible, though infants should be monitored for anticholinergic symptoms [87,123]. Venlafaxine is especially useful in perimenopausal women, as it alleviates vasomotor symptoms with effects comparable to estrogen therapy [[160], [161], [162]]. Paroxetine and escitalopram have more limited evidence for migraine prevention but effectively reduce hot flashes and may be considered when perimenopausal symptoms coexist [[162], [163], [164]].

Prophylactic agents such as topiramate, valproate, and candesartan should generally be avoided in women of childbearing potential due to the fetal risk during the first trimester [165,166]. Topiramate is widely used for migraine prevention and, in MM, has been shown to reduce the frequency, but not severity or duration, of perimenstrual migraine headache attacks [167]. However, it is contraindicated during pregnancy due to its association with major congenital malformations, fetal loss, growth restriction, and neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorder and intellectual disability [165,166]. Given these risks and the high rate of unintended pregnancies, topiramate should not be prescribed to women who may become pregnant. During lactation, topiramate can reach up to 25% of maternal plasma concentrations. While generally well tolerated, infants should be monitored for sedation, irritability, diarrhea, or poor weight gain, and if possible, its use should not be encouraged because insufficient data are available for reassurance of its safety [154]. Valproate remains one of the most teratogenic agents used for migraine prevention, carrying dose-dependent risks of congenital malformations, developmental delay, and cognitive impairment. It is therefore contraindicated during pregnancy and should not be used in women of childbearing potential. Although potentially considered compatible with breastfeeding, its use in reproductive-age women is discouraged due to the lack of sufficient safety data. In the case of candesartan, intrauterine exposure is associated with an increased risk of adverse fetal outcomes, including miscarriage, oligohydramnios, renal failure, and death; therefore, its use is strongly discouraged during pregnancy [168]. While the use of candesartan is not a reason to discontinue breastfeeding, caution is advised in newborn and preterm infants [169].

Prophylactic, migraine-specific treatment

CGRP plays an important role in cardiovascular homeostasis; therefore, it is essential to consider the potential concerns associated with long-term blockade of this pathway. Female migraine patients have a higher risk of cardiovascular events, that increases sharply after menopause [170]. Additionally, women often present with vasospasm of small intramyocardial vessels, where the cardioprotective effects of CGRP are particularly relevant [171,172]. Therefore, women receiving CGRP-targeting therapies may face a higher risk than men during cardiac ischemic events, particularly in the postmenopausal period [173,174], highlighting that sex-specific considerations should extend beyond therapeutic efficacy to include careful consideration of potential cardiovascular risks and reproductive status.

CGRP (receptor)-monoclonal antibodies

To date, no studies have evaluated sex differences in their pharmacokinetic profiles. With respect to efficacy, subgroup and post hoc analyses from phase 3 trials have not identified significant sex-related differences [124,175,176]. Evidence regarding the effectiveness of mAbs for the treatment of MM remains limited; however, secondary analyses and real-world studies suggest that erenumab, fremanezumab, and galcanezumab are effective [155,[177], [178], [179], [180], [181], [182]]. However, migraine attacks often continue to follow a menstrual-related pattern, with episodes occurring more frequently during the perimenstrual period.

During pregnancy, the safety of CGRP (receptor) mAbs remains uncertain. Because CGRP is essential for maternal hemodynamic adaptation and fetal growth, inhibition of this pathway could theoretically increase the risk of hypertension and fetal growth restriction [183]. This concern is particularly relevant given that women with migraine already have an elevated risk of preeclampsia [73,184]. Recent case reports have not identified clinically significant pregnancy-related safety issues in women who conceived or breastfed while receiving erenumab, fremanezumab, or galcanezumab [185,186] and studies in non-human primates have shown no adverse effects of erenumab on pregnancy outcomes or postnatal growth [187]. Nevertheless, because clinical safety in humans has not been established, CGRP mAbs should not be continued during pregnancy. Given their approximate half-life of 1 month [188], discontinuation is recommended 5–6 months prior to conception.

The safety of CGRP mAbs during breastfeeding has not yet been formally evaluated. Clinically significant infant exposure is considered unlikely, as mAbs are poorly absorbed via the infant gastrointestinal tract [189]. However, given the role of CGRP in the gastrointestinal system, theoretical concerns remain regarding potential effects on the developing infant gut microbiome. Currently, the International Headache Society Practice Recommendations advise that these agents may be used with caution after at least two weeks postpartum [190].

Gepants

Post hoc and subgroup analyses from U.S. Food and Drug Administration reviews indicate that gepants reduce monthly migraine days in both episodic and chronic migraine, irrespective of sex [151]. Their non-vasoconstrictive profile makes them a suitable alternative to triptans, particularly in patients with MA or increased vascular risk [191]. However, as previously discussed, the potential cardiovascular implications of long-term CGRP blockade should be carefully considered [170].

Data on the preventive use of gepants in the prophylactic treatment of migraine across different reproductive milestones remain limited. However, their relatively short half-lives make them a potentially favorable option for women planning pregnancy who require migraine-specific preventive treatment, in contrast to longer-acting CGRP mAbs which must be discontinued several months before conception [19]. Current recommendations advise discontinuing gepants at least three days prior to conception [192].

With respect to breastfeeding, two open-label phase 1 studies have evaluated the plasma and milk pharmacokinetics of atogepant and rimegepant, including their excretion in breast milk and the resulting relative infant dose. In both cases, following a single dose, the relative infant dose was <1% [193,194]. As with acute treatment, gepants may be used with caution during lactation, and strategies such as withholding breastfeeding for 8–12 h after administration have been suggested [190].

Future Perspectives on Sex-Specific Migraine Management

Despite the higher prevalence of migraine in women, the role of sex hormones in migraine pathophysiology and their influence on treatment efficacy remain poorly understood. Although women comprise the majority of migraine patients, data from clinical trials are rarely analyzed by sex. Conversely, men are often underrepresented, resulting in subgroup analyses that lack sufficient power to assess efficacy reliably [124,125]. Moreover, transgender and gender-diverse individuals are rarely reported in study populations, limiting our understanding of how treatment responses may differ from those of cisgender populations. Furthermore, sex and gender differences extend beyond pathophysiological mechanisms, affecting the pharmacokinetics of antimigraine drugs, with variations in drug clearance and bioavailability observed for widely used treatments [76,100,101,152]. Collectively, these limitations hinder the ability to identify sex-dependent differences in symptomatology, therapeutic response, and side-effect profiles, as has been observed with triptans [98], thereby representing a major obstacle to advancing sex- and gender-informed care.

In addition to considering sex and gender, it is essential to account for the hormonal status of patients to better understand migraine pathophysiology and optimize therapeutic strategies. Currently, most studies categorize women into broad age groups without accounting for menstrual status, contraceptive use, or menopausal state. A more detailed assessment of reproductive status is therefore crucial to guide personalized treatment. Similarly, in the clinical setting, integrating hormonal status into treatment decisions is key to advancing personalized migraine care. This includes developing sex- and gender-specific efficacy guidelines based on robust pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic data, expanding evidence-based recommendations for management, including the treatment of MM, and fostering multidisciplinary models of care. Furthermore, patient education and shared decision-making can enhance individualized treatment, particularly for women of childbearing age, by providing guidance on safe medication use during conception, pregnancy, and lactation [71].

Conclusion

Taken together, these considerations underscore that advancing migraine care requires a comprehensive, sex- and gender-informed approach that integrates hormonal status, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic differences, and individual patient needs. Bridging the current knowledge gaps will necessitate well designed clinical trials that include adequate representation of women, men, and gender-diverse populations, with careful attention to reproductive and hormonal factors. By combining rigorous research, personalized treatment strategies, and patient-centered education, clinicians can improve therapeutic outcomes and ensure more equitable, effective migraine management across all sex and gender groups.
