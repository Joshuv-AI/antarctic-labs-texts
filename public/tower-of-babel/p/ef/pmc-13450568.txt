Current status of and factors influencing advance care planning participation among adolescents and young adults with cancer: a mixed-methods systematic review
Authors: Shen Kaibo, He Sheng, Huang Baolin, Ju Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvance care planning (ACP) is increasingly recognized as important for adolescents and young adults (AYAs) with cancer, yet evidence on their ACP participation remains fragmented and largely interpreted through adult-oriented models. This mixed-methods systematic review aimed to synthesize the current status of ACP participation among AYAs with cancer and the factors influencing this process.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Library were searched from inception to January 2026. Qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies involving AYAs with cancer were included. Study quality was appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool, and findings were synthesized using a convergent integrated approach.ResultsFifteen studies involving 825 participants were included. ACP participation among AYAs with cancer was characterized by family-consensus-oriented decision-making, psychologically ambivalent and evolving experiences, and a gap between family preparedness and clinical implementation. Structured ACP tools facilitated family discussions and preference documentation, but communication with healthcare professionals remained limited and intervention effects attenuated over time. Participation was promoted by perceived benefits, illness-related triggers, family support, trusting clinician relationships, age-appropriate communication, and organizational support, whereas death anxiety, emotional avoidance, low self-efficacy, low perceived susceptibility, communication barriers, limited professional competence, cultural taboos, and inequities impeded engagement.ConclusionACP participation among AYAs with cancer is a dynamic, relational, and developmentally embedded process rather than a one-time autonomous decision. Future practice should adopt age-appropriate, family-sensitive, and clinically integrated approaches that support repeated ACP conversations across the illness trajectory.Trial registrationThis review was registered in PROSPERO on April 21, 2025 under registration number CRD420251037008.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02175-0.

Introduction

Adolescents and young adults (AYAs) with cancer are generally defined in the oncology literature as patients between the ages of 15 and 39 [1]. This population is at a critical developmental stage characterized by the transition from adolescence to adulthood and faces multiple developmental tasks, including completing education, starting a career, and establishing interpersonal relationships. When cancer is diagnosed and treatment begins during this period, the normal developmental trajectory is often seriously disrupted [2]. These patients must confront not only the disease itself, but also fear of death and uncertainty about the future [3].

Advance care planning (ACP) refers to a process in which patients, family members, and healthcare professionals discuss preferences for future medical care. Its primary goal is to ensure that, when patients lose the capacity to make autonomous decisions, the care they receive remains consistent with their personal wishes [4]. For AYAs with cancer, ACP can help them maintain a sense of control over their lives and preserve autonomy in the context of illness. Studies have shown that AYA patients generally wish to be informed about their condition and to participate in medical decision-making; they also hope to retain the right to choose or refuse treatment and to make plans for personal matters, including how they will be remembered after death [5].

Unlike children, whose decisions are primarily made by their parents, and older adults, who have generally established stable values and accumulated life experience, the AYA population is in a critical period of identity formation [6]. These developmental characteristics give rise to several core features of ACP in the AYA population. First, AYAs tend to seek a level of participation in medical decision-making that is commensurate with their maturity and desire to be regarded as individuals whose perspectives are taken seriously, at the same time, they remain dependent on family support in reality and must negotiate a balance between their own wishes and the feelings of their family members [7]. Second, their values and treatment preferences may change over the course of the disease trajectory and across life stages, requiring ongoing adjustment rather than a one-time determination [8]. Third, for adult AYAs, illness often interrupts the process of establishing an independent social identity and may even result in regression of social roles because of the disease [9]. This means that ACP concerns not only end-of-life choices, but also how to preserve their developing sense of self-worth and social connectedness in the course of medical decision-making. In addition, the age range of 15–39 years spans the legal transition from minor to adult status, making the implementation of ACP more complex. Communication barriers are also more diverse, including concerns about family members' reactions [10], the desire to control information [11], and avoidance on the part of healthcare professionals [12]. These particularities indicate that ACP for AYAs cannot simply adopt an adult-based model.

In recent years, studies on ACP participation among AYA patients with cancer have begun to increase. Existing systematic reviews have identified several demographic and clinical factors associated with ACP participation, such as age, disease stage, and receipt of palliative care. However, most have focused on general adult populations or broader groups of patients with cancer, with limited synthesis and mechanistic explanation specific to the AYA population [13, 14]. At the same time, qualitative studies have further revealed the multidimensional experiences of AYAs in the ACP process, including concerns about family members' emotional reactions and burden, avoidance of death-related topics, and the need to balance information disclosure with autonomous expression [3, 15]. However, very few studies have addressed how multiple factors interact to jointly shape the process of ACP participation among AYAs.

To more effectively promote ACP participation among AYA patients with cancer, it is necessary to adopt an analytical framework that can simultaneously accommodate multilevel influences at the individual, relational, system, and social-environmental levels. The social ecological model (SEM) provides a theoretical approach for understanding such health-related behaviors [16]. It posits that behavior is jointly shaped by factors at the individual level, the interpersonal level, the organizational system level, the community and sociocultural level, and the policy and legal level.

By integrating and interpreting both qualitative and quantitative evidence, this study aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the current status of ACP participation among AYA patients with cancer and to identify influencing factors at the individual, interpersonal, organizational system, sociocultural, and policy levels. Furthermore, drawing on the social ecological model, it seeks to offer an integrated explanation of the cross-level mechanisms shaping ACP participation. This multilevel and integrative perspective may provide more targeted guidance for clinical practice, helping healthcare professionals design more flexible and age-appropriate approaches to ACP communication that respect the developmental characteristics of AYAs and support them in maintaining a sense of control over their lives throughout the course of illness.

Methods

Design

This review was conducted in accordance with the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis Handbook using a convergent integrated approach. The reporting of the review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17]. This review was registered in PROSPERO on April 21, 2025 under registration number CRD420251037008.

Search strategy

Five English databases, including Cochrane Library, PubMed, Web of Science, Cinahl, and Embase, were systematically searched from inception to January, 2026. Search for relevant studies on the experiences and factors influencing the implementation of pre-established medical care for adolescents and young adults with cancer. The search was conducted using a combination of free and subject terms. Detailed search strategies and results for each database can be found in the [Additional file 1]. In addition, the reference lists of included studies were manually screened for additional relevant literature.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Study type: Qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods empirical studies that explicitly reported the experiences of adolescents and young adults with cancer in relation to advance care planning (ACP). (2) Population: Adolescents and young adults (AYA) diagnosed with cancer. In this review, AYA was primarily defined as individuals aged 15–39 years. However, studies including participants younger than 15 years (e.g., 10–14 years) were also considered eligible when two conditions were met: first, the study explicitly described the target population as AYA or used related terms such as teenagers or youth; second, the study aims, intervention design, or discussion clearly addressed developmental issues relevant to the AYA stage and their implications for ACP. This decision was made because age-specific subgroup data were often unavailable, and the review aimed to retain studies whose main focus was conceptually aligned with AYA cancer populations.

Exclusion criteria: (1) duplication of published literature; (2) studies for which full text or valid data were not available; (3) poorly designed studies; and (4) literature such as literature reviews, conference abstracts, and case reports.

Study selection

After duplicate records were removed using EndNote 20, two reviewers (A and B) independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. A third reviewer (C) validated the search strategy and confirmed the final inclusion or exclusion of studies. Any discrepancies arising during the study selection process were resolved through discussion among all three reviewers.

Data extraction

Two researchers used a standardised form modified from the JBI Data Extraction Form to extract data relevant to the characteristics of the included studies, with the main extractions including: (1) Basic information about the included studies (first author, year of publication, country/region, sample size, type of study, purpose of study); (2) Tools and communication styles for intervening in the ACP of adolescents and young adults with cancer. Data were cross-checked in pairs after extraction, and any disagreement could be resolved by two-person discussion or arbitration by a third researcher to minimise bias due to subjective judgement.

Quality assessment

Mixed methods appraisal tool (MMAT) Specifically designed to assess the quality of mixed methods studies [6].Details of the assessment results for each study are provided in the [Additional file 1]. According to the JBI methodology, the decision to include a study can be based on meeting a pre-determined proportion of all criteria. We selected studies that used a 60% cut-off value to avoid over-exclusion and to ensure the quality of included studies.

Data analysis and synthesis

Referring to the convergent integrated approach recommended by the Australian Centre for Evidence-Based Health Care Research (Joanna Briggs Institute, JBI), Researchers qualify quantitative data into themes, categories, or descriptions, which are then aggregated and integrated with qualitative data to form descriptive interpretations of the text. Analysing ‘narrative interpretation' and qualitative data using Thomas and Harden's three-step thematic analysis approach [7]. Aggregate grouped results into specific topics to provide a summary of the evidence for each specific topic.The process of aggregation is described in the (Additional file 1).

Thematic synthesis

A total of 15 studies were included, from which 25 findings were extracted. These were synthesized into 8 themes and further integrated into 2 overarching findings.

Current status of ACP implementation

Family-consensus-oriented decision-making

The integrative analysis indicates that ACP decision-making among AYAs is essentially a form of autonomy exercised within the context of family relationships; its core lies not in expressing individual wishes in isolation, but in seeking consensus with the family.

First, the qualitative evidence reveals the profound implications of this decision-making pattern. AYA decision-making is rarely regarded as a "purely individual choice", rather, it is deeply embedded in a relational network with parents and other family members. When weighing treatment preferences, AYAs proactively take into account their family's emotional burden, practical caregiving pressures, and financial consequences, and may accordingly modify their initial wishes [18].This gives rise to a central tension: while AYAs hope to avoid suffering and maintain quality of life, they are also deeply concerned that their decisions may cause substantial psychological distress to their families. This concern directly leads to hesitation and ambivalence in expressing their preferences, and may even result in wishes that are conditional upon family circumstances [18].

Second, quantitative studies corroborate at the outcome-measurement level the central role of family consensus as a key product of ACP. Results from multiple structured interventions indicate that facilitating family preparedness and consensus is an important indicator of ACP effectiveness. For example, a study based on the FACE intervention found that, at 3 months, AYAs in the intervention group were significantly more likely to agree with the statements "I am prepared for the future" and "Our family has now come to a consensus" [19].Evidence is even stronger with respect to the objective indicator of decisional concordance: over a 12-month follow-up period, AYA-surrogate dyads who received a family-centered ACP intervention were more than three times as likely to enter a high-congruence trajectory as those in the control group (OR = 3.22) [20]. Another intervention study similarly confirmed that, across most prespecified scenarios, the concordance of preferences between AYAs and their family members was significantly higher in the intervention group. At the same time, AYAs in the intervention group were more likely to grant family members decision-making flexibility, allowing them to make context-sensitive judgments on the basis of the AYAs' known wishes [29].

Psychologically ambivalent and evolving experiences

The integrative analysis indicates that AYAs and their families experience ACP with psychological ambivalence, and that psychological outcomes, such as anxiety and depression, show inconsistency across studies and follow-up time points, demonstrating a marked pattern of dynamic change rather than a simple linear improvement.

First, qualitative evidence vividly captures the central tension in the ACP experience as being "distressing but worthwhile." Interview data reveal an inverse emotional structure. On the one hand, participants expressed gratitude for and affirmation of having tools that helped initiate important conversations. On the other hand, they also commonly experienced difficult emotions, including worry, sadness, and anxiety. Notably, many explicitly reported a sense of "relief" or "feeling unburdened" during the process, and ultimately described the overall experience as "distressing but worthwhile." [10].A 3-month follow-up study further confirmed that the emotional responses elicited by these conversations were mixed, encompassing fear, sadness, anxiety, and relief. Nevertheless, participants generally regarded the conversations as helpful, with their core value lying in promoting a shared understanding and alignment of perspectives among family members [26].

Second, quantitative findings on psychological outcomes further highlight the dynamic and uncertain nature of these effects. Results vary across studies and follow-up time points, and in some cases are even contradictory. For example, one study found that generalized anxiety and ACP-specific anxiety decreased significantly within 1 month after the intervention and remained lower thereafter [28]. Another trial likewise showed that adolescents' anxiety generally declined over 3 months, with the intervention group scoring higher on the dimension of peace of mind [30]. However, longer-term follow-up data revealed a different pattern: the FACE-TC trial found no differences between the intervention and control groups in anxiety or depression at 3 and 6 months, but by 12 months, the intervention group had a higher proportion of participants with moderate-to-severe anxiety and depression than the control group, and participants in the intervention group were also more likely to report severe fatigue at 3 months [19]. This finding suggests that psychological and symptom-related outcomes observed after ACP interventions may fluctuate over time. However, these differences should not be directly interpreted as indicating long-term emotional risks attributable to ACP itself; common physiological and psychological changes experienced by AYA cancer patients over a prolonged disease trajectory may also act as confounding factors. Although the trial used random assignment, during follow-up periods ranging from several months to one year, time-varying factors such as disease progression, ongoing treatment burden, symptom accumulation, and changes in prognostic awareness may still influence patients’ long-term emotional and symptom trajectories. Therefore, the existing evidence more appropriately supports the interpretation that psychological and symptom-related outcomes after ACP are temporally variable, rather than simply attributing them to long-term negative effects of ACP interventions.

In contrast, more consistent and positive quantitative evidence has been observed for supportive outcomes. For example, the use of a video-based decision aid significantly reduced caregivers' decisional conflict (from 60 to 28%), and family-centered interventions have also been shown to improve caregivers' positive appraisals of caregiving without significantly increasing their burden or distress [24, 25].

A gap between family preparedness and clinical implementation

Integrated analysis indicates that structured ACP tools have a clear effect in helping AYAs and their families initiate future care discussions, clarify care preferences, and formulate written wishes, but their role is mainly reflected in communication preparation at the family level. Although the ACP process is facilitated or supported by healthcare professionals, the preference expression and documentation outcomes promoted by these tools are more often discussed and confirmed between patients and families, and are less frequently translated into ongoing communication with medical teams, documentation of treatment goals in medical records, or actual use in clinical decision-making. Therefore, structured ACP tools can promote preparation within families, but a gap remains between “family preparation” and “clinical integration”.

First, the evidence consistently shows that structured tools can effectively initiate ACP conversations. Qualitative interviews further support this finding: many family members, friends, and even healthcare professionals recalled that their first ACP conversation with the AYA occurred only after the tool had been used [10]. Particularly importantly, among AYAs who had never discussed this topic with their families at baseline, more than half (51.5%) initiated a family conversation after the intervention, and the vast majority (88.6%) acknowledged that they would not have engaged in such sharing without participation in the study [28]. This highlights the central value of ACP tools in opening up these conversations.

However, analyses tracking the settings in which these conversations occurred show that ACP discussions remained highly confined to the family context. In real-world conversational settings, the proportion of AYAs who shared their preferences with family members (24/27) was far higher than the proportion who communicated with the medical team (10/27) [26]. Quantitative data further support this pattern: following the video-based decision aid (VMC) intervention, 65.1% of AYAs shared the content with family members, whereas only 8.9% shared it with healthcare professionals [10]. More notably, in the BOOST intervention, adolescents' intention to discuss ACP with pediatric oncologists actually declined significantly at both T1 and T2 [21], suggesting that structured tools did not promote patient-clinician ACP communication as expected and may even have been, to some extent, misaligned with or inhibitive of the existing rhythm of clinical communication.

At the level of wish documentation, structured tools likewise demonstrated strong effectiveness. In a 3-month follow-up pilot trial (FACE-TC), both the completion rate of Five Wishes and the rate of filing it in the medical record reached 100% among AYAs in the intervention group, compared with 0% in the control group [30]. In the subsequent large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trial with 18 months of follow-up, which was conducted on this basis, 80% of cases in the intervention group had an advance directive stored in the electronic medical record, compared with only 19% in the control group (adjusted OR≈19.20) [20].

However, the existence of such documents did not effectively translate into actual participation in clinical decision-making. Although preference documents were retained in the electronic medical records of the intervention group, the documentation rate of substantive discussions regarding goals of care remained very low (14% vs. 10%), with no significant difference between the intervention and control groups [20]. A study of a video-based tool similarly found that, although the tool facilitated ACP conversations, the proportion of newly added ACP documentation within 3 months was only 13% [26]. These findings suggest that family-level preparation and documentation of preferences did not simultaneously promote the integration of communication and record updating at the level of the medical team.

Temporal attenuation of ACP effects and the imperative for dynamic updating

The integrative analysis indicates that ACP among AYAs is not a one-time event, but rather a dynamic and iterative process. The effects of a single intervention may attenuate over time, and participants' preferences may also fluctuate with changing circumstances.

First, quantitative evidence clearly reveals the time-limited nature of single-intervention effects and the instability of decision preferences. With respect to the maintenance of effects, studies have shown that the benefits brought about by an intervention may diminish over time. For example, in the FACE-TC trajectory analysis, the intervention group retained a significant advantage in maintaining high decisional congruence at 12 months (OR = 3.22), but this advantage was no longer significant at 18 months (OR = 2.08) [20]. Another trial likewise found that, at 3 months, participants in the intervention group were more likely to agree that they were "prepared for the future" and that "the family had reached a consensus," but these differences disappeared by 12 months [19]. At the same time, decision preferences themselves may also change over time. One study found that, from the post-intervention assessment to the 3-month follow-up, as many as 40% of AYAs and 45% of caregivers changed their overall treatment preferences, with some even shifting from "life extension" to "uncertain" [26].

Second, qualitative evidence elucidates the value of multiple conversations in the ACP process. Longitudinal interviews showed that repeated ACP discussions can help AYAs and caregivers engage in gradual psychological preparation when facing uncertainty and the threat of death. This process allows preferences to evolve from abstract wishes into more refined and concrete expressions, which can eventually be documented in writing and shared with family members [15]. Regarding the question of the best timing for EOL discussions, 38% of adolescents preferred that such discussions occur throughout the entire disease trajectory, which is consistent with the longitudinal and repeatedly updated pattern of experience described above [30]. However, some individual dyads considered discussions about death during treatment to be inappropriate [15], highlighting the importance of respecting individual readiness and differences in timing within this dynamic process.

Individual level

Individual level facilitators

At the individual level, the mechanisms that promote AYAs' participation in ACP are mainly reflected in two pathways, perceived benefits regarding what ACP can offer [23, 28–30], and health status–related events that serve as triggers for ACP engagement [26]. Studies have shown that AYAs' motivation to engage in ACP is often grounded in the perception of multidimensional benefits, including a stronger sense of autonomy and being respected, relief from anxiety and uncertainty, improved family communication, and more efficient use of time and resources [22, 23]. This perceived sense of benefit is often concretized through structured interventions. For example, after the use of a formal tool (VMC), participants showed an immediate reduction in anxiety related to end-of-life planning, and this effect was sustained at the 1-month follow-up [28]. Additionally, in the FACE-TC randomized controlled trial, adolescents' anxiety scores decreased significantly from baseline to the 3-month follow-up in both groups. This finding supports the interpretation that ACP discussions do not necessarily increase psychological burden and may instead be accompanied by emotional improvement [30]. Information gain is also an important source of perceived benefit. Adolescents in the intervention group were significantly more likely than those in the control group to report feeling better informed about end-of-life decision-making, which enhanced their understanding of decisions and sense of control, thereby further strengthening their willingness to participate [26]. In addition to being driven by an active recognition of its benefits, AYA engagement in ACP is often triggered by changes in health status. In an analysis with a 3-month follow-up, one of the most common reasons why AYAs and caregivers initiated ACP conversations within that time window was disease-related considerations [26].

Individual level barriers

At the individual level, factors that hinder AYAs' participation in ACP are mainly concentrated in misconceptions and cognitive misunderstanding, emotional avoidance, low perceived susceptibility (psychological immunity), insufficient self-efficacy, and decisional concerns. First, there is inconsistency and confusion among AYAs, parents/caregivers, and healthcare professionals regarding the concept of ACP [23, 27]. Some individuals misconstrue ACP as caregiver assessment or as a plan limited to end-of-life care, whereas ACP is a broader, ongoing process of discussing and clarifying future care preferences. In addition, medicalized terminology and complex language further hinder understanding, which may in turn evoke fear or negative associations with ACP and reduce willingness to engage in it [27]. At the same time, AYAs have limited opportunities to encounter ACP-related information in their daily lives; even when they have "heard of" ACP, they may still overlook it, further weakening their interest in active participation [23]. Second, death anxiety and emotional avoidance constitute significant barriers [23, 26]. Talking about death can directly provoke anxiety, and in some cultural contexts, discussions of death are believed to invite "bad things," thereby prompting AYAs or their families to avoid such topics. In communication with family members or healthcare professionals, they may also prefer to remain optimistic and avoid these discussions because they are perceived as "too distressing" or "too upsetting." [23]. In addition, interviews and follow-up data also showed that some participants were unwilling to engage in ACP-related conversations because of emotional factors such as fear and anxiety [26]. Third, some AYAs, by virtue of their youth, exhibit a sense of "psychological immunity" and low perceived personal vulnerability [23, 26, 28]. Believing that they are "too young" for ACP discussions, they may have insufficient perceived susceptibility to disease-related threats and to the necessity of ACP [23]. Fourth, even when they recognize the value of ACP, some AYAs still exhibit insufficient self-efficacy. They may lack the motivation and confidence to participate proactively and remain uncertain as to whether ACP is truly needed in their own situation [23]. Finally, decisional concerns and uncertainty permeate the entire process of ACP engagement [18, 26]. AYAs often weigh the desire to avoid suffering and maintain an acceptable quality of life against concerns about placing a burden on their families [18]. In addition, because decision-making is highly context-dependent, they may experience their preferences as "depending on the situation" or characterized by ambiguity and uncertainty [26], which can make preferences difficult to stabilize or delay their expression.

Interpersonal level

Interpersonal level facilitators

At the relational level, two categories of facilitating factors were identified: family support and trusting relationships with physicians and nurses. Family involvement runs throughout the entire ACP process [10, 15, 23, 28–30]. At the stage of preference formation, family support is regarded as an important cue to action and plays a key triggering role in individual decision-making [23], whereas avoidance on the part of family members may prevent ACP from being initiated [28]. During the stage of communication and expression, family involvement can help create a more conducive atmosphere for ACP discussions. As one family member commented, "I think this is very, very good and this is a way to bring up difficult topics to the table" [30]. At the same time, family members reported feeling a sense of relief after participating and emphasized their desire to understand the AYA's true thoughts in order to support more open expression [10, 30]. As one parent stated, "This was a relief. I always want to know what my son thinks" [30]. In addition, at the stage of plan implementation, study findings suggest that family involvement helps translate the preferences of both parties into forms that can be documented and traced [15]. At the same time, trusting relationships with physicians and nurses are a key prerequisite for initiating ACP and sustaining ongoing negotiation. Establishing trust before ACP is introduced can reduce communication difficulties, misunderstandings about the process, and decisional conflict, thereby making patients and their families more willing to communicate with healthcare professionals [27]. In contrast, experiences of distrust may inhibit open communication. For example, one family member expressed concern that information shared with one nurse would quickly spread throughout the care team: "You say one thing to one nurse and it' s round the bloody building" [27].

Interpersonal level barriers

At the relational level, breakdowns in communication between healthcare professionals and patients represent a central barrier to ACP engagement among adolescents and young adults. Specifically, healthcare professionals often fail to initiate ACP discussions proactively [21, 28], Among participants who had not discussed ACP-related issues with healthcare professionals at baseline, open-ended responses explicitly identified the barrier as clinicians' lack of initiative and the absence of opportunities: "Expect health care team to initiate conversation when it is appropriate" [28], "Haven' t gotten any chances to talk about this" [28]. Second, healthcare professionals often continue to frame communication around being "positive" or focusing on the next step in treatment, which systematically delays ACP discussions [28]. Finally, relationship and power hierarchies may further hinder communication. Case studies showed that family members linked poor communication to these hierarchies and felt that healthcare professionals were unwilling to listen to their concerns [27]. Moreover, ACP communication among AYAs is inherently a triadic interaction among patients, families, and healthcare professionals. Its effective implementation requires healthcare professionals to possess triadic communication skills that allow them to attend simultaneously to patients’ individual wishes and family dynamics. However, current training systems for healthcare professionals rarely include specialized skills training for such contexts [27]. As a result, when conducting ACP discussions in the presence of family members, healthcare professionals may find it difficult to balance respect for patients' autonomous expression with responsiveness to family concerns, thereby further increasing the risk of communication breakdowns.

Organizational level

At the organizational level, three categories of facilitating factors were identified: the use of structured tools, appropriate communication approaches, and organizational support. Two categories of barriers were also identified: insufficient competence among healthcare professionals and limitations in organizational resources.

The provision and use of structured ACP tools can promote the engagement of AYAs in ACP, as these tools provide a medium for initiating conversations and sustaining participation. Qualitative evidence indicates that such tools can reduce the burden on caregivers of remembering and recounting information, while also serving as a vehicle and facilitator for subsequent discussions. At the same time, some family members, friends, and healthcare professionals believed that, without the context of the study, AYAs might not have raised their end-of-life preferences proactively [10]. Quantitative data further confirm the facilitating effect of these tools on ACP communication and sharing behaviors [20], In a study by Wiener, after completing the VMC, 65.1% of participants shared their preferences with family members, and 88.6% reported that they would not have engaged in such sharing had they not participated in the study [28]. In addition, the intervention group receiving the video-based decision support tool scored significantly higher than the control group in ACP knowledge and preparedness [25].

At the same time, appropriate communication approaches and organizational training for healthcare professionals provide institutional support for AYA participation in ACP. Hughes emphasized that discussions of ACP should be conducted in language that is open, honest, sensitive, and empathetic, while minimizing the use of technical medical terminology [27]. Meanwhile, greater opportunities for ACP-related training are needed for healthcare professionals, as lack of confidence in their own skills can inevitably hinder the initiation of ACP [27].One study also noted in its clinical implications that healthcare professionals should be provided with ACP communication training and tool-based support to facilitate serious illness communication with AYAs more effectively [15]. In addition, studies have highlighted that ACP is time-consuming and labor-intensive, that pediatric ACP remains less developed than adult ACP, and that problems in information transfer both within and across organizations may also impede its implementation [27]. These barriers all reflect insufficient organizational investment in resources and support for ACP.

Societal level

At the societal level, two key factors influencing ACP participation among AYA patients with cancer were identified, cultural adaptation, and health inequities and racial differences.

Sociocultural taboos, rituals, and customs surrounding death may reduce the acceptability of ACP-related issues among AYAs and their families, and may also make such communication more susceptible to misunderstanding. In a qualitative study conducted in the Chinese context, participants explicitly described the influence of traditional culture and social customs, particularly death-related rituals and practices, as a barrier to ACP engagement [23]. In another study involving longitudinal ACP interviews with young adults and caregivers, participants stated explicitly that, in their culture, "talking about death is taboo." [15]. At the same time, religious and cultural differences may also shape attitudes and preferences toward specific issues, such as body disposition, cremation, and organ donation, thereby affecting the cultural and religious appropriateness of certain items included in ACP tools [15].

Another important finding at the social level is that differences exist across racial groups in the ACP process, but these differences should not be interpreted as indicating a lack of cultural sensitivity in ACP tools. Lyon’s early FACE-TC trial showed that African American families were represented in the study, and the authors explicitly noted that underserved African American families were willing to participate. Ethnic differences in treatment preferences appeared in only one of the six hypothetical illness scenarios [29]. This suggests that FACE-TC, as a family-centered and structured ACP intervention, was acceptable and culturally sensitive among African American families. However, in subsequent larger-scale studies of FACE-TC, Thompkins found that White family caregivers reported lower levels of distress than family caregivers of color [24], while Needle found that White adolescents and their families were more likely than non-White/minority groups to maintain high concordance in treatment preferences [20]. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that FACE-TC lacks cultural sensitivity; rather, they suggest that even culturally acceptable interventions may still produce different preference patterns or outcomes across racial groups.

Discussion

This study found that ACP among AYA patients with cancer often followed a decision-making pattern oriented toward family consensus. This does not mean that family involvement is always beneficial. Rather, current evidence suggests a possible two-sided role of family support in ACP: it can help initiate ACP and support later negotiation [10], while concerns about family emotional burden may make some AYAs more hesitant to express their preferences [31].

Family members can be an important source of support for AYAs during ACP. They may provide emotional support, help organize information, and face difficult decisions together with the patient [32]. At the same time, in families shaped by collectivist values, implicit expectations such as maintaining harmony and avoiding conflict may make some AYAs think more carefully before stating what they really want [33]. This may appear in different forms. AYAs may worry that expressing their true preferences will upset family members or increase the caregiving burden. As a result, they may delay expression, express themselves selectively, or attach conditions to what they say [18]. This does not necessarily suggest that AYAs are being stopped from speaking for themselves. In many cases, it may instead reflect an effort to balance personal wishes with the feelings of family members within an ongoing relationship.

This tension between support and concern is also linked to a broader sociocultural context. The taboo surrounding death is not simply an abstract cultural rule outside the family. It is often reproduced in everyday communication through interactions across generations [34].When parents say things such as "don't say such discouraging things" or "let' s wait until you get better before talking about this," they may be reproducing these taboos without fully realizing it. Under these circumstances, AYAs may become especially sensitive to the emotional atmosphere within the family and more likely to see ACP topics as difficult to raise, which may further reduce their willingness to start such discussions on their own.

This pattern also reflects developmental features that are specific to the AYA population. Unlike older adults, whose role in ACP decision-making is usually clearer, AYAs are not typically the established authority figures within the family. Older adults are often family elders with a more mature sense of self and greater decisional authority [35]. By contrast, AYAs are in what Erikson described as the stage of identity versus role confusion. Their autonomy is still developing. They want to be taken seriously and included in decision-making, but they also continue to rely heavily on their families for emotional and practical support [36]. This places AYAs in a doubly marginal position in ACP. Within the family, they have not yet fully acquired the decisional authority usually given to adults, although they are expected to engage with issues that are treated as adult matters. Within the healthcare system, they may be expected to make rational decisions in the way adults do, yet they often have less decisional experience and less psychological preparedness than older adults. This situation differs from that of pediatric patients, whose decisions are largely made by parents, and also from that of older patients, whose role as decision-makers is usually more established. For AYAs, participating in ACP therefore involves not only deciding what they want, but also learning how to speak as a decision-maker within relationships.

Given these characteristics, ACP interventions and communication strategies for AYAs need to take privacy, developmental stage, and family negotiation into account. AYAs often have to balance dependence on family with self-expression. They want to be understood, but they may not want to upset their families, and they may also recognize that their own views can change over time. For this reason, it may not be enough to simply call for more expression or greater participation. What is needed is an approach that can hold these tensions. A two-stage model, with individual expression followed by family consensus-building, may be one way to do this. Individual conversations can give AYAs a space to clarify their thoughts without pressure from family members. Family discussions can then bring those thoughts into the open in a way that is framed as asking for support rather than asking for permission, which may help move families away from protective silence and toward supportive listening. These conversations can also be revisited at important points along the illness trajectory, so that ACP can be updated as the young person develops. In this way, AYAs may be better able to take part in ACP within the family while still preserving a sense of self and reducing distress for those close to them.

This review also found that attitudes toward ACP among AYAs with cancer are characterized by ambivalence and fluctuation. This ambivalence is not simply a matter of “wanting to engage but being afraid to do so”, rather, it arises from the simultaneous and alternating presence of two distinct internal experiences among patients. On the one hand, when health status deteriorates, symptoms worsen, or patients witness the death of peers, ACP is no longer perceived as an abstract concept, but as a concrete issue closely related to their own future care and family preparedness. Under these triggering circumstances, AYAs may become more aware of the potential value of ACP and develop greater motivation to participate [26]. On the other hand, in more routine contexts, AYAs are still often oriented toward the future, pursuing a normal life and maintaining hope, and death or end-of-life decision-making may be perceived as issues that remain distant from themselves. This form of “psychological immunity” or low perceived personal vulnerability may lead them to temporarily set ACP aside [23]. This does not necessarily indicate denial of the reality of illness, but may instead represent a self-protective strategy for maintaining continuity in daily life and psychological stability [37]. Previous qualitative studies have also shown that AYAs may respond to death-related topics with avoidance because they are “too upsetting,” selective optimism, or temporary unwillingness to discuss such issues [3, 38]. Therefore, the same AYA patient may both understand the importance of ACP and, at certain moments, strongly feel that “now is not the time to talk about it.”

However, this ambivalence reveals only the psychological preconditions for ACP engagement, rather than the full range of barriers. Even at illness-triggered moments, when motivation may temporarily prevail, willingness to participate does not always translate into actual action. The manifestations observed in the included studies, such as “lack of confidence,” “uncertainty about whether one needs to participate,” and “not knowing how to initiate the conversation” [28], suggest that a barrier at the level of self-efficacy may exist between motivation and action. That is, AYAs may be uncertain about whether they can effectively express their preferences and manage the emotional responses that such expression may provoke. This means that AYAs face not only a psychological tension over “whether they want to talk,” but also a capability-related concern over “whether they are able to speak up.” These two barriers may overlap, making the pathway from awareness, to willingness to participate, to actual engagement far more complex than it appears on the surface. At present, evidence directly measuring self-efficacy in the ACP context remains limited, and its mediating role or causal mechanism in the pathway of AYA ACP engagement has not been sufficiently examined. Future research should incorporate self-efficacy into measurement frameworks to clarify its specific role in AYA ACP engagement.

The psychological complexity and capability-related uncertainty described above pose a deeper requirement for clinical practice: healthcare professionals not only need to show sufficient patience toward AYAs and understand the mechanisms underlying their hesitation or refusal, but also need to continually examine whether they themselves hold similar tendencies toward avoidance. Existing research has shown that oncologists also face their own barriers in ACP and EOL discussions, including personal discomfort with death and dying, psychological resistance to communicating prognostic information, and uncertainty about their own communication skills [12, 39]. When such avoidance on the part of healthcare professionals meets AYAs’ psychological resistance, a risk emerges: supporting “non-participation” may represent a genuine respect for young people’s autonomy, but it may also unintentionally become a form of mutually reinforced avoidance. Stiefel defined this phenomenon as an unconscious dynamic between patients and clinicians, in which, when both parties jointly face unresolved psychological distress, avoidance becomes mutually reinforced in the form of “respect,” ultimately exerting a negative impact on quality of care [40]. Although these two situations may appear almost identical in outward behavior, they are fundamentally different in nature. Therefore, while respecting patients’ own pace, healthcare professionals need to engage in ongoing self-reflection and avoid transforming shared discomfort into the systematic postponement of ACP.

The integrated evidence suggests that structured ACP tools may help reduce barriers to participation among AYAs. By providing concrete topics for discussion, possible ways of expression, and textual formats for documentation, these tools enable families who might otherwise find it difficult to begin such conversations to discuss values and preferences earlier and in more specific terms, and to translate these discussions into written form. Most users reported that, without such tools, they would not have engaged in these discussions [28]. However, this effect appears to be almost entirely confined to the family setting: conversations occur mainly among family members and rarely extend to the medical team. Even when preference documents are included in the medical record, they are seldom translated into a basis for clinical decision-making [24]. One study found that, after the intervention, the proportion of adolescents who proactively shared their ACP plans with physicians actually declined, suggesting that adolescents may have developed the mistaken impression that, having already discussed these issues with their families, there was no further need to discuss them with clinicians [28]. This suggests that these tools helped stimulate ACP participation within families, but their effects did not extend well into the healthcare system. Patients' wishes were documented, yet they were not meaningfully incorporated into clinical decision-making.

This disconnect may reflect a gap between what healthcare systems currently provide and what AYA families actually need. One problem is that the use of ACP tools depends heavily on organizational support, including clear workflows, adequate time and staffing, and integration with institutional information systems. In many healthcare settings, however, ACP is still treated as an additional task outside routine care, so sufficient resources are often not allocated [41]. Another issue is that existing ACP tools are mainly designed to support communication within families, while the documents they generate are not well connected to clinical workflows. When families bring these documents to healthcare institutions, there are often no clear procedures for how healthcare professionals should receive them, review their contents, or use them in medical decision-making [42]. Researchers have noted that timely communication of minors' advance directives to relevant healthcare professionals, and the sharing of these preferences with the team in emergency situations, are important for ensuring that patients' wishes are taken up in practice [43]. Without such mechanisms, ACP documents completed by families often remain difficult to integrate into clinical care. A further difficulty is that healthcare professionals may lack both the skills and the confidence needed to discuss ACP, which can make them more likely to avoid these conversations. A substantial body of research has shown that clinicians' discomfort with end-of-life discussions is one of the main barriers to ACP in pediatric practice, and this problem is compounded by the lack of formal communication training and practical frameworks [44]. Many clinicians also worry that initiating conversations about death may undermine patients' hope, and therefore tend to avoid these sensitive topics [45]. When ACP tools are reduced in practice to forms that simply need to be completed, rather than being used as vehicles for ongoing dialogue, their function as communication facilitators is greatly weakened, resulting in less communication between patients and clinicians.

Taken together, the existing evidence supports framing the problem as insufficient mechanisms for clinical integration rather than ineffectiveness of the tools themselves. Even when families have discussed and documented patients' preferences, those wishes may still not be used in time at critical decision points without ongoing communication and information transfer. This may be one reason why family preparedness does not translate well into clinical practice.

This review also suggests that the effects of ACP interventions may weaken over time, which may be related to the changing and unstable nature of decisional preferences among AYAs. Studies have shown that patients' treatment preferences and values may shift not only with changes in health status, but also during periods of relative clinical stability [46]. As a result, an ACP plan made at a single point in time may not fully reflect the circumstances and psychological states patients later experience. When the illness trajectory changes, the original plan may no longer match the patient' s current wishes.

Passive changes in health status, such as disease deterioration, recurrence, or major treatment transitions, are natural points at which patients revisit and update their ACP preferences [47]. However, the emergence of such trigger points does not necessarily lead to timely revision of preferences, when continuous communication between patients and clinicians is lacking, these critical windows may be missed [39]. One mechanism underlying the attenuation of intervention effects may lie precisely here-not that patients lack the need to revise their preferences, but that opportunities for communication through which such needs can be identified are insufficient. When communication is disrupted, the dynamic evolution of preferences is not documented in a timely manner, and an outdated advance directive may reflect only the patient' s past wishes while potentially becoming misleading in actual clinical decision-making [48]. Therefore, establishing a dynamic ACP communication mechanism that allows preferences to be revisited and updated at key clinical milestones is of great importance for reducing the risk of time-related attenuation and ensuring continued alignment between documented wishes and clinical decision-making.

However, the absence of communication opportunities is only a surface-level explanation. For AYAs, a more fundamental barrier may also exist: their understanding of their own situation may be markedly misaligned with clinical reality from as early as the time of diagnosis [49]. This issue is distinct from the “psychological immunity” and selective optimism discussed above, through which AYAs may seek to maintain emotional distance from the threat of death. Inadequate prognostic understanding occurs earlier and concerns whether patients truly recognize the course of their illness, shifts in treatment goals, and the fact that death has become a realistic risk. In practice, AYAs often overestimate their chances of cure and fail to recognize the urgency of ACP at an early stage [49]. When disease progression disrupts AYAs’ original expectations, they may experience stronger avoidance and withdrawal due to the sudden realization of disease severity, including refusal to participate in prognostic discussions or end-of-life care conversations [39]. If healthcare professionals restart ACP solely on the basis of disease milestones without first assessing patients’ subjective understanding of their illness and psychological readiness, ACP may be difficult to carry out as intended. Only when ACP conversations are grounded in an accurate understanding of patients’ actual level of comprehension can preference updating truly reflect their current wishes.

Beyond the cognitive barriers described above, the developmental-stage characteristics of AYAs add another layer of explanation for changes in preferences. AYAs are in a period of identity formation, during which their values and life goals may shift with growth and illness experience. This developmental characteristic means that AYA values and preferences are not only subject to change, but that such change is itself part of their developmental process. One study has noted that the development of self-awareness and social cognition during adolescence can substantially influence the formation of medical preferences and goals [50]. Therefore, for AYA patients, if a single document is used to represent all of their future wishes, a gap may gradually emerge between that record and their actual preferences as they continue to grow and change. Given these characteristics, ACP for AYAs may need to focus less on helping patients make decisions in advance and more on supporting them as their thoughts and preferences change over time. ACP should not be regarded as a task of completing a document, but rather as a continuous process of dialogue that respects the reality of patients' ongoing development. One study has suggested that the focus of ACP should shift toward helping patients and their families make choices that are appropriate to the circumstances at the time they are needed [46]. More specifically, there is a need to establish a mechanism for revisiting ACP regularly or at important milestones. Through the development of long-term, trusting relationships with clinicians, patients may feel more able to express thoughts that change over time, while new considerations that gradually emerge during the course of illness can be recognized in a timely manner and naturally incorporated into subsequent ACP discussions.

Beyond the temporal challenges described above, the race/ethnicity-related findings observed in this review should also be understood within the context of systemic inequality and interactions with the healthcare system, rather than being simply attributed to ACP interventions themselves.

From outside the healthcare system, families of color may already be disadvantaged in several key factors that affect their capacity to participate in ACP before entering ACP interventions. At the economic level, financial disparities exist across racial groups among AYA cancer patients and their families. Data from a national financial navigation program in the United States showed that 85% of Black and 84% of Latinx cancer patients had annual household incomes below $50,000, compared with 65% of White patients [51]. A national screening study of AYA cancer patients further found that minority patients had significantly worse financial toxicity scores and were nearly three times more likely than White patients to report at least one unmet social need, including housing, transportation, or food insecurity [52]. At the same time, cancer diagnosis may further exacerbate pre-existing racial economic disparities. A national cohort study showed that cancer diagnosis significantly worsened the financial gap between Black and White patients in areas such as medication adherence and payment of medical bills [53]. At the cognitive level, health literacy is a key prerequisite for ACP engagement [54], and families of color are at a clear disadvantage in this regard. A prospective study of 459 caregivers of children with cancer found that Hispanic caregivers had significantly lower functional health literacy scores than non-Hispanic White caregivers [55]. Another study confirmed that limited health literacy was independently associated with an overall reduction in caregivers' knowledge of childhood cancer, and Hispanic caregivers were only 17% as likely as non-Hispanic caregivers to correctly understand cancer risk [56]. Disparities in prognostic understanding are also substantial. One study found that minority parents were only 19% as likely as White parents to recognize that their child had a low likelihood of cure, and only 7% as likely to recognize that current treatment was unlikely to be curative [57]. Notably, after adjustment for prognostic understanding, the association between race/ethnicity and high-intensity medical interventions was no longer significant, suggesting that disparities in prognostic understanding may be a key mediating factor contributing to the greater use of high-intensity end-of-life care among children of color.

From within the healthcare system, structural barriers and implicit bias are important factors that may further widen racial differences in ACP among AYA cancer patients. At the epidemiological level, a large retrospective study showed that non-White AYA cancer patients were significantly more likely than White patients to have no documented goals-of-care discussions before death, and that minority children and AYA patients were also significantly less likely to receive palliative care [8]. At the level of clinical communication, oncologists used fewer reflective statements with Black patients, paid less attention to their concerns, and displayed less warmth, revealing the direct influence of implicit bias on the quality of ACP conversations [58]. In addition, mistrust of the healthcare system among Black patients may hinder their participation in ACP [59]. A qualitative study further showed that the knowledge and experiences of critically ill Black patients were often devalued by healthcare professionals, creating barriers to equitable communication and care. Many Black patients were concerned that, if they chose less aggressive treatment options, treatment might be withheld from them because of their race [60].

Therefore, in the future implementation of ACP for AYAs, the focus should not be on simply attributing these differences to family preferences or to the intervention tools themselves. Rather, while maintaining the cultural sensitivity of interventions, it is necessary to establish a more equitable, accessible, and bias-free supportive implementation environment.

This review understood AYA participation in ACP within the framework of the social ecological model and found that factors at different levels may either reinforce or constrain one another. ACP participation among AYA patients with cancer is not determined by factors at any single level, but rather results from the interaction of influences at the individual, interpersonal, organizational, and sociocultural levels. Applying the social ecological model to analyze these influencing factors helps reveal how factors across different levels jointly shape AYAs' experiences of ACP and how these factors interact with one another. At the individual level, AYAs' willingness to engage in ACP depends on multiple psychological factors. Perceived benefits and health status-related triggers serve as sources of motivation for participation, whereas death anxiety and emotional avoidance, psychological immunity, and insufficient self-efficacy constitute barriers to engagement. These psychological states do not arise in isolation; rather, they are shaped not only by the illness itself, but also by the broader family and social environment [61]. At the interpersonal level, family support and patient–clinician relationships serve as key mediators linking the individual and the system. Family support would ideally function as a facilitating resource for ACP; however, under the influence of sociocultural taboos surrounding death, such support may also carry implicit expectations to maintain family harmony, thereby being transformed into decisional concerns at the individual level [62]. Similarly, trusting patient-clinician relationships can help identify moments when disease-related changes trigger the need for ACP; however, when the organizational level lacks communication training, institutional support, and adequate time allocation, patient-clinician communication is more likely to break down [39]. This breakdown is not merely an interruption of dialogue; it also means that signals indicating the evolution of patients' preferences cannot be received by the system. At the organizational level, the availability of structured tools, age-appropriate communication approaches, and organizational support constitutes the basic foundation for implementing ACP. However, there are clear gaps in current organizational provision: tool design tends to focus on family communication while lacking effective interfaces with clinical workflows; insufficient communication training leaves healthcare professionals lacking confidence when addressing ACP-related issues; and inadequate resources and services make it difficult for ACP to be translated into a continuous process. These organizational shortcomings may contribute directly to communication problems at the interpersonal level. They may also shape patients' perceptions at the individual level. When patients see that their documented wishes are not reflected in clinical care, they may begin to feel that expressing those wishes is pointless, which could make them less willing to engage in ACP in the future [20]. At the sociocultural level, death-related taboos and racial differences form part of the broader context in which ACP participation takes place. Taboos around death may operate not only within families, but also within the healthcare system, leading both family members and healthcare professionals to avoid ACP-related discussions [63]. The presence of racial disparities further suggests that culturally acceptable ACP interventions still need to be embedded within a more equitable implementation environment. Future work should not focus only on whether the content of ACP tools is culturally adapted, but should also examine whether families from different backgrounds can receive equal support in terms of economic resources, health literacy support, language services, prognostic communication, patient–clinician trust, and the use of documented preferences in clinical records.

Limitations

This review has some limitations that should be noted. One issue is the substantial heterogeneity across the included studies. Differences were seen in how ACP was defined, as well as in measurement tools, outcome indicators, and follow-up periods. This variability makes it difficult to compare findings directly and may affect the overall interpretation of the evidence.

Another limitation concerns generalizability. Most of the available evidence comes from high-income countries, especially North America and Europe, and the study samples were largely White. Because of this, it remains unclear to what extent these findings apply to settings with different cultural backgrounds, healthcare resources, and sociopolitical conditions.

There are also limitations related to the mixed-methods review design itself. Bringing together quantitative and qualitative evidence can provide a broader and more explanatory understanding, but the strength of that synthesis still depends heavily on the quality of the original studies. In several of the included studies, descriptions of study design, data collection, and analytic procedures were brief or incomplete. This may have affected both the quality appraisal and the interpretation of the evidence during synthesis.

Another issue concerns the broad age range of the AYA population. Although this review focused on individuals aged 15–39 years, this span covers multiple developmental stages, from adolescence to early adulthood, which may differ in decision-making capacity and social context. Because of limited sample sizes and incomplete reporting in the original studies, age-stratified analyses were not possible. In addition, to ensure adequate coverage of the evidence, we used an inclusive strategy for studies with minor deviations in age range (e.g., including participants aged 10–14 years) when their stated aims explicitly focused on the AYA population. As a result, age-specific patterns may not have been fully captured, and the findings should be interpreted with this heterogeneity in mind.

Future research should further promote the standardization of ACP-related concepts and outcome measures, include more AYA populations from diverse cultural and resource settings, adopt longitudinal or prospective study designs, and improve the methodological reporting quality of original studies in order to enhance the interpretive strength and applicability of the evidence in this field.

Conclusions

By integrating quantitative and qualitative evidence, this review provides a comprehensive account of advance care planning (ACP) participation among adolescents and young adults (AYAs) with cancer. The findings suggest that ACP participation in this population is not a linear or one-time act of autonomous decision-making, but a dynamic and relational process shaped by developmental stage, family context, and changes across the illness trajectory.

Using the social ecological model, this review further shows that ACP participation among AYAs is influenced by interacting factors at the individual, interpersonal, organizational, and sociocultural levels. Individual readiness, family support, communication with clinicians, the availability of structured tools, and broader cultural norms jointly shape whether and how AYAs engage in ACP. These findings suggest that barriers to ACP participation cannot be adequately addressed through single-level interventions alone.

These findings have several implications for ACP practice. At the individual level, it is important to address the psychological ambivalence of AYAs by providing stepwise pathways for participation that are aligned with their level of readiness. At the interpersonal level, communication frameworks should be designed to accommodate family negotiation and to help families transform protective silence into supportive listening. At the organizational level, the gap between family preparedness and clinical integration needs to be bridged by establishing effective interfaces between ACP tools and clinical workflows. Across the temporal dimension, ACP practice should shift from an event-oriented approach to a process-oriented one, with reactivation mechanisms aligned with key points in the illness trajectory so that ACP can be continuously revisited and updated as patients grow and as their condition changes. Only by combining a multilevel, coordinated intervention approach with a process logic of ongoing revision can practice truly respond to the complexity and dynamic nature of AYA decision-making and fulfill the fundamental goal of care: supporting AYAs in becoming themselves under the shadow of illness.
