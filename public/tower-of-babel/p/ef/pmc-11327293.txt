Late subsequent Merkel cell carcinoma after 21 years of the first primary diagnosis: When to de‐escalate surveillance?
Authors: Luis J Borda, Courtny S Cushman, Robert J Pariser
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractKey Clinical MessageMerkel cell carcinoma (MCC) presents challenges in surveillance due to varied recurrence rates and uncertain follow‐up protocols, especially in late recurrent cases. These cases need personalized monitoring strategies beyond traditional timelines, such as clinical and molecular factors, in order to optimize patient outcomes.AbstractMerkel cell carcinoma (MCC) is a rare, aggressive skin cancer with neuroendocrine differentiation with a propensity for recurrence following initial treatment. Surveillance strategies for MCC patients lack specificity, and the duration of surveillance remains uncertain, posing challenges in identifying appropriate follow‐up intervals. Therefore, we present a 94‐year‐old woman, with history of stage IA MCC in her left nasal wall 21 years prior, that presented with a dome‐shaped eroded nodule on her left fifth finger. Biopsy showed characteristic MCC features with positive immunohistochemistry for CD56, synaptophysin, and CK20 (perinuclear dotting). The patient opted against further imaging or lymph node biopsy and underwent Mohs micrographic surgery. To date, there has not been any evidence of recurrence at previous sites or development of new primary lesions. This case underscores the need for ongoing surveillance despite long disease‐free intervals. It also stands out as the case demonstrating the longest latency/recurrence‐free interval following the initial diagnosis of MCC in the literature. While most recurrences occur within the first few years post‐diagnosis, our case highlights the exceptional nature of late recurrences and prompts reevaluation of surveillance protocols. Current guidelines recommend surveillance for up to 3 years post‐treatment, but factors, such as patient demographics and tumor characteristics, may warrant extended monitoring periods. Emerging biomarkers, such as Merkel cell polyomavirus status and circulating tumor DNA, show promise in predicting and monitoring recurrences, but their utility in late recurrence detection requires further investigation.Keywords: de‐escalation, late recurrence, Merkel, Merkel cell carcinoma, surveillance

INTRODUCTION

Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC) is a rare, aggressive skin cancer with neuroendocrine differentiation, with an overall incidence of 0.65 cases per 100,000 person‐years. 1 MCC is prone towards recurrence after its initial therapeutic intervention correlating with reduced 5‐year survival rates compared to melanoma (e.g., for localized disease, 75% vs. 94%, respectively). 2 The recurrence rates of MCC vary between 27%–77%, based upon the patients' distinct profiles and tumor attributes within each cohort. 2 Furthermore, there is a lack of appropriate surveillance strategies for patients based on their likelihood of developing a recurrence, 2 , 3 leading to low specificity. 4 Surveillance de‐escalation may also be challenging due to the uncertainty of how the likelihood of MCC recurrence diminishes over time following diagnosis. Therefore, we present a case of a subsequent MCC after 21 years of the first MCC diagnosis in order to emphasize that surveillance may be de‐escalated but never stopped given that MCC sometimes can have a protracted course.

CASE HISTORY

A 94‐year‐old woman, otherwise healthy, presented with a one‐month history of a nodule on the left fifth finger associated with burning sensation. Physical exam showed a 1‐cm dome‐shaped eroded red nodule on the dorsal aspect of the proximal left fifth finger (Figure 1). No lymphadenopathy was found in physical exam. She had a history of a stage IA MCC of the left nasal wall, 21 years prior. The nasal lesion was a small, asymptomatic skin‐colored papule, which was excisional‐biopsied by a 2.5‐mm punch. Physical exam at that time point was negative for lymphadenopathies.

Physical examination. Dome‐shaped eroded erythematous nodule on the dorsal aspect of the left fifth finger.

METHODS

Excisional punch biopsy was compatible with MCC (first MCC, 21 years prior, Figure 2). A wide local excision with forehead flap repair was performed; no evidence of remaining MCC was evidenced in the excisional specimen. Punch biopsy of the current lesion showed expansion of the dermis with sheets and nests of small, basophilic cells with nuclear molding and minimal cytoplasm (Figure 3A). Apoptotic cells and mitoses were present. Immunohistochemistry was positive for CD56, synaptophysin, and perinuclear dotting with CK20 (Figure 3B–D).

Small, dermal neoplasm (A, H&E x10) composed of densely packed, small, dark cells with many mitotic figures (red arrows) (B, H&E ×20). Immunohistochemistry stain shows perinuclear, dot‐like expression of CK‐20 (C).

Histology of second Merkel cell carcinoma on the dorsal aspect of the left fifth finger. Dermis is filled and expanded by presence of sheets and nests of smaller, basophilic cells (A, H&E ×4) showing nuclear molding (orange arrow), mitosis (red arrows) and scattered apoptotic cells (green arrow) (B, H&E x40). The tumoral cells show positivity with synaptophysin (C) and typical perinuclear dot positivity with CK20 immunostaining (D).

CONCLUSION AND RESULTS

A diagnosis of MCC was rendered. She deferred following any blood laboratory tests, imaging, sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB), and/or adjuvant radiation. Patient was treated with Mohs micrographic surgery. Currently, the patient is undergoing thorough total skin checks every 4–6 months, ensuring diligent monitoring. To date, there has not been any evidence of recurrence at previous sites or development of new primary lesions.

DISCUSSION

We describe a 94‐year‐old woman with history of a stage IA MCC on the left nasal wall who developed a subsequent MCC on the left fifth finger 21 years after the initial diagnosis. While our case indisputably represents an exceptional deviation from the norm, 5 as it is distinguished by a late MCC recurrence (Per the National Cancer Institute, recurrent cancer is defined as a “cancer coming back to the same original site or to another place in the body” 6 ), it prompts inquiries into the length of surveillance necessary for MCC patients and the various factors influencing the likelihood of recurrence. 7 The scarcity of information concerning MCC recurrence risk primarily stems from the fact that national databases primarily gather data on survival outcomes rather than disease recurrence occurrences. Rusheen et al. reported a development of MCC recurrence 7 years after the primary diagnosis as a MCC with interval in transit and nodal metastases. 7 An analysis conducted by the Yale Cancer Center MCC Registry revealed a recurrence rate of 26% among patients diagnosed with stage I–III MCC, with 95% of recurrences occurring within the initial 2 years following MCC diagnosis and the latest first recurrence happening at 3.4 years. 7 Furthermore, McEvoy et al. reported a 5‐year recurrence rate of 40%, with 95% of recurrences arising within the first 3 years. 2

Ongoing research is actively investigating factors capable of pinpointing MCC patients with the greatest recurrence susceptibility. The majority of MCC patients who have undergone treatment and remained recurrence‐free for 3 years post‐Schdiagnosis is unlikely to experience a recurrence. 2 , 7 , 8 National guidelines advocate surveillance through imaging and physical examinations, yet the precise duration of follow‐up surveillance remains ambiguous. Current National Cancer Comprehensive Network (NCCN) guidelines suggest regular physical exams for up to 3 years post‐treatment, followed by less frequent examinations thereafter. Imaging is recommended on an “as clinically indicated” basis. 4 Moreover, after the 3–5 year mark, individuals who have previously experienced recurrence and those with immunosuppression continue to exhibit an increased susceptibility to recurrence, needing extended follow‐up periods. 8 Additionally, patients with nonstaging risk factors, such as male sex, advanced age, known primary tumor (for Stage III and IV), and medical comorbidities could have an increased likelihood of recurrence and should have a closer and longer surveillance period beyond 3 years. 2 , 7

The elevated frequencies of distant metastatic recurrence underscore the significance of employing surveillance imaging techniques and serology/molecular testing. This is imperative as such recurrences typically evade detection through clinical examination alone. 2 , 9 Therefore, other variables, such as Merkel cell polyomavirus (MCPyV) status among other molecular testing, may also necessitate prolonged surveillance, particularly in cases of MCPyV‐negative MCC. In addition, patients exhibiting increasing titers of MCpyV oncoprotein antibodies during surveillance may require more frequent monitoring or earlier imaging assessments. 10 , 11 Nonetheless, the utility of MCPyV measures in predicting late recurrences remains uncertain. Moreover, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) emerges as a promising biomarker for detecting minimal residual disease and early recurrence, as well as monitoring systemic therapy responses across various tumor types, including MCC. Several studies indicate the potential of ctDNA levels to detect early MCC recurrence and serve as a monitoring tool for long‐term patient care. 12 Nevertheless, in instances of MCC recurrence, the approach pivots towards personalized treatment strategies, exemplified in our patient's case, where the second occurrence of MCC was addressed solely through Mohs micrographic surgery, in accordance with the patient's preferences.

Additionally, as evidenced in our case, we are not able to distinguish between primary versus metastatic disease without genetic studies, such as array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH) or modalities for detecting MCPyV (e.g., PCR amplification of viral DNA and immunohistochemistry with antibodies specific for MCpV large and small T antigens). 13 , 14 , 15 Ahronowitz et al. utilized aCGH analysis on MCC lesions located on the right cheek and left ankle, occurring within a short timeframe of 4 months. Despite negative findings on SLNB and positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) scans upon presentation, the study revealed identical copy number variation profiles between the two lesions, suggesting that anatomically distinct cutaneous MCCs may signify metastatic dissemination rather than distinct primary neoplasms, even in the absence of lymphatic or involvement of distant organ. 14

Although a monitoring duration of at least 3–5 years for individuals who have undergone treatment for MCC is conventional, the duration and specific surveillance strategies for follow‐up of MCC patients remain uncertain. It is crucial for clinicians to factor in both the stage and nonstaging elements when deciding upon the optimal duration of surveillance for patients. Our case challenges the prevailing three‐year timeframe associated with the expected risk of MCC recurrence, as well as the surveillance recommendations spanning 3–5 years following the resection of loco‐regional disease. It also stands out as the case demonstrating the lengthiest latency/recurrence‐free interval following the initial diagnosis of MCC in the literature. Finally, it highlights that MCC can occasionally behave in a less aggressive manner and that patients diagnosed with MCC should be informed that the likelihood of late MCC recurrence is minimal, but not impossible.
