Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy in a Young Female With Wolff‐Parkinson‐White Syndrome: Anesthetic Implications and Case Report
Authors: Sunil Bhatta, Sukriti Pandit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case highlights that, with thorough preoperative assessment, continuous careful intraoperative monitoring, precise anesthetic selection, and multidisciplinary coordination, patients with incidentally detected Wolff–Parkinson–White (WPW) syndrome can safely undergo laparoscopic cholecystectomy under general anesthesia, demonstrating that meticulous planning effectively mitigates arrhythmic and hemodynamic risks during surgery.

Introduction

Wolff‐Parkinson‐White (WPW) syndrome is a pre‐excitation cardiac disorder characterized by the presence of an accessory atrioventricular (AV) conduction pathway, known as the bundle of Kent, which bypasses the normal AV nodal conduction system [1]. This aberrant conduction results in early ventricular depolarization, often leading to distinct electrocardiographic (ECG) features and a predisposition to supraventricular tachyarrhythmias [2]. WPW is rare, affecting 0.1% to 0.3% of people, but can lead to severe symptoms like tachycardia, syncope, or sudden cardiac death [3]. It is often diagnosed during evaluations for palpitations or arrhythmias and is sometimes found incidentally in preoperative assessments [4].

WPW syndrome manifests on ECG with a short PR interval (< 120 ms), widened QRS complex (> 120 ms), and a delta wave at the onset of the QRS complex [5]. These indicate the fusion of normal AV nodal conduction with pre‐excitation via the accessory pathway, which conducts impulses faster than the AV node [6]. This early activation can lead to re‐entrant tachyarrhythmias, primarily atrioventricular re‐entrant tachycardia (AVRT) [7].

WPW syndrome can sometimes remain latent or without symptoms until it is incidentally detected on an ECG during a routine medical examination or preoperative workup [8]. These incidental diagnoses require careful consideration during anesthesia, particularly for procedures like laparoscopic cholecystectomy, which involve pneumoperitoneum and patient positioning that may affect cardiovascular physiology [9].

The management of anesthesia in patients with WPW syndrome involves the understanding of electrophysiological abnormalities and carefully avoiding conditions that might cause tachyarrhythmias [10]. Factors such as surgical stress, sympathetic stimulation, electrolyte imbalances, hypoxia, hypercarbia, and certain anesthetic drugs can increase the risk of arrhythmia [11]. It is important to carefully select agents that minimally affect AV nodal or accessory pathway conduction to prevent triggering arrhythmias (AVRT, atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response and ventricular fibrillation) [12].

Furthermore, laparoscopic procedures introduce additional complexity due to the creation of pneumoperitoneum, which elevates intra‐abdominal pressure. This increase leads to heightened systemic vascular resistance, decreased venous return, and alterations in heart rate and blood pressure [13]. These hemodynamic changes can intensify arrhythmic risk [14]. Additionally, patient positioning, including the reverse Trendelenburg employed during laparoscopic cholecystectomy, can further affect venous return and cardiac output [15].

The anesthetic plan should prioritize hemodynamic stability, prevent sympathetic overactivity, and closely monitor arrhythmias at each surgical step. Anesthetic agents and medications that minimally affect AV nodal or accessory pathway conduction should be selected. Thorough intraoperative ECG monitoring must be ensured, and immediate intervention should be done if arrhythmias occur. Keeping antiarrhythmic drugs, defibrillation equipment, and ensuring that an expert is present for immediate arrhythmia management, are crucial for patient safety [16].

This case report describes the incidental diagnosis of WPW syndrome in a 24‐year‐old female patient scheduled for elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy. It outlines the recognition of this condition in the preoperative period, the interpretation of its distinctive ECG features, and the anesthetic considerations necessary to ensure patient safety during surgery. The report also discusses the need for a multidisciplinary approach involving anesthesiologists, cardiologists, and surgeons to tailor perioperative management in patients with WPW syndrome, aiming to minimize the risk of arrhythmogenic complications.

Management

Patient Profile and Clinical Background

A 24‐year‐old woman with symptomatic cholelithiasis was set for elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Although she had no prior cardiac history, she was diagnosed with WPW syndrome during pre‐op evaluation. She reported no palpitations, syncope, or arrhythmic episodes. Her cardiovascular exam showed normal heart sounds without murmurs, and her vital signs were stable: heart rate 78 bpm, blood pressure 120/80 mmHg, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. An ECG revealed a short PR interval, delta wave, wide QRS complex, and associated horizontal ST segment depression suggestive of repolarization abnormalities (Figures 1 and 2). Transthoracic echocardiography showed normal left ventricular function and no structural cardiac abnormalities. A cardiology consultation was conducted to assess arrhythmia risk and develop a perioperative management plan. Although tests such as Holter monitoring or electrophysiological studies were considered, they were deferred due to the lack of symptoms and documented arrhythmias.

Laboratory Investigations

The patient's preoperative blood tests showed results within normal limits. Hemoglobin was measured at 13.5 g/dL, total leukocyte counts at 7200/mm3, and platelet count at 250,000/mm3. Blood urea nitrogen was 14 mg/dL, and serum creatinine was 0.8 mg/dL. Electrolyte levels were normal, with sodium at 140 mEq/L, potassium at 4.2 mEq/L, and calcium at 9.2 mg/dL. Liver function tests were within normal ranges (total bilirubin 1.3 mg/dL, AST: 40 IU/L, ALT: 43 IU/L, ALP: 80 IU/L) and the coagulation profile indicated an INR of 1.0.

Anesthetic Goals and Preoperative Preparation

Anesthetic management aimed to avoid sympathetic stimulation, maintain stable hemodynamics and electrolyte balance, and prepare for rapid intervention in case of supraventricular tachycardia or atrial fibrillation. Premedication included oral midazolam (0.02 mg/kg) to reduce anxiety. A cardiology consultation was obtained preoperatively, and antiarrhythmic medications (procainamide, adenosine, esmolol, amiodarone, and lidocaine), along with an external defibrillator, were prepared and made readily available. Anesthetic drugs were selected to minimize the risk of arrhythmias: ketamine was avoided due to its sympathetic‐stimulating effects, which could precipitate tachyarrhythmias, and pancuronium was avoided because its vagolytic action might enhance conduction through the accessory pathway, facilitating re‐entrant arrhythmias. Halothane and diltiazem were also avoided to maintain hemodynamic stability and prevent arrhythmogenic risk. This careful selection of agents, along with vigilant monitoring, was central to ensuring safe anesthesia for a patient with incidentally detected WPW syndrome [9, 10, 11, 12].

Induction and Maintenance of Anesthesia

Anesthesia was induced with intravenous fentanyl (2 mcg/kg) and etomidate (0.2–0.3 mg/kg) for cardiovascular stability. Vecuronium (0.1 mg/kg) provided muscle relaxation. Atropine and glycopyrrolate were avoided unless necessary. Anesthesia was maintained with oxygen, air, and sevoflurane. Normocapnia was ensured to prevent arrhythmias. During anesthesia, the characteristic electrocardiographic features such as the delta wave and short PR interval, transiently normalized due to altered autonomic tone and effects of sevoflurane on conduction pathways (Figures 3 and 4).

Pneumoperitoneum Management

CO2 insufflation created pneumoperitoneum. The intra‐abdominal pressure stayed at 10–12 mmHg to reduce vagal stimulation and cardiovascular compromise, which was lower than the standard 12–15 mmHg. Gradual insufflation prevented sudden pressure increment and sympathetic stimulation. Hemodynamic monitoring was maintained throughout the procedure.

Extubation and Postoperative Care

Neuromuscular blockade was reversed with neostigmine and glycopyrrolate, and it was carefully administered to avoid tachycardia. Extubation done once the patient was fully awake and had intact airway reflexes. Intravenous fentanyl (0.25 mcg/kg) and paracetamol (15 mg/kg) provided pain relief during emergence. Continuous ECG monitoring in the recovery room for 24 h post‐surgery was done to detect any delayed arrhythmias. Hydration and normothermia were ensured throughout the perioperative period.

Follow‐Up

Given the patient's asymptomatic status, absence of documented arrhythmias, and normal cardiac structure and function, electrophysiological study or catheter ablation was not pursued. Postoperative cardiology follow‐up was arranged and continued for 4 weeks to monitor for any future arrhythmic events, in accordance with guideline recommendations for asymptomatic WPW syndrome. In the four‐week follow‐up period, there were no arrhythmic events, which supported the claim for a safe perioperative outcome.

Discussion

WPW syndrome is identified by the existence of an accessory atrioventricular conduction pathway, which can increase the risk of supraventricular tachyarrhythmias. While many individuals with WPW exhibit symptomatic arrhythmias, incidental diagnosis during preoperative evaluations, such as in a 24‐year‐old female patient, presents specific anesthetic considerations. This case underscores the importance of thorough perioperative planning and monitoring to manage potential arrhythmogenic issues during laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Preoperative assessment plays a pivotal role in determining the risk of perioperative arrhythmias. Our asymptomatic patient aligns with cases reported by Smith et al. [17] and Lee et al. [18], showing that elective surgery can be safely performed with careful perioperative management. Risk stratification, including evaluation of accessory pathway properties using non‐invasive testing when feasible, is essential, as high‐risk pathways can predispose even asymptomatic patients to sudden arrhythmic events. Anesthetic management should focus on avoiding sympathetic stimulation, maintaining hemodynamic stability, and selecting agents that do not enhance AV nodal conduction or increase catecholamine release. Our anesthetic approach follows the 2015 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Heart Rhythm Society (ACC/AHA/HRS) guideline, emphasizing avoidance of sympathetic stimulation, cautious selection of anesthetic agents (etomidate and sevoflurane), hemodynamic stability, and readiness for arrhythmia intervention. Specific measures for laparoscopic surgery, including low pneumoperitoneum pressure, gradual insufflation, continuous ECG monitoring, and multidisciplinary collaboration, have been highlighted to provide a structured comparison with existing literature [5, 17, 18].

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy carries risks due to pneumoperitoneum and CO2 insufflation, which can cause sympathetic stimulation and increase the likelihood of arrhythmias. Previous studies have recommended maintaining low intra‐abdominal pressures (10–12 mmHg) and gradual insufflation to reduce vagal and sympathetic responses. Additionally, the use of anesthetic agents such as etomidate and fentanyl for induction, along with careful administration of muscle relaxants, aligns with case study recommendations aimed at minimizing myocardial irritability and ensuring hemodynamic stability [19, 20]. Continuous ECG monitoring and readiness for rapid intervention, including adenosine, esmolol, and external defibrillation, are crucial in managing WPW patients perioperatively [21].

Postoperative care should focus on safe extubation to prevent sympathetic surges and continued ECG monitoring for at least 24 h, as arrhythmias may occur postoperatively. Extended monitoring may be warranted in selected high‐risk patients or those who experience intraoperative arrhythmias. Structured postoperative care, in conjunction with multidisciplinary collaboration, ensures timely recognition and management of arrhythmic events, supporting safe perioperative outcomes [22].

Our case supports that laparoscopic cholecystectomy is safe for patients with WPW syndrome when careful anesthetic and surgical techniques are used. The positive outcome highlights the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration among anesthesiology, cardiology, and surgery.

Conclusion

Incidental WPW syndrome diagnosed during preoperative evaluation necessitates a thorough anesthetic strategy to mitigate the risk of arrhythmias during laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Analysis of existing case reports underscores the importance of avoiding sympathetic stimulation, maintaining stable hemodynamics, and ensuring continuous cardiac monitoring for successful management. This case contributes to the expanding body of evidence indicating that with meticulous preparation and vigilant monitoring, patients with WPW syndrome, whether symptomatic or asymptomatic can safely undergo laparoscopic cholecystectomy without perioperative cardiac complications. Multidisciplinary collaboration remains crucial in optimizing outcomes for such complex cases.
