Incidence, risk factors and tumor spectrum of second primary cancers after gastric cancer: a multicenter cohort study with long-term follow-up

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe development of second primary cancers (SPCs) has become an important late effect in gastric cancer (GC) survivors, yet its incidence, risk factors, and clinical implications remain poorly defined. This study aimed to evaluate the long-term risk of SPCs, identify clinical factors associated with SPC occurrence, and describe detection patterns and stage distribution of SPCs during long-term follow-up.MethodsPatients with GC who underwent curative-intent radical gastrectomy between 2007 and 2021 were identified from the Multidisciplinary Alliance of Gastric Integrative Studies (MAGIS) cohort. Clinical factors associated with SPC occurrence were selected using LASSO-penalized Cox regression, and exploratory risk-based stratification was performed. Stage distribution was descriptively compared between SPCs detected during asymptomatic surveillance and those diagnosed after symptom onset.ResultsAmong 11,027 GC survivors (8,519 men and 2,508 women), 241 (2.19%) developed SPCs during a follow-up of up to 16 years. The cumulative incidence of SPCs was 1.25% (95% CI, 1.03%–1.46%) at 5 years, 2.97% (95% CI, 2.55%–3.39%) at 10 years, and 4.15% (95% CI, 3.40%–4.91%) at 15 years after gastrectomy. The most frequent SPCs were lung (23.2%), colorectal (21.6%), and esophageal (12.9%) cancers, showing marked sex-specific differences: lung cancer predominated in males, whereas cervical cancer was most common in females. Factors associated with SPC occurrence included older age at time of gastrectomy (HR 1.04, 95% CI 1.02–1.05; p < 0.001), chronic HBV/HCV infection (HR 2.15, 95% CI 1.29–3.58; p = 0.003), family history of cancer (HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.16–2.12; p = 0.003), and elevated preoperative systemic inflammation (Log-SIRI; HR 1.86, 95% CI 1.31–2.65; p < 0.001). The derived model stratified patients into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups with corresponding 10-year SPC risks of 2.07%, 3.61%, and 4.33%. SPCs detected during asymptomatic surveillance were more frequently diagnosed at stage I than those identified after symptom onset (47.4% vs 17.6%; p < 0.001).ConclusionsSPCs represent an important long-term health burden among GC survivors, with distinct sex-specific disease patterns. The exploratory risk-scoring model showed preliminary risk stratification, and SPCs detected during asymptomatic surveillance were more often diagnosed at an earlier stage. These findings support further evaluation of risk-adapted, organ- and sex-specific surveillance strategies in methodologically rigorous studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-05018-0.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Second primary cancer, Survivorship

Background

Survival has improved across nearly all cancer types, leading to a steadily increasing number of long-term cancer survivors [1]. As a result, late effects of cancer and its treatment have become an important clinical concern. Second primary cancer (SPC), also referred to as new primary cancer, is among the most serious long-term complications, affecting an estimated 2–37% of cancer survivors over their lifetime depending on age, first primary cancer type, and follow-up duration [1, 2]. Gastric cancer (GC) remains a major global health burden, ranking as the fifth most common cancer worldwide, with nearly two-thirds of new cases in 2022 occurring in Eastern and Southeastern Asia [3, 4]. As advances in early detection and treatment have improved survival [5, 6], the number of GC survivors continues to grow, making SPC an increasingly important survivorship issue [7]. Although previous studies have reported that approximately 5% of GC survivors develop SPC [8], these estimates may vary across populations with different genetic and environmental backgrounds. A clearer understanding of the burden of SPC after GC is therefore essential for identifying high-risk individuals and informing long-term surveillance strategies.

The development of SPC is likely influenced by multiple factors, including genetic susceptibility, prior anticancer treatment, and environmental and lifestyle exposures. Importantly, some risk factors associated with the first primary cancer, such as smoking and alcohol consumption, may also contribute to subsequent malignancies in the same or different organs [9]. However, the role of these shared and potentially modifiable risk factors in SPC development remains insufficiently understood, particularly among GC survivors.

To date, most studies on SPC after GC have focused primarily on incidence, whereas evidence on its determinants and clinical management remains limited. Older age and male sex have been suggested as potential risk factors [10, 11], but the available data are still sparse. Hematologic inflammatory and nutritional markers, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, hemoglobin, albumin, and fibrinogen, are widely used in prognostic assessment for GC [12–16], yet their associations with SPC risk have not been established. In addition, the role of structured follow-up in SPC detection after gastrectomy remains unclear, partly because previous studies have focused mainly on recurrence and gastric remnant cancer rather than evaluating SPCs as a distinct outcome [17, 18]. Since SPCs are relatively uncommon and tend to arise later during survivorship, their relationship with routine follow-up may have been underestimated. Therefore, further large-scale studies are needed to clarify the incidence patterns and risk factors of SPC after GC and to describe how SPCs are detected during long-term follow-up, including their stage distribution at diagnosis. Such evidence may help inform future risk stratification and risk-adapted surveillance strategies.

The Multidisciplinary Alliance of Gastric Integrative Studies (MAGIS) cohort is a comprehensive, multicenter data resource encompassing institutions across China [19, 20]. Using data from the MAGIS cohort, we included 11,027 patients diagnosed with GC who underwent curative-intent radical gastrectomy between January 26, 2007, and January 29, 2021, from five hospitals. In this study, we aimed to investigate the cumulative incidence and clinicopathological factors associated with SPC development among GC survivors and to describe the stage distribution of SPCs according to detection pathway during long-term follow-up.

Methods

The multidisciplinary alliance of Gastric integrative studies (MAGIS) database

The cohort has been described previously [19–21]. In brief, this study is based on the prospectively updated MAGIS database (Registration number: ChiCTR1900026130), which includes GC patients who underwent curative-intent radical gastrectomy between January 2007 and January 2021. The database covers 16 hospitals across China. To minimize the inherent bias of a retrospective design, we selected data exclusively from five high-volume referral hospitals that collected detailed clinicopathological information and conducted prospective follow-up for GC patients. Clinicopathologic data were collected through regular review of medical records and self-reported surveys, including blood test results, tumor and treatment information, demographic and anthropometric characteristics, medical history, lifestyle factors, and clinical events. After baseline assessment, patients were followed every 6 months during the first 3 postoperative years and annually thereafter through outpatient visits, telephone interviews, and medical record review. For the present analysis, follow-up focused on survival status, interval diagnoses of recurrence or new malignancy, and retrieval of relevant diagnostic records. SPCs were ascertained through the electronic medical record system and the Civil Registration System. The study’s cutoff date, marking the latest follow-up update in the MAGIS database, was February 17, 2025.

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: a diagnosis of gastric adenocarcinoma, age over 18 years, curative-intent radical gastrectomy, postoperative survival exceeding half a year, and complete treatment data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: postoperative survival of less than 6 months, did not undergo curative-intent radical gastrectomy, distant metastasis detected before or during surgery, missing treatment or follow-up data, and histology other than gastric adenocarcinoma. After excluding the cases that did not meet these criteria, a total of 11,027 patients were included (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). The study adhered to the STROBE reporting guidelines for observational research.

Primary outcome and risk factors

SPCs were defined as newly diagnosed malignant tumors arising outside the stomach and considered distinct from recurrence or metastasis of the index gastric cancer. SPC ascertainment was based on review of pathology reports, imaging findings, and clinical records, with histological confirmation used when available. Lesions considered compatible with gastric cancer recurrence, metastasis, or direct extension were excluded. The primary outcome was defined as the diagnosis of an extra-gastric SPC after gastric cancer surgery in patients with a postoperative survival duration of more than 6 months. SPC-free survival (SPCFS) was defined as the interval in years from the initial GC surgery to either SPC diagnosis, a competing event, or the end of follow-up. For SPC analyses, disease recurrence or death before SPC diagnosis was treated as the competing event. Postoperative overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from curative-intent gastrectomy to death from any cause, with survivors censored at the last follow-up. OS after SPC diagnosis was defined as the time from the date of SPC diagnosis to death from any cause, with survivors censored at the last follow-up. Survival outcomes according to SPC detection pathway were evaluated only in exploratory descriptive analyses among patients who developed SPCs and had available detection-pathway information.

Predictor selection and model development

SPC risk factors analyzed included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), family history of cancer, smoking status (current/ever vs. never), alcohol consumption (current/ever vs. never), past medical history (prior history of hypertension, diabetes, chronic hepatitis B or C [HBV/HCV] infection, appendectomy and cholecystectomy), primary tumor pathological information (pTNM stage), preoperative laboratory tests (carcinoembryonic antigen [CEA], and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 [CA19-9], hemoglobin, platelets, lymphocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, albumin and fibrinogen), and treatment (type of surgical resection [proximal, distal, or total], any chemotherapy [neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy]).

The NLR, PLR, lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), prognostic nutritional index (PNI), and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) were calculated from routine preoperative laboratory parameters and entered as candidate variables in the LASSO model. Specifically, SII was defined as platelet × neutrophil / lymphocyte, PNI as albumin (g/L) + 5 × lymphocyte (×10^9/L), and SIRI as monocyte × neutrophil / lymphocyte. These inflammatory and nutritional indices have been widely investigated in gastric cancer because they are readily available in clinical practice and have shown potential value in prognostic assessment [12–16]. Given their overlapping blood-cell components and the lack of prior evidence indicating which index would be most informative for SPC occurrence, these indices were included as exploratory candidate variables for variable selection rather than as prespecified definitive predictors.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed between March 1 and April 30, 2025. For categorical variables, group comparisons employed the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (for expected cell counts < 5). Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normal distributions. The cumulative incidence of extra-gastric SPC at 5, 10 and 15 years was estimated via the cumulative incidence function (CIF), accounting for competing risks. Differences in cumulative incidence were evaluated using Gray’s test under the competing-risk framework.

Missing data on our study variables ranged from 0% to 10.6%, including BMI 261 (2.4% missing), prior history of diseases 129 (1.2%), lifestyle history (smoking or drinking) 140 (1.3%), family history of cancer 559 (5.1%), CEA 797 (7.2%), CA19-9 1172 (10.6%), fibrinogen 1024 (9.3%), albumin 375 (3.4%) and blood routine tests 303 (2.7%). CEA, CA19-9, and fibrinogen were retained in the analysis because of their clinical relevance in gastric cancer research and prognosis. Missing predictor values were imputed using the missForest package in R (version 4.4.1), a nonparametric random forest–based method that can accommodate mixed-type clinical variables and nonlinear relations [22]. The completed dataset was used for subsequent analyses.

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed for each candidate factor in relation to SPCFS. Variables with a univariate p-value < 0.2 or deemed clinically relevant were retained for subsequent analysis. Because NLR, PLR, LMR, SII, PNI, and SIRI exhibited right-skewed distributions, log10 transformation was applied before their inclusion as candidate predictors. LASSO-penalized Cox regression was implemented using the glmnet package (v4.1-7) with 10-fold cross-validation to identify predictive variables from the log-transformed data. The 1-standard-error criterion (λ.1se) was used to optimize model parsimony while maintaining predictive accuracy, and only variables with non-zero coefficients at λ.1se were retained. The SPC predictive score was then derived as the weighted sum of the selected variables according to their regression coefficients. Among patients with SPCs and available detection-pathway information, survival outcomes were descriptively summarized according to detection pathway using Kaplan–Meier curves. Because detection pathway was not assigned by design, these analyses were regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and results with p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 11,027 cancer survivors (8,519 males and 2,508 females) were included in the study. The mean age at time of gastrectomy was 58.3 years and the median follow-up duration was 7.4 years (interquartile range [IQR], 5.2–10.3). Among the 11,027 patients, 3,231 (29.3%) had disease recurrence, and 3,930 (35.6%) died during a follow-up period of up to 16 years. The ratio between male patients and female patients was 3.4:1. Incidence of SPCs

Overall, 241 of 11,027 patients (2.19%) developed an SPC during follow-up. Because 33.6% of patients experienced disease recurrence or death within 5 years after gastrectomy (Fig. 1), follow-up observations were concentrated mainly within the first 5 years. As a result, fewer patients remained under long-term observation, while SPC events continued to accumulate during later follow-up, contributing to higher cumulative incidence estimates over time. Therefore, the cumulative incidence of SPC among all patients was 1.25% (95% CI, 1.03%–1.46%) at 5 years, 2.97% (95% CI, 2.55%–3.39%) at 10 years, and 4.15% (95% CI, 3.40%–4.91%) at 15 years following the initial gastrectomy (Fig. 1). The median time from primary gastrectomy to SPC development was 5.2 years (IQR, 2.0–8.4).

Cumulative incidence of recurrence or death and second primary cancer after gastric cancer. The cumulative incidence estimates at 5, 10, and 15 years are shown with 95% CIs in parentheses

The distribution of SPC types in the overall cohort and by sex is summarized in Table 2. In the overall cohort, the most common SPC type was lung cancer, accounting for 23.2% (56/241) of all SPCs, followed by colorectal 21.6% (52/241), esophageal 12.9% (31/241), prostate 5.4% (13/241), liver 3.7% (9/241), and cervical cancers 3.7% (9/241). Among male patients, lung, colorectal, and esophageal cancers predominated, whereas cervical, colorectal, and breast cancers were the most frequent SPCs among female patients. The 10-year cumulative incidence of SPCs was 3.09% in male patients and 2.63% in female patients, without a statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.270) (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). However, the disease spectrum of SPCs exhibited marked sex-based differences. Among male patients, the five most common SPC types were lung, colorectal, esophageal, prostate, and liver cancers, accounting for 74.7% of SPC cases in male patients. In contrast, the most frequent SPCs in female patients were cervical, colorectal, breast, lung, and thyroid cancers, accounting for 66.0% of SPC cases in female patients. For descriptive context only, Table 3 presents the most frequent SPC types in our cohort alongside the most frequent cancer types reported in the 2022 Chinese cancer registry. In this descriptive presentation, esophageal cancer accounted for a relatively larger proportion of SPCs among male gastric cancer survivors, whereas cervical cancer accounted for a relatively larger proportion of SPCs among female gastric cancer survivors.

Risk of SPC by patient characteristics

In exploratory univariable Cox analyses, older age at time of gastrectomy, hypertension history, chronic HBV/HCV infection, family history of cancer, advanced tumor stage, and elevated preoperative CA19-9 were associated with SPC occurrence. Several inflammatory and nutritional indices (NLR, SII, PNI, SIRI, and LMR) also showed associations. Diabetes, appendectomy, cholecystectomy, smoking, and drinking showed no significant association (Table 4).

We used LASSO-Cox regression as an exploratory variable-selection approach to identify clinical factors associated with SPC occurrence. The variable selection methodology is summarized in Fig. S3 (Additional file 1). Four variables were retained through regularization: age, HBV/HCV infection status, family history of cancer, and Log-SIRI. These variables were incorporated into an exploratory SPC score: SPC score = 0.017 × Age + 0.101 × HBV/HCV + 0.078 × Family history of cancer + 0.185 × Log-SIRI. In multivariate models, advanced age at time of gastrectomy (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 1.04; 95% CI, 1.02–1.05; p < 0.001), chronic HBV/HCV infection (HR, 2.15; 95% CI, 1.29–3.58; p = 0.003), family history of cancer (HR, 1.57; 95% CI, 1.16–2.12; p = 0.003), and elevated preoperative Log-SIRI (HR, 1.86; 95% CI, 1.31–2.65; p < 0.001) remained associated with SPC risk after adjustment (Table 4).

For exploratory descriptive stratification, internally derived cutoffs from X-tile software were used to classify patients into low- (< 1.02), intermediate- (1.02, 1.12), and high-risk (> 1.12) groups. Figure 2 shows distinct cumulative incidence patterns across these exploratory risk groups (Gray’s test, p < 0.001). Cumulative 5-year SPC risks were 0.90% (95% CI, 0.65%–1.15%), 1.23% (95% CI, 0.76%–1.71%), and 1.91% (95% CI, 1.41%–2.42%) in the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, respectively; the corresponding 10-year risks were 2.07% (95% CI, 1.60%–2.55%), 3.61% (95% CI, 2.53%–4.70%), and 4.33% (95% CI, 3.36%–5.29%). Compared with the low-risk group, the hazard ratios for SPC were 1.56 (95% CI, 1.11–2.18) in the intermediate-risk group and 2.12 (95% CI, 1.59–2.82) in the high-risk group. The model showed generally similar risk discrimination across stage I, II, and III subgroups, although these subgroup analyses should be interpreted as exploratory (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Given that the model was developed within the present cohort and showed modest separation in absolute risk, this risk stratification should be interpreted as exploratory.

Stage distribution of SPCs by detection pathway

Staging data for SPCs and reasons for clinical presentation at SPC diagnosis were available for 201 of the 241 patients. Among these 201 patients, 58 (28.9%) had stage I disease, 61 (30.3%) stage II, 39 (19.4%) stage III, and 43 (21.4%) stage IV. SPCs were categorized as surveillance-detected or symptom-detected according to presentation status. The proportion of early-stage (stage I) SPC patients was significantly higher in the surveillance-detected group than in the symptom-detected group (47.4% vs. 17.6%, p < 0.001) (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A and S5B). Exploratory survival analyses by detection pathway are summarized in Fig. S5C–F (Additional file 1) for descriptive reference.

Discussion

In this cohort study of 11,027 gastric cancer survivors, we examined the cumulative incidence, clinical correlates, and detection patterns of SPCs after gastrectomy. SPCs represented a clinically relevant long-term survivorship burden, with lung, colorectal, esophageal, prostate, liver, and cervical cancers being the most common types. Advanced age, chronic HBV/HCV infection, family history of cancer, and elevated preoperative Log-SIRI were factors associated with SPC occurrence. In the detection-pathway analysis, SPCs detected during asymptomatic surveillance were more frequently diagnosed at an earlier stage than those identified after symptom onset.

The results of this study reveal that the cumulative incidence of an SPC was 1.25% 5 years after gastrectomy, 2.97% 10 years after gastrectomy, and 4.15% 15 years after gastrectomy, after accounting for competing risks. Because recurrence and death were frequent competing events in this cohort, the cumulative incidence function should be interpreted as the observed probability of SPC diagnosis in the presence of these events, rather than as the latent biological risk of SPC among patients who would otherwise remain alive and recurrence-free. These competing events remove patients from the risk set for subsequent SPC detection and may therefore attenuate the apparent long-term cumulative incidence, particularly at later time points when fewer patients remain under observation. These estimates were broadly consistent with previous studies [10, 23]. For example, a cohort of North Portugal showed an estimated 10-year cumulative incidence of 5.2% for SPC of GC survivors [23]. In addition, a Chinese population-based study indicated that the standardized incidence ratios of SPCs for specific follow-up periods were 1.43, 1.41, and 1.21 at > 10 years, 5–10 years, and 1–5 years, respectively [10]. Taken together, these findings suggest that cumulative SPC incidence continues to increase with longer survival after gastrectomy. This pattern highlights the importance of sustained long-term follow-up in this population.

Notably, the SPC spectrum differed by sex: lung, colorectal, esophageal, prostate, and liver cancers predominated in males, while cervical, colorectal, breast, lung, and thyroid cancers were most frequent in females. In our cohort, esophageal cancer appeared relatively more prominent among male SPC cases, whereas cervical cancer was relatively more prominent among female SPC cases. For descriptive context, Table 3 displays the SPC spectrum observed in gastric cancer survivors alongside the latest population-level cancer incidence data from the Chinese cancer registry [24]. Because registry data specifically describing gastric cancer-related SPCs are not available, these data are presented only as contextual reference to illustrate broad similarities and differences in cancer spectrum. Taken together, these findings suggest that sex-specific SPC patterns may deserve consideration in long-term follow-up planning for gastric cancer survivors. In male survivors, follow-up attention may reasonably focus on the relatively frequent occurrence of lung, colorectal, and esophageal SPCs, whereas in female survivors, attention may be directed toward cervical, colorectal, breast, and thyroid SPCs. These observations may help inform more tailored survivorship surveillance strategies, although further validation is required before specific practice recommendations can be made.

This study demonstrates that GC survivors who are older at time of gastrectomy, have a history of HBV or HCV infection, a family history of cancer, or elevated preoperative systemic inflammation are at increased risk of developing an SPC. In the present analysis, HBV/HCV infection was associated with SPC occurrence; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because only 16 SPC events occurred among 375 infected patients. Of these 16 SPCs, 8 were primary liver cancers, and 8 of the 9 patients who developed liver SPCs had preoperative HBV infection. These descriptive findings indicate that hepatic malignancies may have contributed to the observed association. Although previous studies have reported associations between chronic HBV/HCV infection and the risk of several malignancies [25, 26], they did not specifically assess SPC risk in gastric cancer survivors. Therefore, they provide only limited biologic context for the present finding, which should not be overinterpreted given the limited number of events.

Notably, elevated systemic inflammation as reflected by Log-SIRI was associated with subsequent SPC occurrence, suggesting that inflammatory status at gastric cancer diagnosis may have relevance to later SPC development. This finding extends prior studies in which inflammatory indices were primarily linked to prognosis rather than SPC occurrence in gastric cancer [12, 27]. SIRI may serve as a more informative inflammatory–immune indicator because it integrates neutrophil- and monocyte-dominant pro-tumor inflammation with lymphocyte-related anti-tumor immune surveillance, thereby capturing a broader inflammatory–immune balance. Mechanistically, chronic inflammation is thought to promote tumorigenesis by driving genomic instability, facilitating immune evasion, and stimulating angiogenesis [28, 29]. Although blood-cell-based inflammatory indices share overlapping components and are often correlated, the observed association with Log-SIRI supports a potential link between systemic inflammatory status and SPC development. Further studies are warranted to validate this finding and to clarify whether inflammatory markers may contribute to future SPC risk assessment.

The exploratory risk-scoring model provided preliminary stratification of long-term SPC risk using routinely available clinical variables. However, its interpretation should remain cautious. Although cumulative incidence differed across the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, the absolute differences in risk were modest, and the model has not been externally validated. Accordingly, the model should be viewed as an initial risk-stratification framework rather than a definitive clinical prediction tool, and it is not yet suitable for individual-level clinical decision-making. Future studies should evaluate its discrimination and calibration in independent populations to determine whether the predicted absolute risks correspond to the observed SPC risks, with recalibration performed if necessary before its role in risk-adapted surveillance strategies is assessed.

In the detection-pathway analysis, SPCs detected during routine follow-up were more likely to be diagnosed at an earlier stage than those identified after symptom onset. This descriptive finding suggests that long-term follow-up may provide opportunities for earlier recognition of SPCs among gastric cancer survivors. Earlier-stage detection in the surveillance-detected group should not be interpreted as evidence that structured surveillance improves SPC-specific survival or overall survival, because this observational comparison remains vulnerable to lead-time bias, length bias, and confounding by indication. Lead-time bias may increase the apparent proportion of early-stage SPCs and prolong survival measured from SPC diagnosis by advancing the time of diagnosis. Length bias may enrich the surveillance-detected group with slower-growing or less aggressive SPCs, which are more likely to be asymptomatic and associated with better observed outcomes. Confounding by indication may also arise because patients who undergo regular follow-up may differ from symptom-detected patients in healthcare access, baseline health status, adherence to care, and subsequent treatment opportunities. Therefore, these analyses should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than evidence that structured surveillance improves survival. At present, the absence of randomized trial data and the substantial heterogeneity in follow-up practices preclude definitive conclusions regarding the survival benefit of intensive recurrence-centered surveillance [18, 30, 31]. Nevertheless, our findings support continued attention to SPC detection as an important component of long-term survivorship care after gastrectomy. In this context, future studies may consider whether risk-adapted surveillance strategies can be informed by the clinical risk factors and sex-specific SPC patterns observed in our cohort. In male survivors, attention may be directed toward lung, colorectal, esophageal, prostate, and hepatobiliary malignancies, whereas in female survivors, attention may focus on cervical, colorectal, breast, lung, and thyroid cancers. Such a sex-specific, organ-oriented approach may help inform more individualized survivorship care, although further validation in independent cohorts and methodologically rigorous studies is needed before specific surveillance recommendations can be broadly implemented.

This study has several limitations. First, despite prospective updating of the MAGIS cohort, this was an observational analysis and remains susceptible to selection bias and unmeasured confounding [32]. Second, fewer patients remained under observation at later follow-up time points, particularly beyond 10 years, reducing the precision of cumulative incidence estimates. Third, because several blood-cell-based inflammatory indices were highly correlated, the retention of Log-SIRI in the present multivariable model does not necessarily indicate a uniquely important biological effect. Rather, this finding should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and requires validation in independent cohorts. Fourth, although this study was based on a multicenter cohort, the exploratory risk model was developed within the present study population and has not been externally validated. In addition, because the cutoff values were derived internally within the study cohort, potential optimism bias and overfitting cannot be excluded. Its discrimination was modest and the absolute differences in SPC risk across groups were limited; therefore, external validation and recalibration are needed before broader use in risk-adapted surveillance research. Fifth, the study population consisted entirely of Chinese patients treated at high-volume referral centers, which may further limit the applicability of the findings to other ethnic groups, geographic regions, and healthcare systems. Sixth, although missing data were handled using imputation, residual uncertainty related to missingness may remain, particularly for variables with relatively higher missing rates, and the corresponding findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Finally, the comparison between surveillance-detected and symptom-detected SPCs was observational and remains vulnerable to lead-time bias, length bias, stage migration, and confounding by indication, and thus should be interpreted as an association rather than evidence of a causal survival benefit. These findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in methodologically rigorous studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this cohort study of more than 11,000 gastric cancer survivors found that the cumulative incidence of SPCs increased over time, reaching 4.15% at 15 years. Older age, chronic HBV/HCV infection, family history of cancer, and elevated preoperative systemic inflammation were associated with SPC occurrence in exploratory analyses. The exploratory risk-scoring model provided preliminary stratification of long-term SPC risk. SPCs detected during asymptomatic surveillance were more often diagnosed at an earlier stage than those identified after symptom onset. These observations may inform future studies of risk-adapted, organ- and sex-specific surveillance strategies, although external validation and methodologically rigorous evaluation are needed before broader clinical implementation.
