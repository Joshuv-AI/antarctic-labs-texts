The Serotonin 2B (5‐HT2B) Receptor: A Narrative Review of Preclinical and Clinical Evidence on the Safety Considerations and Therapeutic Potential for the Treatment of Depression
Authors: Gia Han Le, Sabrina Wong, Danica E. Johnson, Diana K. Orsini, Yang Jing Zheng, Kayla M. Teopiz, Christine E. Dri, Joshua D. Rosenblat, Roger S. McIntyre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Major depressive disorder (MDD) and treatment‐resistant depression (TRD) remain leading causes of disability, providing the impetus for receptor‐level treatment strategies beyond monoamine reuptake. The serotonin 5‐HT2B receptor (5‐HT2BR) is uniquely positioned at the interface of central‐antidepressant mechanisms and peripheral cardiac risks. Herein, we reviewed preclinical, translational and clinical literature identified via PubMed and OVID (MEDLINE, Embase, AMED, PsychINFO) databases from inception to September 2025, supplemented by manual searches. Evidence converges that peripheral 5‐HT2BR agonism is mechanistically linked to valvular heart disease (VHD) through mitogenic/profibrotic signaling in valve tissue, consistent with historical signals for anorectic agents (e.g., fenfluramine) and ergot‐derived dopamine agonists, with supportive but limited data for 3,4‐methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA). Classical serotonergic psychedelics bind 5‐HT2BRs; therefore, while contemporary dosing regimens are intermittent, cumulative exposure risks remain insufficiently characterized. Conversely, central 5‐HT2BR signaling appears bidirectional wherein astrocytic 5‐HT2BR activation under sustained serotonergic tone may support metabolic and plasticity programs, whereas neuronal 5‐T2BR antagonism can normalize mesolimbic dopamine output and enhance medial prefrontal glutamatergic activity. Clinically, several approved antidepressant adjuncts (e.g., aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, cariprazine) display 5‐HT2BR antagonism or inverse agonism and improve depressive symptoms without observed valvular signals in trials. The 5‐HT2BR represents a tractable augmentation target for TRD provided that peripheral agonism is rigorously avoided. Consistent with evolving scientific and regulatory safety frameworks, we propose advancing centrally selective, periphery‐sparing 5‐HT2BR antagonists into biomarker‐anchored early cardiac monitoring and preclinical exclusion of valve‐cell 5‐HT2BR agonism. This framework integrates central efficacy with defined safety boundaries, guiding development of mechanistically informed antidepressant therapies.

Psychiatric disorders, including major depressive disorder (MDD), remain among the leading causes of disability worldwide, with mental disorders accounting for approximately 15% of global years lived with disability (YLD) in 2019. 1 MDD independently accounted for over 37 million YLDs in 2019, and epidemiological evidence indicates the lifetime prevalence of MDD is approximately 11–15% globally. 2 , 3 Extant literature reports MDD is associated with decreased quality of life, increased suicidality, as well as substantial economic burden. 4 The pathoetiology of depression is heterogeneous with implicated disturbances in multiple effectors including, but not limited to, monoamines, glutamate, gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA), metabolic and inflammatory systems. 5 , 6

Conventional pharmacotherapy for mood disorders has historically been guided by the monoamine hypothesis, which posits that monoaminergic neurotransmitter imbalance [e.g., serotonin (5‐hydroxytryptamine, 5‐HT), dopamine (DA), and norepinephrine (NE)] underlies the pathophysiology of depression. 7 Notwithstanding research efforts, approximately 30% of persons with MDD do not achieve clinically meaningful response despite two or more adequate monoamine‐based antidepressant trials and are characterized as having treatment‐resistant depression (TRD). 4 , 8 Moreover, it has been reported that anhedonia, motivational and reward‐processing deficits, as well as cognitive impairment often persist in persons with MDD/TRD, despite improvements in mood and/or symptom remission. 9 , 10

The modest efficacy associated with first‐line monoaminergic antidepressants has shifted research attention to mechanism diversification, from indiscriminate increases in intrasynaptic monoamines to receptor‐level circuit tuning with a particular emphasis on molecular pathways integral to plasticity and trophic activity. 11 , 12 , 13 By modulating the cross‐talk between monoaminergic and other neurotransmitter signaling pathways (e.g., glutamatergic), it is posited that receptor‐focused strategies may restore adaptive network dynamics within the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and mesocorticolimbic circuits governing motivation, reward learning and executive control. 14 Accordingly, receptor systems and subtypes that modulate dopaminergic and glutamatergic transmission, whether directly or via serotonergic microcircuits (e.g., 5‐HT2A/2B/2C, 5‐HT1A/1B, interneuron‐gated pathways), represent potential mechanisms for enhanced clinical benefit and hypothesis‐driven antidepressant development. In keeping with this view, convergent evidence posits that both conventional and investigational treatments in depression can modulate circuit activity via integration of multiple receptor systems.

A candidate receptor in the central nervous system (CNS) that integrates disparate neurobiological and cognitive processes (e.g., reward and arousal) is the 5‐HT receptor. 15 Historically, translational work has emphasized the physiology, pathophysiology and pharmacologic opportunity of the 5‐HT2A receptor, particularly regarding cortical plasticity. 16 In recent years, converging evidence implicates the 5‐HT2B receptor (5‐HT2BR) as both a critical target of existing pharmacologic agents for mood disorders and a potential target for future investigational treatments. 17 (pp.5‐), 18 The 5‐HT2BR has been historically relatively better characterized for its peripheral roles, including cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, and vascular function. 19 , 20

Within the foregoing framework, there has been increasing interest in serotonergic psychedelics [e.g., psilocybin and lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD)] and entactogens [e.g., 3,4‐Methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA)], in combination with psychotherapy, as potential treatments for various psychiatric disorders (e.g., mood, anxiety, and substance use disorder). 21 , 22 For example, preliminary evidence indicates that administration of psilocybin with psychotherapy can produce rapid, robust and sustained reductions in depressive symptom severity in persons with MDD as well as TRD. 23 , 24 , 25 Preliminary evidence suggests MDMA‐assisted therapy can improve measures of mood and social functioning in persons with post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) with comorbid depressive symptoms. 26 The 5‐HT2A receptor role as an antidepressant has been instantiated by the observation that 5‐HT2A agonism is a point of pharmacodynamic similarity across select antidepressants as well as select atypical antipsychotics in MDD. In addition, the 5‐HT2A receptor is known to be relevant to the psychedelic effect of serotonergic psychedelics and potentially relevant to the antidepressant effects that have preliminarily been reported. 21

From a safety perspective, however, several serotonergic psychedelics and entactogens, including LSD, psilocin/psilocybin, N,N‐Dimethyltryptamine (DMT), and MDMA, display appreciable 5‐HT2BR interaction and, in several cases, partial agonist activity, raising safety concerns regarding peripheral valvular risk when receptor agonism is sustained or cumulative. 19 , 21 , 27 , 28 , 29 Importantly, the central therapeutic framework considered herein is not based on systemic 5‐HT2B agonism. Rather, emerging evidence suggests that centrally acting 5‐HT2B signaling may be cell‐type‐ and timescale‐dependent: astrocytic 5‐HT2B engagement under sustained serotonergic tone may contribute to slower metabolic and plasticity‐related adaptations relevant to antidepressant response, whereas neuronal/circuit‐level 5‐HT2B antagonism or inverse agonism may provide a distinct augmentation mechanism by normalizing mesolimbic dopaminergic output and enhancing medial prefrontal glutamatergic activity. 30 The foregoing provides a testable framework to pursue central‐antidepressant benefit while avoiding peripheral agonist‐related risk (Figure 1 ).

Herein, we first summarize the toxicology of peripheral 5‐HT2BR agonism, followed by a critical evaluation of the therapeutic implications of central 5‐HT2B signaling in depression‐relevant biology. Subsequently, we will review preclinical, pharmacologic, and translational findings relevant to central 5‐HT2BR antagonism as a mechanistically distinct and still unproven augmentation hypothesis for MDD/TRD. The present review distinguishes three separable questions: (1) whether peripheral 5‐HT2B agonism constitutes a toxicologic constraint, (2) whether central 5‐HT2B signaling participates in depression‐relevant neurobiology in a cell‐type0 and timescale‐dependent manner, and (3) whether central 5‐HT2B antagonism represents a plausible augmentation hypothesis for MDD/TRD. We emphasize that currently available human clinical evidence is indirect, because agents discussed herein are polypharmacologic and do not isolate 5‐HT2B antagonism as the sole driver of efficacy.

SEARCH STRATEGY

A comprehensive search was conducted on PubMed and OVID (i.e., MEDLINE, Embase, AMED, PsychINFO, JBI EBP Databases) from inception until September 30th, 2025. The foregoing databases were searched using combinations of terms related to 5‐HT2B receptors, agonism/antagonism/inverse agonism, valvulopathy/cardiovascular disease, and pharmacotherapeutic agents displaying 5‐HT2B agonism/antagonism (e.g., serotonergic psychedelics, anorectic agents, ergot‐derived dopamine agonists, entactogens). Additional articles were identified via manual Google Scholar searches and by examining the reference lists of relevant publications.

We prioritized the inclusion of peer‐reviewed preclinical, clinical, and translational studies directly addressing the 5‐HT2B receptor pharmacology, safety, and therapeutic potential. Case reports were included when they provided valuable insights into cardiac safety considerations associated with 5‐HT2BR‐dependent effects, as these observations complement findings from preclinical and clinical studies. Conference abstracts and articles not available in the English language were excluded unless unique insights were provided. Findings from meta‐analyses and review articles were included when relevant to provide further context and clarification. As the present paper is a narrative review, study selection was based on relevance and contribution to the aims of the paper rather than a systematic appraisal. Titles and abstracts were reviewed to identify articles relevant to the focus of the present narrative review. Studies not relevant to 5‐HT2BR pharmacology or therapeutic implications (e.g., nonserotonergic receptors) were excluded.

SEROTONIN 5‐HT2 RECEPTOR FAMILY: SIGNALING PATHWAY AND 5‐HT2B RECEPTOR LOCALIZATION

The serotonin receptor family consists of 14 receptor subtypes, organized into six families of G protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs) (i.e., 5‐HT1, 5‐HT2, 5‐HT4, 5‐HT5, 5‐HT6 and 5‐HT7 receptors) and one ligand‐gated cation‐selective channel (i.e., 5‐HT3 receptors). 31 The 5‐HT2 receptor subfamily consists of three receptor subtypes including 5‐HT2A, 5‐HT2B, and 5‐HT2C receptors. 28 The 5‐HT2 receptor subfamily is canonically Gq/11‐coupled, activating phospholipase C‐β (PLC‐β) which catalyzes the hydrolysis of phospholipid phosphatidylinositol 4,5‐bisphosphate (PIP2) into two second messengers including inositol triphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG). 28 The soluble IP3 binds to its receptors on the endoplasmic reticulum, triggering the release of calcium (Ca2+) from intracellular stores while DAG activates protein kinase C (PKC) isoforms. 32

Beyond heterotrimeric G‐protein signaling, 5‐HT2 receptors engage additional effector pathways including GPCR kinase (GRK)‐dependent β‐arrestin translocation which scaffolds the mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) and extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK) (MAPK/ERK) cascade. 33 , 34 , 35 Activation of the MAPK/ERK cascade leads to subsequent phosphorylation of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) response element‐binding protein (CREB), upregulating the transcription of brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) which influences neuronal excitability, functional connectivity as well as potentially contributing to cognitive and behavioral effects. 33 , 36

Within the 5‐HT2R family, 5‐HT2BRs are distinguished by their peripheral effects and safety profile. The 5‐HT2BR is widely expressed in peripheral tissues (e.g., cardiovascular system, gastrointestinal tract, liver, pancreas, immune system, and connective tissue) and is involved in a multitude of physiological and pathological processes, including cardiovascular function, gastrointestinal motility, and fibrosis. 28 , 31 , 35 , 37 , 38 In the CNS, overall 5‐HT2BR expression appears lower than that of 5‐HT2A and 5‐HT2C receptors; however, its central distribution may be relatively selective, with detectable expression in neuroglia and brain microvascular endothelial/perivascular cells. 28 , 31 , 39 Available pharmacologic evidence further indicates that 5‐HT2BR signaling can exert functionally relevant effects on cognition (e.g., learning and memory), motor activities, neuroglial regulation, and dopaminergic signaling. 31 , 40 Consequently, lower overall abundance should not be interpreted as absence of central functional relevance. Rather, the therapeutic hypothesis considered herein is that 5‐HT2BR modulation may influence specific depression‐relevant circuits in a cell‐type‐ and circuit‐dependent manner, as discussed in later sections.

PERIPHERAL 5‐HT2B RECEPTOR AGONISM AND CARDIAC SAFETY

As aforementioned, the 5‐HT2BR is expressed in cardiovascular tissues, including myocardial, endothelial, and vascular smooth muscle cells. 31 While central 5‐HT2BR agonism may exert therapeutic effects, the potential for safety concerns arises when 5‐HT2BRs are agonized in select peripheral tissue. Findings from extant literature suggest the 5‐HT2BR may be involved in multiple cardiovascular diseases, including cardiomyopathy, VHD, and pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). 31 For example, 5‐HT2B agonists (e.g., pergolide, fenfluramine, dexfenfluramine, and benfluorex withdrawal) have been predictably associated with cardiac valvulopathy. 28

Clinical evidence for 5‐HT2B‐associated valvulopathy

Historically, 5‐HT2B‐mediated valvulopathy was reported in persons experiencing excess serotonergic production as a consequence of carcinoid tumor. 41 , 42 It was additionally observed that 5‐HT2B stimulation by 5‐HT and/or 5‐HT memetics triggered molecular and cellular processes that affect valvular structure and integrity. 41

Converging evidence indicates that several pharmacologic agents are associated with cardiac adverse events (e.g., VHD), notably anti‐obesity therapeutics (i.e., fenfluramine, dexfenfluramine, and benfluorex), ergot‐derived Parkinson's disease treatments (e.g., pergolide and cabergoline), and serotonergic psychedelics/entactogens (e.g., 3,4‐methylenedioxymethamphetamine [MDMA] and lysergic acid diethylamide [LSD]). 31 , 43 Across these drug classes, epidemiologic, echocardiographic, and pharmacologic findings converge on the “off‐target” activation of 5‐HT2BRs as the most parsimonious mechanistic link to VHD. 31 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46

Anorectic agents

Racemic fenfluramine and its enantiomer, dexfenfluramine, were appetite suppressants used for the treatment of obesity and withdrawn from the United States (U.S.) market in 1997 due to drug‐induced VHD. 47 , 48 Both agents are biotransformed to norfenfluramine, exhibiting a high affinity for 5‐HT2BRs. 47 Early reports from 1997 to 2000 consistently demonstrated higher rates of valvular regurgitation and unusual morphologic changes among users of fenfluramine–phentermine (fen‐phen), dexfenfluramine alone, or dexfenfluramine and phentermine compared to matched controls (22.7% vs. 1.3% met the case definition for cardiac valve abnormalities, respectively). 49 , 50 Furthermore, a controlled follow‐up study on obese patients who had taken dexfenfluramine and fen‐phen continuously for ≥30 days also reported significantly increased prevalence rates and relative risk (RR) of aortic regurgitation (dexfenfluramine: 8.9%, RR = 2.18; fen‐phen: 13.7%, RR = 3.34; controls: 4.1%) (P < 0.001). 48

Although clinically distinct, benfluorex shares a related serotonergic profile with fenfluramine and dexfenfluramine and has also been associated with VHD. 45 In line with the foregoing findings, a large cohort study on over a million patients with diabetes mellitus reported significantly greater risk of hospitalization for any cardiac valvular insufficiency in the benfluorex group (adjusted RR = 3.1), with lower risk for patients with a lower cumulative dose compared to controls. 51 Moreover, findings from a separate case–control study indicate that 70% of patients with otherwise unexplained mitral regurgitation had prior benfluorex exposure (odds ratio [OR] = 40.4; 95% confidence interval [CI] [9.7, 168.3]). 45

Ergot‐derived dopamine agonists

Pergolide and cabergoline are primarily characterized as dopamine D2 receptor agonists; however, both agents exhibit high potency, full agonism at 5‐HT2BRs. 52 Findings from an echocardiographic prevalence study (N = 155 patients with Parkinson's disease; n = 64 pergolide, n = 49 cabergoline, and n = 42 nonergot‐derived dopamine agonists) indicate a significantly greater prevalence of moderate–severe valvular regurgitation in pergolide (23.4%) and cabergoline (28.6%) users vs. nonergot‐derived dopamine agonists (0%). 53 Furthermore, a dose‐dependent relationship was observed between the risk of regurgitation of any valve and cumulative exposure of dopaminergic agonists. 53 Consistent with the echocardiographic findings, real‐world population‐based incidence data indicate an association between current use of pergolide (incidence‐rate ratio, 7.1; 95% CI [2.3, 22.3]) and cabergoline (incidence‐rate ratio, 4.9; 95% CI [1.5, 15.6]) with newly diagnosed valvular regurgitation. 46 The same association was not observed with current use of nonergot‐derived dopamine agonists. 46 The foregoing findings prompted the withdrawal of pergolide from the U.S. market in 2007. 31

Classic serotonergic psychedelics

Classic serotonergic psychedelics such as psilocybin and LSD produce potent psychoactive effects, primarily due to their partial agonism of the 5‐HT2AR. 29 , 54 In addition to their high affinity for 5‐HT2ARs, several serotonergic psychedelics also interact with dopaminergic and adrenergic receptors, as well as other 5‐HT receptor subtypes, including 5‐HT2B. 21 , 54 , 55 Contemporary reviews of receptor‐binding and functional data indicate that serotonergic psychedelics and related compounds, including LSD, psilocin/psilocybin, DMT, and MDMA, show appreciable 5‐HT2BR interaction, whereas mescaline appears less clearly characterized at this receptor because of comparatively low potency. 28 , 29 For example, LSD has the greatest potency and binding affinity at 5‐HT2B (inhibition constant [Ki] = 0.97), followed by 5‐HT2A (Ki = 4) and 5‐HT2C (Ki = 15) receptors in humans. 55 , 56 More broadly, available evidence suggests that the aforementioned serotonergic psychedelics all warrant consideration in relation to 5‐HT2BR‐mediated valvular risk; however, the strongest functional and clinical safety signal remains for MDMA, whereas direct valvulopathy data for classic psychedelics remain limited. 21

Entactogens

Although sometimes grouped with serotonergic psychedelics due to its serotonergic actions and psychoactive phenomenology, MDMA is more accurately classified as an entactogen rather than a classical psychedelic. Mechanistically and pharmacologically, MDMA's principal action is monoamine release rather than 5‐HT2AR agonism; therefore, it does not robustly elicit the canonical psychedelic marker of the 5‐HT2A‐related head‐twitch response in rodents. 57

Differing from the available literature for serotonergic psychedelics, preclinical and clinical findings have demonstrated an association between MDMA, 5‐HT2B agonism and valvulopathy. Preclinical in vivo and in vitro evidence implicates the integral role of 5‐HT2BRs for several acute MDMA effects (e.g., serotonergic release and hyperlocomotion), providing a mechanistic link to 5‐HT2B‐dependent signaling that is relevant to cardiac safety. 58 In valve‐cell assays, MDMA and its major metabolite, 3,4‐methylenedioxyamphetamine (MDA), elicit prolonged mitogenic responses (i.e., proliferative effects) akin to fenfluramine and norfenfluramine, reinforcing a 5‐HT2B‐linked cardiac safety concerns. 59

Clinically, evidence for VHD in the context of MDMA is limited; however, it is nonzero. A case–control study reported that in 29 subjects currently using or having a prior history of using MDMA, 28% exhibited abnormal echocardiographic results in accordance with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) criteria for appetite suppressant‐induced VHD (e.g., fenfluramine), which was significantly greater compared to the control group (P = 0.002). 60 Furthermore, the prevalence of tricuspid, mitral and mild aortic regurgitation were significantly greater in MDMA users (21%, 45% and 14%, respectively) compared to controls (P = 0.002, P < 0.001, and P = 0.11, respectively). 60 In parallel, a case report of a 33‐year‐old male patient, with no history of cardiac disease but long‐term, high‐frequency use of MDMA, describes the occurrence of valvular regurgitation with histopathology consistent with those observed with other 5‐HT2B‐agonists (i.e., fen‐phen). 50 , 61 By contrast, a recent MDMA‐assisted therapy clinical trial for the treatment of PTSD, employing a brief, intermittent dosing regimen of two to three doses, reported no VHD‐related signals to date; however, the follow‐up duration remains modest and long‐term cardiac safety evaluations are required. 62 , 63

The available preclinical and clinical data implicate 5‐HT2B engagement by MDMA (and MDA) in valvulopathic processes, supporting a precautionary approach. Prospective studies are needed to define exposure‐response relationships and cumulative dose thresholds, delineate long‐term valvular outcomes, and formalize cardiac safety monitoring with baseline and interval echocardiography within clinical protocols.

Mechanistic cascade in valve tissues

In the cardiovascular system, 5‐HT and/or drug agonists (e.g., fenfluramine) bind to 5‐HT2BRs on valvular interstitial cells (VIC) and valve endothelial cells (VEC). 28 , 64 In addition to the effector pathways described in the foregoing section (Section 4) (i.e., Ca2+‐PKC signaling and ERK/MAPK activation), sustained 5‐HT2BR activation may also engage mitogenic and profibrotic processes. 31 In valve cells, 5‐HT2BR activation can upregulate transforming growth factor‐β1 (TGF‐β1) via Src kinase‐ and ERK‐dependent mechanisms. 31 TGF‐β1—a cytokine that mediates fibroblast proliferation, extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, and myofibroblast differentiation—activates SMAD2/3‐dependent transcription (canonical pathway) and the p38 MAPK pathway (noncanonical pathway), promoting VIC proliferation, increased collagen synthesis, and remodeling of valve leaflet (e.g., leaflet thickening) and ECM architecture. 28 , 59 , 64 , 65 , 66

Preclinical data suggests that p38‐dependent, noncanonical TFG‐β1 signaling is necessary for VIC myofibroblast activation and formation of calcific nodules, and 5‐HT2B antagonism disrupts this pathway; details are addressed in the next section. 47 Furthermore, serotonergic agents that do not activate 5‐HT2BRs are unlikely to produce VHD. 47 Accordingly, the Src‐dependent cross‐talk provides a strong rationale to evaluate the cardiac consequences of pharmacologic agents that exhibit 5‐HT2BR agonism.

CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM 5‐HT2B SIGNALING AND IMPLICATIONS FOR DEPRESSION

Persistent partial response and the high prevalence of TRD underscore a central limitation of monoamine reuptake‐based treatment strategies (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor [SSRI], serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor [SNRI]), wherein the pathophysiology of depression may extend beyond simple neurotransmitter imbalance. 67 These observations provide the rationale to investigate cell‐type‐specific serotonergic signaling, with emphasis on astrocytic and neuronal 5‐HT2B, as a potential framework for augmenting efficacy while minimizing peripheral, valve‐related cardiac risks.

As previously discussed, 5‐HT2BRs are also expressed in neuroglia, including microglia and astroglia (i.e., astrocytes). 31 , 68 Convergent preclinical findings demonstrate that sustained serotonergic tone, including chronic SSRI exposure (e.g., fluoxetine ‐ 5‐HT2B agonist on astrocytes), activates astrocytic 5‐HT2BRs. The stimulation of the astrocytic 5‐HT2BRs transactivate epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) via zinc‐dependent metalloproteinases, driving ERK/MAPK pathways and metabolic programs (e.g., glycolysis, glutamate–glutamine cycling) that regulate energy availability required for synaptic plasticity. 68 , 69 In parallel, astrocytic 5‐HT2B signaling has also been implicated in other glial regulatory roles that are relevant to network stabilization, such as homeostatic control and feedback regulation of neuronal activity and neurotransmitter handling (i.e., astrocyte‐neuron hysteresis loop). 17 , 70 , 71

At shorter, circuit‐level timescales (e.g., minutes‐to‐hours), 5‐HT2B can regulate rapid monoaminergic signaling. Specifically, pharmacological inhibition or genetic deletion of 5‐HT2B has been associated with attenuation of MDMA‐evoked 5‐HT release and hyperlocomotion in mice, demonstrating the involvement of 5‐HT2BRs in regulating fast midbrain‐forebrain dynamics. 18 , 58 Consistently, acute SSRI (i.e., paroxetine) administration results in a significant increase in extracellular 5‐HT within 60 minutes; however, only one‐quarter of this increase was observed in mice lacking 5‐HT2B receptors or treated with a 5‐HT2B antagonist. 18

Integrating these lines of evidence, the foregoing findings support a compartment‐temporal model wherein astrocytic 5‐HT2BR engagement under long‐term, sustained serotonergic exposure contributes to slower, consolidative trophic and metabolic programs, while neuronal 5‐HT2BRs participate in faster circuit modulation of monoaminergic and cortical output. The role of 5‐HT2BRs on astrocytes may partially explain the delayed onset of clinical benefit associated with conventional monoaminergic antidepressants despite rapid increases in extracellular serotonin. Moreover, the foregoing findings suggest that 5‐HT2BR signaling in the CNS is implicated in depression‐relevant neurobiological processes and behavioral domains, rather than the full clinical syndrome itself. In Parkinson's disease mouse paradigms, decreases in astroglial 5‐HT2B expression have been observed in parallel with reward‐related behavioral alterations and other behavioral changes relevant to anhedonia and reduced motivation. 72 Consistent trends were also reported in mice exposed to a sleep deprivation paradigm, wherein selective downregulation of 5‐HT2BRs in astrocytes was associated with stress‐related behavioral phenotypes and behavioral readouts relevant to impaired reward processing. 73 In both paradigms, treatment with fluoxetine or an upstream modulator (i.e., leptin) normalized the alterations in astrocytic 5‐HT2B expression and the associated behavioral readouts. 72 , 73 These domain‐level behavioral constructs are summarized in Figure 1 c . In this context, the translational value of such preclinical paradigms lies in relating CNS pharmacodynamic effects and cell‐type‐specific signaling to behavioral readouts relevant to domains such as anhedonia, motivation, reward processing, and executive function, as well as in informing the relationship between CNS activity and potential safety or toxicity windows.

Synthesizing these observations, CNS 5‐HT2B signaling emerges as a cell‐type and time‐dependent contributor to antidepressant biology as well as depressive pathophysiology. Astrocytic 5‐HT2B engagement under sustained serotonergic exposure may support slower metabolic and trophic adaptations relevant to antidepressant response; however, these observations do not endorse systemic 5‐HT2B agonism as a therapeutic strategy given the replicated and well‐established peripheral cardiovascular risks. Instead, the aforementioned findings motivate a more selective framework that distinguishes astrocytic adaptive signaling from neuronal/circuit‐level 5‐HT2B antagonism as a candidate augmentation mechanism. The foregoing distinction provides the rationale for the next section, which examines whether central 5‐HT2B antagonism, particularly in the setting of SSRI/SNRI exposure, may offer a mechanistically distinct route to improve depression‐relevant circuit function while respecting established peripheral safety boundaries. Prospectively defined cardiac safety measures and valve‐cell assay screening remain essential for any translational program involving 5‐HT2B‐directed compounds, and are consistent with the broader scientific and regulatory trajectory surrounding serotonergic agents with potential 5‐HT2BR agonist liability. 74 , 75 , 76 A schematic summary of the compartment‐, cell‐type‐, and timescale‐dependent framework reviewed herein is provided in Figure 1 .

5‐HT2B ANTAGONISM AS AN ANTIDEPRESSANT MECHANISM

Distinct from astrocytic 5‐HT2B engagement under sustained serotonergic tone, the rationale for central 5‐HT2B antagonism as an antidepressant augmentation mechanism arises from convergent pharmacologic, circuit‐level, and indirect clinical data in persons with MDD/TRD.

Indirect clinical signals from multireceptor adjuncts with 5‐HT2B antagonist/inverse agonist activity

Several approved adjunctive pharmacotherapeutics (i.e., atypical antipsychotics) for MDD/TRD exhibit 5‐HT2B antagonism or inverse agonism in recombinant and native‐tissue assays, such as cariprazine, brexpiprazole, aripiprazole, olanzapine (with fluoxetine), and quetiapine. 30 , 77 , 78 To help contextualize the strength of the evidence, we specify key trial‐design features where relevant, including whether studies were open‐label vs. randomized double‐blind placebo‐controlled, whether dosing was fixed or flexible, and whether the primary outcome was met. Importantly, the foregoing agents are polypharmacologic and therefore do not provide target‐selective clinical proof that 5‐HT2BR antagonism, in isolation, drives antidepressant efficacy. Rather, the mentioned agents offer indirect, hypothesis‐generating clinical signals that may be consistent with a 5‐HT2B‐related augmentation framework. The translational value of these agents lies not in target selectivity, but in the observation that several effective adjunctive treatments share measurable 5‐HT2BR antagonist or inverse agonist activity within broader multireceptor pharmacologic profiles.

In addition to dopamine D3/D2 receptor partial agonism, cariprazine also acts as a partial 5‐HT1A agonist and exhibits high‐affinity 5‐HT2BR antagonism (inhibition constant, Ki = 0.58 nM), yielding a pharmacologic profile that may engage mesocorticolimbic circuits relevant to motivation, cognition and depressive symptom domains and pathophysiology. 79 , 80 , 81 However, the available clinical evidence should be interpreted cautiously as it is not target‐selective and derives from study designs with differing evidentiary strength. A 26‐week, phase III, multicenter, open‐label, flexible‐dose study (1.5–4.5 mg/day) primarily evaluated long‐term safety and tolerability of adjunctive cariprazine in adults with MDD. 80 Findings suggest that adjunctive therapy with cariprazine improved the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) and Clinical Global Impression‐Severity (CGI‐S) total scores from baseline to endpoint (mean change in total score was −7.3 and −1.0, respectively). 80 At endpoint, remission (i.e., MADRS total score ≤ 10) and response (i.e., ≥ 50% reduction in MADRS total score) rates were 53.3% and 43.3%, respectively. 80 Given the study design, the foregoing findings are most informative with respect to long‐term safety and tolerability, while also providing supportive clinical context for antidepressant benefit. 80 Across randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled adjunctive trials, findings were mixed rather than uniformly positive: one flexible‐dose study identified significantly greater MADRS improvement with 2.0–4.5 mg/day cariprazine, but not 1–2 mg/day, relative to placebo, 82 whereas another 6‐week fixed‐dose study did not detect a statistically significant difference from placebo on the primary MADRS endpoint or the secondary CGI‐S endpoint for either dose tested (i.e., 1.5 or 3.0 mg/day). 83 A subsequent phase III randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled adjunctive trial also supported a mixed interpretation, reporting significantly greater MADRS improvement with cariprazine 1.5 mg/day, but not 3.0 mg/day, relative to placebo in adults with MDD and inadequate response to antidepressant therapy. 84 Notably, mean baseline‐to‐endpoint changes in cardiovascular safety parameters over 26 weeks were small and nonsignificant. 80 Taken together, the foregoing findings suggest a possible antidepressant signal for adjunctive cariprazine, while also underscoring that its clinical effects may not be attributed specifically to 5‐HT2BR antagonism in isolation. Moreover, the flexible‐dose design of the positive study also limits the extent to which an optimized dose can be inferred from that trial alone. 82

Brexpiprazole is also an atypical antipsychotic that exhibits a similar pharmacological profile to cariprazine, including measurable 5‐HT2BR affinity (5‐HT2B affinity; Ki = 1.9 nM), 85 and was approved for adjunctive use with antidepressants in MDD by the U.S. FDA. 86 Human clinical evidence indicates that adjunctive brexpiprazole is associated with significantly greater response rates than placebo in adults with MDD and inadequate response to antidepressant monotherapy. 86 Long‐term open‐label clinical data also support its safety and tolerability in adults with MDD, with no valvular‐related treatment‐emergent adverse events reported over 26 weeks. 87

Separately, aripiprazole exhibits a unique pharmacological profile from the foregoing atypical antipsychotics, with adaptive pharmacological activity. 88 Depending on endogenous dopamine levels and signaling, aripiprazole may act as a partial agonist or full/moderate antagonist at D2 receptors. 88 Similar to cariprazine and brexpiprazole, aripiprazole is also a partial agonist at 5‐HT1A as well as 5‐HT2A and 5‐HT2C receptors. 88 In comparison to antagonists which function to block receptor activation, aripiprazole displays 5‐HT2BR inverse agonism (Ki = 0.36 nM) to elicit negative intrinsic efficacy, reducing the receptor's activity below the basal level. 88 , 89 In persons with TRD, a significantly greater mean change in MADRS total score was observed with adjunctive aripiprazole (−8.8) than placebo (−5.8; P < 0.001). 90 Post hoc analyses of three randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reveal adjunctive aripiprazole was associated with significantly greater partial (23.9% vs. 17.9%), moderate (23.1% vs. 15.0%), and robust (14.3% vs. 7.4%) response rates compared to placebo in persons with MDD/TRD (all P values < 0.05). 91 No studies reported cardiovascular‐related treatment‐emergent adverse events.

As broader‐spectrum comparators within the adjunctive atypical antipsychotic class, agents with multireceptor antagonism also demonstrate measurable, albeit lower, affinity for 5‐HT2BRs. Within this category, olanzapine (i.e., olanzapine–fluoxetine combination) and quetiapine provide clinically informative reference points for augmentation efficacy and safety. Both agents are prominent antagonists of 5‐HT2A/2C with relatively lower affinity for D2 receptors, as well as exhibit antagonism at 5‐HT2BRs with moderate affinity relative to the foregoing atypical antipsychotics (olanzapine: Ki = 12 nM; norquetiapine, active metabolite of quetiapine: Ki = 14 nM). 92 , 93 As augmentation/adjunctive treatment strategies in MDD/TRD, clinical evidence indicates olanzapine–fluoxetine combination is superior to antidepressant monotherapy and supports relapse prevention (MADRS improvement: P < 0.001). 94 In support of the foregoing findings, similar trends were observed with adjunctive quetiapine compared to placebo in MDD persons with inadequate response to antidepressant monotherapy (MADRS improvement at week‐6: P < 0.01, remission rates: 42.5% vs. 24.5%, respectively, P < 0.01). 95 Notably, metabolic and sedative risks are salient considerations for both agents. 94 , 95

Extending beyond antipsychotic‐class adjuncts, viloxazine is an SNRI that was U.S. FDA approved for the treatment of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in 2021. 96 Preclinical evidence indicates that, in addition to norepinephrine transporter inhibition, viloxazine exhibits agonism and antagonism at the 5‐HT2C and 5‐HT2B receptors, respectively. 96 Historically, viloxazine has been prescribed as an antidepressant in Europe, and it is posited that its antagonistic activity at 5‐HT2BRs on GABAergic interneurons may underlie its antidepressant efficacy via indirect stimulation of serotonin release. 96 Notwithstanding the limited availability of contemporary augmentation trials in the past decade, historical randomized and modern pharmacologic evidence indicate a periphery‐sparing profile (i.e., antagonism, not agonism, at 5‐HT2B) that is conceptually consonant with a central 5‐HT2B framework that may be suitable for biomarker‐anchored evaluation if pursued in depression.

Taken together, the foregoing clinical observations should be interpreted cautiously. While several effective adjunctive agents display 5‐HT2BR antagonist or inverse agonist properties, their antidepressant efficacy likely reflects integrated actions across multiple receptor systems. As such, the current human literature supports clinical plausibility, but not target‐specific confirmation, of 5‐HT2BR antagonism as a therapeutic mechanism in MDD/TRD. Importantly, because these clinical studies evaluated outcomes over conventional trial durations (e.g., 6 weeks or longer), they may not establish rapid antidepressant onset in humans and should not be interpreted as direct evidence for fast‐acting clinical effects.

Mechanistic relevance and therapeutic implications of 5‐HT2B receptor antagonism in depression

The rationale for combining SSRIs with 5‐HT2B antagonism is not that both interventions simply amplify serotonergic output through the same mechanism (Figure 1 ). Rather, the available preclinical evidence suggests a division of labor across timescales and circuit elements wherein sustained serotonergic exposure may engage slower adaptive processes, while 5‐HT2B antagonism may augment treatment by rebalancing downstream dopaminergic and glutamatergic circuit output in the setting of SSRI exposure. In anesthetized rats, single‐unit recordings of the ventral tegmental area (VTA) (i.e., dopaminergic neurons), dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) (i.e., serotonergic neurons), and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) (i.e., glutamatergic pyramidal neurons) showed that selective 5‐HT2BR blockade with LY266097 reversed escitalopram‐induced suppression of VTA dopamine firing and burst activity, returning activity toward control levels. 30 Long‐term administration of LY266097, either alone or in combination with escitalopram, also increased mPFC pyramidal firing and burst activity by 165% and 125%, respectively (Firing: H[3] = 27.3, P < 0.001; Burst: H[3] = 15.2, P < 0.01). 30 The magnitude of this glutamatergic enhancement was comparable to that observed following adjunctive aripiprazole and escitalopram treatment (Firing: 105%, H[3] = 15.8, P < 0.01; Burst: 150%, H[3] = 12.1, P < 0.01). 30 By contrast, monotherapy and co‐administration of LY266097 did not rescue escitalopram‐induced inhibition of DRN 5‐HT neuron firing activity. 30 The present evidence suggests that 5‐HT2BR antagonism may augment SSRI treatment by selectively restoring mesocorticolimbic dopamine output and facilitating cortical glutamatergic activity, rather than by simply increasing serotonergic activity.

The observed enhancement of VTA dopaminergic signaling and augmentation of prefrontal glutamatergic output underscores a mechanistic rationale and pharmacotherapeutic potential of 5‐HT2BR antagonism in persons with TRD. 97 Specifically, dysregulation of mesolimbic dopamine is associated with anhedonia, 98 diminished motivation, and impaired reward learning, 99 whereas reduced medial prefrontal glutamatergic drive contributes to impairments in cognitive functioning such as cognitive control, working memory, and set‐shifting. 100 The foregoing circuit abnormalities are prominent in MDD and frequently persist despite conventional monoaminergic antidepressant therapy, affecting treatment responsiveness, onset of clinical benefit, and durability of effects including elevated relapse rates. 97 Consequently, centrally selective 5‐HT2BR antagonism offers a mechanistic route to normalize dopaminergic transmission and strengthen pyramidal glutamatergic signaling, providing a promising targetable approach for TRD.

As an augmentation strategy, central 5‐HT2B antagonism layered with SSRI/SNRI treatment is hypothesized, based on preclinical electrophysiologic evidence, to normalize mesolimbic dopamine output and amplify medial prefrontal glutamatergic activity within minutes‐to‐hours, effects that are mechanistically dissociable from simple elevations in extracellular 5‐HT. 30 However, this proposed temporal profile remains preclinical and mechanistic, and currently available human clinical data have not yet established rapid antidepressant onset attributable to 5‐HT2BR antagonism. In this framework, the potential clinical value of 5‐HT2B antagonism may lie in improving symptom domains that often persist despite monoamine reuptake inhibition alone, including anhedonia, reduced motivation, impaired reward processing, and executive dysfunction. 30 Early‐phase evaluation of centrally selective, periphery‐sparing 5‐HT2BR antagonists may therefore be justified as a hypothesis‐driven translation strategy to determine whether these circuit‐level effects translate into measurable clinical benefits in humans. Such studies should be anchored to mechanistic biomarkers and prospectively defined cardiac safety measures to respect the established peripheral safety boundary during hypothesis testing.

DISCUSSION

The modest efficacy of conventional monoaminergic antidepressants (i.e., SSRIs and SNRIs) in TRD underscores the need to investigate mechanistically novel approaches with the potential of producing rapid and durable antidepressant effects. The preclinical and clinical evidence reviewed herein positions 5‐HT2B signaling as a potential therapeutic augmentation strategy and pharmacologic target, while simultaneously delineating the translational constraints required to avoid peripheral valvular risks associated with 5‐HT2BR agonism. 19 , 69 Neuronal 5‐HT2BR antagonism can rapidly modulate monoaminergic and prefrontal circuit output, whereas astrocytic 5‐HT2B agonism during sustained serotonergic tone may contribute to slower, consolidative network‐level processes. 30 , 70 In parallel, convergent epidemiologic, echocardiographic and pharmacologic data implicate peripheral 5‐HT2BR agonism in an elevated risk of cardiac valvulopathy (e.g., VHD). 21 , 27 , 29 , 41 , 45 , 49 , 51 , 59

An important competing interpretation should also be acknowledged. Recent drug‐discovery efforts have prioritized the development of peripherally restricted 5‐HT2BR antagonists, in part because centrally mediated 5‐HT2B antagonism has been hypothesized to carry adverse neuropsychiatric effects, including depression, aggression, impulsivity, and suicidality. 28 However, this concern is not straightforward to reconcile with the widespread clinical use of several CNS‐active antipsychotic agents that exhibit measurable 5‐HT2BR antagonist or inverse agonist activity within broader multireceptor profiles. Accordingly, the present evidence does not establish central 5‐HT2BR antagonism as uniformly deleterious or uniformly therapeutic; rather, it suggests that clinical effects likely depend on factors such as CNS penetration, polypharmacology, cell‐type specificity, exposure pattern, and possibly population‐specific vulnerability.

The preceding recommendations align with a longstanding scientific and regulatory safety concern rather than representing de novo proposals. Clinical concern regarding serotonergic valvulopathy identified in the late 1990s following fenfluramine‐associated valve injury, and subsequent mechanistic work implicated 5‐HT2BR activation as a key mediator of drug‐induced valvular heart disease. 44 , 47 FDA public materials had incorporated this concern by the lorcaserin review process in 2012, wherein reduced 5‐HT2B potency was considered reassuring with respect to off‐target valvulopathy risk. 74 In 2020, the FDA‐approved fenfluramine (FINTEPLA) label included a boxed warning for valvular heart disease and pulmonary arterial hypertension, required echocardiographic assessment before, during, and after treatment, and restricted use through a risk evaluation and mitigation strategy (REMS) because of the association between serotonergic drugs with 5‐HT2BR agonist activity and these complications. 75 More recently, FDA's 2023 draft guidance for psychedelic drugs explicitly recommended evaluation of 5‐HT receptor subtype binding, assessment of functional activity at 5‐HT2BRs, microscopic heart valve evaluation in repeat‐dose toxicology studies when 5‐HT2B agonism is identified, and baseline/follow‐up echocardiography with exclusion of participants with preexisting valvulopathy or pulmonary hypertension for chronically administered compounds. 76

Building on the evolving scientific and regulatory framework, future translational efforts should prioritize concurrent evaluations of central‐antidepressant efficacy and peripheral safety by integrating detailed pharmacologic profiling. Specifically, drug discovery and preclinical development should establish 5‐HT2BR agonism in human valve cells as a strict exclusion criterion for candidate therapeutic advancement. Moreover, comprehensive assessment and characterization of ligand signaling bias and binding kinetics are essential to exclude arrestin‐mediated mitogenic signaling and prolonged receptor residence in valve tissue.

The emerging interest in serotonergic psychedelics exemplifies how therapeutic potential converges with uncertainties pertaining to toxicological risk. Classical psychedelics and some ergolines (e.g., MDMA) interact with 5‐HT2BRs, and while modern clinical protocols often utilize intermittent dosing with limited cumulative exposure, there remains a risk for valvular pathology which depends on both pharmacological profiles and exposure patterns. 19 Given the slow progression of VHD, absence of symptoms in short‐term trials does not establish long‐term safety; therefore, protocols involving repeated and/or maintenance dosing should integrate prospective valvular monitoring and exposure‐response analyses that quantify 5‐HT2BR engagement and cumulative dose effects. 44 , 69

Separately, central 5‐HT2BR antagonism represents a hypothesis‐driven and mechanistically plausible augmentation pathway for depression that warrants careful translational evaluation. Specifically, preclinical models demonstrate that 5‐HT2BR blockade normalizes mesolimbic dopamine output and enhances mPFC glutamatergic activity. 30 These system‐level effects may be relevant to residual symptom domains in TRD that often persist despite monoamine reuptake inhibition, including anhedonia, impairments in motivation, reward processing, and executive functioning. 77 , 97 , 98 , 101 However, although these circuit effects can emerge rapidly in preclinical paradigms, currently available clinical augmentation trials have been conducted over conventional multi‐week timeframes and do not yet establish a rapid antidepressant effect in humans. Notwithstanding the limited availability of selective central 5‐HT2B antagonists, converging pharmacological and indirect clinical data support further early phase evaluation of centrally selective, peripherally sparing 5‐HT2B antagonists. Such studies should be designed to determine whether the circuit‐level effects observed preclinically translate into measurable clinical benefit in humans, while recognizing that currently cited adjunctive agents are polypharmacologic and do not isolate 5‐HT2BR antagonism as the sole driver of antidepressant efficacy.

CONCLUSION

The 5‐HT2B receptor reflects a novel mechanistic approach to augment antidepressant response while acknowledging a well‐defined toxicological constraint posed by peripheral receptor agonism. The association between peripheral 5‐HT2BR agonism and valvular risks provides the impetus for developing a strategy that considers both efficacy and safety as coprimary objectives. Future translational and clinical investigations should focus on central 5‐HT2B antagonism for therapeutic augmentation and rigorous exclusion of peripheral agonism, coupled with proactive cardiac valve monitoring. By integrating central efficacy with rigorous peripheral safety measures, future development of 5‐HT2B‐targeted therapies holds the potential to advance mechanistically informed antidepressant treatment for difficult‐to‐treat depression.
