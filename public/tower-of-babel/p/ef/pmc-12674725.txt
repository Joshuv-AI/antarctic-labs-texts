Experiential learning improves college student perceived understanding and strengthens their perceptions of the beef industry
Authors: Victoria Garcia, Alice P Brandao, Tryon A Wickersham, Merritt L Drewery
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Immersive, real-world experiences—such as touring key sectors in the beef industry—improves college student perceived understanding of sustainability, technology, and economic factors underlying beef production. Experiential learning strengthened student perceptions of modern beef production systems.Keywords: animal science, beef cattle, field trip, high impact practices, pedagogy, scholarship of teaching and learning

Introduction

Animal science as a discipline is experiencing significant transformation in post-secondary education as student demographics and, concomitantly, educational needs evolve. More students are entering animal science programs from suburban or urban backgrounds (Albert 2012; Sullivan et al 2023; Carlson et al 2024) and lack prior, hands-on experiences with livestock production or in traditional agricultural systems (Bundy et al 2019; Sullivan et al 2023). As incoming students are increasingly removed from agriculture, they often require foundational instruction in subject matter (e.g., animal husbandry) but also in the broader context of agricultural practices and their societal relevance. Accordingly, there may be a need to adapt animal science curricula to provide experiential learning (EL) opportunities and emphasize applied knowledge that might have previously been acquired through lived experiences (Buchanan 2008; Boerngen and Rickard 2020; Erickson et al 2020; Wickenhauser et al 2021).

Changes in student demographics and educational needs in animal science coincide with ongoing challenges and advancements in livestock production (National Research Council 2015; ­Tedeschi and Mendes 2023) that are complex, systems-based, and often difficult to effectively relay in the traditional classroom, especially without personal context (Parrish et al 2015). To bridge this gap, innovative teaching strategies are required to convey technical knowledge while also immersing students in the realities of livestock systems. One strategy that aligns with these needs is EL, which is a holistic pedagogical approach that emphasizes learning through active engagement and reflection.

Experiential learning combines an individual’s experience, cognition, perception, and behavior, accounting for their overall learning process (Kolb 1984). Kolb (1984) reviewed three EL models (ie, Lewinian’s, Dewey’s, Piaget’s) and described EL as a process whereby concepts are derived from and continuously modified through experience. Experiential learning is heterogenous but guided by a common set of core principles that includes hands-on learning, problem solving processes, addressing real world problems, encouraging student interaction with each other and the content, participating in direct experiences, and using multiple subjects to enhance interdisciplinary learning (Wurdinger and Carlson 2009). These principles create a cycle of learning that includes planning, testing, and reflection.

Undergraduate students enrolled in courses that facilitate EL in beef production have demonstrated positive shifts in perceptions, knowledge, and awareness of industry opportunities (Enos 2012; Harrison 2015; Bowhay et al 2022). Experiential learning has also positively influenced agricultural college students’ sense of belonging (Drewery et al 2023; Sullivan et al 2023), feelings of empowerment, and comfort in working with people from other cultural backgrounds (Drewery and Lollar 2024).

The objective of this study was to assess changes in perceived understanding and perceptions of beef cattle production and changes in sense of belonging in college students pursuing agricultural or related degrees and participating in an immersive multi-day field trip showcasing beef production. “Perceived understanding” was used as a self-reported indicator of students’ confidence in their mastery of beef production concepts—versus measures of their actual understanding—as a complement to more traditional and objective measures of learning outcomes.

Materials and methods

Overview of the field trip

Procedures involving human subjects were approved by the Texas State University (TXST) Institutional Review Board (#8309). Students enrolled in the Department of Agricultural Sciences at Texas State University (n = 9) or the Department of Animal Science at Texas A&M University (TAMU; n = 17) attended a 5-d field trip touring the beef cattle industry in the Texas Panhandle with faculty from TXST (n = 1) and TAMU (n = 2) during May 2024. Students from TXST responded to an advertisement and were selected to participate in the field trip as a co-curricular offering. Students from TAMU applied to participate in the field trip through an open-ended questionnaire and, if selected, enrolled in a compressed course as an intersession.

Funds for the field trip were provided through a USDA Hispanic Serving Institution (HSI) Education Grant (2020-01979) held by TXST and through the Jim Theeck ‘65 Beef Cattle Seminar Endowment held by TAMU. Students attended a faculty-led orientation the day prior to departure and were provided an itinerary of the facilities visited during the tour.

Students and faculty members were divided by university among three 15-passenger vans, with one van for TXST and two for TAMU. Each van contained eight to nine students and one faculty member. Vans departed from College Station on May 20, 2024, and returned to their respective universities on May 25, 2024, after touring the facilities outlined and described in Table 1. The Texas Panhandle, where most of the field trip occurred, is approximately 800 km from both university campuses. Students were encouraged to explore each site and talk with professionals about careers and opportunities in the industry. Students were given binders with the itinerary as well as writing prompts to respond to throughout the trip. Prompts ranged from general notes about each production sector visited to reflections on how the student perceived the impact of what they were experiencing.

Students were also encouraged to interact with each other and the faculty members on the trip; whole group briefings occurred every morning prior to leaving the hotel, meals were consumed as a common group, and faculty/students spent at least 5 h in the vans together each day. Further, students were allowed to interact and explore with each other or on their own for self-guided activities. Students were assigned research topics to think about and discuss with their van to promote reflection, discussion, and critical thinking. One student was responsible for leading the discussion on their topic in each van such that all students were exposed to the same topics over the course of the trip. Topics included beef on dairy genetics, backgrounding methods, geographical and landscape characteristics, feed additives, cotton seed milling, steam flaking, recent natural disasters, utilization of byproducts in finishing rations, and ionophores.

Data collection

To evaluate how the field trip influenced students’ understanding and perceptions of beef industry concepts, we developed electronic survey-based questionnaires comprised of five sections; the survey instruments are available as Supplementary Material. Questionnaires were distributed as a pre-trip survey on the day before the field trip and as a post-trip survey within five days after the field trip. The questionnaires were identical except the post-trip surveys did not include the demographics section. Given the intensive nature of inputs required for the field trip, it was not feasible to conduct a pilot test and, thus, establish validity and reliability for survey instruments that were researcher-developed or substantially altered from their original version. Therefore, we adopted portions of survey instruments that were previously published to develop our own. Any adaptations to the questions and/or response options from their original version are noted below.

Section 1 included seven demographic questions. Section 2 focused on sense of belonging, featuring items such as: “I feel I belong in agriculture,” “People in agriculture accept me,” and “I feel like an outsider in agriculture,” adapted from the scale used by Stout et al (2013)and as implemented by Drewery et al (2023). Section 3 addressed sustainability using a 3-point Likert scale; most questions were adopted from Damico et al (2022). One item—“Do you believe that emphasizing one pillar of sustainability (environmental, economic, or social) negatively impacts the others?”—was derived from Edwards-Callaway et al (2024) and weighting of two of the response options were amended for our research. Section 4 assessed animal welfare perceptions utilizing 3- and 4-point Likert scale questions from Edwards-Callaway et al (2024) and 5-point Likert scale questions from Sullivan et al (2022). Section 5 explored students’ general perceptions of the beef industry. Questions were primarily drawn from Umer and Singh (2024) with six additional items that were self-developed. Responses were measured on a 5-point Likert scale.

Data analysis

As questions were adopted and adapted from previously validated survey instruments, reliability and validity testing were not conducted. Before calculating overall sense of belonging, negatively worded questions were reverse scored then responses were averaged across questions. Data were analyzed with SPSS Version 26.0 (Chicago, IL). Descriptive statistics and paired sample t-tests were used to assess changes in responses from the pre- to post-trip surveys.

Results

Both pre- and post-trip surveys were completed by 26 students (Table 2). Respondents were enrolled at TAMU (17/26, 65.4%) or TXST (9/26, 34.6%), were predominantly female (17/26, 65.4%), and were mainly white or Caucasian (17/26, 65.4%) or Hispanic (8/26, 30.8%). Educational classification included undergraduate underclassman (2/26, 7.7%), undergraduate upperclassman (17/26, 65.4%), Masters (4/26, 15.4%), and Doctoral (3/26, 11.5%). The majority were studying Animal Science (23/26, 88.5%) with other majors including Biomedical Science (1/26, 3.8%) or general agriculture (2/26, 7.7%). Most students self-reported an agricultural background (16/26, 61.5%) and no attempt was made to further qualify or quantify this.

Sense of belonging

A series of 5-point Likert scale questions were used to gauge students’ sense of belonging in agriculture pre- and post-trip. As a result of the trip, there were no changes in students’ overall sense of belonging in agriculture (P = 0.92; Table 3), or any indicator of belonging, including students’ perceptions of people accepting them in agriculture (P = 1.00) or feeling like an outsider in agriculture (P = 0.86).

Sustainability in beef production

A 3-point Likert scale was used to quantify student perceived understanding and perceptions of sustainability in beef production pre- and post-trip (Table 4). Participating in the field trip resulted in students reporting that the concept of sustainability was easier to understand (P = 0.02). There was not a change in students’ perception that sustainability concerns have potential to cause changes in the beef production system (P = 0.82) nor was there a change in their overall perception of the sustainability of beef production (P = 0.09). Notably, students perceived beef production to be sustainable, with an average score of 2.38 pre-trip and 2.65 post-trip where 1 = Low and 3 = High. There was not a difference in perception of the importance of sustainability in students’ professional future (P = 0.71) for an average of 1.21 on a 3.0 scale across both the pre- and post-survey where 1.0 = Highly or totally important and 3.0 = Little or not at all important. Further, there was not a difference between the pre- and post-trip responses when students were asked if emphasizing one pillar of sustainability would negatively impact the other two (P = 0.23).

As a result of participating in the field trip, there were positive shifts in level of agreement that the beef industry is modern and innovative (P < 0.01), although there was not a change in student perception that the beef industry has a modern and innovative image (P = 0.36). There were no significant changes in student perception that beef products are an essential part of a healthy diet (P = 0.10) nor of a sustainable diet (P = 0.14) although there were high levels of agreement with both statements pre- and post-trip, for an average of 4.62 for healthy diet and 4.47 for sustainable diet. Students did not experience a change in their perception that the beef industry treats animals ethically (4.38 versus 4.65; P = 0.11) as a result of the trip.

The field trip increased student perception of their understanding of the different processes involved in beef production (P < 0.01), of the environmental impact of beef production (P < 0.01), of economic factors affecting the beef industry (P < 0.01), and of the role of technology in modern beef production (P < 0.01). As a result of experiencing beef production, student level of agreement that “the environmental impact of the beef industry is a major concern” decreased (P = 0.04) from 4.00 pre-trip to 3.50 post-trip.

There was a shift in response to “Technology improves animal welfare” (P = 0.03) from 4.42 pre-trip to 4.65 on the post-trip. However, there were no changes in student perception that technology improves environmental sustainability (P = 0.33) or economic sustainability (P = 0.10) of the beef industry. Students felt technology improves economic versus environmental sustainability to a greater extent, with an average response across the pre- and post-trip surveys of 4.68 versus 4.43, respectively.

Students experienced a shift in their perception that beef producers contribute positively to the rural economy (P < 0.01), from 4.58 on the pre-trip survey to 4.85 on the post-trip survey, as a result of the field trip. There was not a change in student intent to consider a career or business venture in the beef industry (P = 1.00) with a relatively high score (4.54) reported on both the pre- and post-trip survey.

Discussion

In this study, we administered identical surveys before and after an immersive 5-d field trip, a tour of beef cattle production, in the Texas Panhandle. Trip attendees were college students and faculty from TXST and TAMU. Students ranged across educational levels, with most being juniors or seniors and majoring in animal science. Students were also predominantly female and White. In some instances, our sample was not reflective of departmental demographics at the respective universities. For example, females are 79% of enrolled students in the Department of Animal Science at TAMU but represented only 59% of TAMU students in our sample. Most students participating in the trip self-reported themselves as having an agricultural background (62% overall; 56% from TXST, 65% from TAMU), which likely differs from the general student population within the academic units. These demographics should be considered when interpreting and extrapolating or applying our data to other scenarios.

Experiential learning enhances perceived comprehension

Our findings demonstrate that EL via a tour of beef industry sectors in the Texas Panhandle improved student perceived understanding of technical concepts. Entering the trip, students indicated strong perceived knowledge of the different processes involved in beef production, the environmental impact of beef production practices, economic factors affecting the beef industry, and the role of technology in modern beef production. After the trip, students experienced perceived improvements in their understanding of each of these concepts.

The self-perceived increase in comprehension that students experienced on this trip aligns with that of Foster et al (2018) where, after pre-clinical veterinary students visited dairy farms, feedlots, cow-calf operations, and processing facilities, their perceived competence and actual knowledge in several areas of bovine practice increased. Harrison (2015) demonstrated that college students enrolled in a beef cattle management course featuring EL activities—ie, participation in a contest to develop and execute a management and marketing plan for a group of steers at the university ranch—experienced enhanced learning and knowledge retention. Finally, in research conducted on a previous iteration of the field trip described here, Bowhay et al (2022) observed that 41% of college student attendees journaled about gaining a new perspective on how the beef industry works. Cumulatively, these data indicate that EL opportunities in animal science are academically and professionally beneficial for post-secondary students.

While EL has clear academic benefits, it may be difficult to incorporate into some courses. In a survey of faculty teaching in undergraduate programs at four-year institutions in the U.S., 97% felt that EL enhances life skill (e.g., critical thinking, problem solving, communication) development with 84% attributing this enhanced development to EL requiring more complex cognitive processes (Wurdinger and Allison 2017). However, in that same survey, when faculty were asked what obstacles make EL difficult to implement, 61% responded “not enough time” and 28% responded “not enough money” (Wurdinger and Allison 2017).

The field trip described here was funded by a federal grant as a co-curricular activity (TXST) and an endowment as a curricular offering (TAMU). Similarly, EL described by Enos (2012) and focused on beef cattle production, was supported by a federal grant. As our findings—and those of the impacts of past field trips to the Texas Panhandle (Bowhay et al 2022; Drewery et al 2023) or other EL activities within animal science (Enos 2012)—are overwhelmingly positive, institutions and individual academic units should consider prioritizing similar pedagogy and curriculum to advance their educational impacts and vocational relevance. Alternatively, industry may consider sponsoring field trips to their facilities to enhance recruitment of qualified workers. Although we did not observe a change in student intent to consider a career or business venture in the beef industry, past EL opportunities have (Soliman et al 2021), demonstrating the value of investing in EL at the institutional level to build a strong alumni base.

Our recommendations for practice are to incorporate EL into applied courses (e.g., animal nutrition, livestock production, reproduction, health/welfare) where hands-on experiences can connect theory to practice. If institutional contributions are limited, faculty could leverage local resources (e.g., partner with extension, tour local farms/ranches) to create meaningful context-rich experiences for students. Finally, assessments within EL courses should be thoughtful, evaluating the students’ ability to integrate and apply course content versus solely participate, and reflection should be embedded in the course.

Experiential learning promotes systems-thinking

Students indicated that the concept of sustainability was more understandable as a result of their participation in EL. During the field trip, beef industry representatives explicitly discussed sustainability and cited practices their sector implements to promote sustainability of production. The three pillars of sustainability—economic, environmental, and social—were also discussed with students.

Sustainability is a complex concept and likely requires systems-thinking competency, which is the ability of learners to visualize and analyze complex systems (e.g., beef production) across different domains (e.g., economy, environment, social/society) and scales (e.g., local, regional, global; Wiek et al 2011). Our findings demonstrate that the EL described here provided opportunities for students to grasp the concept of sustainability, perhaps through the holistic, systems-thinking experience of immersing themselves in the beef industry. Past researchers who implemented EL in training workshops for medical residents reported a transformation in how the residents think about health care problems (Kovaric and Gingell 2024), supporting the idea that EL promotes systems-thinking. Wiek et al (2014) called for EL pedagogy to be embedded in sustainability programs of study within higher education to strengthen student ability to address and solve wicked and practical problems.

The trip decreased students’ level of concern about the environmental impact of beef production. Further, although sustainability was not explicitly mentioned in the question, there was an increase in students’ agreement with “Beef producers are contributing positively to the rural economy” after the trip. Taken together, these data indicate that students experienced a transformation in their understanding of sustainability when applied to beef production, extending to their appreciation of how the beef industry satisfies economic and social sustainability, and not just environmental, which is likely the most intuitive and straight-forward pillar for students to grasp. Indeed, Damico et al (2022) surveyed Argentinian and Italian college students enrolled in animal science courses on their knowledge and perceptions of sustainability in livestock systems; students had partial knowledge of the environmental sustainability aspects of livestock production but lacked knowledge of social and economic aspects. We encourage further research into pedagogy that promotes systems-thinking in students pursuing agricultural degrees; this will strengthen the workforce, thus enhancing the sustainability of production and providing societal benefits.

Experiential learning impacts student perceptions of welfare

Student perceptions of animal experiences in the beef industry were relatively positive before the trip. This was expected as Sullivan et al (2022) assessed perceptions of agricultural animal welfare in U.S. college students studying animal science and demonstrated higher perceived welfare for dairy and beef cattle versus poultry and swine. In the current research, most perceptions (55%) of animal experiences increased significantly and positively as a result of the trip, indicating that students felt beef cattle were treated ethically and in accordance with high levels of welfare. It is important to emphasize that, during the field trip, students experienced various sectors of beef production with a focus on concentrated animal feeding operations (ie, feedlots).

Our findings of improved perceptions associated with experiencing beef production contrast those of Ventura et al (2016) who analyzed the effects of a self-guided dairy farm tour on Canadian citizens and reported that 32% of citizens negatively shifted their perceptions of animal welfare within dairy production, 24% positively shifted their perceptions, and 44% did not shift or had nuanced perceptions. Further, Ryan et al (2015) reported that providing education about gestation stall use in swine production decreased consumer support for the practice. The differences between lay citizens and farmers’ views of agricultural animal welfare are established (Vanhonacker et al 2008) and it is likely that, because a high proportion of students participating in our study considered themselves as having an agricultural background and all were pursuing agricultural degrees, mostly as upperclassmen or higher, their views are more similar to those of farmers than to citizens.

The effects of showing people practices that are in employed in livestock production likely depend on the audience and, while the tour described here effectively improved welfare perceptions of animal science students, it may not be effective for those pursuing other degrees/careers or for lay people. This has implications beyond higher education and should be considered by the beef industry at large as there is increasing pressure to bridge deficits in communication with lay consumers ­(Oesterreicher et al 2018) and tours may not be well-understood or received.

Students in our study perceived animal welfare to be highly important to sustainable beef production systems; this perception was present before the field trip and increased numerically after. These data align with findings from Edwards-Callaway et al (2024) who deployed a series of questions related to the integration of animal welfare and sustainable beef systems on members of the U.S. Roundtable for Sustainable Beef and reported that the majority agreed that animal welfare was important to sustainable beef production. Further, almost all individuals surveyed by Edwards-Callaway et al (2024) indicated that animal welfare positively impacts environmental (96% of respondents), economic (96%), and social (100%) sustainability.

In our study, students perceived more positive impacts of animal welfare on the environmental and social pillars of sustainability as compared to economic on both the pre- and post-trip surveys, but agreement was high for each of the items. Students did not perceive that emphasizing one pillar of sustainability would negatively impact another, but it is important to note that we only asked about perceived negative impacts rather than potential positive interactions among the pillars. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that students viewed the pillars as independent or unrelated, but that they did not perceive conflict among them.

Clearly, students participating in the field trip felt that the beef industry implements high welfare practices into production systems and valued welfare to advance sustainable beef production. Our findings suggest that EL can play a critical role in shaping informed, nuanced student perspectives on animal welfare. As students engaged directly with livestock in situ on the field trip, they may have developed an accurate understanding of welfare practices that align with both industry standards and public expectations. This is important as societal attitudes towards animals are changing and consumers are expressing increased concern for agricultural animals, including in the U.S., as reviewed by Alonso et al (2020).

Experiential learning did not impact sense of belonging

Students who do not have traditional agriculture backgrounds may not feel a sense of belonging when introduced to new professional environments, impacting their academic success (Gilani and Thomas 2025) and career matriculation. Therefore, we surveyed students attending the field trip to observe if EL changed belonging.

Sense of belonging was high entering the trip and did not change after. This demonstrates that students felt accepted by others in the industry and that they belong in agriculture. As most students participating in the field trip classified themselves as having an agriculture background, our data may be biased and not representative of all students pursuing animal science degrees. Conversely, 83% of students surveyed by Sullivan et al (2023) did not have an agriculture background and reported a stronger sense of belonging after experiencing EL embedded within an introductory animal science course.

Jaramillo-Vasconez (2025) reported that, in students enrolled in a college of agriculture in the U.S., minority students reported lower levels of belonging with homophily and the presence of gatekeepers emerging from qualitative analyses as possible explanatory factors. Previous research conducted on the Panhandle field trip held in 2022 demonstrated that sense of belonging was lower for ethno-racial minority (ie, Black and Hispanic) students versus majority (ie, White) students before participating in the field trip but, after the field trip, sense of belonging was not different across groups (Drewery et al 2023).

In the current study, there were no differences in sense of belonging between ethno-racial minority versus majority groups (data not shown). As all students attending the field trip held in 2024 actively applied for the opportunity and, in 2022, TXST students were personally approached to attend, perhaps the students attending in 2024 already held high levels of confidence and belonging, which is why we did not detect differences in the pre-trip survey. Previous research suggests that college students who have a higher sense of belonging tend to have more academic self-confidence, higher motivation, and higher levels of academic engagement (Gillen-O’Neel 2021; Maunder 2018; Murphy and Zirkel 2015), supporting this theory.

Limitations

There are limitations associated with this research. We utilized convenience sampling and, as our sample population was relatively small, we did not analyze data by demographics (e.g., educational levels) or other factors (e.g., van the student was in), excepting the effect of ethno-racial identity (minority versus majority) on sense of belonging in agriculture. These analyses could have furthered our understanding of the efficacy of EL for different and specific groups of students but would have required a larger sample population which was not possible due to funding and logistical constraints associated with the field trip.

Students entered the field trip with a strong sense of belonging in agriculture, as well as positive perceptions and strong perceived understanding of beef production, which may have skewed our data. Because of this, it is possible that the impact of the field trip was understated and students earlier in their academic careers and/or who lack agricultural backgrounds and identities would have experienced more profound changes in the outcomes measured here. Our aim was to study a population that was representative of the animal science programs at our institutions but, given the competitive selection process for the field trip, especially at TAMU, students who were already familiar with and passionate about beef production were overrepresented in our sample. It would be interesting to conduct this study on a naïve student population.

Conclusion

This study evaluated changes in college student perceived understanding and perceptions of beef production before and after participating in an immersive multi-day industry field trip in the Texas Panhandle. These data add to a growing repository of literature focused on EL within animal science courses and indicate that EL improved comprehension of the beef industry and provided opportunities for systems-thinking to enhance understanding of complex concepts. We recommend incorporating EL opportunities in applied animal science courses, although time and financial constraints remain challenging. Academic units should consider providing funding for faculty seeking to implement EL within their courses; alternatively, local and regional resources and partnerships could be leveraged.
