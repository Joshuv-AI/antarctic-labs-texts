Distinct associations among human milk microbiota and minerals in relation to early infant growth: a longitudinal study
Authors: Lilian Lopez Leyva, Emmanuel Gonzalez, Corinne F. Maurice, Kristine G. Koski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrently there is a knowledge gap in relation to the interaction of milk components and their association with infant growth. Our aim was to analyze the associations between minerals and the milk microbiome and how they associate with infant growth in the Mam-Mayan indigenous population of Guatemala.MethodsIn this longitudinal study, a total of 114 milk samples were collected from 57 mothers during early (5–46 days) and established (4–6 months) lactation. Concentrations of 12 milk minerals were analyzed. Infant growth was assessed according to the infant growth parameters [weight-for-age (WAZ) and head circumference-for-age (HCAZ) into ≥−1 SD and <−1 SD and length-for-age (LAZ) into ≥−2 SD and <−2 SD]. The milk microbiome diversity was obtained by 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing of the V5–V6 region.ResultsThe milk microbiome diversity differed between infants with normal and impaired growth in weight and length during early lactation, but not in established lactation. In established lactation, milk microbiome diversity only differed between infants with normal and impaired head circumference. Some bacteria could be associated with normal growth, such as Luteococcus peritonei (for all growth parameters measured, that is WAZ, LAZ and HCAZ), Bifidobacterium longum, Lancefieldella parvula, Phascolarctobacterium, Rothia mucilaginosa, Streptococcus agalactiae and Streptococcus pneumoniae (only for the LAZ and HCAZ), Janthinobacterium lividum (only WAZ) and Lactobacillus johnsonii (only HCAZ). Similarly, C. acnes could be associated with impaired growth as it was identified in infants with impaired WAZ and LAZ. Manganese and magnesium emerged as key minerals due to their high number of correlations with the milk microbiome in both normal and impaired growth. Namely, Cutibacterium acnes was differentially abundant (DA) in the mildly underweight and stunted infants and was negatively associated with manganese. On the other hand, in non-stunted and normal head circumference infants Phascolarctobacterium and Streptococcus agalactiae were positively associated with magnesium, and Streptococcus pneumoniae was positively associated with manganese.ConclusionMilk microbial composition was associated with milk mineral concentrations, and both were associated with infant growth status.Trial Registration:ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT02755012 (http://Clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02755012). Introduction

Impaired growth is emerging as a concern in breastfed infants [1–3]. For decades, research on infant growth focused on children older than 6 months of age because of the assumption that milk was providing all the necessary nutrients for adequate infant growth [4–7]. Diverse milk components have been identified as critical for growth, including macronutrients, oligosaccharides, short-chain fatty acids, and endocrine factors [8–11].

Milk minerals for their part have typically been overlooked [12], yet evidence of associations between milk minerals and infant growth is accumulating [13, 14]. For instance, a recent systematic review reported a positive correlation between infant growth and milk concentrations of iodine, manganese, calcium, and zinc [14]. Two studies conducted by our research group reported that low mineral concentrations of calcium, magnesium, potassium, rubidium, and strontium in human milk were associated with impaired infant weight-for-age z score (WAZ), length-for-age z score (LAZ), and head circumference-for-age z score (HCAZ) [2] whereas milk magnesium in early lactation (5-46 days) was negatively associated with linear growth velocity in breastfed infants younger than 6 mo [3]. Milk concentrations of zinc for its part has been described as fundamental for infant growth, development, and cell differentiation [15], as well as in the metabolism of macronutrients and the synthesis of hormones involved in bone growth and metabolism [11]. Therefore, while there is evidence that minerals could play an important role in infant growth, more research is needed to characterize their association with other milk components, including the microorganisms found in human milk - the milk microbiome [16].

Indeed, evidence from the gut microbiome indicates several associations between gut microbial composition and infant growth. For example, healthy growth in the first 6 months is associated with greater Bifidobacterium longum and Streptococcus thermophilus in the infant gut (16), taxa typically associated with breastfeeding, immunomodulatory characteristics and polysaccharide and oligosaccharide metabolism [17, 18]. Data are more limited in milk, but a recent cross-sectional study showed that a higher prevalence of Streptococcus and Actinobacteria species in the milk microbiome positively correlated with infant head circumference, particularly during established lactation [19]. Another cross-sectional study led by the same group reported that the milk microbiome of mildly stunted infants (LAZ < 1.5 SD) had more Streptococcus mitis and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia in early lactation, and Brevundimonas_MS_1, Paracoccus_MS_1, and Streptococcus mitis in established lactation. In contrast, non-stunted infants (LAZ ≥ 1.5 SD) had more Paracoccus aminovorans and Pseudomonas cedrina in early lactation, and Lactococcus lactis in established lactation [20].

The interactions between members of the human milk microbiome with other nutrients such as milk minerals remain poorly explored, despite the fact that some are essential for bacterial growth and survival [21]. In a recent study, we reported a complex and dynamic interplay between milk minerals and the milk microbiome, which seemed to operate independently of maternal diet [22]. Milk iron, manganese and selenium emerged as key minerals for milk microbiome composition [22]. Iron, manganese, and selenium were correlated with members of the phyla Pseudomonadota (Fe and Mn) in early lactation, with Actinomycetota (Fe and Mn) in established lactation, and with Bacillota in both stages of lactation (Mn and Se). Another study reported that calcium was negatively correlated with Streptococcus, Prevotella, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes; magnesium showed a positive relationship with Streptococcus abundance in human milk; and selenium negatively correlated with Staphylococcus [23]. Similarly, as with infant growth and the microbiome, the association between minerals and the microbiome has been better documented in the gut than in human milk. For instance, a review of the human gut microbiome reported that bacterial strains of Streptococcus, Lactobacillus and Bifidibacteria increased the bioaccessibility and bioavailability of foods high in iron, zinc, calcium, selenium, and magnesium [24]. Thus, more studies in human milk microbiome are needed to better understand the complexity of existing associations between minerals and the milk microbiome and their impact on infant growth.

Guatemala has one of the highest exclusive breastfeeding rates in the World, approaching 58.5% [25] and is also home to a unique population of Mam-Mayan mothers, who comply with the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations to exclusively breastfeed for 6 months, have vaginal deliveries, experience limited obesity and do not use antibiotics. Yet, in some rural Mayan regions, the prevalence of child stunting is estimated to be > 58% [26]. Thus, Guatemala has simultaneously high rates of exclusive breastfeeding and impaired growth. We therefore hypothesized that impaired infant growth would be associated with higher loads of opportunistic bacteria linked to the mineral deficiencies in human milk. By sampling longitudinally a cohort of 57 Mam-Mayan mothers and the growth of their infants at 5–46 d postpartum (early lactation) and 4–6 mo postpartum (established lactation), we investigated 1) associations of milk mineral concentrations with the human milk microbiome diversity at early and established lactation and 2) associations of milk minerals and milk microbiome diversity with adequate or impaired infant growth. Ethics approval for this collaborative project with CeSSIAM was obtained from McGill University (IRB A04–B01-12A).

Methods

Study site and participants

This longitudinal study was part of a collaboration between McGill University and the Center for Studies of Sensory Impairment, Aging and Metabolism (CeSSIAM) in the Republic of Guatemala. Field studies were conducted from June 2012 through January 2013 in eight rural Mam-speaking communities of the San Juan Ostuncalco region in Guatemala [27]. The inclusion criteria were indigenous lactating women who had a vaginal delivery to reduce heterogeneity related to delivery mode and who initiated early exclusive or predominant breastfeeding with an average frequency of 12 feedings/day as recommended by WHO [28] with infants at 1) early stage of lactation (5-46 days), and 2) established stage of lactation (109–184 days) and, included infants with no use of medication and normal birth weights. As reported previously [27, 29], less than 15% of infants had a fever, cough vomiting, diarrhea, flu-like symptoms or stomach discomfort the week before samples and measurements were collected. Reports of traditional health conditions were reported at higher rates; these included “susto”, “mal de ojo”, “empuja” and “mollera caida”, but there is no Western equivalent for these. The presence of these symptoms was not controlled in the analyses. The exclusion criteria were: 1) mothers with colostrum (milk < 4 days postpartum), 2) mothers treated with antibiotics during postpartum period and 3) mothers with subclinical mastitis. Maternal age, BMI, parity and infant sex were controlled for in the analyses. Maternal nutritional status and socioeconomic conditions were relatively homogeneous across participants and therefore were not included as covariates in the final models.

Study design

In this longitudinal study, collected milk samples were categorized into 2 lactation stages: early (5–46 d postpartum, n = 57) and established (4–6 mo postpartum, n = 57). A subsequent division was done according to infant growth parameters. Infants were classified according to the infant growth parameters [length-for-age (LAZ), into ≥−2 SD and <−2 SD, weight-for-age (WAZ) and head circumference-for-age (HCAZ) into ≥−1 SD and <−1 SD]. These ranges were chosen to be consistent with our previous studies that measured infant growth [2, 3, 30]. The milk mineral concentrations and milk microbiome composition were analyzed. The power analysis, based on a previous study [19], indicated that a minimum of 20 samples was necessary to reach a power of 0.95 (using a p-value correction of 0.001, a minimum fold change of 2, and considering a 12% loss to follow-up). This higher power threshold was selected to offset known limitations in false positive control when using DESeq2 in high-dimensional microbiome datasets and to increase the reliability of observed associations.

Human milk sample collection and milk composition

Prior to collection, the nipple and areola of the breast were cleaned with 70% ethyl alcohol. Human milk samples were collected during a 3-hour time window in the morning from the breast not recently used for breastfeeding via full manual expression by a trained midwife, who used hand sanitizer before and after collection. This is important since other studies have shown differences in milk microbiome diversity with the use of breast pumps [31–33]. Milk was collected into 60 ml plastic vials and immediately stored on ice. Samples were partitioned into 15 ml tubes at the field laboratory (−30°C) prior to transfer on dry ice to McGill University where they were stored at −80 °C, which is known to preserve the milk microbiome integrity [34], until DNA extraction for microbiome analysis was performed.

Milk mineral concentrations

Human milk concentrations of 12 minerals (Na, Ca, Cu, Cr, Fe, Mg, Mn, K, Rb Se, Sr, and Zn) were measured as described previously [2, 35]. Milk samples were thawed and thoroughly homogenized. Overnight digestion of the samples was completed in plastic DigiTUBEs (SCP Science) through use of trace metal–grade concentrated nitric acid followed by a 3-h reaction with trace metal–grade hydrogen peroxide (30%, Ultrex II, JT Baker), followed by heating at 90 °C for 3 h, as previously described [2]. Minerals were quantified by inductively coupled mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) using a Varian ICP-820 MS (Analytik Jena, Germany) equipped with a Collision Reaction Interface. Calibration standards and quality controls have been previously described [2]. The limits of detection (LOD) for each of the 12 minerals measured on 8 replicates of the lowest calibration standard, were calcium, 1.505 mg/L; copper, 0.396 mg/L; iron, 1.34 mg/L; magnesium, 0.232 mg/L; manganese, 0.005 mg/L; potassium, 4.887 mg/L; rubidium, 0.032 mg/L; selenium, 0.007 mg/L; sodium, 1.816 mg/L; strontium, 0.026 mg/L; and zinc 0.116 mg/L. Milk mineral concentrations were compared to the average mineral content in human milk considered by INCAP (Institute of Nutrition of Central America and Panama) for the Guatemalan population [36].

Anthropometric measurements

Infant length, weight, and head circumference were measured as described previously [29]. Infant WAZ, LAZ, and HCAZ were calculated according to WHO Growth Reference Standards [37] through use of WHO Anthro software version 3.2. The international definition of “moderate malnutrition” (two SD from the WHO standards median) defined moderate stunting as LAZ < −2SD. For WAZ and HCAZ, the international definition of “mild malnutrition” <−1SD was used. WAZ and HCAZ have different cut-offs than LAZ due to an insufficient number of infants below WHO-defined thresholds (WAZ and HCAZ < −2 SD). To apply consistent cut-offs across all growth indicators, would have limited statistical power; consequently, different thresholds were used for LAZ, where sample size permitted more robust analysis. Additionally, these ranges allowed us to be consistent with our previous studies that measured infant growth [2, 3, 30].

DNA extraction

DNA extraction was done using 1 ml of milk with the Quick-DNA Fungal/Bacterial Miniprep kit from Zymo Research according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA was sent to the McGill Genome Centre for quality control, library preparation, and sequencing. Upon reception of the samples, a Picogreen test was done to measure the DNA concentration. As the concentration of the samples was very low, a speed vac was used to concentrate the DNA, and then it was resuspended in 12 uL DNAse/RNAse free water. The V5–V6 region was amplified in two steps using the P609F and P699R primers. PCR amplification was performed in triplicate with controls using the NEB Phusion Taq DNA Polymerase with the following cycle conditions: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s, followed by 30 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 58 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s, and concluded by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The barcoding was done using the Kapa Hifi 2X ready mix with the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 12 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s, and concluded by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Following amplicon amplifications, the samples that amplified were pooled and normalized. The normalization was followed by two 1X AMPure clean-up to remove primer-dimers before quantification using qPCR and the LabChip. The resulting amplicons were sequenced using an Illumina MiSeq platform at the McGill Genome Centre and assembled from 300 paired-end reads; reagent controls were below the detection limit.

Microbial community characterization

The sequencing data were processed using the ANCHOR pipeline for microbial analysis [38]. Reads were aligned and dereplicated using Mothur [39]. Exact sequence variants (ESVs) were selected based on a count threshold of 36 across all samples. Taxonomic annotations were performed using BLASTn against multiple databases, including NCBI 16S RefSeq and NCBI nr/nt, applying criteria of >99% identity and coverage [40, 41]. In cases of multiple hits with 100% identity, NCBI 16S rRNA RefSeq was prioritized due to the high standard of curation. All annotations, particularly species calls, should be considered putative even when sharing 100% sequence identity to a single species due to potential errors in databases [38]. Given the low biomass nature of these samples, to monitor potential contamination during DNA extraction and sample processing, negative controls (i.e., no sample) were included across processing batches and PCR blanks were assessed before sequencing. While these controls did not yield detectable DNA via Qubit and showed no visible amplification on an agarose gel, they were nonetheless included in the sequencing library to ensure full transparency of the background signal. Sequencing results confirmed that these negative controls yielded a negligible number of reads, with the actual samples having an average read count over 30-fold higher than that of the controls. During bioinformatic preprocessing, conservative filtering based on sparsity and prevalence was applied prior to downstream analysis to reduce the influence of rare and inconsistently detected features.

Sparsity and occurrence filter

Feature filtering preceded diversity, differential abundance, and correlation analyses to reduce influence of rare and sparsely observed features. Within each comparison, we retained ESVs observed in at least three samples per condition group. We then computed an abundance-based sparsity for each ESV as S = max_j a_{ij} / Σ_j a_{ij}, where a_{ij} is the post-QC abundance of ESV i in sample j. ESVs with S ≤ 0.90 were retained. All downstream analyses used this filtered set.

Microbial community composition and diversity

Per-sample community composition at the phylum level was summarized with DESeq2 variance-stabilized rlog values computed from raw counts. For comparative visualization, we used these rlog values to construct a cumulative phylum profile, rendered as a flower diagram with a custom D3.js script [42]. Alpha diversity was computed with Shannon and Simpson indices and compared between early and established lactation using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for repeated participants or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests otherwise. Beta diversity was computed with Bray-Curtis dissimilarities in vegan [40] and tested by PERMANOVA with 999 permutations. Unsupervised ordination was performed by principal coordinates analysis on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities (PCoA) using phyloseq and vegan [40, 41]. Canonical Analysis of Principal Coordinates (CAP) was used to visualize stage separation, and the constraint was evaluated by permutation of the constrained model. p values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure with FDR < 0.05 considered significant, and visualizations were generated with ggplot2 [42].

Differential abundance analysis

Differential abundance of microbial taxa between normal and impaired growth at early and established lactation was assessed using DESeq2 [43], a count-based negative-binomial framework selected to model sequencing count data while accounting for differences in library size across samples. Raw read counts were imported into DESeq2, and regularized log transformation (rlog) was applied as variance stabilization method across samples. Differentially abundant ESVs were identified with a significance threshold set at a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.05. For visualization of the results, the phyloseq [44] and ggplot2 [45] R packages were used. Differentially abundant taxa were represented by grouping species by phylum and ordering them according to their log fold change. A dashed red line was used to denote “infinite” log fold changes, indicating that some ESVs were detected in samples from only one group (either normal or impaired growth) and absent in the other.

Correlation analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation was used to examine the relationships between milk mineral concentrations and differentially abundant bacterial species (ESVs) in normal and impaired growth at early and established lactation. Feature associations were quantified using Spearman rank correlations between each outcome and all ESVs that passed QC filtering. p values were adjusted across the full set of tests using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure; results with an adjusted p-value (FDR) <0.05 were retained. For visualization, heatmaps display the subset of ESVs that were differentially abundant. ESVs labeled as “Unknown” were excluded from the final heatmap. The correlation matrix was visualized using the corrplot package, where the intensity of color represented the strength of the correlation (blue = negative correlation, red: positive correlation), and statistical significance was indicated with circles without a cross.

Results

Population characteristics and milk composition at early and established stage of lactation

This longitudinal study included 57 lactating mothers who provided milk samples at early (5–46 days) and established (4–6 mo) lactation, for a total of 114 milk samples. Milk samples were classified by lactation stage and according to the 3 growth parameters (WAZ, LAZ and HCAZ) z-score categories, resulting in the following 6 groups: 1) normal weight (WAZ ≥ −1 SD), 2) mildly underweight (WAZ < −1 SD), 3) non-stunted (LAZ ≥ −2 SD), 4) stunted (LAZ < −2 SD), 5) normal (HCAZ ≥ −1 SD) and 6) mildly-impaired head circumference (HCAZ < −1 SD). Tables 1 and 2 show the population characteristics at early and established lactation, respectively, and compares differences between infants with normal and impaired growth, as defined for WAZ, LAZ, and HCAZ. At early lactation, the maternal age differed between infants with normal and impaired WAZ, and maternal height differed between the groups of stunted and non-stunted infants. Bacterial characteristics of the human milk at early and established lactation

A total of 765 ESVs were assembled and captured 2,883,818 sequence reads across all 114 human milk samples. These could be annotated as 545 species, 171 genera, and 96 family, as well as 220 which could not be recognized as 99% similar (in both identity and coverage) to any known taxa and were labeled as “unknown”. However, these ESVs were retained in the statistical analyses to ensure a comprehensive assessment of microbiome composition. Notably, a subset of these ‘unknown’ taxa that were found to be significant in our differential abundance analyses were subsequently identified (100% identity) using BLASTn against a broader reference database. To ensure robust and reliable microbiome comparisons, we used regularized log (RLOG) transformation for normalization. This approach was chosen for its ability to stabilize variance across different abundance levels. To validate the normalization process, we generated abundance vs. variance plots, which confirmed the absence of stochastic effects and ensured appropriate variance stabilization. As contamination is a known challenge in microbiome studies, we included negative (blank) controls at all steps during sample processing to monitor potential contaminants. Of the 545 ESVs annotated as putative species, the average BLASTn return identity was 99.9% including 458 perfect hits (100% identity). The most abundant ESVs across all samples were Streptococcus salivarius_1, Streptococcus spp., and Agrococcus carbonis_1, making up 12.4%, 7%, and 5.5% of the microbial community, respectively (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Human milk microbiome composition. Discussion

The current study aimed to analyze the associations between milk minerals, the milk microbiome, and infant growth in the Mam-Mayan Indigenous population of Guatemala, using milk samples collected from the same infants during both early and established lactation. Our longitudinal design allowed us to explore the dynamic associations between milk microbiome composition and infant growth at early and established lactation. Our novel findings were, 1) the milk microbiome diversity differed between infants with normal and impaired growth in weight and length during early lactation, but not in established lactation. In established lactation, milk microbiome diversity only differed between infants with normal and impaired head circumference, 2) some bacteria were associated with normal growth, such as Luteococcus peritonei (for all growth parameters measured, that is WAZ, LAZ and HCAZ), Bifidobacterium longum, Lancefieldella parvula, Phascolarctobacterium, Rothia mucilaginosa, Streptococcus agalactiae and Streptococcus pneumoniae (only for the LAZ and HCAZ parameters), Janthinobacterium lividum (only WAZ parameter) and Lactobacillus johnsonii (only HCAZ parameter), 3) similarly, C. acnes was associated with impaired growth as it was identified in infants with impaired WAZ and LAZ parameters and 4) milk manganese and magnesium emerged as important minerals due to the multiple negative and positive associations with milk bacteria in infants with both impaired and normal growth.

Milk microbiome in early lactation is strongly associated with infant growth

Infants with impaired versus normal growth in weight and length ingested different milk microbiomes in early lactation but not at established lactation, which highlights a temporal association between early lactation and the weight and length of infants. During established lactation, only infants with normal and impaired head circumferences ingested different milk microbiomes. Previous research describing associations of the gut microbiome with infant growth characterized two vulnerable periods of infant growth as the first 6 months of life and from 6 months to 2 years (16). However, rather than the broad classification of 0–6 months of life, our results showed that the composition of the milk microbiome early in lactation (5–46 days) is strongly associated with infant normal weight and length. In fact, age has been reported as the most influential factor defining gut microbiome composition and function in early life [46], and possibly the same applies to milk microbiome composition. Furthermore, a systematic review on linear growth failure in low- and middle-income countries concluded that the highest incidence of stunting onset occurred from birth to 3 months and accounted for 23% of all children who experienced stunting by age 24 months [47]. This aligns with our results and underscores the importance of finding ways to detect, treat, and possibly prevent impaired growth before the commonly suggested timing of 6 months of age; the current interventions recommended by the WHO to treat impaired growth between birth and 6 months of age are limited [47]. These interventions in the neonatal period only include delayed cord clamping, neonatal vitamin K administration, and kangaroo mother care [48]. After the neonatal period, the unique recommendation is exclusive breastfeeding [48], which our study and others indicate that it does not reduce infant stunting in a population experiencing chronic undernutrition, as did our study population. [47, 49–51]. Further nutrition recommendations that improve milk composition in early lactation should be explored as potential strategies to reduce the risk of impaired growth in infants.

Associations of the milk microbiome with infant growth

The associations between infant growth and the milk microbiome remain underexplored, with only cross-sectional studies performed to date [19, 20, 52, 53]. One of the cross-sectional studies reported that S. mitis was negatively associated with head circumference, LAZ and HCAZ scores, while S. epidermidis was negatively associated with infant length, and S. parasanguis was positively associated with infant weight and WAZ score [53]. Cheema et. al. found a mix of positive and negative associations of Streptococcus and Staphylococcus species with infant growth. In comparison, most of our associations of Streptococcus and Staphylococcus were in infants with impaired growth WAZ and LAZ and both groups of HCAZ. This might be associated to different species, as we identified S. pneumoniae and S. agalactiae and Cheema et. al. identified S. mitis and S. epidermis. A cross-sectional study in milk microbiome and infant growth conducted in the same population in Guatemala reported similar results [19, 20]. In early lactation, diverse species of Pseudomonas and Streptococcus were differentially abundant in infants with normal WAZ, LAZ and HCAZ scores [19, 20]. In comparison to our study, Pseudomonas also predominated in the normal growth groups (particularly in LAZ), while Streptococcus were differentially abundant in both groups depending on the species. These similar results were expected as milk samples come from the same population, and they were confirmed through this longitudinal study. These findings suggest that Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, and Pseudomonas are consistently implicated in associations with infant growth across cross-sectional studies; however, more longitudinal work is required to contextualize and compare our results.

Bacteria associated with normal growth

We observed more differentially abundant ESVs in the milk of infants with normal growth in WAZ, LAZ and HCAZ parameters in comparison to the milk of infants with impaired growth, indicating more microbial diversity during normal growth. This aligns with previous evidence in the gut microbiome, where microbial diversity increases as infants age, which is typically associated with good health outcomes, including adequate growth [54].

Infants with normal weight ingested milk dominated by ESVs from the Pseudomonadota phyla including P. fluorescens, P. putida and P. trittici. In a cross-sectional study, Pseudomonas were also associated with normal WAZ, LAZ and HCAZ parameters, including the species P. fluorescens, P. koreensis and P. cedrina (19,20). However, in our study, Pseudomonas were also present in milk ingested by infants with impaired growth, so we cannot exclusively associate them with normal growth. Luteococcus peritonei also stands out as it was present in the milk received by infants with normal WAZ, LAZ and HCAZ parameters, although it has not been identified in previous studies that have associated bacteria with infant growth. Further research is needed to confirm the role of Luteococcus peritonei in infant growth. Other important species observed in the milk ingested by infants with normal growth were Janthinobacterium lividum (WAZ) and Lactobacillus johnsonii (HCAZ). Janthinobacterium lividum is recognized for its antibacterial, antiviral, and antifungal properties [55] and Lactobacillus johnsonii is a typical intestinal probiotic known for its contribution to anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and intestinal barrier protection [56].

Interestingly, several ESVs were present in the milk ingested by infants with both normal LAZ and normal HCAZ scores including Bifidobacterium longum, Lancefieldella parvula, Phascolarctobacterium, Rothia mucilaginosa, Streptococcus agalactiae, and Streptococcus pneumoniae. This could indicate the presence of a “core” milk microbiome associated with healthy infant growth in this specific population. In addition, the genera Limosilactobacillus and Corynebacterium were present in the milk of infants with normal LAZ and normal HCAZ, but the species observed for each growth parameter differed. In normal LAZ, the species differentially abundant were Limosilactobacillus fermentum and Corynebacterium fermentum while Limosilactobacillus gastricus and Corynebacterium gastricus were differentially abundant in HCAZ. Importantly, B. longum is known for its probiotic properties and it is a prevalent member of the gut microbiota of breastfed infants [57]. It was recently described as positively implicated in early growth in infants [58] and as regulator of bone formation [57], which could be linked to infant growth. Our results are consistent with positive associations between B. longum and infant growth as it was associated with infants with normal LAZ and HCAZ. Lancefieldella parvula is a lesser-known bacterium, but it has been isolated from human sources, specifically from the oral cavity, suggesting some association with the human microbiome [59]. However, it has not been previously associated with infant growth. Rothia mucilaginosa is typically part of the healthy oral microbiome but can cause opportunistic infections, particularly in immunocompromised individuals [60]. Phascolarctobacterium is prevalent in the gastrointestinal tract and can produce short-chain fatty acids with beneficial immunomodulatory characteristics [61]. Streptococcus agalactiae can be found in the gastrointestinal and genito-urinary tracts and can commonly cause infections in newborns, pregnant women, and immunocompromised individuals [62]. Similarly, Streptococcus pneumoniae is also an identified pathogen, responsible for pneumonia, meningitis, and other serious infections. Surprisingly, both latter species were associated with normal infant growth despite being known for their pathogenicity. None of these species have been associated previously with infant growth, but their presence in milk ingested by infants with normal LAZ and HCAZ could indicate a positive association with infant growth or may reflect differences in their pathogenicity when they interact with other milk components, such as milk minerals.

The mechanisms by which milk bacteria can affect infant growth remain unknown, but in the gut microbiome there is evidence that growth-promoting taxa, such as R. gnavus and C. symbosium, can improve the impaired growth phenotype by driving amino acids away from oxidation in favor of protein synthesis and lean mass formation [63]. It’s possible that the species associated with normal growth identified in this study may act through similar mechanisms to promote growth, but further research is needed.

Bacteria associated with impaired growth

Cutibacterium acnes was present in the milk ingested by infants with impaired WAZ and LAZ and Pseudomonas and Streptococcus were associated with infants with impaired HCAZ at both stages of lactation. In a previous cross-sectional study in the same population, C. acnes was one of the most abundant species reported in the milk microbiome, although it was not associated with any infant growth parameters [53]. Cutibacterium acnes and Streptococcus are commonly found on human skin, in the mouth, and gut [64]. Pseudomonas species are often environmental bacteria found in soil, water, and plant surfaces, but can also be opportunistic pathogens. For example, Cutibacterium acnes is linked to acne, Streptococcus species can cause skin and respiratory infections, and Pseudomonas species are known for opportunistic infections [64]. The fact that C. acnes is not DA in infants with normal HCAZ, but Pseudomonas are, could be explained by the role of C. acnes in homeostasis, as it has been described in the skin’s microbiome [64]. In the skin, C. acnes plays a homeostatic role interacting with other cutaneous microorganisms such as Staphylococcus epidermidis, Streptococcus pyogenes, and Pseudomonas species. On one hand, S. epidermis may limit the over colonization of C. acnes and on the other hand, C. acnes may limit the proliferation of S. aureus and S. pyogenes. These changes in the skin microbiome composition may lead to inflammation [64]. Similar associations could be happening in milk and C. acnes might be differentially abundant in impaired growth because it is actually preventing the proliferation of other pathogens. In fact, it has been shown in cutaneous samples that when C. acnes was present, Pseudomonas species typically were not, and vice versa [65], as we observed in our samples. Further investigation is needed to better understand the interactions between bacteria in the milk.

Associations of milk minerals and milk microbiome on infant growth

Manganese and magnesium emerged as important minerals in relation to milk microbiome composition and infant growth, driven by their numerous associations with milk bacteria, which remained consistent across the various growth parameters (WAZ, LAZ, and HCAZ). For instance, S. pneumoniae was positively associated with manganese and S. agalactiae and Phascolarbacterium were positively associated with magnesium in infants with normal LAZ and HCAZ at early lactation. Pseudomonas was negatively associated with magnesium in infants with normal WAZ and HCAZ also at early lactation. On the other hand and also at early lactation, C. acnes was negatively associated with manganese, but only in infants with impaired weight and length. Previous milk microbiome research has not reported associations involving magnesium or manganese. However, to provide a biologically plausible framework for our results, we highlighted prior evidence of their roles in the gut microbiota below. These comparisons are intended to generate future hypotheses rather than imply causation, as our study did not directly evaluate the mechanistic pathways relating milk minerals to microbial composition or infant growth.

Magnesium

In relation to magnesium, evidence in the gut microbiome shows that adequate levels of magnesium might increase microbial diversity [66, 67]. Its supplementation can help in remodeling the gut microbiota in patients with obesity and type 2 diabetes [68] and improved bone mineral density in postmenopausic osteopenic women [69]. However, dietary magnesium supplementation may induce intestinal dysbiosis in the absence of a magnesium deficiency [70]. Magnesium deficiency in mice reduced Bifidobacteria levels which promotes an inflammatory response [71] and interestingly, low dietary magnesium was associated with microbiota with a higher capacity to harvest energy from the diet [70]. Importantly, Lactobacillus spp. increased the bioavailability of magnesium in the gut [72, 73]. It appears that magnesium levels can be linked to the gut in both positive and negative ways. In our study, milk magnesium concentrations were below the reference values during early lactation. Under these conditions, in infants with normal length and head circumference, magnesium was positively associated with S. agalactiae and Phascolarctobacterium. Extrapolating from gut microbiome research, we hypothesize that this interaction may be associated with improved infant growth. As suggested by García-Legorreta et al. [70], low milk magnesium levels may favor bacteria with a high capacity for energy harvesting, which may be positively linked to infant growth outcomes. On the contrary, in a previous study in this same population, estimated infant intake of magnesium in milk was negatively associated with growth velocity from early to established lactation [3]. The authors hypothesized that this might be because there is evidence that inadequate intakes of magnesium might increase inflammation and impair linear bone growth and underscore poor postnatal growth, can reduce intestinal microbial diversity [74, 75], and lower cecal Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus [71] in mice.

Manganese

As for manganese, we recently reported its association with the milk microbiome—specifically, with Pseudomonadota in early lactation, Actinomycetota in established lactation, and Bacillota across both stages [22]. There is evidence that manganese is essential for bacterial growth [76–78], and it has also been linked to immune function, bacterial survival, and a capacity to promote the virulence of certain pathogens [79, 80]. Since manganese has the capacity to make a pathogen more virulent, and in our study, it was negatively correlated with C. acnes in infants with impaired weight and height, one possibility, based on gut microbiome prior research, is that the interaction between manganese and C. acnes may be further restricting infant growth. On the other hand, manganese was positively associated with S. pneumoniae in infants with normal head circumference during early lactation, suggesting, also drawing on findings from the gut microbiome, that in this context, manganese may be associated with immune-related processes that correlate with growth. These interpretations remain speculative and require further investigation.

Absence of associations for calcium, iron, and zinc

Surprisingly, minerals known to play key roles in infant growth and to interact extensively with gut bacteria—such as calcium, iron, and zinc [24, 81] were not associated with DA bacteria for any of the growth parameters evaluated. One possible explanation is that the effect of these minerals on the milk microbiome may depend on whether their concentrations in milk are adequate or deficient, potentially influencing their interactions with bacteria and, in turn, their bioavailability. In our population, several milk minerals, including calcium, magnesium and selenium in early lactation and calcium, potassium, selenium and sodium in established lactation, were found to be below established reference values, which could help explain why some of these key minerals showed no associations with the milk microbiome, despite their known importance for infant growth [2, 3]. There is evidence in the gut that microorganisms can regulate the levels of micronutrients by modulating their metabolism and absorption [81, 82], a mechanism that may also be relevant in human milk. In this context, interactions between milk minerals and the milk microbiome could be linked to infant growth through processes such as enhanced mineral absorption and utilization, supporting bone development and energy storage [83]. Conversely, shifts in milk microbiome composition or the presence of opportunistic bacteria may be associated with adverse outcomes, including infection, inflammation, diarrhea, and competition for host minerals [84], which could negatively affect infant nutritional status and growth.

It is challenging to distinguish whether the associations between and among milk components are causal in nature or coincidental factors, especially because nutrient deficiencies tend to co-exist and might affect these associations [85]. It is important to contextualize these results to a population with milk mineral concentrations below the reference values, the interaction between milk minerals and milk microbiome might differ depending on adequate or deficient milk mineral concentrations. A previous publication on the same population explored the association between maternal mineral intake and milk mineral concentrations and reported that milk mineral concentrations remain independent from the maternal diet possibly due to homeostatic processes [22]. However, as highlighted by this same publication, other factors could have stronger associations with milk minerals than maternal diet, such as lactation stage, maternal and infant age, parity, maternal physiological status (e.g., bone metabolism, renal function, hormonal regulation), maternal chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes, thyroid disorders), infections, geographic location (e.g., soil composition), and maternal genetic variants [86, 87]. The current results identified milk microbiome as a potential link to milk mineral concentrations, which could be relevant for understanding infant growth. Others have explained that the interaction between milk nutrients and milk microbiome is plausible considering that microorganisms depend on their hosts to obtain essential nutrients, so variation in milk nutrients may influence which microorganisms survive in the mammary gland and constitute the milk microbiome [85]. Further research is needed to clarify how these interactions between milk components relate to infant growth and if these interactions are the same in nutrient-deficient and nutrient-adequate mothers.

Nutritional immunity: a critical mechanism

Nutritional immunity is a fundamental concept for characterizing the interplay between host nutrients at the host-pathogen interface, where the host employs diverse strategies to limit metal availability while bacteria evolve sophisticated acquisition mechanisms [21, 88]. As the majority of enzymes in both bacteria and vertebrates require metal cofactors—specifically zinc, iron, manganese, and copper [89]—these elements serve as focal points for metabolic regulation. Previous research has utilized genome-scale metabolic modeling, to predict how dietary deficiencies in these minerals limit metabolic flux through critical pathways like oxidative phosphorylation, thereby impacting growth [90]. Furthermore, host genetic factors, as highlighted by Zhernakova et al. (2016) [91], may further modulate these host-microbe interactions, adding a layer of complexity to how individual infants respond to the nutrient environment.

In our current study, we observed that higher milk magnesium concentrations were linked to bacteria associated with improved growth outcomes (LAZ and HCAZ). While these findings align with earlier associations regarding magnesium and growth velocity [3], our results did not show universal consistency across all growth indices (WAZ, LAZ, HCAZ). Notably, manganese showed an inverse association with Cutibacterium acnes, suggesting specific regulatory roles for different trace elements.

The influence of dietary transition metals on host-bacterial dynamics is a critical, but an evolving, field [79]. Beyond the most studied elements (zinc, iron, manganese), others—including copper, nickel, cobalt, and molybdenum—are diet-derived and may be altered through supplementation [79]. However, clinical evidence regarding how maternal supplementation practices specifically alter the milk microbiome remains limited. Because the mammalian host must maintain homeostasis to avoid the dual risks of deficiency and toxicity, the microbiome may act as a mediator of these fluctuations.

Our previous work [22] highlighted differential associations such as positive correlations between milk magnesium/manganese and specific microbiota contrasted with negative associations for zinc, copper, and iron. Emerging evidence suggests the infant gut microbiome at birth is specialized. While many taxa are resistant to environmental metal starvation, they remain susceptible to specific metal restrictions [92, 93]. For instance, whereas certain Lactobacillus species are sensitive to manganese and zinc limitation, their growth can be enhanced by increased iron availability, suggesting that maternal iron status may fundamentally shape the early colonization of Lactobacillus [94]. Furthermore, shifts in Bifidobacterium abundance, often observed as lactation progresses to mature milk, may reflect adaptations to the evolving nutritional profile of human milk [95]. Future research must prioritize the interrelationships among the milk microbiome, maternal diet, and targeted supplementation to optimize infant growth and development.

There are several limitations associated with this study. We acknowledge the value of more advanced statistical models for future research, that should monitor larger cohorts of lactating mothers and include more time-points to evaluate changes over time. Only having data of two time-points made it impossible to identify trends. Similarly, the observational design limits our findings to associations; consequently, causality cannot be inferred. The infant health status was not controlled and could be affecting our results. The absence of maternal height as a covariate represents a limitation, as it is an important determinant of infant growth and may contribute to residual confounding [96–98]. The exclusive inclusion of vaginal deliveries limits the generalizability of our findings to caesarean-delivered populations, which are known to differ in milk microbiome composition [99–102]. Finally, our findings should be interpreted in light of potential reverse causality, as infant growth status may be associated with milk composition, including its microbial and mineral profiles, complicating the interpretation of observed associations.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings reveal complex, novel associations between human milk microbiota, mineral concentrations, and infant growth. While these findings are strictly associative, they provide a biologically plausible basis for future research into how milk components may collectively influence infant growth. Future mechanistic studies are needed to determine if these associations represent a modifiable factor for infant growth along with maternal health and environmental influences.
