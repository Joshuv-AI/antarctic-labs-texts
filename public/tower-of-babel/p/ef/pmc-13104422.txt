Characterizing visual compensation for proprioceptive impairments during the subacute phase of stroke
Authors: Lydia M. Kuhl, Matthew J. Chilvers, Troy M. Herter, Stephen H. Scott, Sean P. Dukelow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProprioception, the sense of limb position and movement, is impaired in over 50% of stroke survivors. Proprioceptive impairments are known to impede motor recovery, yet proprioception is rarely a focus of stroke rehabilitation. One strategy aimed at facilitating proprioceptive recovery and/or compensation is to have patients look at their arms while performing tasks. We have previously shown that many stroke survivors cannot use vision to compensate for impaired proprioception at ~ 10 days post-stroke, but it is unknown if compensation occurs at later timepoints. We aimed to characterize performance of stroke survivors on a position sense task with and without vision of their limbs over the first 6 months post-stroke.MethodsWe used a robotic exoskeleton to assess 89 stroke survivors at 2, 6, 12, and 26 weeks post-stroke on an arm position matching task, first without and then with vision of the upper limb. Three measures of position sense (Variability, Contraction/Expansion, and Shift) were used to classify participants into one of five groups: normal (unimpaired in both conditions), full compensation (impaired without vision, unimpaired with vision), partial compensation (impaired in both conditions but significantly improved with vision), absent compensation (impaired in both conditions and did not significantly improve with vision), and maladaptive compensation (unimpaired without vision but impaired with vision).ResultsOver time, all measures of position sense improved at a group level in both no vision and vision conditions. While more participants were able to compensate over time for proprioceptive deficits using vision, up to 26% of participants displayed absent or maladaptive compensation at 2 weeks post-stroke, and up to 11% of participants displayed these behaviours at 26 weeks post-stroke. Notably, at 26 weeks post-stroke, up to 22% of participants still had impaired task performance in both the no vision and vision conditions.ConclusionsMany stroke survivors could not use vision to compensate for proprioceptive deficits during the first 6 months post-stroke. This may be due to visual problems or issues integrating visual and proprioceptive information. Greater understanding of vision, proprioception, and their integration during the subacute phase of stroke is needed to target treatments to improve stroke recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-01911-0.

Introduction

Over 50% of stroke survivors have impairments in proprioception, the perception of limb position (position sense) and movement (kinesthesia) in space without the use of vision [1–3]. Proprioception is important for carrying out everyday tasks [4, 5] and has a large influence on motor planning and action guidance [6, 7]. It is known that proprioceptive deficits can impede motor recovery after stroke, yet proprioception is often not the primary focus in stroke rehabilitation [8, 9].

A therapy strategy for those with upper limb proprioceptive impairments is to have patients look at their arms while completing tasks [10–12]. Although this is not how we behave during natural movement, this strategy is supported by evidence from healthy individuals, where adding visual input to a proprioceptive or motor task often leads to better outcomes than when vision is not used [7, 13, 14]. Following this premise, it is thought that employing the use of vision should help an individual to compensate for impaired proprioception post-stroke [11, 15].

The use of vision to help compensate for proprioceptive deficits has been previously examined at around one week post-stroke in assessments of both position sense and kinesthesia [11, 15]. These studies found that many stroke survivors do not improve proprioceptive task performance when using vision or even get worse at the task. In the present study, we were interested in understanding whether the ability to compensate for proprioceptive loss with vision changes over time during the subacute phase (1 week to 6 months post-stroke). This timeframe is important, as most recovery and rehabilitation occurs during this phase [16–19].

In this study, we used a robotic assessment of arm position sense, both with and without vision of the arms, to study the extent to which vison can help compensate for proprioceptive deficits during the subacute period of stroke. We aimed to (1) assess proprioceptive recovery post-stroke and (2) understand changes in how individuals use vision to compensate for proprioceptive deficits over the subacute period of stroke.

Methods

Participant recruitment

Participants with first-time unilateral subacute stroke were recruited from the acute and inpatient rehabilitation stroke units at Foothills Medical Centre, as well as the Dr. Vernon Fanning Centre (Calgary, Alberta, Canada). Inclusion criteria for stroke participants were: 18 years or older, first-time clinical presentation of unilateral stroke, and the ability to understand the task instructions. Exclusion criteria for stroke participants were: history of other neurological disease or injury (e.g., Parkinson’s disease, Multiple Sclerosis), recent musculoskeletal injuries to the arms, neck, or back, and cerebellar or brainstem stroke. Stroke participants were assessed at four timepoints: 2, 6, 12, and 26 weeks post-stroke. At each timepoint, stroke participants completed both clinical and robotic assessments. To make comparisons between the data collected in this study and a control population, we used a previously collected database of 799 neurologically intact adult control participants. The study was approved by the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board, and informed consent was given by every participant.

Robotic assessments

A robotic assessment of upper limb position sense was performed in a Kinarm Exoskeleton Lab (Fig. 1A; Kinarm, Kingston, Ontario, Canada) [20]. Participants sat in a wheelchair base and their arms were supported against gravity by troughs at the upper arm and forearm. The linkages of the exoskeleton were adjusted such that the elbow and shoulder rotational axes of the robot aligned with the elbow and shoulder joints of the participant. The exoskeleton was able to exert forces on the upper arms to facilitate passive movement.

Arm position matching task

In this study, an arm position matching task (APM) [1] was performed twice, first with vision of the arms occluded by an opaque screen and fabric bib to block vision of the arms and shoulders (Fig. 1B; no vision condition), and then with full vision of the arms (Fig. 1C; vision condition). APM task instructions read to each participant are shown at the top of the Supplemental file. Each participant got the same instructions followed by two or three practise matches before the task began.

In the APM task, the robot moved the participant’s affected arm (passive arm) to one of nine different pseudorandomized positions. The targets were arranged in a square configuration (eight peripheral targets, one central target; Fig. 1D), and the targets were spaced 10 cm apart. The central target of the task was positioned such that the participant’s shoulder was horizontally abducted by 30° and the elbow was flexed at 90°. The robot moved the affected arm using a bell-shaped velocity profile (max speed < 1 m/s) [1]. Once movement of the affected arm was completed by the robot, participants were asked to mirror match the position of the affected arm with their unaffected arm (active arm) [1]. Once the participant felt the match was complete, they verbally told the operator, who then cued the robot to move to the next trial. Each target was presented once per block, and each participant completed 6 blocks for a total of 54 trials. This task has been previously validated for use in stroke patients [1].

Three measures were computed from APM task data (Fig. 1E-G): (1) Variability, standard deviation of trial-to-trial consistency of the mirror matches in the X and Y directions; (2) Contraction/Expansion (C/E), ratio of the area covered by the active arm relative to the area covered by the passive arm; and (3) Systematic Shift (Shift), amount of systematic error of the active arm positions relative to the passive arm positions in the X and Y directions [21]. The formulas used to calculate these measures can be found in Dukelow et al., 2010 [1] and Additional File 1.

Parameter Z scores

We used a previously collected dataset of 799 control participants (Kinarm, Kingston, Ontario, Canada) to generate a normative model for the no vision condition of the APM task that adjusted for age, sex, and handedness, as required [22, 23]. The normative model was then used to quantify performance for each stroke participant by factoring in their age, sex and handedness and defining performance in Z units (Z score). Z scores represent how many standard deviations a given stroke survivor was from the mean of the normative model.

In this analysis, we used the no vision normative model to generate both the no vision and vision APM Z scores, meaning that we examined whether stroke participants could use vision to correct their proprioceptive performance to within the bounds of normal for controls performing the same task without vision. For one-sided parameters (Variability and Shift), a Z score of > 1.65 delineated abnormal performance compared to a healthy control. For the two-sided parameter (C/E), we calculated the absolute value of the Z scores such that a Z score of > 1.96 was used to classify abnormal performance. These thresholds represent the 95th percentile of the control dataset. More detail on the calculation of Z scores can be found here: https://kinarm.com/download/kst-summary-analysis-version-3-10/.

Classification of compensation

We classified participants into one of five groups based on their ability to use vision to compensate for impaired position sense. The groups were: (1) normal: unimpaired on both no vision and vision conditions; (2) full compensation: impaired in the no vision condition, but unimpaired in the vision condition (i.e. when using vision, could correct to normal performance compared to controls performing without vision); (3) partial compensation: impaired in the no vision condition and, while there was a statistically significant improvement in the vision condition, performance was still impaired (for further detail, see Statistical Analysis below); (4) absent compensation: impaired in both conditions and did not exhibit significant improvement in the vision condition; (5) maladaptive compensation: unimpaired in the no vision condition and impaired in the vision condition.

Clinical assessments

We collected clinical assessment data from all participants to provide a clinical measure of performance to supplement the robotics metrics. Participants completed the Chedoke-McMaster Stroke Assessment (CMSA) [24], Thumb Localizer Test (TLT) [25], confrontation visual field testing [26], and the Behavioural Inattention Test (BIT) [27] at each timepoint. The CMSA impairment inventory assessed physical impairment on both the affected and unaffected arms and hands. Participants were scored out of 7 for each assessment, with 1 indicating maximum impairment (no volitional movements, no elicited reflexes, flaccid paralysis) and 7 indicating no impairment. The TLT assessed proprioception, where the affected arm was moved by a therapist and the participant attempted to localize and grab the thumb of the affected arm with their eyes closed. A TLT score of 0 indicated that the person had no difficulty locating their thumb, 1 meant that they missed the thumb but could correct, 2 meant that they located their arm and ran their hand up it to locate their thumb, and 3 indicated they did not find their thumb at all. Neglect was diagnosed if an individual scored < 130 out of a possible 146 points on the BIT.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were done in SPSS (version 29) [28]. Significance for all statistical analyses was set to p < 0.05. Linear mixed models were used to determine significant differences between no vision and vision conditions as well as between timepoints. Condition (no vision/vision) and timepoint (2, 6, 12, 26 weeks) were included as fixed effects and participants were included as a random effect. Bonferroni correction was used on all post-hoc comparisons (timepoint*condition compared against condition, timepoint*condition compared against timepoint). For full statistics, see Supplemental Table 1, Additional File 2.

To distinguish between partial and absent compensation, we used the raw robotics data to calculate Variability and Shift per target (6 trials/target) and C/E per block (9 trials/block) for each participant. We used a one-sided permutation test (924 permutations) to compare each participant’s per target or per block performance in the no vision condition with their performance in the vision condition. If the mean in the no vision condition was greater than the mean in the vision condition and the test was significant, it was counted as a significant improvement.

Results

Demographics

Eighty-nine stroke survivors performed the arm position matching task with and without vision of the arms. Stroke survivors completed the task at 2, 6, 12, and 26 weeks post-stroke. Stroke survivors included 62 males and 27 females with a mean age of 61.6 ± 11.8 years. Stroke survivors were mostly right-handed (4L/85R).

Clinical assessments

Clinical assessment data collected from stroke survivors are outlined in Table 1. Many stroke survivors exhibited improvements over time on the CMSA (affected and unaffected arm and hand scores), Thumb Localizer Test (TLT), and BIT, and confrontation testing of visual field deficits. CMSA scores for the unaffected arm and hand were ≥ 6 in all participants at all timepoints, which mitigates any concern about ipsilateral motor impairments in the upper limb. Correlation analyses between the TLT and APM task parameter Z Scores at 2 weeks post-stroke are found in Supplemental Fig. 1, Additional File 2).

APM task performance in no vision and vision conditions over time

Between timepoints, there was a significant improvement in mean performance in the stroke group in both no vision and vision conditions from 2 weeks post-stroke to 6 weeks post-stroke in all measures (Fig. 2). In Variability, there was also a significant improvement from 6 weeks to 12 weeks post-stroke in the no vision condition (Fig. 2A), but in all other measures at all other timepoints there were no other significant improvements in performance. At all timepoints, performance in the vision condition for Variability and C/E was significantly better than the no vision condition (Fig. 2A, B). There were significant differences in Shift between the no vision and vision conditions at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, and 12 weeks post-stroke, but not at 26 weeks post-stroke (Fig. 2C).

Many participants exhibited impaired position sense at 26 weeks post-stroke

Cumulative sum plots for all measures are shown in Fig. 3. For Variability (Fig. 3A), 65% of the stroke group were impaired in the no vision and 40% were impaired in the vision condition at 2 weeks post-stroke. By 26 weeks post-stroke, 29% were impaired in the no vision condition and 22% were impaired in the vision condition (Fig. 3A). For C/E (Fig. 3B), 51% of the stroke group were impaired in the no vision condition and 27% were impaired in the vision condition at 2 weeks post-stroke. By 26 weeks post-stroke, 29% were impaired in the no vision condition and only 13% were impaired in the vision condition. For Shift, 27% of the stroke group were impaired in the no vision condition and 21% were impaired in the vision condition at 2 weeks post-stroke (Fig. 3C). By 26 weeks post-stroke, 12% were impaired in the no vision condition and 9% were impaired on the vision condition.

Many participants could not use vision to compensate for impaired proprioception

At 2 weeks post-stroke, 41% of the stroke group exhibited partial, absent, or maladaptive compensation for Variability (Fig. 4A), 27% exhibited partial, absent, or maladaptive compensation for C/E (Fig. 5A), and 21% exhibited partial, absent, or maladaptive compensation for Shift (Fig. 6A). At 6 weeks and 12 weeks post-stroke, there was an increase in the number of individuals in the normal group for Variability (Fig. 4B, C), C/E (Fig. 5B, C), and Shift (Fig. 6B, C) compared to 2 weeks post-stroke. At 26 weeks post-stroke, most participants showed normal or full compensation for all measures. However, 22% of participants were in partial, absent, or maladaptive compensation groups for Variability (Fig. 4D), 13% were in partial, absent, or maladaptive compensation groups for C/E (Fig. 5D), and 9% were in partial, absent, or maladaptive compensation groups for Shift (Fig. 6D). Complete group proportions are presented in Table 2. Most, but not all, participants who had partial, absent, or maladaptive compensation across time exhibited visuospatial neglect, visual field deficits, or both (Supplemental Figs. 2–7, Additional File 2).

Compensation group proportions over time

Examining fluctuations of compensation between timepoints is also important for understanding compensatory behaviours over time (see Supplemental Table 2, Additional File 2). For Variability, many individuals with full compensation moved to the normal group between all timepoints (Fig. 4E). Interestingly, 33–50% of participants with absent compensation stayed in the absent group across all timepoints, and 17–38% of participants with partial compensation moved into the absent compensation group over time.

For C/E, the normal group retained 89–94% of individuals within the group across time (Fig. 5E). Most people with full compensation stayed in the full compensation group or moved into the normal group between all timepoints. At 26 weeks, however, 25% of participants in the full compensation group moved into the absent compensation group. 29–40% of participants with absent compensation stayed in the absent group across timepoints, and there were few participants in absent and maladaptive compensation at all timepoints.

The normal group retained between 81% and 91% of individuals over time for Shift (Fig. 6E). However, 4% of individuals in the normal group moved into the maladaptive compensation group between 6 and 12 weeks post-stroke, and 8% of the normal group moved to the absent compensation group. The partial compensation group split evenly at 2 weeks post-stroke to become normal, full, absent, or maladaptive at 6 weeks post-stroke.

Discussion

Research on the integration of visual and proprioceptive information in healthy individuals has found that using vision combined with proprioception improves task performance compared to using only proprioception [7, 14, 29]. Previous research from our lab has shown that this is not always the case at ~ 10 days post-stroke for both position sense [11] and kinesthesia [15]. Up to 48% of participants in these studies either did not significantly improve on proprioceptive tasks with vision or performed worse when given vision of their arms. Here, we extended the investigation into position sense to explore compensatory patterns over the first 6 months after stroke.

Based on studies of neurologically normal adults, we expected that using vision during a task would improve position sense [7, 14, 29, 30]. In many cases, this was true post-stroke. However, we were also interested in how performance and the ability to compensate for proprioceptive deficits using vision changed over time. Performance on the APM task significantly improved in both no vision and vision conditions of the task between 2 and 6 weeks post-stroke. More individuals were in the normal and full compensation groups as time elapsed, and the largest increase in percentage of individuals in the normal group also aligned with the significant improvement we saw in performance on both task conditions between 2 and 6 weeks post-stroke. However, even at 26 weeks post-stroke, between 9% and 22% of individuals could not fully compensate for impaired proprioception using vision. Beginning to understand the timecourse in which vision can be used to compensate for impaired position sense during the subacute phase of stroke gives us a better comprehension of the evolution of proprioceptive recovery and potential compensatory mechanisms that can be used to facilitate proprioceptive recovery. Ultimately, this can help inform rehabilitation practises to target the correct mechanisms and timing needed for proprioceptive rehabilitation. Given that 22% of stroke participants were impaired on Variability, 13% were impaired on C/E, and 9% were impaired on Shift in the vision condition at 26 weeks post-stroke, many stroke survivors with proprioceptive impairments might benefit from targeted interventions.

There is a window of time from ~ 1 week to ~ 3 months post-stroke where most recovery occurs and rehabilitation is considered to be most effective [17, 31, 32]. The end of the early subacute phase (~ 3 months post-stroke) is typically when motor recovery begins to plateau, and functional gains diminish [17, 19]. Across all measures in both no vision and vision conditions, significant improvement in group means only occurred between 2 weeks and 6 weeks post-stroke. Besides this epoch aligning with the “critical period” of stroke recovery, 2 to 6 weeks post-stroke is when most of the participants would have also been receiving either inpatient rehabilitation or been part of an outpatient post-stroke therapy program where they may have learned compensatory strategies for their deficits or gained some level of functional recovery. Additionally, if visuospatial neglect or visual field deficits were present during the first three months post-stroke, participants may have learned compensatory behaviours. Visual field deficits typically remain stable over time [33], but neglect has some level of recovery, especially in the first three months post-stroke that could also affect task performance [34].

Recovery of motor function is often the focus of subacute stroke rehabilitation [8–10]. However, literature shows that sensory impairments negatively impact motor recovery [8, 9, 35]. At 26 weeks post-stroke, 9%-22% of participants were impaired on Variability, C/E, and Shift in the no-vision condition. This shows that proprioceptive deficits are not resolved by 6 months post-stroke and could impede overall recovery. In addition, it demonstrates that designing and implementing proprioceptive rehabilitation during the first three months post-stroke could have a significant positive impact on motor recovery.

Those who displayed full or partial compensation likely had primary proprioceptive issues (i.e. proprioceptive deficits without the presence of visual deficits). However, finding that participants had either absent or maladaptive compensation at 26 weeks post-stroke indicated that other problems, such as visual field deficits, visuospatial neglect, and/or issues with multisensory integration, were likely at play. These individuals were not able to adequately use other resources, such as vision, to compensate for their proprioceptive deficits (Supplemental Figs. 2–7, Additional File 2). Vision plays a large role in movement planning [6, 7], meaning that visual problems, such as visuospatial neglect, visual field deficits, or oculomotor dysfunction, could negatively affect position estimation and movement planning in the vision condition. Many of our participants had visuospatial neglect (n = 12), visual field deficits (n = 5), or both (n = 8) (Supplemental Figs. 2–7, Additional File 2), and some individuals’ inability to compensate using vision may have been because of these issues. Those with visual field deficits as well as visuospatial neglect and those with only visual field deficits were the most impaired on the vision condition of the APM task at 2 weeks and 6 weeks post-stroke in all parameters (Supplemental Figs. 5–7, Additional File 2). Another possible reason that an individual was unable to compensate for poor proprioception using vision is that they were unable to correctly integrate visual and proprioceptive information.

Examining the use of visual compensation techniques for proprioceptive deficits has previously been restricted to a single timepoint [11, 15], or has been more focused on mathematical frameworks for sensory integration instead [36–39]. Current frameworks for how multisensory integration is carried out include optimal integration theory [40] and divisive normalization [37, 41]. Optimal integration theory states that sensory information is weighted and integrated based on its amount of variance/error, and divisive normalization states that sensory inputs are weighted based on normalized neuronal contribution to local neuronal activity. Some studies on visual/proprioceptive integration have also proposed direction-specific weighting of inputs [36, 39], with proprioception being more accurate in the radial plane and visual information more accurate in the azimuth. However, visual-proprioceptive integration after stroke is not well understood from a behavioural standpoint. Future work should examine individuals who are unable to compensate, such as those in the absent or maladaptive groups. This will help us to better understand, characterize, and treat multisensory integration deficits post-stroke. Eye movements, proprioception, and motor capabilities should also be separately examined with the goal of parsing out integration deficits versus primary oculomotor, primary motor, or primary proprioceptive problems.

Limitations

This study was not designed to test any interventional approaches and instead focused on assessment. The data does not allow us to comment on robotic intervention versus standard of care proprioceptive rehabilitation, nor does it allow any suggestions for intervention implementation or recommendation. However, these are important next steps in the improvement of proprioceptive rehabilitation post-stroke.

Although the individuals in this study did not have obvious oculomotor impairments on a bedside examination, the data collected for this study did not include an instrumented assessment of oculomotor function, which is the next step in understanding the unexpected behaviours seen (especially individuals in the absent and maladaptive compensation groups).

Though we followed participants through the same timeframe post-stroke, we recognize that there was individual variability in the amount and type of rehabilitation the participants received. This may have affected the amount of recovery in our sample and potentially the degree of compensation an individual could display. Although stroke care across Canada follows the Canadian Stroke Best Practise guidelines for the delivery of stroke rehabilitation (https://www.strokebestpractices.ca/recommendations), which defines the base level of care a person should receive, individual care plans can vary.

Another consideration for these results is that the Kinarm allows assessment of proprioception of the shoulder and elbow but does not directly assess the wrist or hand due to the nature of the exoskeleton. We also recognize that the APM task does have motor involvement, though it is a primarily proprioceptive task. However, the match is performed with the unaffected arm, and the CMSA scores of our population do not give us any cause to worry about ipsilateral motor deficits (all CMSA ≥ 6 in unaffected arm and hand).

Conclusion

These results highlight the presence of various phenotypes of compensatory behaviours for proprioceptive deficits during the subacute phase of stroke. Further, they underscore the importance of developing a better understanding of visual-proprioceptive integration post-stroke, both behaviourally and anatomically. Future work should examine visual impairments, proprioceptive deficits, motor functioning, and multisensory integration in stroke survivors and how they affect one another during task performance. An increased understanding of these impairments and their behavioural phenotypes will help development of targeted rehabilitation paradigms to help people reach their maximum recovery potential during the subacute phase of stroke.
