Safe and Localized Lentiviral Gene Delivery via Injectable Mesoporous Scaffolds for Potent Antitumor Immunity
Authors: Thanh Loc Nguyen, Yeon‐ju Kim, Jihye Im, Dong Kwang Min, Hyunsu Shin, Thuy An Trinh, Ngoc Man Phan, Min‐jeong Son, Ji‐yeon Shim, Ye‐lim Lee, Yujeong Ha, Kwang‐il Lim, Jaeyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLentiviral vectors offer potent and sustained gene expression, but their in vivo use remains limited by safety concerns, particularly the risks of systemic dissemination and insertional mutagenesis. To address these limitations, we present a spatially confined lentiviral gene delivery method that enables safe and efficient in vivo targeted transduction of dendritic cells (DCs) via a modular injectable scaffold. By attaching lentiviral vectors encoding a tumor antigen on the surface of mesoporous silica scaffold preloaded with granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor, we create a self‐assembling scaffold that recruits DCs and promotes their localized transduction. This platform minimizes systemic vector dissemination and restricts genome integration to recruited immune cells, enabling prolonged antigen expression and efficient MHC‐I‐mediated antigen presentation. In melanoma models, this method elicits robust antigen‐specific CD8+ T‐cell responses, enhances effector memory generation, and improves antitumor immunity. Combination with immune checkpoint blockade further potentiates therapeutic outcomes. This strategy establishes a versatile and safe in vivo lentiviral delivery system with potential applications across cancer, infectious, and autoimmune diseases.Keywords: cancer immunotherapy, dendritic cells, mesoporous silica, viral transduction

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy seeks to induce durable adaptive immune responses for effective cancer treatment and prevention. A critical step in eliciting tumor‐specific immunity is the effective delivery of tumor antigens to antigen‐presenting cells (APCs), particularly dendritic cells (DCs) [1]. DCs internalize and process tumor antigens and present them on both major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC‐I) and class II (MHC‐II) molecules. This process leads to the activation of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and CD4+ helper T cells, which is critical for triggering effective antitumor responses [2]. Notably, sustained and endogenous antigen expression in DCs, as opposed to transient protein administration, can be particularly advantageous for priming robust and durable immune responses [3].

To achieve prolonged antigen availability, gene‐based antigen delivery has been explored as an alternative to protein‐based vaccination [4]. Among various gene delivery platforms, retroviral and lentiviral vectors have been widely utilized in gene therapy because of their ability to integrate transgenes into the host genome, thereby enabling sustained transgene expression [5]. In addition, these vectors can accommodate large genetic payloads (up to approximately 7 kb) and typically do not elicit significant immune responses [4, 5].

However, the intrinsic tendency of these viral vectors to integrate into the host genome poses significant risks. Retroviruses tend to integrate near gene regulatory regions, whereas lentiviruses preferentially integrate into coding regions, increasing the risk of insertional mutagenesis and potential oncogenesis. Additionally, uncontrolled systemic dissemination of viral particles during in vivo administration can lead to unintended genetic modifications in off‐target tissues, thereby increasing the likelihood of adverse effects [6]. Consequently, retroviral and lentiviral vectors are primarily used for ex vivo cell engineering, which mitigates oncogenic risks but requires complex manufacturing workflows and substantial costs.

To directly address these longstanding and substantial safety and delivery challenges, we developed a modular, spatially confined method for precise in vivo lentiviral gene delivery to DCs. Our strategy integrates lentiviral vector technology with a self‐assembling scaffold composed of high–aspect–ratio mesoporous silica microparticles preloaded with immunomodulatory agents [7, 8]. Upon subcutaneous injection, the scaffold forms a local depot that both recruits DCs and immobilizes lentiviral particles, enabling localized transduction exclusively within the scaffold microenvironment (Figure 1). This approach bypasses the need for ex vivo cell manipulation, directly programming antigen‐specific immune responses within the host.

Schematic of localized in vivo lentiviral transduction of DCs for enhanced cancer immunotherapy. A toll‐like receptor 9 (TLR9) agonist and a DC chemoattractant (granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor, GM‐CSF) were preloaded into high‐aspect‐ratio mesoporous silica rods (MSRs), which were subsequently mixed with lentiviral particles (LVPs) encoding a tumor antigen gene. LVPs were adsorbed onto the MSR surface prior to subcutaneous injection. Upon injection, the subsequent release of the chemoattractant recruited host DCs to the scaffold, enabling localized lentiviral transduction while limiting systemic LVP dissemination. Transduced DCs then matured, presented tumor antigenic epitopes on their MHC molecules, and migrated to draining lymph nodes (dLN), where they activated antigen‐specific CD8+ T cells. This response led to effector memory T‐cell expansion and T‐cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment (TME) in addition to a reduction in the regulatory T cell (Treg) population and the promotion of M1 macrophage polarization. When combined with anti‐PD‐1 immune checkpoint blockade, this approach significantly inhibited the growth and lung metastasis of melanoma.

The immobilization of vectors on the scaffold surface driven by electrostatic interactions between negatively charged silica and positively charged regions within the viral envelope proteins resulted in strong, stable binding with minimal vector desorption under physiological conditions. These interactions preserved lentiviral infectivity while achieving strict spatial confinement of vector activity—a key safety feature for in vivo gene delivery. The therapeutic potential of lentivirally engineered DCs was validated in both prophylactic and therapeutic B16‐OVA melanoma models. Compared to protein antigen–loaded scaffolds, our lentiviral platform induced significantly stronger antigen‐specific T cell responses, significantly delayed or completely prevented tumor development in the prophylactic setting. In therapeutic models, combining our platform with anti–PD‐1 immune checkpoint blockade further suppressed tumor growth, reduced regulatory T cell (Treg) infiltration, and repolarized tumor‐associated macrophages toward a proinflammatory M1 phenotype. These findings highlight the superior immunological potency and therapeutic efficacy of in vivo DC programming using scaffold‐guided lentiviral delivery.

Beyond DCs, our system provides a generalizable strategy for programming other immune or migratory cell populations in vivo. Scaffold architecture and chemokine cues can be modulated to target monocytes, macrophages, or tissue‐resident lymphocytes for applications in infectious disease, autoimmunity, or regenerative medicine. Importantly, this method bypasses the technical complexity and regulatory burden of ex vivo cell therapies by enabling direct, durable, and spatially restricted gene delivery in vivo. By unifying precise vector localization, immunological control, and translational scalability, this platform offers a fundamentally new approach to immune cell engineering and therapeutic gene delivery.

Results and Discussion

Construction of a Lentiviral Vector Carrying the Ovalbumin Gene

We designed a lentiviral vector to deliver the gene encoding ovalbumin (OVA), a well‐established model tumor antigen, into DCs. First, we constructed a plasmid containing the essential elements for producing the vector genomic RNA. This plasmid contained the OVA transgene under the control of a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter to ensure robust expression (Figure 2a,b). Using this plasmid, we generated lentiviral vectors with a third‐generation self‐inactivating packaging system.

For vector production, HEK293T cells, which serve as the packaging cell line, were transfected with four different plasmids: (1) a transfer plasmid encoding the viral genome, (2) a Gag‐Pol plasmid encoding lentiviral structural (Gag) and enzymatic (Pol) proteins, (3) a plasmid encoding the vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein (VSVG), and (4) a plasmid encoding the Rev regulatory protein involved in RNA trafficking and splicing. Lentiviral vectors were harvested at two and three days post‐transfection and concentrated via ultracentrifugation.

The lentiviral vectors carrying the OVA gene (designated as LVPOVA) were subsequently quantified using reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‒qPCR). The vector preparation contained genomic lentiviral particles at a concentration of 1.12 × 1010 particles/mL (Figure S1). To assess the efficiency of gene delivery and expression of LVPOVA, OVA mRNA levels were measured in HEK293T cells transduced with the vectors. For this analysis, total RNA was extracted from the cells 11 days post‐transduction—a time point sufficient for vector genome integration. The cellular RNA molecules were then converted into complementary DNAs (cDNAs), and the resulting DNAs were quantified using RT‒qPCR.

The introduction of LVPOVA into HEK293T cells led to clear expression of the OVA gene (Figure 2c). Increasing the volume of applied vector samples from 0.6 to 11.5 µL resulted in a corresponding increase in OVA mRNA levels, from 3.73 × 103 copies to 5.20 × 104 copies. However, the increase was not linear at the highest dose; OVA mRNA levels plateaued at 11.5 µL, indicating saturation of gene expression at high vector input.

In Vitro Lentiviral Delivery of the OVA Gene Promotes the Immunological Activation of DCs

We hypothesized that delivering antigen‐encoding genes to DCs via lentiviral vectors would increase endogenous antigen production and promote DC maturation, thereby boosting epitope presentation on MHC molecules and strengthening adaptive immune responses. To test this hypothesis, we transduced bone marrow‐derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) in vitro with LVPOVA at varying doses: low (224 particles/cell), medium (672 particles/cell), and high (1,120 particles/cell). Compared with untreated controls, BMDCs treated with all doses exhibited increased expression of CD86 and MHC‐II, key activation markers of DCs, with higher doses inducing more pronounced DC activation (Figure 2d,e). Additionally, high‐dose LVPOVA treatment significantly increased the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), by both BMDCs and RAW 264.7 cells (a macrophage cell line) (Figure 2f–i). These results indicate that LVPOVA treatment promotes DC activation and maturation, which are essential for effective downstream T‐cell priming [9, 10].

Notably, incubation of BMDCs with LVPOVA resulted in robust presentation of the OVA‐derived epitope SIINFEKL on MHC‐I molecules, whereas no such presentation was detected in cells treated with a control LVP encoding green fluorescent protein (GFP) (LVPGFP) (Figure 2j,k). This result demonstrated that tumor antigen‐encoding LVPs can efficiently transduce DCs and enable the presentation of antigenic epitopes on MHC‐I. Successful viral transduction was further confirmed by prominent expression of GFP in BMDCs treated with LVPGFP, whereas no GFP signal was detected in the LVPOVA‐treated cells (Figure 2l,m).

Attachment of LVPs to Mesoporous Silica Microrods (MSRs)

To achieve targeted in vivo lentiviral transduction of host DCs while minimizing systemic vector dissemination, we attached LVPs onto MSRs, which have previously been shown to be biodegradable and biocompatible following subcutaneous injection [11]. Additional histological evaluation in the present study further confirmed the absence of significant organ‐level abnormalities (Figure S2). For efficient visualization of the vector binding to the MSRs, we genetically fused the cytoplasmic domain of VSVG with GFP to create LVP@GFP (Figure 2n). Following a 1‐h incubation, confocal fluorescence imaging revealed strong green fluorescence signals across the MSR surface, indicating successful LVP attachment (Figure 2o).

Both the LVPs and MSRs presented negative charges (−16.1 and −17.4 mV, respectively), and the formation of the LVP‐MSR complex resulted in a further reduction in the surface potential (−36.3 mV) (Figure 2p), supporting effective viral particle binding to the MSRs. Notably, while VSVG is negatively charged overall as an envelope protein used for pseudotyping the lentiviral vectors, it also contains multiple subdomains enriched in positively charged residues (Figure 2q). This structural feature may mediate the efficient binding of the lentiviral vectors to the overall negatively charged MSRs. After an initial wash to remove unbound LVPs, no significant release of the particles was observed during subsequent washes, confirming that the LVPs can be tightly attached to the surface of the MSRs (Figure 2r).

3D MSR Scaffold Enables Localized in Vivo Lentiviral Transduction of Host DCs

Prior to in vivo administration, we assessed the stability of LVPs over time. LVPGFP was incubated at 37°C in serum‐free medium for 1, 3, and 5 days and subsequently used to transduce HEK293T cells. The remaining infectious LVPs after the thermal incubation were quantified by counting GFP‐expressing cells three days post‐transduction. As anticipated, the functional viral particle count decreased progressively with increasing incubation duration (Figure S3). Specifically, incubation for 1 and 3 days led to reductions in viral infectivity of approximately 41% and 86%, respectively. Notably, incubation for 5 days resulted in an approximately 1000‐fold decrease in infectious particle count. Consequently, our results suggest that successful in vivo cell transduction is achievable if LVPs enter target cells within approximately three days post‐administration.

We next aimed to achieve localized in vivo transduction of host DCs. To this end, MSRs were first loaded with GM‐CSF as previously described [7, 12]. Upon subcutaneous injection, the microrods spontaneously assemble in situ into a 3D scaffold at the injection site (Figure 3a), forming a micrometer‐scale macroporous architecture at the injection site (Figure 3b,c). GM‐CSF was incorporated into the MSRs to promote recruitment of host immune cells, including DCs, into the interconnected inter‐particle spaces (Figure 3d,e). Subsequently, LVP@GFP was adsorbed onto the MSR surface, generating LVP@GFP‐bound MSRs (MSR+LVP@GFP). One day after administration via different routes, skin tissues or nodules that formed at the injection site were harvested. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging revealed minimal GFP fluorescence in the mice injected with LVP@GFP (s.c.) alone, whereas an approximately 6.5‐fold increase in the mean GFP radiance signal was detected in the nodules formed at the sites injected with MSR+LVP@GFP (s.c.) compared with LVP@GFP (s.c.) (Figure 3f,g). Notably, GFP‐positive signals were detected in the isolated cells from the dLNs of the MSR+LVP@GFP‐treated mice (Figure 3h), indicating that LVPs are likely captured by recruited DCs and transported to local LNs.

Next, LVPGFP was adsorbed onto the GM‐CSF‐loaded MSR surface, generating LVPGFP‐bound MSRs (M+LVPGFP). Following subcutaneous injection, the MSRs self‐assembled into a 3D scaffold [7, 13], which gradually released GM‐CSF and effectively recruited host DCs to the injection site. This localized microenvironment can facilitate interactions between recruited DCs and surface‐bound LVPs, thereby enhancing localized in vivo lentiviral transduction efficiency. Indeed, flow cytometry analysis confirmed a substantially greater percentage of GFP+ cells in nodules from the group subcutaneously injected with M+LVPGFP than in those from the control group receiving GM‐CSF‐loaded MSRs without lentiviral vectors (Figure 3i,j). Importantly, approximately 60% of the GFP+ cells coexpressed CD11c and CD11b (Figure 3i,k), which are well‐established markers of DCs, indicating successful localized in vivo lentiviral transduction of DCs. These results demonstrate that the MSR scaffold effectively promotes the viral transduction of host DCs. This enhancement is likely attributed to the microenvironment created by the MSRs, which facilitates the recruitment of DCs and enables close contact between the cells and lentiviral vectors.

Activated DCs are expected to migrate to the dLNs, where they present antigenic information to naïve T cells, thereby triggering adaptive immune responses against antigen‐expressing targets (Figure 3l). To assess whether DCs transduced locally by LVPGFP at the MSR scaffold can successfully migrate to the dLNs, we isolated the inguinal LNs near the subcutaneous injection site and performed fluorescence imaging. For comparison, the dLNs from mice injected with LVPGFP alone (without MSRs) were similarly analyzed. The GFP fluorescence intensity in the LNs of the M+LVPGFP group was approximately 3.0‐fold greater than that in the LNs of the control group (naïve animals), whereas the fluorescence intensity in the LNs of the mice receiving LVPGFP alone did not differ significantly from that in the LNs of the control mice (Figure 3m,n). These findings indicate that the MSR scaffolds facilitate DC recruitment to the injection site, enable efficient local transduction of DCs, and promote subsequent DC migration to the dLNs. While sub‐100 nm nanoparticles can passively reach LNs via lymphatic drainage [14], our data suggest that MSR scaffolds support active DC‐mediated trafficking of lentiviral vectors (∼ 100 nm in size) to the dLNs. Furthermore, the dLNs harvested from the M+LVPGFP group were visibly larger than those from the other treatment groups (Figure 3o). Correspondingly, a greater number of immune cells were identified in the enlarged LNs of the M+LVPGFP group, with cell counts approximately 5.2‐ and 1.7‐fold greater than those of the naïve animals and the LVPGFP group, respectively (Figure 3p). These results indicate that the increased immune cell accumulation was driven by localized lentiviral transduction at the MSR scaffold.

To assess long‐term systemic dissemination of lentiviral vectors, vector genome biodistribution was quantified by qPCR in major organs 30 days after administration. This time point was selected to evaluate persistent vector retention rather than transient circulation, which is relevant for biosafety assessment of integrating lentiviral systems. In the liver, no statistically significant differences were observed among groups, although the intravenously injected free vector group showed the highest average genome copies (Figure S4). In the spleen, vector genome levels were significantly higher in the intravenously injected free vector group compared with the MSR‐bound lentiviral vector delivered subcutaneously, while no other pairwise comparisons reached statistical significance. Importantly, subcutaneous delivery—either free or MSR‐bound—resulted in vector genome levels comparable to uninjected controls in both liver and spleen. Collectively, these findings indicate that MSR‐mediated subcutaneous delivery does not increase long‐term systemic dissemination and reduces splenic accumulation relative to intravenous administration, supporting an improved safety profile. Furthermore, MSR‐functionalized LVPs enable localized in vivo DC transduction while limiting systemic spread.

In Vivo Injection of M+LVPOVA Provoked Systemic Adaptive Immunity

After confirming that MSRs can effectively mediate localized in vivo lentiviral transduction of host DCs, we next examined whether this approach could induce systemic adaptive immune responses. To this end, mice were subcutaneously injected with GM‐CSF‐loaded MSRs combined with lentiviral vectors encoding the OVA gene (M+LVPOVA). For comparison, we also included a group receiving MSRs loaded with both GM‐CSF and OVA protein (M+OVA) as a positive control and a PBS‐injected group as a negative control (Figure 4a).

Flow cytometric analysis revealed that approximately 20% of the cells in the nodules of the M+LVPOVA group were CD11c+CD11b+ DCs, whereas 16% of those in the M+OVA group were CD11c+CD11b+ DCs (Figure 4b,c), suggesting that the presence of lentiviral vectors enhances DC recruitment beyond the level observed with protein antigen delivery alone. This effect is likely attributable to the immune system's innate sensitivity to viral components, which may trigger immune activation signals [15].

Given that M+LVPGFP injection increased the number of immune cells in the LNs (Figure 3p), we further assessed the cellular composition of the LNs in the mice treated with M+LVPOVA. The number of CD11c+CD11b+ DCs in the dLNs was 2.8‐fold greater in the M+LVPOVA group than in the M+OVA group (Figure 4d). Similarly, the population of activated DCs—identified by high expression levels of CD86 or MHC‐II—was increased by 2.0‐ and 3.1‐fold, respectively, in the M+LVPOVA group relative to the M+OVA group (Figure 4e,f). These results suggest that lentiviral vectors, when delivered via GM‐CSF‐loaded MSRs, promote DC maturation more effectively than protein antigens do.

Importantly, the M+LVPOVA group also exhibited significantly greater numbers of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (1.7‐fold) and CD4+ helper T cells (2.2‐fold) in the dLNs than did the M+OVA group (Figure 4g,h). These findings indicate that, compared with the protein‐based M+OVA formulation, M+LVPOVA elicited stronger DC and T‐cell responses, underscoring the superior immune‐stimulating capacity of lentiviral vector‐based vaccination. The robust recruitment of host DCs into the MSR scaffold enabled efficient localized lentiviral transduction (Figure 3i–k), thereby expanding the pool of activated DCs and driving potent T‐cell priming in the LNs following the migration of transduced DCs.

To assess functional T‐cell responses post‐vaccination, we performed intracellular cytokine staining for TNF‐α and IFN‐γ and subsequent flow cytometry analysis. Notably, M+LVPOVA treatment significantly increased the percentages of TNF‐α‐ and IFN‐γ‐producing CD8+ T cells compared with those in the control group, whereas M+OVA injection elicited minimal cytokine production (Figure 4i,j; Figure S5). Furthermore, M+LVPOVA treatment induced a marked expansion of SIINFEKL‐specific CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, which was not observed in the M+OVA group (Figure 4k; Figure S6). These findings demonstrate that M+LVPOVA effectively induces robust antigen‐specific cytotoxic CD8+ T‐cell activation.

To evaluate whether the vaccination strategy promotes memory T‐cell formation, which is essential for long‐term immune protection [16], we analyzed the expression of memory‐associated surface markers of T cells. A significant increase in effector memory T cells (CD44+CD62L−) was observed in both CD4+ (Figure 4l,n) and CD8+ T‐cell populations (Figure 4m,o) in the M+LVPOVA and M+OVA groups, with a more pronounced effect in M+LVPOVA‐treated mice. In contrast, no notable changes were detected in central memory T‐cell populations (CD44+CD62L+) across the groups (Figure S7). These results suggest that sustained antigen presentation by lentiviral vectors within the MSR scaffolds enhances the generation of effector memory T cells and supports durable immune responses. Importantly, histological analysis revealed no significant pathological abnormalities in major organs following the subcutaneous administration of any vaccine formulation (Figure S8), indicating that both LVPs and MSRs are well tolerated in vivo.

Incorporation of a TLR9 Agonist Enhances the Antitumor Efficacy of M+LVPOVA Vaccination

To further enhance the immunogenicity of the M+LVPOVA vaccine, we incorporated cytosine‐phosphate‐guanine oligodeoxynucleotides (CpG‐ODNs), a TLR9 agonist known to promote DC maturation and induce Th1‐biased immune responses [17], into MSRs along with GM‐CSF, yielding the M+C+LVPOVA formulation. CpG‐ODN and GM‐CSF were previously shown to exhibit sustained release from MSRs using the same formulation platform, with an initial burst followed by prolonged release [18]. This release profile is consistent with the progressive recruitment of CD11c+ DCs observed over 7–14 days after subcutaneous injection in the present study. The prophylactic efficacy of both M+LVPOVA and M+C+LVPOVA formulations was assessed in a B16‐OVA melanoma challenge model (Figure 4p). Compared with no treatment, the subcutaneous administration of either formulation significantly suppressed tumor growth (Figure 4q). Notably, M+C+LVPOVA vaccination conferred superior tumor protection, with only 1 out of 6 mice developing tumor on day 20 after tumor innoculation, compared with 3 of 6 in the M+LVPOVA group and 7 out of 7 in the untreated cohort (Figure 4r).

Additionally, prophylactic vaccination with M+C+LVPOVA did not significantly alter myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSC) populations within the tumor (Figure S9). Instead, vaccination markedly enhanced infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes into the TME. A modest shift toward M1‐type inflammatory macrophages was also observed. Injection of M+C+LVPOVA into healthy animals altered the immune cell composition in the dLNs, with a significant increase in the proportion of B cells (Figure S10). This observation suggests a potential link between B‐cell expansion in dLNs and enhanced T‐cell responses in tumors.

To further disentangle the immunostimulatory effects of viral particles from antigen‐specific responses, we conducted a prophylactic study comparing M+C+LVPOVA and M+C+LVPGFP in the B16‐OVA melanoma model (Figure S11). M+C+LVPOVA vaccination strongly suppressed tumor growth, whereas the antitumor effects of M+C+LVPGFP vaccination were only partial and variable. Together, these results indicate that antigen encoding within the lentiviral vector is critical for eliciting robust and tumor‐specific immunity. These findings support the concept that the combination of vector‐mediated antigen expression and innate immune stimulation within the MSR platform synergistically enhances tumor antigen‐specific adaptive immune responses. Thus, the M+C+LVPOVA formulation represents a promising candidate for prophylactic cancer vaccination strategies.

The M+C+LVPOVA Vaccine Synergizes with Immune Checkpoint Blockade to Suppress Lung Metastasis

Building upon the robust adaptive immune responses elicited by the M+C+LVPOVA vaccine (hereafter referred to as Vax), we next evaluated its therapeutic potential in a metastatic cancer model. To simulate pulmonary metastasis, B16‐OVA melanoma cells were intravenously administered to mice one day prior to treatment initiation. The mice then received one of the following three treatments: (1) subcutaneous injection of Vax alone, (2) Vax combined with intraperitoneal administration of an anti‐PD‐1 antibody (Vax+a‐PD1), or (3) the anti‐PD‐1 antibody alone (a‐PD1) (Figure 5a). While a‐PD1 monotherapy moderately reduced the metastatic burden, both Vax and Vax+a‐PD1 significantly suppressed tumor growth and progression in the lungs (Figure 5b,c). The enhanced antitumor efficacy of Vax is attributed to potent vaccine‐induced cellular immune responses (as shown in Figure 4), while the PD‐1 blockade (a‐PD1) likely complements this effect by relieving T‐cell exhaustion [19]. Histological analysis via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining confirmed a pronounced reduction in the number of metastatic tumor clusters within the lungs of the mice that received the Vax+a‐PD1 treatment (Figure 5d).

The numbers in the flow cytometry plots indicate the percentage of gated cell populations. The data in panels (c, e, g, i) are presented as the means ± SDs. Statistical significance was determined using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test for panel (c) and Dunnett's multiple comparison test for panels (e, g, i). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Tregs play a crucial role in cancer immunotherapy, as they can hinder antitumor immunity and promote tumor growth by suppressing effector T cells [20]. Immunophenotyping of lung tissues revealed that Vax treatment significantly reduced the percentage of Tregs (Figure 5e,f), an effect not observed with a‐PD1 monotherapy. Given the immunosuppressive role of Tregs within the TME, a reduction in their population suggests that Vax reprograms the TME to favor antitumor immunity.

To further assess whether Vax promotes the formation of protective immunological memory, we analyzed lung‐resident memory T cells, as the lung is increasingly recognized as an important lymphoid site for immune surveillance and long‐term immunity [21]. Both CD4+ and CD8+ effector memory T‐cell populations were significantly increased in the lungs of mice treated with either Vax or Vax+a‐PD1, whereas no increase was observed with a‐PD1 monotherapy (Figure 5g–j). Notably, there was no significant difference in the induction of memory T cells between the Vax and Vax+a‐PD1 groups, suggesting that Vax is the primary driver of memory T‐cell differentiation. This finding is consistent with the notion that PD‐1 blockade primarily reinvigorates exhausted T cells rather than initiating de novo memory T‐cell formation [15, 22]. Together, these results demonstrate that Vax effectively suppresses lung metastasis through the induction of local effector memory T cells and reprogramming of the TME and that these effects can be further potentiated by immune checkpoint inhibition.

M+C+LVPOVA Vaccine Synergizes with PD‐1 Blockade to Treat Solid Melanoma via TME Modulation

Encouraged by the potent efficacy of Vax+a‐PD1 therapy in suppressing lung metastasis, we next evaluated its therapeutic potential in a solid melanoma model. Tumor‐bearing mice were treated with two doses of Vax and four doses of an anti‐PD‐1 antibody following the regimen (Figure 6a). Notably, the combination therapy significantly delayed tumor progression compared with that in the untreated control group (Figure 6b).

Alteration of the TME following LVP‐based scaffold vaccination combined with PD‐1 blockade. (a) Experimental design: Mice bearing B16‐OVA tumors were treated with the LVP‐based scaffold vaccine (Vax) on days 5 and 12 post‐tumor inoculation in combination with anti‐PD1 antibody administration (n = 4 for the untreated group, n = 5 for the Vax+a‐PD1 group). (b) Tumor volumes were measured over time to assess the treatment efficacy. (c) Frequencies of Foxp3+ Tregs within the TME. (d, e) Quantification of tumor‐infiltrating CD4+ (d) and CD8+ (e) T cells per milligram of tumor tissue. (f) M1/M2 macrophage polarization was assessed by the ratio of CD86 to CD206 expression within the F4/80+ macrophage population. (g, h) Concentrations of anti‐OVA IgG1 and IgG2a antibodies in the serum of the mice at the study endpoint. The data in panels (b–h) are presented as the means ± SDs. Statistical analysis was performed using an unpaired Student's t test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess the impact of the therapy on the TME, we analyzed immune cell populations within the excised tumors. The Vax+a‐PD1 treatment resulted in a marked reduction in the percentage of Tregs (Figure 6c), mirroring the results observed in the lung metastasis model (Figure 5e). Furthermore, the number of tumor‐infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells per milligram of tumor tissue was significantly elevated in the vaccinated mice (Figure 6d,e). Although no significant differences were observed in the M1 (F4/80+CD86+) and M2 (F4/80+CD206+) macrophage populations between the vaccinated and untreated groups (Figure S12), the ratio of proinflammatory M1 to immunosuppressive M2 macrophages was significantly increased in the treated tumors (Figure 6f). This finding indicates a shift in macrophage polarization toward an antitumor phenotype (from M2 to M1 macrophages). Additionally, serum analysis revealed elevated levels of anti‐OVA IgG1 antibodies in the Vax+a‐PD1 group, with no corresponding increase in the level of anti‐OVA IgG2a (Figure 6g,h), implying that the treatment also induced a concurrent tumor‐specific humoral immune response.

Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed a significant prolongation of survival following treatment with Vax+a‐PD1; however, therapeutic efficacy declined sharply at late stages of disease (Figure S13). Monotherapy with either a‐PD1 or Vax resulted in limited and heterogeneous tumor growth suppression, whereas combination treatment with Vax and a‐PD1 led to consistent and pronounced tumor control (Figure S14). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the combination of lentiviral vector‐based scaffold vaccination and PD‐1 immune checkpoint blockade induces multifaceted antitumor responses in solid tumors. These include TME remodeling, increased T‐cell infiltration, repolarization of macrophages toward the M1 phenotype, and the induction of antigen‐specific humoral immunity, highlighting the therapeutic potential of this synergistic strategy in cancer immunotherapy.

Antigen format and presentation efficiency critically determine vaccine‐induced antitumor immunity. Although diverse antigen formats—including full‐length proteins, peptides, tumor lysates, and viral particles—have been explored in cancer vaccine development [23], their efficacy is often limited by poor antigen persistence, inefficient MHC‐I presentation, and suboptimal DC activation. Protein‐ and peptide‐based vaccines predominantly engage exogenous processing pathways that inefficiently access MHC‐I, resulting in weak CD8+ T cell responses [24]. In contrast, gene‐based vaccines such as DNA and mRNA vaccines promote endogenous antigen expression and improved cross‐presentation [25, 26], yet sustained antigen expression remains challenging without repeated administration [27]. To overcome these limitations, we developed a modular, scaffold‐based lentiviral delivery platform that enables stable in situ transduction of DCs while minimizing systemic vector dissemination. Lentiviral vectors can transduce nondividing cells and integrate transgenes into the host genome, supporting durable antigen expression [28, 29], and their immobilization on mesoporous silica scaffolds co‐loaded with GM‐CSF and adjuvants enabled spatially confined gene delivery and enhanced immune cell recruitment of target immune cells. This approach overcomes the trade‐off between transduction efficiency and biosafety that has limited the in vivo use of integrating vectors. By restricting transduction to immune cells physically localized within the scaffold, we achieved long‐term antigen expression while minimizing off‐target risk.

This confined in vivo transduction strategy facilitates localized genomic programming of DCs, leading to efficient CD4+ and CD8+ T cell priming, expansion of effector memory populations, and robust tumor control in both prophylactic and therapeutic melanoma models. Combination with checkpoint blockade further enhanced therapeutic efficacy, particularly in suppressing metastatic progression and reshaping the tumor microenvironment. Mechanistically, scaffold‐mediated recruitment and retention of host DCs increased local vector exposure and antigen presentation without the need for ex vivo manipulation. Beyond DC targeting, the modularity of the platform allows adaptation to other immune cell populations, offering a broadly applicable strategy for spatially defined immune programming in cancer and other immune‐mediated diseases.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be noted. First, direct tracking of transduced DC migration and antigen persistence following local transduction would provide deeper mechanistic insight into subsequent T‐cell activation. Second, given the increased abundance of B cells in dLNs (Figure S10), further characterization of vaccine‐induced changes in B‐cell phenotype and function will be important to better define their roles. Third, although combining Vax with immune checkpoint blockade enhanced early antitumor responses, this effect was not fully sustained at later stages (Figure S13). This attenuation may reflect tumor‐mediated immunosuppressive mechanisms, such as T‐cell exhaustion or compensatory inhibitory pathways observed during tumor progression [30, 31]. Together, these limitations highlight the need to further optimize combinatorial strategies—particularly treatment regimens and agent sequencing—to minimize overlapping toxicities, maximize synergy, and limit resistance, thereby achieving durable tumor control [32].

Conclusion

The platform described in this study offers a modular strategy for spatially restricted gene delivery in vivo, with demonstrated efficacy and safety in dendritic cell targeting. Its architecture may be extended to engineer other immune or stromal cell populations by tuning scaffold composition and vector cargo. Combination of non‐integrating viral vectors, multiplexed genetic payloads, or stimulus‐responsive scaffold components could further enhance control and broaden applicability. As an alternative to ex vivo cell engineering, this in situ programming approach holds promise not only for cancer vaccination but also for applications in infectious disease, autoimmunity, and tissue regeneration. Continued development along these lines could help re‐establish lentiviral vectors as viable tools for clinical gene delivery in anatomically and immunologically defined settings.

Experimental Section

Materials

4′,6‐Diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI), 10% formalin solution, tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA), ionomycin, Pluronic P123 (average Mn = 5800), ammonium fluoride (NH4F), and ovalbumin (≥ 98%) were purchased from Sigma‒Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). RPMI 1640 medium, collagenase type I, and collagenase type IV were obtained from Gibco (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Recombinant murine GM‐CSF was purchased from PeproTech (Rocky Hill, NJ, USA). DNase I and bovine serum albumin (BSA) were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Murine class B CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (CpG‐ODNs, sequence: 5′‐TCCATGACGTTCCTGACGTT‐3′) were synthesized by Genotech (Daejeon, South Korea).

Antibodies

eFluor 450‐conjugated antibodies against CD86, CD25, IFN‐γ, and CD44; fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)‐conjugated antibodies against MHC‐II, CD3, CD206, and TNF‐α; allophycocyanin (APC)‐conjugated antibodies against CD11c, CD8, and Foxp3; R‐phycoerythrin (PE)‐conjugated antibodies against CD11b, F4/80, CD62L, and CD45; PE‐Cyanine7 (PE‐Cy7)‐conjugated antibodies against CD4 and the OVA257–264 peptide (SIINFEKL) bound to H‐2Kb were all purchased from eBioscience (San Diego, CA, USA). Fc receptor (FcR) blocking reagent was obtained from Miltenyi Biotec (Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). The PE‐conjugated H‐2Kb OVA tetramer (SIINFEKL) was purchased from MBL (Nagoya, Japan).

Construction of Plasmids for the Production of Lentiviral Vectors

The OVA gene was inserted into the transfer vector plasmid of a third‐generation lentiviral vector system [33, 34, 35]. The OVA coding sequence was amplified from the pcDNA3‐OVA plasmid using Phusion High‐Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) with the following primers: OVA_forward: 5′‐TTTTACCGGTATGGGCTCCATCGG‐3′ and OVA_reverse: 5′‐TTTTGAATTCTTAAGGGGAAACACATCTGC‐3′. PCR amplification was performed on a T100 thermal cycler (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 98°C for 2 min; 30 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 12 s, annealing at 56°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 20 s/kb; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 5 min and holding at 10°C for 5 min. The amplified OVA gene fragment was digested with AgeI and EcoRI and ligated into the corresponding sites of the pFCGW lentiviral transfer vector (5′LTR‐HIV‐1 flap‐pCMV‐eGFP‐WPRE‐3′LTR), replacing the eGFP gene sequence. The resulting construct, named pFCOW, encoded the OVA antigen under the control of the CMV promoter (5′LTR‐HIV‐1 flap‐pCMV‐OVA‐WPRE‐3′LTR) (Figure 2a). To generate fluorescently labeled LVPs, an envelope protein expression plasmid was engineered by fusing the gene encoding mAzamiGreen (mAG) — a green fluorescent protein — to the DNA site corresponding to the C‐terminus of VSV‐G. The mAG gene sequence was amplified by PCR using Phusion High‐Fidelity DNA Polymerase with the following primers: mAG_forward: 5′‐AAAATTAATTAATATGGTGAGCGTGATCAAGCCCG‐3′ and mAG_reverse: 5′‐AAAAGCGGCCGCTTACTTGGCCTGGCTGGGCAG‐3′. The PCR conditions were identical to those used for OVA gene amplification. The amplified mAG gene was cloned and inserted into the PacI and NotI restriction sites of the pcDNA IVS VSVG plasmid, yielding a DNA construct encoding the fluorescently tagged VSV‐G used for the production of labeled lentiviral vectors.

Cell Culture and Lentiviral Vector Packaging

HEK293T cells were cultured in Iscove's modified Dulbecco's medium (IMDM; Gibco Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco Life Technologies) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Gibco Life Technologies) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For lentiviral vector packaging, HEK293T cells were seeded in 10 cm dishes and cotransfected using a calcium phosphate‐based method with the following plasmids: 10 µg of the vector genome plasmid (pFCOW or pFCGW), 5 µg of the Gag‐Pol polyprotein plasmid (pMDLg/pRRE), 3.5 µg of the envelope protein plasmid (pcDNA IVS VSVG), and 1.5 µg of the splicing regulatory protein plasmid (pRSV‐Rev plasmid). At 14 h post‐transfection, the cells were washed twice with PBS to remove residual plasmids, and replenished with fresh medium. The lentiviral vector‐containing supernatant was harvested at two and three days post‐transfection, filtered through a 0.45 µm syringe filter, and concentrated by ultracentrifugation in 20% (w/v) sucrose using an SW32Ti rotor (Optima XE‐100; Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) at 24 000 rpm for 2 h at 4°C. For LVP@GFP production, the same protocol was followed, except that the envelope plasmid used was pcDNA IVS VSVG + mAG, which encodes the mAG‐tagged VSV‐G fusion protein.

Titration of Lentiviral Particles

The concentration of the lentiviral particles in samples was quantified using RT‒qPCR, which specifically targeted the minus‐strand strong‐stop DNA region—a component of the viral genomic cDNA within the long terminal repeat (LTR) (Figure 2b). To generate a standard curve, a tenfold serial dilution of the lentiviral vector transfer plasmid pFCOW (ranging from 1 to 10− 5 ng) was prepared to establish a correlation between the DNA concentration and quantification cycle (Cq) values. Similarly, tenfold serial dilutions of the viral vector stock (LVPOVA) were analyzed to determine the genomic vector particle concentration. Each sample was mixed with TOPreal SYBR Green qPCR PreMIX (low ROX; Enzynomics, Daejeon, South Korea), and the primers used are listed in Table S1. RT‒qPCR was conducted on a CFX Connect Real‐Time PCR System (Bio‐Rad) using the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min; followed by 55 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, annealing at 55°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was performed by gradually increasing the temperature from 50°C to 95°C at 1°C per second. Amplification curves were generated, and Cq values were automatically calculated using the CFX Manager software. The genomic titer of the lentiviral vectors was determined by plotting sample Cq values against the standard curve generated from the analysis of the serially diluted plasmid DNA molecules. To determine the transducing titer of the LVPGFP samples, HEK293T cells were transduced, and the percentage of eGFP‐positive cells was quantified seven days post transduction using a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The transducing titer, expressed as transducing units per milliliter (T.U./mL), was calculated using the following formula: T.U./mL = {(Number of cells at transduction × % eGFP‐positive cells) × 1000}/Volume of viral vector sample used at transduction (µL).

mRNA Quantification

To evaluate the antigen expression capacity of LVPOVA following cellular transduction, HEK293T cells (7.95 × 104 cells) were transduced at multiplicities of infection (MOIs) of 0.5, 2, and 10 (corresponding to 0.6, 2.4, and 12.0 µL of the vector sample, respectively), as calculated on the basis of the LVPGFP titers. Three days post‐transduction, the cells were transferred to 6‐well plates. On day 11 post‐transduction, 3 × 106 cells were harvested, and total RNA was extracted using the Direct‐zol RNA Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). One microgram of the extracted RNA was reverse‐transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the TOPscript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Enzynomics, Daejeon, South Korea) on a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio‐Rad) with the following thermal program: 25°C for 10 min (for random hexamer priming), 50°C for 60 min (cDNA synthesis), and 95°C for 5 min (enzyme inactivation). RT‒qPCR was performed with 50 ng of cDNA per reaction for both untransduced and transduced HEK293T cells, with six technical replicates per condition. Primer sets specific for OVA mRNA and human GAPDH mRNA (a housekeeping control, GenBank No. NM_002046.7) were utilized. The reaction mixture contained TOPreal SYBR Green qPCR PreMIX (low ROX; Enzynomics) and the following primers: OVA_F: 5′‐GCCAATGAGAACATCTTCTACTGC‐3′, OVA_R: 5′‐CCTGGTGCTGTCTTTTGCA‐3′, GAPDH_F: 5′‐CTGCCACCCAGAAGACTGTGGATG‐3′, GAPDH_R: 5′‐CCAGTAGAGGCAGGGATGATGTTCTG‐3′. RT‒qPCR was conducted using the CFX Connect Real‐Time System (Bio‐Rad) under the same cycling conditions described above. Relative expression of OVA mRNA was quantified using the ΔΔCq method and normalized to GAPDH expression, utilizing the CFX Maestro Software (Bio‐Rad).

MSR Preparation and Characterization

MSRs were prepared following a previously reported method [13]. Briefly, 4 g of the amphiphilic block copolymer Pluronic P123 was dissolved in 130 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, 20 mL of hydrochloric acid (HCl) and 46 mg of ammonium fluoride (NH4F) were added to the solution. Next, 8.6 g of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS; 98% purity) was introduced, and the mixture was stirred at 40°C for 20 h. The resulting mixture was then aged at 100°C for 24 h. The reaction product was filtered through a 50 µm sieve wetted with ethanol and dried to obtain MSR powder. Surfactant removal was achieved by calcination at 550°C for 5 h to extract the Pluronic P123 template. The morphology and structure of the formed MSRs were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM‐2100F, JEOL), scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JSM‐7000F, JEOL), and optical microscopy. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was used to determine the surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution. The zeta potentials of the MSRs and LVP were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK).

Microscopy

A total of 5 mg of MSRs was mixed with 180 µL of LVP@GFP solution. Immediately after mixing, 10 µL of the mixture was placed on a microscope slide (Marienfeld, Germany) and covered with a 0.13–0.16‐mm‐thick cover glass. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a TCS SP8 confocal fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with a 100× Olympus UPlanSApo oil immersion objective lens. Images were acquired using the LAS X software (Leica Microsystems) and subsequently processed using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Animals

Female C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were obtained from OrientBio (Seongnam, South Korea) and housed in a specific pathogen‐free facility throughout the study. Mice were acclimatized for at least one week before the initiation of experimental procedures. The following housing conditions were maintained: a standard temperature of 23°C ± 2°C and a relative humidity of 40%–60%. Additionally, the mice were housed under a 12‐h light/dark cycle (lights from 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m.). All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sungkyunkwan University (SKKUIACUC approval no. 2023‐08‐20‐1 and no. 2025‐09‐05‐1).

BMDC Culture and Activation Study

BMDCs were prepared by harvesting bone marrow cells from the femurs of female C57BL/6 mice. The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum (HI‐FBS), 50 µm β‐mercaptoethanol, 1% penicillin‒streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL GM‐CSF. The culture medium was changed on days 3 and 6 following isolation. On days 6 to 8 of differentiation, nonadherent and loosely adherent cells were collected for downstream in vitro experiments. To assess DC activation and lentiviral transduction, BMDCs were seeded in 6‐well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and incubated with three different doses of LVPOVA (low, medium, and high doses, corresponding to 224, 672, and 1120 particles per cell, respectively) for five days. After incubation, the cells were harvested using a cell scraper and washed with fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer, and the cell culture supernatant was collected for cytokine quantification by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). To minimize nonspecific binding of the antibodies, FcR blocking reagent was applied for 10 min at 4°C. The cells were subsequently washed and stained with fluorochrome‐conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD11c, CD86, and MHC‐II for 20 min at 4°C in the dark. After staining, the cells were washed and resuspended in FACS buffer for flow cytometry analysis. IL‐6 and TNF‐α levels in the cell culture supernatant were quantified using DuoSet ELISA kits (R&D Systems) following the manufacturer's instructions.

In Vitro Transduction of BMDCs

On day 6 of differentiation, BMDCs were seeded at 106 cells/mL per well in 6‐well plates and subsequently treated with either LVPOVA (6.72 × 108 particles/mL) or LVPGFP (3.36 × 108 particles/mL). Seven days post‐treatment, the cells were harvested using a cell scraper, washed with FACS buffer, and incubated with FcR blocking reagent for 10 min at 4°C. The cells were then stained with fluorochrome‐conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD11c and SIINFEKL–H2Kb for 20 min at 4°C in the dark. Flow cytometry was performed to assess GFP expression and SIINFEKL presentation in DCs.

In Vivo Scaffold Formation

MSRs (1 mg; 40 mg/mL in PBS) were incubated with GM‐CSF (1 µg; 1 mg/mL in PBS) for 6 h at room temperature under gentle agitation, followed by lyophilization. The dried formulation was mixed with an additional 4 mg of MSRs and injected subcutaneously into the mouse flank using a 17G needle. Five days post‐injection, the assembled nodules were harvested and analyzed by SEM and optical microscopy. Using the same procedure, RITC‐labeled MSRs were prepared for fluorescence imaging. Retrieved nodules were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound, cryosectioned, stained with DAPI, and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Ti‐U, Nikon).

Ex Vivo Imaging of Scaffolds or Skin Tissues

Five days after subcutaneous injection of PBS (control), subcutaneous or intravenous injection of LVP@GFP (2.5 × 109 particles), or subcutaneous injection of MSR+LVP@GFP into mice, the skin tissues (or nodules) at the injection sites and the adjacent inguinal LNs were carefully excised. The harvested tissue samples were immediately subjected to fluorescence imaging using an in vivo imaging system (Spectral Lago X, Spectral Instruments Imaging) to detect GFP signals. The fluorescence intensity, measured as mean GFP radiance (photons/sec/cm2/sr), was used to evaluate the extent and localization of viral particles in both skin tissues and dLNs. Single immune cells from LNs were isolated, and GFP expression was observed using a confocal microscope.

Cell Analysis of the Scaffold

Nodules were excised and mechanically minced into small fragments after injection of the GM‐CSF‐loaded MSRs (control) and M+LVPGFP (2.5 × 109 particles) into mice. The tissue fragments were then digested in RPMI medium containing 1 mg/mL collagenase type IV at 37°C for 15 min. The resulting cell suspensions were filtered through 40‐µm cell strainers to isolate individual cells and then washed with cold PBS. To analyze DC recruitment and in vivo transduction by LVPGFP, the isolated cells were first incubated with FcR blocking reagent for 10 min at 4°C to minimize nonspecific antibody binding. The cells were subsequently stained with fluorochrome‐conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD11c and CD11b for 20 min at 4°C in the dark. Flow cytometry analysis was performed, and GFP+ cells were identified by gating relative to control samples derived from scaffolds loaded with GM‐CSF only.

Ex Vivo Imaging of dLNs

Five days after subcutaneous injection of PBS (control), LVPGFP (2.16 × 109 particles), or MSR+LVPGFP, dLNs were harvested and immediately imaged using an IVIS (Lumina XR, PerkinElmer). Fluorescence intensity was quantified as total radiant efficiency of GFP. LN area was analyzed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA), and cell numbers were determined using a MACSQuant VYB flow cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany).

Vaccine Preparation and Immunization

To prepare the vaccine formulations, 1 mg of MSRs (at a concentration of 40 mg/mL in PBS) was incubated with 1 µg of GM‐CSF (1 mg/mL in PBS) for 6 h at room temperature under gentle shaking, followed by lyophilization. Specifically, 100 µg of OVA (10 mg/mL in PBS) was loaded onto 4 mg of MSRs (40 mg/mL in PBS) under the same incubation conditions. Similarly, 100 µg of CpG‐ODNs (4 mg/mL in ultra‐purified DI water) was adsorbed onto 4 mg of MSRs (40 mg/mL in ultrapure deionized water). All formulations were lyophilized after 6 h incubation and stored at −20°C until use. For subcutaneous vaccination, the following formulations were prepared: M+OVA vaccine: 1 mg of GM‐CSF‐loaded MSRs and 4 mg of OVA‐loaded MSRs were mixed thoroughly in 180 µL of PBS and injected subcutaneously into the flanks of the mice using a standard 17G needle. M+LVPOVA and M+LVPGFP vaccines: 1 mg of GM‐CSF‐loaded MSRs was mixed with 4 mg of blank MSRs and 180 µL of either LVPOVA or LVPGFP. The mixture was administered subcutaneously using a 17G needle. M+C+LVPOVA vaccine (Vax): 1 mg of GM‐CSF‐loaded MSRs was combined with 4 mg of CpG‐loaded MSRs and 180 µL of LVPOVA. This formulation was also injected subcutaneously into the mouse flank with a 17G needle.

Tissue Processing

At the designated time points, the inguinal LNs, lungs, and spleens were excised and mechanically dissociated in cold PBS. The resulting cell suspensions were then filtered through 40‐µm cell strainers, spun down, and washed with cold PBS. Red blood cells in the spleen and lung samples were lysed with ACK lysis buffer (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland). Following lysis, LN cells and splenocytes were counted and prepared for flow cytometric analysis through surface marker staining.

Analysis of LN Cells

Single‐cell suspensions prepared from inguinal LNs were incubated with FcR blocking reagent for 10 min at 4°C. The cells were then stained with fluorochrome‐conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD11c, CD11b, CD86, MHC‐II, CD3, CD4, and CD8 for 20 min at 4°C in the dark. After staining, the cells were washed, resuspended in FACS buffer, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Analysis of Cellular Response in Spleen

For intracellular cytokine analysis, 2 × 106 splenocytes were stimulated in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with ionomycin (1 µg/mL) and phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA; 100 ng/mL) at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 5 h. BD GolgiStop (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA; Cat# 554724) was added during the final 4 h to inhibit protein transport. After stimulation, the cells were washed and incubated with FcR blocking reagent for 10 min at 4°C to minimize nonspecific binding. Subsequently, surface staining was performed with fluorochrome‐conjugated antibodies against CD4 and CD8 for 20 min at 4°C in the dark. The cells were then fixed and permeabilized using the Intracellular Fixation & Permeabilization Buffer Set (eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA; Cat# 88‐8824‐00), followed by intracellular staining with antibodies against IFN‐γ and TNF‐α. The cells were washed again and resuspended in FACS buffer for flow cytometry analysis. To analyze OVA‐specific CD8+ T cells, splenocytes were stimulated with 100 µg/mL OVA protein for 3 days. After incubation, the cells were washed and stained with H‐2Kb OVA tetramer‐SIINFEKL at 4°C for 40 min. Surface staining with anti‐CD3 and anti‐CD8 antibodies was then performed, and the cells were analyzed by flow cytometry to quantify antigen‐specific CD8+ T cell populations.

In Vivo Antitumor Study

For the prophylactic tumor model, C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously vaccinated with M+LVPOVA or M+C+LVPOVA or left untreated. Seven days post‐vaccination, the mice were challenged subcutaneously with 1 × 106 B16‐OVA melanoma cells per mouse. For the therapeutic tumor model, mice were first inoculated with B16‐OVA cells and then treated subcutaneously with the M+C+LVPOVA vaccine (Vax) on days 5 and 12 post‐inoculation. In addition, an anti‐PD‐1 monoclonal antibody (a‐PD1; 100 µg per mouse) was administered intraperitoneally on days 5, 9, 13, and 17 post‐inoculation. The tumor size was measured over time using a caliper, and the tumor volume (V) was calculated using the following standard formula: V = (length × width2)/2. The therapeutic study was terminated on day 19 after tumor inoculation. Tumors were excised for TME analysis, and blood was collected for evaluation of humoral responses. To minimize animal suffering, the mice were humanely euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation (flow rate: 3 L/min) when the longest tumor dimension exceeded 18 mm.

In Vivo Lung Metastasis Study

To establish a lung metastasis model, C57BL/6 mice were intravenously injected with 1 × 106 B16‐OVA melanoma cells one day prior to the initiation of treatment. The Vax was administered subcutaneously on days 1 and 8 following tumor cell inoculation. Anti‐PD‐1 antibody (BioXCell, Lebanon, NH, USA; Cat# BE0146) was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 100 µg per mouse on days 1, 5, 9, and 13 post‐tumor cell inoculation. On day 15, the mice were sacrificed, the lungs were harvested and photographed, and the number of tumor nodules on the lung surface was counted. Each lung sample was bisected: one half was fixed for histological analysis by H&E staining, and the other half was processed for cell isolation to analyze infiltrating T‐cell populations.

Analysis of Cellular Compartments in the TME

Following the therapeutic study, the tumors were excised, weighed, and minced into small fragments. The fragments were enzymatically digested in PBS containing 1.5 mg/mL collagenase types I and IV and 0.2 mg/mL DNase I at 37°C for 30 min in a 24‐well plate. The digested suspension was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer, washed with cold PBS, treated with ACK lysis buffer (Lonza) to remove red blood cells, and filtered again through a 40 µm strainer. The resulting single‐cell suspensions were resuspended in FACS buffer for counting and flow cytometry staining. To analyze the Treg population and macrophage polarization in the TME, the cells were incubated with FcR blocking reagent for 10 min at 4°C, followed by staining with fluorochrome‐conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD4, F4/80, CD86, and CD206 for 20 min at 4°C in the dark. The cells were then fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (eBioscience; Cat# 00‐5523‐00), followed by intracellular staining with a fluorochrome‐conjugated anti‐Foxp3 antibody for 40 min at 4°C. For the detection of tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes, the cells were incubated with FcR blocking reagent, stained for the surface markers CD45, CD4, and CD8, and then analyzed by flow cytometry. To assess Treg populations in the metastatic lungs, single‐cell suspensions were prepared and treated with FcR blocking reagent for 10 min at 4°C. The cells were then stained with fluorochrome‐conjugated anti‐CD3 and anti‐CD4 antibodies for 20 min at 4°C, fixed, permeabilized with the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set, and stained with a fluorochrome‐conjugated anti‐Foxp3 antibody for 40 min at 4°C prior to flow cytometric analysis.

Analysis of Memory T Cells in Spleen and Metastatic Lungs

Single‐cell suspensions were prepared from spleens and metastatic lungs at a density of 1 × 106 cells per sample. The cells were first incubated with FcR blocking reagent for 10 min at 4°C in the dark to prevent nonspecific binding. Subsequently, surface staining was performed using fluorochrome‐conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD3, CD4, CD8, CD62L, and CD44 for 20 min at 4°C in the dark. After staining, the cells were washed, resuspended in FACS buffer, and analyzed by flow cytometry to assess the percentages of effector and central memory T‐cell subsets.

Humoral Immune Responses

Mice were anesthetized, and blood was collected via submandibular vein puncture. The samples were left at room temperature for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 20 min at 4°C. The resulting serum was collected and stored at −80°C until analysis. The levels of anti‐OVA IgG1 and anti‐OVA IgG2a antibodies were quantified using mouse anti‐OVA IgG1 and mouse anti‐OVA IgG2a antibody assay kits (Chondrex, Inc., Woodinville, WA, USA; Cat# 3013 and 3015) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Histological Study

Organs were fixed in 10% formalin buffer for at least two days. Following fixation, the samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 4 µm‐thick slices along the coronal plane. The tissue sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin and examined using an optical microscope (ECLIPSE Ti‐U, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Flow Cytometry

All stained samples were analyzed using a MACSQuant VYB flow cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). Forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) parameters were used to exclude dead cells and debris. Single‐cell populations were identified by gating on forward scatter height (FSC‐H) versus forward scatter area (FSC‐A). The percentage of positively stained cell populations was determined by comparison with cells stained with isotype control antibodies or unstained cells. Gating strategies are provided in Figure S15. Flow cytometry data were processed and analyzed using the FlowJo software (version 10.0; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Statistical Analysis

All the quantitative data are presented as the means ± SDs unless otherwise stated. Statistical significance between two groups was assessed using an unpaired, two‐tailed Student's t test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. All the statistical analyses were conducted using the GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.2). A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism. All collected data were included in the analyses. For prophylactic experiments, mice from the same litter were randomly assigned to cages prior to vaccination. For therapeutic studies, mice were randomly injected with tumor cells prior to vaccination. Sample sizes were not predetermined by statistical calculations. Throughout all the animal experiments, two investigators blinded to the group assignments monitored the animals and collected the data.
