Perceived social support and depression: longitudinal mediating roles of gratitude and meaning in life
Authors: H M Tian, Y Q Zhou, X L Chang, Y F Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study uses two longitudinal batches of data to explore the relationship between perceived social support and depression, and the chain mediating effect of gratitude and meaning in life in college students.MethodsA total of 833 participants completed the questionnaire survey, including perceived social support multidimensional scale, gratitude scale, meaning in Life scale and depression subscale of DASS-21. Among them, 380 were male college students and 453 were female college students. (The mean age of the subjects was 19.43 ± 0.81 years).Results(1)There is a significant correlation between perceived social support, gratitude, meaning in life and depression. (2)Perceived social support and depression at two time points showed a significant negative correlation. (3)Gratitude and meaning in life played a chain mediating role between perceived social support and depression at two time points.ConclusionPerceived social support can longitudinally and significantly predict depression in college students through the independent mediating effect of gratitude and meaning in life and the chain mediating effect of gratitude and meaning in life. These results provide empirical support for the design of more targeted interventions involving gratitude and meaning in life to combat depression.Keywords: Perceived social support, Depression, Gratitude, Meaning in life

Introduction

Negative emotions are a hot topic in college students’ mental health. Some researchers believe that most mental health problems occur in early adulthood, and depression are considered important indicators of mental health [1]. Early studies found a high prevalence of depression among college students [2], and researchers further suggested that the prevalence of depression among college students was on the rise [3]. In recent studies, it has been found that depression are still in a high incidence state among adolescents [4]. The results showed that 22.2%, 34.3%, and 37.3% of college students showed moderate to severe symptoms of stress, anxiety, and depression, respectively [5]. College students are in a critical period of transition from youth to adulthood, rapid physical and mental development, and gradually towards maturity and independence. They are faced with multiple pressures such as changes in environment and identity, and the choice of study and employment. Depression seriously affect the mental health, study and interpersonal relationship of college students, which makes it more difficult for college students in multiple stressful situations. However, Perceived social support has a positive impact on mental health and quality of life, and individuals who receive more social support will reduce depression, but the internal mechanism is worth further study. Therefore, it is of significant importance to investigate the factors influencing depression and to understand its potential mechanisms in order to formulate effective intervention strategies.

Perceived social support and depression

Perceived social support refers to the support that individuals experience in society, such as being respected, supported, and understood [6]. It is an individual’s subjective judgment of social support that can be obtained from friends, family and other channels. A large number of literatures have explored the relationship between social support and negative emotions, and the results are roughly the same, that is, good social support can help reduce the generation of negative emotions and promote the healthy development of individual mental health and quality of Life [7–9]. According to the modified stress-buffering model, social support can benefit individuals in both high-pressure and low-pressure environments; however, the greater the stress associated with the event an individual experiences, the more significant the role that social support can play [10]. Research by Attar-Schwartz et al. [11] suggests that increased social support can significantly negatively predict internalization problems in adolescents. Individuals reporting higher levels of depressive symptoms demonstrated significantly lower levels of perceived social support. Inadequate social support from all sources was associated with an increased risk of depression [12]. Low social support may deepen an individual’s depression or increase an individual’s risk of depression [13], which can be ameliorated by increasing social support. It can be seen that a high level of social support can suppress depression.

The mediating role of gratitude

Gratitude is defined as a positive emotional reaction to the recognition of benefits received from others’ kindness. It is theorized to operate as a “benefit detector,” triggering awareness of received favors when others act kindly. This capacity is thought to develop gradually through ongoing social interactions [14]. When individuals have higher social support, their development has a dominant gain function, which makes them produce more positive emotions in life [15]. The extent to which an individual recognizes and interprets social support and assistance plays a critical role in determining whether they experience gratitude after receiving help. Individuals with high perceived social support are more able to feel the support, understanding and respect from the social group, thus creating gratitude experience and emotion [16]. Alfieri et al. [17] found that emotional support from the mother had an important protective effect on gratitude levels in adolescents.

Moreover, previous research has identified gratitude as a protective factor against negative emotional states. Longitudinal evidence further indicates that gratitude serves as a significant negative predictor of depression [18]. Gratitude may help women at risk for depression to accept their depressive symptoms and thus improve mental health [19]. Moreover, previous research indicates that gratitude serves a range of adaptive functions, including stress reduction, mitigation of negative emotions, alleviation of depressive symptoms, as well as enhanced coping abilities and increased well-being. Through an intervention study, Chen and Ishak found that guiding students to keep a gratitude journal or to reflect on experiences for which they are grateful can help prevent depression and promote psychological well-being [20]. It can be inferred from previous literature that gratitude may mediate the relationship between perceived social support and depression.

The mediating role of meaning in life

Meaning in life refers to the individual’s perception that life has coherence, purpose and significance [21]. Steger et al. divided the sense of meaning in life into two parts: “seeking” and “experiencing”, that is, “constructing the meaning of life and understanding the goal” and “understanding and identifying with one’s goal, purpose and mission in life“ [22]. According to the theory of the main effect of social support [23], social support has a general gain effect. The more social support an individual has, the better he or she can adapt to the society and experience more sense of life meaning from the environmental system and life. Studies have also shown that positive interpersonal relationships and social support are important for individuals to experience and construct meaning in life [24, 25]. When people perceive support from society, connection with others enhances a sense of meaning by indicating that their lives matter [26], whereas failure to do so greatly diminishes these feelings. Therefore, social support is intrinsically linked to the sense of meaning in life, as it facilitates individuals’ comprehension and interpretation of life’s purpose and significance.

Existential theories have emphasized the relevance of meaning in life to depression and that having meaning in life can combat the anhedonia commonly seen in depression and maintain intrinsically motivated behavior [27, 28]. Subsequent empirical studies have revealed a negative correlation between meaning in life and depression, indicating that both the search for meaning and the presence of meaning are inversely associated with depressive symptoms [29]. A longitudinal study revealed that meaning in life was a significant predictor of depression [30]. In general, individuals with a strong sense of meaning in life are more likely to have positive experiences of life, whereas meaning crisis is associated with negative emotions and can even independently predict depression [31–33]. It can be inferred from previous literature that meaning in life may mediate the relationship between perceived social support and depression.

The chain mediating effect of gratitude and sense of meaning in life

According to extended-construction theory, individuals who experience gratitude tend to develop a more positive perception of their environment, thereby fostering a stronger sense of social belonging and connectedness [34]. This enhanced relational framework provides greater opportunities for individuals to recognize meaning in their lives [35]. This view has also been confirmed by a number of studies. Some studies have found that gratitude can affect the exploration and realization of life meaning. Individuals with high levels of gratitude exhibit heightened attention to positive experiences and emotions, which facilitate the generation of meaning, enhance subjective well-being, and promote active engagement in the search for meaning in life. There is a significant positive correlation between gratitude and sense of meaning in life [36, 37]. When individuals have highly grateful positive emotions, their cognitive thinking patterns will be improved. Individuals who repeatedly experience the positive affect of gratitude and maintain flexible cognitive levels may also increase their subjective experience of purpose and value in life [38]. Building on the established association between gratitude and meaning in life, it is plausible to propose that these variables serve as sequential mediators in the relationship between perceived social support and depression.

The present study

This study aimed to explore the relationship between perceived social support, gratitude, meaning in life, and depression through mediation analysis. Based on the previous literature, this study proposed the following hypotheses: (a) Perceived social support has an impact on depression; (b) Gratitude played a mediating role in the relationship between perceived social support and depression. (c) Meaning in life played a mediating role in the relationship between perceived social support and depression. (d) Gratitude and meaning in life played a chain mediating role in the relationship between perceived social support and depression. This study used a chain mediation model to explore for the first time the mechanism of action between perceived social support and depression using longitudinal data.

Method

Participants and procedure

Ten classes were recruited through classroom teaching, a total of 833 college students completed the first survey, and the second survey was completed after an interval of 3 months. There were 380 males and 453 females, and no subjects were missed in the two surveys. The study procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants signed an informed consent form before participating in the study. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the authors’ university(HNU-ESS-20230730).

Measures

Social support multidimensional scale

Social Support multidimensional Scale (MSPSS) developed by Zimet et al. was used [39]. The scale consists of three dimensions and a total of 12 self-rating items to measure the degree to which individuals subjectively perceive support from friends, family, and other sources of support (relatives, teachers, classmates, etc.). Each item describes a source of support, and participants are asked to rate their actual feelings on a scale of 1 (not at all true) to 7 (absolutely true). Higher scores indicated more self-perceived support. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients in this study were 0.951.

Gratitude questionnaire

Gratitude was measured using the Gratitude questionnaire(GQ-6) prepared by McCullough et al. [40], which assesses individual differences in gratitude emotional traits by assessing the frequency and intensity of individual gratitude experience and the density and breadth of events that trigger gratitude emotions. A total of six items are rated on a 7-level scale, with 1 indicating complete disagreement and 7 indicating complete agreement. A higher score indicates a stronger tendency to be grateful. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the first and second time in this study were 0.784 and 0.748.

Meaning in life questionnaire

Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ), compiled by Steger in 2006 [22], consists of two dimensions: experiencing the meaning of life and seeking the meaning of life. The scale consists of ten items, which are scored by Likert 7 points, from 1 = very inconsistent to 7 = very consistent. The higher the score, the higher the level of cognition or motivation of the individual’s meaning of life.The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the first and second time in this study were 0.889 and 0.878.

Depression questionnaire

Depression was measured using the depression subscale of the 21-item Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21) developed by Lovibond et al. [41]. There were 7 items in total, and the item score was 4 points. Frequency/severity was rated on a series of 4-point scales ranging from not at all to perfectly well matched. The higher the score, the more severe the participant’s depressive symptoms in the most recent period. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the first and second time in this study were 0.961 and 0.964.

Statistical analyses

SPSS 26.0 and Process 4.1 were used for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were performed for all variables in SPSS, and Pearson correlation analysis was used to explore the correlation between variables. The Process 4.1 plugin was used for mediation analysis, confidence intervals without zero indicated significant corresponding effects, P < 0. 05 statistically significant.

Results

Common method bias test

To address potential common method bias, several procedural and statistical remedies were implemented following recommended methodological practices. Procedurally, participants were assured of strict confidentiality to minimize evaluation apprehension and social desirability bias. The survey design incorporated reverse-scored items and randomized question sequences to mitigate automatic response patterns and anchor effects. Statistically, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess the severity of common method variance. The unrotated factor solution indicated that the first factor accounted for 36.012% of the total variance, which is below the recommended threshold of 40% [42]. This result indicates that the data of this study did not show a significant dominant factor in the principal component factor analysis, and therefore, there is no obvious common method bias.

Preliminary analyses

Pearson correlation analysis showed that perceived social support, gratitude, meaning in life were negatively correlated with depression at both time points, that is, higher levels of social support, gratitude, and meaning in life were associated with lower levels of depression. There was a positive correlation among perceived social support, gratitude and meaning in life at two time points. Longitudinal mediation analysis

The immediate mediating effect of the two time points was tested, and on this basis, the longitudinal mediating effect of gratitude and meaning in life at different times was further investigated. Perceived social support at T1 was used as the predictor variable, gratitude and meaning in life at T1 and T2 were used as the mediating variables, and depression at T2 was used as the dependent variable to construct a longitudinal mediation model. All the mediation analyses in the study controlled for gender and age.

The results of mediating effect test showed that, without adding mediating variables, perceived social support at T1 significantly affected depression at T2 (β= −0.227, p <.01). When T1 gratitude and T1 meaning in life were mediating variables, the following results were found: First, perceived social support at T1 significantly positively affected gratitude at T1 (β = 0.435, p < 0. 01), and gratitude at T1 significantly negatively affected depression at T2 (β= −0.099, p <.05). The independent mediating effect size of T1 gratitude was − 0.038 (95%CI = [−0.080, −0.006]), accounting for 14.126% of the total effect. Second, perceived social support at T1 significantly positively affected life meaning at T1 (β = 0. 411, p < 0. 01), life meaning at T1 significantly negatively affected depression at T2 (β= −0.249, p < 0. 01). The independent mediating effect size of T1 meaning in life was − 0.122(95%CI = [−0.173, −0.074]), accounting for 45.353% of the total effect. Third, T1 gratitude significantly positively affected T1 meaning in life (β = 0.155, p <.01), and the chain mediating effect of T1 gratitude and T1 meaning in life was significant and the magnitude was − 0.020(95%CI = [−0.034, −0.010]), accounting for 7.435% of the total effect. Discussion

This study examined the mechanism underlying the relationship between perceived social support and depression using longitudinal data collected at two time points. The findings provided evidence that gratitude and meaning in life are significant variables, serving not only as mediators between perceived social support and depression but also establishing a sequential mediation pathway from perceived social support to depression.

Perceived social support and depression

The results indicated that perceived social support at T1 was significantly negatively correlated not only with depression levels at T1, but also with depression at T2. Further analysis using a structural model demonstrated a significant negative regression coefficient from T1 perceived social support to T2 depression, suggesting that greater perceived social support predicts reduced depressive symptoms over time. This result is consistent with existing empirical literature [43, 44]. The primary finding of this study strongly supports the core tenets of both the Main Effect Model [23], According to the main effect model of social support social support generally has a protective effect. Individuals who receive social support can better resist stress and improve their mental health, thereby reducing the risk of depression. Our longitudinal data demonstrate that this beneficial effect exhibits temporal persistence. Specifically, higher levels of social support at T1 help individuals accumulate psychological resources, which not only exert a protective effect on concurrent depressive symptoms(T1) but also effectively mitigate the risk of future depression (T2). The current study’s findings confirm that social support exerts a protective effect against depression over an extended period.

The mediating role of gratitude

The results of this study demonstrate that gratitude at T1 mediates the relationship between perceived social support at T1 and depression at T2. Additionally, gratitude at T2 also serves as a mediator in this relationship. Firstly, the positive association between perceived social support and gratitude, observed through longitudinal data at two time points, aligns with previous research findings [45]. According to Adam Smith’s theory of moral sentiments, individuals are more likely to experience gratitude when they perceive help from others; the greater the support received, the higher the levels of gratitude expressed [46, 47]. Consequently, individuals who perceive higher levels of social support tend to exhibit increased gratitude, while those lacking such perceptions may have lower gratitude levels. Our longitudinal data analysis indicates that sustained social support (T1) fosters a enduring disposition towards gratitude (T2). This suggests that perceived social support still significantly influences individual gratitude levels over time. Secondly, longitudinal data at two time points reveal that gratitude negatively predicts depression, consistent with prior studies [48]. Gratitude acts as a protective factor, mitigating negative emotions and psychological conditions [49]. The present study reaffirms gratitude as a protective factor against depression using longitudinal data. Furthermore, the results of the longitudinal mediation indicated that gratitude serves as a significant mediating mechanism through which perceived social support predicts reduced levels of depression over time. This result provides empirical support in the form of longitudinal data for the expansive construction theory of gratitude [40], gratitude broadens an individual’s attention span and enhances cognitive flexibility, facilitating the perception of social support from various sources. This expanded awareness helps individuals effectively resist negative emotions like depression.

The mediating role of meaning in life

The results of this study indicate that life meaning at the T1 time point mediates the relationship between perceived social support at T1 and depression at T2. Additionally, life meaning at T2 also mediates the relationship between perceived social support at T1 and depression at T2. Firstly, perceived social support can positively influence the sense of meaning in life. This is consistent with the findings of previous studies [50],

Furthermore, our longitudinal data indicates that the external recognition provided by supportive social interactions is gradually internalized over time, leading to the formation of a stronger and more stable sense of personal meaning in life. Perhaps it is because perceived social support, as an important psychological resource, can maintain an individual’s physical and mental health, motivate more people to engage in learning and work [24], and enhance people’s experience of the meaning of life over a period of time. Secondly, meaning in life can inhibit depression. The research results indicate that the sense of meaning in life has a negative impact on depression. That is, the stronger a person’s sense of meaning in life is, the lower the possibility of experiencing depressive symptoms now and in the future. Having meaning in life can counteract the anhedonia common in depression and maintain intrinsically motivated behavior [28]. Consequently, individuals with a higher sense of life meaning invest more time and effort in pursuing life goals and continuously motivate themselves to strive for them [51], thereby maintaining their emotional health and protecting themselves from depression. Thirdly, the mediation results also suggest that life meaning mediates the relationship between perceived social support and depression. Social support functions as a critical precursor by fulfilling fundamental psychological needs for purpose and meaning. This enhanced sense of meaning in life subsequently serves as a vital internal resource, facilitating a more coherent and purposeful interpretation of life experiences. It may have promoted adaptive psychological processes, including goal-directed behavior and active coping strategies, which ultimately reduce the propensity for developing depressive symptoms over time.

The chain mediating effect of gratitude and sense of meaning in life

The results of this study indicated not only that gratitude and meaning in life at Time 1 had a chain mediating effect on perceived social support and depression but also that gratitude and meaning in life at Time 2 exhibited a similar chain mediating effect on perceived social support and depression. Utilizing data from two distinct time points, the study confirmed that individuals with greater gratitude are more inclined to discover meaning in their lives. This corroborates previous research [52] and offers additional empirical support for the extended theory of gratitude. In essence, highly grateful individuals tend to focus on positive experiences and emotions that contribute to the creation of meaning, thereby promoting a sense of purpose and further encouraging the pursuit of life’s meaning. In summary, the mediation model elucidated the impact of perceived social support on depression through the lenses of gratitude and meaning in life. This suggests that perceiving social support is particularly vital for contemporary young people. With a solid foundation of relationships, individuals are more likely to feel grateful for their surroundings, which in turn provides more opportunities to recognize that their lives are meaningful [35] and reinforces their inclination to sustain a meaningful life as a means to counteract negative emotions [53].

Limitations

Firstly, although this study is a longitudinal study, it only includes data from two groups separated by a relatively short time period. Compared to cross-sectional studies, it has improved in this aspect, but it still fundamentally limits the depth of causal inference. To better analyze the dynamic changes within and between individuals, and to explore potential pathways, future research will need to adopt a multi-stage longitudinal study design that covers multiple time points and has a relatively long interval between each point, and to employ more powerful analytical techniques. Secondly, although the self-report measurement methods that have been thoroughly validated are employed, they are still susceptible to common method bias and common informant bias. The measurement responses of participants to the variables may be influenced by transient emotional states, social expectations, or underlying personality traits. Future research would benefit from the combination of multiple method assessments to cross-verify the research results and enhance their validity. Thirdly, there may be other significant factors influencing the relationship between perceived social support and depression, as well as its mechanisms. Future studies could explore these additional factors to ascertain their distinct roles. Lastly, this study was limited to a sample of Chinese college students, and thus, the generalizability of the findings should be approached with caution. Future research should strive to replicate these findings in a more diverse population.

Conclusion

This study employed a two-wave longitudinal design to explore the mechanism of action between social support and depressive symptoms. The results of longitudinal mediation analysis indicated that gratitude and sense of meaning in life played significant mediating roles between social support and depression. Through the chain mediation paths at two time points, perceived social support could predict a decrease in depression levels over time. This finding not only verified the previous cross-sectional research findings but also provided more compelling inferential evidence for this mediation path by establishing the temporal sequence among the variables. The results of this study have enriched the etiological model of depression and confirmed the significant driving role of social factors in the dynamic development of mental health. Furthermore, the study offers novel perspectives for intervention in depression. These results imply that enhancing an individual’s social support network, elevating levels of gratitude, and strengthening the sense of meaning in life should become core targets for preventing and intervening in depression.
