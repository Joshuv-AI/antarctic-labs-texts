Background

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), characterized by accelerated destruction and insufficient production of platelets, is a type of haemorrhagic disease that poses serious health hazards to humans [1–6]. ITP is commonly thought to be an immune-mediated disorder [3, 7]; thus, corticosteroids are recommended as the standard first-line therapy. However, only 30–50% of adults achieve a sustained response after glucocorticoids are discontinued [8, 9] and prolonged exposure is often associated with intolerable side effects [10–13]. These unsatisfactory treatment outcomes indicate that the pathogenic mechanism underlying ITP requires further investigation.

Previous studies have revealed that the bone marrow (BM) microenvironment of patients with ITP is impaired [14, 15]. Macrophages (MΦs), which serve as essential cellular components in the BM microenvironment, have been reported to regulate haematopoiesis [16–19] and megakaryopoiesis [20–22] (a branch of haematopoiesis [23–27]). MΦs can be divided into classically activated (M1) MΦs and alternatively activated (M2) MΦs, with unique phenotypes and functions [28–32]. M1 MΦs are thought to be capable of proinflammatory responses, whereas M2 MΦs tend to repair damaged tissues [33, 34]. M1 MФs have inhibitory effects on haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and megakaryocytes (MKs), whereas M2 MФs have opposite effects. Moreover, Zhao et al. reported that the PI3K–AKT pathway is essential for M2 MФs to support megakaryopoiesis, which is associated with the occurrence of prolonged isolated thrombocytopenia after allogeneic HSC transplantation (allo-HSCT) [28]. Although M1/M2 imbalance in the spleen has been reported in ITP [35, 36], whether aberrant MΦ polarization in the BM is involved in the occurrence of ITP remains elusive.

Thrombopoietin (TPO) is a critical cytokine for HSC maintenance and megakaryopoiesis [37, 38]. Emerging evidence suggests that TPO can stimulate MK differentiation, followed by the release of many platelets [39]. In vitro and in vivo experiments revealed that TPO facilitated MK colony formation and increased the number, size, and ploidy of MKs. When genes encoding TPO or its receptor were knocked out in mice, the numbers of MKs and platelets decreased to 12–15% of the normal values [40–42]. Moreover, TPO is thought to be related to multiple signalling pathways throughout the process of platelet production, including the PI3K–AKT pathway in MKs [43]. Nevertheless, whether TPO can regulate the PI3K–AKT pathway in BM MΦs and its ability to regulate haematopoiesis need to be further elucidated.

In the current study, a BM MΦ-specific PI3K-knockout murine model was constructed to investigate the role of the PI3K–AKT pathway in regulating the haematopoiesis-supporting ability of MФs in vivo. In addition, the roles of BM MΦ polarization and the PI3K–AKT pathway in regulating haematopoiesis were evaluated in a clinical cohort of patients with ITP. Furthermore, the therapeutic mechanism through which TPO treatment corrects BM MΦ polarization to improve haematopoiesis was explored in patients with ITP through in vitro experiments.

Methods

Establishment of a BM MΦ-specific PI3K-knockout murine model

Class IA PI3Ks are encoded by the gene Pik3r1, which is located on chromosome 13 in mice [44]. The gRNA to the Pik3r1 gene, the donor vector containing loxP sites, and Cas9 mRNA were coinjected into fertilized mouse eggs to generate targeted conditional knockout offspring (genotype: flox/+). Heterozygous targeted mice were subsequently intercrossed to generate homozygous targeted mice (genotype: flox/flox), which were used as control mice. The subsequent step involved breeding a homozygous targeted mouse with a tissue-specific Cre deletion to generate mice that were heterozygous for a targeted allele and hemizygous/heterozygous for the Cre transgene. Finally, heterozygous Cre+ mice were bred with homozygous mice to obtain BM MΦ-specific PI3K-knockout mice (genotype: flox/flox, lyz2-Cre). The pups were screened by the assay as described in the Supplementary Material (Figure S1).

The mice were subsequently euthanized at week 6 to obtain tissue for histology and cytology. The kinetics of the peripheral blood were analysed, and BM cell suspensions were collected from the femurs and tibias of each mouse to evaluate the MФ subsets by flow cytometry. MФ subsets were identified by CD163, CD86, F4/80, and CD11b expression (BioLegend, USA). For histopathological analysis of the BM, the femurs were obtained and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

The murine model was constructed at the Cyagen Transgenic Animal Center. All the mouse experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital.

Patients and controls

BM MФ subsets were detected in a clinical cohort comprising 18 age-matched patients with ITP, including patients with primary ITP (N = 6), patients with corticosteroid-sensitive ITP (N = 6), and patients with corticosteroid-resistant ITP (N = 6). BM MФs from patients with primary ITP were used for in vitro experiments. Six age-matched healthy donors for allo-HSCT at Peking University People’s Hospital were enrolled as normal controls (HCs) in our study.

Corticosteroid-sensitive ITP was defined as the absence of bleeding symptoms, a platelet count ≥ 30 × 109/L, and at least a 2-fold increase from baseline within 4 weeks of corticosteroid treatment, which was confirmed at 2 different time points at least 7 days apart [45–47]. Corticosteroid-resistant ITP was defined as an insufficient response to at least 4 weeks of standard corticosteroid treatment. This resistance manifested as one of the following: platelet levels < 30 × 109/L, platelet levels ≥ 30 × 109/L but accompanied by bleeding symptoms, or failure to achieve a 2-fold increase from baseline [14, 45–47] (Table S1).

Detection, isolation, cultivation, characterization, and functional analyses of primary BM MՓs

BM MФ subsets were detected using flow cytometry [28, 29]. M1 MФs were identified by CD68 and CCR2 expression, and M2 MФs were identified by CD163 and CX3CR1 expression. BM MФs from HCs or patients with ITP were cultured as described previously [28, 29, 48]. Briefly, BM mononuclear cells (BMMNCs) from HCs or patients with ITP were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using lymphocyte separation medium (GE Healthcare, USA) and then differentiated into cultivated HC MФs or ITP MФs for 7 days in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated foetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 100 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (PeproTech, USA). Cultivated BM MФs were evaluated in phagocytosis and migration assays as previously described [28, 29, 48]. To measure the intracellular levels of cultured MФs, phospho(p)-PI3K and p-AKT were evaluated by mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) (mean ± SEM).

Reagent treatment in vitro

BM MФs were treated with recombinant human thrombopoietin (rhTPO, Sansheng Pharmaceutical, China), with a concentration of 8 ng/mL used for in vitro experiments. In the coculture experiments, 20 ng/mL vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF, Gibco, USA) and 1 µg/mL VEGF monoclonal antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were added to the media. The human CD34+ cells and MФs were treated with VEGF or VEGF antibody for 24 h.

Cultivation and differentiation of MKs

BM CD34+ cells were isolated from BMMNCs from HCs and patients with ITP using a CD34 MicroBead kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) [49–52]. CD34+ cells (5 × 105 per well) were subsequently cultured for 15 days in StemSpan™ SFEM (Stem Cell Technologies, Canada) supplemented with 100 ng/mL SCF, 100 ng/mL TPO, and 10 ng/mL IL-3 (PeproTech) to promote MK differentiation. Quantifications of the MK count, MK polyploidy distribution, and platelet production were performed as previously described [28, 53], and the expression level of GPIb (CD42) was evaluated by MFI (mean ± SEM).

Coculture experiments of BM HSCs or MKs with BM MФs

To evaluate the ability of BM MФs to support HSCs and MKs, after 7 days of culture, cultured MΦs were cocultured with HSCs or MKs separately. Before coculture, the MФs were carefully washed three times with 1 mL of 1 × PBS to elute rhTPO. As mentioned above, the extracted BM CD34+ cells were cocultured with MФs at a ratio of 2.5:1 (MK coculture experiments) and 1:1 (HSC coculture experiments) in 24-well plates. Notably, StemSpan™ SFEM containing 100 ng/mL SCF, 100 ng/mL TPO, and 50 ng/mL FLT3 was used to maintain HSCs for 4 days, while 100 ng/mL SCF, 100 ng/mL TPO, and 10 ng/mL IL-3 were used to promote differentiation of the MKs for 15 days. At the end of coculture, the status and functions of the HSCs were evaluated by apoptosis analysis, a colony-forming unit (CFU) assay, and a 5‐ethynyl‐20‐deoxyuridine (EdU) assay. MKs were evaluated for apoptosis, MK ploidy percentage, and platelet production.

Apoptosis analysis

To evaluate apoptosis, HSCs or MKs were incubated with Annexin-V (BioLegend) and 7-amino-actinomycin D (BD Biosciences, USA) for 10 min and then analysed with BD FACSDIVA v8.0 Software [28, 29, 48].

CFU assay

After 4 days of coculture, 2 × 103 CD34+ cells were plated in 24-well plates with MethoCult™ H4434 Classic (Stem Cell Technologies) and cultured for 14 days. CFU-erythroid (CFU-E), burst-forming unit erythroid (BFU-E), CFU-granulocyte/macrophage (CFU-GM), and CFU-graulocyte, erythroid, macrophage and megakaryocyte (CFU-GEMM) numbers derived from CD34+ cells were measured [48, 51, 52].

EdU assay

After 4 days of coculture with BM MΦs, the HSCs were incubated with 10 µM EdU (RiboBio, China) in 24-well plates for 24 h at 37 °C [54]. In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, the percentages of EdU-positive cells were analysed in BD FACSDIVA v8.0 software to quantify cell proliferation [48].

RNA sequencing and data analysis

BM MKs were labelled with CD41a and then subjected to fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for RNA-seq analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with DESeq2. GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses were performed using the R packages ‘clusterProfiler’, ‘pathview’ and ‘ggplot2’ [48, 55].

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

BM plasma from patients with ITP and HCs or supernatant from cultured MФs and coculture media were collected. VEGF, von Willebrand factor (vWF) and IL-10 levels were measured using ELISA kits (vWF: Thermo Fisher Scientific; VEGF and IL-10: R&D Systems, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 10.3.1. Paired t test was performed on paired data. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare more than two groups. The Mann‒Whitney U test was used for continuous variables. The data are presented as the means ± SEMs, and P values < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

BM MФ-specific PI3K-knockout mice exhibited an increased M1/M2 ratio and a sharp decrease in megakaryopoiesis and platelet production

Similar to a previous adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated BM MФ-specific PI3K-knockdown mouse model [28], a more precise BM MФ-specific PI3K-knockout mouse model was constructed by CRISPR/Cas9 (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated protein 9) technology to further investigate the impact of PI3K deficiency on MФ polarization and megakaryopoiesis in vivo (Fig. 1A; Figure S1A). Compared with control mice, BM MФ-specific PI3K-knockout mice exhibited an increased BM M1/M2 ratio (Figs. 1B–D and 3.55 ± 0.58 vs. 1.34 ± 0.27, P = 0.0079).

Notably, the peripheral platelet count was significantly lower in the PI3K-knockout mice than in the control mice (Fig. 1E and 666 ± 152 vs. 1435 ± 77, P = 0.0079). The white blood cell and red blood cell counts and haemoglobin concentration did not significantly differ between the two groups (Figure S1B). Moreover, the cellularity of MKs in the BM of PI3K-knockout mice was significantly lower than that in the BM of control mice (Fig. 1F). These results suggested that the suppression of the PI3K–AKT pathway had a negative effect on M2 MФs, which supports MK maturation and platelet production in vivo.

RNA-seq indicated that the platelet activation pathway was downregulated in the MKs of PI3K-knockout mice

To explore the regulatory mechanism underlying abnormal MФ polarization mediated by the PI3K–AKT pathway in MK maturation and platelet production, MKs from the BMs of PI3K-knockout mice (N = 5) and control mice (N = 5) were sorted by flow cytometry (Fig. 1A).

There were 593 genes whose expression differed between PI3K-knockout mice and control mice, including 378 downregulated genes and 215 upregulated genes (Fig. 1G). Gene ontology (GO) pathway enrichment analysis revealed that angiogenesis, platelet activation, MK development, blood coagulation, vasculogenesis, platelet aggregation, and positive regulation of VEGF receptor signalling pathway were decreased in PI3K-knockout mice (Fig. 1H). Notably, the initiator of the platelet activation pathway is the ligand receptor response of vWF-GPIb, and its expression level was significantly decreased in PI3K-knockout mice (Fig. 1I). These results further revealed the potential impact of PI3K in BM MФs on megakaryopoiesis and thrombopoiesis in vivo.

There was no significant difference in the MK counts between the HCs and patients with ITP (Fig. 2B). The platelet count was significantly lower in patients with ITP than in HCs (Fig. 2C; 21 ± 10 vs. 39 ± 8, P = 0.0381). As shown in Figs. 2D and E, compared with that in patients with ITP, MK apoptosis in HCs was lower (41.4% ± 3.2% vs. 60.5% ± 3.5%, P = 0.0075). These findings indicated that MK maturation and platelet production were impaired in patients with ITP.

Increased BM M1/M2 MФ ratio was present in the BM of patients with ITP and was related to a downregulation of the PI3K–AKT pathway

Aberrant BM M1/M2 polarization, characterized by a significant increase in the percentage of M1 MФs (Figs. 3A, B; primary ITP vs. HC: 1.2% ± 0.1% vs. 0.5% ± 0.1%, P = 0.0105; corticosteroid-resistant ITP vs. HC: 1.4% ± 0.1% vs. 0.5% ± 0.1%, P = 0.0080) and a decrease in the percentage of M2 MΦs, was observed in patients with ITP compared with those in HCs (Figs. 3A, C; primary ITP vs. HC: 14.5% ± 1.1% vs. 30.1% ± 3.4%, P = 0.0059). Together, the results revealed that the BM M1/M2 ratio was elevated in patients with ITP, especially in patients with corticosteroid-resistant ITP (Fig. 3D; primary ITP vs. HC: 0.085 ± 0.011 vs. 0.018 ± 0.003, P = 0.0050; corticosteroid-resistant ITP vs. HC: 0.094 ± 0.022 vs. 0.018 ± 0.003, P = 0.0381).

To further explore the differences in the polarization-related signalling pathways in MΦs, the intracellular expression levels of p-PI3K and p-AKT were analysed separately. Notably, higher expression levels of p-PI3K (Fig. 3E; HC vs. primary ITP: 5768 ± 783 vs. 2570 ± 450, P = 0.0043; HC vs. corticosteroid-sensitive ITP: 5768 ± 783 vs. 3921 ± 627, P = 0.0436; HC vs. corticosteroid-resistant ITP: 5768 ± 783 vs. 1832 ± 544, P = 0.0070) and p-AKT (Fig. 3F; HC vs. primary ITP: 4723 ± 758 vs. 2688 ± 438, P = 0.0403) were detected in BM MΦs from HCs.

Decreased migration, phagocytosis, and haematopoiesis-supporting ability of BM-MФs were observed in patients with ITP

To evaluate the functions of primary BM-MФs derived from patients with ITP or HCs, migration and phagocytosis were analysed (Fig. 4A). The migratory (Fig. 4B; 102 ± 9 vs. 151 ± 19, P = 0.0285) and phagocytic (Fig. 4C; 88.5% ± 1.9% vs. 94.1% ± 0.5%, P = 0.0266) functions of MФs from patients with ITP were significantly attenuated compared with those from HCs.

To investigate the influence of BM MФs on HSCs in vitro, coculture experiments of normal BM CD34+ cells with ITP-MФs and HC-MФs were performed. After 4 days, the proliferation, apoptosis, and CFU plating efficiency of the CD34+ cells were analysed. Compared with those in the HC group, the number of EdU-positive HSCs in the ITP group was notably lower (Figs. 4D, E; 52.5% ± 4.0% vs. 74.1% ± 1.0%, P = 0.0014), and the percentage of apoptotic HSCs was markedly increased (Figs. 4F, G; 19.6% ± 2.0% vs. 11.2% ± 1.0%, P = 0.0140). In addition, compared with those cocultured with MФs from HCs, HSCs cocultured with MФs from patients with ITP had lower CFU plating efficiency (Fig. 4H), as determined by CFU-E (18 ± 3 vs. 38 ± 3, P = 0.0027), BFU‐E (12 ± 1 vs. 30 ± 2, P = 0.0002), CFU-GM (8 ± 1 vs. 19 ± 2, P = 0.0066), and GEMM (3 ± 1 vs. 6 ± 1, P = 0.0264).

To further explore the effects of BM-MФs on megakaryopoiesis, coculture experiments of normal BM-MKs with MФs from patients with ITP and HCs were performed. After 15 days of coculture, compared with the ITP group, the HC group showed decreased MK apoptosis (Fig. 4I; 35.1% ± 1.7% vs. 52.0% ± 2.7%, P = 0.0003) and a lower percentage of 2 N MKs (Fig. 4J; 24.4% ± 1.3% vs. 42.3% ± 2.9%, P = 0.0013).

Taken together, these data suggested that BM MФs from patients with ITP were deficient in promoting haematopoiesis.

TPO could improve the functions of BM-MФs from patients with ITP

To investigate whether pharmacological activation of the PI3K–AKT pathway could regulate MФs, cultured BM-MФs from patients with ITP were treated with TPO, which is considered associated with the PI3K–AKT pathway (Fig. 5A). After treatment with TPO for 24 h, MФs expressed higher levels of p-AKT (Fig. 5B; 2033 ± 170 vs. 1875 ± 149, P = 0.0424) and p-PI3K (Fig. 5C; 1512 ± 84 vs. 1470 ± 73, P = 0.0309), and the M1/M2 ratio of MФs was decreased after treatment (Fig. 5D; 1.057 ± 0.126 vs. 1.276 ± 0.131, P = 0.0407).

Compared with untreated MФs, TPO increased the migration (Figs. 5E and F; 157 ± 16 vs. 122 ± 14, P = 0.0046), phagocytosis (Figs. 5G and H; 93.1% ± 1.4% vs. 85.2% ± 3.0%, P = 0.0345), and haematopoiesis-supporting abilities of MФs by increasing the number of EdU-positive cells (Figure S2A; 64.4% ± 4.6% vs. 57.7% ± 4.2%, P = 0.0176), decreasing the number of apoptotic HSCs (Figure S2B; 11.4% ± 2.4% vs. 15.8% ± 3.3%, P = 0.0489), and elevating CFU plating efficiencies (Fig. 5I; CFU-E: 20 ± 3 vs. 16 ± 2, P = 0.0199, BFU‐E: 11 ± 2 vs. 9 ± 2, P = 0.0212, CFU-GM: 6 ± 1 vs. 4 ± 0, P = 0.0409, and GEMM: 4 ± 1 vs. 2 ± 0, P = 0.0180).

To further confirm the importance of the PI3K–AKT pathway in regulating the megakaryopoiesis-supporting ability of MФs, MKs differentiated from CD34+ BM cells from HCs were cocultured separately with TPO-treated MФs and untreated MФs. After 15 days of coculture, MФs in the TPO-treated group exhibited better megakaryopoiesis-supporting ability, as indicated by decreases in MK apoptosis (Figure S2C; 47.3% ± 6.4% vs. 57.0% ± 6.5%, P = 0.0176) and the percentage of 2 N MKs (Fig. 5J; 30.8% ± 5.6% vs. 40.9% ± 5.8%, P = 0.0018) compared with those in the untreated group.

To explore the mechanism underlying the enhanced haematopoiesis-supporting ability of BM MФs, the media from the cultivated BM MФs were analysed using ELISAs. Compared with untreated MФs, BM MФs treated with TPO secreted higher levels of VEGF (Fig. 5K; 53.5 ± 0.9 pg/mL vs. 46.9 ± 1.4 pg/mL, P = 0.0076) and IL-10 (Fig. 5L; 346.7 ± 106.1 pg/mL vs. 59.4 ± 37.3 pg/mL, P = 0.0368).

Discussion

Consistent with the findings of our previous study [28], the results of the current study further validated that the PI3K–AKT pathway plays a critical role in BM MФs supporting megakaryopoiesis in a BM MФ-specific PI3K-knockout mouse model. Additionally, the increased BM M1/M2 MФ ratio with impaired haematopoiesis-supporting abilities were observed in patients with ITP, which could be ameliorated by TPO in vitro. Mechanistically, our data indicated that the release of VEGF by M2 MФs might facilitate megakaryopoiesis through the upregulation of vWF-GPIb signalling in MKs. Therefore, the current study indicated that altering the PI3K–AKT pathway with TPO to modulate BM M1/M2 MФ polarization might be a potential mechanism through which haematopoiesis could be repaired in patients with ITP.

MФs are essential components of the BM haematopoietic microenvironment [16, 17, 19, 56], and the plasticity exhibited by activated MФs has been found to orchestrate haematopoietic processes [57]. Luo et al. reported that M2 MФs promoted HSC self-renewal, but M1 MФs showed an inverse trend. Specifically, M2 MФs promoted HSC expansion in coculture experiments and enhanced long-term repopulation ability in SCID mice [58]. In recent studies, increased M1/M2 MФ ratio has been shown to play an important role in pathological states [29, 59, 60]. An unbalanced BM M1/M2 ratio and dysfunctional BM MФs were shown to play a role in patients with poor graft function and in patients with thrombocytopenia after allo-HSCT [28, 29]. Notably, the derangement of cellular immunity has been proven to be crucial in the pathogenesis of ITP, manifested as a type-1 polarized immune response in MФs [61]. Consistent with these findings, our results revealed increased BM M1/M2 MФ ratio with a deficiency in haematopoiesis promotion ability in patients with ITP compared with HCs. Thus, it is important to explore how to restore aberrant MΦ polarization and improve the haematopoiesis-supporting ability of MΦs.

The PI3K–AKT pathway profoundly contributes to MФ polarization [62–66]. Genetic evidence has suggested that the PI3K–AKT pathway negatively regulates LPS-induced M1 MФ polarization [67]. Ruckerl et al. demonstrated that the PI3K–AKT pathway is a critical enhancer of M2 MФ polarization and proliferation [68]. Our previous study revealed that PI3K deficiency in BM MФs affected megakaryopoiesis and platelet production in BM MФ-specific PI3K-knockdown mice constructed using adeno-associated serotype 2 viral vectors [28]. In the current study, we provided further evidence that suppression of the PI3K–AKT pathway affected BM MФ polarization and megakaryopoiesis in BM MФ-specific PI3K-knockout mice, which were generated by CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Thus, upregulating the PI3K–AKT pathway to correct aberrant BM MФ polarization may be a potential therapy for improving haematopoiesis in patients with ITP.

TPO primarily plays a critical role in MKs and platelet production [69, 70]. Through the JAK–STAT, MAPK, and PI3K–AKT pathways in MKs, TPO promotes MK survival, proliferation, and polyploidization [71–73]. In addition, TPO affects the biology of HSCs [74, 75]. Fox et al. demonstrated that TPO strongly promoted the self-renewal and expansion of HSCs in mice receiving marrow transplantation [75]. Nevertheless, the regulatory effect of TPO on BM MФs and its underlying mechanism remain to be elucidated. Our data indicated that increased BM M1/M2 MФ ratio with declined levels of p-PI3K and p-AKT in MФs from patients with ITP which could be upregulated by TPO in vitro.

The rhTPO is a full-length glycosylated cytokine produced by Chinese hamster ovary cells that has the same amino acid sequence and similar pharmacological effects as endogenous TPO does. It has been approved by the China State Food and Drug Administration as a second-line treatment option for patients with ITP [76, 77]. Notably, the combination of dexamethasone with rhTPO is recommended as a frontline treatment for patients with ITP because it significantly improved the initial response in newly diagnosed patients with ITP in a prospective, multicentre, randomized trial [78]. According to our data, patients with corticosteroid-sensitive ITP showed a decreased BM M1/M2 ratio and an increased p-PI3K expression in MФs than those from patients with primary ITP. This is consistent with the anti-inflammatory functions of corticosteroid, which leads to the M2 phenotype in MФs [79]. Considering that rhTPO promoted haematopoiesis through reversing the increased BM M1/M2 MФ ratio, the better clinical response to the combination of dexamethasone with rhTPO [78], may be associated with the synergistic effect of rhTPO and corticosteroid on BM MФs. A similar mechanism was also observed for the use of TPO-receptor agonists (TPO-RAs) for the treatment of patients with aplastic anaemia [80]. Based on the previous work and the current study, we speculate that in addition to its direct effects on MKs and HSCs, TPO may promote haematopoiesis through an indirect mechanism depending on restoring both BM M1/M2 MФ polarization and MФ function.

Although further functional validation with a larger sample size and using primary human BM macrophages from ITP patients or in humanized ITP models is needed, our data indicated that VEGF might be one of the pivotal mediators of the crosstalk whereby BM MФs regulate MK function, which could be enhanced by TPO. We are aware, however, the concentration used and the short exposure time of rhTPO in vitro may not strictly reflect physiological or therapeutic conditions. Moreover, the downstream signalling mechanisms in regulating MKs will be further investigated in the future.

Conclusions

Overall, our findings revealed that increased BM M1/M2 ratio with downregulation of the PI3K–AKT pathway in MФs is associated with impaired megakaryopoiesis, which may be related to the occurrence of ITP. Moreover, we found that TPO improved the functions of BM MФs from patients with ITP by restoring MФ polarization in vitro, which may partially explain why patients with ITP respond better to corticosteroids combined with TPO therapy or other second-line treatment options, such as TPO-RA.
