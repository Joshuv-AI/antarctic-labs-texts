The C-terminal T234 residue of VP3 is required for KREMEN1 receptor-dependent infectivity and pathogenesis of coxsackieviruses A10 and A8
Authors: Xingyu Yan, Zeyu Liu, Kexin Liu, Zhenlin Yang, Jianxing Wang, Chao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coxsackievirus A10 (CVA10) is a major causative agent of hand, foot and mouth disease and utilizes KREMEN1 (KRM1) as its cellular receptor. While our previous work identifies VP2 residue K140 as a universal anchor for KRM1 binding among KRM1-utilizing enteroviruses, the functional significance of other receptor-interface residues remains poorly characterized. Here, through structure-guided mutagenesis, we demonstrate that VP3-T234, a completely conserved residue at the C-terminus of VP3, is essential for CVA10 infectivity. The T234A mutation does not affect virion assembly but abolishes both KRM1 binding and cellular attachment. Interestingly, this requirement shows remarkable virus specificity: the homologous residue is critical for CVA8, but is not required for other KRM1-utilizing enteroviruses including CVA2–CVA6 and CVA12. The T234A mutation significantly attenuates the pathogenesis of both CVA10 and CVA8 in neonatal mice. Moreover, the CVA8-T234A mutant provides complete protection as an attenuated vaccine against lethal CVA8 challenge. Our findings establish a model wherein KRM1 engagement relies on the conserved VP2-K140 anchor complemented by virus-specific secondary residues, with VP3-T234 representing a key determinant for CVA10 and CVA8. These insights advance our understanding of enterovirus-receptor interactions and provide new directions for vaccine development.

Introduction

Species A enteroviruses (EV-As) are significant human pathogens responsible for a spectrum of diseases, including hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD), herpangina, and severe neurological complications (Howley et al., 2022). Among them, coxsackievirus A10 (CVA10) has emerged as one of the leading causes of HFMD outbreaks worldwide, posing a substantial health threat to infants and young children (Fu et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2025). The clinical burden of CVA10 and related EV-As underscores the urgent need for effective prophylactic measures, which require a deep understanding of the initial step of infection: viral entry into host cells.

Enterovirus cellular entry is mediated by specific interactions between the viral capsid and cellular receptors. A major group of EV-As, including CVA2, CVA3, CVA4, CVA5, CVA6, CVA8, CVA10, and CVA12, utilizes KREMEN1 (KRM1) as a functional receptor (Staring et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2025). Structural analysis of the CVA10–KRM1 complex shows that KRM1 spans the canyon, a characteristic surface depression, and contacts residues from all three major capsid proteins (VP1–VP3) (Cui et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2020). In particular, the VP2 EF-loop (residues 138–143) has been identified as a critical region for receptor engagement (Cui et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2020). Moreover, this region constitutes a neutralizing epitope, as antibodies against the VP2 EF-loop have been shown to confer protective immunity in mice (Dai et al., 2019). Structural and functional studies further revealed that within this loop, VP2-K140 acts as a universal anchor essential for KRM1 binding among all KRM1-utilizing enteroviruses (Cui et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2020; Ke et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025), whereas VP2-N142 is specifically required for CVA10 infection (Li et al., 2023). Despite these insights, the molecular landscape of KRM1 recognition remains incomplete. It is still unknown which additional residues in VP1 and VP3 contribute to KRM1 receptor binding among different enteroviruses. Moreover, how KRM1 accommodates the substantial capsid sequence variation among enterovirus serotypes while maintaining broad recognition is not understood. These questions motivate the current study.

In this study, we systematically characterized the functional contributions of capsid residues in VP1 and VP3 at the CVA10–KRM1 interface through structure-guided mutagenesis. We identified VP3-T234 as a residue essential for CVA10 infectivity, whose mutation disrupts KRM1 binding while maintaining virion assembly. Extending this analysis across KRM1-utilizing enteroviruses revealed a virus-specific requirement for the homologous site: VP3-234 is critical for CVA10 and CVA8, but is not required for CVA2–CVA6 and CVA12. The VP3-T234A mutation markedly attenuated the pathogenesis of both CVA10 and CVA8 in neonatal mice. Furthermore, the CVA8 VP3-T234A mutant served as an attenuated vaccine that conferred full protection against lethal wild-type CVA8 challenge. Together, these findings establish a model of KRM1 engagement featuring the conserved VP2-K140 anchor complemented by virus-specific residues such as VP3-T234 in CVA10 and CVA8, advancing our understanding of enterovirus–receptor interactions and informing vaccine development.

Results

The VP3-T234 residue is critical for CVA10 viability

Our previous studies identified VP2-K140 and VP2-N142 as essential residues for CVA10 interaction with the KRM1 receptor and for viral infectivity (Li et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025). To systematically investigate whether additional capsid residues contribute to CVA10 infection, we analyzed the cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the CVA10–KRM1 complex (PDB: 7BZU) (Cui et al., 2020). This analysis revealed six residues in VP1 (T163, T209, S217, R288, D289, and R290) and eight in VP3 (R90, T180, G181, G182, D185, E232, T234, and V238) that form close contacts with the KRM1 receptor. We therefore selected these residues for further functional analysis.

Each residue was individually mutated to alanine in a T7 promoter-driven CVA10 infectious clone (strain Kowalik) (Li et al., 2023). The mutant plasmids were co-transfected into HEK 293T cells together with a T7 RNA polymerase (RNA-pol)-expressing plasmid to rescue the corresponding CVA10 mutant viruses. The rescued viruses were propagated in human rhabdomyosarcoma (RD) cells, sequenced, titrated using the TCID50 assay, and assessed for sensitivity to neutralization by soluble hKRM1-Fc (human KRM1 ectodomain fused to the Fc portion of human IgG1 (Li et al., 2023)) (Fig. 1A). All CVA10 mutants, with the exception of VP3-T234A, produced detectable viral titers, although the titers varied among the different mutants. VP3-T234A is hereafter referred to as T3234A, following the enterovirus structural biology convention where VP3 residues are numbered starting from 3001. The viable CVA10 mutants retained sensitivity to neutralization by hKRM1-Fc (Fig. 1B). Structural analysis of the CVA10–KRM1 complex (PDB: 7BZU) showed that Thr234, situated in the VP3 C-terminal region, is positioned at the virus-receptor interface in close proximity to KRM1 (Fig. 1C and D). Among all KRM1-contacting residues identified within a 4 Å cutoff, VP3-T234 forms specific atom-to-atom contacts with KRM1 residues H126, G127, and N128 (Cui et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2020) (Fig. 1D and E). These structural observations suggest that VP3-T234 may contribute to KRM1 receptor recognition through its spatial location and atomic contacts (Cui et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2020). Notably, the previously identified universal anchor VP2-K140 (Liu et al., 2025) is located on the opposite side of the canyon region from VP3-T234 (Fig. 1C and D).

The CVA10 T3234A mutant assembles into virions in 293T cells but fails to initiate infection in RD cells

The enterovirus life cycle includes attachment, internalization, uncoating, replication, assembly, and release. Transfection of HEK 293T cells with a CVA10 infectious clone bypasses the early entry steps (attachment, internalization, and uncoating), enabling direct viral replication followed by particle assembly and release. We used this system to determine whether the T3234A change interferes with replication, assembly, or release. CVA10-WT or CVA10-T3234A infectious clones were co-transfected with a T7 RNA-pol plasmid into 293T cells. We first analyzed the 293T culture supernatants by Western blot using anti-CVA10 VP0, VP1, and VP3 antibodies. A characteristic 25–40 kDa protein profile (corresponding to VP0, VP2, VP1, and VP3) was clearly detected in both CVA10-WT and T3234A samples, while mock-transfected supernatants (empty vector plus T7 RNA-pol) showed no signal (Fig. 2B). This result indicates that the T3234A mutation does not disrupt capsid protein expression or proteolytic processing.

We next used a sandwich ELISA to assess whether the capsid proteins assemble into structurally intact virions. Viral particles were captured with a polyclonal anti-CVA10 antibody and detected with monoclonal antibody (MAb) 2A9, which recognizes a conformation-dependent epitope present only in properly folded capsids (Fig. 2C). CVA10-WT and T3234A samples produced similar signals (Fig. 2D), demonstrating that the T3234A mutation does not prevent the formation and release of antigenically intact viral particles.

We next asked whether the assembled T3234A mutant virions are capable of establishing a productive infection in RD cells. Supernatants from transfected 293T cells were used to inoculate RD cells. After 1 h of adsorption, unbound virus was removed by PBS washing, and the cells were further cultured for 2 days to allow viral replication and spread. The resulting RD cell supernatants were collected for analysis (Fig. 2E).

Western blot analysis of the RD culture supernatants revealed strong signals for VP0, VP2, VP1, and VP3 in CVA10-WT-infected cultures, demonstrating robust viral propagation. In contrast, no capsid proteins were detected in supernatants from RD cells inoculated with the T3234A mutant or with mock-transfected 293T supernatants (Fig. 2F). Consistently, sandwich ELISA analysis of the same RD culture supernatants showed high levels of viral particles only in the WT group, while signals for the T3234A mutant remained at background levels, comparable to the mock control (Fig. 2G).

Together, these results indicate that while the CVA10-T3234A mutant assembles into antigenically intact viral particles in 293T cells, these particles are unable to initiate a productive infection in RD cells. This defect suggests that the VP3-T234 residue plays a role in early infection steps, likely attachment or entry into susceptible cells.

SDS-PAGE analysis of the purified preparations confirmed the presence of the capsid proteins VP1, VP2, and VP3 for both viruses (Fig. 3B), indicating successful isolation of viral particles with intact protein composition. Furthermore, negative-stain electron microscopy revealed that both purified preparations consisted predominantly of ∼30-nm spherical particles exhibiting typical enterovirus morphology (Fig. 3C), confirming the integrity of the virions for subsequent analyses. To evaluate whether the purified virions maintained antigenic integrity, we assessed their reactivity with anti-CVA10 polyclonal sera by ELISA. Both purified CVA10-WT and CVA10-T3234A particles exhibited efficient, dose-dependent binding to the anti-CVA10 sera, with comparable binding curves (Fig. 3D). This result indicates that the T3234A mutation does not alter the overall capsid conformation.

We next compared the infectivity of purified viruses. When diluted to an equal protein concentration (1 μg/mL) and titrated on RD cells, CVA10-WT yielded a titer of 106.43 TCID50/mL, whereas CVA10-T3234A reached only 102.29 TCID50/mL (Fig. 3E). This represents a reduction in infectivity of more than 10,000-fold, establishing a direct link between the T3234A mutation and a loss of viral function.

Since cellular attachment is the critical first step in enterovirus infection, we examined whether the infectivity defect stemmed from a binding deficiency. RD cells were incubated with purified virions (0.1 μg/mL) at 4 °C for 2 h, and cell-associated virus was quantified by reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). The T3234A mutant exhibited approximately 70% reduced binding to RD cells compared to the WT virus (Fig. 3F).

To determine whether this cell binding defect resulted from impaired receptor interaction, we performed receptor-binding ELISA. ELISA plates were coated with serially diluted CVA10 WT and T3234A virions and then probed with hKRM1-Fc. While CVA10-WT bound to hKRM1-Fc in a virus dose-dependent manner, CVA10-T3234A showed no detectable binding even at the highest concentration tested (4 μg/mL) (Fig. 3G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the T3234A mutation abrogates the ability of CVA10 to bind the KRM1 receptor, accounting for the severe defects in cellular attachment and infectivity observed in RD cells.

The VP3-T234 residue is essential for CVA10 pathogenicity in neonatal mice

To evaluate the role of VP3-T234 in CVA10 pathogenesis, two-day-old ICR mice (n = 11 per group) were intraperitoneally (i.p.) inoculated with purified CVA10-WT or CVA10-T3234A virions (10−4 ng per pup). Mice inoculated with CVA10-WT rapidly developed progressive clinical signs, including limb weakness and paralysis, and reached 100% mortality by 6 days post-infection (dpi). In contrast, the CVA10-T3234A mutant exhibited significantly attenuated virulence, with 73% of pups (8/11) surviving throughout the observation period (P < 0.0001, Log-rank test; Fig. 4A). To investigate the virological basis for this attenuation, tissue viral loads were assessed at 4 dpi. Limb muscle and spinal cord, the major sites of CVA10 replication, were harvested, and viral RNA was extracted and quantified by RT-qPCR (experimental timeline in Fig. 4B). TCID50 titration was not performed for the CVA10-T3234A mutant, because this virus cannot productively infect RD cells (Figs. 2 and 3), making infectivity-based quantification unfeasible. The CVA10-T3234A mutant displayed markedly reduced replication in both tissues (Fig. 4C). In limb muscle, viral genome copies in the CVA10-T3234A group ranged from 4.1 × 104 to 5.6 × 106 per mg tissue (geometric mean = 4.0 × 105), representing a 138-fold reduction compared to the CVA10-WT group (range: 2.2 × 107 to 1.7 × 108; geometric mean = 5.5 × 107). Similarly, in spinal cord, the CVA10-T3234A mutant showed a 100-fold decrease (geometric mean = 1.4 × 105) relative to WT (geometric mean = 1.4 × 107). These results demonstrate that the T3234A mutation severely impairs viral replication in vivo. Collectively, the consistency between survival, clinical disease, and tissue viral loads indicates that the T3234A mutation attenuates CVA10 lethality by drastically reducing viral replication at the primary sites of infection.

To assess virus-induced tissue damage, limb muscle samples from infected and PBS-injected control mice were harvested at 4 dpi for histological analysis. H&E-stained sections were evaluated for myositis and assigned a severity score (0: normal; 1: mild; 2: moderate; 3: severe). All mice in the CVA10-WT group exhibited mild to severe myofiber necrosis, with cases distributed as 3 mild, 5 moderate, and 5 severe. In contrast, the CVA10-T3234A group showed markedly less pathology: 2 of 13 mice displayed normal muscle architecture, 9 exhibited mild necrosis, and only 2 showed moderate damage, with no severe cases observed (Fig. 4D). Consequently, the mean histopathologic score for the CVA10-T3234A group was significantly lower than that of the WT group (Fig. 4E). This reduction in muscle damage is consistent with the decreased viral loads observed in these tissues (Fig. 4C), demonstrating that the T3234A mutation attenuates CVA10-induced muscle damage in vivo.

The VP3-T234 residue is critical for CVA8 infectivity

Multiple enteroviruses, including CVA2, CVA3, CVA4, CVA5, CVA6, CVA8, and CVA12, have been reported to employ KRM1 as a cellular receptor (Staring et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2025). To determine whether the VP3-T234 residue is conserved among these viruses, we retrieved all available VP3 sequences from the NCBI database (as of September 2025) and performed a multiple sequence alignment. The alignment revealed that this position is strictly conserved as a threonine (T) in a subset of these viruses, including CVA10, CVA2, CVA3, CVA4, and CVA8. The VP3-T234 residue is critical for CVA10 infectivity (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Given its strict conservation as a threonine in CVA2, CVA3, CVA4, and CVA8, we next asked whether this residue is functionally important for these viruses. To address this, we utilized previously established chimeric infectious clones of CVA2 (strain HN), CVA3 (strain XZ), CVA4 (strain FJ), and CVA8 (strain FJ), in which the open reading frame (ORF) of the CVA10 backbone was replaced with the corresponding ORF from each virus (Liu et al., 2025). We introduced the T3234A mutation into each of these clones (Fig. 5B). The wild-type and mutant viruses were generated in 293T cells following co-transfection of the respective chimeric infectious clone plasmid together with a T7 RNA-pol plasmid, amplified in RD cells, and then subjected to titration, sequencing, and neutralization sensitivity assays using soluble hKRM1-Fc. The CVA2-, CVA3-, and CVA4-T3234A mutants all yielded detectable viral titers, although these were reduced by at least one log10 compared to their respective wild-type counterparts (Fig. 5C). Notably, all three mutants remained susceptible to neutralization by soluble hKRM1-Fc. In contrast, the CVA8-T3234A mutant produced no detectable infectious titer, suggesting that the VP3-T234 residue is critical for CVA8 viability (Fig. 5C).

To determine whether the T3234A mutation affects CVA8 replication, assembly, or release independently of viral entry, we bypassed receptor-mediated entry by transfecting 293T cells with chimeric CVA8 infectious clones (containing the CVA8 ORF flanked by CVA10 UTRs), either wild-type or carrying the T3234A mutation (Fig. 5B). Western blot analysis of the 293T cell supernatants collected at 72 h post-transfection revealed comparable levels of the capsid proteins VP0, VP2, VP1, and VP3 in both the wild-type and T3234A samples, whereas mock-transfected controls showed no signal (Fig. 5D). This indicates that the T3234A mutation does not impair capsid protein expression, assembly, or particle release.

We next assessed the infectivity of the released CVA8 particles by inoculating RD cells with the 293T-derived supernatants. After adsorption at 37 °C for 1 h, unbound virions were removed by washing, and the cells were further cultured for 2 days. Analysis of the resulting RD cell supernatants showed strong viral protein signals in cultures inoculated with CVA8-WT, indicative of productive infection and viral spread. In contrast, no capsid proteins were detected in RD supernatants exposed to the CVA8-T3234A mutant (Fig. 5E). Together, these findings suggest that while the CVA8-T3234A mutant assembles into and releases viral particles, these particles are non-infectious due to a defect in initiating infection in susceptible cells. This highlights an essential role for the VP3-T234 residue in an early step of the CVA8 life cycle, likely attachment or cellular entry.

We next asked whether the divergent residues at VP3-234—serine in CVA5 and CVA12, and glutamine in CVA6—contribute to viral infectivity and receptor usage. To test this, we introduced the S3234A mutation into chimeric infectious clones of CVA5 (strain ZZ285) and CVA12 (strain Texas) (Liu et al., 2025), and the Q3234A mutation into a native CVA6 (strain 141) infectious clone (Yang et al., 2015) (Fig. 5B). The resulting mutants—CVA5-S3234A, CVA12-S3234A, and CVA6-Q3234A—were successfully rescued. All three mutants produced viral titers comparable to their wild-type counterparts and remained fully sensitive to neutralization by soluble hKRM1-Fc (Fig. 5C), indicating unimpaired receptor interaction. These results demonstrate that, in contrast to the critical threonine in CVA10 and CVA8, the VP3-S234 and Q234 residues are not required for infectivity or KRM1 interaction in CVA5/CVA12 and CVA6, respectively. This functional divergence underscores the virus-specific role of VP3-234 among KRM1-utilizing enteroviruses.

Due to the inability of the CVA8-T3234A mutant to infect RD cells (Fig. 5, Fig. 6), TCID50 titration could not be applied; therefore, viral RNA quantification was used. To assess muscle pathology, limb sections from CVA8-infected mice were examined at 4 dpi (Fig. 7B). Based on H&E-stained sections, myositis was scored on a severity scale: 0: normal; 1: mild; 2: moderate; 3: severe. All animals in the CVA8-WT group developed moderate to severe myofiber necrosis, with 5 exhibiting moderate and 6 displaying severe pathology. By contrast, the CVA8-T3234A group showed minimal muscle damage: 5 of 11 mice presented normal tissue architecture, and the remaining 6 displayed only mild necrosis, with no moderate or severe cases detected (Fig. 7D). Accordingly, the mean histopathologic score was significantly lower in CVA8-T3234A-infected mice than in WT-infected controls (Fig. 7E). The concordance between reduced viral loads (Fig. 7C) and diminished muscle injury (Fig. 7D and E) indicates that the T3234A mutation limits CVA8-induced myopathology in vivo.

Discussion

Enterovirus infection is initiated by specific interactions between viral capsid and cellular receptors. We previously demonstrated that VP2-K140 acts as a universal and essential anchor for KRM1 binding among all KRM1-utilizing enteroviruses (Liu et al., 2025). Specifically, VP2-K140 forms hydrogen bonds and salt bridges with KRM1 residues D88 and D90, providing the primary binding energy (Liu et al., 2025). Extending this finding, we now propose a refined model of KRM1 receptor engagement in which this conserved primary site (VP2-K140) is functionally complemented by virus-specific secondary residues to enable productive infection. A key illustration of this complementary mechanism is provided by the residue VP3-T234. In CVA10, the T3234A mutation abolished infectivity by specifically disrupting KRM1 receptor binding, without affecting capsid assembly or release (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). This phenotype was conserved in CVA8, where the same mutation also severely impaired KRM1 binding and infectivity (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). In contrast, alanine substitutions at the homologous site in CVA2–CVA6 and CVA12 had minimal impact on viral fitness and these mutants remained sensitive to neutralization by soluble KRM1-Fc (Fig. 5). Together, these results underscore a divergent evolutionary strategy among KRM1-utilizing enteroviruses: while all rely on the conserved VP2-K140 anchor, each employs a distinct set of secondary residues—such as VP3-T234 in CVA10 and CVA8, and other unidentified residues in remaining serotypes—to facilitate effective KRM1 receptor engagement. Consistent with this, as shown in Fig. 1C and D, VP2-K140 and VP3-T234 are located on opposite sides of the CVA10 canyon region, together anchoring the KRM1 receptor.

A key finding of this study is that VP3-T234 is critical for KRM1 binding in a virus-specific manner, despite being highly conserved in multiple other enterovirus species (Fig. 5). We speculate this virus-specific dependency stems from differences in the structural microenvironment of the receptor-binding canyon. Even if the residue itself is conserved (threonine in CVA2-CVA4, CVA8, and CVA10), subtle variations in neighboring amino acids or the local backbone conformation may alter its precise positioning and interaction geometry with KRM1. In CVA10 and CVA8, VP3-T234 appears essential for establishing productive receptor contacts, whereas in CVA2-CVA4, alternative residues in the vicinity may compensate for its loss. This hypothesis remains speculative, and definitive answers will require future determination of the cryo-EM structures of KRM1 complexes with CVA2, CVA3, CVA4, and CVA8, followed by comparative analysis with the already reported CVA10-KRM1 structures (Cui et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2020). Given that our functional conclusions for each serotype are based on a single isolate, the question of whether these findings can be generalized naturally arises. However, analysis of all available VP3 sequences retrieved from the NCBI database as of September 2025 revealed 100% or near-100% conservation of residue 234 within each serotype (Fig. 5A), strongly suggesting that the observed virus-specific requirement is a stable serotype trait applicable to other clinical isolates. Nevertheless, validation with multiple strains per serotype would be important and necessary.

The impaired KRM1 receptor binding resulting from the T3234A mutation led to significantly attenuated virulence in vivo. Both CVA10 and CVA8 mutants were highly attenuated in neonatal mice (Fig. 4, Fig. 7). Although these mutants failed to establish productive infection in cell culture (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 5, Fig. 6), they still replicated to detectable—yet substantially lower—levels in mouse tissues, with viral RNA levels at least one order of magnitude below wild-type (Fig. 4, Fig. 7). This residual replication may indicate the presence of alternative entry pathways in the complex in vivo environment. Importantly, the markedly reduced viral loads were directly associated with diminished histopathological damage and significantly improved survival rates (Fig. 4, Fig. 7). This clear relationship between impaired receptor binding in vitro—complete for CVA10 (Fig. 3G) and partial for CVA8 (Fig. 6F)—and attenuated disease in vivo (Fig. 4, Fig. 7) underscores the functional importance of this virus-specific site.

The functional significance of VP3-T234 extends beyond viral entry to inform potential vaccine design. We therefore assessed whether the CVA8-T3234A mutant could serve as an attenuated vaccine candidate. Neonatal mice receiving prime-boost immunization with this candidate were fully protected against subsequent lethal challenge with a heterologous CVA8 strain (Fig. 8). These immunized animals not only survived but also showed substantially reduced viral replication and markedly less muscle damage compared to controls (Fig. 8). Together, these results validate a targeted vaccine strategy: by precisely disrupting key receptor-binding residues, we can generate attenuated yet highly immunogenic candidates against KRM1-dependent enteroviruses. Nevertheless, because the CVA8-T3234A mutant relies on a single amino acid substitution, the error-prone nature of the viral RNA polymerase poses a potential risk of genetic reversion and restored virulence. To mitigate this concern, future vaccine development should combine additional attenuating mutations, such as those at other critical receptor-binding residues (e.g., VP2-K140), thereby reducing the likelihood of simultaneous reversion and improving safety.

Our functional analysis of the CVA10-KRM1 binding interface revealed that alanine substitution at specific VP1 residues—including T209, R288, and R290—severely impaired viral infectivity (by up to 5.5 log10) without conferring strong resistance to KRM1-Fc neutralization (Fig. 1B). This dissociation between infectivity and receptor binding indicates that these residues are not essential for KRM1 engagement, but may instead mediate interactions with other host factors required for productive infection, such as co-receptors or entry-facilitating proteins. These results support a model in which CVA10 entry involves a multi-step process that extends beyond KRM1 binding, explaining the profound infectivity loss observed in these mutants despite their retained receptor recognition.

Conclusions

This work delineates a refined model for enterovirus engagement of the KRM1 receptor, comprising a universal anchor (VP2-K140) supplemented by virus-specific secondary residues, with VP3-T234 being critical for CVA10 and CVA8. The profound attenuation of the T3234A mutant in vivo, coupled with its efficacy as an attenuated vaccine, directly links these molecular interactions to pathogenesis and protective immunity. Our findings thus bridge fundamental virology and translational immunology, highlighting the capsid-receptor interface as a promising target for developing interventions against KRM1-utilizing enteroviruses.

Materials and methods

Cells and viruses

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RD; ATCC CCL-136) and human embryonic kidney (HEK 293T; ATCC CRL-3216) cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C. CVA8 strain 14-943/GS/CHN/2014 (GS14; GenBank ID: MT648781) (Song et al., 2020) was propagated in RD cells. Viral titer was determined using the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay on RD cells.

Proteins and antibodies

The recombinant His-tagged hKRM1–Fc protein, comprising the human KRM1 ectodomain (residues A23–G373) fused to a human IgG1 Fc tag, was expressed in HEK 293F cells and purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography (Li et al., 2023). Polyclonal antibodies against CVA10 VP0, VP1, and VP3 were raised in rabbits by immunization with the corresponding E. coli-expressed recombinant proteins of the CVA10 Kowalik strain (GenBank ID: AY421767), following established protocols (Zhang et al., 2018). Similarly, polyclonal antibodies against CVA8 VP0, VP1, and VP3 were generated in BALB/c mice using recombinant proteins derived from the CVA8 GS14 strain. Anti-CVA10 and anti-CVA8 sera were generated by immunizing BALB/c mice with purified CVA10 (strain Kowalik) or CVA8 (strain GS14) virions formulated in alum adjuvant (Li et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025). The CVA10-neutralizing MAb 2A9 (mouse IgG) was generated in our laboratory according to established protocols (Zhang et al., 2021, 2022).

Structural analysis of the CVA10–KRM1 complex

The structural analysis was performed using the published cryo-EM structure of the CVA10-KRM1 complex (PDB: 7BZU), solved at pH 5.5 (Cui et al., 2020). All structural figures were prepared using UCSF Chimera (v1.15) (Pettersen et al., 2004).

Recovery of wild-type and mutant enteroviruses

The wild-type CVA10 infectious clone (pVAX-CVA10-WT) of the Kowalik strain (GenBank: AY421767) was synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China) (Li et al., 2023) and used as a positive control. Mutant CVA10 clones were generated from this template by site-directed mutagenesis using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), with all constructs verified by sequencing.

Chimeric infectious clones for CVA2, CVA3, CVA4, CVA8, and CVA12 were constructed in our previous study (Liu et al., 2025) by replacing the ORF of the CVA10-Kowalik infectious clone with the corresponding ORF from the following strains: CVA2 strain HN202009 (HN; GenBank: MT992622) (Ji et al., 2021), CVA3 strain XZ99086-1999 (XZ; National Microbiology Data Center [NMDC]: NMDCN0003ADI), CVA4 strain 2012FJND201 (FJ; NMDC: NMDCN0005SQA), CVA8 strain 2011FJQZ146 (FJ; NMDC: NMDCN0005SQC), or CVA12 strain Texas-12 (Texas; GenBank: AY421768). In this study, we extended this approach to generate a new chimeric clone for CVA5 using the ORF from strain 2023ZZ285 (ZZ285; NMDC: NMDCN0007O5O). The CVA6 infectious clone (pSVA-CVA6-141), derived from strain TW-2007-00141 (141; GenBank ID: KR706309) (Yang et al., 2015), was kindly provided by Dr. Tong Cheng. Site-directed mutagenesis was subsequently performed on all parental clones (CVA2–CVA6, CVA8, CVA12) to generate the respective mutant viruses.

To rescue viruses, HEK 293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 2 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. Transfection was performed using a mixture of 200 ng infectious clone plasmid and 300 ng T7 RNA polymerase plasmid in 150 μL Opti-MEM (BasalMedia, Shanghai, China), combined with 1.5 μL Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) in 150 μL Opti-MEM. After 30 min incubation, the mixture was added to the 293T cells. The medium was replaced with DMEM containing 1% FBS at 6 h post-transfection, and virus-containing supernatants were collected at 72 h.

Supernatants from transfected 293T cells were used to inoculate RD cells for subsequent analysis. For initial screening and titer determination, the supernatants were directly added to RD cells in 24-well plates, and culture supernatants were harvested at 72 h post-infection for RT-PCR sequencing and TCID50 assays. For detailed characterization of selected mutants, an adsorption-based protocol was employed: RD cells were inoculated with 293T-derived supernatant at 37 °C for 1 h, washed with PBS to remove unbound virus, and further cultured for 48 h before supernatant and cell lysates were collected for Western blot or ELISA.

Viral neutralization assay

Serial dilutions of hKRM1-Fc protein (50 μL/well) were mixed with an equal volume of virus preparation containing 100 TCID50 of wild-type or mutant strains in 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Then, 20,000 RD cells were added to each well. Cytopathic effects (CPE) were recorded after 3 days of culture. The neutralization endpoint was defined as the lowest protein concentration that completely inhibited CPE.

SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis

Protein samples were denatured in SDS loading buffer at 100 °C for 10 min and separated by SDS-PAGE. Gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (Sangon, Shanghai, China) for total protein visualization. For western blotting, proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes and probed with the following primary antibodies: horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-β-actin antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), rabbit polyclonal antibodies against CVA10 VP0, VP1, and VP3, or mouse polyclonal antibodies against CVA8 VP0, VP1, and VP3. After incubation with species-matched HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech), protein bands were visualized using an ECL substrate (Merck).

Sandwich ELISA

Biotinylation of the anti-CVA10 MAb 2A9 was performed using EZ-Link™ Sulfo-NHS-LC-LC-Biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by purification with Zeba™ spin desalting columns as directed by the manufacturer. For the ELISA procedure, plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with mouse anti-CVA10 polyclonal serum (1:1000 in PBS). After blocking with 5% skim milk in PBST for 1 h at 37 °C, 50 μL of cell culture supernatant containing virus was added per well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Following PBST washes, biotinylated MAb 2A9 (50 ng/well) was applied and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. After additional washes, streptavidin-HRP (1:5000; Proteintech) was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Signals were developed with TMB substrate for 5 min, the reaction was stopped, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Virus purification

Recombinant CVA10 and CVA8 viruses were rescued by transfecting 293T cells in 15-cm dishes with corresponding infectious clone plasmids and T7 RNA polymerase plasmid using polyethylenimine (PEI) max 40K (PolySciences). Briefly, for each 15-cm dish, 15.3 μg of infectious clone plasmid and 22.9 μg of T7 RNA polymerase plasmid (total DNA 38.2 μg) were co-transfected using 114 μg of PEI (1 mg/mL), corresponding to a mass ratio of DNA to PEI of approximately 1 to 3. At 48 h post-transfection, culture supernatants were harvested and subjected to precipitation with 10% polyethylene glycol (PEG) 8000 (Sangon, Shanghai, China) and 200 mM NaCl at 4 °C overnight. After centrifugation, the precipitate was resuspended in 0.15 M PBS and clarified by low-speed centrifugation. The resuspended virus was then purified through a 20% sucrose cushion by ultracentrifugation at 27,000 rpm for 4 h, followed by additional purification on a 10%–50% sucrose gradient at 39,000 rpm for 3 h. Virus-containing fractions were identified by SDS-PAGE, and protein concentrations were quantified using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Electron microscopy

Purified virus samples (100 μg/mL in PBS) were adsorbed onto glow-discharged carbon-coated grids, negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate, and air-dried. Images were acquired using a Tecnai G2 Spirit transmission electron microscope (FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA) operated at 200 kV.

Antibody-binding ELISA

Purified CVA10 or CVA8 viral particles were serially diluted in PBS and coated onto ELISA plates overnight at 4 °C. After blocking with 5% milk-PBST, the plates were incubated with mouse anti-CVA10 serum (1:10,000) or anti-CVA8 serum (1:2000) in 1% milk-PBST for 1 h at 37 °C. Following PBST washes, goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (1:10,000; Proteintech) was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Color development was performed using TMB substrate, the reaction was stopped, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Virus attachment assay

To assess virus attachment, RD cells in 24-well plates were cooled and incubated with purified CVA10 or CVA8 particles (0.1 μg/mL, 500 μL/well) at 4 °C for 2 h. After binding, unbound viruses were removed with cold PBS washes. Total RNA was extracted with Trizol reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and cDNA was subsequently generated using PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). Quantitative PCR was carried out with SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara). Viral RNA levels were quantified and normalized to β-actin mRNA. The qPCR amplification primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Receptor-binding ELISA

To compare the receptor-binding activities of wild-type and mutant CVA10 and CVA8 viruses, serial dilutions of purified viral particles were immobilized on ELISA plates overnight at 4 °C. After blocking with 5% milk, the plates were incubated with 100 ng/well of hKRM1-Fc for 1 h at room temperature. Following washes, bound hKRM1-Fc was detected using HRP-conjugated anti-human IgG (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) with an additional 1 h incubation at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm after color development.

In vivo infection assay

Two-day-old ICR mice were intraperitoneally (i.p.) inoculated with purified wild-type and mutant CVA10 (10−4 ng/pup) or CVA8 (5 × 10−4 ng/pup) virions to assess viral virulence. Mice were observed daily for 14 days for survival and clinical symptoms, which were scored as follows: 0, healthy; 1, reduced mobility; 2, limb weakness; 3, paralysis; 4, death.

To quantify tissue viral loads, mice were sacrificed at 4 days post-infection (dpi). Limb muscle and spinal cord samples were processed, with tissue homogenates prepared in Trizol reagent. Viral RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis were performed as described above, using infectious clone plasmids as standards for absolute quantification.

For histopathological examination, limb muscle tissues harvested at 4 dpi were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, processed for longitudinal sectioning, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) by Servicebio Company (Wuhan, China), with subsequent examination by light microscopy. Histopathological alterations were scored using a semi-quantitative 0–3 system as follows: Grade 0, normal muscle architecture; Grade 1 (mild), local myocyte lesions or multifocal destruction with mild inflammatory processes; Grade 2 (moderate), widespread degradation of myofibrils or diffuse inflammatory processes; Grade 3 (severe), marked necrosis with diffuse inflammatory processes and structural collapse.

Vaccine efficacy of CVA8-T3234A in neonatal mice

One-day-old ICR mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Mice were primed via intraperitoneal injection with purified CVA8-T3234A virions (5 × 10−3 ng per pup) or PBS (control) on postnatal day 1, followed by a booster immunization with the same dose on day 6. On day 12, mice were challenged i.p. with 3.2 × 106 TCID50 per pup of wild-type CVA8 (strain GS14) and monitored daily for 14 days for survival and clinical signs (scoring criteria as described above).

For virological and histopathological analysis, a parallel cohort of mice was euthanized at 4 days post-challenge. Limb muscle and spinal cord tissues were harvested. Viral titers in tissue homogenates were determined by TCID50 assay on RD cells, and muscle pathology was evaluated on H&E-stained sections as described above.

Sequence conservation analysis

To assess the conservation of residue 234 in the VP3 protein, VP3 sequences from CVA2, CVA3, CVA4, CVA5, CVA6, CVA8, CVA10, and CVA12 were retrieved through a BLASTP search of the NCBI protein database (as of September 2025). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using CLC Sequence Viewer (v8.0), with specific attention to the conservation status of position 234 across the aligned sequences.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.
