Recurrent Left-Sided Stroke
Authors: Khadijah T. Hussain, Najah Khan, Poulina Q. Uddin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke management proves challenging with caseous necrotic mitral valve and aortic valve Lambl's excrescences.Case SummaryA 68-year-old woman presented with right-sided weakness. Four months prior, she had a left middle cerebral artery cerebrovascular accident and was started on aspirin, clopidogrel, statin, and angiotensin receptor blocker. New left-sided infarcts were identified, without significant carotid disease. Findings on transesophageal echocardiography indicated severe triple-vessel coronary artery disease, mitral caseous necrosis without valvular dysfunction, and Lambl's excrescences. Clopidogrel was switched to apixaban, and a loop recorder was placed. Coronary revascularization was deferred, with plans for surgical management of the Lambl's excrescences.DiscussionRecurrent left-sided strokes without significant carotid disease suggest a cryptogenic etiology. Caseous mitral necrosis and Lambl's excrescence are uncommon causes of cardioembolic stroke, but they must be considered. Management is not standardized but typically involves antiplatelets or anticoagulation, and surgical options must also be discussed.

History of Presentation

A 68-year-old woman presented with right arm numbness and tingling as well as weakness and dysarthria that started the night before admission. She sought medical attention the following morning when her symptoms did not improve. These symptoms were reminiscent of those that she had with a stroke 4 months earlier. She had been adherent to the aspirin, clopidogrel, and statin since that time. She denied associated headache, nausea, chest pain, shortness of breath, or palpitations. She denied tobacco or alcohol use. On admission, her blood pressure was 186/65 mm Hg, with heart rate of 71 beats/min, respiratory rate of 17 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation of 99% on room air. Her physical examination was notable for slowed speech, with word-finding difficulties and paraphasia. Her face was symmetric with no drift in extremities, and sensation to touch was intact and symmetric. Finger-tap testing was slightly slower on the right. She had no frank ataxia on heel-to-shin testing. No specific abnormalities were appreciated on cardiac or pulmonary auscultation.

Past Medical History

In addition to the left middle cerebral artery cerebrovascular accident 4 months prior to the current admission, the patient also had severe right carotid artery stenosis, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, hypothyroidism, and type 2 diabetes mellitus. She had a history of endocervical endometrioid adenocarcinoma and thyroid cancer.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included stroke or transient ischemic attack, with the differential for etiologies including ischemic stroke from hyperlipidemia or carotid disease, hypercoagulable state in the setting of history of malignancies, thromboembolic stroke from atrial fibrillation or endocarditis, and cryptogenic stroke. Less likely differentials included mass occupying cerebral lesion, autoimmune condition such as multiple sclerosis, and spinal cord injury.

Investigations

Computed tomography (CT) of the head and CT angiogram of the head and neck did not demonstrate acute bleed or large-vessel occlusion. The patient underwent brain magnetic resonance imaging without contrast, which demonstrated new left frontal, temporal, and parietal infarcts. Carotid ultrasound showed calcified plaque in the right carotid bulb and proximal right internal carotid artery resulting in >70% diameter reduction, but less than near-occlusion. The left internal carotid artery was noted to have mild calcified plaque with <50% stenosis.

Transthoracic echocardiogram showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 68%, severe mitral annular calcification (MAC) with preserved mitral valve function, mild aortic valve sclerosis with preserved opening, and mild left atrial dilation. Injection of saline contrast revealed no interatrial shunt.

Transesophageal echocardiogram redemonstrated left atrial dilation, MAC, and mild aortic valve sclerosis with preserved opening. It also revealed heavy Lambl's excrescences on the aortic valve. There was no thrombus detected in the left atrial appendage. Management

On initial presentation, the patient was not a candidate for thrombolytic therapies. Her work-up revealed caseous necrosis involving the mitral valve as well as Lambl's excrescences of the aortic valve; both are uncommon but possible etiologies of thromboembolism and recurrent stroke. As such, apixaban was added to her medication regimen, in lieu of aspirin and in addition to clopidogrel. She was medically optimized for blood pressure control and lipid-lowering therapy. An implantable loop recorder was placed to monitor for occult arrhythmias. She remained asymptomatic from her severe CAD and was recommended outpatient cardiology follow-up for ischemic evaluation. She was also recommended to follow up with cardiothoracic surgery as an outpatient for her valvular pathologies.

Outcome and Follow-Up

On outpatient follow-up 1 month after admission, she was recovering well with regard to her aphasia and weakness. She was able to ambulate with a cane, which she had been using previously. Of note, she was having exertional chest tightness and dyspnea but was able to conduct her daily activities. The implantable loop recorder has shown no evidence of atrial fibrillation or other arrhythmias for 7 months. She is scheduled for future outpatient cardiac catheterization, with possible stent placement.

Discussion

Lambl's excrescences are small, mobile, filamentous echogenic densities that arise on native aortic and mitral valves at the line of valve closure. These phenomena are thought to represent fibrin strands and result from wear and tear of the valve. It is a rare cause of cardioembolic stroke and has been reported a few times in the literature as case reports1 but should be considered and evaluated for in the differential diagnosis of cryptogenic stroke. Caseous necrosis of the mitral annulus is also an uncommon cause of cryptogenic stroke, but studies have demonstrated an association between mitral annular calcification and stroke.2 Multifocal lesions, as seen in our patient, suggest cardioembolic phenomenon. Though there is no consensus for treatment, surgery is likely preferred if the valve can be repaired. The option of medical therapy with anticoagulation remains if there are surgical contraindications.

Conclusions

The differential diagnosis for etiology of recurrent cryptogenic strokes is broad. Carotid disease can be a red herring depending on its laterality, and when indicated, other etiologies of cryptogenic stroke should be investigated. In the case of our patient, malignancy, hypercoagulability, arrhythmias, and ventricular thrombi were explored, given the embolic appearance of her recurrent strokes.

Determining the cause of cryptogenic stroke can be challenging and may require a multimodal imaging approach given the complementary roles of various modalities (Table 2). Lambl's excrescence and caseous mitral annulus necrosis are both rare but recognized sources of cardioembolism, and distinguishing between them requires careful clinical evaluation, imaging, and sometimes histopathologic evaluation. Our patient was treated with anticoagulation for dense Lambl's excrescences noted on the aortic valve. She also had caseous necrosis of the mitral valve with preserved mitral valve function. Although caseous mitral annulus could potentially contribute to cerebrovascular accident, it was deemed less likely given the appearance on transesophageal echocardiography; regardless, medical management for the interim remained the same with anticoagulation. She is pending evaluation by the cardiothoracic surgery team, as she may benefit from surgical intervention. The timing and type of surgical intervention must be strategically prioritized. Given mitral function is preserved, the mitral valve is unlikely to be intervened upon. However, if stroke work-up were completed and negative, she would likely benefit from aortic valve repair, as Lambl's excrescence does confer risk of recurrent stroke. Additionally, given her symptomatic severe CAD, ischemic evaluation should be completed, and planning should also be done to simultaneously prioritize coronary artery bypass graft in surgical management.
