Recent advances in Epstein–Barr virus vaccines development from mechanistic exploration to clinical translation
Authors: Liwei Hong, Juju Zhou, Junjuan Tan, Xiaoqian Zhu, Weihan Chen, Song Xie, Baotian Huang, Kai Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpstein–Barr virus (EBV) is a ubiquitous oncogenic virus linked to malignancies and autoimmune diseases. No licensed vaccines are currently available. Recent advances in EBV biology have enabled rational antigen selection and accelerated development of multivalent vaccines using platforms such as mRNA and nanoparticles. This review outlines key mechanisms, vaccine targets, and platform innovations, and discusses major challenges and future directions for EBV vaccine development.Subject terms: Cancer, Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), the first identified human oncogenic virus, is a ubiquitous γ-herpesvirus. Approximately 95% of adults worldwide are seropositive and carry the virus in a lifelong latent infection. EBV infection is closely associated with a broad spectrum of human diseases, ranging from acute infectious mononucleosis (IM) to various malignancies such as Burkitt’s lymphoma, Hodgkin’s lymphoma (HL), nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), certain gastric cancers, and post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorders (PTLD)1. In recent years, growing epidemiological and biological evidence has strongly linked EBV infection to the development of autoimmune diseases like multiple sclerosis (MS), further highlighting its significant global public health impact2,3. It is estimated that EBV-associated cancers account for approximately 1.5 million new cases annually worldwide, representing a substantial disease burden4. The virus infects B lymphocytes and epithelial cells through complex molecular mechanisms, establishes latent infection, and employs multiple strategies to evade host immune surveillance, ultimately inducing cellular malignant transformation under specific conditions5,6.

EBV is a double-stranded DNA virus with a genome of approximately 184 kb, encoding nearly 100 viral proteins. Structurally, the EBV virion consists, from the inside outward, of a DNA core, a nucleocapsid, a tegument layer, and an outer lipid envelope. The envelope surface is embedded with glycoprotein protrusions, which are crucial for viral infection of host cells. A schematic diagram of the EBV structure is shown in Fig. 1.

General structure of Epstein–Barr virus.

From the core outward: the viral DNA genome is enclosed within an icosahedral nucleocapsid. This is surrounded by the tegument, a protein-rich layer containing proteins such as BMRF2. The outer envelope is derived from the host cell membrane and is embedded with multiple viral glycoproteins essential for viral invasion, including the major attachment protein gp350/220, the fusion complex (gH/gL and gB in its prefusion state), and other glycoproteins such as gp42, gM/gN, gp150, and gp78/55.

Despite over six decades of widespread recognition of EBV’s pathogenicity, there are currently no licensed prophylactic or therapeutic EBV vaccines or specific antiviral drugs for EBV-associated malignancies in clinical practice7. This situation underscores the urgency and importance of developing effective EBV vaccines. Based on the nature of the targeted antigens, current vaccine strategies can be classified into two categories: one based on viral particle components aimed at inducing neutralizing antibodies to block viral entry, and the other based on viral latent or lytic antigens aimed at activating virus-specific T‑cell responses to eliminate infected cells. These two antigen classes are not strictly segregated in terms of application scenarios: particle‑based vaccines are primarily used prophylactically to prevent primary infection, whereas latent/lytic antigen‑based vaccines have been explored both for immunotherapy in patients with established EBV‑associated tumors and, in some instances, for prophylactic vaccination to establish early T‑cell immunity.

Early vaccine research focused on the viral envelope glycoprotein gp350 to induce neutralizing antibodies. While this approach could reduce infectious mononucleosis incidence, it failed to effectively prevent viral infection. In recent years, significant progress in EBV vaccine development has been achieved through deeper understanding of EBV cellular invasion mechanisms, latency and lytic cycle regulation, virus-host interactions, and immune evasion mechanisms. Researchers have precisely identified multiple key viral antigen targets, encompassing membrane glycoproteins that mediate viral entry, nuclear antigens and membrane proteins crucial for maintaining latency and inducing cellular transformation8. Concurrently, the application of cutting-edge technological platforms-including mRNA vaccines, nanoparticle/virus-like particle (VLP) vaccines, and recombinant viral vector vaccines-has provided unprecedented opportunities for the design and delivery of EBV vaccines9. These novel strategies have demonstrated encouraging protective effects in preclinical models, particularly in humanized mice and non-human primate models, laying a solid foundation for clinical translation10,11. This review systematically summarizes EBV’s biological characteristics and pathogenic mechanisms, thoroughly explores antigen selection strategies for key vaccine targets, comprehensively introduces technological advances across various vaccine development platforms, and analyzes current challenges in EBV vaccine research by integrating preclinical evaluation models and the latest developments in related immunotherapies. It also outlines future directions, aiming to provide theoretical guidance for accelerating the clinical translation of safe and effective EBV vaccines.

Biological characteristics and pathogenic mechanisms of EBV

As a γ-herpesvirus, EBV possesses unique biological characteristics that shape its complex interaction patterns with the host, including efficient infection, latency, immune evasion, and the eventual induction of various disease states. EBV primarily infects human B lymphocytes and epithelial cells, with its life cycle comprising two distinct states: lytic replication and latent infection. The virus enters cells by binding to host cell receptors via its surface glycoproteins, subsequently establishing lifelong latent infection within the host. During latency, the viral genome persists in the nucleus as an episome, expressing only a limited set of latency-associated proteins, thereby effectively evading host immune surveillance12. These latent proteins, such as EBV nuclear antigen 1 (EBNA1), latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1), and latent membrane protein 2 (LMP2), modulate host cell signaling pathways to promote cell proliferation and suppress apoptosis, and are key molecular underpinnings of EBV oncogenicity13. In addition, EBV has evolved multiple immune evasion mechanisms that facilitate persistent infection and limit immune clearance. It is precisely this delicate equilibrium of infection, latency, and immune evasion that enables EBV not only to coexist long-term with its host but also to disrupt this balance under specific conditions, leading to severe consequences such as malignant tumors and autoimmune diseases14.

Viral invasion mechanisms and cell tropism

EBV exhibits unique cellular tropism, primarily infecting human B lymphocytes and epithelial cells. This process is mediated by complex interactions between multiple viral envelope glycoproteins and specific receptors on host cell surfaces. The virus’s invasion mechanisms vary across cell types, which provides multiple critical targets for preventive vaccine design. The distinct mechanisms and latency stages of EBV infection in primary target cell types are illustrated in Fig. 2. In B cells, viral attachment is mainly mediated by the binding of gp350 to CR2/CD21, followed by the interaction of gp42 with HLA-II, which, together with gH/gL and the core fusion protein gB, triggers membrane fusion15,16. Recent studies have also identified R9AP as a key common co-receptor that mediates EBV entry into both B cells and epithelial cells by initiating membrane fusion17. For epithelial cells, EBV’s invasion mechanism is distinct and generally less efficient, but direct cell-to-cell contact likely represents the primary route of infection. Studies indicate that desmosome core protein 2 (DSC2) serves as the primary receptor for EBV invasion of epithelial cells, requiring DSC3 as a co-factor. DSC2 directly interacts with the viral gH/gL complex to promote membrane fusion18. In addition, molecules such as integrins and EphA2 may also bind to gH/gL and cooperate with R9AP to mediate viral entry19,20. These key glycoproteins involved in viral attachment, receptor recognition, and membrane fusion (gp350, gH/gL, gB, gp42) constitute the primary targets for inducing neutralizing antibodies and form the core antigenic repertoire for prophylactic vaccine design.

Immune evasion strategies during latency and lytic cycles

The key to EBV’s successful establishment of lifelong latent infection lies in its sophisticated immune evasion mechanisms, which enable its prolonged persistence in immunocompetent hosts. The virus evades host immune surveillance by restricting antigen expression during latency and actively suppressing immune signaling pathways during lytic cycles. Following EBV infection of B cells, distinct latency programs (Latency Types I, II, III) are established, characterized by the expression of a limited set of latent proteins21, as shown in Fig. 2. During the lytic cycle, although the virus expresses a large number of proteins and thereby increases the likelihood of immune recognition, it can still counter immune clearance through multiple mechanisms22,23. EBV can also modulate the cytokine milieu, such as through IL-10 secretion, and remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby promoting the accumulation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and M2 macrophages and creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment24,25. These immune evasion mechanisms not only help maintain viral latency but also create conditions favorable for tumor development. Therefore, targeting latent antigens and inducing antigen-specific T-cell responses constitute the core strategy of therapeutic vaccine design.

EBV-associated malignancy and key molecular mechanisms

As the first human tumor virus discovered, EBV has been widely confirmed to be oncogenic and is closely associated with multiple malignancies of lymphoid and epithelial origin26,27. A common feature of these tumors is the persistent presence of the EBV genome in tumor cells, along with the expression of specific viral latent proteins. These proteins drive malignant transformation through multiple pathways, including interference with the cell cycle, suppression of apoptosis, promotion of proliferation and angiogenesis, and remodeling of the TME28. Simultaneously, complex interactions between viral proteins and host cell signaling pathways, coupled with virus-induced chronic inflammation and immunosuppressive microenvironments, collectively constitute the core mechanisms driving EBV-associated tumorigenesis29,30. Deepening our understanding of these oncogenic molecular mechanisms not only reveals how EBV drives cancer but also provides a robust theoretical foundation for developing therapeutic vaccines and immunotherapies targeting these key viral proteins.

EBV-associated autoimmune diseases

In recent years, the association between EBV and autoimmune diseases, particularly MS, has become a research focus with breakthrough progress31. A large body of epidemiological evidence indicates that EBV infection is the strongest risk factor for the development of MS. Nearly all patients with MS are EBV seropositive, and EBV infection increases the risk of MS by 32-fold, suggesting that EBV infection is a necessary but not sufficient condition for the development of MS32. Current studies suggest that EBV may trigger MS through mechanisms such as molecular mimicry, aberrant B-cell activation, maintenance of chronic inflammation, and immune dysregulation33,34. Beyond MS, EBV has also been implicated in the development and progression of multiple autoimmune diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and Sjögren’s syndrome, with underlying mechanisms that may likewise involve molecular mimicry and immune dysregulation35. These new findings have not only profoundly changed our understanding of the etiology of diseases such as MS but have also opened entirely new avenues for the prevention and treatment of these autoimmune diseases through the development of anti-EBV vaccines or targeted therapies.

Key target antigen selection for EBV vaccines

The successful development of EBV vaccines relies on the precise selection of key target antigens capable of effectively eliciting persistent and potent humoral and cellular immune responses in the host. Based on the nature of the targeted antigens, EBV vaccine antigens can be broadly classified into two categories: one consists of invasion‑associated glycoproteins located on the viral particle surface that mediate viral attachment, receptor recognition, and membrane fusion; the other comprises latent and lytic antigens expressed within infected cells, which are closely associated with latency maintenance, viral reactivation, and tumor‑related immune recognition36. The core strategy of the former is to induce potent neutralizing antibodies to block viral entry into host cells, and thus these antigens are predominantly employed in prophylactic vaccine design37. The latter primarily activate cellular immunity, particularly EBV‑specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and helper T cells, to recognize and eliminate infected or malignant cells, thereby occupying a central role in therapeutic vaccine development. However, this correspondence is not absolute. Certain latent or lytic antigens can also be incorporated into prophylactic strategies to enhance immune control over post‑infection viral amplification, latency establishment, and reactivation processes. Conversely, invasion‑associated glycoproteins may be combined with other antigens to broaden the overall protective scope. Therefore, understanding the design logic of EBV vaccines from the perspective of antigen categories and their immunological functions facilitates a more comprehensive grasp of the intrinsic connections and developmental trends among different vaccine strategies. Viral particle-associated glycoprotein antigens (gp350, gH/gL, gB, gp42)

Viral particle-associated glycoprotein antigens represent the most important class of antigens in EBV vaccine design. Their common characteristic is localization on the viral envelope surface, where they directly participate in viral attachment, receptor recognition, and membrane fusion, thereby serving as core targets for inducing neutralizing antibodies. These antigens are typically prioritized for prophylactic vaccine design, with the primary goal of blocking viral entry into its main target cells—B cells and epithelial cells38. EBV invasion is mediated by a complex multi-protein machinery in which various membrane glycoproteins play crucial roles, making them key targets for prophylactic vaccine development. While traditional gp350 vaccines can induce neutralizing antibodies and partially prevent infectious mononucleosis, they fail to effectively block EBV infection. This has prompted researchers to focus on the core fusion machinery of viral invasion. This machinery primarily consists of gB, the gH/gL complex, and gp42, which plays a decisive role in B-cell infection. gB, a Class III fusion protein common to all herpesviruses, serves as the final executor mediating fusion between the viral envelope and host cell membranes, and is itself a major target for neutralizing antibodies. The gH/gL heterodimer plays a key role in triggering membrane fusion, responsible for interacting with epithelial cell receptors such as EphA2, integrins, DSC2, as well as the newly identified universal receptor R9AP39. gp42 specifically binds to HLA class II molecules on the surface of B cells, triggering B-cell infection process. Since these proteins are indispensable for viral invasion, antibodies targeting them effectively neutralize the virus. Consequently, gB, gH/gL, and gp42 are considered core antigens for developing next-generation EBV prophylactic vaccines40.

gp350 is the most abundant glycoprotein on the EBV envelope and was the earliest vaccine candidate antigen studied. It mediates initial viral attachment by binding to the CD21 receptor on B-cell surfaces41. Subunit vaccines based on gp350 induced neutralizing antibodies in early clinical trials and demonstrated some preventive efficacy against infectious mononucleosis. However, they failed to completely prevent EBV infection, suggesting that protection targeting a single antigen is limited. Blocking the gp350-mediated pathway alone is insufficient to provide complete protection42,43.

gH/gL complex is a conserved, essential component of membrane fusion in all herpesviruses, playing a central role in EBV infection of both B cells and epithelial cells as the key trigger for mediating membrane fusion. Studies indicate that monoclonal antibodies targeting gH/gL effectively neutralize infections across multiple cell types, making it an attractive target for a broad-spectrum neutralizing antibody vaccine44. Animal model studies confirm that gH/gL-based vaccines, particularly when presented as nanoparticles, induce potent and persistent neutralizing antibody responses45.

gB, a conserved Class III fusion protein within the herpesvirus family, directly mediates membrane fusion and represents another conserved and important vaccine target. Its indispensable role in viral invasion makes it a crucial choice for inducing functional antibodies. Recently developed chimeric virus-like particle (CVLP) vaccines successfully presented an immunogenic epitope on gB, inducing potent neutralizing antibodies and providing complete protection in mouse and humanized mouse models, demonstrating the feasibility of vaccine design based on key gB epitopes46. Meanwhile, through structural and functional analyses, recent studies have identified a broadly protective antibody, Fab5, which recognizes a conserved region within γ-herpesvirus gB and confers cross-genus protection. This finding highlights gB as a structurally conserved vulnerability site and underscores its potential as a target for broad-spectrum vaccine design47.

gp42 is a unique glycoprotein that specifically mediates EBV entry into B cells by binding to HLA class II molecules on the surface of B cells16. Consequently, antibodies targeting gp42 effectively block B-cell infection. Recent studies revealed that potent humanized monoclonal antibodies targeting specific gp42 epitopes (e.g., 2B7, 2C1) not only efficiently neutralize viral infection in vitro but also provide robust protection in humanized mouse models, revealing critical neutralizing sites on gp42 that are vulnerable to attack48. A large-scale population cohort study further demonstrated that high levels of anti-gp42 IgG antibodies are significantly associated with a reduced risk of NPC incidence, providing robust epidemiological evidence supporting gp42 as a preventive vaccine target49,50.

Latent and lytic antigens (EBNA1, LMP1, LMP2)

In contrast to viral invasion-associated glycoproteins, latent and lytic antigens are primarily expressed within infected cells and hold significant biological relevance during persistent EBV infection, viral reactivation, and EBV-associated tumors, thereby serving as key targets for inducing T‑cell immune responses. Vaccine designs based on these antigens typically focus on activating and enhancing the host cellular immune response, particularly virus-specific CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and CD4+ helper T cells, to recognize and eliminate cells harboring latent infection. Consequently, they have long been considered an important direction for therapeutic vaccine development. Additionally, when the virus transitions from latency to the lytic replication cycle, it expresses a series of lytic proteins that are also recognizable by T cells. Strategies targeting lytic antigens aim to control periodic viral reactivation, reduce viral dissemination, and potentially indirectly control tumors by eliminating a small number of tumor cells entering the lytic cycle. Vaccine-mediated presentation of these latent or lytic antigens can disrupt the immunosuppressive TME, reactivating or expanding depleted EBV-specific T cells within patients to achieve immune clearance of tumors. Thus, precise selection and effective presentation of these T-cell epitope-rich latent and lytic proteins are critical for developing effective EBV therapeutic vaccines. Nevertheless, for a virus such as EBV, which establishes lifelong latency, the goals of prophylaxis are not necessarily limited to blocking initial viral entry. They can also include enhancing host immune control over post-infection viral amplification, latency establishment, and subsequent reactivation processes. From this perspective, latent and lytic antigens also hold potential for prophylactic applications, particularly when incorporated into multi-antigen, multi-platform integrated vaccine designs to address the limitations of strategies relying solely on neutralizing antibodies.

EBNA1 is the only viral latent protein universally expressed across all types of EBV-associated malignancies. It is responsible for viral genome replication and maintenance, serving as a core protein for sustaining viral latency. Although EBNA1 has evolved mechanisms to evade proteasome degradation and MHC class I presentation through its GAr domain, studies indicate that optimizing antigen design (e.g., GAr domain deletion) or utilizing alternative antigen presentation pathways (e.g., cross-presentation) can still effectively induce EBNA1-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses51. EBNA1-based TCR-T cell therapies have demonstrated potential for targeting EBV-positive tumors52.

LMP1 and LMP2 are key membrane proteins expressed in Latency Type II and III malignancies (e.g., NPC, HL, PTLD). They serve not only as oncoproteins driving cellular malignant transformation but also contain multiple immunogenic T-cell epitopes. LMP1 drives cell proliferation and survival by continuously activating multiple signaling pathways through CD40 receptor mimicry53. LMP2 (including LMP2A and LMP2B) functions similarly to a persistently activated B-cell receptor, providing survival signals to latently infected B cells54. Consequently, LMP1 and LMP2 represent critical therapeutic targets for these specific tumor types. LMP1/LMP2-based dendritic cell (DC) vaccines, peptide vaccines, mRNA vaccines, and viral vector vaccines have demonstrated the ability to induce specific CTL responses and antitumor activity in preclinical and early clinical studies. For example, an mRNA vaccine targeting LMP2 demonstrated potent anti-NPC activity in mouse models55,56, while TCR-like T-cell therapies targeting specific LMP2 peptides also exhibited highly efficient tumor-killing activity57.

BZLF1 is a lytic phase antigen. Although latent phase antigens are primary targets for therapeutic vaccines, targeting EBV lytic phase antigens also exhibits unique therapeutic potential. When latent EBV is activated to enter the lytic cycle, it sequentially expresses a series of viral proteins. Among these, the immediate-early protein BZLF1 (also known as Zta) serves as the key transcriptional activator initiating the entire lytic replication cascade. Although most EBV-associated tumor cells remain in a latent state, a small fraction of cells within tumors spontaneously enter the lytic cycle, expressing lytic antigens such as BZLF1. These cells not only serve as a source of viral reservoirs but may also become targets for immune system attack. Studies have demonstrated that incorporating lytic antigens (e.g., BZLF1) into multivalent vaccines can induce stronger T-cell responses and superior protective efficacy51.

Advantages of multivalent and chimeric antigen design strategies

Given the complexity of EBV’s invasion mechanism and the diversity of immune evasion strategies, the protective efficacy of monoantigen vaccines is often limited. Consequently, multivalent vaccines and chimeric antigen design have emerged as mainstream strategies in current EBV vaccine development. Their core rationale lies in simultaneously targeting multiple critical stages of the viral life cycle to elicit broader and more synergistic immune responses. Multivalent prophylactic vaccines aim to achieve comprehensive neutralizing protection by combining multiple membrane glycoproteins (e.g., gp350, gH/gL, gB, gp42) to simultaneously block diverse viral binding and fusion pathways with B cells and epithelial cells. For example, covalently displayed chimeric nanoparticles (Chimeric-NPs) presenting gB and gH/gL induced significantly higher levels of neutralizing antibodies in mice and non-human primates compared to mixed monovalent antigens58. Another study developed a pentavalent modified Ankara vaccinia virus vector vaccine (MVA-EBV5-2) containing gp350, gB, and a gH/gL/gp42 complex. This vaccine induced superior neutralizing antibody potency in rhesus macaques compared to a monovalent gp350 vaccine and effectively reduced EBV infection rates in humanized mouse models59. Furthermore, a cocktail combination of nanoparticle vaccines encapsulating gH/gL, gB, and gp42, respectively, demonstrated superior protective efficacy compared to any single nanoparticle vaccine in humanized mouse models, providing durable prevention against EBV-associated lymphoma.

For therapeutic vaccines, multivalent strategies aim to simultaneously target multiple latent-phase and/or lytic-phase antigens. This increases the number of T-cell-recognizable epitopes, prevents immune escape due to downregulation or mutation of individual antigens, and elicits broad-spectrum cytotoxic activity against tumors of different latent types. A vaccine based on the TianTan poxvirus vector, simultaneously expressing latent-phase antigens EBNA1 and EBNA3B alongside lytic-phase antigen BZLF1, demonstrated potent T-cell activation and complete protection against EBV-induced B-cell lymphoma in humanized mouse models51. A recombinant BCG vaccine fusing BZLF1 with LMP2 also demonstrated good immunogenicity and antitumor activity60. These findings indicate that lytic-phase antigens like BZLF1 are important additions to therapeutic vaccines, and strategies targeting both latent-phase and lytic-phase antigens hold promise for achieving more comprehensive and durable immune control over EBV-associated tumors. Similarly, multi-epitope fusion protein vaccines designed using immunoinformatics to incorporate multiple CTL and helper T lymphocyte (HTL) epitopes have been demonstrated to possess high theoretical immunogenicity and broad population coverage61,62. Collectively, these studies indicate that rationally integrating multiple key antigens represents a critical direction for developing next-generation, highly effective EBV vaccines. The advantages of these multivalent strategies include: first, the ability to simultaneously block distinct viral entry pathways into B cells and epithelial cells; second, reduced risk of immune escape through single mutations by targeting multiple non-overlapping vulnerable sites; and finally, potential synergistic enhancement of overall neutralizing antibody potency and breadth.

EBV vaccine development strategies and cutting-edge technology platforms

In recent years, the landscape of EBV vaccine development exhibits a continuous evolutionary trajectory from classical platforms toward emerging technologies. Relatively mature platforms, namely recombinant protein subunit and viral vector vaccines, remain fundamental pillars of current EBV vaccine research, underpinned by well-established safety profiles and extensive empirical accumulation. Building upon this foundation, researchers have developed advanced optimization strategies, such as nanoparticle-based delivery and multivalent antigen display, to address the suboptimal immunogenicity typically associated with soluble subunit vaccines63. By mimicking native viral architecture and presenting antigens in a highly repetitive, multivalent array, these approaches profoundly enhance B-cell activation, thereby augmenting the magnitude and durability of antibody responses. Recently, the mRNA vaccine platform has garnered unprecedented attention following its remarkable success during the COVID-19 pandemic; its intrinsic advantages of rapid, flexible design and scalable manufacturing render it an ideal candidate for EBV vaccine development64. Furthermore, cellular vaccine platforms, such as dendritic cell (DC) vaccines, have additionally enriched the technological repertoire for EBV vaccine formulation. Subunit and nanoparticle vaccine platforms

Subunit vaccines consist of purified viral proteins or peptides, and early EBV vaccine research primarily focused on the gp350 subunit vaccine, which demonstrated safety and induced neutralizing antibodies, yet failed to achieve optimal protective efficacy. However, through optimized antigen design (e.g., engineered gB protein) and novel adjuvants (e.g., Amphiphile-CpG), subunit vaccine immunogenicity has been significantly enhanced65,66.

To overcome insufficient immunogenicity, nanotechnology has been extensively applied in constructing vaccine delivery systems. Nanoparticle vaccine platforms mimic the natural structure of viruses by presenting antigens in an ordered, repetitive manner on nanoparticle surfaces. This approach more effectively activates BCR signaling, promotes germinal center formation, enhances antigen delivery to lymph nodes and DC presentation, and induces robust and persistent antibody responses67. Recent studies demonstrate significant potential for multiple nanoparticle platforms in EBV vaccine development, including self-assembled protein nanoparticles (e.g., ferritin), liposomes, and virus-like particles. For instance, displaying gp350 or its key epitope fusion protein (L350) on self-assembled ferritin nanoparticles significantly enhanced neutralizing antibody titers68. Liposome-based nanoparticle vaccines, mimicking viral structure by covalently attaching gp350D protein to the liposome surface, similarly induced higher levels of specific antibodies and T-cell responses in mice. More sophisticated designs include chimeric nanoparticles co-presenting multiple antigens, such as those simultaneously delivering gB and gH/gL, which induced neutralizing antibody levels far exceeding those of mixed monovalent antigens69. Furthermore, encapsulating antigens alongside molecular adjuvants like CpG and MPLA within nanoparticles created a “cocktail-style” nanovaccine. This formulation efficiently activated DCs, inducing broad-spectrum neutralizing antibodies against gH/gL, gB, and gp42, and provided exceptional protective efficacy in humanized mouse models70. Virus-like particles (VLPs) are non-infectious particles mimicking viral structure but lacking genetic material. Chimeric VLPs based on hepatitis B core antigen (HBcAg), by presenting key epitope peptides (αB) of gB, also successfully induced high-titer neutralizing antibodies and achieved complete protection against EBV infection46. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that nanoparticle platforms, by optimizing antigen presentation, represent a key technology for enhancing the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of EBV vaccines.

Viral vector vaccine platform

Building upon the antigen delivery and structural optimization represented by subunit and nanoparticle strategies, viral vector vaccines offer another important technological approach. Viral vector vaccines use engineered, replication-deficient or attenuated viruses as carriers to deliver EBV antigen genes into host cells for in vivo expression and immune induction. Modified vaccinia virus Ankara (MVA) is a commonly used vector in EBV vaccine research. Researchers constructed a recombinant MVA vaccine expressing LMP2A (MVA-LMP2A), which effectively induced LMP2A-specific CTLs in mouse models and demonstrated significant antitumor activity, providing a candidate therapeutic approach for NPC55.

To develop a multifunctional vaccine capable of simultaneously stimulating humoral and cellular immunity, researchers further developed a multivalent MVA vector vaccine. For example, the MVA multivalent vaccine (MVA-EBV5-2), containing five EBV invasion-associated glycoproteins (gp350, gB, gH, gL, gp42), induced superior neutralizing antibody responses in rhesus macaques compared to the monovalent gp350 vaccine and effectively protected humanized mice against EBV infection59. Another significant study utilized the vaccinia virus as a vector to construct a composite vaccine simultaneously expressing latent-phase antigens (EBNA1, EBNA3B), lytic-phase antigens (BZLF1), and membrane glycoproteins (gH/gL). This multivalent vaccine strategy elicited potent T-cell responses targeting multiple antigens in humanized mice and provided up to 100% protection against EBV-induced lethal B-cell lymphoma, demonstrating that cellular immunity targeting multiple stages of the viral life cycle is crucial for preventing EBV-associated diseases51. These studies indicate that viral vector platforms, particularly MVA, are powerful tools for developing multivalent EBV vaccines, particularly those requiring potent T-cell immunity, such as therapeutic vaccines.

mRNA vaccine platform

While classical platforms continue to be optimized, mRNA vaccines, as one of the most rapidly advancing nucleic acid vaccine technologies in recent years, offer a more efficient technological option for EBV vaccine development. This platform was validated during the COVID-19 pandemic due to its flexible design, short development timeline, and capacity to induce robust humoral and cellular immune responses, and has quickly become a focal point in vaccine development for complex pathogens such as EBV. mRNA vaccines deliver mRNA encoding target antigens into cells via delivery systems like lipid nanoparticles. They leverage host cell translation mechanisms to synthesize antigen proteins, thereby mimicking viral infection processes and effectively activating MHC class I and II antigen presentation pathways.

In the EBV vaccine field, the mRNA platform demonstrates unique advantages. Its core strength lies in its “plug-and-play” design, enabling rapid design and production of mRNA molecules encoding any target antigen, significantly shortening the vaccine development cycle. For EBV, mRNA vaccines can be used for both prevention and treatment. For prophylactic vaccines, researchers have developed replicon RNA vaccines encoding key EBV membrane glycoproteins (e.g., gH/gL). Delivered via lipid nanoparticles, these vaccines successfully induced high-titer, persistent neutralizing antibodies in animal models and effectively prevented lethal EBV infection in humanized mice44. For therapeutic vaccines, mRNA vaccines are particularly well-suited for targeting EBV-associated tumors due to their ability to effectively induce cellular immune responses. Multiple studies have designed mRNA vaccines encoding EBV latent-phase antigens (e.g., LMP2, EBNA1). For example, one study developed a liposome-based LMP2-mRNA vaccine that effectively delivered to draining lymph nodes in an NPC mouse model, promoted DC maturation and antigen presentation, activated LMP2-specific T-cell responses, and significantly inhibited tumor growth71. Another study found that mRNA vaccines encoding truncated latent proteins rich in T-cell epitope regions (e.g., Trunc-LMP2A, Trunc-EBNA1) more effectively activated antigen-specific immune responses and demonstrated stronger antitumor effects compared to full-length protein vaccines72. Moreover, Moderna has advanced two mRNA vaccines into clinical trials: mRNA-1189 (NCT05164094), a prophylactic vaccine encoding multiple EBV glycoproteins, and mRNA-1195 (NCT05831111 and NCT06735248), a therapeutic vaccine encoding latent antigens. This marks a substantial advancement in the application of mRNA technology for EBV vaccines.

Cell vaccine platforms and other emerging platforms

Cell-based vaccines, particularly DC vaccines, constitute a personalized immunotherapy approach. This strategy involves loading a patient’s own DCs with EBV antigens—such as LMP1/LMP2 peptides or EBV lysates—ex vivo and then reinfusing them to harness the potent antigen-presenting capacity of DCs for activating EBV-specific T cells. In a Phase I clinical trial for recurrent or metastatic NPC, a vaccine using CD137L agonist-induced DCs loaded with EBV antigens (CD137L-DC-EBV-VAX) exhibited good tolerability and was associated with prolonged patient survival73. Beyond vaccines, broader cellular immunotherapies, such as the adoptive transfer of EBV-specific CTLs, have achieved notable success in treating EBV-associated diseases like PTLD74,75.

Moreover, other emerging platforms and strategies continue to emerge. The application of computational biology tools such as immunoinformatics and deep learning has greatly accelerated the screening and optimization of vaccine antigenic epitopes61,76. For instance, the deep learning model MUNIS accurately predicts CD8+ T-cell epitopes presented by HLA-I molecules with greater precision than traditional methods, successfully identifying and validating novel EBV immunogenic epitopes77. These diverse technical platforms and strategies complement each other, providing a rich toolkit for tackling the complex pathogen EBV. This enables the development of next-generation EBV vaccines that combine safety, broad-spectrum efficacy, and high efficiency.

Comparative evaluation of EBV vaccine platforms

The development of EBV vaccines has progressed from protein subunit vaccines to multiple emerging platforms, including nanoparticle-based vaccines, viral vector vaccines, mRNA vaccines, and DC vaccines. Although no EBV vaccine has yet been licensed, the major platforms have shown distinct characteristics in terms of immune induction, clinical applicability, and translational potential. Overall, each platform offers specific advantages while also facing inherent technical challenges.

Protein subunit vaccines were the earliest EBV vaccine platform to enter clinical investigation. They are characterized by favorable safety but relatively weak immunogenicity, and therefore often require potent adjuvants. In addition, single-antigen strategies provide limited coverage against the multiple entry pathways used by EBV to infect both B cells and epithelial cells. Nanoparticle vaccines represent an important optimization of subunit-based strategies. Through multivalent and repetitive antigen display, they can markedly enhance B-cell activation and germinal center responses, thereby promoting durable immune memory78,79. However, their development is complicated by challenges in antigen design, purification, and large-scale production, and most candidates remain in the preclinical stage. Viral vector vaccines enable efficient intracellular antigen expression and induce strong cellular immunity, making them particularly attractive for therapeutic vaccine development. Nevertheless, pre-existing anti-vector immunity and vector-directed immune responses may reduce efficacy and limit repeated booster administration. Furthermore, from a safety perspective, although attenuated vectors such as MVA have demonstrated favorable safety profiles, certain viral vectors may carry a potential risk of integration into the host genome. mRNA vaccines have emerged as one of the most innovative platforms in EBV vaccine development. Their advantages include flexible design, rapid multivalent antigen construction, the ability to induce both humoral and cellular immunity, and scalability in manufacturing. However, their application is still limited by molecular instability, cold-chain dependence, and delivery-system requirements. DC vaccines exploit the potent antigen-presenting capacity of dendritic cells to activate EBV-specific T-cell responses and are therefore mainly explored in the immunotherapy of EBV-associated malignancies. However, their clinical use is restricted by complex preparation, high cost, and limited scalability.

Overall, these platforms are not mutually exclusive, but rather complementary. The core features, advantages and limitations of the principal EBV vaccine platforms are summarized in Table 1. Future progress in EBV vaccine development will likely rely on rational platform integration, such as combining mRNA technology with nanoparticle delivery systems, multivalent antigen design with novel adjuvants, and vaccine strategies with immune checkpoint blockade, thereby further enhancing both protective efficacy and clinical translational value.

Preclinical evaluation models

Before entering clinical trials, EBV vaccine development must undergo rigorous preclinical evaluation to systematically assess safety, immunogenicity, and protective efficacy. This phase focuses on selecting and applying animal models that simulate human EBV infection and associated disease pathology, alongside establishing reliable immunological and virological assessment methods. However, EBV exhibits strict species specificity, infecting only humans, presenting significant challenges for preclinical research. No conventional laboratory animal can fully replicate the human infection process and disease spectrum. Consequently, researchers have developed multiple alternative models, including infecting natural hosts of closely related γ-herpesviruses or constructing immunodeficient mouse models capable of incorporating components of the human immune system. In these models, the potential of candidate vaccines is comprehensively evaluated by measuring vaccine-induced antibody titers, neutralizing activity, T-cell response intensity and function, as well as the suppression of infection, viremia, and tumorigenesis following viral challenge. Humanized mouse models are critical tools for studying EBV infection and immune responses. By transplanting human hematopoietic stem cells (CD34+ HSCs) or peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) into severely immunodeficient mice (e.g., NSG mice), a human immune system is reconstituted, enabling EBV infection, latency, and transformation. These models successfully mimic infectious mononucleosis-like symptoms following primary EBV infection, including high viral load, CD8+ T-cell lymphocytosis, and the development of EBV-induced B-cell lymphoproliferative disorders that resemble Latency Type III lymphoma80. Humanized mice have been extensively used to evaluate the protective efficacy of antibodies induced by preventive vaccines in neutralizing virus, blocking infection, and preventing lymphoma development. They are also employed to assess the ability of therapeutic vaccine-induced T cells to control tumor growth.

Non-human primate models, such as rhesus macaques and common marmosets, serve as the “gold standard” for evaluating vaccine safety and immunogenicity due to their high similarity to humans in immune systems and genetic backgrounds. Rhesus macaques can be infected with their endogenous rhesus lymphocryptovirus (rhLCV), which exhibits high homology with EBV in genomic structure and pathogenicity. Consequently, the rhLCV/rhesus macaque infection model faithfully replicates EBV infection and pathogenesis in humans, serving as a robust surrogate model for studying EBV pathogenesis and vaccine strategies. Common marmosets, however, can be directly infected by EBV and develop lymphoma, providing a platform for direct evaluation of EBV vaccines81. Multiple EBV vaccine studies, such as the MVA-based multivalent vaccine and the gH/gL nanoparticle vaccine, have undergone immunogenicity assessment in the rhesus monkey model and demonstrated robust antibody and T-cell responses.

Alternative viral models, primarily murine gamma herpesvirus 68 (MHV68), serve as natural pathogens capable of establishing a complete infection cycle (acute infection, latency, reactivation) in common mice. Despite differences between MHV68 and EBV, their core biological processes are similar, making MHV68 a convenient and cost-effective tool for studying universal immune mechanisms and vaccine strategies against this class of gamma herpesviruses.

Challenges and future directions in EBV vaccine development

Current challenges in EBV vaccine development

Antigenic variability represents one of the primary challenges in EBV vaccine development. Large-scale genomic sequencing analyses reveal significant geographic variation in the EBV genome. Certain viral variants previously associated with specific cancer types are now understood to be more closely linked to the geographic origin of the virus. Such variation may alter peptide sequences of key vaccine target antigens, leading to changes in T-cell epitopes or neutralizing antibody binding sites. This reduces vaccine universality and efficacy across diverse populations. For instance, studies have identified multiple non-synonymous mutations in the coding sequences of current vaccine targets (e.g., gB, gH/gL, BZLF1)82. Future efforts can integrate global EBV genomic surveillance, structural biology, and immunoinformatics to systematically screen highly conserved functional epitopes and T-cell epitopes across different geographic lineages, thereby establishing a more universally applicable framework for antigen selection. Concurrently, vaccine strategies based on multi-antigen and multi-epitope designs are expected to enhance coverage across different strains and populations while balancing immune breadth and functionality83.

Adjuvants selection and delivery systems optimization are also critical components. Selecting adjuvants that can safely and effectively induce the desired type of immune response (such as Th1-biased cellular immunity) and designing carriers that efficiently deliver antigens to target immune cells represent common challenges in all modern vaccine development.

Immune persistence is critical for all vaccine success. As a virus capable of lifelong latent infection, EBV can switch between lytic replication and latent infection states, effectively evading immune surveillance during latency through restricted expression of viral antigens. An ideal vaccine must not only induce neutralizing antibodies to block initial viral infection but also stimulate T-cell immunity to clear established latent infections. Furthermore, the vaccine-induced immune memory must be sufficiently long-lasting and robust to provide effective protection upon primary exposure or viral reactivation. Most current preclinical studies primarily focus on short-term immune responses and protective effects. The mechanisms underlying the formation of long-term immune memory, its duration, and how adjuvants and delivery systems can optimize immune persistence remain areas requiring in-depth investigation. Future research directions may include prime‑boost vaccination strategies, application of novel adjuvants that better promote germinal center reactions and long‑lived plasma cell generation, and development of delivery platforms that target lymph nodes and antigen‑presenting cells to enhance long‑lasting humoral immunity and memory T‑cell responses. Particularly for a virus such as EBV, which establishes long‑term latency and undergoes periodic reactivation, the ability to simultaneously establish durable neutralizing antibody responses and high‑quality memory T‑cell responses will be crucial for achieving long‑term protection.

Ideal animal models require further development. There remains a lack of ideal animal models capable of fully mimicking human EBV infection and associated diseases—particularly tumors and autoimmune disorders—significantly limiting the predictive value of preclinical research. While humanized mice and non-human primate models provide valuable research platforms, they are constrained by high costs, operational complexity, and an inability to replicate all features of the human immune system.

Assessing vaccine efficacy presents additional challenges. Since EBV-associated malignancies typically develop over years or decades, clinical trials using cancer incidence as an endpoint are large-scale, time-consuming, and costly. Consequently, identifying reliable immunological surrogate endpoints is critical. Currently, neutralizing antibody titers are considered a potential indicator of preventive vaccine efficacy, while the intensity and functionality of T-cell responses are key for therapeutic vaccine evaluation. However, it remains unclear what levels and types of immune responses are required to achieve effective protection in humans, introducing uncertainty in setting clinical trial endpoints and assessing vaccine efficacy.

Challenges and perspectives for prophylactic EBV vaccines

Evidence from existing clinical trials suggests that the concept of “failure” in prophylactic vaccines requires more precise definition. Previous clinical trials suggest that prophylactic EBV vaccines are not entirely ineffective, but rather have failed to achieve the ideal endpoint of preventing EBV infection. The most representative example is the gp350 subunit vaccine, whose phase II trial (NCT00430534) showed a significant reduction in the incidence of IM and the induction of relatively durable neutralizing antibody responses, but showed no prevention of asymptomatic EBV infection42. This finding indicates that early single-antigen strategies may attenuate the clinical manifestations of primary EBV infection, yet remain insufficient to achieve sterilizing immunity. Such a limitation—reducing IM incidence without preventing EBV infection—has become one of the central dilemmas in current prophylactic EBV vaccine development84. This likely reflects two major biological constraints. First, EBV exhibits broad cellular tropism, making it difficult for single-antigen vaccines to fully block all viral entry pathways into host cells37,79. Second, as a γ-herpesvirus capable of establishing lifelong latency, EBV persists in memory B cells and evades immune clearance through minimal antigen expression; thus, even robust vaccine-induced humoral and cellular responses are unlikely to eradicate latently infected cells completely, rendering sterilizing immunity particularly difficult to achieve85. For this reason, the goal of prophylactic vaccination against EBV may need to shift from simply preventing infection to preventing EBV-associated diseases.

Against this background, next-generation vaccine candidates appear more promising in theory, although their true clinical success remains uncertain. For example, the multivalent mRNA vaccine mRNA-1189 uses a lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery system to encode five EBV envelope glycoproteins (gp350, gH, gL, gp42, and gp220), thereby offering broader antigenic coverage than earlier single-antigen gp350 vaccines and, in principle, a greater capacity to block multiple viral entry routes. In addition, the success of mRNA platforms in COVID-19 vaccines provides practical support for their ability to induce potent neutralizing antibody responses. Nevertheless, the actual clinical efficacy of mRNA-1189 remains difficult to quantify at present.

Moreover, if EBV vaccines are to be implemented in the general population, vaccination strategy and potential long-term consequences will require careful consideration. Because most adults worldwide are lifelong asymptomatic carriers of EBV, a long-standing co-evolutionary balance may exist between humans and the virus. Whether blocking natural infection during infancy or adolescence might disrupt this equilibrium and lead to unforeseen immunological consequences remains unknown. Widespread vaccination could also alter the epidemiology of EBV infection, potentially delaying primary infection to older ages and thereby increasing the risk of IM. These issues remain largely theoretical, and there is currently no direct evidence that EBV vaccines would cause clear harm in healthy populations. On the contrary, given the substantial disease burden of EBV-associated malignancies and autoimmune diseases, the public health benefits of vaccination are likely to outweigh the current theoretical risks. Even so, these questions will require continued evaluation through long-term follow-up, real-world evidence, and post-marketing surveillance before and after widespread implementation.

Future directions

Given the complex immunosuppressive microenvironment of EBV-associated tumors, monotherapy often yields suboptimal results. Therefore, combining EBV vaccines with other immunotherapies to establish synergistic combination strategies represents a crucial future direction for enhancing therapeutic efficacy86.

Vaccines combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) represent a highly promising combination. EBV-associated tumors, such as NPC and certain gastric cancers, frequently overexpress PD-L1, a key mechanism for their immune evasion87. Therapeutic EBV vaccines activate and expand tumor antigen-specific T cells, while ICIs (e.g., anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies) release these T cells from suppression within the TME, restoring their cytotoxic function. This combination holds promise for generating synergistic antitumor effects with a “1 + 1 > 2” effect. Preclinical studies have begun exploring this combination strategy with promising outcomes. For instance, co-administration of the LMP2 mRNA vaccine with PD-1 inhibitors reverses T-cell exhaustion and generates synergistic antitumor effects88. Future clinical research will focus on optimizing the timing and dosing of combination therapies, as well as identifying patient populations most likely to benefit from this approach.

Vaccine combination with adoptive cell therapy (ACT) represents another promising pathway. Therapeutic vaccines can “seed” and amplify EBV-specific T cells in vivo, while ACT (e.g., EBV-CTLs, CAR-T, TCR-T) rapidly delivers large numbers of highly active effector cells89. Their combination may enhance ACT efficacy and persistence by creating a supportive cytokine environment for infused effector cells through vaccine-induced immune responses, thereby promoting their infiltration and survival at tumor sites. Furthermore, vaccines can serve as consolidation or maintenance therapy following ACT to prevent tumor recurrence90. Future synergistic strategies hold great promise: employing therapeutic vaccines as “primary immunization” to activate and expand tumor-specific T cells in vivo, followed by ACT as a “booster”; or combining vaccines with cell therapies to jointly overcome the tumor-suppressive microenvironment91.

Combining vaccines with other therapies, such as NK cell therapy or targeted drugs, also shows promise. One study demonstrated that an EBV multi-epitope mRNA vaccine, when used in combination with in vitro-expanded NK cells, synergistically enhanced killing effects against NPC by improving T-cell and NK-cell infiltration and function within the TME92,93. Furthermore, certain targeted therapies, such as MMP9 inhibitors, have been found to mitigate the suppression of TCR-T cell function by EBV-induced M2 macrophages, suggesting the feasibility of combining vaccines/cell therapies with targeted drugs to jointly remodel the tumor immune microenvironment25. The development of these combined strategies will propel the treatment of EBV-associated diseases into a new era of precision and individualization.

Conclusion and prospectives

After over six decades of exploration, EBV research has progressed from viral discovery and elucidation of pathogenic mechanisms to a new phase of precision interventions targeting prevention and treatment. As a pathogen closely associated with multiple major human diseases—including various malignancies and autoimmune disorders (particularly multiple sclerosis)—EBV’s substantial global health burden makes the development of effective EBV vaccines a public health priority. Today, we possess unprecedented insights into how EBV invades B cells and epithelial cells through its complex glycoprotein system, and how it employs latent and lytic proteins to evade immune surveillance and drive cellular carcinogenesis. These fundamental breakthroughs provide a robust theoretical foundation for rational vaccine design, enabling precise selection of key antigen targets for prevention and treatment.

Regarding prophylactic vaccines, research focus has expanded from the single gp350 antigen to include multivalent combination strategies encompassing gH/gL, gB, and gp42. Extensive preclinical evidence indicates that simultaneously targeting multiple stages of viral invasion could induce broader and more potent neutralizing antibodies, thereby achieving more comprehensive protection. Key clinical trials of prophylactic EBV vaccine are summarized in Table 242,43,94–96. For therapeutic vaccines, targeting latent-phase antigens—such as EBNA1, expressed in all EBV-associated tumors, and LMP1/LMP2, expressed in specific tumors—has become a core strategy for activating specific T-cell immunity. Past and ongoing clinical trials on therapeutic EBV vaccine are shown in Table 373,97–104. The selection of these targets is strongly supported by the clinical success of adoptive cell therapies (e.g., EBV-CTLs and TCR-T cells). These therapies not only validate the immunogenicity and therapeutic potential of these targets but also provide reference standards for evaluating vaccine immunogenicity.

Technological innovation has been the key driver propelling EBV vaccine development into the fast lane. Next-generation vaccine technologies, represented by mRNA, nanoparticles, and viral vectors, have significantly enhanced antigen immunogenicity, delivery efficiency, and the type and intensity of immune responses induced. mRNA technology, in particular, offers powerful tools for tackling the complex EBV virus due to its rapid design and production advantages, coupled with its ability to simultaneously stimulate both humoral and cellular immunity. These advanced platforms have demonstrated encouraging protective efficacy in preclinical models highly mimicking human infection, such as humanized mice and non-human primates. Multiple vaccine candidates have entered or are poised to enter clinical trials, signaling the dawn of clinical application for EBV vaccines.

Looking ahead, EBV vaccine development will advance toward greater precision, efficiency, and broad-spectrum coverage. First, “rational design” integrating structural biology and immunoinformatics will become mainstream. By resolving the structures of key antigen-neutralizing antibody complexes, researchers can precisely identify viral vulnerable sites and design optimized antigens that focus immune responses on these critical epitopes, thereby enhancing vaccine efficacy and specificity. Second, multivalent and multi-platform combination strategies will emerge as significant trends. For instance, cocktail combinations of mRNA or nanoparticle vaccines encoding multiple glycoproteins, or prime-boost vaccination approaches (e.g., viral vector priming followed by mRNA or subunit booster shots), hold promise for inducing broader, more durable humoral and cellular immune responses. Third, given the causal link between EBV and MS, developing prophylactic vaccines targeting late-adolescent primary infection could fundamentally reduce or even eliminate MS risk, representing one of EBV vaccines’ most transformative potential applications. Fourth, combining therapeutic vaccines with other immunotherapies like immune checkpoint inhibitors may disrupt the immunosuppressive microenvironment of EBV-associated tumors, offering a crucial complement to existing treatments. Finally, as understanding of EBV genomic diversity and its disease associations deepens, developing pan-vaccines capable of covering major global strains will be crucial for ensuring equitable and effective vaccine deployment worldwide. Although challenges remain, with ongoing advances in basic research and continuous innovation in vaccine technology, we have reason to believe that safe and effective EBV vaccines will eventually emerge. These vaccines promise revolutionary breakthroughs in preventing and treating EBV-related diseases, significantly reducing the global disease burden.
