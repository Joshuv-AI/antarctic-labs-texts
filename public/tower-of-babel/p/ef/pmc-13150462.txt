Right Ventricular Function and Mortality in Critically Ill Patients With Heart Failure and Cardiogenic Shock
Authors: Kirsten M. Lipps, Garima Dahiya, Maan Jokhadar, Courtney E. Bennett, Dustin B. Hillerson, Bradley W. Ternus, Ruben J. Crespo-Diaz, Jacob C. Jentzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesRight ventricular dysfunction (RVD) is associated with poor prognosis in patients with acute heart failure (HF), including cardiogenic shock (CS). We evaluated the association of RVD by transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) with mortality in cardiac intensive care unit (CICU) patients with HF.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort of patients with HF admitted to the Mayo Clinic CICU (2007–2018) who had a TTE within one day of admission. TTE-derived global assessment of right ventricular (RV) function was graded on a semi-quantitative scale. Outcomes included in-hospital and one-year mortality.ResultsAmong 2,450 patients (median 71.6 years, 38.4% female, 91.2% White), 75.7% had RVD (36.9% moderate/severe RVD). Global assessment of RVD correlated with advanced Doppler hemodynamics. Short-term mortality increased according to RVD severity (adjusted odds ratio, 1.30 per degree of RVD; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.15–1.47, p<0.001), with similar findings after stratification by left ventricular function and CS (25.2% of the cohort). One-year mortality increased with RVD severity (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.21 per degree of RVD; 95% CI, 1.14–1.28, p<0.001). Severe RVD was associated with 51.2% one-year mortality (median survival 9.9 months). Patients with moderate/severe RVD without CS had similar mortality to those with mild/no RVD with CS.ConclusionsRVD is common among CICU patients with acute HF, and TTE-derived global assessment of RV function correlates with advanced Doppler hemodynamics. RVD severity by global assessment is associated with higher short- and long-term mortality, highlighting the potential utility of point-of-care ultrasound for risk stratification in CICU patients.

INTRODUCTION

While research has traditionally focused on left ventricular (LV) function in patients with heart failure (HF) and cardiogenic shock (CS), right ventricular dysfunction (RVD) is an increasingly recognized source of morbidity and mortality.1) Severe RVD is associated with exceptionally poor mortality,2) especially in patients with CS, where in-hospital death occurs in up to 60%.34) In this population, isolated RVD is associated with worse prognosis compared to isolated LV dysfunction, and is comparable to that observed in biventricular dysfunction.5)

Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) is the standard method for bedside evaluation of RVD, with many different metrics used to quantify right ventricular (RV) function. Among these, tricuspid annulus plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), RV end-diastolic diameter, and estimated right atrial pressure (RAP) have been associated with mortality in patients with HF.46) Doppler-based measurements reflecting RV and pulmonary hemodynamics carry strong prognostic value in cardiac intensive care unit (CICU) populations,6789) but these may not be easily measured at the bedside in all patients. In contrast, point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) for evaluation of RVD in critically ill patients often relies on a visual assessment of RV function supplemented by limited measurements. Yet, little is known about whether global evaluation of RV function is useful for prognostication despite widespread use at the bedside. Consequently, we evaluated the association of RVD with mortality among a large cohort of CICU patients admitted with HF.

METHODS

Study population

We screened a previously constructed database of consecutive unique patients admitted to the Mayo Clinic CICU from January 2007 to April 2018. We included patients with an admission diagnosis of HF, based on International Classification of Disease Ninth Revision and Tenth Revision (ICD-9/ICD-10) diagnostic codes, who had a consultative TTE within one day of CICU admission. We excluded patients without available data for RV function on the TTE report. The study conformed with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (approval No. #25-003855). All patients provided written informed consent for their medical records to be used for research.

Data sources

Demographic, clinical, and outcome data were extracted from the electronic health record as previously described.10111213) Admission diagnoses were defined as ICD-9/ICD-10 codes documented within one day of CICU admission and were not mutually exclusive. Validated electronic algorithms were used to calculate acute illness severity scores.10111213)

Echocardiographic data were extracted from an institutional database, which contained finalized clinical echocardiographic reports, as interpreted by a single, expert, academic echocardiographer (Supplementary Table 1).689) All individuals at our institution who interpret TTE are required to be a diplomate of the National Board of Echocardiography (which involves successful completion of the Examination of Special Competence in Adult Echocardiography) and attain Level III training according to the American College of Cardiology Core Cardiovascular Training Statement.14) Each echocardiographic parameter in the database was recorded as a single, final “best” value, which may have represented an individual measurement or an average of multiple measurements; we could not determine which method was utilized.

The echocardiographers at our institution adhere to American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) guidelines when interpreting echocardiograms. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was used to grade severity of LV dysfunction.15) RV function was graded based on the totality of data by the expert interpreting echocardiographer; we were unable to ascertain on an individual patient level which echocardiographic parameters were used by the echocardiographer to grade RV function. The 2005 ASE Recommendations for Chamber Quantification acknowledge that although quantitative methods existed for assessment of RV function, at that time, real clinical world assessment remained mostly qualitative.16) The 2010 ASE Guidelines for the Echocardiographic Assessment of the Right Heart in Adults, which were the first dedicated to assessment of RV function, recommend incorporation of multiple variables, including at least one quantitative measure; these, however, do not prioritize specific parameters or provide a grading framework to characterize the severity of RV dysfunction.17) We subsequently classified the reported RV function on a semi-quantitative scale to simulate real-world assessment of RV function in critically ill patients and treat RV function as a continuous variable for analysis: 0, normal; 1, borderline; 2, mild/mild-moderate; 3, moderate/moderate-severe; 4, severe.

Additional Doppler measurements were determined from TTE reports, as previously described (Supplementary Table 2).89) Estimated right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP), based on the RAP (either directly measured or estimated based on inferior vena size and collapsibility) and tricuspid regurgitation peak velocity (TRV), was used as a surrogate for pulmonary artery (PA) systolic pressure. Measures of RV-PA coupling included the ratio of tricuspid annulus peak systolic velocity (S’) to either the RVSP or TRV, the ratio of TAPSE to RVSP, and the effective PA elastance (calculated as RVSP/stroke volume).6)

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included 30-day and one-year mortality. Mortality was determined by electronic chart review and extracted automatically from the electronic health record and Minnesota State registries.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported as number (%), and groups were compared using χ2 tests. Continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range), and groups were compared using Kruskal-Wallis tests. Correlations between continuous variables were evaluated using Spearman rho coefficients; RV function was treated as an integer for correlations. Predictors of in-hospital mortality were evaluated using logistic regression to calculate odds ratio and 95% confidence interval (CI) values. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to determine 30-day and one-year mortality, with groups compared using log-rank tests. Predictors of in-hospital mortality were evaluated using Cox proportional-hazards models to calculate hazard ratio and 95% CI values. Multivariable models were adjusted for the following variables selected a priori: age, sex, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation III score, Charlson Comorbidity Index, admission diagnoses (shock, cardiac arrest, and respiratory failure), Braden score,18) number of vasoactive drugs, in-hospital cardiac arrest, and use of invasive mechanical ventilation, temporary mechanical circulatory support, cardiac catheterization, and dialysis. Statistical analysis was performed using BlueSky 10.3.1 Pro (BlueSky LLC, Chicago, IL, USA).

RESULTS

Study population

Out of 12,428 unique CICU patients, 6,008 had an admission diagnosis of HF, and 4,272 had a TTE within one day of CICU admission. A total of 2,450 patients had available data for RV function and were included. The analyzed population was 71.6 (61.8, 80.4) years old, and 38.4% were females (Table 1). Acute myocardial infarction was present in 1,120 (45.7%) patients and CS in 618 (25.2%); other critical care diagnoses included respiratory failure (43.1%), cardiac arrest (16.8%), and sepsis (11.1%). Critical care therapies were used in 72.6%, including positive-pressure ventilation in 43.8% and vasoactive drugs in 37.3%. Patients with CS were older and sicker, with more critical care diagnoses and therapies received (Table 1). Median LVEF was 35% (25%, 52%) overall, and 75.3% of patients had abnormal LVEF based on ASE criteria; LVEF was lower in patients with CS (29% vs. 38%, p<0.001; Table 2). Patients with CS also had higher RAP, pressure-adjusted heart rate, RAP/mean arterial pressure, PA elastance, and RVSP/systolic blood pressure and had lower RV stroke work index (Table 2, all p<0.001).

RV function

RV function was classified as “Normal” in only 24.3% of patients, including 18.8% with CS and 26.1% without (Figure 1). At least moderate RVD was present in 36.9% and was more common in patients with CS (49.7% vs. 32.6%, p<0.001). Baseline clinical characteristics differed by RV function, and patients with ≥ Moderate RVD were younger and sicker (Table 3). Patients with more severe RVD had lower LVEF, higher RAP and RV systolic pressure, and worse values for PA elastance, RV-PA coupling (S’/RVSP, S’/TRV, TAPSE/RVSP), pressure-adjusted heart rate, and RV stroke work index (Table 4, all p<0.001). Doppler markers reflecting RV and pulmonary hemodynamics correlated with semi-quantitative RV function grade, with several correlation coefficients >|0.4| (Supplementary Table 3). The strongest inverse correlations were for longitudinal RV motion (TAPSE and S’) and RV-PA coupling (S’/RVSP and S’/TRV) while the strongest direct correlations were related to RAP.

In-hospital and 30-day mortality

A total of 322 (13.1%) patients died during hospitalization, including 196 (8.0%) CICU deaths. In-hospital mortality was higher for patients with CS (30.4% vs. 7.3%), who accounted for 58.4% of all hospital deaths. Mortality increased according to severity of RVD overall and when stratified by admission diagnosis of CS (Figure 2). Similar increases in mortality were seen with more severe RVD when stratified by LVEF (Figure 3). Mortality at 30 days was higher for patients with ≥ Mild RVD (Supplementary Figure 1A), especially with concomitant CS (Supplementary Figure 1B); patients with CS and ≥ Moderate RVD were at substantially higher risk (30-day mortality 40.7% vs. 25.6% for CS with ≤ Mild RVD). After multivariable adjustment, more severe RVD was incrementally associated with higher in-hospital mortality (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.297 per grade; 95% CI, 1.149–1.468; p<0.001; Supplementary Table 4).

One-year mortality

A total of 834 (34.0%) patients died within one year of CICU admission, and 251 (15.5%) had follow-up less than one year but were alive at last follow-up. One-year mortality was substantially higher for patients with CS (47.1% vs. 32.3%; Figure 4A, Supplementary Figure 3A). One-year mortality increased incrementally with RVD severity, particularly ≥ Moderate RVD (Figure 4B, Supplementary Figure 3B). Patients with severe RVD had a median survival of 9.9 months and one-year mortality of 51.2% (vs. 26.3% with “Normal” RV function). One-year mortality was highest for patients with CS and ≥ Moderate RVD (median survival 4.0 months, one-year mortality 56.1%); patients with either CS or ≥ Moderate RVD had similar mortality (Figure 4A). After multivariable adjustment, worse RVD was incrementally associated with higher one-year mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.21 per grade; 95% CI, 1.14–1.28; p<0.001; Supplementary Table 4). Each grade of RVD above borderline was associated with higher mortality (all p<0.05; Supplementary Table 5); risk was highest with ≥ Moderate RVD (aHR, 1.59; 95% CI, 1.38–1.84; p<0.001; Supplementary Table 6).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the largest study to evaluate the prognostic value of TTE-derived global RV function in a large cohort of CICU patients with HF. In this cohort, RVD was common, occurring in more than three-fourths of patients. More than one-third of patients overall and one-half of patients with CS had at least moderate RVD, and these patients were sicker than those with better RV function. Importantly, TTE-derived Doppler parameters of RV function and hemodynamics correlated well with global assessment of RV function reported by the expert interpreting echocardiographer, which mirrors the real-world evaluation of RV function in critically ill patients, in whom limited imaging windows may preclude incorporation of all possible data points.

In this cohort, greater RVD severity was associated with higher short- and long-term mortality. ≥ Moderate RVD was incrementally associated with higher mortality even when patients were stratified according to Doppler measures reflecting RV-PA coupling and hemodynamics; much of this risk seems to have been conferred by high RAP reflecting systemic congestion from RVD. Finally, ≥ Moderate RVD was associated with especially poor long-term prognosis even in the absence of LV dysfunction or CS, with more than one-third of patients dying by one-year, a survival rate which was equivalent to that of CS patients without RVD.

Among patients with decompensated HF, though isolated RVD is uncommon, biventricular dysfunction is frequently encountered.1920) RVD is likewise prevalent among patients with CS, occurring in more than one-quarter who have acute myocardial infarction-associated CS and greater than one-half of patients with HF associated CS.321) We observed a high prevalence of RVD and abnormal RV hemodynamics in our cohort, particularly among patients with CS. This is consistent with our prior analysis comparing patients with HF associated CS and acute myocardial infarction associated CS, which found that while RVD was highly prevalent in both groups, it was more common in HF associated CS, occurring in 84% vs. 72%, respectively.22) In our previous analysis, severe RVD was observed in ~10% of patients with CS and was also more frequent in those with HF associated CS.22)

Previous literature has also described that patients with RVD are more acutely ill compared to those with “Normal” RV function.3) Similarly, in our cohort, RVD was associated with higher severity of illness scores, more multi-organ system dysfunction, and greater need for critical care therapies. This is unsurprising given that systemic congestion and low cardiac output contribute to renal and hepatic failure.1) Interestingly, in our cohort, patients with RVD were younger than those without RVD. One potential explanation for this is that conditions associated with RVD such as pulmonary arterial hypertension, myocarditis, genetic cardiomyopathies, and congenital heart disease are more prevalent in younger populations, who also have lower prevalence of acute myocardial infarction, which typically causes LV dysfunction.232425) Altogether, given the association of RVD with greater illness severity, timely diagnosis and treatment are paramount for HF patients with RVD.

For patients with decompensated HF, including those with CS, RVD is associated with poor prognosis.2345926) In a previous study of 1,085 CICU patients with CS from this cohort, in-hospital mortality was highest among those with isolated RVD (~40%) or biventricular dysfunction (~37%), as compared to isolated LV dysfunction (~25%).9) Similarly, in a cohort of 292 patients with CS due to acute myocardial infarction, short-term mortality in those with RVD was 56%, compared to 34% without RVD.4) We observed a marked stepwise increase in mortality according to the severity of RVD, with especially high mortality for patients with ≥ Moderate RVD and concomitant LV dysfunction or CS. Importantly, even mild RVD was associated with higher short-term mortality compared to “Normal” RV function.

RVD was also associated with higher long-term mortality, with more than one-half of patients with severe RVD dying by one year and a median survival of only 10 months after admission. At one year, we observed similar mortality for patients with ≥ Moderate RVD without CS and those with ≤ Mild RVD with CS; this remarkably high mortality in non-CS patients with significant RVD was unexpected and underscores the hazard conferred by RVD. Multiple previous studies have described the association between RVD and long-term prognosis in patients with diverse etiologies of RVD with the worst outcomes occurring in patients with CS, where >50% mortality at one-year has been observed.24) Consequently, although acute RVD has immediate mortality implications, long-term prognosis remains grim even for patients that survive to hospital discharge, and among our patients, was comparable to that of CS. This highlights the importance of close follow-up for patients with acute HF and RVD admitted to the CICU, in dedicated HF or multi-disciplinary post-CICU recovery clinics.27)

Given the prognostic implications of quantifying RV function during acute illness, accurate assessment by echocardiography is paramount. We have previously demonstrated the prognostic value of Doppler-derived parameters of RV function and right heart hemodynamics among patients admitted to the CICU.6789) However, in this study, we observed that quantitative hemodynamic data correlated well with a semi-quantitative, pragmatic approach for global assessment of RV function, which can be gleaned through POCUS. However, both the global assessment of RV function and specific hemodynamic measurements were complementary for risk profiling. This has important implications, as compared to consultative TTE, POCUS is more readily available and requires less experience for satisfactory performance and interpretation. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated good correlation between POCUS and consultative TTE and between the interpretation skills of experienced and novice clinicians.2829) POCUS is a reliable, efficient, and cost-effective method for serial assessment and is especially useful for monitoring response to interventions; knowing that assessment of RVD by POCUS is useful for risk stratification will facilitate deployment in the CICU.30) In summary, our study expands upon existing literature, which highlights the prognostic value of Doppler-derived parameters of RV function in patients with acute HF and CS, by demonstrating the utility of a simple and easily applied semi-quantitative assessment of RVD at the bedside, mirroring real-world clinical practice, in which limited imaging windows may preclude the ability to obtain more advanced hemodynamic parameters of RV function. However, further studies would be helpful in validating our findings, specifically those which evaluate POCUS performed by the treating clinician versus consultative TTE for global assessment of RV function in critically ill patients.

The retrospective nature of this study limits evaluation of causal relationships, and confounding variables may have influenced outcomes despite careful analysis. In addition, the cohort was derived from a single, quaternary, academic medical center, and the population was predominantly White males, which may affect external validity. Furthermore, only patients who had a consultative TTE within one day of CICU admission and who had RV function reported were included, which may have introduced selection bias, whereby patients who were less sick at presentation, admitted during non-business hours, or who had non-diagnostic TTE images may have been excluded. We acknowledge that patients with severe critical illness may be more difficult to position for optimal RV imaging; alternatively, there could be a bias toward reporting RVD severity only when abnormal. Notably, only ~40% of all CICU admissions with HF during the period were included in this analysis, and many had missing data for one or more Doppler measurements, also contributing to potential selection bias. Finally, we were unable to ascertain the specific etiology of RV dysfunction, which has previously been demonstrated to have important prognostic implications.2) However, this is not uncommon in critically ill patients admitted to the CICU, where RV dysfunction may be multifactorial or unknown due to incomplete evaluation.

We acknowledge the subjectivity typically encountered when assessing RV function by bedside TTE, especially when image quality is limited, as is common in critically ill patients. In addition, objective markers are not always available or consistent in this context, though our analysis demonstrated that objective markers of RV function correlated with semi-quantitative RVD grade. We also acknowledge that guidelines for RV assessment evolved during the study period, and some objective measures were not commonly reported. In addition, we utilized the interpretations of expert echocardiographers at our institution rather than independently reviewing images, and we were unable to determine inter-observer or intra-observer variability. Furthermore, we were unable to ascertain on an individual patient level which echocardiographic parameters of RV function were prioritized by the interpreting echocardiographer in grading RV dysfunction severity. However, global assessment of RV function is commonly reported by clinical echocardiographers without indicating which variables (subjective and objective) are incorporated into this assessment. Despite these limitations, our analysis suggests that, however RV function is evaluated, globally RVD is a powerful prognostic marker.

We were unable to determine the level of physiologic support, including use of positive pressure ventilation, vasoactive and inotropic therapies, or temporary mechanical circulatory support, at the time of TTE, which may have impacted measurements of RV function and hemodynamics. We acknowledge that the use of these therapies, which impact loading conditions, may contribute to RVD. However, this is reflective of the contemporary CICU population, which has become increasingly complex with higher prevalence of multi-organ failure. Regardless of whether RVD is entirely intrinsic or exacerbated by adverse loading conditions from critical care therapies, such as positive pressure mechanical ventilation or vasopressors, it remains prognostic. Finally, our one-time TTE assessment of RV function represents a single snapshot of a dynamic state, which can be affected by loading conditions, critical care therapies, and numerous other pathophysiologic mechanisms; this underscores the need to perform serial evaluation of RV function using standardized multicomponent assessment in future studies.

In this large cohort of CICU patients, RVD occurred in >75%, and a pragmatic, semi-quantitative approach to global assessment of RV function by TTE correlated with advanced Doppler data. Compared to “Normal” RV function, even mild RVD was associated with higher mortality; severe RVD was associated with exceptionally poor prognosis, comparable to that of LV-predominant CS. Our findings support the prognostic value of a pragmatic, semi-quantitative approach to global assessment of RV function by TTE or POCUS in critically ill patients with HF, in whom limited imaging windows may preclude the ability to obtain more advanced hemodynamic data. Further prospective studies would be helpful to validate our findings, particularly focusing on utilization of POCUS by the treating clinician compared to consultative TTE and utilizing serial assessments of RV function.
