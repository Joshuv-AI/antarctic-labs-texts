First encounters with mortality: a qualitative study of novice nurses’ experiences with a patient’s death under their care in Iran
Authors: Amir Shahzeydi, Mansour Dianati, Faramarz Kalhor, Zahra Hadian Jazi, Mahdi Fallah Ghohroudi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient death is an inescapable and emotionally demanding reality in nursing, frequently causing significant stress, particularly for novice nurses. Their limited experience often leaves them psychologically vulnerable to the distress associated with patient mortality and unprepared for sensitive end-of-life communication. Existing research predominantly focuses on experienced nurses, leaving a gap in understanding the unique challenges faced by novice nurses, especially within culturally distinct contexts like Iran, where cultural and religious frameworks profoundly shape grief and death perceptions. This qualitative study aimed to deeply explore the experiences of novice Iranian nurses when encountering patient death in clinical practice.MethodsA qualitative content analysis, guided by Graneheim and Lundman (2004), was used. Twenty novice nurses (less than 36 months clinical experience) were purposively recruited from Iranian hospitals. Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews. Transcribed interviews underwent rigorous analysis: identifying meaning units, condensation, coding, categorization, and theme development. MAXQDA 2020 facilitated the process. Trustworthiness was ensured via credibility (member checking), dependability (audit trail), confirmability (team discussions), and transferability (thick description).ResultsThe analysis revealed a significant disconnect between the nurses’ intense personal experiences and the lack of institutional support. Six major themes emerged, detailing their journey from the initial Emotional Turmoil and grappling with Ethical Paradoxes, to identifying significant Systemic Gaps in support and education. This journey prompted a challenging Professional Transformation, forcing nurses to engage in Cultural-Spiritual Meaning-Making and a personal search for Seeking Balance.ConclusionNovice nurses are left to navigate the trauma of patient death within a system that fails to adequately prepare or support them. The findings highlight an urgent need for healthcare organizations to implement structured support, such as mandatory debriefing and ethics consultations, and for nursing curricula to integrate practical, high-fidelity training in end-of-life care. Supporting novice nurses is essential for fostering resilience, ensuring compassionate care, and strengthening the nursing profession.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Novice nurses, Patient death, Experience, Coping strategies, Qualitative content analysis, Iran

Introduction

Despite significant technological advancements and an expanding array of medical interventions, death remains an inevitable reality [1]. It is often perceived as a profoundly unsettling experience, marked by inherent uncertainty about its timing and circumstances, and is frequently regarded as a taboo subject across diverse cultures [2]. Consequently, attitudes toward death are generally negative, shaped by a complex interplay of factors including occupation, gender, educational background, personal life experiences, and belief systems, all of which influence an individual’s perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors regarding mortality [3].

The death of a patient represents a profound and challenging aspect of clinical practice [4]. When unexpected, such an event is often perceived not as a natural outcome but as an abnormal disruption of the life process [5], which can have severe psychological impacts on healthcare providers [6]. Empirical evidence underscores this effect; for instance, one study found that more than half of a sample of medical residents (54.2%) exhibited symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) following patient deaths [7]. Similarly, research among Polish midwives revealed that experiencing patient death led to feelings of helplessness (57.4%) and depression (48.1%) [8]. Compounding this psychological distress, medical staff typically have very little opportunity to process their grief, as they are afforded minimal time to rest after the loss of a patient under their care. They frequently report emotions of professional failure, hopelessness, despair, and helplessness in the aftermath [9].

Among healthcare professionals, nurses typically spend the most time with patients and often develop the strongest therapeutic relationships, making them particularly vulnerable to the emotional impact of patient loss [10, 11]. Consequently, caring for a dying patient is often described as a deeply painful and stressful experience for nursing staff [12]. As frontline responders to patient death, nurses are directly responsible for post-mortem care and must navigate the complex physical, emotional, and relational dimensions of mortality [13]. Empirical studies highlight the prevalence of this distress. Kostka et al. (2021) reported that a significant majority of nurses exhibited strong emotional reactions to patient death, with rates of 68.57% in the emergency department, 55.88% in internal medicine, 55.88% in surgery, and 51.28% in intensive care units [4]. Further quantifying these emotions, Cybulska et al. (2022) found that following a patient’s death, 73.4% of nurses experienced sadness, 58.5% felt helplessness, and 43.6% reported feelings of regret [6]. Similarly, a study in Iran revealed that 75.1% of nurses felt incompetent, 69.1% expressed an unwillingness to care for dying patients, and 27.3% experienced regret and sadness during end-of-life care provision [14]. It is important to recognize that reactions to death are highly individual and difficult to predict. A nurse’s response is often influenced by their prior experiences in caring for patients at the end of life. Therefore, supporting nurses in identifying and understanding their own perceptions and attitudes toward death is crucial. This self-awareness is a fundamental prerequisite for developing effective coping strategies and adapting to the challenging realities of patient mortality [13]. This process of adaptation and coping is often significantly more difficult for novice nurses, who lack the experiential framework of their more experienced colleagues [15, 16].

A novice nurse is typically defined as a newly graduated individual with less than 36 months of clinical experience [17, 18]. The transition from student to practicing professional is often experienced as a profound shock [19], primarily due to the sudden increase in responsibilities and the associated challenges in adapting to this new role [20]. This difficulty is especially pronounced in end-of-life care. A study by Naseri-Salahshour et al. (2020) in Iran found that novice nurses frequently reported feelings of incompetence in complex clinical environments. These feelings manifested as a lack of self-confidence, inadequate communication skills with critically ill patients and their families—particularly when delivering bad news—and an inability to make decisive decisions in critical situations [21].

Recent studies confirm that new nurses recognize the critical importance of end-of-life care; however, they consistently report feeling inadequately prepared to manage the dying process [15, 16]. This lack of preparedness has significant emotional and professional consequences. For example, in intensive care units, novice nurses describe feeling unprepared, unsupported, inexperienced, profoundly distressed, and isolated following their first experience with a patient’s death [16]. The impact is equally severe in pediatric settings; upon first encountering a dying child, nurses often report a lack of psychological readiness, leading to intense stress, confusion, and physical symptoms such as sweating, blushing, and tachycardia, all of which can negatively affect their ability to care for other patients [22, 23]. Further illustrating this global phenomenon, a study conducted in Chinese palliative care units found that novice nurses commonly experienced fear, anxiety, shock, sadness, and regret when a patient died, sometimes to the extent of having recurring nightmares about the deceased [24].

Consequently, novice nurses face significant challenges during their initial encounters with patient death, which can lead to serious physical and psychological distress. It is imperative that these reactions receive increased attention and systematic support [25]. Collectively, these factors contribute to negative perceptions of the nursing profession, emotional exhaustion, intentions to leave the field, and feelings of emptiness and burnout [26]. A root cause of this issue is that nursing education often fails to adequately prepare students for the inevitability of patient death, leaving them without the necessary competence or emotional readiness to confront it [25, 27]. However, if this challenge is properly addressed through targeted support and intervention, a novice nurse’s encounter with death can become a valuable opportunity for profound learning and professional growth [28]. Such support can, in turn, improve caregiving behaviors by reducing the associated anxiety [29]. This underscores the critical need for research across diverse clinical departments to identify existing gaps and develop effective strategies to address this challenge and leverage it as an opportunity for clinical advancement. Despite this need, there remains a notable scarcity of information regarding novice nurses’ initial reactions to patient death, their coping mechanisms, and the subsequent impact of these experiences on their professional performance [30]. Existing studies on this subject are limited in number [31] and are often confined to specific clinical specialties, such as intensive care, pediatrics, or palliative care [16, 22–24]. In Iran, the only identified study exploring novice nurses’ experiences with patient death was conducted by Vahdaninya et al. in 2016. However, this study was limited in scope to particular clinical wards and did not provide concrete clinical strategies for managing the adverse effects of these experiences [32].

Given this gap in the literature, a qualitative study is essential to explore the experiences of novice nurses across diverse clinical departments—including surgical, specialty, pediatric, neonatal, and palliative care—when encountering their first patient death. This approach will also illuminate the adaptive strategies they employ to navigate the resulting challenges. Unlike quantitative methods, qualitative inquiry serves as a powerful tool for uncovering and explaining the complex realities of the clinical environment, providing a deeper understanding of nuanced phenomena within the healthcare system [33]. It enables researchers to gain rich, insightful knowledge of the context and lived experiences, thereby establishing a foundation for developing practical models to modify and improve current processes. Therefore, this qualitative study aims to address this gap by exploring the lived experiences of novice nurses in Iran—across a range of clinical departments—as they navigate their first encounter with patient death.

Methods and materials

Research design

This study adopted a qualitative content analysis approach, guided by the methodological framework of Graneheim and Lundman (2004), to explore the experiences of novice nurses encountering patient death [34]. This methodology was specifically chosen to provide rich, in-depth insights into this complex human phenomenon within its social, cultural, political, historical, and economic contexts. The analytical framework for content analysis, as outlined by Graneheim and Lundman (2004), systematically encompassed the entire research process from data collection to the generation of themes, ensuring a rigorous and systematic approach to understanding the latent and manifest meanings within the data. To enhance methodological rigor and transparency, the study also adhered to the COREQ (COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative Research) 32-item checklist, which facilitates comprehensive and systematic reporting of qualitative studies [35].

Participants and sampling

The study population consisted of novice nurses, defined as having less than 36 months of clinical experience. This timeframe was chosen based on Patricia Benner’s “From Novice to Expert” model, which characterizes nurses in this period as advancing from beginner to competent stages [36]. While acknowledging that high turnover rates in some local contexts may lead to nurses with this level of experience being considered “experienced” in their units, this theoretical definition was maintained to ensure consistency with the established stages of professional nursing development.

A purposive sampling strategy was employed to recruit participants who could provide rich, insightful data directly relevant to the research question. To ensure a diverse range of perspectives, maximum variation sampling was also incorporated. This was achieved by purposefully selecting participants from a wide range of hospital settings to capture a broad spectrum of experiences. These included various surgical units (e.g., general, vascular, orthopedic, oncology, and cardiac surgery), critical care units (ICU, CCU, NICU, PICU), emergency departments (adult, pediatric, and cardiac), and specialized internal medicine and cardiology wards. Variation was also sought across different genders, work shifts, and lengths of early career experience.

Following ethical approval, recruitment was facilitated through liaison with hospital nursing offices. The process continued until data saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new significant themes or significant insights emerged from three consecutive interviews [37, 38]. The final sample comprised 20 novice nurses (13 women and 7 men), with ages ranging from 22 to 26 years. Detailed demographic information is presented in Table 1.

Data collection

The primary data for this study were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews with novice nurses. Interviews were conducted by two members of the research team (Dr. Hadian and Mr. Shahzeydi). Dr. Hadian holds a PhD in Nursing with specialized training in qualitative interviewing, and Mr. Shahzeydi is an experienced qualitative researcher. Importantly, neither interviewer had any prior clinical or managerial relationship with the participants.

A specially developed interview guide (Box 1) was used to explore participants’ experiences, emotions, and coping strategies related to patient death. The semi-structured format allowed flexibility to pursue emergent topics while ensuring that core areas were consistently addressed. Each session was scheduled during the nurse’s personal time, in a private location of their choosing, to respect professional responsibilities and ensure comfort and confidentiality. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation, and participants were reminded of their right to withdraw at any stage. Interviews began with introductory questions to build rapport, followed by open-ended prompts encouraging participants to share detailed narratives of their encounters with patient death. Probing questions were used to clarify and deepen responses. The interviewers adopted an empathetic, non-judgmental stance, allowing participants to speak freely without interruption. Sessions lasted between 45 and 90 min and were audio-recorded with permission.

Additional data sources were gathered to enrich the dataset and support triangulation. These included participants’ voluntarily shared reflective journals and the researchers’ field notes, which captured non-verbal cues (e.g., emotional expressions, silences), contextual observations, and immediate analytical reflections. At the end of each interview, participants were invited to share further comments or clarifications, and interviewers expanded their field notes immediately afterward to document fresh insights. Following data collection, all recordings were transcribed verbatim by [M. F. Gh]. Each transcript was cross-checked against the original audio to ensure accuracy. To maintain confidentiality, transcripts were anonymized and all study data were stored securely in accordance with ethical guidelines. Transcripts were then returned to a purposeful sub-sample of participants for their review and comment, which confirmed that the data accurately captured their experiences.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using qualitative content analysis, rigorously guided by the methodological framework of Graneheim and Lundman (2004) [34]. While Cowles and Rogers’ Grief (1991) may have informed your conceptual understanding of grief [39], it is crucial to clarify that the analytical process itself adhered strictly to Graneheim and Lundman. This approach was chosen for its systematic yet flexible nature in processing large volumes of textual data, moving from manifest content (what is explicitly stated) to latent content (the underlying meaning), while explicitly addressing the influence of researcher pre-understandings.

To enhance the trustworthiness of the analysis and minimize researcher bias, the analysis was conducted by a team of three researchers whose expertise in nursing and qualitative methods has been previously described. A systematic and collaborative approach was then employed. First, two researchers independently coded the initial three transcripts to form their preliminary interpretations. Second, they met to compare their codes, discuss discrepancies, and collaboratively develop a unified coding framework. Throughout the analysis, the entire research team held regular meetings to discuss emerging themes, challenge interpretations, and ensure the findings remained grounded in the participants’ narratives. Any disagreements in interpretation were resolved through discussion and by returning to the original data. A senior qualitative researcher provided oversight to facilitate consensus and ensure analytical rigor. This iterative and collaborative process ensured that the final themes were a collective and well-substantiated interpretation of the data, rather than the product of a single researcher’s bias. The organization, coding, and retrieval of data were facilitated by MAXQDA 2020 software; the three team members involved in the analysis were highly proficient with this tool, having received formal training.

Trustworthiness

The trustworthiness of the study’s findings was ensured by addressing the criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability, as outlined by Guba and Lincoln (1994) [40].

The specific procedures implemented to meet each criterion are detailed below.

To ensure credibility—that the findings are a true reflection of the participants’ experiences—several strategies were employed. Prolonged Engagement was achieved as the research team maintained a four-month presence in the hospital setting, which was crucial for building rapport and trust with participants and gaining a deep, contextual understanding of their environment. To further enrich the data, researchers provided their contact information to all participants, encouraging them to share any additional reflections or information that arose after the formal interview, ensuring an ongoing channel for communication. Additionally, Member Checking was conducted; after the initial analysis of each interview, a summary of key statements and preliminary interpretations was sent to each participant for their review and feedback, which was then used to verify accuracy and refine the analysis. Furthermore, Peer Debriefing was performed by having the emergent themes and the analytical process reviewed by an independent senior qualitative researcher who was not part of the study. This expert provided an external check, challenged the research team’s assumptions, and helped mitigate potential researcher bias.

Dependability—ensuring the research process is consistent, logical, and well-documented—was established by creating a comprehensive audit trail. This trail provides a transparent record of the entire research process and includes anonymized audio recordings, verbatim transcripts, the interview guide and all its revisions, detailed notes from all research team meetings, and memos documenting all analytical decisions and the evolution of the coding framework.

Confirmability— ensuring the findings are grounded in the data and not shaped by researcher bias—was primarily achieved through two key strategies. First, the comprehensive audit trail provided a transparent record of the research process. Second, the rigorous, team-based analytical process, as detailed previously in the Data Analysis section, ensured that findings were derived from the data through systematic discussion and consensus, thereby minimizing the influence of individual researcher bias.

Finally, to enable transferability and allow readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other contexts, this manuscript provides a rich, thick description of the research context, the participant selection criteria, the detailed demographic characteristics of the sample (as presented in Table 1), and the comprehensive data collection and analysis procedures. This detailed information provides readers with the necessary context to make informed judgments about the potential relevance and transferability of the findings to other similar settings or populations.

Results

This qualitative content analysis study explored the experiences of novice nurses in confronting patient death, revealing a rich tapestry of challenges, coping mechanisms, and transformative processes. Through thematic analysis of the interview data, six overarching themes and their associated sub-themes were identified, providing a comprehensive understanding of this critical aspect of early nursing practice. These themes illuminate the emotional, professional, cultural, systemic, and ethical dimensions of their experiences. Table 2 provides a summary of the themes, sub-themes, and relevant quotations.

2. On your first day as a nurse, what were your thoughts about facing patient death, and what actions did you think you would take? After your first experience, how did your actual response compare to those initial expectations?

3. Looking back, is there anything you wish you had done differently during or after your first encounter with a patient’s death?

4. What advice would you offer to other novice nurses facing their first patient death?

Emotional turmoil

The most immediate and visceral response novice nurses described was a wave of intense and often overwhelming emotions. This turmoil was not a single feeling but a complex constellation of shock, grief, and profound self-doubt that was powerful enough to shake their professional confidence to its core. This distress was particularly acute during their first encounter with mortality or when the patient was young.

This emotional turmoil was not merely a feeling of sadness; it frequently manifested in severe physical and psychological symptoms like panic attacks, nightmares, and insomnia that disrupted their daily lives. Participants recounted how the trauma seeped into their subconscious, triggering involuntary physiological responses and robbing them of rest. As one nurse detailed:

…For a while after this incident, I would get a sense of panic; my heart rate would go up, and I would jolt awake from sleep… I had nightmares, and my sleep became very irregular. The content of the nightmares was the resuscitation process and the family’s distress. I was like this for two or three weeks… (P15)

Beyond the immediate physiological reactions, the experience left a lasting sensory imprint on the nurses. They described being haunted by vivid, intrusive memories of the patient’s final moments—the sights, the sounds, and the feeling of helplessness. These memories were not just recollections; they were powerful emotional triggers that could resurface without warning, as one nurse powerfully conveyed while becoming emotional during the interview:

…When you asked about the first death, the image of that child is still in my mind: his belly full of ascites, his yellow skin, his thin arms and legs… I was so scared and shocked. After his death, it was the first time I had to run an ECG on a flatline. It was a very bad day… (P7, crying during the interview)

Shock and Guilt. For nearly all participants, the first encounter with patient death was characterized by a profound sense of shock and personal responsibility. This was often intertwined with intense feelings of guilt, as nurses replayed the events in their minds, questioning their actions, knowledge, and competence. They articulated a painful internal narrative of personal failure, wondering if a different action might have led to a different outcome. This self-blame was a heavy burden that made them question their suitability for the nursing profession itself. (See Table 2 for illustrative quotes).

Ethical paradoxes

Novice nurses often found themselves navigating complex ethical gray areas with little formal guidance, leading to significant moral distress. These paradoxes arose from conflicts between their professional duties, personal values, institutional policies, and the desires of patients and families, leaving them feeling powerless and uncertain.

…We arrived at the bedside and saw his daughter begging us not to resuscitate… while on the other side, his niece was covering his mouth, saying, ‘I won’t let you put a tube in his mouth.’… This itself is a challenge: should I resuscitate when they don’t want it, or not? It was a real question for me what to do in such a situation… (P13)

Hesitation in Critical Decisions. Participants expressed profound uncertainty and internal conflict when faced with life-and-death decisions, particularly regarding resuscitation orders for patients with a poor prognosis. They felt caught between the technical imperative to act and the humanistic question of whether their actions were causing more harm than good, especially when the patient’s own wishes were unknown and family members were in disagreement. (See Table 2 for illustrative quotes).

Systemic gaps

Participants consistently identified significant deficiencies in the healthcare system, both in their education and in their workplace, which left them feeling unprepared and unsupported in dealing with patient death. They described a system that was adept at clinical training but failed to address the human and emotional dimensions of nursing.

…The only thing I remember about death is the last part of the fundamentals textbook that said religious values should be respected… That was the entire summary of death we were taught. These things I’m talking about, which are very important… are completely missing from the references… (P3)

Lack of Formal Support System. An overwhelming sentiment was the feeling of being left alone to process these traumatic events. Nurses described a complete absence of structured, institutional support systems, such as debriefing sessions, peer support groups, or readily available psychological counseling, to help them navigate the emotional aftermath of a patient’s death. (See Table 2 for illustrative quotes).

Professional transformation

The encounter with mortality was a powerful, double-edged sword that profoundly shaped the nurses’ professional identity and practice. It simultaneously fostered a deeper sense of compassion while also leading to significant disillusionment and questioning of their career choice.

…In this field that I’m in (oncology), whatever I do and every effort I make ultimately has a sense of fruitlessness and yields no results. This doesn’t give me a good feeling… It doesn’t make me love nursing more… (P12)

Increased Sensitivity and Empathy. For many, the painful experience was channeled into a more compassionate and meticulous form of nursing. Having witnessed the finality of death and the grief of families, they became more attuned to the small acts of comfort and care that could make a significant difference for both the patient and their loved ones. This trauma, once processed, became a source of enhanced empathy. (See Table 2 for illustrative quotes).

Cultural-spiritual meaning-making

In their struggle to comprehend mortality, nurses actively drew upon—and sometimes questioned—their personal, cultural, and spiritual belief systems. This process of meaning-making was highly individual and essential for contextualizing their experiences and finding a sense of peace or purpose.

…This experience made me appreciate my own life more, to value every single moment. To enjoy a simple cup of tea with my family… I don’t want to be like those who were on their deathbeds saying ‘if only.’ This has been my most important takeaway… (P5)

This perspective, often rooted in religious or spiritual beliefs, helped frame death as a meaningful process rather than a purely negative event, allowing for a greater sense of acceptance. Seeking balance

In response to the intense emotional and ethical challenges, novice nurses actively developed and relied on a range of coping strategies to restore their emotional equilibrium and create a sustainable professional life. These strategies were largely self-initiated and focused on both internal and external sources of support.

…The other nurses came and told me, ‘This is a normal thing, you have to be ready for it, this happens a lot in this field.’… And I calmed down a bit… (P20)

Social and Peer Support. The most immediate and effective source of comfort came from peers. Sharing experiences with colleagues who truly understood the situation provided validation, reduced feelings of isolation, and offered practical advice. Discussing events with close friends or family was also a common strategy, though the shared understanding with fellow nurses was often described as more helpful. (See Table 2 for illustrative quotes).

Discussion

This qualitative study provides a nuanced and comprehensive understanding of novice nurses’ profound experiences when confronting patient death, consistently echoing and significantly expanding upon established literature in nursing, psychology, and bioethics. The six identified themes – Emotional Turmoil, Ethical Paradoxes, Systemic Gaps, Professional Transformation, Cultural-Spiritual Meaning-Making, and Seeking Balance – collectively underscore the pervasive emotional, psychological, and professional impact of these critical events, while simultaneously illuminating key areas for enhanced support, education, and policy reform.

The pervasive Emotional Turmoil described by novice nurses, characterized by acute shock, pervasive guilt, and profound grief, aligns directly with established theoretical constructs such as compassion fatigue and secondary traumatic stress [41, 42]. However, our findings suggest that novice nurses, due to their limited clinical experience and still-forming professional identities, are uniquely susceptible to these reactions. The “first death” experience, often recounted as a seminal moment, appears to trigger intense emotional and even physical manifestations, including panic attacks and insomnia, consistent with the immediate and overwhelming nature of acute stress responses [43–45]. While the observed progression towards a “normalization of death” might appear adaptive for emotional self-preservation, this desensitization warrants careful monitoring. It presents a potential double-edged sword: offering a degree of emotional buffering against overwhelming distress, yet simultaneously posing a risk for emotional blunting or depersonalization, which could subtly erode the empathetic core of nursing practice if not consciously managed [42, 46]. This delicate balance between self-protection and maintaining compassion requires explicit attention in professional development.

This internal turmoil was frequently compounded by the Ethical Paradoxes that underscore the complex moral environment in which nurses operate. The observed hesitation in critical decision-making and the profound conflicts arising from unrealistic family expectations highlight the frequent and debilitating experience of moral distress. Here, nurses recognize the ethically appropriate course of action but are constrained from implementing it due to various factors, including institutional policies, physician orders, or conflicting familial demands [47, 48]. Navigating these dilemmas necessitates not only advanced communication skills and sophisticated ethical reasoning but also robust institutional support for shared decision-making processes, where nurses play a pivotal role in mediating between complex medical realities and often emotionally charged family desires [49]. The distress from medical decisions, even those made by others, further indicates the profound emotional and ethical burden nurses carry when they perceive care to be misaligned with patient values or professional integrity.

These individual struggles are situated within larger Systemic Gaps identified by participants, pointing to critical structural deficiencies within both healthcare institutions and foundational nursing education. The consistent reporting of a glaring lack of formal, readily accessible support systems, including professional psychological counseling and structured debriefing mechanisms, represents a critical unmet need that resonates with widespread calls for improved mental health resources for healthcare workers globally [50, 51]. The perceived inadequacy of practical training in managing death and effectively communicating with grieving families highlights a persistent and concerning chasm between theoretical nursing education and the complex, emotionally charged realities of clinical practice [52, 53]. Addressing these systemic deficiencies is not merely a matter of individual nurse well-being; it is paramount for ensuring the delivery of high-quality, compassionate, and ethically sound end-of-life care.

The combination of intense emotional burden and a lack of systemic support inevitably leads to a challenging Professional Transformation. This theme illuminates a profound paradox of growth through adversity that is central to nursing development. While confronting death demonstrably fosters heightened empathy and sensitivity, thereby enhancing compassionate care [54, 55], it simultaneously carries the significant risk of moral distress, feelings of futility, and a desire to exit the profession [56]. This finding is particularly critical as it highlights a vulnerable juncture where the absence of timely support can contribute significantly to early career attrition in a profession already facing workforce shortages. The unaddressed emotional burden can lead to a fundamental disillusionment with the nursing role itself, underscoring the urgent imperative for proactive support systems.

In the face of these professional and emotional challenges, nurses turn inward to Cultural-Spiritual Meaning-Making, a deeply personal process of grappling with mortality. Our findings powerfully underscore how individuals draw upon their unique cultural heritage, religious frameworks, and familial narratives to construct coherent interpretations of death, a process fundamental to coping with profound loss [57, 58]. The reported challenges to deeply held beliefs, particularly when confronting the death of innocent children, reflect the pervasive existential struggle with suffering [59, 60]. Conversely, the redefinition of life’s value suggests a profound shift in personal perspective, a common and often positive outcome of confronting mortality [61]. This dimension emphasizes the necessity of holistic, culturally sensitive support that respectfully acknowledges the diverse spiritual landscapes of healthcare providers.

Ultimately, nurses actively engaged in Seeking Balance through a repertoire of coping strategies, consistent with resilience-building models in high-stress occupations [62, 63]. The pervasive reliance on social support from colleagues emerges as particularly salient, reinforcing the critical role of peer debriefing and shared experiential processing in mitigating psychological distress [61, 64]. Beyond formal support, the organic formation of informal peer networks provides a vital space for emotional ventilation and validation. The engagement in personal restorative activities and the conscious effort towards work-life separation underscore a growing recognition of self-care as a professional imperative, essential for preventing occupational burnout and promoting long-term career sustainability [65, 66].

Limitations

While this study offers rich, in-depth insights into the experiences of novice nurses, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations inherent to qualitative research. The findings, while comprehensive for the participant group, may not be directly generalizable to all novice nurses across diverse healthcare settings, cultures, or geographic regions. The reliance on self-reported data introduces potential for recall bias or social desirability bias. Future research could benefit from longitudinal designs to capture changes in experiences and coping mechanisms over time, as well as mixed-methods approaches to quantify the prevalence of these challenges and validate qualitative insights across larger populations. Comparative studies across different healthcare systems or cultural contexts would also provide valuable cross-cultural perspectives.

Conclusion

This study illuminates the profound and isolating journey of novice nurses during their first encounters with patient death. The findings reveal a distressing disconnect: while new nurses experience intense emotional turmoil, navigate complex ethical paradoxes, and search for personal meaning, they are confronted by stark systemic gaps in institutional and educational support. This forces them into an unsupported and solitary process of professional transformation, where they must manage trauma and moral distress largely on their own. The central finding is not simply that novice nurses struggle, but that they do so within a system that fails to adequately prepare or support them for one of the most challenging aspects of their profession.

The implications of these findings are both urgent and clear, demanding immediate action. To address the acute emotional distress and prevent burnout, healthcare organizations must implement structured, mandatory support systems. This includes routine post-event debriefing sessions to help nurses process shock, grief, and guilt in a safe environment; readily accessible, confidential psychological counseling to provide ongoing professional support; and accessible ethics consultation services to empower nurses and foster a culture of open dialogue when they are caught between medical directives and patient or family needs. Concurrently, to address the identified educational deficiencies, nursing curricula require fundamental reform to bridge the gap between theory and practice. This calls for the integration of high-fidelity simulations focused on end-of-life scenarios, alongside robust, practical training in end-of-life communication, grief management, and applied ethical decision-making from the earliest stages of education.

Ultimately, supporting novice nurses through patient death is not merely an act of compassion; it is a fundamental requirement for building and retaining a resilient, empathetic, and effective nursing workforce. The responsibility rests with healthcare systems and educational institutions to ensure these professionals are not left to face such defining moments alone.
