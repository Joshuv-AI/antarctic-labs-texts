Motivational interviewing chatbot improves lifestyle in primary healthcare settings in a pragmatic randomised controlled trial
Authors: Clive Ho Yin Wong, Mabel Ngai Kiu Wong, Wai Kai Hou, Fridolin Sze Thou Ting, Yuk Mun Ng, Chetwyn Che Hin Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-communicable diseases (NCDs) impose escalating demands on community-based primary healthcare (PHC) systems globally. Motivational interviewing (MI) is effective but difficult to scale due to resource and training constraints. We conducted the first pragmatic trial evaluating whether an automated chatbot operationalising MI principles could improve lifestyle behaviours when integrated into routine PHC settings. This pragmatic, open-label, multicentre randomised controlled trial across three Hong Kong PHC facilities randomised 627 adults aged 45–75 with or at risk of hypertension or diabetes. Participants received either a 12-week automated MI chatbot intervention plus usual care or usual care alone. Modified intention-to-treat analysis (n = 460) showed significant improvements in the intervention group compared to usual care in physical activity (576 MET-min/week), fruit/vegetable intake (0.27 portions/day), and committed action (0.95 a.u.) at 12 weeks. Larger effects among adherent participants suggested a dose–response relationship. Exploratory subgroup analyses showed strong effects among participants with low baseline physical activity (537 MET-min/week) and systolic blood pressure reductions among hypertensive participants (−5.03 mmHg). Effects were sustained at 9-month follow-up. No serious adverse events were reported. This scalable MI chatbot offers a pragmatic approach to NCD prevention in resource-constrained settings. Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05949320, registered 29 June 2023.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) pose a growing global health burden. Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributable to NCDs rose from 1.47 billion in 2010 to 1.73 billion in 20211, and are projected to dominate all diseases by 20502. The World Health Organisation (WHO) advocates primary healthcare (PHC) as a holistic, people-centred approach to NCD prevention and management3. However, the scalability and accessibility of PHC services are hindered by limited resources, time constraints, and travel distances4. Motivational interviewing (MI), an evidence-based counselling approach that supports behaviour change by exploring ambivalence and strengthening intrinsic motivation, has demonstrated effectiveness for improving physical activity, diet, and clinical outcomes in people with or at risk of NCDs5.

However, MI is traditionally delivered face-to-face or by telephone and requires trained practitioners and substantial consultation time, limiting its scalability in resource-constrained settings. Recent advances in mobile health (mHealth) technologies, particularly conversational agents (chatbots), offer promising solutions for delivering automated health behaviour interventions at scale. Systematic review evidence demonstrates that chatbots can produce significant improvements in physical activity, dietary intake, and sleep behaviours6.

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 132 randomised controlled trials evaluating MI-based interventions for physical activity identified only six studies (4.5%) that delivered MI through internet or mobile applications5. All six employed multicomponent designs combining MI with other digital supports (web-based resources, telephone counselling, self-monitoring tools, or SMS); two targeted physical activity alone, and four addressed both physical activity and diet. None used fully automated conversational agents. Beyond MI-based interventions, a small number of pragmatic trials have integrated digital lifestyle interventions into primary healthcare settings. The StopDia trial evaluated a combined digital and group-based intervention among adults at increased diabetes risk in Finland7, while the MINISTOP 2.0 trial tested a smartphone app integrated into child health visits for obesity prevention in preschool children in Sweden8. However, both trials either employed multicomponent approaches requiring face-to-face contact or restricted their focus to narrow, risk-defined populations (diabetes risk or childhood obesity).

Despite evidence that digital interventions can support behaviour change, critical gaps remain in translating this potential into routine PHC practice for broader adult populations at risk of multiple NCDs. First, existing digital MI interventions have not been tested as standalone, fully automated tools that can be deployed without additional human counselling or in-person visits, limiting their scalability in resource-constrained settings. Second, pragmatic trials integrating digital interventions into PHC have targeted specific high-risk groups (adults at elevated diabetes risk or children at risk of obesity) rather than the heterogeneous populations typically seen in PHC, where individuals present with varying combinations of NCD risk factors including hypertension, diabetes, and obesity. Third, no trial has evaluated whether a fully automated MI chatbot can be effectively integrated into routine PHC workflows to deliver personalised behaviour change support at scale across diverse adult populations. Addressing these gaps is essential to realising the WHO vision of PHC-centred NCD prevention.

The primary objective of this study was to assess the effects of a fully automated mobile app–based e-MI chatbot on lifestyle changes (physical activity and fruit and vegetable consumption) in adults who have or are at risk of hypertension and diabetes within primary healthcare settings at 12 weeks. We hypothesised that participants assigned to the e-MI intervention would demonstrate greater improvements in both outcomes compared with those receiving usual services. Secondary objectives were to evaluate changes in motivation/readiness for change and assess intervention reach and engagement. We also explored effects on cardiovascular risk markers including blood pressure and waist circumference, and assessed sustainability of effects in the intervention group at 9 months.

Results

Study participants

Six hundred twenty-seven people were randomised, with 416 assigned to the intervention group and 211 to the usual care group (Fig. 1). Following randomisation, 78 participants in the intervention group did not receive the allocated intervention due to smartphone incompatibility, and 1 in the usual care group did not complete the assessment. Of the 548 who received their allocated intervention (338 intervention, 210 usual care), 52 intervention and 36 usual care participants were lost to follow-up at 12 weeks, resulting in 286 and 174 completing the 12-week assessment, respectively. These 460 participants with complete baseline and 12-week data formed the primary modified ITT analysis population. Assessment timeline is presented in Fig. 2. Compliance

In the intervention group, 70.3% of the 286 participants who completed follow-up completed at least 50% of the intervention content, and 49.3% demonstrated higher engagement, achieving 70% or more of the programme (Supplementary Table 2).

Post-intervention primary and secondary outcomes

The modified ITT analysis revealed that the intervention group achieved significantly greater increases than usual care in physical activity (adjusted mean difference: 576 MET-min/week [95% CI: 173–978], d = 0.27, pHolm = 0.010) and committed action (0.95 a.u. [95% CI: 0.42–1.49], d = 0.34, pHolm = 0.002), with a modest increase in fruit and vegetable consumption (0.27 portions/day [95% CI: 0.00 to 0.53], d = 0.19, pHolm = 0.047; Table 2). CACE analysis indicated larger effects among adherent participants: physical activity (812 MET-min/week [95% CI: 279– 1368], d = 0.38, pHolm = 0.010), fruit and vegetable consumption (0.38 portions/day [95% CI: 0.01–0.75], d = 0.27, pHolm = 0.046), and committed action (1.34 a.u. [95% CI: 0.61–2.07], d = 0.47, pHolm = 0.002; Fig. 3; Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Sensitivity analyses addressing all 167 missing participants confirmed the robustness of findings for physical activity and committed action across worst-case, best-case, and multiple imputation scenarios; fruit and vegetable consumption effects were significant under MI-ITT (p = 0.018) and best-case scenarios but attenuated under worst-case assumptions (p = 0.082; Supplementary Table 5).

Subgroup analysis

Three families of exploratory subgroup analyses revealed differential intervention effects across baseline risk factors (Table 3; Fig. 4; Supplementary Table 6). Participants with low baseline physical activity achieved greater increases in energy expenditure (537 MET-min/week, 95% CI: 42–1032, d = 0.65, p = 0.034, not significant after Holm–Bonferroni correction). Those with low motivation showed significant improvements in committed action (1.00 a.u., 95% CI: 0.45–1.54, d = 0.37, pHolm = 0.002; OR = 2.73 for progression to action/maintenance, pHolm = 0.032). Participants with central obesity achieved greater waist circumference reduction (−1.31 cm, 95% CI: −2.54 to −0.09, d = −0.24; OR = 1.83 for ≥3 cm reduction, pHolm = 0.014), while hypertensive participants showed greater systolic blood pressure reduction (−5.03 mmHg, 95% CI: −9.07 to −0.99, d = −0.38, p = 0.015, not significant after Holm–Bonferroni correction; OR = 2.38 for ≥5 mmHg reduction, pHolm = 0.022). No significant subgroup effects were observed for fruit and vegetable consumption, BMI, prehypertension, or diastolic blood pressure. Intervention effects were comparable between participants with clinical diagnoses and those at risk only: both groups showed similar improvements in physical activity (clinical: 290 MET-min/week; at-risk: 337 MET-min/week) and fruit and vegetable consumption (clinical: 0.19 portions/day; at-risk: 0.35 portions/day), with no significant between-group differences (Supplementary Table 7).

Exploratory longitudinal sustainability analysis (intervention group only)

The following longitudinal data include only the intervention group; the absence of a concurrent usual care group at this timepoint precludes causal attribution of sustained changes to the intervention.

Longitudinal analyses from baseline (T1) to 3-month post-intervention (T2) and 9-month follow-up (T3) demonstrated sustained improvements across primary and secondary outcomes (Table 4; Fig. 5). Physical activity increased progressively, with significant gains at 9 months (T3 − T1: 1253 MET-min/week [95% CI: 372–2135]); effects were particularly pronounced among initially inactive participants (T3 − T1: 933 MET-min/week [95% CI: 572–1295]). Fruit and vegetable consumption increased significantly by 3 months and remained stable at 9 months (T3 − T1: 0.30 portions/day [95% CI: 0.09–0.51]). Committed action improved and was maintained through follow-up (T3 − T1: 0.51 a.u. [95% CI: 0.10–0.92]). Clinical markers also improved progressively: waist circumference decreased by 3.92 cm (−4.98 to −2.85) among those with central obesity, while systolic blood pressure decreased by 12.88 mmHg (−17.30 to −8.45) among hypertensive participants.

Adverse event

No adverse events attributable to the intervention were reported during the trial period.

Discussion

The smartphone-delivered e-MI chatbot was associated with increased physical activity and fruit and vegetable consumption among participants with or at risk of diabetes and hypertension. Both clinical and at-risk participants demonstrated comparable improvements, with particularly strong effects observed among subgroups with low baseline physical activity. The behavioural changes achieved were clinically meaningful, supporting the feasibility of integrating motivational interviewing principles into scalable digital health tools for chronic disease management in primary healthcare settings.

To our knowledge, this is the first fully automated MI-based chatbot intervention integrated into primary healthcare settings for community-dwelling adults with or at risk of NCDs. Two prior PHC-based digital intervention trials have been reported: StopDia targeted adults at elevated diabetes risk in Finland7, and MINISTOP 2.0 targeted parents of preschool children at obesity risk in Sweden8. While StopDia’s self-determination theory-based web app alone was ineffective (though combined web app and group counselling improved diet quality but not physical activity), our MI-based smartphone chatbot successfully promoted positive changes in both diet and physical activity as a standalone tool through embedded counselling elements that enabled participants to explore personal values and engage in change talk, with 70% of participants completing at least half the intervention content, reflecting high engagement and the chatbot’s acceptability.

These findings contribute novel evidence consistent with the effectiveness of a chatbot-delivered motivational interviewing intervention in a primary healthcare population. Unlike previous research focusing on specific chronic conditions such as diabetes9 or hypertension10,11, or targeted single behaviours like physical activity12,13, our e-MI intervention addressed multiple lifestyle factors simultaneously. Furthermore, unlike previous PHC trials targeting narrow risk-defined populations, our intervention included community-dwelling adults with varying combinations of NCD risk factors, demonstrating the feasibility and effectiveness of comprehensive e-MI techniques delivered through a digital platform in primary healthcare settings. Unlike many traditional interventions, the fully automated nature of the chatbot may offer a scalable and potentially cost-effective approach for addressing key lifestyle factors that contribute to chronic diseases. By helping to address barriers such as limited resources and accessibility, e-MI has the potential to complement existing PHC services, though cost-effectiveness analyses are needed to confirm this.

The increase in physical activity of 576 MET-min/week is clinically significant, with our overall effect (d = 0.27) comparable to benchmarks from chatbot interventions (SMD = 0.28)6 and eHealth/mHealth interventions (SMD = 0.34)14. The e-MI was remarkably more effective among participants with low baseline physical activity (d = 0.65 vs. d = 0.27 overall). Sustained improvements at 9 months (1253 MET-min/wk, 0.30 portions/day from baseline) contrast with deteriorating effects in a meta-analysis of 21 mHealth trials (d = 0.15–0.25)15.

The modest increase of 0.27 portions/day aligns with literature (27% of digital interventions achieved 0.3–0.5 servings)16. Results less favourable than fruit-and-vegetable-only eHealth interventions (0.6–1.2 servings/day)14. The pragmatic design, targeting multiple lifestyle behaviours simultaneously and reflecting real-world clinical populations, may partly explain the attenuation compared to single-behaviour interventions. Furthermore, local dietary patterns in Hong Kong, where cooked vegetables predominate over raw vegetables in traditional diets, may have constrained responses to fruit and vegetable recommendations17. Nevertheless, applying dose–response estimates from a meta-analysis of 95 prospective studies, the observed increase of approximately 22 g/day (0.27 servings 80 g/serving) corresponds to estimated reductions of 0.9–1.0% in cardiovascular disease risk and 1.1% in all-cause mortality risk—likely underestimates given the steeper dose–response at lower baseline intakes—representing potentially clinically meaningful benefits if sustained at the population level18.

Committed action, a marker of behaviour readiness and motivation, showed notable improvement (d = 0.34), with pronounced effects among low-motivation subgroups (d = 0.37; OR for clinically meaningful improvement = 2.734). This finding aligns with MI theory: by creating space for participants to explore ambivalence and strengthen intrinsic motivation through daily conversations, the chatbot may have facilitated internalisation of behaviour change intentions into committed action. Sustained improvements at 9 months suggest that these motivational shifts may have contributed to lasting changes in behaviour and commitment to healthy practices, although the absence of a concurrent control group at this timepoint limits causal inference

The CACE analysis, which estimates treatment effects among adherent participants, yielded consistently larger effect sizes than the modified ITT analysis across all outcomes (physical activity: d = 0.38 vs 0.27; fruit and vegetable consumption: d = 0.27 vs. 0.19; committed action: d = 0.47 vs. 0.34). This pattern is consistent with a dose–response relationship, whereby participants who engaged more fully with the chatbot content appeared to achieve greater behavioural improvements. The consistency in direction between ITT and CACE estimates, combined with the absence of qualitative differences between the two approaches, strengthens confidence that the observed effects reflect genuine treatment benefits rather than selection artefacts among compliant participants.

The intervention also yielded clinically meaningful improvements in cardiovascular risk markers among at-risk subgroups. Among hypertensive participants, the intervention group showed greater systolic blood pressure reduction at 12 weeks compared to usual care (−5.03 mmHg, p = 0.015, not significant after Holm–Bonferroni correction), with participants also being significantly more likely to achieve a clinically meaningful reduction of ≥5 mmHg (pHolm = 0.022). At 9 months, the intervention group demonstrated further reductions from baseline (−12.88 mmHg); however, these longer-term effects should be interpreted cautiously as they were not compared against a concurrent usual care group and may reflect natural variation or regression to the mean. Participants with central obesity showed greater waist circumference reduction at 12 weeks (−1.31 cm vs usual care; OR = 1.83 for ≥3 cm reduction, pHolm = 0.014), with progressive improvements observed at 9 months (−3.92 cm from baseline). These anthropometric and clinical improvements, achieved through lifestyle changes alone without pharmacological intervention, highlight the potential for chatbot-delivered behavioural interventions to contribute to cardiovascular risk reduction in primary healthcare populations, while recognising that longer-term effects require confirmation in studies with extended controlled follow-up.

This study has several limitations. First, the open-label design combined with reliance on self-reported primary outcomes, introduces potential performance and reporting bias; observed effect sizes should be interpreted as potential upper bounds of the true treatment effect. However, objective clinical measurements assessed by blinded outcome assessors provide triangulating evidence that mitigates concerns about reporting bias alone driving the observed effects. Future studies should incorporate objective measures such as accelerometery and AI-assisted photo-based dietary intake logging to provide objective verification of self-reported outcomes.

Second, the pragmatic inclusive design—which maximised external validity by enroling a heterogeneous population without restrictions on baseline lifestyle behaviours—introduced substantial baseline variability that complicated treatment effect estimation. Participants who already exceeded physical activity and dietary guidelines at baseline had limited capacity for further measurable improvement, and although additional health benefits accrue beyond guideline thresholds, marginal returns diminish progressively19. These participants were also subject to regression toward the mean, which attenuates observed treatment effects in change-score analyses. In multicomponent lifestyle interventions across populations with varying non-communicable disease severity, stratifying by all relevant baseline dimensions is statistically infeasible20. Consequently, conventional intention-to-treat analyses averaging across participants with vastly different baseline capacities may underestimate effects in responsive subgroups21. Future pragmatic trials should adopt heterogeneity of treatment effect approaches using multivariable baseline risk models to estimate treatment effects across the continuum of baseline capacity rather than relying on summary averages21,22.

Third, the 9-month follow-up included only the intervention group without a concurrent usual care comparison, limiting causal attribution of sustained changes. Future studies should employ a double-blind design with an active control arm—for example, a chatbot delivering factual health information only—to enable controlled long-term comparisons and better isolate the specific effects of MI-based delivery.

Fourth, the post-randomisation exclusion of 78 intervention participants due to smartphone incompatibility introduces potential selection bias. These participants had significantly lower education levels than included participants (Fisher’s exact p = 0.005; Supplementary Fig. 1), suggesting that lower education may be associated with use of older smartphones running outdated Android versions incompatible with the app. While comprehensive sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of primary findings (Supplementary Table 5), the attrition rate among participants who received the intervention (16.1%, 88/548) was notably lower than rates reported in meta-analyses of digital health interventions: 43% (95% CI: 29–57%) for app-based chronic disease interventions23, 29.6% (95% CI: 25–34.3%) for smartphone-based diabetes management24, and 35–40% for digital dietary interventions25. Future smartphone-based interventions should ensure backward compatibility with older devices and operating system versions, and consider platform-agnostic delivery methods such as instant-messaging applications (e.g. WhatsApp) with browser-based participant dashboards to minimise technology-related exclusions.

Fifth, generalisability is limited by the sample characteristics: participants were predominantly female (67%), older adults aged 60–69 years, and recruited from urban Hong Kong. However, this demographic profile mirrors the actual service user population at the participating PHC facilities, where older adults and female service users similarly predominate, suggesting that the study sample is representative of real-world PHC settings. The higher proportion of female participants is also consistent with women’s higher response rates in health surveys26,27 and greater utilisation of primary healthcare services28,29, yet men bear a disproportionate burden of NCD-related morbidity and mortality30,31. This participation–burden mismatch underscores the need for recruitment strategies that actively engage men in NCD prevention research. The under-representation of younger and working-age adults likely reflects lower perceived health urgency among younger populations and the inability of employed individuals to attend extensive in-person assessments during working hours. Future studies should consider entirely remote designs that embed questionnaire-based assessments within the app, reserving only brief physiological measurements (e.g. blood pressure, anthropometrics) for opportunistic in-person visits, thereby reducing participant burden and broadening demographic reach.

This pragmatic randomised controlled trial demonstrates that a fully automated chatbot delivering motivational interviewing (e-MI) significantly improved physical activity, fruit and vegetable consumption, and committed action at 12 weeks compared with usual care in adults with or at risk of hypertension and diabetes in primary healthcare settings. Observational follow-up of the intervention group at 9 months suggested that behavioural improvements were sustained and clinical markers continued to improve; however, the absence of a concurrent usual care group at this timepoint precludes causal attribution of these longer-term changes to the intervention. Future research should include extended controlled follow-up periods to establish the durability of intervention effects.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a two-arm, parallel-group, open-label, superiority randomised controlled trial with 2:1 allocation ratio, conducted across three District Health Centre Express (DHCE) facilities in Hong Kong.

All DHCE members aged 45–75 years who had been identified through nurse-led interviews and electronic health record verification as having or being at risk of hypertension or diabetes were eligible. The only exclusion criterion was ownership of a smartphone incompatible with the intervention application (i.e. neither iOS nor Android).

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee at The Education University of Hong Kong (reference number: 2022-2023-0302) on 13 March 2023 in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent. The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05949320; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05949320). The project was created on ClinicalTrials.gov on 29 December 2022 and key study information was entered in early January 2023; however, due to an administrative miscommunication, the research team did not instruct the university’s Research and Development Office to release the registration record, and it was not publicly released until 29 June 2023, after the first participant was enroled on 3 April 2023. Registration was therefore retrospective with respect to the first enrolment but prospective with respect to the majority of participant recruitment and all primary outcome data collection.

A protocol amendment approved on 26 July 2023 revised the target sample size from 1600 to 613 participants because (1) DHCE could not provide sufficient physical space for concurrent assessment sessions, and (2) many potential participants were interested in the intervention but declined to participate in the research assessments due to the burden of repeated questionnaires. The a priori power analysis estimated 429 participants; the final analysed sample exceeded this estimate.

Full details of the study protocol, intervention delivery, outcome measures, and statistical procedures are provided in Supplementary 1 (study protocol) and the Supplementary Information.

Randomisation and blinding

Participants were randomised 2:1 (intervention:usual care) using stratified block randomisation based on centre membership (three levels) and clinical condition (four levels). The randomisation sequence was computer-generated by a co-author (F.T.) not involved in participant enrolment or data collection. Group assignment was determined sequentially from the pre-generated sequence when participants signed the informed consent form. Enrolment staff who obtained consent and assigned participants to groups had access to the next allocation in the sequence but could not predict future assignments due to the use of variable block sizes. Participants were not blinded to group allocation due to the nature of the intervention. Outcome assessors conducting clinical measurements remained blinded to group allocation throughout the study.

Procedure

The study employed a two-group design with a usual care group. Both groups were assessed at baseline (T1) and 12 weeks (T2); the 12-week assessment served as the primary endpoint for the controlled comparison. As a pragmatic decision, the usual care group began the e-MI intervention immediately after completing the T2 assessment, allowing them to receive the intervention without unnecessary delay. The intervention group had an additional follow-up at 9 months (T3, 39 weeks from baseline) to assess sustainability of effects. The usual care group’s 12-week assessment was conducted prior to their receipt of the e-MI intervention, ensuring an uncontaminated comparison between groups at the primary endpoint.

Participants registered online and attended face-to-face assessment sessions. At each session, participants completed questionnaires on tablet computers and underwent clinical measurements by staff blinded to allocation. The intervention group began the 12-week e-MI programme immediately after baseline; the usual care group began after the 12-week assessment. Participants received HK$300 in supermarket coupons as compensation.

Interventions

The 12-week e-MI intervention delivered daily automated motivational interviewing conversations through a smartphone app to promote physical activity and healthy eating (detailed protocol in Supplementary 1). Participants selected their primary health concern (diabetes or hypertension) and preferred communication schedule during app installation.

The intervention systematically incorporated the four core MI processes—engaging, focusing, evoking, and planning—to facilitate behaviour change32. Content was tailored to participants’ health conditions and stage of change according to the Transtheoretical Model33. Each daily exchange included personalised greetings, targeted health messages, strategic questioning using Change Talk principles, and reflection prompts. The app employed a fifteen-cycle delivery framework covering five thematic areas, with stage-of-change assessments every fourth cycle. Three optional modules provided disease management information, micro-habit development, and progress tracking. Content was co-created with multidisciplinary DHCE clinical teams.

The usual care group received monthly health education newsletters and maintained access to standard DHCE programmes (patient empowerment programme, health brochures, health talks).

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes were physical activity (MET-minutes/week, measured by International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form [IPAQ-SF])34 and fruit and vegetable consumption (portions/day, measured by Hong Kong Health Behaviour Survey)35 at 12 weeks. The secondary outcome was committed action score (from University of Rhode Island Change Assessment Scale [URICA])36. Additional measures included anthropometrics (weight, height, BMI, waist circumference), blood pressure, readiness for change (Contemplation Ladder)37, and intervention compliance (conversations completed/90 total). The Supplementary Information details all measures and scoring methods.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was estimated via simulation for a 2:1 randomised trial using ANCOVA (power = 0.80, effect size = 0.25, baseline-outcome correlation = 0.50)38. The estimated sample size was 429; assuming 30% attrition39, the target was 613 participants.

Primary and secondary outcomes

Two analytical strategies were employed. First, modified intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis included all randomised participants with available outcome data at 12 weeks (286 intervention, 174 usual care; complete-case analysis). ANCOVA was used to estimate between-group differences in change scores, adjusting for age and sex40. Second, Complier Average Causal Effect (CACE) analysis used instrumental variable estimation with a 50% compliance threshold to assess treatment effects among adherent participants41. All analyses used 5000 bootstrap resamples for 95% confidence intervals and calculated Cohen’s d as the treatment effect estimate divided by the residual standard deviation. To control the family-wise error rate, Holm-Bonferroni correction was applied across the three primary and secondary outcomes (physical activity, fruit and vegetable consumption, committed action). Analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.3) using the ivreg, parameters, and emmeans packages. No interim analyses were planned or conducted, and no stopping guidelines were pre-specified.

Following randomisation, 78 intervention participants were excluded post-randomisation due to smartphone incompatibility, and 1 usual care participant did not complete the assessment. These participants were structurally excluded before any intervention delivery and have no outcome data. The rationale for adopting a modified ITT approach rather than imputing outcomes for all 627 randomised participants is detailed. In brief, the 78 excluded intervention participants were never exposed to treatment, creating structural missingness that violates the missing-at-random assumption required for valid imputation. Little’s MCAR test confirmed that data were not missing completely at random (p < 0.001), supporting the use of MAR-based sensitivity methods. Demographic analysis confirmed that the excluded participants differed from included intervention participants primarily in education level (Fisher’s exact p = 0.005), with a higher proportion having only primary education (19.2% vs 7.3%; Supplementary Fig. 1). Comprehensive sensitivity analyses addressing all 167 missing participants (78 app problem + 1 did not complete + 88 lost to follow-up) confirmed the robustness of findings across worst-case, best-case, and multiple imputation scenarios.

Subgroup analyses

Three families of exploratory subgroup analyses examined whether intervention effects varied among participants with specific baseline risk factors: (1) Self-reported measures—insufficient physical activity (<600 MET-min/week), inadequate fruit and vegetable consumption (<5 portions/day), and low motivation (precontemplation or contemplation stage); (2) Anthropometric measures—elevated BMI and central obesity; and (3) Physiological measures—prehypertension and hypertension. Holm-Bonferroni correction was applied within each family to control family-wise error rates.

For each subgroup, odds ratio analyses additionally examined the likelihood of achieving pre-specified clinically meaningful improvements (+120 MET-min/week physical activity, +1 portion/day fruit and vegetable, stage progression to action or maintenance, −3 cm waist circumference, −5 mmHg systolic or −3 mmHg diastolic blood pressure) using age- and sex-adjusted logistic regression with 5000 bootstrap confidence intervals, regardless of whether mean differences reached statistical significance, as clinically meaningful thresholds may capture individual-level treatment responses not reflected in group means.

We additionally examined intervention effects stratified by baseline health status (clinical diagnosis vs. at-risk) to determine whether the e-MI intervention was equally effective across the spectrum of health conditions, from diagnosed chronic disease to asymptomatic at-risk status.

Exploratory longitudinal sustainability analysis (intervention group only)

The intervention group’s 9-month follow-up assessed longitudinal changes across baseline (T1), 3-month post-intervention (T2), and 9-month follow-up (T3) for primary outcomes (physical activity, fruit and vegetable consumption), committed action, and subgroups that demonstrated significant intervention effects at T2 (low baseline physical activity, low motivation, central obesity, hypertension). Linear mixed models with random intercepts for participants addressed missing data from 12 participants lost between T2 and T3. Changes are reported as estimated marginal means with 5000 bootstrap 95% confidence intervals.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the modified ITT findings, three sensitivity analyses addressed all 167 missing participants (78 excluded due to smartphone incompatibility, 1 who did not complete assessment, and 88 lost to follow-up).

Little’s MCAR test formally rejected the null hypothesis of data missing completely at random (χ2=148.3, df = 39, p < 0.001), confirming that attrition was associated with baseline characteristics and supporting the use of sensitivity methods that adjust for observed covariates under the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption.

First, an intention-to-treat analysis with multiple imputation (MI-ITT) included all 627 randomised participants. Missing 12-week outcome values were imputed using classification and regression trees (CART) via the mice package in R. The imputation model incorporated baseline and follow-up outcome variables, demographic predictors (age, sex, education), health status indicators (diabetes, hypertension), baseline clinical categories (stage of change, BMI group, blood pressure category, central obesity), treatment assignment, and treatment-by-baseline interactions. Fifty imputed datasets were generated with 30 iterations each; results were pooled using Rubin’s rules.

Second, a worst-case scenario assumed zero change from baseline for all 167 missing participants, providing a lower bound on intervention effectiveness.

Third, a best-case scenario assigned each arm’s observed mean change to its respective missing participants, providing an upper bound.

Adverse event monitoring

Adverse events were monitored non-systematically throughout the trial period. Participants were instructed to report any adverse events or concerns directly to the research team via WhatsApp.

Protocol deviations

Three deviations from the registered analysis plan (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05949320) are disclosed:

Modified ITT vs full ITT. The protocol specified full ITT for all randomised participants. The primary analysis employed a modified ITT approach (n = 460) because 78 intervention participants excluded post-randomisation due to smartphone incompatibility were never exposed to treatment, creating structural missingness that violates the missing-at-random assumption. Comprehensive sensitivity analyses including MI-ITT for all 627 randomised participants confirmed robustness.
