Multimorbidity Clusters Among People Aged 65 Years and Over in Australia: A Nationwide Cross‐Sectional Data Linkage Study
Authors: Weisi Chen, Christine Y. Lu, Sarah N. Hilmer, Alice A. Gibson, Edwin C. K. Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo identify sex‐specific multimorbidity patterns in Australia, using the Rx‐Risk index (a medication‐based measure), to: (i) estimate the prevalence of chronic treated conditions; (ii) map network‐based multimorbidity clusters; and (iii) examine how these clusters vary by age, socio‐economic status and geographic remoteness.DesignAustralian nationwide cross‐sectional study using linked Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) and Medicare Benefits Schedule (MBS) data.SettingAustralian residents aged ≥ 65 years with at least one PBS and/or MBS claim between 1 July 2022 and 30 June 2023.Main Outcome MeasuresSex‐specific network‐based multimorbidity clusters and cluster profiles by age, socio‐economic status and geographic remoteness.ResultsA total of 4,435,784 individuals (mean age, 74.8 years; 53.2% female) were included. Multimorbidity (≥ 2 conditions) was present in 76.1% of the cohort. Three consistent multimorbidity clusters were identified in both sexes: cardiovascular–metabolic, neuropsychiatric–functional decline and inflammatory–musculoskeletal–cancer. The prevalence of these clusters and their component conditions varied across sociodemographic groups, with higher prevalence observed in individuals aged ≥ 85 years and those living in socio‐economically disadvantaged areas. Minimal differences were observed between metropolitan and non‐metropolitan regions.ConclusionsMultimorbidity was highly prevalent among older Australians with at least one PBS and/or MBS claim during the study year, with multimorbidity clusters showing marked sociodemographic variation in prevalence. These findings highlight the heterogeneity in treated conditions captured in administrative claims and provide insights to inform future research and policy planning for prevention and management of multimorbidity in an ageing population.

Introduction

Multimorbidity, commonly defined as the co‐occurrence of two or more chronic health conditions within one individual, is increasingly prevalent worldwide and particularly common in older adults [1, 2]. Globally, more than half of adults aged 65 years and older live with multimorbidity, with the complexity of health profiles rising sharply from the younger old (65–74 years) to the oldest old (≥ 85 years) [3, 4]. Its presence is associated with poorer quality of life, functional decline, higher mortality and greater healthcare use [5, 6, 7]. A global meta‐analysis found that older people with multimorbidity had 2.52 higher odds of hospitalisation compared with those without [8].

Individuals with multimorbidity often face complex healthcare needs and are more likely to experience polypharmacy, increasing the risk of adverse drug events [9, 10]. Care for these patients is often fragmented, involving multiple providers and services that focus on treating individual conditions rather than person‐centred care, leading to poorer health outcomes and preventable adverse events [11]. Despite the substantial burden and the recognition of these complex needs in clinical guidelines [12, 13], the trends, patterns and determinants of multimorbidity remain suboptimally understood.

Over the past decade, studies of multimorbidity in primary care and general population samples have reported varying estimates and patterns depending on data sources, populations, definitions and analytical methods [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Studies using Rx‐Risk conditions typically apply prescription‐based indices from administrative claims, electronic health records or registry data, defining multimorbidity as the presence of two or more conditions and measuring it through condition counts or total Rx‐Risk scores in general older populations and disease‐specific cohorts [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Although Australian research explored multimorbidity patterns by age and sex in subsets of the population 20 years ago [16], evidence on contemporary national‐level patterns across socio‐economic and geographic groups remains limited.

In this study, we examined sex‐specific multimorbidity patterns among older Australian adults with at least one Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) claim and/or Medicare Benefits Schedule (MBS) claim during the study year by: (i) estimating the prevalence of these conditions; (ii) mapping network‐based multimorbidity clusters; and (iii) assessing how these cluster profiles vary by age group, socio‐economic status and geographic remoteness. To understand the complex needs of an ageing population, we analysed age groups among those aged over 65 (65–74, 75–85, ≥ 85) years.

Methods

This study is reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist (STROBE checklist, Supporting Information). The study was approved by the NSW Population and Health Services Research Ethics Committee (2024/ETH00910).

Study Design, Data Sources and Population

This nationwide cross‐sectional study used linked individual‐level PBS and MBS data from the National Health Data Hub. The PBS captures subsidised prescriptions dispensed to Australian residents [26], and the MBS records subsidised medical and diagnostic services [27].

The study population included people aged ≥ 65 years with at least one PBS and/or MBS claim between 1 July 2022 and 30 June 2023, representing the most recent complete financial year at the time of analysis. Baseline was defined as the earliest record of a PBS dispensing or MBS service date, whichever occurred first. Sociodemographic variables used to describe the study population at baseline included sex, age, area‐level socio‐economic disadvantage and remoteness of area of residence. Sex refers to the biological classification of individuals as recorded in the dataset. Area‐level socio‐economic disadvantage and remoteness of area of residence were derived from individuals' residential postcodes and classified using the 2021 Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) Socio‐Economic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA) Index of Relative Socio‐economic Disadvantage (IRSD) deciles and the ABS Remoteness Area standard, respectively.

Rx‐Risk Conditions

A set of 52 Rx‐Risk conditions were identified from the PBS dataset using the Rx‐Risk comorbidity index, which defines the conditions based on Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classifications of active pharmaceutical ingredients (Table S1) [24]. Rx‐Risk conditions represent medication‐based proxies for chronic conditions, with some categories (e.g., antiepileptics, anticoagulants, antipsychotics) reflecting long‐term therapeutic use rather than specific diagnoses. For combination products, active ingredients were separated and assigned to individual ATC codes. A condition was considered present if an individual had at least two PBS dispensings for that condition between 1 July 2022 and 30 June 2023. The ≥ 2 dispensing threshold was used to improve specificity by excluding one‐off or short‐term prescriptions. The Rx‐Risk index has been validated as a proxy for treated chronic conditions and has demonstrated predictive validity for important outcomes such as all‐cause mortality [23, 25].

Rx‐Risk conditions were examined separately for males and females, with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) included only in the analyses for males. Multimorbidity was defined as the co‐occurrence of two or more conditions within one person. Complex multimorbidity was defined as the co‐occurrence of three or more conditions from three or more body systems within one person [28].

Statistical Analyses

The prevalence of Rx‐Risk conditions was estimated per 100 population, with corresponding Wilson 95% confidence intervals. Analyses were stratified by sex and conducted at both the individual condition and body system levels to capture detailed and high‐level patterns of multimorbidity.

While descriptive approaches highlighted the frequency of individual conditions and co‐occurring pairs or triplets, they were limited in capturing the complex structural interconnections across multiple conditions simultaneously. To address this, we constructed sex‐specific multimorbidity networks based on Rx‐Risk conditions. In these networks, nodes represent conditions and edges represent the strength of association (co‐existence) between condition pairs [29]. Associations were quantified using odds ratios (ORs) from pairwise logistic regression models, adjusted for age, area‐level socio‐economic disadvantage and remoteness of area of residence, and implemented in R with the glm package. The use of ORs reduced distortion from highly prevalent conditions disproportionately influencing the network structure, while covariate adjustment addressed skewed age distributions as well as sociodemographic and geographic variation. To focus on prevalent, meaningful associations and improve OR stability, only Rx‐Risk conditions with ≥ 1% prevalence in the general population were included (28 in males and 27 in females, excluding BPH in females). Edges were retained if the OR was > 1; this meant that we included only positive associations, to focus on co‐occurring conditions [29]. Clusters within the networks were identified using Louvain modularity optimisation, implemented in R with the igraph package. Within each cluster, the three dominant Rx‐Risk conditions were defined as those with the strongest overall connectivity (measured by sum of ORs across all connected edges), capturing both the number and strength of associations [30, 31], and are shown as larger nodes. As an additional preplanned analysis, clustering was stratified by age groups (65 to < 75, 75 to < 85 and ≥ 85 years) and these data are presented in Figure S1.

To examine variation in cluster profiles by age group, socio‐economic status and geographic remoteness, we compared the proportion of people assigned to each cluster (≥ 1 cluster condition) and the prevalence of component Rx‐Risk conditions, stratified by sex. Bootstrap resampling estimated 95% confidence intervals for cluster prevalence, accounting for overlapping cluster membership, and Wilson confidence intervals were used for component conditions.

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses included: (i) Yule's Q as the similarity measure; (ii) increasingly stringent OR thresholding (1.05–1.20); and (iii) random sampling of independent, sex‐specific samples with re‐estimation of the network. Network‐level metrics are detailed in Table S2. To assess whether observed sociodemographic contrasts were driven by individuals with a single condition in that cluster, we restricted cluster membership to individuals with ≥ 2 conditions per cluster.

Data cleaning was conducted in SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute), while statistical analyses and data visualisation were conducted in R (version 4.4.1, R Development Core Team).

Results

Among the 4,435,784 adults aged ≥ 65 years (mean age, 74.8 years) included in the study; 2,076,550 were male and 2,359,234 were female (53.2% female). The mean (standard deviation [SD]) age at baseline was 74.4 (7.1) years for males and 75.2 (7.8) years for females. Most participants resided in major cities (65.9% of males, 68.1% of females), and about one in five lived in the most disadvantaged areas (18.3% of males, 17.6% of females) (Table 1).

Sex Differences in Rx‐Risk Conditions

Hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and gastro‐oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) were the most prevalent Rx‐Risk conditions in both sexes, affecting 65.9%, 54.9% and 31.0% of males, and 62.4%, 47.3% and 35.4% of females, respectively (Figure 1). At the body system level, cardiovascular conditions were slightly more prevalent in males than females (77.6% vs. 74.3%), whereas digestive (32.9% vs. 37.7%), musculoskeletal (24.7% vs. 30.6%), neurological (20.0% vs. 24.1%), mental health (20.2% vs. 30.8%), metabolic/endocrine (23.5% vs. 24.1%) and respiratory (14.7% vs. 17.2%) conditions were less common in males (Figure 1).

Overall, multimorbidity burden was substantial and similar across sexes, with a mean (SD) of 3.5 (2.5) Rx‐Risk conditions in males and 3.5 (2.5) in females. Over three‐quarters of older adults had at least two Rx‐Risk conditions (76.1% in both sexes), and nearly one‐third had five or more Rx‐Risk conditions (31.3% in males vs. 31.0% in females) (Table S3).

Sex Differences in Combinations of Rx‐Risk Conditions

The most common Rx‐Risk condition pair was hypertension–hyperlipidaemia (44.7% of males, 36.9% of females), which frequently co‐occurred with other conditions (Tables S4 and S5). At the body system level, the most prevalent combinations were: cardiovascular and digestive (pairs) in both sexes; cardiovascular, digestive, musculoskeletal diseases (triplets) in males; and cardiovascular, digestive, mental health (triplets) in females (Figure S2, Table S6). Complex multimorbidity (co‐occurrence of three or more conditions across three or more body systems) was present in 44.5% of individuals (42.0% in males, 46.8% in females) (Table S3).

Sex Differences in Network Clusters of Rx‐Risk Conditions

Sex‐specific network analysis identified three major clusters that were consistently observed in both males and females: cardiovascular–metabolic, neuropsychiatric–functional decline and inflammatory–musculoskeletal–cancer, each distinguished by node colours in Figure 2. The cardiovascular–metabolic cluster comprised hypertension, congestive heart failure, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes, arrhythmia, anticoagulants, angina, antiplatelets, glaucoma, age‐related macular degeneration and gout. The neuropsychiatric–functional decline cluster comprised antipsychotics, depression, constipation, antiepileptic use, BPH (excluded in females), urinary incontinence, pain, dementia, anxiety/sedatives and Parkinson's disease. The inflammatory–musculoskeletal–cancer cluster comprised cancer, inflammatory autoimmune disease, inflammation, chronic airway disease and osteoporosis.

Sex Differences in Network Clusters of Rx‐Risk Conditions by Age Group, Socio‐Economic Status and Geographic Remoteness

All three clusters (cardiovascular–metabolic, neuropsychiatric–functional decline and inflammatory–musculoskeletal–cancer) varied significantly by age and socio‐economic status in both sexes, while differences between metropolitan and non‐metropolitan areas were minimal.

The proportion of people with at least one condition in each of the three clusters increased substantially in the oldest age group (Figure 3a,b). Within each cluster, the largest absolute differences between age groups 65 to < 75 years and ≥ 85 years for males were observed for anticoagulants (10.8% [95% CI, 10.8%–10.9%] vs. 29.7% [95% CI, 29.6%–29.9%]) in the cardiovascular–metabolic cluster, pain (13.2% [95% CI, 13.1%–13.3%] vs. 27.4% [95% CI, 27.2%–27.6%]) in the neuropsychiatric–functional decline cluster and GORD (25.9% [95% CI, 25.8%–26.0%] vs. 42.1% [95% CI, 41.9%–42.4%]) in the inflammatory–musculoskeletal–cancer cluster (Figure 3a,b). For females, the corresponding differences observed in each cluster were hypertension (52.9% [95% CI, 52.8%–52.9%] vs. 77.5% [95% CI, 77.4%–77.7%]), pain (15.1% [95% CI, 15.0%–15.1%] vs. 36.6% [95% CI, 36.5%–36.8%]) and osteoporosis (11.2% [95% CI, 11.2%–11.3%] vs. 26.8% [95% CI, 26.6%–26.9%]), respectively (Figure 3a,b).

The proportion of people with at least one condition in each of the three clusters was higher in the most disadvantaged areas compared with the least disadvantaged areas (Figure 3c,d). Within each cluster, the largest absolute differences for males were observed for diabetes (25.2% [95% CI, 25.0%–25.3%] vs. 17.2% [95% CI, 17.1%–17.3%]) in the cardiovascular–metabolic cluster, pain (20.9% [95% CI, 20.7%–21.0%] vs. 11.9% [95% CI, 11.8%–12.0%]) in the neuropsychiatric–functional decline cluster and GORD (33.9% [95% CI, 33.8%–34.1%] vs. 27.5% [95% CI, 27.4%–27.6%]) in the inflammatory–musculoskeletal–cancer cluster (Figure 3c,d). For females, the corresponding differences observed in each cluster were hypertension (67.9% [95% CI, 67.8%–68.0%] vs. 56.7% [95% CI, 56.6%–56.9%]), pain (25.5% [95% CI, 25.4%–25.7%] vs. 16.1% [95% CI, 16.0%–16.2%]) and GORD (39.3% [95% CI, 39.2%–39.5%] vs. 30.9% [95% CI, 30.8%–31.0%]), respectively (Figure 3c,d).

The proportions of people with at least one condition in each of the three clusters and the prevalence of individual Rx‐Risk conditions were similarly distributed across metropolitan and non‐metropolitan areas (Figure 3e,f). However, when non‐metropolitan areas were further stratified into regional and remote/very remote areas, the prevalence of all three clusters was lowest in remote/very remote areas (Tables S7–S10).

Sensitivity analyses demonstrated the robustness of the network analysis (Tables S2 and S11; Figure S3). Restricting membership to those with ≥ 2 conditions per cluster reduced absolute cluster prevalence, but the overall sociodemographic gradients remained directionally consistent (Table S12).

Discussion

In this large, population‐based study of more than 4.4 million older Australians with at least one PBS and/or MBS claim during the study year, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and GORD were the most prevalent Rx‐Risk conditions in both sexes. Our findings highlight a substantial burden of multimorbidity, with more than three‐quarters of older adults having at least two Rx‐Risk conditions, nearly half having three or more, and nearly one‐third having five or more. Further, we identified three consistent multimorbidity clusters: cardiovascular–metabolic, neuropsychiatric–functional decline and inflammatory–musculoskeletal–cancer. Multimorbidity cluster prevalence was higher among individuals in the most socio‐economically disadvantaged areas and in the older age groups, highlighting inequities in the population distribution of treated conditions captured in administrative claims. To our knowledge, this is the first study in the Australian context to examine multimorbidity cluster prevalence by disaggregated age groups separating the oldest old, and by both socio‐economic status and geographic remoteness.

Direct comparison with other studies on multimorbidity clusters is challenging due to the heterogeneity in the populations studied, the types and number of conditions included, as well as differences in data sources and analytical methods [32]. Nevertheless, our findings are broadly consistent with those of a systematic review of 14 studies on multimorbidity patterns, supporting the existence of three broad categories of cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, mental health problems and musculoskeletal disorders [17].

The cardiovascular–metabolic cluster is most prevalent and widely reported [14, 17, 32, 33, 34], reflecting shared underlying risk factors such as insulin resistance, obesity and unhealthy lifestyles [35]. This underscores the ongoing need for management of cardiometabolic risk factors and preventative interventions in older adults. The neuropsychiatric–functional decline cluster comprises mental health, neurological disorders and associated functional impairments, highlighting the importance of integrated care that addresses cognition, mental wellbeing and functional support. The co‐occurrence of these conditions likely reflects shared biological mechanisms, including vascular pathology, chronic inflammation and behavioural factors [36]. Musculoskeletal clusters have been reported in other studies [14, 37]. Interestingly, we found cancer clustered with inflammatory disorders and musculoskeletal conditions, possibly reflecting shared inflammatory pathways [38].

We observed minimal differences in cluster composition across sexes, although the prevalence of individual conditions varied. Cardiovascular conditions were slightly more common in males, consistent with international evidence showing higher prevalence of coronary heart disease, hypertension and related risk factors among men [39, 40]. In contrast, females had higher prevalence of digestive, musculoskeletal, neurological, mental health, metabolic/endocrine and respiratory conditions, aligning with other existing literature [3, 41]. The differences likely reflect biological, hormonal and social factors, highlighting the importance of a sex‐specific approach to the prevention and management of multimorbidity in older adults.

Cluster prevalence increased with age, reflecting the accumulation of multimorbidity over the life course and the importance of stratifying people aged ≥ 65 years by age subgroups. Conversely, we observed that some conditions—including diabetes, inflammation, cancer and inflammatory autoimmune disease—were more common in the 65‐ to < 75‐year age group. This finding may reflect survivor bias and age‐related changes in prescribing patterns. Although the findings are limited by differences in sample sizes across age groups, we observed age‐related variations in cluster composition in both sexes. While the cardiovascular–metabolic cluster persisted with age, adults aged ≥ 85 years tended to exhibit more integrated, multisystem cluster patterns, such as the co‐occurrence of eye, urological, gastrointestinal, cancer and inflammatory conditions, compared with the more system‐specific clusters observed in those aged 65 to < 75 years (Figure S2). All three clusters were more common in individuals residing in the most disadvantaged areas, highlighting existing inequalities in health outcomes. This finding aligns with a recent national census analysis, which reported that the prevalence of nine of 10 chronic diseases increased with greater socio‐economic disadvantage in Australia [42]. Pain showed some of the greatest disparities across the socio‐economic groups for both sexes, reflecting a substantial burden and highlighting the need for targeted management strategies in disadvantaged populations. We observed minimal differences in metropolitan and non‐metropolitan areas, which may reflect equitable access to medications provided through the PBS. However, when further stratifying non‐metropolitan areas into regional and remote/very remote areas, we observed that clusters were least prevalent in remote/very remote areas, likely due to reduced access to medications, healthcare services and specialist treatment in remote settings. These findings highlight geographic and socio‐economic variation in multimorbidity patterns, informing future research and policy planning for prevention and management of multimorbidity in an ageing population.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, multimorbidity was defined based on medication use because diagnosis information is not routinely available from primary care data in Australia. Although we used the most recently published Rx‐Risk index, some PBS‐subsidised medicines for certain chronic conditions available during the study period may not have been captured. This approach may underestimate conditions managed outside of PBS pharmacotherapy (e.g., over‐the‐counter, private prescription or specialist‐supplied medications, and non‐pharmacological care) and overestimate or misclassify conditions treated with drugs that have multiple indications. If treatment patterns vary by sociodemographic factors, geographic locations or care model, differential misclassification may occur. Nevertheless, the medication‐based definition captures treated conditions, provides clinically relevant information and provides a complementary perspective to diagnosis‐based approaches [43, 44, 45] with studies demonstrating reasonable agreement [24]. Second, very healthy older adults who did not have any PBS or MBS claims during the study period were not captured but likely represent a small fraction of the population. Our cohort approximates the national ≥ 65‐year population in 2022 [46]; however, healthcare users may differ systematically from non‐users. Third, the similarity metrics (pairwise logistic regression ORs) were adjusted for age, socio‐economic status and remoteness, but edge weights may remain confounded by unmeasured factors. Fourth, rarer conditions (< 1% prevalence) were not modelled, so clusters primarily represent common multimorbidity patterns rather than an exhaustive taxonomy of all clinically relevant conditions. Fifth, clusters are non‐mutually exclusive, so membership proportions overlap and are not directly comparable to latent class approaches. Lastly, the cross‐sectional study design limits causal inference and temporal assessment of multimorbidity. Longitudinal studies are needed to investigate the progression, clustering dynamics and health outcomes associated with multimorbidity in this population.

Conclusion

In this large, nationwide study of older adults in Australia, we identified three consistent multimorbidity clusters in both sexes: cardiovascular–metabolic, neuropsychiatric–functional decline and inflammatory–musculoskeletal–cancer. Cluster prevalence varied by age group, socio‐economic status and geographic remoteness. These findings highlight the heterogeneity in treated condition patterns captured in administrative claims and provide insights to inform future research and policy planning for the prevention and management of multimorbidity in an ageing population.
