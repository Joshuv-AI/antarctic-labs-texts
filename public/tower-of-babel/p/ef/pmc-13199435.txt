MnMR-GenA: a morphological recombination genetic algorithm for jailbreak attacks in low-resource language
Authors: Yan Li, Gang Wang, Hao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the widespread application of large language models (LLMs) across diverse scenarios, jailbreak attacks, in which carefully crafted prompts bypass built-in safety mechanisms, have become a significant security concern, particularly in low-resource language settings. Low-resource languages refer to those with limited training corpora and scarce annotated data, and due to their relatively low proportion in mainstream pretraining datasets, they tend to exhibit higher jailbreak success rates. Existing methods largely rely on directly translating prompts from high-resource languages into target low-resource languages, which leads to limited scalability and poor prompt concealment. In addition, evolutionary attack methods often suffer from single-level search and insufficient diversity. To address these issues, this paper proposes MNMR-GenA, a genetic algorithm-based jailbreak framework for low-resource languages. Building upon traditional genetic algorithms, the proposed method designs mutation strategies by leveraging the structural characteristics of agglutinative languages, and employs multi-granularity collaborative optimization at the word, sentence, and paragraph levels to alleviate the local optimum problem. Meanwhile, role-playing and scenario-nesting mechanisms are introduced to enhance prompt concealment. Compared with direct translation methods, the proposed approach improves the diversity and concealment of jailbreak prompts. Experimental results on multiple mainstream LLMs show that the proposed method achieves an average jailbreak success rate of approximately 80% while maintaining model query costs, and maintains high attack success rates under two mainstream defense mechanisms, PPL and SmoothLLM, indicating stable attack performance under defensive interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47434-5.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved unprecedented dialogue understanding and generation capabilities1,2 by simulating the complexity of human language and possessing powerful reasoning capabilities in text generation and language understanding3–5. With the widespread deployment and application of these models (e.g. LLM-based multi-intelligence systems and human-computer interaction systems), the security problems brought by them are becoming more and more serious, especially jailbreak attacks. Jailbreak attack6–8 refer to the situation where attackers, by inputting specific instructions or prompts, leverage the language understanding and generation capabilities of large models to bypass the built-in security protection measures and privacy protection mechanisms of the models, and induce the models to produce harmful outputs that violate security policies.

The success of jailbreak attacks can be attributed to two primary factors: objective competition and mismatched generalization. Objective competition arises from conflicts among a model’s language modeling9, instruction following10, and safety alignment objectives11, whereby attackers can craft prompts that steer the model away from its safety goals, eliciting harmful outputs. Mismatched generalization occurs when the datasets used for safety training are substantially smaller than the model’s pretraining corpus, leaving certain domains insufficiently aligned with safety constraints. Low-resource languages are characterized by severely limited training corpora and annotation resources, which results in their persistent underrepresentation in pretraining data. Consequently, models exhibit markedly weaker safety alignment in these languages compared to high-resource languages. Prior studies have demonstrated that simply translating jailbreak prompts from high-resource languages into low-resource languages can substantially increase attack success rates without introducing additional attack strategies, revealing cross-lingual vulnerabilities in LLMs arising from mismatched generalization12,13.

Existing jailbreak attacks are typically categorized into three types: manually designed attacks, white-box attacks, and black-box attacks. Manually designed attacks rely on hand-crafted prompt templates, such as DAN14 , but are labor-intensive and difficult to generalize across different models and scenarios15,16. White-box attacks exploit gradient information during training to optimize prefixes or suffixes that induce malicious responses, as exemplified by GCG17; however, they often generate semantically incoherent sequences that can be easily detected by perplexity-based detectors. Black-box attacks, which operate without access to internal model parameters, employ heuristic or evolutionary search to iteratively generate effective prompts, as in GPTFuzzer18. While these methods enable automated search, they are typically limited to single-level operations, constrained search spaces, and high computational costs. Moreover, random crossover and mutation operations often disrupt semantic structures, reducing prompt readability. Many of these approaches have been evaluated only on relatively small or weaker models, resulting in limited cross-model transferability.

Although translation-based attack methods have been shown to improve jailbreak success rates, they remain confined to the original prompt structure and fail to fully exploit the morphological characteristics of low-resource languages. As a result, the induced search space is inherently limited, and the resulting attacks lack sufficient stealthiness. To bridge this gap, this work presents the first systematic investigation of the vulnerabilities of agglutinative languages–a representative class of low-resource languages–in the context of jailbreak attacks, and proposes MNMR-GenA, a novel framework capable of automatically generating highly stealthy jailbreak prompts while achieving consistently high attack success rates. Unlike conventional approaches that rely solely on translation, MNMR-GenA incorporates evolutionary optimization on top of existing prompts, employing genetic algorithms to perform multi-granularity crossover and mutation at the word, sentence, and paragraph levels. This design substantially enlarges the combinatorial search space and effectively mitigates premature convergence to local optima. Furthermore, we integrate role-based conditioning and scenario-driven prompt construction19,20, enabling the generated prompts to preserve natural linguistic contexts and further enhancing their stealthiness.

In summary, our main contributions are threefold. (1) To address the limited search space of existing approaches caused by their insufficient exploitation of the morphological structure of low-resource languages, we propose MNMR-GenA, a genetic-algorithm-based jailbreak framework for large language models in low-resource settings. By incorporating morpheme recombination rules tailored to agglutinative languages and leveraging evolutionary optimization, MNMR-GenA enables the automatic generation and iterative refinement of jailbreak prompts, thereby achieving efficient and effective attacks with minimal manual intervention. (2) To mitigate the tendency of genetic algorithms to converge to local optima, we design fine-grained crossover and mutation strategies operating at multiple linguistic levels. In addition, we introduce a multi-objective fitness evaluation scheme that jointly considers refusal-trigger signals and prompt–response coherence, allowing for more accurate identification of successful jailbreak prompts. (3) To enhance the stealthiness of generated jailbreak prompts, we further develop a role-playing and scenario-nesting module that embeds attacks within naturalistic contexts, increasing prompt diversity and yielding adversarial samples with stronger camouflage and deceptive capacity.

Related works

LLM jailbreak attack

A large language model is a model based on Transformer21 architecture and using deep learning technology to perform self-supervised learning on large-scale unlabeled text datasets, usually containing billions or even trillions of parameters, such as GPT (GPT-39, GPT-411), T522, LLaMA23, DeepSeek R12, Qwen24, etc. LLMs simulate the complexity of human language, possess powerful reasoning ability in text generation and language understanding, realize unprecedented dialogue understanding and generation ability, and have been widely used in many fields such as academia, medicine, law, finance, education, etc. Most LLMs are securely aligned with fine-tuning techniques to prevent abuse25 in malicious activity and ensure LLM responses are consistent with safe human value26,27. However, jailbreak attacks bypass internal security mechanisms designed to trigger harmful output28. Compared with traditional network security attacks, jailbreak attacks are more subtle and difficult to prevent. This attack method not only exposes the potential security vulnerability of large models, but also directly threatens user privacy, data security and social trust29.

The core mechanism of jailbreak attacks lies in exploiting a model’s high sensitivity to contextual framing and role-based instructions. By embedding otherwise explicitly prohibited requests into carefully structured jailbreak templates, attackers disguise malicious intent as seemingly legitimate task descriptions. Once the model is misled into accepting this fabricated context, it may bypass its original safety constraints and respond to the attacker’s intent. For instance, an explicit illegal query would typically trigger a refusal; however, when embedded within a jailbreak template, the model often interprets it as a contextual requirement and consequently produces content that would otherwise be disallowed. Early studies demonstrated this vulnerability through manually crafted prompts such as “Do anything now”14 or “Ignore previous prompts”30, which successfully induced harmful outputs. Nevertheless, such manually designed templates are time-consuming to construct and exhibit poor adaptability, limiting their effectiveness across different models and linguistic settings.

To automate the attack process, subsequent studies introduced gradient-based optimization strategies that manipulate model inputs by appending trainable prefixes or suffixes to the original prompt and iteratively optimizing them toward the attack objective. GCG17 enhances jailbreak success by fixing the initial response, exhaustively enumerating candidate tokens at each optimization step, evaluating their corresponding loss values, selecting the optimal replacement, and updating the adversarial suffix accordingly. However, the suffixes produced by GCG typically consist of semantically meaningless token sequences, leading to poor readability and making them highly vulnerable to perplexity-based detection methods. Beyond gradient-based approaches, several studies attempt to bypass model safety mechanisms by encrypting malicious content or injecting specially crafted code31, encoding inputs using Base64 schemes that map each byte to multiple textual characters7, or reformulating malicious queries as structured components resembling database queries, such as SQL-like expressions32. These methods heavily depend on specific encoding schemes or attack assumptions, which limits their portability and generalization, and makes them ineffective when confronted with complex linguistic structures.

In recent years, evolutionary algorithms have been increasingly adopted in jailbreaking attacks to improve prompt readability and generation efficiency. GPTFuzzer18 initializes the search with manually crafted jailbreaking prompts as seeds, applies mutation to generate new candidates, and iteratively expands the seed pool by retaining successful mutations. AutoDAN33 designs distinct crossover strategies at both sentence and paragraph levels and leverages LLM-driven mutation to enhance diversity, enabling iterative optimization of jailbreak prompts. LLM-Virus34 employs LLMs to perform crossover and mutation operations and selects the most effective templates through fitness-based evaluation. BlackDAN35 formulates jailbreaking as a multi-objective optimization problem and applies evolutionary algorithms to jointly optimize attack success rate (ASR), stealthiness, and semantic relevance. SMJ36 adopts a genetic algorithm to generate jailbreak prompts that remain semantically close to the original query, thereby bypassing LLM safety mechanisms. Collectively, these approaches demonstrate the effectiveness of evolutionary algorithms for automating jailbreak prompt generation. However, existing evolutionary methods are predominantly evaluated on high-resource languages and operate over relatively shallow search hierarchies. Moreover, crossover and mutation operations often disrupt semantic coherence, resulting in limited portability across models and languages.

Beyond evolutionary approaches, a small number of studies have explored translation-based attacks to expose cross-lingual vulnerabilities in LLMs arising from mismatched generalization. Low-resource languages are typically defined as languages with almost no unlabeled data, with some unlabeled but minimal labeled data, or with only a small amount of labeled data. Yong et al.12 demonstrate that translating harmful English inputs into low-resource languages can successfully jailbreak LLMs. Deng et al.13 further observe that as language resource availability decreases–from high-resource to low-resource languages–the likelihood of unsafe model outputs increases substantially, with low-resource languages exhibiting approximately three times the probability of generating harmful content. Nevertheless, existing approaches largely rely on direct translation and fail to exploit the morphological characteristics of low-resource languages, resulting in a constrained prompt search space and limited attack stealthiness.

Figure 1 presents a taxonomy of jailbreaking methods and their corresponding attack pathways, including direct jailbreaking, conventional jailbreaking, translation-based jailbreaking, and evolutionary jailbreaking. These approaches exhibit substantial differences in both attack mechanisms and prompt generation strategies. Motivated by these distinctions, this study focuses on agglutinative languages in low-resource settings. Such languages are characterized by high morphological complexity, in which a single word can encode a complete sentence meaning through the successive attachment of multiple morphemes. The resulting morpheme diversity and the exponential expansion of the combinatorial space create a substantially larger operational space for prompt generation and optimization via evolutionary algorithms, thereby enabling a systematic investigation of the vulnerabilities of low-resource languages in jailbreaking attacks.

Evolutionary algorithm-based attacks

Evolutionary algorithm is an optimization algorithm based on biological evolution principle, which is used to find the optimal solution of complex problems. as shown in Fig. 2. Evolutionary computation includes four typical methods: genetic algorithm37, genetic programming38, evolutionary strategy39 and evolutionary programming40. Evolutionary algorithms start from any initial population and evolve the population to better and better regions of the search space through random selection, crossover and mutation processes. Evolutionary algorithms are widely used in multi-objective optimization tasks41 and dynamic environments42. The researchers used genetic algorithms to generate adversarial cues, using mutation and selection processes to dynamically explore and identify effective cues and enhance their ability to bypass LLM safety. Some studies have used LLM to enhance crossover and mutation diversity43. LLM can also improve its task performance44 through multi-objective optimization of evolutionary algorithms, such as hint engineering45.

Genetic algorithm diagram.

In this study, we choose the genetic algorithm as the core optimization strategy, mainly for the following reasons: (1) The evolutionary mechanism of the genetic algorithm can explore high-dimensional, nonlinear discrete structures in the search space. Performing gradient operations on discrete data will lead to the generated attack samples lacking semantic coherence and readability. (2) Genetic algorithms can explore a broader combination space by iteratively optimizing in a discrete search space through selection, crossover, and mutation. (3) The multi-objective optimization of genetic algorithms can better identify the prompts of successful jailbreaking. (4) The existing manual jailbreak prompts can serve as prototypes for the initial population of genetic algorithms, enabling the genetic algorithm to effectively find suitable jailbreak prompts in the discrete combination space within a limited number of iterations, thereby enhancing optimization efficiency and success rate. To break through the limitations of existing translation-based jailbreak methods in terms of prompt scalability and diversity, we designed a morpheme recombination scheme for agglutinative languages. Through genetic algorithms, crossover and mutation operations are carried out at the word level, sentence level, and paragraph level, enabling the evolutionary process to search in a finer structural space. Meanwhile, we adopted multi-objective optimization and a multi-objective optimization method for fitness evaluation, comprehensively considering the rejection trigger factors and the consistency of prompt-response, so as to more accurately identify jailbreak prompts with actual attack effects.

Role-playing and scene nesting

Attackers craft harmless characters and deceptive scenarios to manipulate the target LLM to perform actions that would normally be avoided under normal security measures. PMA46 creates a guided target model for a specific misused instruction, uses role-tuning cues that match that instruction, and automatically performs jailbreaks using large language model assistants. DeepInception47 leverages the anthropomorphic power of large language models to create multi-layered nested scenarios to hide hostile intent, hypnotize LLM behavior, and use adaptive strategies to bypass security barriers to generate harmful responses. Our approach introduces character and scene modules designed to increase the invisibility of jailbreak cues.

This study incorporates role-playing and scene-nesting modules to enhance the stealthiness of jailbreak prompts. Role-playing establishes a plausible contextual framework, enabling the model to follow implicit attack instructions while remaining consistent with the assigned character behavior. Scene nesting, in turn, diffuses and attenuates the explicit attack intent by embedding the core instructions within a hierarchically structured semantic context, thereby reducing the likelihood of triggering refusal mechanisms. When integrated with evolutionary algorithms, this combination enables automated multi-level search and structured scene nesting, resulting in the generation of jailbreak prompts that are both highly stealthy and effective.

Proposed methodology

An overview is shown in Fig. 3. To systematically explore the capability of large language models to withstand jailbreaking attacks in agglutinative languages, a class of low-resource languages, we design a genetic-algorithm-based jailbreak prompt optimization method. By integrating the morphological complexity of low-resource languages with evolutionary search mechanisms, this method enables the optimization and automated generation of jailbreak prompts. Specifically, during the crossover and mutation stages of the genetic algorithm, we design a multi-level optimization strategy guided by the morphological characteristics of agglutinative languages: at the word level, morpheme recombination is employed to reconstruct the internal morphological structure of agglutinative words; at the sentence level, multi-point crossover is applied to reorder syntactic and semantic units; and at the paragraph level, role-playing and scene-nesting module exchange operations are introduced to adjust the overall contextual structure. This multi-level optimization strategy operates across different granularities, effectively expanding the search space of jailbreak prompts and thereby increasing the probability that the genetic algorithm converges to an optimal solution. On this basis, the multi-level optimization strategy works in concert with the selection and fitness evaluation processes of the genetic algorithm, driving the population to evolve progressively across iterations and enabling the automated generation of jailbreak prompts with high stealthiness and high success rates.

Population initialization

We use a genetic algorithm to optimize prompts. Considering that population initialization is necessary, and that it can significantly affect the convergence speed of the genetic algorithm, selecting a high-quality and diverse initial population can prevent the algorithm from prematurely converging to suboptimal solutions.

In existing translation-based jailbreak attack methods, existing jailbreak prompts are typically translated into the target language using publicly available translation tools (e.g., Google Translate Basic API). These prompts have been validated as effective in specific jailbreak attack scenarios and can serve as initial candidates. Following this idea, we translate them into low-resource agglutinative languages, including Mongolian (MN), Turkish (TR), and Guarani (GN), to construct the initial population. On this basis, in order to enhance the expressive diversity of the initial population while preserving the original attack semantics, this paper introduces a back-translation–retranslation strategy. The target language prompts are first translated back into the original language and then translated again into the target language, thereby generating diverse expressions while maintaining semantic consistency.

This strategy not only effectively preserves the attack logic and semantic intent of the original jailbreak prompts, but also significantly improves the diversity of the initial population, expands the search space, and provides richer and higher-quality candidate solutions for subsequent evolutionary optimization with the genetic algorithm, thereby reducing the risk of premature convergence to suboptimal solutions.

Fitness evaluation

Within the genetic algorithm framework, our goal is to evolve prompts that can consistently bypass the safety mechanisms of large language models. A successful jailbreak prompt should avoid triggering explicit refusal or safety warnings while still guiding the model to produce outputs that closely align with the intended malicious behavior. To quantitatively and optimizably model this dual objective, we introduce two evaluation metrics–Intention Consistency Score (ICS)48 and Rejection Loss–and integrate them through a weighted combination to form a unified fitness function. This weighted formulation provides a clear and optimizable fitness signal for the evolutionary search process, while also facilitating an analysis of the inherent trade-off between refusal avoidance and intent consistency in jailbreak prompt generation.

The Intention Consistency Score (ICS) measures the degree to which the model’s generated responses align with the malicious query intent. For a malicious set of queries , the attacker enters LLM after carefully constructing and gets a set of responses . The objective of the jailbreak attack is to ensure that the model’s responses remain consistent with the malicious queries while conflicting with human-aligned values. Large language models do not always refuse to answer malicious queries directly; instead, they may produce off-topic responses or append mitigating suggestions. The ICS evaluates the consistency between a malicious query Q and the model response R to determine whether the LLM has been successfully jailbreaked. ICS scores are divided into four levels, with Level 2 originally defined as indicating a successful jailbreak. Crossover

The cue of a large model is essentially a piece of text data, specifically, a paragraph of text is composed of one or more sentences grouped around a common theme through logical relationships, and sentences are composed of words or phrases according to grammatical rules. Therefore, in order to make better use of the huge search space of text data, we propose different fine-grained crossover strategies for word level, sentence level and paragraph level, that is, space exploration at word level first, then integration to sentence level, and finally exploration of paragraph level space.

where In addition, how to set the appropriate mutation rate became a key challenge. If the mutation rate is too low, the population will quickly converge, lack of new exploration, and thus fall into local optimization; if the mutation rate is too high, it will lead to excessive destruction of the population structure, close to random search, but increase the computational cost. For this reason, we introduce reinforcement learning mechanism into genetic algorithm, which regards the dynamic adjustment of mutation rate as a decision-making process and adaptively selects the appropriate mutation rate.

In reinforcement learning, Markov decision process has elements including state, action, strategy and reward. In MDP simulations, agents perceive the current state of the system and act on the environment according to policies, thereby changing the state of the environment and receiving rewards. In the process of genetic algorithm, fixed mutation rate cannot adapt to different evolutionary stages. In the early stage of evolution, larger mutation rate is needed to expand the search space, and in the later stage, small mutation rate is used to protect excellent individuals. Therefore, we use reinforcement learning to construct Markov decision process for dynamically adjusting mutation rate, aiming at finding more effective mutation rate in finite time cost.

End condition

The success of a jailbreak is determined by a termination criterion that combines rejection signal detection, intention consistency evaluation, and a maximum iteration limit. The algorithm terminates when any of the following conditions is met: (1) the current iteration reaches the preset maximum, which is determined through hyperparameter experiments to ensure sufficient exploration of the search space under limited computational resources while avoiding unnecessary over-iteration; (2) no keywords from the predefined set L are detected in the first K words of the LLM response and intent consistency reaches Level 3 or higher, MnMR-GenA will terminate and return the current best jailbreak hint, which has the highest fitness score.

To reduce possible evaluation bias, we also supplement this with keyword-based validation, where hints earn extra points for avoiding explicit rejection. This principled evaluation design enables accurate fitness evaluation while maintaining optimization efficiency, accurately identifying successful strategies within our expanded space.

Experiments results

Experiment settings

Data

We use two datasets to evaluate the large model’s jailbreak protection: AdvBench49 and HarmBench50 as our original hints.They contain 520 and 400 examples of harmful behavior, respectively, generic large-model security test queries for high-risk behaviors such as illegal activity, hate/violence, fraud, and privacy invasion. The original prompts were translated into agglutinative languages, including Mongolian (MN), Turkish (TR), and Guarani (GN), to investigate attack effectiveness in low-resource language settings.

Model

We employed two open-source models, DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen-2.5-7B, and one closed-source model, GPT-3.5 turbo. Open-source models facilitate hyperparameter tuning and repeatable experiments, while the closed-source model represents real-world systems with higher security. Comparing across these model types allows us to evaluate the stability of our method under different security levels.

Baseline

We selected PAIR19, GPTFuzzer18 and AutoDNA-HGA33 as our baselines and evaluated them on AdvBench and HarmBench. These methods respectively represent iterative and traditional genetic-algorithm-based prompt optimization strategies. Comparison with these baselines demonstrates the advantages of our approach in search space exploration, efficiency, and jailbreak success rate. Due to the randomness of evolution, our experiment recorded average performance over three runs.

Indicators

The primary evaluation metric is the jailbreak success rate (ASR). Additionally, we employ text perplexity (PPL) measured by GPT-4o as an auxiliary metric to indirectly assess the naturalness and semantic coherence of generated prompts. We also use average query count (Avg.Q) to evaluate attack efficiency. To determine optimal population size and maximum iteration count, we tuned hyperparameters on both the open-source model DeepSeek-R1 and the closed-source GPT-3.5 turbo, ensuring that the selected settings perform effectively across both model types.

Implementation details

We conducted the experiment using an NVIDIA RTX 4090GPU with 24GB of video memory. Closed-source models accessed through apis do not need to be loaded locally, significantly reducing the GPU memory requirements. For MNMR-GenA, we conducted extensive experiments for adjustment and finally set the population size N to 15, the maximum iterative step size to 8, the crossover rate to 0.5, and the mutation rate to [0.2,0.4]. For PAIR, we use the default configuration parameters N=20 and K=3. For GPTFuzzer, we set the query limit to 72. For Auto-HGA, we set N=15, the maximum iterative step size is 5, the crossover rate is 0.5, and the mutation rate is 0.7.

Hyperparameters study

The performance of genetic algorithms is highly sensitive to the configuration of key hyperparameters. In this study, we systematically tuned the population size, maximum number of iterations, crossover rate, mutation rate, and fitness weights to analyze their effects on jailbreak attack success rate (ASR) and query overhead (Avg.Q).

First, we examine the impact of population size and maximum iterations. As shown in Fig. 9, when the population size is set to 15 and the maximum number of iterations to 8, the average ASR reaches a peak of 81%. This configuration theoretically ensures sufficient coverage of the search space while avoiding the computational redundancy associated with overly large populations. A smaller population limits the search capability and may overlook potentially high-fitness prompts, whereas an excessively large population, although increasing diversity, requires evaluating a substantially larger number of individuals per iteration, causing Avg.Q to rise sharply and thereby increasing computational cost (Fig. 10). Experimental results indicate that a population size of 15 achieves a favorable balance between maintaining high ASR and controlling computational overhead.

Ablation study

To further verify the effectiveness of multi-level (word level, sentence level, paragraph level) optimization prompts, module exchange to increase concealment, and reinforcement learning to adjust mutation rates, we conducted ablation experiments.

The results are shown in Fig. 14. The translated prompt is more aggressive than the original prompt, verifying that translation-based attacks can effectively increase the success rate of jailbreaking. Multi-level optimization of the text by word, sentence and paragraph has expanded the search space, reduced the computing cost, and improved the attack performance and computing speed. Adding R or S modules alone has little impact on ASR and the average Q value. However, when both R and S modules are added simultaneously, the success rate of jailbreak attacks significantly increases and the average query time decreases, indicating that the combination of modules has a synergistic effect on the concealment of hints and the effectiveness of attacks. In addition, reinforcement learning is utilized to adjust the mutation rate, enabling the algorithm to maintain a high attack success rate while enhancing search efficiency.

Defense performance study

We evaluated the performance of MNMR-GenA under two defense mechanisms: perplexity-based (Perplexity, PPL) defense and automatic defense (SmoothLLM). Perplexity was measured using GPT-4o, which, as a thoroughly trained and general-purpose language model, possesses strong language understanding capabilities. SmoothLLM enhances model robustness by introducing local perturbations through random noise added to the input.

As shown in Fig. 15, for DeepSeek-R1, the jailbreak success rate of GPTFuzzer under PPL defense is 0.24, that of AutoDNA-HGA is 0.57, and that of MNML-GA is 0.65. Under SmoothLLM defense, they were 0.18, 0.53, and 0.64 respectively. For Qwen-2.5-7B, the success rates under PPL defense were 0.32, 0.65, and 0.72 respectively. The values under SmoothLLM defense are 0.24, 0.68, and 0.71. For GPT-3.5 turbo, the values under PPL defense were 0.37, 0.59, and 0.62 respectively, and under SmoothLLM defense were 0.26, 0.68, and 0.66 respectively.

Conclusions and future prospects

Conclusions

By investigating the defensive capacity of large language models against jailbreak attacks in low-resource language settings, we propose MNMR-GenA, a genetic-algorithm-based framework that integrates multi-level fine-grained optimization to expand the search space, role-playing and scenario-nesting modules to enhance prompt stealthiness, and reinforcement learning–driven mutation rate adjustment to reduce computational cost. Experimental results demonstrate that MNMR-GenA achieves high jailbreak success rates on both open-source and closed-source models, while generating prompts with strong concealment and semantic coherence. More importantly, this study reveals potential cross-lingual vulnerabilities of large language models in low-resource language scenarios, underscoring the necessity of strengthening LLM safety mechanisms in low-resource language environments.

Future prospects

Cross-model generalization. This study has been validated only on a limited set of models, including open-source models such as DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen-2.5-7B, as well as the closed-source GPT-3.5 turbo. Its transferability to other mainstream model architectures–such as large-scale language models in the LLaMA family, Falcon, and MPT–remains to be systematically evaluated. Future work will extend the evaluation to a broader range of models and architectures to comprehensively assess the generality and cross-model adaptability of MNMR-GenA.

Language coverage and low-resource settings. The current experiments focus on three agglutinative languages–Mongolian, Turkish, and Guarani–and do not yet cover a wider spectrum of low-resource language types. Future research will incorporate more diverse linguistic settings, along with varied contexts and tasks, to validate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed method in more complex and heterogeneous low-resource environments.

Detection tool development. As large language models continue to advance, their outputs are no longer constrained to fixed templates or explicit patterns. Consequently, traditional detection approaches based on keyword matching or simple intent-consistency judgments increasingly exhibit limitations when confronted with unknown or stealthy attack strategies. We therefore encourage future work to develop dynamic jailbreak behavior detection mechanisms that can enhance defenses against real-time jailbreak attacks.

Potential ethical and security risks. Jailbreak prompt generation techniques inherently carry risks of misuse, particularly when applied to sensitive content or low-resource language scenarios. Future work should strictly adhere to ethical guidelines and explore appropriate safety constraints and protective mechanisms, so as to advance LLM security research while mitigating potential risks of abuse.

Ethical approval

This study proposes a framework for automatically generating jailbreak prompts to evaluate the safety of large language models in low-resource language environments. The proposed method could theoretically be misused to attack insufficiently aligned models, thereby inducing outputs that do not conform to usage policies or human values. In low-resource language scenarios, due to limited available corpora and relatively weaker safety alignment, such risks may be more pronounced. The method can systematically generate diverse attack prompts, which may increase jailbreak success rates and reduce the cost of manually designing prompts. In unconstrained settings, this capability could be used to bypass existing safety mechanisms, posing potential risks to deployed model systems. The objective of this work is to analyze potential vulnerabilities of models in low-resource language scenarios from a safety evaluation perspective. Currently, jailbreak attacks typically rely on manually crafted prompts, and systematic studies focusing on low-resource languages remain limited. By introducing an automated generation framework, this study attempts to explore model safety in low-resource language environments and provide references for subsequent defense research. During the experiments, we only used open-source models or legally authorized proprietary models, and all experiments were conducted in controlled environments. No targeted damage or malicious fine-tuning was performed on any specific model. In addition, we did not release a complete toolchain or reproducible interfaces that could be directly used for real-world attacks, in order to reduce potential misuse risks. From a research perspective, analyzing safety issues in low-resource languages can help promote the design of more targeted defense methods and improve model safety alignment capabilities. We encourage future work to further investigate model safety in low-resource language environments while adhering to ethical guidelines and security constraints.
