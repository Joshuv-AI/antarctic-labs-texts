Perioperative Butorphanol for the Prevention of Postoperative Pulmonary Complications After Thoracoscopic Lung Resection: A Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Trial
Authors: Xinghe Wang, Yuqi Zhang, Yan Wu, Tianyu Yin, Jing Xia, Linlin Zhao, Si Zeng, Kunwei Wu, Jia Sun, Su Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePostoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) remain common after thoracoscopic lung resection. Butorphanol, a κ-opioid receptor agonist with analgesic and potential opioid-sparing properties, may influence postoperative respiratory recovery. This trial evaluated whether perioperative butorphanol reduces PPCs after elective thoracoscopic lung resection.Patients and MethodsIn this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial conducted at a tertiary teaching hospital in China from January to July 2025, adults aged 18–65 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I–III scheduled for elective thoracoscopic lung resection were assigned to receive butorphanol or placebo. The butorphanol group received an intravenous bolus of 10 μg/kg before anesthesia induction followed by 5 μg/kg/h until the end of surgery. The control group received volume-matched saline. The primary outcome was PPCs within 7 postoperative days.ResultsOf 520 randomized patients, 506 were included in the modified intention-to-treat analysis. PPCs occurred in 54 of 254 patients (21.3%) in the butorphanol group and 83 of 252 patients (32.9%) in the control group (relative risk, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.48–0.87; P = 0.004), corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of 11.7% and a number needed to treat of 9. The difference was mainly driven by lower rates of atelectasis and pleural effusion. Butorphanol was also associated with higher QoR-40 scores at 24 h, lower opioid consumption, and a modestly shorter hospital stay. Safety outcomes were similar between groups.ConclusionPerioperative butorphanol reduced composite PPCs within 7 days after elective thoracoscopic lung resection, mainly through reductions in atelectasis and pleural effusion, without increasing early safety events.Keywords: postoperative pulmonary complications, butorphanol, lung injury, pulmonary protection, lung surgery

Introduction

Thoracoscopic lung resection has become a common surgical approach for pulmonary lesions because of its minimally invasive nature and favorable recovery profile compared with open thoracotomy. Nevertheless, postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) remain common after lung resection and represent an important source of postoperative morbidity, delayed recovery, and increased healthcare use.1–3 Inadequate postoperative analgesia after thoracic surgery may restrict deep breathing and effective coughing, thereby promoting airway secretion retention, alveolar collapse, and atelectasis. The reported incidence of PPCs after lung surgery varies widely, from 14% to 59%, depending on patient risk profile, surgical approach, perioperative management, and the definitions applied.4,7 Strategies to reduce PPCs are therefore clinically important for patients undergoing thoracic surgery.

Intraoperative lung-protective ventilation is widely used to mitigate ventilator-associated lung injury during thoracic surgery, particularly during one-lung ventilation (OLV).8 However, the optimal ventilation strategy remains uncertain. Although some studies have reported improvements in postoperative oxygenation or pulmonary function with lung-protective ventilation strategies,9,11 others have not shown consistent benefits in postoperative respiratory outcomes.12,15 These mixed findings suggest that ventilation-based approaches alone may be insufficient to prevent PPCs in all patients and support the investigation of adjunctive perioperative strategies.16

Butorphanol is a mixed agonist–antagonist opioid analgesic that primarily acts as a κ-opioid receptor (KOR) agonist.17 Studies suggest that KOR activation may modulate inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and pulmonary vascular endothelial function.18,20 In addition, our previous clinical study showed that butorphanol improved oxygenation and reduced dead-space ventilation in patients with obesity undergoing laparoscopic bariatric surgery.21 These findings provide a rationale for exploring whether butorphanol may influence perioperative pulmonary physiology. However, whether butorphanol can reduce clinically defined PPCs after thoracoscopic lung resection remains unclear.

We therefore conducted a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to evaluate the effect of perioperative butorphanol on the incidence of PPCs within 7 postoperative days. We hypothesized that butorphanol would be associated with a lower incidence of PPCs compared with placebo.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial conducted at the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University, Jiangsu, China. The trial was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500096165, on 20 Jan 2025) and was approved by the institutional ethics committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (XYFY2024-KL657-01). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and is reported in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines.22 Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Patients

Eligible patients were adults aged 18 to 65 years with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status of I to III who were scheduled to undergo elective thoracoscopic lung resection under general anesthesia. Patients were excluded if they had a body mass index (BMI) >30 kg/m2; required emergency surgery; required preoperative mechanical ventilation; had a history of acute lung injury, acute respiratory distress syndrome, or respiratory failure; had severe hepatic dysfunction, defined as hepatic failure or Child–Pugh class B or C; had renal failure, defined as serum creatinine >177 μmol/L; had heart failure, defined as New York Heart Association class >II; had previous lung surgery; had uncontrolled asthma; had impaired pulmonary function, defined as forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) <60% of the predicted value; or had contraindications to butorphanol.

Randomisation and Masking

Randomisation was performed by an independent investigator who was not involved in patient recruitment, perioperative management, outcome assessment, or data analysis. A computer-generated randomisation sequence was created in a 1:1 ratio using permuted blocks of four with a fixed random seed (438729). Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. After a participant had been enrolled, the next envelope in sequence was opened only by an independent research nurse responsible for preparing the study solution. Patients were assigned to receive either butorphanol or placebo. The research nurse prepared butorphanol and saline placebo in identical syringes with the same volume, appearance, and labeling. The nurse was not involved in intraoperative care, postoperative management, outcome assessment, or statistical analysis. The attending anaesthesiologists, surgeons, patients, postoperative care staff, outcome assessors, and data analysts remained blinded to group assignment throughout the trial. Group allocation was not disclosed until completion of the primary analysis, unless unblinding was required for patient safety.

Interventions

Patients assigned to the butorphanol group received an intravenous bolus of butorphanol 10 μg/kg before induction of anesthesia, followed by a continuous intravenous infusion of 5 μg/kg/h until the end of surgery. The dosing regimen was selected based on previous perioperative studies of intravenous butorphanol and our prior clinical experience, with the aim of providing perioperative analgesia and a potential opioid-sparing effect while limiting excessive sedation and respiratory depression.21,23 Patients assigned to the control group received an equivalent volume of normal saline, administered with the same bolus and infusion schedule. All other aspects of anesthesia, ventilation, analgesia, and postoperative care were standardized between the two groups.

Perioperative Management

Perioperative care was standardized in both groups and was conducted in accordance with enhanced recovery recommendations for thoracic surgery.24 Standard monitoring included electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, invasive arterial blood pressure, bispectral index monitoring, and neuromuscular blockade monitoring.

Anesthesia was induced with intravenous midazolam 0.05–0.1 mg/kg, propofol 2–4 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.3–0.5 μg/kg, and cisatracurium 0.1–0.3 mg/kg. After tracheal intubation with a double-lumen tube, anesthesia was maintained with intravenous propofol, titrated to maintain a bispectral index between 40 and 60. Remifentanil was infused at 0.1–0.2 μg/kg/min for intraoperative analgesia. Lactated Ringer’s solution was infused at 3–4 mL/kg/h, and colloid solutions or blood products were administered only when clinically indicated. Mean arterial pressure was maintained within 20% of baseline using vasoactive drugs when necessary.

Mechanical ventilation was standardized according to lung-protective ventilation principles.25 During two-lung ventilation, tidal volume was set at 6–8 mL/kg predicted body weight, with positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5 cmH2O. During one-lung ventilation, tidal volume was reduced to 4–6 mL/kg predicted body weight, with PEEP maintained at 5 cmH2O. The fraction of inspired oxygen (FIO2) was adjusted between 0.5 and 0.8 to maintain SpO2 ≥95% or according to clinical need. The inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio was set at 1:2, and respiratory rate was adjusted to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide between 35 and 45 mmHg. Recruitment maneuvers were performed after tracheal intubation, at the initiation of one-lung ventilation, after resumption of two-lung ventilation, and at the end of surgery, using a sustained airway pressure of 30 cmH2O during two-lung ventilation or 20 cmH2O during one-lung ventilation for 15–20s.

All patients received standardized prophylactic multimodal analgesia before the end of surgery. Approximately 30 min before the anticipated completion of surgery, or before skin closure, the thoracic surgeon performed local wound infiltration under direct visualization. Ropivacaine 0.375% 20 mL, equivalent to 75 mg, was infiltrated slowly and incrementally into all thoracoscopic port sites and the chest drain site, including the intercostal muscle layer, deep fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin edges. If additional volume was required to cover all incision sites, the solution was diluted with normal saline to a total volume of 30 mL without increasing the total dose of ropivacaine. Aspiration was performed before each injection to reduce the risk of intravascular administration. At the same time point, all patients received intravenous acetaminophen 1 g over 15 min; for patients weighing less than 50 kg, the dose was adjusted to 15 mg/kg.

At the end of surgery, neuromuscular blockade was antagonized with neostigmine 40–60 μg/kg and atropine 0.5 mg when clinically indicated after recovery of train-of-four responses. Extubation was performed after patients met standard extubation criteria. Postoperative analgesia was provided using patient-controlled intravenous analgesia containing sufentanil 2 μg/kg and tropisetron 10 mg diluted with 0.9% saline to a total volume of 100 mL. The device was programmed with a background infusion of 2 mL/h, a bolus dose of 0.5 mL, and a lockout interval of 15 min. Analgesia was titrated to maintain a visual analogue scale (VAS) pain score <4. If pain relief remained inadequate after three consecutive patient-controlled boluses, rescue analgesia was provided with intravenous fentanyl 25–50 μg, repeated at 10–15 min intervals as needed until the VAS score was <4 or clinically relevant adverse effects occurred.

Endpoints

The primary outcome was the incidence of composite postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) occurring within 7 postoperative days or before hospital discharge, whichever occurred first. PPCs were defined according to established consensus definitions and included respiratory infection, atelectasis, respiratory failure, bronchospasm, pleural effusion, and pneumothorax (Supplementary Table S1).5,26 A PPC event was recorded if any one of these predefined criteria was met during the assessment period. For the time-to-event analysis, the event was defined as the first occurrence of any PPC component.

Secondary outcomes included unplanned admission to the intensive care unit (ICU); total opioid consumption, expressed as morphine milligram equivalents (MME, Remifentanil and fentanyl were converted using 1 μg = 0.1 mg morphine equivalent, and sufentanil was converted using 1 μg = 1 mg morphine equivalent. Total MME was calculated as the sum of morphine-equivalent doses from all included opioids); postoperative pain intensity assessed using a 0–10 cm visual analogue scale (VAS), where 0 indicated no pain and 10 indicated the worst imaginable pain; the Quality of Recovery-40 (QoR-40) score at 24 h postoperatively; and length of postoperative hospital stay.

Safety outcomes included extubation time, delayed emergence, and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) within 24 h after surgery. Extubation time was defined as the interval from discontinuation of anesthetic agents to removal of the tracheal tube. Delayed emergence was defined as failure to achieve adequate responsiveness or tracheal extubation within 60 min after discontinuation of anesthetic agents. PONV was defined as any episode of nausea, retching, or vomiting within the first 24 h postoperatively.

Data Collection

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were recorded on the day before surgery. On the day of surgery, radial arterial blood samples were collected before induction of anesthesia, 1 h after the initiation of one-lung ventilation, and at the end of surgery. Arterial blood gas variables were measured using a Roche Cobas B123 point-of-care blood gas analyzer (Roche Group, Basel, Switzerland). The respiratory index was calculated as the ratio of the alveolar–arterial oxygen gradient to arterial oxygen tension: respiratory index = (PAO2 − PaO2) / PaO2. Peripheral venous blood samples were collected before induction of anesthesia and at 0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h postoperatively. The 0 h postoperative sample was collected immediately at the end of surgery. Plasma concentrations of interleukin-6 (IL-6), endothelin-1 (ET-1), and Clara cell secretory protein 16 (CC16) were measured using sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (Wuhan Cloud-Clone Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Perioperative variables, including intraoperative respiratory variables, anesthetic and opioid consumption, fluid administration, extubation time, and postoperative analgesic use, were recorded by trained investigators who were blinded to group allocation. Primary and secondary outcomes were assessed by investigators who were not involved in intraoperative management and remained blinded to treatment assignment. PPCs were assessed daily from the day of surgery until postoperative day 7 or hospital discharge, whichever occurred first. Routine chest radiographs and laboratory tests were performed on postoperative day 2 or 3, and additional examinations were obtained when pulmonary complications were clinically suspected. Atelectasis was assessed using postoperative chest imaging and diagnosed according to the predefined criteria in Supplementary Table S1.

For PPC adjudication, all suspected events were reviewed according to the predefined diagnostic criteria listed in Supplementary Table S1. If feasible, radiographic findings were interpreted by clinicians blinded to group allocation. Disagreements regarding PPC diagnosis were resolved by discussion with a senior investigator who was also blinded to treatment assignment. Safety outcomes were collected from anesthesia records, PACU records, and postoperative follow-up assessments. Extubation time and delayed emergence were assessed at the end of anesthesia and during PACU recovery. Postoperative nausea and vomiting were assessed during the first 24 h after surgery.

Sample Size

The sample size was calculated based on the incidence of composite PPCs within 7 postoperative days. Based on previous evidence, we assumed a PPC incidence of 31% in the control group after lung resection.27 We considered a reduction to 20% in the butorphanol group to be clinically relevant, corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of 11%, a relative risk reduction of approximately 35%, and an anticipated number needed to treat of less than 10. With a two-sided α level of 0.05 and 80% power, 494 evaluable patients were required to detect this difference. Allowing for an anticipated dropout rate of 5%, we planned to enroll 520 patients, with 260 patients in each group.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD or median (IQR), according to their distribution, and categorical variables were summarized as number and percentage. Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2-test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Ordinal categorical variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test.

The primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed according to the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) principle. For binary outcomes, treatment effects were reported as relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For the primary outcome, the absolute risk reduction and number needed to treat (NNT) were also calculated. For the primary outcome, a post-hoc sensitivity analysis was performed using a multivariable logistic regression model adjusted for preoperative risk factors for PPCs. For continuous outcomes, treatment effects were reported as mean differences or median differences with 95% CIs, as appropriate. Cumulative incidence curves for the first PPC event were derived from Kaplan–Meier estimates and compared between groups using the Log rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were estimated using a Cox proportional hazards model. Repeatedly measured respiratory variables and biomarkers were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance, with group, time, and group-by-time interaction included in the model. Bonferroni correction was applied for post hoc comparisons across time points. There were no missing data for the primary outcome. Missing data were limited to PACU-related secondary or safety outcomes. Ten patients were transferred to the ICU after surgery, including 4 in the butorphanol group and 6 in the control group; therefore, extubation time, pain score at PACU discharge, and delayed emergence were not directly observed for these patients. These 10 patients accounted for 2.0% of the modified intention-to-treat population, and the missing values were handled using multiple imputation. Analyses of secondary outcomes, individual PPC components, and safety outcomes were considered exploratory and were not adjusted for multiplicity unless otherwise specified. For the QoR-40 score, a between-group difference greater than 6.3 points was considered clinically meaningful, according to the previously reported minimal clinically important difference. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R version 4.1.1.

Results

Study Population

Between January 25 and July 15, 2025, 700 patients were assessed for eligibility, of whom 520 were enrolled and randomly assigned to the butorphanol group or the control group. Six patients in the butorphanol group and eight patients in the control group were excluded from the mITT population because surgery was cancelled or consent was withdrawn before administration of the study intervention. Therefore, 506 patients were included in the mITT analysis, including 254 patients in the butorphanol group and 252 patients in the control group. Furthermore, seven patients in the butorphanol group and four patients in the control group were excluded from the per-protocol analysis because of conversion to thoracotomy after randomisation. Primary and Secondary Outcomes

The overall incidence of composite PPCs within 7 postoperative days was 27.1% (137/506). PPCs occurred in 54 of 254 patients (21.3%) in the butorphanol group and in 83 of 252 patients (32.9%) in the control group (RR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.48–0.87; P = 0.004; Table 2). This corresponded to an absolute risk reduction of 11.7% and a number needed to treat of 9 (95% CI, 5–26). The per-protocol analysis yielded consistent results, with PPCs occurring in 21.1% of patients in the butorphanol group and 32.7% of patients in the control group (P = 0.005). Time-to-event analysis also showed a lower cumulative incidence of first PPC events in the butorphanol group than in the control group (HR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.44–0.86; log-rank P = 0.004; Figure 2). In the post-hoc sensitivity analysis adjusted for preoperative risk factors for PPCs, perioperative butorphanol remained associated with lower odds of PPCs (adjusted OR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.34 to 0.80; P = 0.003; Figure 3).

Among the individual components of the composite PPC outcome, atelectasis and pleural effusion occurred less frequently in the butorphanol group than in the control group (both P < 0.05; Table 2). No significant between-group differences were observed for respiratory infection, respiratory failure, bronchospasm, or pneumothorax. These component-level analyses should be interpreted as exploratory.

Total opioid consumption was lower in the butorphanol group than in the control group (median, 322 [IQR, 255–382] vs 364 [IQR, 282–437] MME; median difference, −42; 95% CI, −59 to −22; P < 0.001; Table 2). Postoperative pain scores were also lower in the butorphanol group at 6 and 12 h after surgery (both P < 0.001), whereas pain scores at PACU discharge and at 24 and 48 h did not differ significantly between groups.

The QoR-40 score at 24 h was higher in the butorphanol group than in the control group (median, 162 [IQR, 149–174] vs 153 [IQR, 133–165]; median difference, 8 points; 95% CI, 4–12; P = 0.003). The butorphanol group also had a shorter postoperative hospital stay (mean, 4.9 ± 1.6 vs 5.3 ± 1.8 days; mean difference, −0.4 days; 95% CI, −0.69 to −0.10; P = 0.008). There was no significant difference in unplanned ICU admission between the two groups (Table 2).

Respiratory Variables

Intraoperative respiratory variables are presented in Table 3. Baseline respiratory variables were comparable between the two groups. Compared with the control group, the butorphanol group had lower driving pressure at 1 h after the initiation of one-lung ventilation and at the end of surgery (both P < 0.001). Dynamic pulmonary compliance and oxygenation index were higher in the butorphanol group at the same time points (all P < 0.001). Tidal volume, peak inspiratory pressure, and respiratory index did not differ significantly between groups at any assessed time point.

Safety Outcomes

Extubation time did not differ significantly between the two groups (mean difference, 0.20 min; 95% CI, −1.29 to 1.69; P = 0.792; Table 2). The incidence of delayed emergence was also similar between groups (RR, 1.11; 95% CI, 0.67–1.85; P = 0.685). Postoperative nausea and vomiting occurred in 75 patients (29.5%) in the butorphanol group and 68 patients (27.0%) in the control group, with no significant between-group difference (RR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.83–1.45; P = 0.525).

Discussion

In this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of patients undergoing elective thoracoscopic lung resection, perioperative intravenous butorphanol was associated with a lower incidence of composite PPCs within 7 postoperative days compared with placebo. The absolute risk reduction was 11.7%, corresponding to a number needed to treat of 9. The apparent benefit was mainly driven by reductions in atelectasis and pleural effusion. Butorphanol was also associated with lower opioid consumption, lower pain scores at 6 and 12 h postoperatively, and a higher QoR-40 score at 24 h. However, the reduction in postoperative hospital stay was modest and should be interpreted cautiously.

PPCs are common after thoracic surgery and are associated with increased morbidity, delayed recovery, and greater healthcare use.3 Their pathogenesis is multifactorial, involving surgical trauma, one-lung ventilation, inflammatory activation, postoperative pain, impaired cough and deep breathing, opioid-related adverse effects, and changes in respiratory mechanics.8 In the present study, butorphanol was associated with lower postoperative IL-6, ET-1, and CC16 concentrations at several time points. These findings are consistent with a potential attenuation of perioperative inflammatory and lung epithelial injury responses. Experimental data also suggest that κ-opioid receptor activation may modulate inflammatory signaling, endothelial function, and oxidative stress.28,29 CC16 is a major functional protein secreted by Clara cells in the pulmonary bronchiole mucosa.30 Oxidative stress and increased alveolar-capillary permeability significantly enhance the expression of CC16, which has been evaluated as a biomarker of pulmonary injury.31,32 The above results suggest that butorphanol exerts anti-inflammatory and alveolar-capillary barrier protecting effects.

During thoracic surgery, double-lumen tubes, one-lung ventilation, airway manipulation, and reduced ventilated lung volume may increase airway resistance and airway pressure, thereby contributing to ventilator-associated lung stress.33,34 In this study, dynamic pulmonary compliance and oxygenation index were higher, and driving pressure was lower, in the butorphanol group at 1 h after the initiation of one-lung ventilation and at the end of surgery. Lower driving pressure during one-lung ventilation has been associated with fewer PPCs in thoracic surgical patients.35 However, the present trial was not designed to determine whether these intraoperative respiratory variables mediated the effect of butorphanol on PPCs. These findings should therefore be interpreted as physiological correlates rather than definitive mechanistic evidence.

Pain control is another plausible contributor to the lower incidence of selected PPC components. Postoperative pain after thoracic surgery may restrict deep breathing, coughing, sputum clearance, and early mobilization, thereby increasing the risk of atelectasis and other respiratory complications.36 In this trial, butorphanol was associated with lower pain scores after surgery and lower total opioid consumption. It is notable that between-group differences in pain scores were observed only at 6 and 12 h, not at 24 or 48 h; this temporal pattern suggests that the analgesic benefit may be most relevant during the immediate recovery period. Moreover, the absolute differences in pain scores were small and did not reach a commonly used minimal clinically important difference for pain intensity on a 0–10 scale. Because both groups received standardized multimodal postoperative analgesia, pain scores in the control group were also maintained at relatively low levels, but at the cost of greater opioid consumption. Therefore, the analgesic relevance of butorphanol in this trial may be better reflected by its opioid-sparing effect than by the small numerical reduction in pain scores. As a κ-opioid receptor agonist with analgesic properties, butorphanol may provide perioperative analgesia while reducing the need for additional opioids.17,37,40 Reduced opioid exposure could be clinically relevant because opioid-related sedation, nausea and vomiting, and respiratory depression may adversely affect postoperative recovery. In the present study, extubation time, delayed emergence, and postoperative nausea and vomiting did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that the butorphanol regimen used in this trial was not associated with a measurable increase in these early safety outcomes.

The QoR-40 score at 24 h was higher in the butorphanol group, with a median between-group difference of 8 points. This difference exceeded the established minimal clinically important difference of 6.3 points for the QoR-40,41 suggesting that the improvement may be clinically meaningful. This finding is consistent with the observed reductions in early postoperative pain and opioid consumption. PPCs have been associated with prolonged hospital stay in previous studies.42,43 In the present trial, postoperative hospital stay was statistically shorter in the butorphanol group; however, the absolute difference was only 0.4 days. Given the influence of discharge practices, institutional pathways, and non-clinical factors on hospital stay, the clinical relevance of this difference should be interpreted cautiously.

Although the primary outcome showed a statistically significant and clinically relevant reduction in composite PPCs, this finding should be interpreted in the context of the composite endpoint structure. The between-group difference was mainly driven by lower rates of atelectasis and pleural effusion, whereas respiratory infection, respiratory failure, bronchospasm, and pneumothorax did not differ significantly between groups. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence that butorphanol prevents all forms of PPCs, but rather as evidence of a reduction in selected components of the composite PPC outcome in this study population. These components may plausibly be influenced by improved analgesia, better cough and deep-breathing capacity, lower opioid exposure, and more favorable perioperative respiratory mechanics.8,44 The lower rate of pleural effusion might reflect reduced perioperative alveolar-capillary or endothelial perturbation;21,29 but this interpretation remains speculative. In contrast, more severe or clinically distinct PPC components, including respiratory failure, respiratory infection, bronchospasm, and pneumothorax, were not significantly different between groups. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence that butorphanol reduces all types of PPCs or severe pulmonary complications. Rather, the results suggest a potential reduction in selected components of a composite PPC outcome, and the component-level analyses should be considered exploratory.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center and enrolled a relatively selected surgical population, which may limit the external validity of the findings. Patients with several high-risk perioperative characteristics, including obesity, impaired pulmonary function, heart failure, and renal dysfunction, were excluded. Therefore, the results should be interpreted primarily in low- to moderate-risk patients undergoing elective thoracoscopic lung resection and may not be directly generalizable to the broader thoracic surgical population encountered in routine clinical practice. Second, PPCs were assessed as a composite outcome, and the trial was not powered to detect differences in individual PPC components. Accordingly, component-level comparisons should be interpreted as exploratory. Third, although perioperative biomarkers and respiratory variables were measured, the study was not designed to establish causal pathways linking butorphanol to PPC reduction. The mechanistic findings should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating. Fourth, only short-term postoperative outcomes were assessed. Further studies are needed to determine whether butorphanol influences longer-term pulmonary recovery, readmission, or other patient-centered outcomes. Finally, although pleural effusion occurred less frequently in the butorphanol group, chest tube drainage volume and duration of chest tube placement were not systematically collected as prespecified outcomes. Therefore, the clinical and functional significance of the lower radiological incidence of pleural effusion requires further investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, among patients undergoing elective thoracoscopic lung resection, perioperative intravenous butorphanol was associated with a lower incidence of composite PPCs within 7 postoperative days, primarily driven by reductions in atelectasis and pleural effusion. Butorphanol was also associated with improved early quality of recovery and lower opioid consumption, without a significant increase in delayed emergence or postoperative nausea and vomiting. Further studies are needed to confirm these findings, clarify mechanisms, and determine generalizability to higher-risk thoracic surgical populations.
