Introduction

Historically, guidelines were developed by panels of physicians who formulated recommendations based on their opinions on results of literature reviews [1]. This early approach lacked a formal framework for evaluating the underlying evidence [1, 2]. While limitations such as the absence of evidence‐based decision‐making, reduced transparency, inadequate stakeholder representation, and a heightened risk of bias persist to some extent today, they have been mitigated through the development of robust methodologies for guideline creation [2]. The GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) methodology, along with guideline appraisal checklists such as AGREE II, are invaluable tools in ensuring guidelines are created to a high standard [2].

Despite the establishment of a robust Evidence‐to‐Decision framework for guideline formulation, many medical professionals remain unfamiliar with these processes [3]. This highlights the need to raise awareness of existing tools and methodologies for creating high‐quality guidelines.

To address these unmet needs, Gutflix launched a new series, How to develop guidelines. Gutflix is a free online content and learning platform developed by UEG that hosts UEG Week recordings, educational online courses and articles, podcasts, guidelines and more, which could be personalised to learning needs and progress.

The online course series ‘How to develop guidelines’ explores the essential steps of robust guideline development and offers actionable advice from start to finish of any guideline development journey.

The most recent episode 2 addressed a clinical question: Does tranexamic acid (TXA) work for lower gastrointestinal bleeding (LGIB)? and the essence is recaptured in this article.

Formulate clinical question into a PICO.

Formulating a PICO (Patient Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes) is a vital component of the GRADE methodology and is thus essential for creating evidence‐based guidelines [2]. When deciding the Patient Population, we can consider demographics (age, sex, race, etc.), the condition being explored, as well as its sub‐type or severity [2]. The Intervention may be pharmacological, non‐pharmacological, or surgical/procedural. You also need to consider the specifics of the intervention; for example, if it is a drug, what route or dose is going to be explored? [2] The Intervention will be contrasted with the Comparator, which can be either placebo or alternative interventions. Finally, a number of Outcomes may be selected, which will then be stratified by members of the panel as per the GRADE methodology.

For example, in evaluating TXA for lower GI bleeding, the topic is broad and the scope might be refined by breaking it down into PICO elements as:

Patient Population: adults with LGIB;

Using PICO in this way facilitates the creation of a focused and measurable research question, enabling an accurate and robust literature search.

A targeted literature search for guideline development.

A systematic review of guidelines must be rigorous but not indiscriminate. The aim is not to capture every publication mentioning TXA and LGIB but rather to apply an a priori clinical question and prespecified criteria to enable a transparent, targeted search.

In this case, we sought randomised controlled trials (RCTs) of TXA in gastrointestinal bleeding from which LGIB data could be extrapolated. We also considered observational or cohort studies with adequate sample size. We excluded studies beyond this scope, such as those on non‐GI bleeding, upper GI bleeding only, or small observational series (< 100 participants).

Using the defined PICO and searching PubMed, MEDLINE, and EMBASE with the support of an information specialist, we retrieved 21 titles, including three RCTs and one large observational study. These were sufficient for evidence synthesis. Importantly, the targeted approach ensured that no key studies were missed. Efficiency did not come at the expense of completeness.

Data extraction and creating a ‘Summary of Findings’ table.

Adequately powered, high‐quality RCTs provide the strongest evidence base. Large observational or comparative studies can contribute when sufficiently powered. As a rule of thumb, for dichotomous outcomes, a study should include > 300 events and a sample size larger than a single well‐designed RCT; for continuous outcomes, > 400 participants are desirable.

In GRADE, RCT evidence begins at ‘high certainty’ of evidence rating but may be downgraded, whereas non‐randomised evidence begins at ‘low certainty’ rating but can be upgraded if robust. For the outcome of mortality, two RCTs and one observational study were eligible. These were analysed separately, as mixing study designs is discouraged given their inherently different methodology and different risks of bias between the two study designs.

Across all three studies, the conclusion was consistent: TXA did not reduce mortality compared with control (Table 1). This process will be repeated for other outcomes (five to seven outcomes per guideline, as per Cochrane review) that were chosen and ranked by panel members.

Conducting a panel discussion and formulating recommendations.

Panel discussion is a critical stage where evidence is translated into practice statements. This should not be a simple exercise of voting but a structured application of the GRADE Evidence‐to‐Decision (EtD) framework. EtD requires consideration of desirable and undesirable effects, certainty of evidence, patient values, balance of effects, resources, cost‐effectiveness, equity, acceptability, and feasibility. These elements are often neglected but are essential for sound guideline development.

In our example, the evidence demonstrated no mortality benefit for TXA in LGIB. Thromboembolic events were more frequent among patients receiving TXA, although the difference was not statistically significant. Furthermore, there was no evidence that TXA reduced blood transfusion requirements. Rather than focusing solely on balancing desirable and undesirable effects, it is necessary to incorporate all EtD elements. The potential costs of treating thromboembolic events and the additional costs and resources associated with TXA use must be considered. Equity and accessibility are unlikely to be limiting factors, as TXA is widely available across Europe. On balance, the panel concluded that TXA should not be recommended for LGIB.

The resulting statement was

Recommendation:

‘Tranexamic acid should not be used for patients with acute lower gastrointestinal bleeding.’

Justification:

‘High‐quality evidence showed no mortality benefit compared with placebo. Use of TXA incurs additional costs and may increase thromboembolic risk.’

Summary

Guidelines should ultimately deliver evidence‐based recommendations with transparent rationale. Whilst many guidelines have adopted GRADE to appraise evidence [5, 6, 7, 8], the EtD elements that influence the implementability of recommendations remain underdeveloped.

Evidence synthesis and appraisal are increasingly reproducible and widely shared, as reflected by the rapid growth in systematic reviews and meta‐analyses in recent years. Yet, the true value of guideline work lies not only in compiling evidence but also in the expertise‐driven, nuanced interpretation that EtD demands. It is this deliberative process—balancing evidence with feasibility, equity, and real‐world context—that ultimately determines the credibility and implementability of recommendations, and we would emphasise its central importance.

Using the example of TXA in LGIB, we have illustrated key steps in the process. We hope this article underscores the importance of EtD considerations and contributes to further improvement in the quality of guidelines that are adoptable and implementable across healthcare settings.

Other important aspects of guideline development—such as outcome selection, nuanced interpretation of evidence, and the development of Good Clinical Practice statements in the absence of high‐certainty evidence—are covered in more depth in the Gutflix series, How to Develop Guidelines, which provides step‐by‐step instruction and comprehensive references.
