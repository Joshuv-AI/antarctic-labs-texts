Multi-omics reveals flavor characteristics landscape of muscles in Chinese broilers
Authors: Jiahao Li, Xinyi Zheng, Yaohua Li, Zhen Zhou, Ziyu Sang, Shenghua Wei, Siyu Guo, Yu Sun, Danfeng Cai, Zhaofeng Zhang, Yu Mo, Bolin Cai, Qinghua Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Qingyuan partridge chickens are valued as a high-quality Indigenous broiler breed in China, characterized by their distinctive and superior meat flavor. To systematically investigate the flavor precursor basis and molecular signatures underlying this quality, we integrated volatile metabolomics, metabolomics, lipidomics, and RNA-seq analyses across five anatomical parts: breast muscle (BM, Pectoralis major), abdominal muscle (AM, External abdominal oblique), back muscle (BAM, Latissimus dorsi), thigh muscle (TM, Tensor fasciae latae), and drumstick muscle (DM, Gastrocnemius). Metabolomics identified linoelaidic acid and malic acid, among others, as potential contributing substances of muscle parts. Lipidomics revealed distinct abundances of specific lipid species, including PE (20:4_18:0) and PS (18:0_21:0). RNA-seq revealed that differentially expressed genes were enriched in multiple flavor-associated pathways. Integrated multi-omics analysis further identified 83 metabolites, 97 lipids, and 1 volatile compound with strong interactive roles in defining the flavor precursor landscape, and notably, benzaldehyde emerged as a critical volatile marker for part discrimination. In summary, this multi-omics investigation uncovered potential factors of the flavor profile in Qingyuan partridge chickens, providing a reference for leveraging regional chicken specialties.

Introduction

As the second most consumed source of meat protein globally, chicken has emerged as a significant component of the modern diet (Jin et al., 2021a; Pellattiero et al., 2020). Consumer preference varies for different parts, appealing to particular segments of the population (Khan et al., 2015a). For instance, the breast meat, being low in fat and high in protein, is favored by health-conscious individuals and fitness enthusiasts; while the thigh meat gains popularity for its richer flavor and higher fat content (Chen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2024). China possesses abundant genetic resources of local chicken breeds, which have been effectively conserved and utilized (Li et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2024). As a premium poultry breed in China, Indigenous broilers are highly esteemed in the consumer market for their superior meat flavor, unique texture, and cultural value (Wu et al., 2024; Z. Yang et al., 2025). However, their high market value has created a discrepancy with the relatively broad breed certification standards. This has led to frequent market issues such as mislabeling and fraudulent substitution, where ordinary or low-quality chickens—lacking the genuine breed's performance and flavor—are sold under the indigenous broiler name. This situation underscores the importance of comprehensively characterizing both the flavor profile and the flavor precursor basis of indigenous broilers.

Metabolomics offers a robust means to identify metabolites in chicken, providing a holistic and systematic analysis of the meat's metabolome (Li et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025). This technique has seen growing adoption in meat quality studies (R. Liu et al., 2026; Lv et al., 2025). Specifically, volatile metabolomics, dedicated to flavor research, is frequently used in characterizing flavor attributes (Y. Li et al., 2024; L. Zhou et al., 2025). Its integration with standard metabolomics enables full metabolite coverage and the mapping of the chicken metabolome (Zhang et al., 2024). Lipids are pivotal for flavor development, contributing to unique meat flavors through diverse reactions (L. Chen et al., 2025; Y. Zhang et al., 2025a). Lipidomics, which serves as a tool for detecting all lipid species in chicken, has proven invaluable in meat quality research (Ali et al., 2025a; Y. Xu et al., 2025). Transcriptomics delves into the genetic basis of meat quality (Li et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025). The combined use of metabolomics with other omics platforms presents an effective strategy for pinpointing meat quality-related genes and pathways (Z. Zhou et al., 2024). Despite this, research on chicken meat quality remains largely confined to single-omics studies, failing to provide an integrated assessment of substances and gene expression. Understanding the flavor formation, molecular basis, and gene expression in different chicken parts is critically important for product development and marketing (C. Xu and Yin, 2024). Consequently, utilizing a multi-omics framework to explore flavor traits, gene expression, and global metabolite profiles across various chicken parts emerges as a vital and underexplored avenue for future research.

This study employed for the first time an integrated analysis of volatile metabolomics, metabolomics, lipidomics, transcriptomics, and their conjoint datasets to investigate the flavor composition, metabolite deposition, and key genes potentially involved in flavor formation in five chicken muscles: breast muscle (BM, Pectoralis major), abdominal muscle (AM, External abdominal oblique), back muscle (BAM, Latissimus dorsi), thigh muscle (TM, Tensor fasciae latae), and drumstick muscle (DM, Gastrocnemius). We conducted a comprehensive characterization of the flavor profiles across these parts of Qingyuan partridge chickens, which elucidated the metabolic and genetic basis underlying the high-quality meat flavor. This work offers novel insights into consumer-oriented variations in meat quality and nutritional value, and provides potential molecular targets for future improvement of chicken meat flavor.

Materials and methods

Experimental animal information

Experimental chickens were obtained from Fengxiang Partridge Development Co., Ltd., (Guangdong, China). This study utilized 18-week-old female Qingyuan partridge chickens as the experimental subjects. This age and sex were selected based on the rationale that Qingyuan partridge chickens reach stable body size and weight at 18 weeks, which is considered the standard market age, and females represent the primary commercial product. All animal husbandry and management practices strictly adhered to the Chinese National Standards GB/T 5916-2020 and NY/T 1871-2010. All animal experiments and procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of South China Agricultural University (Approval ID: SCAU-2023f273).

Sample processing and collection

To ensure representativeness, six individuals were randomly selected for analysis. The slaughter procedure was conducted in compliance with the Chinese National Standard GB/T 19478-2018. Chickens were allowed free access to water but were fasted for 12 h prior to slaughter. Immediately after slaughter, the BM, AM, BAM, TM, and DM were collected, yielding a total of 30 muscle samples (5 parts × 6 chickens, with six biological replicates, n = 6). The samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 2 h and subsequently stored at −80°C for subsequent RNA-seq, metabolomic, lipidomic, and volatile metabolomic analyses.

Sample preparation and LC-MS analysis

Untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed on chicken samples from five distinct anatomical parts, with six biological replicates per part. All preparation steps were conducted on ice. Following thawing, tissues were homogenized under liquid nitrogen, and 20 mg aliquots were weighed into 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes. Subsequently, 400 μL of 70% (v/v) aqueous methanol, spiked with internal standard(s), was added for metabolite extraction. The samples were then vortexed at 1500 rpm for 5 min and allowed to stand for 15 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, 300 μL of the supernatant was collected and subjected to a second centrifugation under identical conditions for 3 min. Finally, 200 μL of the clarified supernatant was used for LC-MS analysis.

Liquid chromatography was performed using a Vanquish system (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). The supernatant was separated on an ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 Column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) maintained at 40°C. The mobile phases consisted of (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. Mass spectrometric analysis was conducted on a Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). Data were acquired in both positive and negative ionization modes. The ionization voltages were set to 3800 V for ESI+ and 3400 V for ESI-. The vaporizer temperatures were 350°C (ESI+) and 650°C (ESI-). The sheath gas and auxiliary gas flow rates were 60 and 20 arbitrary units, respectively, and the ion transfer tube temperature was 320°C. The mass scan range was 70-1000 Da for both MS1 and MS2, with resolutions of 60,000 and 15,000, respectively. The top 3 most intense ions from the MS1 scan were selected for fragmentation, with dynamic exclusion enabled to avoid repetitive MS/MS spectra.

Raw data were converted to mzML format using ProteoWizard. Peak extraction, alignment, and retention time correction were performed using the XCMS package. Features with a missing rate >50% across sample groups were filtered out. Peak areas were then normalized using the Support Vector Regression (SVR) method. Metabolite identification was carried out by querying against public databases (PubChem, MetLin, HMDB, and KEGG) and a custom-built in-house database.

Sample preparation and HS-SPME-GC-MS analysis

As described in the preceding section, the same six biological replicates were used for volatile metabolomics analysis. All sample preparation steps were performed on ice. After thawing, tissues were homogenized under liquid nitrogen, and a homogenous aliquot of 0.2 g was weighed into a headspace vial. To each vial, 20 μL of internal standard solution (10 μg/mL) and 0.2 g of NaCl were added. Headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) was employed for extraction using an SPME Arrow device (CTC Analytics AG, Zwingen, Switzerland). The SPME Arrow fiber (120 μm DVB/CWR/PDMS; Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) was conditioned at 250°C for 2 h prior to use. For extraction, the sample vial was incubated at 95°C for 5 min with agitation, after which the fiber was exposed to the headspace for 15 min at the same temperature. Thermal desorption was carried out in the GC injector at 250°C for 5 min.

Chromatographic separation was achieved on a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA) with helium as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. The injector temperature was set to 250°C. The oven temperature program was as follows: initial temperature of 40°C held for 3.5 min, increased to 100°C at 10 °C/min, then to 180°C at 7 °C/min, and finally to 280°C at 25 °C/min, with a final hold for 5 min.

Mass spectrometric detection was performed in selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode. The interface, quadrupole, and ion source temperatures were maintained at 280°C, 150°C, and 230°C, respectively, with an electron ionization energy of 70 eV. Volatile metabolites were identified by matching their mass spectra and retention indices against a custom-built database (MetWare).

Sample preparation and UPLC-MS analysis

As outlined previously, the same six biological replicates were utilized for lipidomic analysis. All procedures were conducted on ice. Following thawing, tissues were homogenized under liquid nitrogen, and a 20 mg aliquot was accurately weighed into a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube. Lipid extraction was performed by adding 1 mL of ice-cold extraction solvent (methyl tert-butyl ether: methanol, 3:1 v/v, containing internal standards) to each tube, followed by vortexing for 15 min. Subsequently, 200 μL of water was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 1 min. Phase separation was achieved by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. A 200 μL aliquot of the upper organic layer was collected and dried using a CentriVap centrifugal concentrator (LABCONCO, KC, USA). The dried lipid extracts were reconstituted in 200 μL of a 1:1 (v/v) acetonitrile: isopropanol solution by vortexing for 3 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min. The resulting supernatant was subjected to UPLC-MS analysis.

Liquid chromatography was performed using an ExionLC™ AD UHPLC system (Sciex, Shanghai, China). The separation was achieved on a Thermo Accucore™ C30 column (2.6 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) maintained at 45°C. The mobile phases consisted of (A) acetonitrile/water (6:4, v/v) and (B) acetonitrile/isopropanol (1:9, v/v), both containing 0.1% formic acid and 10 mmol/L ammonium formate. The flow rate was set at 0.35 mL/min. The gradient elution program (all ratios v/v) was as follows: 0 min, 80% A/20% B; 2 min, 70% A/30% B; 4 min, 40% A/60% B; 9 min, 15% A/85% B; 14 min, 10% A/90% B; 15.5 min, 5% A/95% B; 17.3 min, 5% A/95% B; 17.5 min, 80% A/20% B; 20 min, 80% A/20% B.

Mass spectrometric detection was carried out on a QTRAP® 6500+ tandem mass spectrometer (Sciex, Shanghai, China) equipped with an ESI source. The source parameters were set as follows: ion spray voltage, 5500 V (positive) and −4500 V (negative); temperature, 500°C; curtain gas, 35 psi. For each transition in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, declustering potentials and collision energies were individually optimized. Lipid identification was based on the retention time, precursor/product ion pairs, and matching against a custom-built database (MWDB, MetWare Database). Quantification was achieved using the MRM mode on the triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. The full names of the lipids identified in this experiment are listed in Supplementary A.1.

Sample preparation and data analysis for RNA-seq

As described in the previous sections, the same six biological replicates per muscle part were used for transcriptomic analysis. Total RNA was extracted from the samples. The concentration and integrity of the isolated RNA were assessed using a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer/Microplate Reader and a Qsep400 Bioanalyzer, respectively. Following quality control, RNA-seq libraries were constructed and used to prepare DNA Nanoballs (DNBs). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina DNB platform (Illumina NovaSeq 6000). Use HISAT2 to align the sequencing reads to the reference genome (GRCg7b, GCF_016699485.2). Differential expression analysis between the five tissue types was conducted using the DESeq2 package. The resulting P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini & Hochberg method. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 and an absolute fold change greater than 2 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses for the identified DEGs were performed based on the hypergeometric test.

Screening, annotation, and enrichment analysis of DEVs, DEMs, and DELs

Differentially expressed volatile compounds (DEVs), differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs), and differentially expressed lipids (DELs) were screened based on a variable importance in projection (VIP) value > 1 from the orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model and a P-value <0.05 from Student's t-test. Prior to OPLS-DA, the data were log2-transformed and mean-centered. Permutation testing (200 iterations) was conducted to validate the model and avoid overfitting. Functional annotation and enrichment analysis of the identified DEVs, DEMs, and DELs were performed using the KEGG database. Significantly enriched pathways were determined based on P-values derived from the hypergeometric test.

Statistical analysis

Data processing encompassed normalization via Z-score standardization ((x - mean)/standard deviation) and logarithmic transformation (Log2 and Log10). Prior to principal component analysis (PCA), the data were scaled using Z-score normalization. For OPLS-DA, data underwent both z-score normalization and logarithmic transformation. All visualizations were generated using R software (version 4.2.2). Statistical significance between two groups was determined by Student's t-test, with a P-value <0.05 considered significant. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson correlation and the Mantel test. Correlations with an absolute coefficient |r| > 0.80 and a P-value <0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Results

Different part of muscles showed significantly diverse pattern in flavor profiling

To investigate whether flavor differences exist in chicken from different parts, volatile metabolomic profiling of five muscle tissues was performed using HS-SPME-GC-MS. A total of 1172 volatile metabolites with CAS identifiers were detected and categorized into functional groups including Aldehydes, Ketones, Esters, Hydrocarbons, Alcohols and Amines, Heterocyclic Compounds, Benzene and Substituted Derivatives, Terpenoids, Organic Acids and Their Derivatives, Nitrogen Compounds, Ethers, Halogenated Hydrocarbons, and Others (Fig. 1b and Supplementary A.2). Among all identified volatiles, the top three categories by number were Aldehydes, Ketones, and Esters (29.18%), followed by Hydrocarbons (23.21%), and Alcohols and Amines (14.85%). Specifically, Aldehydes, Ketones, and Esters showed the highest relative content in the AM group, Hydrocarbons were most abundant in the BM group, and Alcohols and Amines were highest in the AM group (Fig. 1c). Given that different compound classes impart distinct flavor characteristics, it can be hypothesized that the accumulation of these class-specific substances contributes to the flavor variation among muscle parts. As the breast is the primary and largest consumable section and the main constituent of chicken meat, subsequent analysis prioritized revealing differences between the breast muscle and other parts. OPLS-DA models demonstrated clear separation between different muscle tissues (Fig. 1d–g). Metabolites might play crucial roles in flavor formation in different part of chicken muscles

To investigate the relationship between flavor variation and metabolites in chicken, untargeted metabolomic analysis of five chicken muscle parts was performed using LC-MS in both T3_positive and T3_negative modes, detecting a total of 2173 metabolites (Fig. 2a and Supplementary A.7). Based on their chemotaxonomic classification, these metabolites were categorized into 19 classes. Among them, lipids and lipid-like molecules constituted the largest proportion (29.68%), followed by organic acids and derivatives (18.73%). PCA effectively distinguished between the groups, with the first two principal components explaining at least 28% of the total variance, demonstrating good interpretability of the PCA model (Fig. 2b–e). Similarly, OPLS-DA also achieved clear separation between groups (Fig. 2f–i). In the BMDM, BMTM, and BMAM comparisons, the Q2 exceeded 0.8. In the BMAM group, 391 significantly up-regulated and 278 significantly down-regulated DEMs were identified (Fig. 2j and Supplementary A.8). The major categories of up-regulated substances were lipids and lipid-like molecules as well as organic acids and derivatives, with compounds showing large fold changes including 3-Palmitoyl-sn-glycerol and LPE (0:0_16:0). In the BMDM group, 422 significantly up-regulated and 261 significantly down-regulated DEMs were identified (Fig. 2l and Supplementary A.10). The major up-regulated categories were lipids and lipid-like molecules and organic acids and derivatives, with large fold changes seen in L-Homocysteic acid and Succinyladenosine. In the BMTM group, 427 significantly up-regulated and 289 significantly down-regulated DEMs were screened (Fig. 2m and Supplementary A.11). Based on the above results, it can be inferred that organic acids and derivatives and lipids and lipid-like molecules are the primary categories contributing to metabolic differences between tissues. Compared with the other groups, the BMBAM group exhibited the fewest DEMs, indicating a higher similarity in metabolites between these tissues, which is consistent with the PCA results.

The Venn diagram illustrates the intersections of DEMs among the comparison groups, revealing 150 metabolites common to all four comparisons (Fig. 2n). We performed K-means cluster analysis on the screened DEMs (Fig. 2o). Interestingly, the cluster analysis precisely grouped the DEMs into five clusters, which corresponded to the five tissue parts. KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted on these five DEM clusters. The results showed that DEMs clustered in the BAM group were primarily enriched in the following pathways: glycerophospholipid metabolism, lysine degradation, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and butanoate metabolism. DEMs clustered in the TM group were mainly enriched in pathways related to unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, glycerolipid metabolism, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, linoleic acid metabolism, and terpenoid backbone biosynthesis. DEMs clustered in the AM group were significantly enriched in pathways including efferocytosis, primary bile acid biosynthesis, ether lipid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and steroid biosynthesis. DEMs clustered in the DM group showed major enrichment in purine metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, glutathione metabolism, FoxO signaling pathway, and phosphatidylinositol signaling system. DEMs enriched in the BM group were primarily associated with metabolic pathways, histidine metabolism, autophagy, calcium signaling pathway, and ubiquinone and other terpenoid-quinone biosynthesis. KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on all DEMs (Fig. 2p). The figure displays the major pathways associated with the differential metabolites across the five tissue types, including glycerophospholipid metabolism and nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism. The activity of glycerophospholipid metabolism directly reflects the dynamic turnover of muscle cell or organelle membranes, which is crucial for post-injury repair or growth processes. In our previous research, the identified DEMs were also enriched in glycerophospholipid metabolism, suggesting the role of this pathway in influencing meat flavor (J. Li et al., 2025). Comparative analyses for the two groups are presented in Fig. S5. In the BMAM group, the identified metabolic pathways included glycerophospholipid metabolism, histidine metabolism, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acid (Fig. S5a). In the BMBAM group, glycerolipid metabolism, the FoxO signaling pathway, and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism were identified (Fig. S5b). In the BMDM group, the differential metabolites were primarily associated with nucleotide metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, and purine metabolism pathways (Fig. S5c). In the BMTM group, the significantly enriched pathways included the GnRH signaling pathway, glycerolipid metabolism, and glycerophospholipid metabolism (Fig. S5d). A clustering heatmap shows a subset of DEMs (Fig. S4). Hierarchical cluster analysis was performed on the tissues from different parts. The results showed that BM and BAM exhibited similar metabolic profiles, while DM, TM, and AM clustered together with similar profiles, and a significant distinction existed between these two major clusters. Interestingly, the content of lipids and lipid-like molecules was higher in the DM, TM, and AM groups, whereas the opposite pattern was observed in the BM vs. BAM comparison. Conversely, organic oxygen compounds and benzenoids were present at higher levels in the BM vs. BAM comparison and lower in the DM, TM, and AM groups. These findings suggest that different metabolites possess distinct expression patterns across various muscle tissues.

Lipidomic further identified the important metabolites driving the flavor pattern formation in chicken muscles

In the metabolomic analysis, lipids and lipid-like molecules were identified as the primary category of metabolic differences between flavor-distinct parts. To investigate the lipid disparities in muscle tissues from different flavor-related parts, quantitative lipidomics was performed on five muscle tissues using UPLC-MS. A total of 1021 lipids were detected (Fig. 3a and Supplementary A.12). The most abundant category was GP (590), followed by GL (236) and SP (109). Different lipid classes showed significant variations across the muscle tissues. Specifically, compared to BM, the contents of FA, PR, SP, and ST were significantly higher in AM and DM; the GL content was significantly higher in AM; and the contents of FA, PR, and SP were significantly higher in TM (Fig. 3b). Discussion

Qingyuan partridge chickens are among the most valuable indigenous genetic resources and are prized for their superior meat flavor, as are pigs and cattle. (Kong et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). While the ultimate flavor of meat is recognized to be highly dependent on cooking and processing methods, the intrinsic flavor precursor composition of raw muscle provides the substrate foundation for these thermal reactions (Bleicher et al., 2022; Khan et al., 2015b; S. Wang et al., 2023). However, the flavor precursor profiles of different anatomical parts and their potential to develop distinct cooked flavors remain largely unexplored. To address this, we selected five muscle tissues from Qingyuan partridge chickens and first compared their volatile compound profiles under a standardized thermal extraction condition using HS-SPME-GC-MS. Prompted by the observed volatile differences, we then characterized the non-volatile metabolites, lipids, and transcriptomic signatures of the raw muscles. Integrative analysis identified a set of metabolites, lipids, and volatile compounds associated with part-specific flavor formation potential, and highlighted a molecular interaction network that may underlie the differential precursor signatures among muscle parts.

Previous studies on chicken have primarily focused on comparisons with other breeds, lacking a comprehensive investigation into its own flavor profile and underlying molecular characteristics (Lei et al., 2025; J. Xu et al., 2026). This study revealed flavor differences among various muscle parts of Qingyuan partridge chickens and characterized the metabolite and lipid profiles across these parts. Compared to BM, AM was characterized by sweet, fruity, and green/grassy notes, while TM exhibited a similar profile with relatively lower volatile abundance. DM displayed sweet, fruity, and woody aromas, and BAM was distinguished by a rose-like nuance in addition to sweet and green/grassy notes. These part-specific aroma profiles were associated with differential volatile accumulation patterns. Across all groups, 3(2H)-furanone, dihydro-2-methyl- exhibited the highest ROAV, suggesting its fundamental contribution to chicken flavor regardless of anatomical origin. However, due to structural complexity and the absence of representative muscle samples, this research did not examine the flavor and metabolite composition of wing muscles. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct dedicated studies on wing muscles in future research.

The central role of lipid metabolism in chicken flavor is well supported by recent studies. Glycerophospholipids and triglycerides have been identified as the major lipid sources contributing to key aroma-active volatiles in Chinese native chickens, with specific phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylcholine species releasing unsaturated fatty acids upon heating to generate aldehydes such as hexanal and (E, E)-2,4-decadienal (Y. Chen et al., 2025; Hua et al., 2025). These findings are in line with our observation that glycerophospholipid metabolism and glycerolipid metabolism were enriched across transcript, lipid, and metabolite levels in the benzaldehyde-associated module. Among volatile compounds, aldehydes consistently dominate chicken aroma profiles. A large-scale analysis of Chinese local chickens identified hexanal and 1-octen-3-ol as the main aroma compounds, with total aldehyde content ranking highest among all volatile classes (Jin et al., 2021b). Benzaldehyde has been further recognized as a distinguishing marker for chicken, discriminating it from other meats (Ahamed et al., 2023). Our interaction network extends these observations by identifying benzaldehyde as the most highly connected volatile, suggesting that it lies at the intersection of coordinated lipid and amino acid metabolism. Part-specific volatile differences have been documented across different anatomical cuts (He et al., 2024). Our integrated multi-omics data complement these findings by revealing that such sensory divergence is accompanied by coordinated transcriptomic and metabolic reprogramming, providing a molecular basis for the distinct aroma profiles. This multi-omics strategy aligns with recent recommendations for unraveling the biochemical mechanisms underlying chicken flavor (Ali et al., 2025b). However, it should be noted that our conclusions are drawn exclusively from Qingyuan partridge chickens at a single market age, and are therefore breed-specific and time-point-specific. The present study does not capture the dynamic molecular changes that may occur across different growth stages, and longitudinal studies spanning multiple developmental time points are warranted to further elucidate how these part-specific flavor profiles are established over time. Furthermore, the generalizability of these findings across other indigenous breeds, feeding regimes, and processing methods requires further investigation.

Volatile metabolomics, metabolomics, lipidomics, and transcriptomics have been widely employed as powerful tools to dissect key traits in numerous studies (Gao et al., 2025; Han et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026). Volatile metabolomics identified flavor compounds that differ among tissues and confirmed flavor variations across different chicken muscle parts. Metabolites play a crucial role in chicken meat quality (Han et al., 2025; Y. Wang et al., 2024). Metabolomic analysis revealed distinct metabolic profiles among muscles and performed correlation analyses with flavor substances, screening multiple flavor-related DEMs. Lipids significantly contribute to meat flavor formation (Lee et al., 2025; Y. Zhang et al., 2025b). Targeted lipidomic analysis characterized the lipid profiles of different muscle parts and identified multiple flavor-associated DELs. To pinpoint key molecular markers related to flavor from a multitude of substances, we conducted RNA-seq and performed correlation analysis, obtaining several flavor-linked molecular markers. However, this study still has certain limitations in the analysis of molecular regulatory mechanisms. Therefore, it is necessary to strengthen functional validation at the cellular biology level in future research.

In our integrated multi-omics analysis, benzaldehyde emerged as the most highly connected volatile metabolite in the interaction network. WGCNA further identified a co-expression module significantly associated with benzaldehyde. This module was enriched for glycerophospholipid metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, and phenylalanine metabolism. Rather than indicating direct biosynthesis, this pattern suggests an in vivo precursor programming mechanism, in which differentially expressed genes remodel membrane phospholipids and modulate phenylalanine availability prior to slaughter. During cooking, the accumulated unsaturated lipids are released and undergo thermal autoxidation to generate reactive carbonyls (BAWN, 1953), which are recognized as key promoters of the Strecker degradation of amino acids (Hidalgo and Zamora, 2016; Zhan et al., 2025). Benzaldehyde levels are known to rise during early cooking when lipid oxidation predominates (Yao et al., 2025). In parallel, differentially expressed metabolites such as malic acid link to the TCA cycle and may further influence phenylalanine flux. Lipid-derived carbonyls can attack phenylalanine, driving its Strecker-type conversion to phenylacetaldehyde and subsequent oxidation to benzaldehyde (Hidalgo et al., 2005, 2005, 2005; Hidalgo and Zamora, 2019). Collectively, these findings trace a coherent chain from differential gene expression through membrane lipid remodeling, unsaturated fatty acid release, thermal lipid oxidation, and reactive carbonyl generation, ultimately coupling with phenylalanine degradation to drive benzaldehyde accumulation. Benzaldehyde thus bridges gene-regulated lipid and amino acid metabolism with heat-driven flavor formation. This mechanistic model, while grounded in established biochemistry and supported by our statistical associations, would benefit from further substantiation, for instance through isotope labeling or controlled thermal reaction systems.

This study characterized the flavor compounds, metabolite and lipid profiles, and gene expression patterns across five muscle parts of Chinese broilers. Integrative analysis further pinpointed key substances involved in flavor formation. These findings offer valuable insights into the flavor development of this breed and serve as a reference for its geographical authentication and traceability. It should be noted that this work focused on the flavor precursor basis of raw meat and its thermal conversion potential, constituting an early step toward a more complete understanding of chicken flavor. A limitation is that the downstream link from raw material to cooked meat flavor and consumer perception was not addressed, and the flavor implications derived from the multi-omics data should therefore be considered as evidence-based hypotheses rather than definitive sensory conclusions. Future investigations should incorporate varied cooking methods, such as boiling, roasting, steaming, and frying, to clarify how raw precursor profiles interact with processing to shape the final cooked flavor, thereby enabling a more comprehensive assessment of the flavor qualities of indigenous broilers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study employed an integrated multi-omics approach to characterize flavor variations across five muscle tissues of Chinese Broiler. Network analysis identified benzaldehyde as the most highly connected volatile metabolite, and WGCNA revealed a co-expression module significantly associated with benzaldehyde content. This module was enriched for glycerophospholipid metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, and phenylalanine metabolism, linking gene-regulated lipid and amino acid metabolism to benzaldehyde accumulation through thermal lipid oxidation and Strecker degradation during cooking. Across all muscle parts, 3(2H)-furanone, dihydro-2-methyl- exhibited the highest relative odor activity value, defining it as a core contributor to chicken aroma. These findings provide a molecular framework connecting precursor metabolism with flavor formation and offers a foundation for quality evaluation and targeted breeding of indigenous chickens.
