The impact of synbiotic on serum sCD163/sTWEAK, paraoxonase 1, and lipoproteins in patients with chronic heart failure: a randomized, triple-blind, controlled trial
Authors: Shakiba Shoaei Matin, Farzad Shidfar, Nasim Naderi, Ahmad Amin, Fatemeh Sadat Hosseini-Baharanchi, Afsaneh dehnad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular disease is one of the leading causes of death worldwide. Evidence suggests that alterations in the gut microbiome could play a role in cardiovascular diseases, including heart failure. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effect of synbiotics on serum paraoxonase 1(PON1), soluble CD163/soluble TNF-like weak inducer of apoptosis (sCD163/sTWEAK), and lipid profile, which are involved in heart failure in patients with chronic heart failure. In this triple-blind randomized clinical trial, 90 eligible patients were included in the study. They were randomly assigned to receive one capsule (500 mg) of synbiotics or a placebo daily for ten weeks. Serum PON1, sCD163/sTWEAK, and lipid profiles were measured at the beginning and end of the study. The data were analyzed by SPSS 24, and the p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Among 90 patients who met the inclusion criteria, 80 completed the study. The primary outcomes showed a small effect on sTWEAK, with an adjusted standard mean difference (SMD) of 0.2. However, no significant changes were observed in sCD163/sTWEAK (SMD: 0.16). Secondary outcomes indicated no changes in PON1, total cholesterol (TC), or LDL-C levels. However, there was an increase in HDL-C levels (adjusted SMD: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.02–0.91) and a decrease in TG and TC/HDL levels (adjusted SMD: − 0.5 and − 0.3, respectively) in the synbiotic group. A favorable effect of synbiotics on sTWEAK, HDL, TG, and TC/HDL of patients with heart failure was observed, but no statistically significant effect was found on sCD163/sTWEAK, PON1, LDL, and TC factors.Keywords: Synbiotic, Heart failure, Lipid profile, Paraoxonase, sCD163/sTWEAK, CholesterolSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cardiology, Diseases

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a syndrome caused by a disorder in the ability of the heart to fill with or eject blood1. The prevalence of HF is 1–2% of the adult population in developed countries, growing to more than 10% among people over 702. The American Heart Association statistical update for 2021 estimates the prevalence of HF at 6 million, which is 1.8% of the total US population3. Heart failure involves diverse pathological processes reflected in bloodstream biomarkers, prompting research to enhance screening and early diagnosis. Regulating these biomarker concentrations is vital for prognosis and treatment strategies4.

PON-1 is an HDL-associated protein with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Oxidative stress is known as one of the pathological factors of heart failure5. The results of a cohort study of patients with heart failure showed that decreased PON1 activity led to more long-term side effects, including death, myocardial infarction, and stroke6. Reduced serum PON1 concentrations in patients with CHD indicate a potential diagnostic marker for increased risk and adverse outcomes in chronic heart failure6–8. HDL is synthesized in the gut, where probiotics are highly functional. The gut also produces and releases PON1 into circulation. Low HDL is believed to be a metabolic syndrome component in heart failure patients, and higher HDL levels are linked to better chances of survival in heart failure9–12.

Inflammatory cytokines and their receptors play a key role in heart regeneration and the progression of heart failure13. One of the most important inflammatory cytokines studied thus far is tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and members of the interleukin family14,15. TNF-like weak inducer of apoptosis (TWEAK) is a multifunctional cytokine from the TNF family that regulates cell growth, migration, and survival16. TWEAK, as a membrane protein that is released into the bloodstream and called soluble TWEAK (sTWEAK), has angiogenic properties and is known to induce myoblast differentiation and tumor apoptosis17–19. In patients with heart failure, unlike other cytokines, sTWEAK decreases and is associated with dysfunction and risk of death in patients20. The decrease in serum sTWEAK levels in heart failure may result from its binding to the Fn14 receptor, which increases under pathological conditions, leading to decreased sTWEAK levels in circulation and heightened inflammation. Moreover, sTWEAK levels may decrease due to clearance by the CD163 scavenger receptor, a membrane protein with a soluble form (sCD163) measurable in serum, reflecting membrane-bound CD163 stores and expressed by monocytes and macrophages. This process may occur rapidly during inflammation or macrophage activation. The sCD163/sTWEAK ratio increases in cardiovascular disease and is a superior marker for cardiovascular mortality compared to either molecule alone21,22. It has been suggested that lower levels of sTWEAK in the plasma are associated with a poor prognosis, independent of established risk markers such as NT-proBNP20.

Studies show changes in gut microbiota among HF patients, which can cause inflammation involved in the pathophysiology of HF. Probiotics and synbiotics may help prevent cardiovascular diseases and heart failure by altering gut microbes23. Probiotic supplements may help control cardiovascular risk factors like hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and inflammation24. Probiotics reduce inflammation by repairing the gut barrier, lowering proinflammatory signals, increasing short-chain fatty acid production, and enhancing immune responses. Certain bacteria also induce T cell apoptosis, modulate the NF-κb pathway, and increase anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL10) while decreasing proinflammatory markers (IL-6) and (TNF-α)25,26. To our knowledge, no research has explored the impact of synbiotics on sCD163/sTWEAK, Lipid profile, and PON1 levels in HF patients. Our primary outcome is sCD163/sTWEAK levels, while secondary outcomes include PON1 and lipid profiles. We hypothesized that synbiotics could reduce the serum ratio of sCD163/sTWEAK, increase PON1, and improve lipid profile in these patients. These findings could offer new insights and potential strategies for improving HF management and outcomes and revolutionize how we approach heart failure treatment.

Method

Participants

2. The participants were selected from heart failure patients referred to Shahid Rajaie Hospital, Tehran, Iran, between October 2019 and August 2020. All items included in the checklist of Extending the CONSORT Statement to Randomized Trials of Nonpharmacologic Treatment were fulfilled. The eligibility criteria were: heart failure patients aged between 30 and 70 years with LVEF < 40 (left ventricular ejection fraction), who had been approved by a cardiologist and had received standard treatments for heart failure for at least three months, and the medications had reached the maximum tolerable and constant dose; patients were in one of I–III stages of the New York Heart Association classifications (NYHA). Exclusion criteria were considered as follows: being reluctant to cooperate, having other diseases, including chronic and acute liver disorders (hepatitis B, C, etc.), diabetes, thyroid disorders, severe renal impairment (creatinine ≥ 300 mmol/l), lung diseases, inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, cancer, and acute infections, smoking, taking any nutritional supplements within the last two months, taking corticosteroids within the last four weeks, taking antibiotics in the last three months, taking anti-inflammatory drugs except low-dose aspirin (80 mg daily), having a history of gastrointestinal surgery and rheumatic heart disease, having prosthetic heart valve, pregnancy, and lactation, insulin injection, BMI > 30, consuming less than 80% of supplements during the study period, and having changes in the type and dose of heart failure medication.

Ethical aspects

This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Iran University of Medical Sciences (approval no. IR.IUMS.REC.1398.1329, IR.IUMS.REC.1399.707, IR.IUMS.REC.1398.1330) and was registered in the Iranian Clinical Trials Register on 04/11/2020 (registration no. IRCT20091114002709N55). We confirm that all research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent forms and signatures were received from all the participants before any intervention. The participants were told that they could withdraw from the study at any stage of the project if they did not wish to complete the project and were assured that their information would remain confidential.

Study design

This study was performed as a randomized, triple-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group clinical trial in patients with heart failure. The participants entered the run-in period and did not consume any probiotic products or yogurt for two weeks before the beginning of the study. During the study, patients were asked not to consume yogurt, probiotic products, or nutritional supplements. They were also asked to maintain their regular life, including physical activity and diet. The participants were randomly divided into two groups. The experimental group (n = 45) received one synbiotic capsule (each capsule = 0.5g), and the control group (n = 45) received one placebo capsule (each capsule = 0.5g) per day for ten weeks. They were asked to keep the supplements in the refrigerator. The patients’ compliance was checked by telephone, and they were requested to bring their capsule boxes on the last visit.

Sample size

For each of the study outcomes, and according to the available sources, the sample size for each group was calculated, leading to four people in each group based on the sCD163 variable. Four people were not enough to perform the study, and since this is a supplementary article, the sample size was calculated and reported according to two secondary outcomes, and the highest number (39 samples according to diastolic blood pressure) was determined as the sample size. A sample size of 45 patients in each of the two study groups, with a dropout rate of up to 15%, a power of 80%, and a 5% probability of type 1 error, was planned to detect a significant reduction in diastolic blood pressure in the synbiotic group, compared with the placebo group after eight weeks, at the end of the study27.

Randomization

The patients were randomly assigned through permuted blocked randomization, size of 4, to receive either synbiotic or placebo using a list generated by R Package blockrand. The sealed sequentially numbered capsules were provided. The patients’ adherence was followed by routine calls and counting the returned capsules every session. Trial personnel and the patients were blinded. The outcome assessor and data analyst were also blinded.

Intervention

Participants were randomly divided into two groups. One group (n = 45) received one synbiotic capsule (0.5 g) which contained Lactobacillus casei (109 CFU), Lactobacillus acidophilus (1.5 × 1010 CFU), Lactobacillus rhamnosus (3.5 × 109 CFU), Lactobacillus bulgaricus (2.5 × 108 CFU), Bifidobacterium breve (1010 CFU), Bifidobacterium longum (5 × 108 CFU), Streptotus thermophilus (1.5 × 108 CFU), and excipients including fructooligosaccharide (38.5 mg), lactose, magnesium stearate, talc, and silicon dioxide. The other group (n = 45) received one placebo capsule (0.5g) containing lactose, magnesium stearate, talc, and silicon dioxide daily for ten weeks. Synbiotic and placebo capsules were produced by Zist-takhmir Company. In a study examining the effect of the probiotic Saccharomyces bulardii on patients with heart failure, it was found that the proposed treatment with probiotics was safe and well tolerated, and there were no reports of side effects in patients28. The synbiotic and placebo capsules were the same in appearance, odor, weight, and packaging, and only the code on the package (A or B) was different. Patients were asked to store synbiotic capsules in the refrigerator.

Assessments

Anthropometric measures

The participants’ weight with minimum coverage and without shoes was measured using Seca scales with an accuracy of 100 g, and the standing height was measured without shoes by a gauge with an accuracy of 0.5 cm at the beginning and end of the study. BMI was calculated by using the formula (BMI = weight/height2). For most adults, an ideal BMI is in the range of 18.5–24.9 (kg/m2). BMI scores of 25–29.9 (kg/m2) are considered overweight. Normal and overweight BMI were included in the present study.

Dietary intake and physical activity

Dietary information was collected at the beginning of the study and the end of the 10th week by using 24-h food recall for three days (2 non-holidays and one weekend). The usual energy intake, macronutrients, and micronutrients were calculated using Nutritionist IV software. The amount of physical activity was assessed using the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ)29 at the beginning and end of the study. The metabolic equivalent was defined as the sum of all physical activities per minute during the week.

Biochemical assessment

To measure blood factors, venous blood samples were taken from patients at the beginning of the study and the end of the 10th week after 10–12 h of fasting by an expert laboratory technician. Blood samples were stored at − 80 °C during the study. The concentrations of sCD163, sTWEAK, and PON1 were measured using the ELISA kit (Crystal Day Biotech Co. in Shanghai, China). The kit employs a double-antibody sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with a sensitivity of 5.51 ng/ml for sTWEAK, 0.11 ng/ml for sCD163, and 0.15 ng/ml for PON1. The photometric method was used to determine the serum lipid profile levels.

Statistical analysis

Mean (standard deviation) was used to describe quantitative data. Frequency (%) was used for qualitative data. T-test and chi-square test were used to compare baseline qualitative and quantitative measures, respectively. The mean difference (MD) (%95 confidence interval) for outcomes was calculated for the synbiotic versus placebo group. Standardized mean difference (SMD) (95% confidence interval) was calculated as the effect size, which is interpreted as no effect (0 < SMD < 0.2), small effect (0.2 ≤ SMD < 0.5), moderate effect (0.5 ≤ SMD < 0.8), and large effect (SMD ≥ 0.8)30. Normality assumption was investigated using Q-Q plot, skewness and kurtosis indices, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. All the outcomes were normal or near normal. Adjusted MD and SMD were provided by ANCOVA to estimate the treatment efficacy and control for confounders, including baseline measurement, BMI change, and sex because BMI change was significant between groups. Moreover, the frequency of sex was more than 10% different between the groups. Intention-to-treat analyses were performed. Multiple imputation was used for missing values. Analyses were performed using SPSS 26. The significance level was set to 0.05.

Results

Ninety patients who met the inclusion criteria were randomly assigned to the synbiotic and placebo groups. Four patients were excluded from the synbiotic group and six patients from the placebo group, and then eighty patients were analyzed27.

There were no significant differences between the characteristics of the two groups at the start of the study, such as age, BMI, ejection fraction, and medications. BMI change was highly significant between groups (Synbiotic: − 0.41 ± 0.81 vs. placebo: 0.53 ± 0.66, P < 0.001)27.

No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding energy intake, macronutrients, and micronutrients27.

Regarding sCD163, the adjusted MD (95% CI) was 0.5 (− 0.92, 1.9), with an adjusted SMD of 0.17 (− 0.27, 0.61). Similarly, the study found that the adjusted MD for sCD/sTWEAK was 0.001, with a confidence interval ranging from -0.001 to 0.002 and an adjusted SMD of 0.16 (− 0.28 to 0.60). Secondary outcomes

No significant changes were observed regarding Paraxonase1, TC, and LDL-C. The adjusted SMD was 0.17 (− 0.26, 0.61) for Paraxonase1 and − 0.13 (− 0.57, 0.30) for TC. LDL-C initially showed a standardized mean difference (SMD) of − 0.3 (95% CI: − 0.78 to 0.09), which was − 0.02 (95% CI: − 0.46, 0.41) after adjustment. Importantly, the MD for LDL-C changed from − 14.28 mg/dl to an adjusted MD of − 0.55, indicating the impact of the adjustment on the observed values. Moreover, TC/HDL showed an adjusted SMD of − 0.30 (95% CI: − 0.74 to 0.13), while HDL-C showed an adjusted SMD of 0.46 (95% CI: 0.02 to 0.91), indicating a favorable change in HDL-C levels. TG had a significant decrease in the intervention group compared to the placebo group, with an adjusted SMD of − 0.50 (95% CI: − 0.94 to − 0.053), highlighting the potential effectiveness of the intervention in lowering TG levels (Table 2).

Discussion

According to the results, consuming synbiotics for ten weeks has a small effect on sTWEAK values and the number needed to treat (NNT) of 9, indicating that one patient from nine who used synbiotics experienced an increase in sTWEAK higher than average (SMD: 0.2; NNT:9). This study is the first to investigate the impact of synbiotic consumption on serum sTWEAK levels. A recent study comparing probiotic yogurt with regular yogurt in CHF patients found a significant increase in sTWEAK levels after 10 weeks of probiotic yogurt, suggesting a desirable effect in reducing heart failure risk31. No substantial changes in sCD163 values were observed in our study after the intervention (adjusted SMD: 0.17). Additionally, our research is the first to investigate the impact of synbiotic consumption on serum sCD163 levels. In a study on probiotic yogurt, in line with our study, no statistically significant changes were observed between groups31. The shorter duration of the intervention compared to the studies reporting the effect of probiotics on sCD163 has been mentioned as the reason for the lack of favorable results32,33, and the use of a higher dose of probiotics (more than 109CFU d−1) has been suggested for better results. No effect of 10-week synbiotic supplementation on the sCD163/sTWEAK ratio was observed in our study (adjusted SMD: 0.16, 95% CI: − 0.28, 0.6). The wide confidence interval suggests that a longer duration of the intervention (more than 3 months) may have a significant effect on this factor. The substantial contrast between the raw and adjusted numbers for sCD163 and sCD163/sTWEAK ratio underscores the critical influence of confounding factors in our analysis. This indicates that at least one of the confounding factors considered in our adjustments is likely to be significant. It has been observed that decreased sTWEAK concentration and increased sCD163/sTWEAK ratio were significantly and independently associated with long-term cardiovascular mortality20,21,34. The NYHA functional class is significantly correlated with sTWEAK plasma levels, and a significant decrease in sTWEAK has been observed with increasing NYHA class20. Based on the findings mentioned above, it is suggested that further studies be performed in this field with different types of probiotics and prebiotics for a longer period.

At the end of the study, no significant difference in PON1 levels was observed between the synbiotic and control groups. From eleven patients who received synbiotics, one patient experienced an increase in PON1 levels higher than the average (SMD: 0.17; NNT:11). Ahmadian et al.35 reported no significant impact on total oxidant and paraoxonase status due to probiotics consumption in type 2 diabetic patients after six weeks which was consistent with the results of our study (SMD: − 0.103; 95% CI: − 0.613, 0.408). The results of a study suggest that PON1 levels may influence HF, and a significant relationship between the antioxidant activity of PON-1 and the NYHA classification for HF was reported36. Following the intervention in the current study, a weak grade of efficacy on the HDL value was noticed (SMD: 0.46; 95% CI: 0.02, 0.91; NNT: 4). In line with our study, Shakeri et al.37 reported a slight increase in serum HDL-C levels, with one out of every four patients experiencing a greater than average rise in HDL levels (SMD: 0.46; NNT:4). A study found that HDL-C levels were inversely correlated with Hs-CRP levels and NYHA classification38. Increasing 1 mg/dl in HDL cholesterol can reduce mortality by 1%10. Therefore, the observed 2.75 mg/dL increase in HDL-C in this study has significant clinical and public health implications. Medium-grade effect has been observed in terms of TG and Out of every four patients who received synbiotics compared with a placebo, one patient experienced a reduction in TG levels lower than the average (adjusted SMD: -0.5; 95% CI: -0.94, -0.053; NNT: 4). Comparing the reduction in triglycerides to the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) for this variable reveals that this reduction is clinically significant (MCID: 2 mmol/L). The study showed no reductions in LDL and TC levels following intervention after adjustment (SMD: -0.02 and -0.13, respectively). A meta-analysis found that probiotics in fermented dairy products significantly reduced total cholesterol and LDL, while probiotics in capsule form did not show significant reductions39. In contrast with the results of our study, one meta-analysis demonstrates that synbiotic supplementation significantly affects TC [WMD (95% CI) = − 8.43 (− 11.90 to − 4.95) mg/dL, and LDL [WMD (95% CI) = − 5.08 (− 7.61, − 2.56) mg/dL, P-value < 0.001]. The analysis further indicates that RCTs involving patients with hypercholesterolemia or hypertriglyceridemia showed the most reduction in TC and LDL levels. RCTs with Type 2 diabetes and prediabetes patients also demonstrated significant decreases in cholesterol, while no significant changes were observed in other patient groups. Interestingly, lower doses of probiotics (< 1 × 1010CFU/day) were found to be more effective in lowering serum total cholesterol levels compared to higher doses (more than 1 × 1010 CFU/day)40. Theoretically, lowering TC and LDL-C levels reduces the prevalence of cardiovascular disease, and clinical trials have shown that it may also improve outcomes in patients with heart failure39. In contrast, some observational studies have shown that higher TC and triglyceride levels are associated with longer life expectancy in heart failure patients; however, the mechanisms remain unclear and require further research10,41. It has been suggested that outcome benefits associated with a higher baseline TC level may be attributed to its nutritional performance and energy resources42–44. In the present study, the data shows that synbiotic supplementation had a small effect on TC/HDL-C levels after adjustment and From every six patients who received synbiotics compared with placebo, one patient experienced a reduction in TC/HDL-C ratio lower than average (aSMD: − 0.3, NNT: 6). In Nabhani et al.'s study on pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) for six weeks, they found no significant impact on the TC/HDL-C ratio. This could be attributed to using different probiotic strains, doses, and durations (SMD: 0.12; NNT: 15)45. Some studies have suggested that the TC/HDL-C ratio is the most significant predictor of ischemic heart disease (IHD)46. Synbiotic administration can control lipid profiles through various mechanisms, including assimilation of cholesterol by probiotics, reduced cholesterol uptake in the small intestine, and conversion of cholesterol to coprostanol. Probiotics also produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), which promote lower cholesterol and improved lipid metabolism47. A systematic review found that synbiotic supplements have a stronger impact on lipid profiles in diabetic patients compared to synbiotic foods48. ITT analysis indicated that lost-to-follow-up cases were missing at random. Thus, the results were reliable and not affected by potential biases from missing data (Table 1).

The key strength of the present is that this is the first study evaluating the effect of synbiotics in patients with heart failure. Moreover, the patients were selected in a way that ensured no significant difference between the characteristics of the two groups before the intervention, indicating very accurate randomization. Due to these distinctive features of the study, we believe that the results of our study are reliable. However, there are some limitations to the present study. Due to financial issues, we could not analyze the intestinal microbiome before, during, and after synbiotic consumption to assess its composition changes after a 10-week intake of synbiotics.

Conclusion

The use of synbiotics had a slight effect on the sTWEAK value but did not significantly affect the sCD163/sTWEAK ratio. Additionally, synbiotics had a beneficial impact on lipid profiles in patients with heart failure. HDL levels increased, while TG and TC/HDL levels decreased; however, there were no significant effects on LDL, TC, and paraoxinase1 levels. This is the first clinical study to assess the impact of synbiotics on patients with chronic heart failure. Further clinical studies are recommended to investigate paraoxinase1, sTWEAK, and sCD163 levels in these patients over a longer intervention period and with a larger sample size.
