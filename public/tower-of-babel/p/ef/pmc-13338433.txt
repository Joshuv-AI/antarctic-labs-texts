Physics-informed deep learning-based adaptive beamforming for phased array weather radar
Authors: Tarek Sallam, Ahmed M. Attiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study introduces a beamforming framework based on physics-informed deep-learning for phased array weather radar systems, with particular emphasis on configurations involving a large number of antenna elements. The proposed approach is designed to achieve both high computational efficiency and reliable weather target detection. Physical constraints governing array behavior are explicitly embedded into the learning process by means of a tailored loss function that minimizes the mismatch between the predicted array output and a predefined reference response. To assess the effectiveness of the physics-informed deep neural network (PIDNN) beamformer, its performance is evaluated against two classical neural network architectures commonly used in the computer science domain and also represent two different learning-based alternatives: a shallow radial basis function neural network (RBFNN) and a deeper convolutional neural network (CNN). Simulation results demonstrate that the PIDNN consistently outperforms these models in terms of beamforming accuracy and computational time. Furthermore, the practical applicability of the proposed method is verified using real-world measurements acquired from the phased array weather radar operated at Osaka University (PAR@OU). The experimental results confirm that the PIDNN enables rapid and accurate reflectivity estimation while exhibiting superior clutter suppression compared with the other methods.

Introduction

In recent years, phased array radar technology has played an essential role in the timely and reliable observation of atmospheric processes, due to its capability to support rapid and accurate weather sensing1–5. In contrast to conventional mechanically steered radars, phased array radars (PARs) employ electronic beam steering together with adaptive digital beamforming (DBF), enabling the acquisition of radar measurements at significantly higher temporal resolution and with enhanced measurement precision6–10.

The phased array radar at Osaka University (PAR@OU)11 is equipped with electronic scanning in elevation and adaptive DBF, while the antenna array is mechanically rotated in azimuth. The system transmits a fan-shaped beam, and subsequently receives the backscattered signals across 128 array elements. A major challenge associated with fan beam transmission is the presence of high sidelobes that amplify clutter signals. As a result, measurements obtained from PAR systems are often significantly affected by elevated ground-clutter power12.

Traditional Fourier beamforming (FB) determines the excitation weights by computing the phase shifts between array elements, enabling electronic steering of the beam toward the desired directions. In weather radar applications, the Capon (CP) beamforming approach has been widely employed13,14. However, CP requires a sufficiently a lot of pulses to produce reliable results, and its performance degrades when applied to PAR systems with as few as 16 pulses. To address this limitation, an iterative procedure of the minimum mean square error (MMSE) beamformer has been proposed15. While MMSE can achieve sidelobe suppression with fewer pulses, it exhibits convergence issues at small elevation angles, leading to poor performance, particularly when applied to real measurement data16.

In16, a radial basis function neural network (RBFNN) is employed to determine the weight vector for PAR beamforming, demonstrating a robust and computationally efficient approach using both the simulated and experimental data. The network achieved a good performance regardless of the number of pulses, outperforming conventional beamforming techniques. Nevertheless, due to its shallow architecture and the restricted number of hidden layers, the generalization capability of the network is constrained, particularly when applied to large antenna arrays17–19.

In contrast, deep learning–based approaches can model complex nonlinear relationships more effectively20. For instance, a deep-learning neural network was developed in21 to perform an adaptive DBF; however, it was trained using true CP weights, which poses limitations since CP performs poorly when only a few pulses are available, such as 16 pulses. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), a specialized form of deep architectures, have demonstrated strong capabilities in capturing spatial patterns and learning high-dimensional representations from complex data22–25, and have attracted significant attention in radar meteorology26,27. In28, a CNN was successfully applied as fast adaptive beamformer for PARs using both simulated and real measurements. Despite these successes, CNN-based methods remain heavily dependent on large labeled datasets, which in antenna and array processing applications typically require extensive simulations or computationally intensive electromagnetic solvers, resulting in substantial time and computational costs29.

To address the limitations inherent in conventional data-driven neural network approaches, this work proposes a physics-informed deep neural network (PIDNN) for adaptive beamforming in weather PAR systems, validated using both simulated and real data. In contrast to methods that depend on extensive labeled datasets, the PIDNN embeds governing physical principles directly into the learning process through a customized loss function30. Physics-informed neural networks have been extensively employed to solve a wide range of physics-based problems and partial differential equations31. Recently, the PIDNNs has been proposed for various electromagnetics applications32–36. Moreover, a PIDNN has been recently applied to the 2D adaptive beamforming of phased array antennas37. However, to the best of the author’s knowledge, its application to adaptive beamforming for PAR systems has not been previously reported. Thus, concerning the novelty of this work, this is the first time that a PIDNN is applied to the adaptive beamforming of PARs.

In the present study, the PIDNN’s loss function is formulated as the mean squared error between the predicted array response and a predefined reference signal. By minimizing this loss function, the network is able to infer near-optimal excitation weights without the need for prior labeled training data. In simulation, the PIDNN is evaluated using a large-scale 32 × 32 planar array antenna and demonstrates an excellent performance in estimating both point and distributed targets when compared with the RBFNN and CNN approaches, in terms of accuracy as well as computational efficiency. Furthermore, when applied to real measurements acquired by the PAR@OU system, the proposed PIDNN achieves more accurate reflectivity estimation with reduced clutter contamination and shorter processing time relative to the other methods.

Methodology

The Wiener weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{Wiener}}$$\end{document} defined in (12) is not appropriate for direct real-time implementation due to its computational complexity. To mitigate this issue, the Wiener solution can be approximated using a carefully designed neural network. In this framework, the array observations are first preprocessed, after which the corresponding autocorrelation matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{R}}_{x}\:$$\end{document}is provided as input to the network. The network then outputs an estimated weight vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}_{\mathrm{Wiener}}$$\end{document}, which serves as an efficient approximation of the original Wiener solution.

PIDNN beamformer

The proposed PIDNN acts as an array synthesizer. In classical array synthesis, the target radiation pattern is specified a priori, while the corresponding element excitation coefficients are unknown and must be determined. In contrast to conventional data-driven neural networks that require extensive labeled datasets to learn complex nonlinear mappings, the PIDNN operates using a single unlabeled input sample, namely the feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{z}$$\end{document}, without requiring predefined excitation labels. During an optimization process, the network estimates the excitation vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{w}$$\end{document}. By enforcing consistency between the synthesized array output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:y\:$$\end{document}and a reference signal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:d$$\end{document}, the PIDNN implicitly learns excitation weights that satisfy the physical constraints encoded in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{z},$$\end{document} thereby accomplishing the beamforming objective. Consequently, the loss function Loss defined in (11) is formulated as the mismatch between the array response and the reference signal.

From this perspective, the PIDNN may be viewed as a neural network-based optimizer that iteratively updates its internal parameters to minimize the loss function, thereby indirectly determining the optimal array excitation coefficients. Consequently, the proposed PIDNN framework differentiates itself from existing approaches in that its training (optimization) process does not require any external labeled dataset; instead, it is driven solely by an embedded physics-informed objective, defined as the mean squared error between the synthesized array response and the reference signal (which is the loss function in our case). In short, the PIDNN basically does not need a training. Meaning that it is an online optimization process rather than a training procedure.

The proposed PIDNN comprises an input layer with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N\left(N-1\right)\:$$\end{document}neurons, followed by four hidden layers, each containing 300 units, and an output layer with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:2N$$\end{document}neurons corresponding to the real and imaginary components of the excitation weights. The hyperbolic tangent (Tanh) function is used as the activation function throughout the network. Parameter updates are performed using the Adam optimizer39, with an initial learning rate of 0.01 that gradually decays at a rate of 0.0005 during the optimization process. Since the training relies on a single input sample, the batch size is set to one. The optimization is terminated when the Loss function defined in (11) decreases below 0.01.

Estimation of point targets

In this study, point targets are reconstructed and compared against their corresponding ground-truth values. The CNN and RBFNN models are trained using point-target data spanning elevation angles from − 30° to 90° in increments of 0.5°, with the mean received power randomly sampled between 0 and 60 dB. All training targets share a mean Doppler velocity of 9 m/s and are located within the same azimuth and range bin. For performance evaluation, all beamforming methods are applied to a test scenario involving two point targets positioned at elevation angles of 30° and 35°, respectively, within the same range bin and azimuth. The corresponding mean received powers are set to 20 dB and 50 dB, with mean Doppler velocities of 8 m/s and 10 m/s, respectively.

Figure 2 illustrates convergence characteristics of the PIDNN by depicting the loss function defined in (11) as a function of the iteration for the two point-target scenarios. As observed, the proposed model converges rapidly, reaching convergence in fewer than 75 iterations and within approximately 1 s for both targets. The final Loss values achieved by the PIDNN are 0.004 and 0.009 for the left and right targets, respectively.

Conclusion

This paper has introduced a PIDNN for adaptive beamforming in phased array weather radar systems. The proposed approach was evaluated using both point and distributed target scenarios. Its performance was first assessed through simulations on a large-scale array operating with a limited number of pulses, where the results demonstrate that the PIDNN effectively suppresses sidelobes and accurately estimates signal power. In comparison with conventional data-driven neural network methods, namely the RBFNN and CNN, the PIDNN consistently exhibits superior beamforming performance. In addition, the PIDNN requires significantly less execution time than the RBFNN and CNN approaches.

The effectiveness of the proposed method was further verified using real measurement data acquired by the PAR@OU system. The results confirm that the PIDNN is well suited for real-time beamforming in operational phased array weather radars, achieving reflectivity estimates with reduced clutter and an execution time of less than one second per elevation angle. Unlike traditional neural network approaches that rely on large labeled training datasets, the PIDNN operates without such data requirements. Instead, its optimization process is guided exclusively by a physics-informed loss function defined by the mismatch between the array response and a reference signal. These findings demonstrate the capability of the PIDNN to enable accurate and efficient a real-time adaptive beamforming for PAR applications. As a potential application, the PIDNN approach can be extended to pulse compression weather radars for ranging with high resolution41. Moreover, in the current study, the real-world validation focuses on a single rainfall event, however, the proposed approach can be easily extended to multiple weather scenarios. Future work will address these issues beside comparisons with more recent deep learning architectures (e.g., transformers or hybrid models).
