Introduction

Amblyopia, a common visual disorder and a source of significant distress, often manifests as reduced visual acuity in one or both eyes [1]. It is a neuro-developmental disorder, caused by abnormal visual input from one or both eyes during the critical period of visual development in early childhood [2].

For over 200 years, monocular occlusion has been the cornerstone of treatment. While effective, this approach has several potential disadvantages, which include stigmatization and self-consciousness, discomfort, and inconvenience to patients and their caregivers. Moreover, improvements in visual acuity with monocular therapy are not always paired with improvement in other visual functions, such as stereoacuity and contrast sensitivity [3].

With advancements in amblyopia research, the current understanding is that amblyopia is a binocular rather than monocular disease. During the critical period for visual development, an abnormal visual experience is thought to lead to binocular dysfunction and interocular suppression at the level of the visual cortex. Not only can this negatively impact monocular functions, such as the best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of the affected eye, but also binocular functions, including stereoacuity, which require the integration of input from both eyes at the level of the visual cortex [2]. Furthermore, a direct relationship has been appreciated between the degree of interocular suppression and the degree of visual acuity deficit, further strengthening the argument that poor visual acuity in amblyopia is secondary to a primary binocular deficit. Hence, it may be postulated that addressing interocular suppression and improving binocular function would be key in the management of amblyopia [4].

The need to effectively target interocular suppression has driven a surge in research on novel binocular approaches to treatment. With binocular therapy, both eyes work simultaneously, but the eye affected by amblyopia is encouraged to assume the primary role in performing a specific visual task [5]. Figure 1 illustrates the dichoptic approach, where dichoptic viewing conditions are created so that each eye views complementary components of a single image, and interocular contrast is balanced in a way that counters the suppression of input from the affected eye and promotes binocular fusion [4]. This type of therapy is highly versatile, being offered in many forms, including video games, movies viewed through anaglyph glasses, and virtual reality headsets [6].

The primary objective of this systematic review is to evaluate the effect of binocular therapy versus standard therapy (patching or atropine eye drops) on best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), adherence, and patient or caregiver reported outcomes among children (9 years and younger) with amblyopia. Secondary outcomes include change in other visual outcomes (e.g., stereoacuity and contrast sensitivity), reported barriers to treatment, reported side effects, and sustained improvement in BCVA on follow up. This study aims to systematically review the literature and shed light on outcomes of importance to patients, their caregivers, and their healthcare providers, inspiring patient-centered, evidence-based discussions and informed decision-making.

Objectives

This review aims to evaluate the effects of binocular therapy for amblyopia versus standard therapy (patching or atropine eye drops) on BCVA, adherence to treatment, and patient or caregiver reported outcomes among children (9 years or younger). Methods

This systematic review protocol adheres to the Preferred reporting items for systematic review and meta-analysis protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 [7] [see Additional file 1] and follows the best practices outlined by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [8]. The protocol has been registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO CRD42024502274) [9]. The current stage of the review at the time of protocol submission is detailed in Appendix A. Updates made to the PROSPERO record are documented in the online record and are described in Appendix B.

Search methods

Information sources

The databases that will be searched include: Public MEDLINE (PubMed), Excerpta Medica Database (EMBASE), and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). Trial registries that will be searched include ClinicalTrials.gov and World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP). These sources have been searched from inception to January 2024, and searches are planned to be rerun to include data up to March 2026, as more than 12 months have elapsed since the initial search. Additionally, references and citation lists of eligible trials and relevant reviews will be searched. Study investigators will be contacted for missing or unpublished data.

Search strategy

The search strategy includes terms related to the condition, intervention, and study design. Relevant articles will be selected according to pre-set eligibility criteria. Keywords related to outcomes and comparisons will not be included in the search strategy to avoid the exclusion of any relevant studies. Only English articles will be included in the full-text screening, although no language filter will be set. There are no restrictions on the publication period date (though date limitations will be included in the search update to avoid duplication of efforts). The search strategies for each of the databases and registries are presented in Appendix C.

Data collection and analysis

Data management

Search results, including titles, abstract, publication year, and authors will be uploaded to Rayyan [14]. Duplicate results will be removed and reports from the same study will be identified (e.g., by registration number) and collated.

Selection process

Two blinded reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts using Rayyan [14] to identify studies that may meet eligibility criteria. These articles will then be subjected to full-text screening to confirm eligibility by two blinded, independent reviewers. Disagreements in both the initial and full-text screening stages will be resolved by discussion and/or a third reviewer.

Data extraction

Two independent authors will use a standardized data extraction form (made using Microsoft Excel) to collect data related to study identification, methodology, and outcome information. Risk of bias information

Risk of bias information

Risk of bias in individual studies

Two independent reviewers will individually assess the quality of included studies using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB-2) [15]. Discrepancies will be resolved by discussion, and/or by a third reviewer. Cochrane’s visualization tool for risk of bias assessments in a systematic reviews (Robvis) [16] will be used to display findings. Sensitivity analysis will be done, excluding studies at higher risk of bias, to assess the impact on the measure of effect. Note: Risk of bias will only be assessed for primary outcomes.

Measures of treatment effect

Difference in means will be used for continuous data measured in the same units, and standardized mean difference will be used for continuous data measured by different but combinable scales (e.g., patient satisfaction assessed by two validated and correlating scales). For “change from baseline” outcomes, if some studies only measure post-interventional data, they may be combined with mean change in score data, and difference in means may be used [11]. For proportions (e.g., adherence), difference in proportions will be used. For dichotomous data, relative risk, relative rate, or odds ratios may be used. Ordinal data may be treated as continuous data or collapsed into binary data. All effect measures will be presented with 95% confidence intervals. For side effects and barriers, we expect rates and counts of rare events (dichotomous). In this case, Mantel-Haenszel method or Peto odds ratio may be used if appropriate [11].

Assessment for meta-biases

Publication bias risk will be assessed using a funnel plot if at least 10 studies are included. If protocols/trial registrations are available, studies will be evaluated for selective reporting of outcomes. Also, it will be noted whether these protocols/trial registrations were published prior to or after recruitment of study participants.

Data synthesis

Quantitative synthesis, using Cochrane’s Review Manager Cochrane’s Review Manager (RevMan Version 9.6.0, or the most updated version at the time of data synthesis), will only be performed if two or more studies assessing similar interventions and comparisons are sufficiently homogeneous to be combined. Outcomes will be combined using standard pairwise meta-analysis, using random-effects model for significant heterogeneity, and presented with 95% confidence intervals.

Qualitative synthesis will be presented, including details of included studies, inclusion and exclusion criteria, participant sociodemographic and baseline characteristics, interventions and comparisons, strengths and limitations, and potential sources of bias. Additionally, in case a meta-analysis cannot be performed, a visual display method, such as tabulated data or forest plots without summary estimates may be appropriate [20]. Analysis will follow the guidelines presented in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, especially the chapters corresponding to qualitative [21] and quantitative [11] synthesis.

Heterogeneity assessment

For clinical heterogeneity, the differences between patients, interventions, and outcomes in compared groups will be considered. For methodological heterogeneity, differences in design and internal validity of studies will be considered. Finally, statistical heterogeneity will be assessed visually, where forest plots showing large studies with wide, non-overlapping confidence intervals are concerning for heterogeneity, and statistically, where an I2 statistic greater than 50% or Q statistic less than 0.05 are concerning for heterogeneity.

Confidence in cumulative evidence

Summary of findings will be presented for primary outcomes, and the contributing body of evidence will be assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) working group methodology [23], using GRADEpro Software [24]. Two blinded reviewers will independently assess the overall certainty of evidence for each of the primary outcomes, and a consensus will be achieved by discussion and/or by involving a third reviewer. The Summary of Findings table will include the primary outcomes of this review, assessed at 2–4 weeks, 4–8 weeks, 8–12 weeks, and 12–16 weeks from treatment initiation (or the grouping strategy used for meta-analysis). The quality of evidence will be characterized as high, moderate, low, or very low. Only RCTs will be included in this study, and certainty of evidence may be lowered by the following GRADE domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and dissemination bias. Certainty of evidence may be raised by the following GRADE domains: large effect, dose-response gradient, and opposite residual confounding.

Discussion

Amblyopia is a relatively common disorder that can impact children and their caregivers, with effects possibly extending into adulthood. Given the gravity of this condition, the advancements in the understanding of its pathogenesis, and the need for more comprehensive treatment methods, there has been a surge in research on binocular therapy in the treatment of amblyopia. This review will provide a comprehensive synthesis on the use of binocular therapy to treat amblyopia in children of 9 years and younger, looking at change in BCVA, adherence to treatment, and patient or caregiver reported outcomes. Other outcomes of interest include changes in other visual outcomes (e.g., stereoacuity and contrast sensitivity), reported barriers to treatment, reported side effects, and sustained improvement in BCVA at follow-up.

Methodological strengths

This protocol adheres to the PRISMA-P 2015 guidelines [7] and aligns closely to the best practices outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [8]. One strength of the protocol is that multiple information sources are searched, including 3 databases and 2 clinical registries, in addition to screening reference and citation lists. Furthermore, the search strategy follows the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) guidelines [25] and does not include outcomes or comparisons, enhancing its sensitivity. The strategy also employs published screening filters developed specifically for RCT [26] and trial [27] searches. To make sure results remain relevant, an update to the search is planned prior to final data extraction and analysis. Apart from this, another strength of the protocol lies in the plan to use validated tools, including RoB-2 [15] for risk of bias and the GRADE [23] method to evaluate the overall certainty of evidence. Basing decisions related to quality of evidence on standardized guidelines provides a framework for making sound judgements and may help reduce bias, while improving the interpretability of results by readers familiar with these guidelines. Another strength is that subgroup analysis is planned to detect differences based on baseline patient characteristics, which can improve the applicability of the review results and help caregivers, clinicians, and policy-makers predict response to binocular therapy. Finally, this protocol and the preceding PROSPERO registration both extensively describe the eligibility criteria and planned methods for analysis, reducing the risk of reporting bias.

Methodological limitations

Despite efforts to design a methodologically sound protocol, some limitations must be acknowledged. Regarding the search strategy, input from an information specialist was not possible due to unavailability at our institute as well as in neighboring institutes. Furthermore, no plan has been made to search gray literature, which may increase the risk for publication bias. Regarding eligibility criteria, non-English articles will not be included due to language limitations, which may lead to the over-representation of research from English-speaking countries. Moreover, as this is mainly a review of interventions, only RCTs will be included, which is expected to reduce the ability to detect both serious and rare side effects. Furthermore, the exclusion of non-parallel RCTs may exclude otherwise relevant studies. Another limitation is that included RCTs may be more likely to originate from areas of higher socioeconomic level, as many binocular therapy modalities require more expensive technology compared to conventional treatment. It is possible that this can affect the generalizability of the review results, including the impact of treatment on visual outcomes, adherence, patient/caregivers reported outcomes, and reported barriers and side effects. Finally a significant amount of heterogeneity is anticipated, given the many types of available binocular treatments and techniques. This may hinder the ability to combine results and perform a meaningful quantitative synthesis.

Anticipated impact

Although reviews have been conducted on the use of binocular therapy to treat amblyopia, there remains a need to evaluate and make sense of the growing body of evidence. Many recent reviews mainly focus on visual outcomes (e.g., [28, 29]), which are definitely important, but are not the only determinants of effectiveness, especially if the goal is to provide wholistic and comprehensive patient care. Given the complex nature of amblyopia and how pervasive its effects are on children and their families, the nuances of treatment options must be considered. For this reason, this review has given importance to outcomes such as adherence, patient or caregiver reported outcomes, reported barriers and side effects, and sustained improvement after treatment cessation. A deeper understanding of these outcomes may further guide patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers to make informed, patient-centered decisions regarding appropriate individualized treatment. Furthermore, it may help guide policy makers to best allocate resources to patients who are expected to benefit most. In addition to the expected clinical impact, well-conducted reviews can help inform future research by highlighting the gaps in the current body of evidence as well as the potential areas for growth.
