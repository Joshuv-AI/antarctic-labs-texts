Feasibility of automatic screw planning via transformer-based shape completion from RGB-D imaging
Authors: Aidana Massalimova, David Bauer, Nicola Cavalcanti, Fabio Carrillo, Farshad Mazda, Philipp Fuernstahl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate pedicle screw placement (PSP) is essential in spinal fusion surgery. Conventional navigation relies on computed tomography (CT) or fluoroscopy, which involves ionizing radiation and requires an error-prone registration procedure. We propose a pipeline that enables PSP planning directly on vertebral point clouds reconstructed from intraoperative RGB-D scans, using the SurgPointTransformer network. The system detects screw entry and pedicle regions, estimates initial trajectories, and refines them via anatomically constrained optimization. We evaluated our method on nine ex-vivo cadaveric specimens, comparing RGB-D–based planning to a CT-based baseline using both RGB-D reconstructions and ground-truth CT meshes. No significant differences were found in entry-point offset (3.53 ± 1.30 mm vs. 3.90 ± 1.29 mm), pedicle-center offset (1.58 ± 0.58 mm vs. 1.68 ± 0.59 mm), trajectory-angle error (7.31 ± 3.34 vs. 7.67 ± 3.59); all . Safety analysis using the Gertzbein–Robbins classification showed 100% radiologically optimal screw placement (grade A) with both methods. PSP planned from RGB–D reconstructions of the exposed dorsal surface alone achieved planning-level accuracy comparable to CT-based planning on the entire vertebral body. Prospective intraoperative validation is required to establish execution accuracy and clinical outcomes.Subject terms: Anatomy, Engineering, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Pedicle-screw placement (PSP) is essential for spinal fusion surgery, providing stable internal fixation while the vertebrae fuse, and is commonly used to treat conditions such as degenerative disc disease, spondylolisthesis, trauma, or tumors1. In PSP, screws are inserted through the vertebral pedicles and connected by rods to create a rigid bone-implant construct. Screw malpositioning can cause breaches of the pedicle walls with the risk to injure nerves or the dura, and may compromise the spinal stability2. Freehand techniques report malposition rates up to 41% in the lumbar spine and 55% in the thoracic spine3, which has driven development of computer-assisted orthopedic surgery (CAOS) systems.

CAOS workflows rely on high-resolution preoperative imaging, typically computed tomography (CT), to visualize the vertebrae in 3 dimensions (3D), enabling planning of the screw trajectories prior to surgery. According to the AO Foundation guidelines, the ideal entry point lies where the transverse-process midline meets the lateral margin of the superior articular facet, with a trajectory parallel to the superior endplate and aligned along the pedicle axis4. Although CT offers excellent cortical detail, many intraoperative workflows still depend on intraoperative fluoroscopy or cone-beam CT to register the patient anatomy to the preoperative plan. This registration can be time-consuming, introduce alignment errors if the patient moves or anatomy changes, and add radiation exposure5–7.

Automatic PSP planning methods aim to replace and streamline manual planning. Early CT-based approaches used simple image thresholding together with 2 dimensional (2D) geometric rules. Wicker et al.8 estimated screw parameters from pedicle width on thresholded CT slices, but provided no quantitative validation. Knez et al.9 applied 3D parametric modeling to predict screw diameter, length and insertion angle, reporting deviations of 0.4 mm, 5.8 mm and 7.6 respectively, and improved predicted fixation strength in 99 % of cases. Atlas-based methods register an anatomical template to patient scans, transferring expert-defined screw trajectories automatically. Goerres et al.10 achieved surface registration errors of 1.29 ± 0.15 mm and trajectory deviations of 1.12 ± 0.67 mm at the pedicle center using coherent point drift registration. Vijayan et al.11 obtained 95 % pedicle-center alignment with a root-mean-square error of 2.0 ± 0.5 mm using an atlas from forty cases. Bertram et al.12 compared Brainlab’s atlas-based Spinal Planning Platform to manual planning and found that 92.9 % of planned trajectories met Gertzbein-Robbins Grades A or B, while reducing planning time by about 7 s per screw (averaging 20 s per vertebra). However, atlas-based strategies struggle when patient anatomy significantly deviates from the template, such as in cases of severe scoliosis or degeneration.

Biomechanical optimization methods incorporate patient-specific data like bone mineral density (BMD) and pull-out force (POF). Ma et al.4 optimized CT-based trajectories to maximize POF according to regional BMD, reporting twice the POF of standard placements across 126 screws. Zhao et al.13 extended optimization to multi-level fixation, balancing screw safety and rod alignment, achieving 88 % breach-free placements and 100 % Gertzbein Grade A accuracy over 420 screws. More recently, machine learning (ML) approaches based on preoperative CT data have become state-of-the-art, outperforming most previous approach in terms of accuracy. Siemionow et al.14 evaluated a commercial ML-based PSP system on 208 lumbar screws and achieved 100 % Gertzbein Grade A and 99 % Ravi Grade 1 placements. Wang et al.15 used a 3D U-Net for vertebra segmentation and heatmap-based keypoint detection, achieving 97.7 % Grade A placements in eighty-eight patients, with mean angular deviations of 1.63 (transverse) and 1.39 (sagittal). Scherer et al.16 compared a deep-learning (DL) model to Brainlab’s atlas-based Elements Spine & Trauma 3D planning software (v1.0.0.172) on 256 screws. The DL model succeeded in 100 % of cases with a head-center deviation of 3.93 mm and an angular error of 4.46, while the atlas-based system failed in 19 % of cases. Xiong et al.17 reported DL-planned screws yielded over 200 N greater POF across multiple BMD groups in 242 screws placed in forty-three patients.

More recently, approaches to directly plan screw trajectories on intraoperative imaging data have been also explored to reduce radiation exposure and eliminate registration errors. Davoodi et al.18 used intraoperative ultrasound (US) to guide PSP, achieving orientation errors of -0.34 ± 3.66 (sagittal) and 4.32 (axial). Ao et al.19 employed reinforcement learning on intraoperative US-based 3D reconstructions and reported 99 % safety. Advanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques, such as spoiled gradient echo sequences, can provide CT-like bone contrast20. Altorfer et al.21 demonstrated the feasibility of PSP using CT-like 3D MRI, achieving 99% acceptable Gertzbein-Robbins grades (89% Grade A, 10% Grade B) in a cadaveric study of 100 screws.

As an alternative to radiation-based navigation methods, our previous work introduced SurgPointTransformer-a transformer-based network capable of reconstructing complete vertebral geometry from partial intraoperative RGB-D observations22. This approach eliminates the need for intraoperative registration and radiation exposure. Building on this foundation, we now present a PSP planning pipeline that computes screw trajectories directly from the reconstructed complete point clouds of the vertebrae. We hypothesize that accurate and safe PSP can be achieved using only stereo-based point clouds, by combining robust anatomical landmark detection with anatomically constrained trajectory optimization. We validate this hypothesis using the SpineDepth dataset23 and benchmark our RGB-D-based planning against a CT-based baseline.

RGB-D dataset

We used the previously published SpineDepth dataset23, which comprises recordings of ex-vivo lumbar spinal fusion procedures performed on ten cadaveric specimens in a realistic surgical setting. Each surgery was captured from two synchronized viewpoints using ZED Mini stereo cameras (Stereolabs Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA), providing RGB-D data of the exposed spine. To reflect typical intraoperative conditions, the surgeon performed a midline dissection while preserving ligaments and minimizing soft-tissue removal; detailed experimental procedures are provided in23. Vertebral levels ranged from T9 to S5, and visible landmarks-such as spinous processes, laminae, and facet joints-were confirmed to resemble in vivo anatomy. To maintain consistency with prior studies, we excluded the first specimen due to atypical surgical exposure. The dataset includes synchronized vertebral pose annotations, 3D surface meshes of vertebrae segmented from CT scans, and expert-defined screw trajectories. The CT-derived meshes of lumbar vertebrae (L1–L5) were rigidly aligned to the RGB-D coordinate frame, as described previously22, and are denoted .

Shape completion

In our previous work22, we introduced a transformer-based pipeline for vertebral shape completion from RGB-D input. The pipeline consists of two stages: (1) segmentation of RGB-D data to isolate partial 3D point clouds of individual vertebrae, and (2) shape completion using our SurgPointTransformer network to reconstruct complete vertebral geometries. In this study, we utilized shape-completion results from a leave-one-specimen-out evaluation on nine cadaveric specimens. For each fold, the validation set contained 320 randomly sampled frames covering five lumbar vertebral levels (L1–L5), yielding 1,600 reconstructed point clouds (), each with 4,096 points per frame. Across nine folds (specimens), this resulted in 14,400 reconstructions and 45 corresponding ground-truth vertebral meshes () for evaluation. Poisson surface reconstruction was applied to to obtain . Training strategy

All point clouds were randomly split into 80% for training and 20% for validation. To improve model robustness, we augmented the training data tenfold using a combination of geometric transformations. Specifically, each training sample was randomly rotated by up to along the x-, y-, and z-axes, uniformly scaled within a range of [0.9, 1.1], translated by up to along each axis, and randomly flipped along the three Cartesian axes. This augmentation strategy was designed to improve generalization across vertebral shape and orientation variability.

Training was performed using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 16 and an initial learning rate of . The exponential decay rates for the first and second moment estimates (betas) were set to (0.9, 0.999). A StepLR scheduler reduced the learning rate by a factor of 0.5 every 20 epochs to ensure stable convergence. The model was trained using a multiclass negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss function.

For each vertebral side , the objective is to compute an anatomically valid pedicle screw trajectory that remains entirely within the cortical bone boundary. As initialization, we use the predicted anatomical regions produced by the landmark detector-entry-point regions and pedicle regions . These serve as inputs to a mesh-based optimization procedure that refines the screw trajectory by estimating the optimized entry point , midpoint , and direction . The screw radius and the maximum screw length are then derived.

The optimization procedure comprises two stages: (1) estimating initial screw parameters, and (2) refining the trajectory through constrained optimization to ensure cortical containment and anatomical feasibility.

Discussion

This study shows that intraoperative RGB-D surface reconstructions can support pedicle screw planning with accuracy comparable to CT-based planning, while avoiding ionizing radiation and image-to-patient registration. We introduced a pipeline that combines vertebral shape completion, landmark detection, and anatomically constrained trajectory optimization. The approach couples learning-based perception with a purely geometric planner: shape completion reconstructs a vertebral point cloud from RGB-D; landmark detection yields entry and mid-pedicle region centroids; and these learned priors initialize a deterministic, constraint-driven optimization that minimizes a signed-distance breach penalty under sagittal midline non-crossing, convergent-orientation, and axial-targeting constraints.

Despite the lower fidelity and potential noise of RGB-D data, trajectory accuracy closely matched CT-derived planning. Across nine cadaveric specimens, the RGB-D pipeline produced mean errors of , , and , with no statistically significant differences versus CT-based plans. Screw-geometry offsets were small: (RGB-D) vs. (CT), and (RGB-D) vs. (CT); when aggregated across specimens, was significantly lower for CT (). All RGB-D-planned screws were assigned Gertzbein-Robbins Grade A, indicating full pedicle containment at the planning stage. Optimization runtimes were shorter for RGB-D ( s) than for CT ( s), likely because RGB-D reconstructions contain fewer faces/points than CT meshes, reducing per-iteration signed-distance evaluations under identical stopping criteria. Qualitative overlays (see Fig. 6) can look different because the pipelines start from different surfaces and initializations (complete, high-resolution CT cortex vs. Poisson-reconstructed RGB-D from partial views); under identical constraints, the optimizer may converge to nearby local optima that remain clinically equivalent.

A key strength of the method is its constrained optimization, which explicitly models pedicle morphology and enforces geometric safety constraints. In contrast to end-to-end regressors of screw parameters12,14, this design yields interpretable failure modes and adds geometric safeguards against cortical breach during planning. Trajectory diversity in our evaluation arose from inter-subject and inter-level anatomical variability, multiple RGB-D reconstructions per level (viewpoint/occlusion/noise differences), and bilateral planning; the constraint set further discourages unrealistic paths.

Clinical limitations and translational pathway

In open surgery, only posterior elements (spinous processes, laminae, facets) are typically visible, with additional occlusions from soft tissue, retractors, and instruments. The pipeline is designed for partial visibility: shape completion forms a watertight surface from incomplete scans, and landmark detection operates on regions rather than single points, reducing sensitivity to local dropouts. Realistic failure modes remain-e.g., insufficient posterior coverage or fragmented reconstructions-where landmarks may be unreliable and optimization should be gated. To mitigate this, we plan (i) real-time coverage/uncertainty metrics to decide when planning is safe and (ii) multi-view sweep recommendations to close coverage gaps. Generalizability to atypical/pathological anatomy (scoliosis, fractures, severe degeneration) is not yet established; our conservative radius margin and constraints help but do not eliminate risk. End-to-end latency (acquisition reconstruction planning) was not benchmarked against CT navigation; in this study, CT meshes were produced offline by manual segmentation. Practical deployment will also require stable sensor mounting, periodic calibration checks, surgeon-system interaction for scan feedback and trajectory acceptance/rejection, and attention to sterility.

On the application side, the same pipeline can: (i) provide intraoperative decision support for open lumbar fusion; (ii) integrate with robotic guidance as a constraint-checker/trajectory refiner; (iii) deliver AR overlays of planned screws after surface-based registration; and (iv) adapt to non-ionizing reconstructions such as ultrasound, where the geometric safeguards remain applicable. A pragmatic pathway includes standardized multi-view scanning with coverage/uncertainty gating, validation under posterior-only exposure in a simulated OR (retractors, drapes, instrument occlusions), measurement of full pipeline latency, and a prospective feasibility study with surgeon-in-the-loop quality assurance.

In summary, RGB-D-based surface reconstruction can support full PSP planning with accuracy comparable to CT while maintaining geometric safety guarantees at the planning stage. The constraint-driven design balances explainability and robustness, laying groundwork for practical, deep learning-assisted spinal navigation with reduced reliance on radiation and intraoperative imaging. While these results support planning robustness under partial and noisy reconstructions, prospective intraoperative validation is still required.
