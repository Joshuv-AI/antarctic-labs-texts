Holistic integrative analysis of circulatory parameters: breath-by-breath analysis for circulatory parameters in subjects without obstructive sleep apnea
Authors: Jia-Hao Chen, Xing-Guo Sun, Jiang Huang, Ben Xie, Fan Xu, Meng-Jun Xiang, Zeng-Fei Zhang, Qing-Qing Zhou, Chao Shi, Yan-Fang Zhang, Ji-Nan Wang, Fang Liu, You-Hong Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe theory of Holistic Integrative Physiology and Medicine (HIPM) emphasizes the perspective of integrated regulation, which includes interconnected systems of respiration, circulation, and metabolism. Based on this perspective, we aim to comprehend the relationship between respiration and circulatory parameters such as heart rate variability (HRV) and blood pressure variability (BPV), thereby clarifying the interrelation of two essential physiological systems.MethodsThis study conducted a retrospective analysis of data obtained during polysomnography (PSG) and cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET). These parameters included the mean amplitude of HRV, HRV percentage (HRV%), mean amplitude of systolic blood pressure variability (SBPV-M), mean amplitude of diastolic blood pressure variability (DBPV-M), along with the percentages of SBPV-M (SBPV-M%) and DBPV-M (DBPV-M%). Furthermore, we analyzed the relationship between nasal airflow respiratory cycles and circulatory cycles. We analyzed two groups: seven individuals without known diseases and eight patients with chronic diseases, all without sleep apnea syndrome on PSG.ResultsOur study demonstrated significant differences between individuals without known diseases and patients with chronic diseases. In control group, the peak oxygen uptake (peak V˙O2) was 97.82±18.51 %Pred, the anaerobic threshold (AT) was 83.61±10.46 %Pred, the peak work rate was 111.09±31.01 %Pred, the HRV was 6.15±1.52 bpm and HRV% was 11.15%±3.71%, respectively. These values were significantly higher than those observed in patients with chronic diseases (P values were 0.009, 0.05, 0.005, <0.001 and 0.001). In the context of SBPV-M, DBPV-M, SBPV-M% and DBPV-M%, the values for individuals without known diseases were 2.27±0.83 mmHg, 2.12±0.40 mmHg, 2.07%±0.71%, and 3.10%±0.66%, respectively. No significant changes were seen when compared to those in patients with chronic diseases (P values were 0.36, 0.20, 0.66, and 0.08). The ratio of respiratory cycles to circulatory cycles was nearly 1:1 in both groups.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates two principal observations: (I) precise synchronization between respiratory cycles and cardiovascular variability cycles during stable sleep phases; (II) lower HRV magnitudes observed in participants with chronic diseases compared to controls.Keywords: The Holistic Integrative Physiology and Medicine (the HIPM), cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET), respiratory heart rate variability (respiratory HRV), blood pressure variability (BPV)

Key findings

• The consistent 1:1 ratio observed between respiratory and circulatory cycles in both groups supports the Holistic Integrative Physiology and Medicine (HIPM) concept. This suggests a clear relationship between respiration and circulatory parameters.

What is known and what is new?

• The reduction of resting heart rate variability (HRV) and the increase in blood pressure variability are closely linked to the onset and progression of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disorders.

• Our study revealed significant differences in HRV parameters between individuals without known diseases and patients with chronic diseases. HRV and percentage of HRV (HRV%) in 7 individuals without known diseases were observed to be 6.15±1.52 bpm and 11.15%±3.71%, respectively. These values were significantly higher than those recorded in patients with chronic diseases (P<0.001 and P=0.001). Interestingly, the ratio of respiratory cycles to circulatory cycles was around 1:1 in both groups.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• There is a synchronous relationship between the fluctuation of respiration and circulatory parameters. This holistic perspective is essential for a comprehensive assessment and therapy of health conditions that span both respiratory and circulatory dynamics.

Introduction

With the aging population, the prevalence of chronic diseases, particularly cardiovascular diseases, is rising in China, leading to increasing economic burdens on both people and society. This trend reveals a significant problem with public health (1-3). The human body is a complex, interconnected system with closely interwoven components. However, the rapid development of medicine, driven by specialization and labor division, has resulted in fragmented medical knowledge, causing challenges to healthcare delivery. The specialization in medicine has not significantly reduced the incidence and prevalence of chronic diseases (4,5). Clinicians often treat patients from a limited specialty perspective, leading to increased pharmaceutical use and therapy interventions for patients with chronic conditions to manage abnormal indicators. This worsens the economic burden on both society and people, causing a negative cycle.

To address these challenges, we propose the concept of Holistic Integrative Physiology and Medicine (HIPM). HIPM seeks to facilitate the integration of knowledge across core medical fields, offering a potential pathway to re-examine medicine from a comprehensive perspective, consistent with principles of systemic medicine (6-10). This approach aims to bridge advanced medical knowledge with practical therapeutic skills, while considering sociological, environmental, and psychological contexts. By shifting away from an exclusive focus on ‘diseases’ toward a holistic understanding of the ‘patient’ within their living environment, HIPM aligns with recent calls for patient-centered care. Through iterative refinement, HIPM attempts to translate research evidence into clinical insights, transform clinical consensus into practical experiences, and integrate technological advancements with healthcare delivery. By synthesizing facts, experiences, and medical expertise, HIPM represents an evolving approach to improving healthcare outcomes (10).

Respiratory heart rate variability (HRV), first identified by Professor Ludwig (11), is characterized by heart rate acceleration during inspiration and deceleration during expiration. While this phenomenon has been extensively studied for its physiological significance, its functional relevance remains debated (12-15). Traditional perspectives suggest that HRV may enhance oxygen intake efficiency through improved pulmonary blood flow synchronization with airflow (16-19), reduce cardiac workload (20), and stabilize systemic circulation (21). However, alternative interpretations propose that HRV could primarily reflect mechanical interactions between respiration and cardiac function, where fluctuating intrathoracic pressures induce heart rate changes via the Starling mechanism. Current research indicates HRV’s potential dual nature: it may serve both as a functional regulatory mechanism and as an epiphenomenon of cardiopulmonary interactions. Clinically, diminished HRV correlates with disease progression in heart failure, hypertension, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (13,14,22), though whether this represents causal pathophysiology or secondary manifestation requires further investigation. These contrasting views highlight the need for continued research to elucidate HRV’s precise role in human physiology.

Blood pressure is a dynamic physiological parameter that continuously adapts to meet organ perfusion demands across changing conditions. This adaptive capacity, termed blood pressure variability (BPV), reflects the cardiovascular system’s ability to modulate pressure levels in response to physiological needs (23,24). While BPV naturally differs among individuals and responds to daily stressors, excessive fluctuations have been strongly associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes (23,24). Current research consistently links elevated BPV to increased risks of both cerebrovascular and cardiac pathologies (25-27). However, the precise regulatory mechanisms governing BPV remain incompletely characterized, particularly regarding its differential impacts across vascular beds (28). This knowledge gap continues to drive fundamental investigations in cardiovascular physiology.

We assert that HRV and BPV are expressions of the integrated regulation among respiration, circulation, and metabolism. This hypothesis is consistent with the principles of HIPM. We examined the correlation the relationship between the respiratory cycle and HRV and BPV during two pivotal phases: post-sleep onset and pre-awakening. Our methodological approach included sophisticated monitoring tools to precisely assess respiratory patterns and HRV, thus revealing the interplay between HRV and BPV concerning breathing cycles.

We hypothesize that there is a clear relationship between the respiratory and the variability of circulatory parameters, and changes in respiration can affect the values of circulatory parameters. We try to explain this phenomenon through the theory of HIPM, and provide some human experimental data for the development of HIPM. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-24-521/rc).

Methods

Participants

We included seven individuals without known diseases and eight patients with chronic diseases who underwent polysomnography (PSG) for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) screening. All participants had previously completed cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) and PSG at Fuwai Hospital. The Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital approved the research protocol (approval No. 2023-2236). The study was carried out in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion criteria for seven individuals without known diseases:

No history or current occurrence cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, coronary heart disease, stroke, or any other chronic diseases, and no clinical diagnosis of any diseases;

No history or current occurrence cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, coronary heart disease, stroke, or any other chronic diseases, and no clinical diagnosis of any diseases;

A PSG assessment verifying the absence of sleep apnea hypopnea syndrome, with an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) less than five events per hour.

A PSG assessment verifying the absence of sleep apnea hypopnea syndrome, with an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) less than five events per hour.

Inclusion criteria for patients with chronic diseases (2,29-35):

A definitive prior diagnosis of one or more of the following chronic diseases: coronary artery disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, etc.;

A definitive prior diagnosis of one or more of the following chronic diseases: coronary artery disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, etc.;

A PSG assessment verifying the absence of sleep apnea hypopnea syndrome, with an AHI less than five events per hour.

A PSG assessment verifying the absence of sleep apnea hypopnea syndrome, with an AHI less than five events per hour.

Exclusion criteria for all participants:

All participants must refrain from coffee, alcohol, sedatives, and any other substances that may influence sleep for one week before monitoring. This is to ensure that sleep and cardiovascular parameter measurements are not influenced by these substances.

CPET evaluation

CPET scheme

The CPET was performed with the Quark PFT Ergo system, produced by COSMEDS.R.L. in Italy. To ensure the precision of airflow exchange data, the system was calibrated daily with a metabolic simulator prior to patient service. Before commencing the exercise test, participants first completed a static pulmonary function test in a seated position. Following this, an electromagnetically braked cycle ergometer was used. The power was progressively elevated according to criteria established by the Harbor-UCLA Medical Center (36,37).

CPET data analysis method

Data for all measured parameters were initially assessed from the software of the Quark PFT Ergo cardiopulmonary exercise testing system by COSMED S.R.L., Italy. The raw data were segmented second-by-second basis utilizing a breath-by-breath methodology (36). Subsequent analysis was then conducted following standard calculation principles.

PSG monitoring and evaluation

PSG monitoring process

The SOMNOscreenTM plus RC polysomnography device, produced by SOMNOmedics in Germany, was utilized to measure several parameters, including nasal airflow, heart rate, blood pressure, photoplethysmographic (PPG) pulse wave, electroencephalogram (EEG), and electromyogram (EMG). To acclimatize to the environment and apparatus and alleviate the “first night effect”, all participants were advised to wear the PSG device in the ward during daytime. The wear duration spanned continuously for 48 hours, encompassing two days and two nights. The first night was used to familiarize themselves with the equipment and ward, while the data collected in the second night was used for analysis. Data points collected during sensor separation or malfunction, as well as artifacts arising from changes in patient positioning, would be removed from the statistical analysis.

PSG data calculation and analysis

The participant’s sleep status was assessed based on EMG, EEG, body motion signals, and snoring detector data. PSG monitoring data was categorized into sleep and wakefulness periods. Participants utilized PSG for continuous monitoring in the ward throughout a duration of 48 hours. The nasal catheter respiratory airflow data was sampled at 128 Hz, the ECG R-R interval at 256 Hz, and the indirect blood pressure measurement via pulse wave transit time (PTT) at 256 Hz. The RS-651 sphygmomanometer from Beijing Xinxing Yangsheng Technology was utilized to obtain two blood pressure readings. The mean of these readings was utilized to calibrate the baseline blood pressure obtained from the PSG.

The nasal airflow velocity of 0 indicates the transition between inspiratory and expiratory phases, a flow velocity of less than 0 indicates the process of inspiratory, and a flow velocity of larger than 0 indicates the expiratory process. The continuous nasal airflow velocity decreases from 0 to less than 0, subsequently increases to above 0, and ultimately decreased back to 0, completing a complete respiratory cycle. Calculation method of respiratory cycles. In respiratory physiology, each breathing cycle contains two key processes: inspiration and expiration. The cycle is quantified by airflow rate, reflecting the velocity of air entering and exiting of the lungs. A key point in this cycle occurs when the airflow rate reaches zero, signifying the transition or ‘breathing switch’ between inspiration and expiration. During inspiration, the airflow velocity initiates at 0, escalates as air enters the lungs, then decreases back to 0, indicating the conclusion of the inspiration phase. Conversely, during expiration, the airflow velocity again starts from 0 and increases as the lungs contract to expel air, reaching a maximum rate before decelerating back to 0, thus completing the expiration phase and the entire breathing cycle. In the context of sleep, we denote these respiratory cycles as B-1, B-2, … B-n, where each notation represents an individual breathing cycle. The term B-n denotes the total number of respiratory cycles recorded during a sleep duration.

The heart rate (HR) was calculated from the R-R interval on an ECG. Heart rate =60,000/R-R interval (ms). The Domino software (version 3.0) was utilized to modify the blood pressure baseline and subsequently calculated the actual blood pressure values.

One HRV cycle includes a cycle of heart rate rising from its minimum value to its maximum value, followed by a descent from the maximum value back to the minimum value. HRV was calculated by the mean value of the ascending branch and descending phases of heart rate inside a single HRV cycle, whereas one HRV% is calculated by HRV divided by the average heart rate for that HRV cycle. Different HRV cycles would be generated according to the patient’s sleep duration, and the HRV cycle during the patient’s whole night’s sleep would be used for statistical analysis. Please refer to Figure 2 for details.

Calculation method of HRV and HRV%. In our study, we define two key metrics for assessing HRV: HRV-a and HRV-d. HRV-a is the absolute value of the difference between the minimum and maximum heart rates observed during the heart rate’s rising phase in a respiratory cycle. Conversely, HRV-d denotes the absolute value of the difference between the maximum and minimum heart rates during the decline phase of heart rate. One entire HRV cycle, denoted as HRV-n, includes the heart rate rising from its lowest point (recorded as HRV-a) to its highest point, followed by a descent back to the lowest point (recorded as HRV-d). Throughout the sleep period, these HRV cycles are tracked and measured as HRV-1, HRV-2, …, HRV-n, with HRV-n reflecting the total number of HRV cycles during sleep. To calculate the value of a single HRV cycle, the formula employed is: One HRV = (HRV-a + HRV-d)/2. Additionally, we calculate the HRV% for each cycle. HRV% represents the proportion of the HRV value for a certain cycle relative to the average heart rate of that same cycle. For example, the initial HRV cycle, HRV%-1 is determined by dividing the HRV value of HRV-1 by the mean heart rate during HRV-1. HR, heart rate; HRV, heart rate variability; HRV%, HRV percentage.

We utilized finger pulse wave analysis to determine the count of blood pressure variability cycles (BPV-n). The peak of the main wave indicates systolic blood pressure (SBP), whereas the nadir indicates diastolic blood pressure (DBP). The sampling rate of pulse wave was 128 Hz. The peak and nadir of the main wave are isolated and sorted individually. The rise of SBP or DBP from its minimum to maximum point, followed by fall down to the minimum, constitutes a cycle of systolic blood pressure variability (SBPV) or diastolic blood pressure variability (DBPV). SBPV-n represents the total SBPV cycle during sleep. The calculating method for the DBPV cycle number (DBPV-n) aligns with that of SBPV-n. The calculating method for SBPV amplitude (SBPV-M) or DBPV amplitude (DBPV-M) involves averaging the absolute values of the lowest-highest (rising) and the highest-lowest (falling) during fluctuations in blood pressure. The percentage of SBPV-M (SBPV-M%) was calculated as the ratio of SBPV-M to mean SBP in a single cycle, SBPV-M/SBP*100%; similarly; the percentage of DBPV-M was the ratio of DBPV-M to mean DBP in a single cycle, that is, DBPV-M/DBP*100%. The BPV cycle during the patient’s whole night’s sleep would be used for statistical analysis. Please refer to Figure 3A,3B for details.

Calculation method of SBPV. (A) The methodology for calculating SBPV-M parameters. The sequence of SBP rising from its lowest point to the highest and subsequently declining back to the lowest forms a cycle of SBPV-M, denoted as SBPV-M1, SBPV-M2, ..., up to SBPV-Mn, where ‘n’ represents the total number of SBPV cycles throughout the night. The SBPV-M cycle consists of two key measurements: the absolute values of the ascending phase and the descending phase of blood pressure, labeled as SBPV-M-a (ascending) and SBPV-M-d (descending), respectively. The SBPV-M for a single cycle is calculated as the average of these two values, i.e., One SBPV-M= (SBPV-M-a + SBPV-M-d)/2. The mean SBPV-M for the entire night is calculated by averaging the SBPV-M values from all cycles. The percentage change in SBPV-M for each cycle, denoted as SBPV-M%, is calculated by dividing the SBPV-M of a specific cycle by the mean SBP for that cycle, and multiplying the result by 100%. The methodology for calculating DBPV-M and its percentage change (DBPV-M%) follows the same principles established for SBPV-M. (B) The methodology for calculating the BPV-n using photoplethysmographic data. The peak of the main wave in the pulse wave data represents the SBP, while the trough represents the DBP. The peak and trough values of the main wave are separately extracted and sequenced. A complete cycle of SBPV is defined as the SBP rising from its lowest point to the highest and then descending back to the lowest. Similarly, a DBPV cycle follows the same pattern for DBP. These cycles are denoted as SBPV-1, SBPV-2, …, SBPV-n, where SBPV-n represents the total count of systolic blood pressure variation cycles occurring during sleep. The approach to calculate the DBPV-n mirrors that of SBPV-n. Each DBPV cycle tracks the fluctuations of DBP from its minimum to maximum levels and back throughout the sleep period. BPV-n, number of blood pressure variation cycles; DBP, diastolic blood pressure; DBPV-n, number of diastolic blood pressure variability cycles; SBP, systolic blood pressure; SBPV, systolic blood pressure variability; SBPV-M, mean amplitude of systolic blood pressure variability; SBPV-M%, percentages of SBPV-M.

Statistical analysis

The data were processed and analyzed with SPSS software, version 26. The results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or number (%) for regularly distributed and categorical variables, or as median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed variables. Normally distributed continuous data were analyzed utilizing unpaired t-tests. Nonparametric continuous data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Binomial data were examined with Fisher’s exact test. The significance level was set at 5%. Graphical representations were produced utilizing Origin software, version 2021.

Discussion

This study was designed to investigate the relationship between respiratory patterns and circulatory parameter variability, and focus on how respiratory changes influence cardiovascular dynamics. Our findings demonstrate significant coupling between respiratory cycles and circulatory parameters across both control individuals and chronic disease patients. Specifically, the observed 1:1 phase synchronization between breathing rhythms and circulatory parameters cycles provides experimental evidence supporting the hypothesized respiratory-circulatory interaction. These results advance the theoretical framework of HIPM by quantifying the temporal coordination between respiratory and cardiovascular systems in human subjects.

Our study including seven control individuals and eight patients with chronic diseases, revealed significant differences in HRV parameters between the two groups. HRV and HRV% were significantly lower in the chronic disease group compared to the control group. However, in the control group, the HRV% was 11.15±3.71, indicating significant individual variability. This wide range is consistent with previous research (17-19,38), indicating that HRV% can significantly differ across individuals without known diseases. The reduction in HRV observed in our study among older individuals and patients with cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and metabolic chronic diseases aligns with previous research findings (12,39). These findings emphasize the influence of aging and chronic diseases on the autonomic nervous system’s control of heart rate. The significant reduction in HRV and HRV% in patients with chronic conditions indicates diminished cardiac adaptation to physiological demands, providing crucial insight into the broader effects of chronic diseases on cardiovascular health.

Our comprehensive analysis revealed no significant statistical changes in blood pressure and BPV parameters between the control and chronic disease groups. This may be partially due to the limited sample size utilized in our study. Subsequent research will aim to include a bigger cohort for enhanced analysis and maybe clearer results. A further factor affecting our findings may be the particular emphasis on variations in circulatory parameters during sleep. All participants in this study were not diagnosed with OSA and demonstrated similar breathing patterns during sleep. It’s essential to recognize that sympathetic nerve activity is generally reduced during normal sleep (40), which may have reduced the variability in circulatory parameters. This may have resulted in the noted absence of significant statistical differences in BPV between individuals without known diseases and patients with chronic diseases. Subsequent research will investigate the correlation between normal breathing patterns and changes in circulatory parameters, especially with the start of OSA in affected patients.

Our study showed a considerable age disparity, with the control group being significantly younger than the patients with chronic diseases. The age disparity is a significant aspect to consider, since it may affect cardiopulmonary function and, thus, the study’s outcomes. We observed that Peak V˙O2, AT and peak work rate were significantly higher in control group compared to those with chronic diseases. Peak V˙O2 and AT are essential parameters that reflect the integrated metabolic function of the heart and lungs, whereas peak work rate indicates exercise intensity (41). The data indicate that total cardiovascular function and exercise capacity are better in individuals without known diseases. Moreover, research demonstrates that the active, actions begun by the human cerebral cortex can modify physiological states such as respiration, subsequently affecting other systems (42,43). Human respiration during sleep is largely steady, principally regulated by the autonomic (neurohumoral) and non-autonomic (cerebral cortex) nerve systems. This stability renders respiration and heart rate less vulnerable to external factors, creating an optimal environment for investigating the complex activities of the respiratory and circulatory systems. Consequently, performing these tests during nocturnal sleep provides a distinctive chance to evaluate these processes in their most stable condition, reducing the likelihood for external disruption.

Our study has revealed an important finding: during stable breathing phases in sleep, there exists a consistent near-unity relationship between the number of respiratory cycles and HRV cycle and BPV cycles. Specifically, the ratios of respiratory cycle number to HRV cycle number (B-n/HRV-n), to SBPV cycle number (B-n/SBPV-n), to DBPV cycle number (B-n/DBPV-n), and the ratio of SBPV cycle number to DBPV cycle number (SBPV-n/DBPV-n) for each subject consistently approached 1. This near-one ratio indicates a coordination between respiratory cycles and variations in circulatory parameters. Three plausible interpretations emerge:

Respiratory rhythm acts as pacemaker for circulatory oscillations (top-down modulation);

Respiratory rhythm acts as pacemaker for circulatory oscillations (top-down modulation);

Brainstem nuclei generate coupled respiratory-cardiovascular rhythms (common driver);

While our data cannot discriminate these mechanisms, the first interpretation aligns with respiratory sinus arrhythmia literature (44). Furthermore, this study offers human experimental evidence that supports the holistic concept of ‘integrated regulation of respiration, circulation, and metabolism’. It emphasizes the interdependence of body systems, confirming that they operate in a coordinated manner rather than in isolation, significantly impacting one another. This insight not only supports the theory of integrated regulation but also enhances our comprehension of the physiological relationship between respiratory cycles and cardiac function, providing a more comprehensive perspective on biological regulatory systems. This synchronization phenomenon, regardless of directionality, fundamentally challenges organ-system isolation models.

Conventional physiology supports that respiratory regulation is primarily influenced by parameters such as PaO2, PaCO2, and [H+]a, which adjust thoracic intrapulmonary pressure via a complex network which includes afferent nerves, the dorsal medullary respiratory control center, efferent nerves, and respiratory muscles. This process is precisely regulated by peripheral chemoreceptors situated in the aortic body and carotid artery, coordinating the inspiratory and expiratory phases (45,46). However, this perspective, although correct, fails to comprehensively represent the essential interaction with the circulatory system. Cheyne-Stokes respiration, frequently seen in patients with severe congestive heart failure, illustrates the complex interplay between respiratory and circulatory dysfunctions (47). This insight has prompted us to devise an integrated circulatory loop. This loop, which includes the anterior and posterior systems of the left ventricle and includes components such as the pulmonary vein, left atrium, left ventricle, aorta, and carotid artery, forms a comprehensive respiratory regulation mechanism. This integrated system offers a more comprehensive explanation for the regulation including external respiration, circulation, and internal respiration or metabolism, as illustrated in Figure 1. In this context, it is essential to recognize that the synergy between effective breathing and circulation is crucial for transporting oxygen to the mitochondria, the cellular powerhouse of energy metabolism. This synergy guarantees sufficient energy production to satisfy the body’s requirements. Moreover, baroreceptors located within the peripheral chemoreceptors are crucial for the regulation of circulatory pressure. The medulla oblongata, an essential center for both circulatory and respiratory functions, illustrates the significant connection between these systems. Consequently, fundamental circulatory parameters such as heart rate and blood pressure are affected by respiratory rhythms, emphasizing that these fluctuations are variations are primarily driven by respiration.

The HIPM emphasizes a fundamental principle: the human body acts as an entity, with no single system operating in isolation. Each biological system, though unique in its activities, is tightly interconnected and exerts impact on others. This is especially apparent in the deep interconnection of the circulatory and respiratory systems. For example, changes in breathing patterns can directly alter heart rate and blood pressure, illustrating the respiratory system’s influence on circulatory dynamics. Conversely, the effectiveness of the circulatory system in supplying oxygen to tissues and eliminating carbon dioxide considerably impacts respiratory efficiency. In addition to these two systems, HIPM recognizes the comprehensive integration of physiological activities. The interaction between the circulatory and respiratory systems is additionally regulated by the neurological and endocrine systems, which regulate both in reaction to internal and external inputs. Comprehensive viewpoint significantly influences our approach health to disease. Treating a single system in isolation may prove less effective than a comprehensive strategy that accounts for the intricate relationships among many biological systems. This approach may result in enhanced diagnostic methods, improved treatment strategies, and a deeper comprehension of disease prevention, all aimed at preserving the intricate equilibrium of the interconnected systems within the body.

PaO2, PaCO2, and [H+]a are essential variable wave signals in arterial blood, identified as primary regulators of respiration (48). These signals display wave-like fluctuations, as evidenced by our beat-by-beat study of umbilical cord blood in newborns. It has been noted that a decrease in the amplitude of these signals to a specific threshold initiates the first breath in neonates (49,50). Similar patterns have been noted in animal studies (51,52), indicating a fundamental process in the start of respiration. These signals are conveyed to the aortic and carotid bodies by left ventricular ejection, initiating changes in respiratory phases. Peripheral fast-response chemoreceptors in these bodies govern these phases through a ’strong-strong’ and ‘weak-weak’ methodology. A wave signal of greater amplitude generates a proportionately larger succeeding breath, whereas a smaller wave signal produces a diminished breath. The amplitude of these wave signals progressively decreases as blood circulates throughout the body, approaching nearly zero in venous blood. Once venous blood returns to the right heart and undergoes gas exchange in the alveoli, becomes re-enriched with these signals. Furthermore, these chemoreceptors transmit signals to the slow-response respiratory center in the medulla oblongata, with an approximate delay of 30 seconds. The delay arises as regulatory signals must navigate cerebral arteries and the blood-brain barrier to access the cerebrospinal fluid encasing central chemoreceptors. The timing of signal perception by central receptors is thus delayed compared to peripheral chemoreceptors, a critical factor in maintaining stable breathing patterns. The slow-response center detects these respiratory signals approximately 30 seconds in ahead and adjusts the respiratory rhythm accordingly. The fast and slow-response centers collaborate to ensure respiratory stability (7-9,53). In addition to their direct respiratory effects, these wave signals can influence the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems, either directly or after processing in the central nervous system. This results in fluctuating fluctuations in the tension of neurons innervated by these systems, which directly affects cardiovascular functions. Consequently, essential metrics such as stroke volume, blood pressure, and heart rate exhibit consistent fluctuations in reaction to these respiratory signals.

Besides other causes, inadequate ejection from the left ventricle is essential to heart function. During each ventricular cycle, leftover blood from the previous contraction persists in the left ventricle. The leftover blood subsequently combines with the freshly oxygenated blood from the pulmonary veins, which is abundant in wave signals following gas exchange. This mixing reduces the amplitude of the wave signal, a phenomenon that is particularly prominent in patients with heart failure exhibiting a reduced ejection fraction (54,55). As aging progresses, while left ventricular ejection fraction may not significantly decrease at rest, there is a considerable reduction in systolic function. This leads to a reduced maximal heart rate and ejection fraction during exercise, indicating a reduction in cardiac reserve capacity. The aging process induces systemic changes, including arteriosclerosis, reduced elasticity, and compliance of the aorta and peripheral arteries, resulting in heightened cardiac afterload. These changes result in diminished left ventricular systolic function, hence facilitating the onset and progression of heart failure (56-58). The CPET assessments performed in our study on patients with chronic diseases further substantiate these observations. We found that the overall cardiopulmonary function in patients with chronic conditions was significantly lower compared to that of individuals without known diseases. These findings highlight the significant substantial influence of age and cardiac changes on total heart health, particularly in the context of chronic diseases.

Wave-like signals in the body can induce similar wave-like changes in the tension of the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems. These fluctuations directly affect cardiovascular functions. However, as the cardiovascular system ages, there is a significant decline in in its response to changes in nerve tension. This decreased response can be attributed to several age-related physiological changes, including decreased vascular elasticity, changes in receptor sensitivity, and reduced cardiac output. This decreased reactivity leads to a reduction in HRV and HRV%. HRV, which is regulated by the autonomic nervous system, decreases with advancing age. This is because the aging cardiovascular system has reduced responsiveness to autonomic nerve inputs that induce HRV. Despite significant individual variability in HRV levels, a common finding across studies is that elderly adults exhibit reduced HRV compared to younger adults (59,60). This age-related reduction in HRV is probably attributable to the cumulative effects of the changes within the cardiovascular system throughout time. It highlights the significance of considering age as a critical variable in studies related to HRV and autonomic function.

Limitations

The sample size in this study is limited, which imposes limits in the interpretation of our findings. This study is undertaken within the framework of HIPM, which remains in its formative phases. Consequently, we had challenges in selecting patients who comprehend or resonate with this holistic approach, so constraining the pool of available volunteers. The study’s requirement for uninterrupted PSG monitoring for 48 hours presented a considerable obstacle. Therefore, our research findings may exhibit certain variances or constraints. Third, while PTT provides continuous non-invasive blood pressure monitoring advantages, its accuracy may be influenced by arterial stiffness variations and body position changes during prolonged monitoring, this potential measurement bias should be considered when interpreting beat-to-beat BPV analyses. Fourth, the age discrepancy between chronic disease participants and individuals without known diseases introduces potential confounding.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates two principal observations: (I) precise synchronization between respiratory cycles and cardiovascular variability cycles during stable sleep phases; (II) lower HRV magnitudes observed in participants with chronic diseases compared to controls.
