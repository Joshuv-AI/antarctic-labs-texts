Potential of oral combination lipid‑lowering therapy beyond statins: a simulation‑based perspective from the SANTORINI study
Authors: Raul D. Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Legend for the visual abstractSequential theoretical addition of ezetimibe and bempedoic acid increased LDL‑C goal achievement and was associated with modeled reductions in MACE. Risk estimates were inferred from LDL‑C lowering in the simulation.Image, graphical abstract

Elevated blood cholesterol levels are a cause of atherosclerosis, and lowering low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is central to preventing atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and prolonging life [1]. Statins are the primary agents for lowering cholesterol; however, there is now evidence that further cholesterol reduction beyond what is achieved with statins alone offers additional benefits.

Strong evidence from randomized controlled trials involving hundreds of thousands of patients shows that the benefits of cholesterol-lowering therapy are proportional to the amount of LDL-C removed from plasma [[2], [3], [4]]. The 22 % relative risk reduction in major ASCVD events per 38.7 mg/dL (1 mmol/L) LDL-C reduction observed with statins [2] is also seen with ezetimibe, bempedoic acid, and monoclonal PCSK9 inhibitors [3,4]. Considering this, current guidelines recommend more intensive LDL-C lowering and more restrictive goals, especially in those considered at high and very ASCVD risk [5]. Unfortunately, these recommendations add complexity to an already complex field, and most people who need cholesterol control still have inadequate cholesterol levels [1].

Injectable monoclonal and siRNA PCSK9 inhibitors induce very robust additional reductions in LDL-C on top of statins [1]. However, these medications are still expensive and may not be reimbursed in all health systems. Currently, ezetimibe and bempedoic acid, both oral agents, are less potent than PCSK9 inhibitors but may offer more affordable alternatives to achieve the proposed LDL-C goals, depending on one’s ASCVD risk.

In the current issue of the journal, Ray et al. explore the potential additional benefits of the ezetimibe and bempedoic acid-ezetimibe combination for patients who persist with high cholesterol despite statin or statin and ezetimibe combination therapy, using data from the European SANTORINI registry [6].

In SANTORINI, a registry of individuals considered at high and very high ASCVD risk, only approximately one-third of patients achieved LDL-C goals at one year, despite statin use by 95 % and oral combination therapy in fewer than 40 % of patients. These data underscore the limitations of statin monotherapy and highlight the need for treatment intensification when LDL-C goals are not met. Using observed LDL-C levels and treatment patterns as a baseline, the investigators applied Monte Carlo simulations to estimate the potential impact of sequentially adding ezetimibe and, if necessary, bempedoic acid in patients not at LDL-C goal and not already receiving these agents. Under these modeled conditions, the proportion of patients achieving LDL-C targets increased from 28.4 % to 43.8 % with ezetimibe and further to 63.1 % with the addition of bempedoic acid. In theory, 78 % would achieve an LDL-C < 70 mg/dL and 62 % would achieve an LDL-C < 55 mg/dL, the recommended goals for patients at high- and very-high risk, respectively [5]. These findings suggest that, in principle, nearly two-thirds of patients who remain above target on statins could achieve recommended LDL-C levels through optimization of oral combination therapy alone.

Considering the previously established association of induced LDL-C lowering with ezetimibe and bempedoic acid [4] and the reduction in ASCVD events, the projected improvements in LDL-C were accompanied by estimated reductions in long-term cardiovascular risk. Importantly, these reductions were not directly observed clinical outcomes, but rather inferred from expected LDL-C lowering based on prior outcome trials. Within this framework, the SANTORINI simulations estimated relative risk reductions of 7.6 % and 13.4 % following the sequential addition of ezetimibe and bempedoic acid, respectively. When applied to a predicted residual 10-year cardiovascular risk of 25.1 %, these relative reductions translated into absolute risk reductions of 2.0 % and 3.4 % over a decade. Although modest at the individual level, such absolute risk reductions are clinically meaningful when applied across large populations at elevated baseline risk.

Nevertheless, these findings must be interpreted in the context of important limitations inherent to simulation-based analyses. The projected benefits reflect modeled scenarios rather than observed clinical interventions and therefore represent estimated, not realized, outcomes. The simulations assume consistent treatment initiation, adherence, and persistence, assumptions that may not hold in routine practice.

Relevantly, the modeled strategy assumes sequential, stepwise intensification, with clinicians adding one agent at a time when LDL-C targets are not met. In reality, clinical inertia remains a major barrier to lipid optimization. Delays in treatment escalation, competing clinical priorities, and therapeutic complacency often prevent patients from advancing through successive treatment steps. As a result, the sequential addition of ezetimibe, followed later by bempedoic acid, may not reliably occur, potentially limiting real-world LDL-C goal attainment. In this context, the simulation may overestimate what is achievable through gradual intensification and, paradoxically, highlights the challenge of translating theoretical benefit into practice.

Additional limitations include reliance on average LDL-C reductions, which do not fully capture interindividual variability in treatment response. Differences in baseline LDL-C, statin intensity, metabolic factors, drug tolerability, and comorbid conditions may result in a broader distribution of outcomes than those modeled. Moreover, cardiovascular risk reductions were inferred indirectly from LDL-C changes rather than from adjudicated events, an approach that, while well supported by existing evidence[[2], [3], [4]], necessarily involves assumptions about long-term risk trajectories and competing risks.

The analysis also focused on patients not receiving bempedoic acid or PCSK9 inhibitors at baseline and did not model escalation beyond oral therapies. As such, the findings specifically inform the potential to optimize current oral combination therapy rather than the full spectrum of lipid-lowering strategies available to patients at highest risk.

Despite these limitations, the SANTORINI analysis conveys a clear message (visual abstract). The persistent shortfall in LDL-C goal attainment is not primarily due to a lack of effective therapies, but to underutilization and delayed intensification of existing options. Oral agents such as ezetimibe and bempedoic acid offer complementary mechanisms, favorable tolerability, and scalability, making them particularly well suited for broader use in routine practice. The looming arrival of novel, very robust, and potentially more affordable oral PCSK9 inhibitors will help facilitate LDL-C control [7]. However, all these medications will only work if we prescribe them and if patients take them, and given that it may take some time for cholesterol reduction to benefit atherosclerotic plaque biology, the sooner we prescribe them and if patients comply, the better. The authors should be congratulated for their efforts.
