Immediate Zero-Contrast Ductal Closure Following Emergent Valvotomy in Pulmonary Atresia With Intact Ventricular Septum
Authors: Brian Mendel, Hannah Soetjoadi, Radityo Prakoso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe timing of patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) closure after valvotomy in pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum (PA-IVS) remains controversial.Case SummaryA 23-day-old neonate with PA-IVS and tripartite right ventricle underwent valvotomy. Immediate PDA closure was subsequently performed because systemic diastolic pressure remained low despite adequate antegrade pulmonary flow, suggesting clinically significant ductal runoff with risk of systemic hypoperfusion. Given the patient's impaired renal function, the PDA closure was conducted using zero-contrast technique under minimal fluoroscopic guidance.DiscussionConcomitant valvotomy and PDA closure may be considered in selected PA-IVS patients when antegrade pulmonary flow is adequate but ductus arteriosus remains hemodynamically significant.Take-Home MessageTiming of ductal occlusion in PA-IVS requires individualized hemodynamic judgment, with zero-contrast PDA closure strategy as feasible alternative in renal dysfunction patients.

History of Presentation

A 23-day-old neonate was referred from a secondary hospital with persistent cyanosis since birth, with poor feeding and inadequate weight gain. His current medications were 1 mg captopril twice daily and 1.5 mg sildenafil 3 times daily.Take-Home Messages•The urgency of intervention of PA-IVS and PDA was driven not only by the underlying ductal-dependent physiology but also by hemodynamic instability.•Immediate PDA closure can be considered after valvotomy when intraprocedural hemodynamics confirm adequate antegrade pulmonary flow and stable RV output.

On admission, his body weight was 3,300 g with oxygen saturation of 80% on room air, blood pressure 78/30 (46) mm Hg, heart rate of 140 beats/min, and respiratory rate 30 beats/min. His physical examination revealed perioral cyanosis, with a continuous murmur at the upper left sternal border and a grade III/VI pansystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border that increased with inspiration, suggestive of patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) and tricuspid regurgitation. There was no sign of hepatomegaly or peripheral edema.

Past Medical History

Because of maternal asthma, the patient was delivered via cesarean at 32 weeks’ gestation, with a birth weight of 3,000 g and length of 59 cm. He required 11 days of ventilatory support in the neonatal intensive care unit. There were no prior extracardiac comorbidities or family history of congenital heart disease.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the patient's age at presentation with central cyanosis, ductal-dependent congenital heart disease was suspected. Differential diagnosis included pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum (PA-IVS), critical pulmonary stenosis, and Tetralogy of Fallot with severe right ventricular (RV) outflow obstruction.

Investigations

Chest radiography showed cardiomegaly with a cardiothoracic ratio of 71% and clear lung fields, and electrocardiogram revealed sinus rhythm at 169 beats/min with a left ventricular hypertrophy pattern. Impaired renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate 25 mL/min/1.73 m2 calculated with modified Schwartz formula; serum creatinine 0.85 mg/dL) was also observed on admission.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed PA-IVS with pulmonary annulus diameter of 9.3 mm. RV assessment demonstrated a tripartite morphology with well-developed inlet, apical trabecular, and infundibular components (Figure 1, Video 1A). The tricuspid valve was also adequately formed (z-score of +1.01), despite having a severe regurgitation (maximum velocity of 4.2 m/s and tricuspid valve gradient of 71 mm Hg). No echocardiographic evidence of RV-dependent coronary circulation was detected.Figure 1Baseline Transthoracic Echocardiography Demonstrated an Atretic Pulmonary Valve With No Antegrade Flow Across the Right Ventricular Outflow Tract (Single Asterisk) and a Patent Ductus Arteriosus With Continuous Left-to-Right Shunt

In addition, the defect was accompanied by a PDA, measuring 4 to 5 mm, with a left-to-right shunt and a peak gradient of 56 mm Hg (Figure 1, Video 1B). Two secundum atrial septal defects were also identified at 3 mm and 4 mm in size, both exhibiting right-to-left shunting. Left and right ventricular systolic function were also preserved.

Management

Prostaglandin was not used in this patient and the ductus remained patent spontaneously. This factor influenced both the timing and strategy of intervention, allowing for procedural planning without pharmacologic ductal maintenance. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia via femoral access. A 4/5 F slender sheath was introduced through the femoral artery, followed by retrograde advancement of a 3.5/5 F Judkins Right guiding catheter into the main pulmonary artery. The catheter tip was then positioned just anterior to the pulmonary valve annulus, appearing as a 90° bend in the lateral fluoroscopic projection. A limited amount of contrast was selectively injected from the pulmonary arterial side to delineate the pulmonary valve anatomy and to ensure accurate localization prior to perforation.

Using the stiff (back) end of a 0.014-inch Runthrough Floppy coronary guidewire, the atretic pulmonary valve was successfully perforated (Figure 2, Video 2), establishing access to the RV. After positioning the catheter and balloon just across the stenotic area, balloon pulmonary valvotomy was performed using 10.0 × 40.0-mm Mini Tyshak balloon (Figure 3, Video 3). However, postvalvulotomy aortic pressure was 53/18 (33) mm Hg. Given the low systemic diastolic pressure and significant ductal flow (Figure 4, Video 4), the decision was made to proceed with PDA device closure. The PDA was antegradely cannulated from the femoral vein using 3.5/4-F Judkins Right diagnostic catheter and a soft hydrophilic guidewire (Figure 5, Video 5). After exchanging to a stiff guidewire, a 6-F steerable delivery sheath was advanced through the PDA into the descending aorta (Figure 6, Video 6). Using the zero-contrast technique, a 10/8 mm Konar-MF Occluder (Lifetech) was deployed (Figure 7, Video 7). A relatively larger occlusion device was intentionally selected because of the size of the PDA and the use of a delivery system that differs from classical large PDA sheaths. This choice was made to ensure stable positioning and complete occlusion. As confirmation of adequate device seating and only a mild central residual PDA shunt, the occluder was detached. Outcome and Follow-Up

Over several days, the patient improved clinically and was successfully extubated. Serial echocardiography confirmed stable device position, no residual ductal shunt, preserved biventricular function, mild residual pulmonary stenosis, and moderate tricuspid and pulmonary regurgitation (Figure 8, Video 8). He was discharged in stable condition with a low-dose captopril for a month. At 8-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic with oxygen saturation of 95% on room air. Discussion

The decision to close a PDA in PA-IVS warrants specific justification, as it diverges from the conventional strategy of initial observation of RV adequacy with consideration of temporary PDA stenting when needed. In general practice, management is not guided solely by anatomical considerations, but rather by the overall hemodynamic profile. In cases in which antegrade RV output remains insufficient following valvotomy, maintaining ductal patency, or even performing PDA stenting, may be appropriate to maintain pulmonary blood flow while allowing time for RV adaptation.

However, in the present case, the clinical scenario differed significantly. Despite satisfactory RV recruitment after valvotomy, the presence of a large PDA resulted in substantial diastolic runoff into the pulmonary circulation. This led to a reduction in aortic diastolic pressure, raising concern for compromised systemic perfusion, particularly at the level of end organs such as the mesenteric circulation. Given this unfavorable hemodynamic condition, early PDA closure was deemed necessary to restore aortic diastolic pressure and improve coronary and systemic perfusion. Although this approach contrasts with the more conservative paradigm of delayed reassessment, it was considered justified based on the immediate hemodynamic compromise and the risk of ongoing end-organ hypoperfusion.1, 2, 3

The pulmonary valvotomy was performed using a minimal-contrast approach, whereas the PDA closure was undertaken using a true zero-contrast technique, guided solely by fluoroscopy and echocardiography without any contrast administration. Pulmonary arteriography in the lateral projection was performed to accurately delineate the anatomical location of the atretic pulmonary valve and guide the valvotomy. This targeted use of contrast was intended to enhance procedural precision while maintaining a low contrast burden. Retrograde approach was associated with a significantly shorter procedural duration while achieving outcomes comparable to the antegrade technique. Specifically, total procedural time was reduced to 58.64 minutes (95% CI: 32.97-84.29; P = 0.008), with a concomitant decrease in PA–RV crossing time by 27 minutes (95% CI: 14.01-39.99; P = 0.02).4 The procedure was carried out at the level of the semilunar pulmonary valve cusp using the stiff end of a 0.014-inch guidewire, followed by stepwise balloon dilatation with diameters of 1.5 mm, 3 mm, and subsequently 8 to 10 mm. Furthermore, we clarified the use of a longer balloon (40 mm), which improves procedural stability and minimizes the risk of the “watermelon seed effect,” an important consideration in small neonatal anatomies.4 PDA closure was performed using a zero-contrast technique under echocardiographic guidance. In the TTE arch view, the catheter was initially visualized within the descending aorta and subsequently advanced toward the aortic arch under continuous TTE guidance. On the parasternal short-axis TTE view at the level of the great arteries, the catheter was observed traversing the PDA from the descending aorta into the PA. Finally, in the TTE arch view, the occlusion device was seen securely deployed within the PDA following release from the delivery cable.5

Study limitations

The balloon trial occlusion test is commonly recommended. In this case, a balloon occlusion test was not performed because the team had high confidence in RV adequacy based on postvalvotomy hemodynamics, including improved RV pressure and contractility, as well as stable oxygenation.

Conclusions

The timing of ductal closure after valvotomy in PA-IVS requires individualized hemodynamic judgment that must balance the risks of premature ductal elimination against those of persistent overcirculation. This approach reflects a highly selected clinical scenario, including spontaneous ductal patency, favorable RV response after valvotomy, and hemodynamic indications for early PDA closure. This strategy should not be generalized as standard practice.
