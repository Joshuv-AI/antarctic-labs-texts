Multimodal ion-gated transistor based on 2D superionic conductor for in-memory computing in deep learning
Authors: Bo Tong, Tianming Du, Jinhong Du, Xin Yu, Jinmeng Tong, Jiajun Xu, Wanying Li, Zhibo Liu, Chen Li, Wencai Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For neuromorphic computing, integrating multiply-accumulate operations and nonlinear activation within a single device can reduce latency and power consumption while improving computational efficiency. However, these operations impose conflicting requirements: multiply-accumulate requires highly linear and non-volatile resistance states, while activation requires diverse nonlinear synaptic behaviors. These contrasting demands make integrating both functions in a single device challenging. Here, we construct a multimodal ion-gate transistor using 2D CdPS3-Li as dielectric layer and MoS2 as channel material. The layered structure of CdPS3-Li facilitates anisotropic ion transport for Li+ storage and produces strong ion-electron coupling, resulting in high-linearity and non-volatile resistance states under electrical pulses. Moreover, Cd vacancies in CdPS3-Li attract and trap photo-generated holes from MoS2, leading to rich nonlinear behavior under light pulses. Therefore, the CdPS3-Li transistor can simultaneously perform both operations. The CdPS3-Li transistor arrays achieved high accuracy in handwritten digit classification, offering a promising hardware solution for neuromorphic computing.

Introduction

Brain-inspired neuromorphic computing, renowned for its low power consumption and high parallelism, is widely considered as a promising solution to overcome the von Neumann bottleneck1–4. The multiply-accumulate (MAC) operation and nonlinear activation are two fundamental components of neuromorphic computing5,6. Implementing MAC and nonlinear activation as two separate units needs digital-to-analog converters (DACs) for interconnection, resulting in power consumption that significantly exceeds that of the MAC operations alone. In contrast, integrating both functions within a single device can reduce latency, power consumption, and integration complexity, thereby improving overall computational efficiency7,8. However, these two operations impose distinct device requirements: MAC operation requires devices capable of producing highly linear and non-volatile resistance states, whereas nonlinear activation demands devices that can generate diverse nonlinear synaptic behaviors to introduce nonlinearity into neural networks9–13. These conflicting demands make the integration of both functions into a single device highly challenging.

Using electrical pulses for MAC operation and light pulses for nonlinear activation offers an effective solution to this challenge. Electrical pulses can directly perform MAC operation through crossbar memory arrays, providing high efficiency and reduced energy consumption12,13. Meanwhile, postsynaptic currents (PSCs) generated by synaptic devices under light pulses can be modeled by a double-exponential function, which exhibits pronounced nonlinear characteristics, thus providing a feasible means to implement activation functions (Fig. 1a). Moreover, this approach allows multiple devices to output the same nonlinear activation simultaneously, ensuring uniform activation across all devices to enable parallel computing while minimizing interference with the MAC circuitry14–16. Various multimodal synapses have been developed, modulating device conductivity through defect migration, charge trapping, material phase change, or ferroelectricity17–21. However, these mechanisms often cause uncontrollable stochasticity, high operation voltages, and low linearity, significantly affecting computational accuracy22. In contrast, ion-gated transistors using ionic conductors as dielectric layers can modulate channel conductance via ion-electron coupling by intercalating or ejecting ions. They offer advantages such as low operating voltage, low power consumption, and easy regulation22–24. However, existing ion-gated transistors struggle to respond to both electrical and light signals, limiting their ability to achieve multimodal functionality. Additionally, the isotropic nature of current ionic conductor dielectric layers weakens ion-electron coupling effects. This results in low conductance ratios, poor long-term synaptic plasticity, and inferior memory performance25–29. To address these challenges, there is an urgent need for a new type of ionic dielectric material that can precisely control ion transport, achieve non-volatile ion storage, and effectively trap photo-generated charges.

Here, we construct a multimodal ion-gate transistor using 2D CdPS3-Li as the dielectric layer and 2D MoS2 as the channel layer. The unique layered structure of CdPS3-Li facilitates highly anisotropic ion transport, resulting in the non-volatile storage of Li+ and a strong ion-electron coupling effect to generate high-linearity and non-volatile resistance states (R² ≈ 0.99, retention>104 s) under electrical pulses. Additionally, Cd vacancies in CdPS3-Li can attract and trap photogenerated holes from MoS2 through Coulombic interactions to exhibit rich non-linear dynamic behaviors (conductance ratio of 105) under light pulses. Consequently, the CdPS3-Li transistor can simultaneously perform MAC operations and implement activation functions, enabling the physical realization of the complete forward propagation process in deep learning. We further developed CdPS3-Li transistor arrays to recognize handwritten digit images, achieving an accuracy exceeding 97.5%, which offers a promising hardware solution for neuromorphic computing.

The construction of multimodal CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistors

We synthesized CdPS3-Li bulk crystals by chemical vapor transport (CVT) method (Fig. 1c). According to X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Fig. 1d) and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) absorption spectra (Supplementary Fig. 1), the crystals show similar structure with the CdPS3-Li membranes assembled by monolayer CdPS3 nanoflakes containing Cd vacancies30, where the vacancies were found to significantly boost the ion transport in the nanochannels to achieve a superhigh in-plane ion conductivity. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) peak at 56 eV confirms the presence of Li+ (Fig. 1e). However, due to the small atomic number, Li+ ions were hard to be observed by high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) imaging. Notably, such crystals contain abundant Cd vacancies, as evidenced by plan-view HAADF-STEM images (Fig. 1f) and the peak splitting in FTIR spectra (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The cross-sectional HAADF-STEM images clearly reveal that CdPS3-Li crystals have a van der Waals (vdW) layered structure, with an interlayer spacing of 6.55 Å (Fig. 1g). However, the Cd vacancies enable the crystals excellent hydration ability and exceptional hydrophilicity (Supplementary Fig. 2), resulting in a significantly expanded interlayer spacing (∼11.8 Å) under ambient conditions (~40% RH, 28 °C) (Fig. 1d), which corresponds to the intercalation of bilayer water molecules. Importantly, the crystals intercalated with water remain the well-ordered layered structure. Such highly anisotropic layered structure leads to a significant difference in the transport barriers between the in-plane and out-plane directions. Because of the well-ordered nanochannels and widened interlayer spacing, Li+ can rapidly transport along the in-plane direction through interlayer water molecules30–32. In contrast, the CdPS3-Li layers strongly restrict the out-plane transport of Li+.

We fabricated 2D CdPS3-Li nanoflakes by mechanical exfoliation of bulk crystals (Supplementary Fig. 3), which have atomically flat surface. The Li+ transport characteristics of 2D CdPS3-Li nanoflakes along the in-plane and out-plane directions were investigated by impedance spectroscopy under ambient conditions. Notably, the in-plane ionic conductivity is 0.134 S cm−1, while the out-plane ionic conductivity significantly decreases to 0.0258 S cm−1 (Fig. 1h). These results demonstrated the highly anisotropic Li+ transport behavior in 2D CdPS3-Li nanoflakes. Moreover, the 2D CdPS3-Li nanoflake exhibits a high dielectric constant (5.2) comparable to SiO2 (Supplementary Note 1) and a wide bandgap (Supplementary Fig. 5). Therefore, 2D CdPS3-Li nanoflake is expected to be an ideal dielectric layer to construct ion-gated transistors.

We further constructed CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor (Fig. 2a) by using 70 nm-thick CdPS3-Li as the dielectric layer, monolayer MoS2 as the channel material, and 3-layer (3 L) graphene as the top gate (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Monolayer MoS2 was selected for its direct bandgap (~1.8 eV) and exceptional optoelectronic properties (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). The output curve of the CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor exhibits excellent linearity (Supplementary Fig. 7a), suggesting an ohmic contact between the source/drain electrodes and the MoS2 channel. The transfer curve of the CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor shows obvious n-type semiconductor behavior with a large on/off current ratio (~106, Fig. 2b) with ultralow gate leakage current (<5 pA, Supplementary Fig. 7b) and demonstrates a pronounced hysteresis (Fig. 2b). This hysteresis arises from the transport of Li+ to the MoS2/CdPS3-Li interface under gate voltage, resulting in ion-electron coupling effect and the formation of a double electric layer (Supplementary Fig. 8). Furthermore, the hysteresis windows become larger under slower sweep rates or wider gate voltage ranges (Supplementary Fig. 9b, c) because more Li+ can accumulate at the MoS2/CdPS3-Li interface, generating a stronger ion-electron coupling effect. This can be confirmed through Kelvin Probe Force Microscopy (KPFM) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 10). When a positive voltage is applied to the CdPS3-Li/MoS2 vertical heterostructure, Li+ in the CdPS3-Li transport vertically to the MoS2 surface. We measured the surface potential after the voltage was applied, which indicates a 0.55 eV decrease in the work function of MoS2 (Fig. 2c). Additionally, we scanned the MoS2 surface potential after removing voltage at 1- and 5-min intervals, observing a slight increase of the potential (Fig. 2c). Additionally, CdPS3-Li crystals contain abundant Cd vacancies and such negatively charged vacancies can attract and trap the photo-generated holes of MoS2 through Coulombic interactions, facilitating the transfer of photo-generated holes from MoS2 into CdPS3-Li (Supplementary Fig. 11a). The calculation shows that the charge densities of S atoms on the CdPS3-Li side are significantly increased, demonstrating the occurrence of charge transfer33–35 (Supplementary Fig. 11b). We further conducted femtosecond-transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy on MoS2 and MoS2/CdPS3-Li heterostructures. Both ground-state bleaching and excited-state absorption peaks in the range of 600 to 750 nm can be observed, decaying over a timescale from 1 ps to 0.5 ns (Fig. 2d–g). Compared to MoS2, the intensities of both signals in the MoS2/CdPS3-Li heterostructure were significantly enhanced, indicating an increased number of particles transitioning from the ground state to the excited state (Fig. 2d, e). By fitting the relaxation times of the MoS2 and MoS2/CdPS3-Li heterostructures, we obtained exciton lifetimes (Supplementary Fig. 11c): for MoS2, τ1 = 42 ps, τ2 = 156 ps; for MoS2/CdPS3-Li, τ1 = 28 ps, τ2 = 227 ps. The significant difference in dynamics confirms the photo-hole transfer from MoS2 into CdPS3-Li (Supplementary Note 2). This behavior demonstrates the capability of CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistors to exhibit dynamic behavior under light pulses.

The multimodal synaptic behavior of CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistors

We next investigated the synaptic behavior of the CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor under electrical pulse. When applying a positive gate voltage pulse, Li+ would transport to the CdPS3-Li/MoS2 interface along the out-plane direction and generate ion-electron coupling effects, thereby enhancing channel conductivity to obtain a spike current. Upon removing the voltage, the majority Li+ could be remained at the interface, resulting in a non-volatile storage (Fig. 3a). Conversely, a negative gate voltage pulse induced the removal of Li+ from the interface, leading to a decrease in channel conductivity (Fig. 3b). Unlike ion-gated transistors using polymer electrolytes or solid electrolytes as dielectric layer, the highly anisotropic Li+ transport characteristic in CdPS3-Li significantly enhanced the storage capacity of Li+ at the interface, resulting in a long retention time over 104 s (Fig. 3c). The unchanged Raman spectra of MoS2 after multiple repeated tests confirm that Li+ is stored only at the CdPS3-Li/MoS2 interface without diffusion into MoS2 (Supplementary Fig. 12). By applying paired electrical pulses to the device, paired-pulse facilitation (PPF) and paired-pulse depression (PPD) effects can be induced (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14). 2D CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor shows excellent long-term-potentiation/depression (LTP/LTD) behavior with ultra-high linearity (Fig. 3d). As the number of positive gate voltage pulse gradually increased, more Li+ were stored at the CdPS3-Li/MoS2 interface, leading to a gradual increase in channel conductivity. On the contrary, each negative gate voltage pulse could erase partial Li+, thus gradually reducing the channel conductivity. In addition, the in-plane superionic transport of CdPS3-Li prevented Li+ aggregation, ensuring uniform distribution of Li+ at interface after the removal of the gate voltage pulse, which caused almost equal fluctuations in synaptic current. Thus, the 2D CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor showed exceptional linearity in both writing and erasing processes (R2 = 0.9985 and 0.9993, respectively) (Fig. 3d). To evaluate the endurance of the CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor, we applied successive positive and negative gate voltage pulses to switch the device between high- and low-resistance states. The device exhibited no performance degradation over 4000 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 16). To assess its cycle-to-cycle variations, we applied 2200 consecutive electrical pulses, conducting 15 programming and erasing cycles (Fig. 3e). The results indicated minimal differences between cycles, demonstrating high stability and reliability. The transistor also had good ambient stability, thermal stability, and cycling stability (Supplementary Fig. 17). Statistical analysis of 32 separate transistors shows each device operates normally with consistent threshold voltages and Ion (Supplementary Fig. 18 and 19).

Meanwhile, we tested the synaptic behavior of CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistors from 273 K to 323 K and found significant variations in performance. As shown in Fig. 3f and g, the range of conductance modulation of the device increases as the temperature increases (from ~0.07 μA at 273 K to 0.43 μA at 323 K). This phenomenon occurs because higher temperatures lower the ion migration barrier, which enhances conductivity. Significantly, the synaptic weight of the 2D CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor can be modulated by adjusting the gate voltage (Fig. 3h) or pulse width (Fig. 3i). At low gate voltages and short pulse width, a limited number of Li+ migrated to the interface, resulting in a rapid current decline following voltage removal. In contrast, higher gate voltages and wider pulse widths facilitated the storage of more Li+, further increasing the synaptic weight. Additionally, as the gate voltage and pulse width increase, the difference between the spike and memory currents decreases (Fig. 3j, k). It is worth noting that when the voltage increases to 500 mV and the pulse width extends to 1 ms, the current decline is completely eliminated after the electrical pulse is removed (Fig. 3l). We further fine-tuned the device algorithms at a source-drain voltage of 1 V under electrical pulses, achieving 64 resistive states without overlap (Fig. 3m), which lays the foundation for high-precision neuromorphic computing. Notably, the electrical state modification time of device can be reduced to as low as 1 μs (Supplementary Fig. 20a). Although its response is slower than that of complementary metal oxide semiconductor (CMOS) circuits, the device can be integrated with CMOS circuits for neural network training. In such system, it performs forward propagation while CMOS circuits execute backward propagation for gradient calculation. This configuration allows one backward propagation module to coordinate multiple forward modules in parallel, thereby minimizing idle time and enhancing overall throughput13,36.

Besides the highly-linear non-volatile resistance state output observed under electrical pulse, Coulombic interactions between Cd vacancies in CdPS3-Li and photo-generated holes in MoS2 could induce non-linear dynamic behavior in response to light pulses. When applying a light pulse under the VGS = 0 V, CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor generated PSCs, which subsequently decayed over time (Fig. 4a). More importantly, the relaxation time, defined as the time for the inhibitory effect (ΔI) to decay to 1/e of its initial value, could be modulated by varying the light intensity, pulse width, and number of pulses, thereby influencing the fitting function of the decay curve. Wider pulse widths and higher light intensities increase the number of photo-generated holes in MoS2, leading to longer relaxation times (Fig. 4d, e). Quantitative analysis showed a roughly linear relaxation time increase with incremental pulse widths and light intensities (Fig. 4f, g). Additionally, under the same light intensity and pulse width, an increased number of light pulses also produced a stronger cumulative effect, leading to longer relaxation times (Supplementary Fig. 22). As shown in Fig. 4d, since the transistor can achieve synaptic dynamic behavior under dim light (1.02 mW cm−2) and short pulses (100 μs), it exhibited a low light energy consumption of only 2.05 fJ to generate one PSC in the device. Meanwhile, Li+ in 2D CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor can be driven under short pulses width (1 μs) and ultra-low gate voltages (100 mV), as shown in Fig. 3h. This led to an ultra-low electrical energy consumption of 20 aJ to drive a single synaptic potentiation or depression operation (calculation details see Methods). It is noted that the CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor exhibited a large conductance ratio of 105 (Fig. 3c) and simultaneously achieving a highly linear resistance state under electrical pulses with ultra-low energy consumption (Fig. 4h, i and Supplementary Table 1). Therefore, the CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor has the potential to function as hardware for implementing activation functions and MAC, thereby improving system integration and computational efficiency.

Construction of CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor arrays for in-memory computing in deep learning

We further explored the ability of the CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor to simultaneously perform a MAC operation and an activation function during forward propagation in a convolutional neural network (CNN, Supplementary Fig. 25). For the MAC operation, we designed a 16 × 10 cross-bar dual-gate arrays (Fig. 5a). In this array, the bottom gates of devices in the same row were connected as ‘word lines’, utilizing BN as the bottom gate dielectric material, while the top gates of devices in the same column were connected as ‘bit lines’, with CdPS3-Li serving as the top gate dielectric material. The sources and drains of devices in the same row were connected as ‘source lines’ and ‘drain lines’, respectively. Only devices that were simultaneously selected by both ‘bit lines’ and ‘word lines’ will be activated (Supplementary Note 3). In the experiment, all 160 devices in the 16 × 10 array were tested, with 153 devices functioning effectively (95.63% yield). These devices demonstrated n-type transistor characteristics with on/off ratio exceeding 105 (Supplementary Fig. 26). The distributions of threshold voltage and on-state current were consistent (Supplementary Fig. 27). During the forward propagation process, the resistance states of devices in MAC array (weights) were effectively adjusted (Fig. 5b). Meanwhile, the activation function is implemented using a 2 × 8 array (Fig. 5c). In each epoch, light pulses with distinct parameters are applied to the array, generating PSC curves (Fig. 5d). The time vector (X = [X1, X2, X3, …, X16]) is derived from the results of the previous layer’s MAC operations in the neural network and serve as the input for extracting the current from the PSC curves. Each value in this time vector corresponds to the specific time at which the current is measured from an individual device. These current values are then collected to form the output current vector, completing the activation function computation. Finally, the output current vector is converted to the voltage vector to serve as the voltage inputs for the subsequent layer of MAC (Fig. 5e). The device array requires 5.2 ms to complete a light-pulse-induced activation operation (Fig. 5d), a timescale compatible with the operating speeds of MAC circuits in neuromorphic computing systems37.

Figure 5f illustrates the typical forward propagation process for handwritten digits classification using the CdPS3-Li ion-gate transistor array. The results indicated that as training epochs progressed, the model’s attention gradually focused on the key areas of the target digits, while both accuracy and loss stabilized. Due to the large conductance ratio and high-linearity resistance states of the device, the CNN achieved a training accuracy of 98.56% and a validation accuracy of 97.86% at the 50th epoch (Fig. 5g), enabling accurate image recognition and classification (Supplementary Fig. 28). These results fully demonstrate that CdPS3-Li ionic-gated transistors can not only efficiently perform MAC but also provide nonlinear activation functions. Moreover, light activation and electrical MAC operations are assigned to separate arrays in our system, with a focused laser selectively illuminating only the activation array, leaving the MAC array unexposed and free from optical interference (Supplementary Fig. 29). This functional separation is compatible with integrated photonic-electronic platforms for neuromorphic computing.

Discussion

In the human brain, synapses involve connections between multiple neurons, and each synapse can release various types of neurotransmitters. These neurotransmitters can activate various types of receptors, which influence the excitatory or inhibitory nature of neurons, thereby establishing distinct synaptic weights-a fundamental principle of parallel processing27,38,39. An important feature of 2D CdPS3-Li is that the Li+ ions can be exchanged into other ions such as K+ and Mg2+ through solution replacement (Supplementary Fig. 30), yielding a variety of highly anisotropic layered superionic conductors. This allows us to construct multi-ion synapses (Supplementary Fig. 31a) to simulate parallel processing in the human brain. The cooperative effect among multi-ion synapses significantly enhances the overall synaptic weight (Supplementary Fig. 31b). In this configuration, each ion species contributes differently to conductance modulation, and their combined action generates a broader and more flexible range of synaptic weights than a single-ion synapse can achieve. As a result, multi-ion synapses provide an analogy to the cooperative signaling of neurotransmitters in biological neural networks, thereby offering a foundation for simulating brain-inspired parallel information processing.

Methods

Synthesis of CdPS3-Li crystals

CdPS3-Li crystals were synthesized by one-step CVT method. All chemicals including Cd (99.95%), P2S5 (99%), Li2S (99.9%), S (99.999%), and I2 (99.99%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar, Macklin, or Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. and used without further purification. Briefly, 2 g reagent powders with a specific stoichiometric ratio were mixed in a glovebox with nitrogen atmosphere and then vacuum sealed in a quartz ampoule. The sealed ampoule was placed in a dual-temperature zone furnace with the raw chemicals side set at 750 °C and the opposite side at 650 °C for 5 days. The as-synthesized powders were collected and stored under ambient conditions. The CdPS3-K and CdPS3-Mg crystals were fabricated using ion exchange method by immersing the CdPS3-Li crystals in 0.5 M KCl and MgCl2 aqueous solutions, respectively, for more than one week.

Characterizations of CdPS3-Li and CdPS3-Li /MoS2 heterojunction

HAADF-STEM characterizations were carried out on an aberration-corrected JEM ARM 200 F scanning transmission electron microscopes equipped with a cold field emission gun operated at 200 kV. Cross-sectional samples of CdPS3-Li were made by focused ion beam cutting on Thermo Scientific Helios G4 CX DualBeam system, where the samples were cut at 30 kV and milled with the voltage gradually decreasing from 30 kV to 5 kV to minimize damage. A Bruker Dimension Icon AFM was used for thickness, morphology and surface potential tests, as well as KPFM characterizations. Optical images were collected by a Nikon LV-ND100 microscope. XPS (ESCALAB 250 instrument with Al Kα and He I radiation sources) was used to characterize the element composition of CdPS3-Li. The XPS spectra were fitted using the XPS peak 4.1 software in which a Shirley background was assumed. FTIR spectra were measured using a UV-vis absorption spectrometer (JACSO V-550). The absorption spectra were measured using a UV-vis-NIR spectrometer (Varian Cary 5000). The XRD (Rigaku, D/MAX 2400 and PANalytical, X’Pert PRO using Cu Kα radiation) was used to characterize the structure of CdPS3-Li crystal. The impedance and capacitance measurements were carried out on Autolab electrochemical workstation (PGSTAT204) with frequencies ranging from 20 Hz to 1 MHz and an alternating potential of 150 mV. Fabrication and measurements of 2D CdPS3-Li ion synaptic transistor

We first deposited Ti/Au (Ti/Au: 5 nm/50 nm) as source and drain electrodes on a 300 nm SiO2/Si substrate. The electrodes were then patterned by photolithography, electron beam evaporation, and lift-off processes. Then, the mechanically exfoliated 2D MoS2 nanoflakes were placed on the source and drain electrode. After that, a 2D CdPS3-Li nanoflake was transferred to fully cover MoS2 channel by dry method. Finally, multilayer graphene was transferred to connect CdPS3-Li and gate electrode. The mechanical exfoliation of highly oriented pyrolytic graphite (HOPG), MoS2 crystal and CdPS3-Li crystal was performed using 3 M scotch tape to obtain multilayer graphene, and 2D MoS2 and CdPS3-Li nanoflakes. The tape was gently pressed onto the crystal surface to ensure good contact. It was then carefully peeled off to exfoliate thin nanoflakes. Multiple exfoliation attempts may be necessary to obtain nanoflakes of suitable thickness. After that, the tape was smoothed onto the Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) surface to eliminate air bubbles and ensure close contact between the nanoflakes and the PDMS. The tape was then removed, and suitable sizes and thicknesses of the nanoflakes were identified on the PDMS surface. Subsequently, the nanoflakes were transferred to the target device surface, followed by heating to facilitate their release. Finally, the PDMS stamp was slowly peeled off.

The current of the devices was measured using a Cascade M150 probe station at room temperature, with an Agilent B1500A Semiconductor Device Parameter Analyzer. All pulse measurements were carried out with pulse measurement units in a Keithley 4200A-SCS Parameter Analyzer using home-built LabVIEW programs. For photo response measurements of the ion synaptic transistor, we applied light pulses of 516 nm that were controlled by a laser controller (Thorlabs, ITC4001). The laser controller applied a current of 0.01 mA, yielding a light-source power conversion efficiency (η) of 4.9%.

The optical power was measured with Thorlabs’ Optical Power Meter. The laser has a diameter of 320.06 μm. The intensity of the laser spot is uniform, approximately following a Gaussian distribution from the central region to the edge (Supplementary Fig. 32a). Electrical measurements are conducted using a B1500A semiconductor analyzer, which can detect pulses as short as 1 μs. This is adequate to record laser-induced current changes in the device at rising/falling edges. It accurately captures transitions even under 100 μs light pulses (Supplementary Fig. 20b). The laser employed can generate light pulses as short as 10 μs, meeting the device testing requirements.

Fabrication of 2D CdPS3-Li ion synaptic transistor cross-bar array

Materials for CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor array

In CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor array, MoS2, BN, multilayer graphene and CdPS3-Li serve as the channel layer, bottom dielectric layer, top and bottom gate electrodes, and top dielectric layer, respectively. Monolayer MoS2 film on sapphire substrate, 10-layer BN on Cu, and monolayer graphene film on Cu substrates were all prepared by the chemical vapor deposition (CVD) method. These films completely cover the surfaces of 1 × 1 cm2 Cu and sapphire substrates. Before use, these 2D material films were transferred onto the target substrate by a solution etching transfer method40. In detail, the transfer medium, polypropylene carbonate (PPC) was first spin-coated on the CVD-grown film. After etching away the sapphire or Cu substrates by H3PO4 or copper etchant (Alfa Chemistry), the 2D material films were lifted by the target substrate. Finally, the PPC was washed off in a benzyl ether solution. Note that, multi-layer graphene films were obtained by repeating the transfer process three times. This transfer process effectively maintains film integrity and prevents damage (Supplementary Fig. 32b), making it suitable for constructing large-area device arrays. CdPS3-Li nanoflake was obtained by mechanical exfoliation from CdPS3-Li crystal. The process begins with mechanically exfoliating CdPS3-Li single crystals using adhesive tape into CdPS3-Li nanoflakes on the surface of a blank silicon wafer. After selecting nanoflakes of the desired size, a pre-fabricated PPC/PDMS stamp is carefully attached to the silicon wafer with CdPS3-Li nanoflakes. By leveraging the adhesive properties of PPC and PDMS during the heating and cooling processes, the PPC and nanoflakes are lifted and transferred from the PDMS substrate to the target substrate. During this attachment process, it is crucial to ensure good contact between the nanoflakes and the substrate while avoiding the formation of bubbles or wrinkles. After transfer, the PPC is removed by immersing in anisole solution.

Fabrication of 16 × 10 CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor arrays

We first transferred CVD-grown graphene film from Cu onto SiO2/Si substrate and patterned it using Electron Beam Lithography (EBL) (ABM/6/350/NUV/DCCD/M) with an exposure time of 4.5 s and oxygen plasma (PR500) technology (Supplementary Fig. 33a–c and Supplementary Fig. 34a). Then, we deposited electrodes of 5 nm Ti/20 nm Au by electron beam deposition with the ULVAC ei-501z system for bottom ‘word line’ (Supplementary Fig. 33d and Supplementary Fig. 34b). The deposition rates are 0.13 nm/s for Ti and 0.19 nm/s for Au. Next, we transferred the BN thin films and etched away the excess areas by the RIE-10NR reactive ion etching to serve as bottom dielectric material (a gas ratio of CF3: O2 = 20 sccm: 4 sccm, a gas pressure of 2 Pa, a power setting of 50 W, and a reaction time of 2 mins) (Supplementary Fig. 33e and Supplementary Fig. 34c). Then, we transferred CVD-grown MoS2 film from sapphire onto surface of BN and patterned it using EBL and oxygen plasma technology (Supplementary Fig. 33f, g and Supplementary Fig. 34d). Then, we deposited electrodes of 5 nm Ti/20 nm Au by electron beam deposition with the ULVAC ei-501z system for source-drain electrodes of the MoS2 transistors on the same row (Supplementary Fig. 33h and Supplementary Fig. 34e). Then, we transferred CdPS3-Li nanoflake (50 μm × 60 μm) onto the device surface using a dry method with PPC/PDMS as the transfer stamp (Supplementary Fig. 33i and Supplementary Fig. 34f). Subsequently, we transfer 3 L graphene onto the surface of the device and patterned it using EBL and oxygen plasma technology (Supplementary Fig. 33j, k and Supplementary Fig. 34g, h). Finally, we deposited ‘bit line’ electrodes of Ti/Au to connect the gate electrodes of transistors by electron beam deposition to obtain the device array (Supplementary Fig. 33l).

The area of the device array is 50 μm × 50 μm. The multilayer graphene, MoS2, and BN used in this study are all cm-scale thin films. The CdPS3-Li nanoflake, obtained through mechanical exfoliation, measures 50 μm × 60 μm. All these materials can fully cover the device area (Supplementary Fig. 34g, h).

The fabrication of a 2 × 8 CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor array is nearly identical to that of a 16 × 10 CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor array, except that it does not include a bottom gate.

Density functional theory calculations

All the calculations are performed in the framework of the density functional theory with the projector augmented plane-wave method, as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package41. The exchange-correlation potential is modeled using the generalized gradient approximation by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof42 in conjunction with the DFT-D343 method for van der Waals corrections. The cut-off energy for plane wave is set to 450 eV. The energy criterion is set to 10−5 eV in iterative solution of the Kohn-Sham equation. All the structures are relaxed until the residual forces on the atoms have declined to less than 0.02 eV/Å.

Measurement setup for MAC and activation using CdPS3-Li ion-gated transistor

The measurement setup comprises a dark box with a probe station used to measure the synaptic responses of the device under both light and electrical pulses (Supplementary Fig. 35). Specifically, a signal generator outputs electrical pulse signals, and an oscilloscope verifies the accuracy of these signals. A Python script directly converts MNIST images into electrical pulse parameters (amplitude, width, and number) and transmits them to the signal generator via USB. These electrical pulse as the input signals are applied to the device array to perform matrix multiplication (Supplementary Fig. 36) and output current signal. A laser controller generates light pulses to induce PSC in the device. The PSC is read through the source-drain electrode of the device as the output signal, thereby completing the activation function (Supplementary Fig. 36). All the output currents achieved under electrical pulse and light pulse are read by a Keysight semiconductor parameter analyzer, which finally transmits all measured data back to the PC for further processing and analysis as the output of the neural network.
