Prevalence, antimicrobial resistance, and risk factors of Salmonella in Cattle, environment, and workers at gombe township abattoir, Nigeria, with an assessment of workers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices
Authors: Naason Boyi, Asinamai Athiliamai Bitrus, Benshak John Audu, Wison Bertu James
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abattoirs represent critical interfaces for zoonotic pathogen transmission, yet remain poorly characterized in many low-resource settings. This cross-sectional study investigated the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance, and risk factors of Salmonella in cattle, environment, and workers at Gombe township abattoir, Nigeria, with an assessment of workers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices. A total of 366 samples were collected from slaughtered cattle (caecal and carcass swabs), abattoir environments (slaughter floors, effluents, water for carcass washing), processing equipment (knives, tables, washing basins), and abattoir workers (hand and clothing swabs). Salmonella isolation was performed using standard cultural and biochemical methods. Overall, 47 of 366 samples (12.8%) were positive for Salmonella spp., with significant variation across sampling points (χ²=17.72, df = 9, p = 0.047). Highest contamination was recorded in effluents (22.5%) and slaughter floor swabs (21.9%). Molecular confirmation of ten representative isolates by PCR amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene yielded the expected 1500 bp product; isolates were identified as presumptive Salmonella spp. on the basis of cultural and biochemical characteristics. Resistance was predominant against ampicillin (95.7%) and azithromycin (63.8%), while ciprofloxacin (93.6%) and ceftriaxone (95.7%) retained high efficacy. MDR was detected in 76.6% of isolates. Abattoir workers showed a bimodal knowledge profile (50% good, 50% poor) and predominantly positive general hygiene attitudes (80%) and poor practices (80%). The presence of standing wastewater (OR = 4.03; 95% CI: 3.05–6.17; p = 0.028) was associated with Salmonella detection. Gombe Township Abattoir constitutes a significant reservoir of multidrug-resistant Salmonella, underscoring the urgent need for integrated One Health interventions targeting hygiene, surveillance, and antimicrobial stewardship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05723-1.

Introduction

Meat from food-producing animals provides high-quality protein, essential fatty acids, vitamins, and minerals vital for human health [1]. However, meat is highly perishable and susceptible to microbial contamination at every stage of the production continuum, from on-farm management through transportation, slaughter, processing, distribution, and consumption [2]. Inadequate hygiene, poor infrastructure, weak regulatory oversight, and informal handling practices amplify foodborne disease risks, particularly in low- and middle-income countries like Nigeria [3]. Among bacterial pathogens, Salmonella species remain a leading cause of foodborne morbidity and mortality worldwide [4, 5].

Salmonella spp. are Gram-negative, rod-shaped bacteria of the family Enterobacteriaceae, primarily classified under Salmonella enterica, which accounts for most human and animal infections [6]. Key serovars such as Typhimurium, Enteritidis, and Dublin are frequently linked to gastroenteritis, invasive disease, and outbreaks. Transmission to humans occurs mainly via consumption of faecally contaminated meat, especially during post-slaughter operations like evisceration and carcass dressing [7, 8].

Food animals, particularly cattle, poultry, and swine, serve as primary reservoirs, often carrying Salmonella asymptomatically in their intestines and contaminating carcasses and environments during slaughter [9, 10]. Additional transmission routes include contaminated water and direct animal contact. Globally, Salmonella causes an estimated 93 million gastroenteritis cases and 155,000 deaths annually, with Africa bearing a disproportionate burden [11, 12].

In Nigeria, surveillance gaps obscure the true burden of salmonellosis [13, 14]. Historical data from Lagos State (1999–2008) indicate substantial morbidity and mortality, yet recent location-specific epidemiological information is scarce. Abattoirs are critical control points but often feature suboptimal hygiene, inadequate waste management, and poor inspection practices that facilitate Salmonella persistence [15].

Beyond food safety, Salmonella contributes significantly to antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Unregulated antimicrobial use in livestock drives multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains, with Nigerian studies reporting resistance rates exceeding 80% to multiple classes [16, 17]. This compromises treatment and underscores the need for integrated One Health surveillance.

In Gombe State, daily cattle slaughter meets growing protein demands, yet empirical data on Salmonella prevalence, AMR profiles, and risk factors across cattle, abattoir environments, and workers are lacking. This study therefore aimed to determine the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and associated risk factors of Salmonella species isolated from slaughtered cattle, the abattoir environment, and abattoir workers at Gombe Township Abattoir.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted at Gombe Township Abattoir, Gombe State, North-Eastern Nigeria. Gombe State lies between latitudes 10.08°N and 11.02°N and longitudes 11.02°E and 11.18°E (Fig. 1). It shares borders with Borno and Yobe States to the north and east, Adamawa and Taraba States to the south, and Bauchi State to the west. The state has an estimated population of 2.4 million, with many residents engaged in farming and livestock production. Gombe Township Abattoir serves as the primary slaughter and meat distribution hub in the state.

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted between August and November 2025 to determine the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and associated risk factors of Salmonella spp. in slaughtered cattle, the abattoir environment, and abattoir workers.

Study population

The study population included slaughtered cattle presented for routine slaughter, abattoir workers involved in slaughtering and meat handling, and the abattoir environment (equipment, surfaces, water, and effluents).

Sample size determination

Sample size was calculated using the formula for single population proportion [18]: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n=\frac{{Z}^{2}\cdot\:P\cdot\:(1-P)}{{d}^{2}}$$\end{document}where n= required sample size, Z= standard normal deviation (1.96 at 95% confidence), P= expected prevalence, and d= desired absolute precision. Expected prevalences of 15.5% (abattoir environment), 4.8% (slaughtered cattle), and 4.2% (workers) from Aworh [11] were used. Calculations were performed for each category, with a 5% precision at 95% confidence level. A total of 333 samples was obtained. Adjusting for a 10% non-response/sampling error yielded 366 samples, allocated as follows: slaughtered cattle [caecal swabs (n = 42), carcass swabs (n = 42)]; equipment [knives (n = 45), tables (n = 40), washing trough (n = 40)]; environment [slaughter floor (n = 32), effluents (n = 40)]; water samples (pooled); abattoir workers [hands (n = 42), clothes (n = 42)].

Sampling technique

A simple random sampling technique was employed for the selection of slaughtered cattle, environmental, and equipment samples throughout the study period (August-November 2025). The sampling frame for slaughtered cattle comprised all cattle presented for slaughter on each sampling day at Gombe Township Abattoir. At the beginning of each visit, the total number of cattle scheduled for slaughter was obtained from the abattoir slaughter register; each animal was assigned a unique identification number, and the required number of animals was selected using a random-number table. Both caecal and carcass swabs were collected from each selected animal, such that the 42 caecal and 42 carcass samples were obtained from the same 42 randomly selected cattle. For environmental and equipment samples, all eligible slaughter-floor points, knives, tables, washing basins, and other designated sampling units available during each visit constituted the sampling frame; individual units were assigned identification numbers and selected by simple random sampling. Water samples were collected from the single operational water source used for carcass washing during each visit. Hand and clothing swabs were collected from 42 of the 45 abattoir workers who consented to swabbing, the remaining 3 having declined. For the KAP survey, all 45 eligible workers engaged in slaughtering, carcass processing, meat handling, and sanitation were invited to participate, and 20 (44.4%) provided written informed consent and completed the questionnaire. Sampling was repeated across visits between August and November 2025 until the predetermined sample size for each category was reached.

Sample collection

Samples were collected aseptically using sterile cotton swabs pre-moistened with buffered peptone water (BPW). Carcass and surface swabs were taken from approximately 100–400 cm² areas using standardized templates where applicable. Water samples (50 mL) used for carcass washing were collected in sterile containers. All samples were labelled, placed in ice packs, and transported within 4–6 h to the Microbiology Laboratory, Gombe State University. Sample collection was carried out over 24 visits to the abattoir between August and November 2025. To avoid duplication, each abattoir worker, animal, and carcass was sampled only once during the study period; no individual contributed more than one set of samples or completed the questionnaire more than once.

Isolation and identification of Salmonella

Isolation followed standard methods [19], 0.5 mL of water or pooled swabs were transferred into 9 mL buffered peptone water (BPW) and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Furthermore, 0.1 mL of pre-enriched culture was transferred to 10 mL Rappaport-Vassiliadis broth (RVB) and incubated at 42 °C for 24 h. Another 1 mL was transferred to 10 mL selenite F broth (SFB) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. A loopful from each enrichment broth was streaked onto Salmonella-Shigella agar (SSA) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Presumptive colonies were sub-cultured onto Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Pink colonies with black centres on XLD were considered presumptive Salmonella. On Salmonella-Shigella agar, circular, transparent colonies with black centres (H2S production). On Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate, red colonies with black centres. Gram Staining was performed as described by Cheesbrough [19] and Salmonella appears as Gram-negative rods.

Biochemical tests

Biochemical characterization of the isolates was performed in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines [20]. Presumptive Salmonella isolates demonstrated the following biochemical characteristics: catalase-positive, oxidase-negative, indole-negative, motile, Voges-Proskauer-negative, citrate-positive, urease-negative, and characteristic reactions on triple sugar iron (TSI) agar, including an alkaline slant/acid butt with H₂S production. Reactions on Kligler iron agar were also assessed as part of the biochemical identification process.

Molecular confirmation of isolates

Genomic DNA was extracted from presumptive Salmonella colonies using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with slight modifications [21]. Briefly, bacterial cells from overnight cultures were suspended in 140 µL of sample buffer. Lysis was performed by adding 560 µL of lysis buffer, vortexing for 15 s, and incubating at room temperature for 10 min. Then, 560 µL of absolute ethanol was added and vortexed for another 15 s. The mixture was transferred into a spin column and centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 1 min. The column was washed with 500 µL Buffer AW1 (6,000 × g for 1 min) and 500 µL Buffer AW2 (14,000 rpm for 3 min), followed by an additional dry spin at 20,000 × g for 1 min. DNA was eluted in 60 µL of elution buffer after 1 min incubation at room temperature and centrifuged at 6,000 × g for 1 min. Extracted DNA was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and stored at -20 °C until use.

The 16 S rRNA gene was amplified for molecular confirmation of the isolates. PCR was performed in a 20 µL reaction volume containing 4 µL PCR master mix, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 2 µL DNA template (50–100 ng/µL), and 12 µL nuclease-free water. Universal 16 S rRNA primers (F: 5′-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′ and R: 5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) were used. Thermal cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 58 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min [22].

PCR products were analysed by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide in 1X TAE buffer. Five microlitres of each amplicon mixed with loading dye was loaded into the wells and run at 80 V for 40–45 min. A 1 kb DNA ladder was used as a molecular weight marker. The gel was visualised under UV transillumination. The presence of a clear band at approximately 1,500 bp was consistent with successful amplification of bacterial genomic DNA. Identification as presumptive Salmonella spp. was based on cultural and biochemical characteristics; the universal 16 S rRNA target is not genus-specific and was used as a general molecular check rather than for definitive identification. Positive (Salmonella Typhimurium ATCC 14028) and negative (no-template) controls were included in every PCR run.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar (Oxoid) per CLSI guidelines [20]. Antibiotics tested (from BD Sensi-Discs) were: azithromycin (15 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), and ceftriaxone (30 µg). Salmonella enterica ATCC 14,028 was used as a quality positive control strain. Zones were interpreted according to CLSI [20] breakpoints.

The antimicrobial panel was selected to represent six distinct antimicrobial classes of clinical and public-health relevance to salmonellosis and to permit assessment of multidrug resistance across classes. Ciprofloxacin (fluoroquinolone), ceftriaxone (third-generation cephalosporin), and azithromycin (macrolide) are the principal agents recommended for treating invasive non-typhoidal and typhoidal Salmonella infections and are classified by the World Health Organization as Highest Priority Critically Important Antimicrobials for human medicine, making resistance surveillance in food-animal and environmental isolates a One Health priority. Ampicillin (aminopenicillin) and chloramphenicol (phenicol) are historical first-line agents for salmonellosis and serve as markers of long-standing, classical resistance, while gentamicin (aminoglycoside) is widely used in both human and veterinary practice in Nigeria. The selected agents are commonly used in the study region, are supported by CLSI interpretive criteria, and align with antimicrobials routinely reported in regional and global Salmonella surveillance, facilitating comparison with previous studies.

Multidrug resistance

Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as acquired resistance to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial classes [23] and this was the criteria used in this study. The Multiple Antibiotic Resistance (MAR) index was calculated as: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{MAR}=\frac{a}{b}\:$$\end{document}where a= number of antibiotics to which the isolate was resistant, and b= total number of antibiotics tested [24].

Knowledge, attitude and practice survey

A structured questionnaire was developed specifically for this study to assess workers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding Salmonella, food safety, hygiene, and antimicrobial use and resistance. The questionnaire was reviewed by experts in veterinary public health and epidemiology to ensure content validity. It was subsequently pre-tested among five abattoir workers at a different slaughter slab within Gombe Local Government Area that was not included in the main study, and these pre-test participants were excluded from the final survey. Feedback obtained during pre-testing was used to refine the instrument by rewording technical terms to enhance comprehension, translating selected questions into Hausa and Fulfulde to facilitate communication with respondents, and rearranging sections to improve the logical flow of questions. The revised questionnaire was administered via face-to-face interviews at Gombe Township Abattoir. All 45 eligible abattoir workers were invited to participate, and 20 (44.4%) provided written informed consent and completed the survey; because the entire eligible workforce was approached rather than sampled, no separate sample-size calculation was performed for this component. KAP responses were scored as follows: For knowledge, each of the 10 items was scored as 1 (correct response) or 0 (incorrect or “do not know” response), giving a possible score range of 0–10. Respondents scoring ≥ 60% (i.e., ≥ 6 out of 10) were classified as having good knowledge, while those scoring < 60% were classified as having poor knowledge. For attitude, each of the 10 items was scored on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree), giving a possible score range of 10–50. Respondents scoring ≥ 60% of the maximum possible score (i.e., ≥ 30) were classified as having a positive attitude, while those scoring < 60% were classified as having a negative attitude. For practice, each of the 11 items was scored on a five-point frequency scale (1 = Never to 5 = Always), giving a possible score range of 11–55. Respondents scoring ≥ 60% of the maximum possible score (i.e., ≥ 33) were classified as having good practices, while those scoring < 60% were classified as having poor practices. Internal consistency of the KAP instrument was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. Values of ≥ 0.90, 0.80–0.89, and 0.70–0.79 are interpreted as excellent, good, and acceptable internal consistency, respectively [25]. The overall Cronbach’s α for the KAP scores was calculated as 0.981 and separately for knowledge, attitude and practice were estimated as 0.947, 0.900, and 0.987, respectively.

Quality control

Media sterility was checked, and performance controls were used. Reference strains (Salmonella Typhimurium ATCC 14028, E. coli ATCC 25922, Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212) were included. Aseptic techniques and equipment calibration were maintained throughout.

Data analysis

Data were summarised using tables, charts, and figures. Prevalence was calculated as: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{Prevalence (\%)} = \frac{\text{Number of positive samples}}{\text{Total samples tested}} \times 100$$\end{document} with 95% confidence intervals. Chi-square tests assessed associations, and logistic regression identified risk factors (SPSS Version 27, Chicago, USA). A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Occurrence of presumptive Salmonella spp. in abattoir samples

A total of 366 samples collected from slaughtered cattle, the abattoir environment, equipment, and abattoir workers were examined for presumptive Salmonella spp. Out of these, 47 samples were positive, giving an overall prevalence of 12.8% (Table 1). A Chi-square test showed statistically significant difference in the distribution of Salmonella isolates across sampling points (χ² = 17.72, df = 9, p = 0.047) (Table 1).

Biochemical characterization of presumptive Salmonella isolates

A total of 47 presumptive Salmonella isolates recovered from different sampling points were subjected to standard biochemical tests for confirmation. The biochemical reactions were uniform among isolates obtained from the same sampling points; therefore, representative isolates were selected for tabulation. All 47 isolates (100%) were Gram-negative rods and motile. Citrate utilization and catalase positive was observed in 47 isolates (100%), while indole and urease were negative in 45 isolates (95.7%). On triple sugar iron (TSI) agar, 44 isolates (93.6%) produced an alkaline slant and acidic butt (red slant/yellow butt) with hydrogen sulphide (H2S) production, and gas production was observed in 42 isolates (89.4%). Based on the combination of cultural characteristics and biochemical reactions, 47 isolates (100%) were identified as Salmonella sp. (Table 2).

Molecular detection of Salmonella isolates

Molecular confirmation of the Salmonella isolates was carried out using PCR amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene. A total of 10 representative isolates were selected from the 47 Salmonella isolates previously confirmed by cultural and biochemical characterization. One isolate was obtained from each sampled source, namely the caecum, carcass, slaughtering floor, washing basin, effluent, processing water, knife, table, clothes, and hands, to ensure representation across the different sampling sites. Amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene produced an expected band of 1500 bp for the representative isolates consistent with bacterial genomic DNA of the expected size. Genus-specific molecular confirmation and serotyping were not performed (Fig. 2).

Antimicrobial susceptibility profile of Salmonella isolates.

High levels of resistance were observed against ampicillin, with 45 out of 47 isolates (95.7%) classified as resistant. Resistance to azithromycin was detected in 30 isolates (63.8%), while 10 isolates (21.3%) showed intermediate susceptibility. All isolates were fully susceptible or intermediate, with no resistance observed to ciprofloxacin and ceftriaxone, with 44 (93.6%) and 45 (95.7%) isolates classified as susceptible, respectively. Variable susceptibility patterns were observed for chloramphenicol and gentamicin. Resistance to chloramphenicol was observed in 13 isolates (27.7%), while 18 isolates (38.3%) showed intermediate susceptibility. Resistance to gentamicin was detected in 6 isolates (12.8%), with 15 isolates (31.9%) showing intermediate susceptibility (Table 3).

Antimicrobial susceptibility profile of Salmonella isolates by sample type

The distribution of antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of Salmonella isolates by sampling point is presented in (Table 4). Resistance to ampicillin was widespread across all sampling points, with resistant proportions ranging from 88.9% among effluent isolates to 100.0% among caecal, carcass, slaughter floor, water, and abattoir worker isolates. Resistance to azithromycin was also detected across most sampling points, with the highest proportion observed among effluent isolates (77.8%) (Table 4). Intermediate susceptibility to ciprofloxacin was detected at low frequencies among isolates from caecal swabs, slaughter floor swabs, and equipment surfaces. No intermediate susceptibility to ceftriaxone was observed in isolates from any sampling point (Table 4). High levels of susceptibility to ciprofloxacin and ceftriaxone were observed across all sampling points, with overall susceptibilities of 93.6% and 95.7%, respectively (Table 4).

Antimicrobial resistance patterns of Salmonella isolates

Multidrug resistance was defined as resistance to three or more classes of antimicrobial agents. Out of the 47 Salmonella isolates, 36 isolates (76.6%) were classified as multidrug resistant, while 11 isolates (23.4%) were non-MDR. The highest proportion of MDR isolates was observed among isolates recovered from water samples (100.0%), followed by effluent isolates (88.9%), caecal swabs (85.7%), and carcass swabs (80.0%). MDR prevalence among isolates from equipment and abattoir workers was 66.7% each (Table 5). A total of 36 out of 47 Salmonella isolates (76.6%) exhibited MDR to at least 3 different antimicrobial classes. The most frequently (25.5%) observed MDR pattern was AMP-AZM-CHL (MAR index = 0.50), followed by AMP-AZM-CHL-GEN (23.4%, MAR index = 0.67). All resistance patterns included resistance to ampicillin (Table 6).

The age distribution showed that 8 respondents (40.0%) were aged 36–45 years, 4 (20.0%) were aged 26–35 years, 3 (15.0%) were aged 18–25 years, 3 (15.0%) were aged 45–55 years, and 2 (10.0%) were aged above 55 years. Knowledge

Knowledge that Salmonella can be transmitted to humans through contaminated meat was reported by 10 respondents (50.0%), while none (0.0%) considered the statement false and 10 respondents (50.0%) did not know. Awareness that healthy-looking animals can carry Salmonella infection was low, with only 4 respondents (20.0%) answering correctly, whereas 10 respondents (50.0%) answered incorrectly and 6 (30.0%) did not know. Knowledge that poor hand hygiene can increase the spread of Salmonella was reported by 10 respondents (50.0%), while none (0.0%) answered incorrectly and 10 respondents (50.0%) did not know.

All respondents (20; 100.0%) correctly identified that contaminated knives and equipment can spread infection, with no incorrect or uncertain responses recorded. Knowledge that unsafe water used during slaughter can contaminate meat was reported by 10 respondents (50.0%), while none (0.0%) answered incorrectly and 10 respondents (50.0%) did not know. Awareness that wearing protective clothing can reduce contamination during meat processing was reported by only 4 respondents (20.0%), whereas 10 respondents (50.0%) answered incorrectly and 6 (30.0%) did not know.

Knowledge that proper cooking of meat reduces the risk of Salmonella infection was reported by 10 respondents (50.0%), while none (0.0%) answered incorrectly and 10 respondents (50.0%) did not know. Only 4 respondents (20.0%) were aware that improper use of antibiotics in animals can contribute to antimicrobial resistance, while none (0.0%) answered incorrectly and 16 respondents (80.0%) did not know. Similarly, awareness that antimicrobial-resistant bacteria can be transmitted from animals to humans was reported by 4 respondents (20.0%), whereas 10 respondents (50.0%) answered incorrectly and 6 (30.0%) did not know. Knowledge that regular cleaning and disinfection help prevent food contamination was reported by 10 respondents (50.0%), while none (0.0%) answered incorrectly and 10 respondents (50.0%) did not know.

Overall, 10 respondents (50.0%) demonstrated good knowledge, while 10 respondents (50.0%) had poor knowledge. The mean knowledge score was 4.30 ± 3.67, indicating a generally low level of knowledge among the respondents (Table 8).

Attitudes

Handwashing before handling meat received mixed responses. Four respondents (20.0%) strongly agreed that it is necessary, 4 (20.0%) agreed, 4 (20.0%) were neutral, 4 (20.0%) disagreed, and 4 (20.0%) strongly disagreed. Most respondents supported the use of protective clothing during slaughter operations, with 12 (60.0%) agreeing and 4 (20.0%) remaining neutral, while 4 (20.0%) disagreed. None strongly agreed or strongly disagreed. Similarly, 4 respondents (20.0%) strongly agreed and 8 (40.0%) agreed that regular hygiene training should be mandatory for abattoir workers, whereas 8 (40.0%) were neutral. All respondents (20; 100.0%) agreed that equipment should be cleaned after every slaughter operation. The importance of safe water for hygienic meat processing was also widely recognized, with 4 respondents (20.0%) strongly agreeing and 16 (80.0%) agreeing. Only 4 respondents (20.0%) agreed that antimicrobial resistance is a serious public health concern, while 8 (40.0%) were neutral and another 8 (40.0%) disagreed. Most respondents supported stricter enforcement of hygiene regulations by abattoir management, with 12 (60.0%) agreeing and 8 (40.0%) remaining neutral. Likewise, 16 respondents (80.0%) agreed that regular inspection of abattoirs improves food safety, while 4 (20.0%) were neutral. Four respondents (20.0%) strongly agreed and 12 (60.0%) agreed that workers have a responsibility to prevent contamination, while 4 (20.0%) were neutral. A similar pattern was observed for adherence to food safety practices during busy periods, where 12 respondents (60.0%) agreed and 8 (40.0%) were neutral. Overall, 16 respondents (80.0%) had a positive attitude toward food safety and hygiene practices, whereas 4 (20.0%) had a negative attitude. The mean attitude score was 36.20 ± 5.25 (Table 9).

Practice

Handwashing before handling meat was inconsistent, with 4 respondents (20.0%) indicating that they always washed their hands, 4 (20.0%) often did so, 4 (20.0%) sometimes practiced handwashing, 4 (20.0%) rarely did so, and 4 (20.0%) never washed their hands before handling meat. Handwashing after handling carcasses was less common. Four respondents (20.0%) reported washing their hands often, 4 (20.0%) sometimes, 8 (40.0%) rarely, and 4 (20.0%) never. The use of soap during handwashing was also limited, with 4 respondents (20.0%) reporting frequent use, 4 (20.0%) using it sometimes, 4 (20.0%) rarely, and 8 (40.0%) never using soap. Similarly, only 4 respondents (20.0%) often wore protective clothing during work, while 8 (40.0%) rarely wore protective clothing and another 8 (40.0%) never did so. Cleaning of knives after processing each carcass was infrequent. Four respondents (20.0%) reported doing so often, 4 (20.0%) rarely, and 12 (60.0%) never cleaned their knives after each carcass. Cleaning of work surfaces after use was reported by 4 respondents (20.0%) often, 8 (40.0%) sometimes, and 8 (40.0%) rarely.

The use of clean water during meat processing was one of the more common practices observed. Eight respondents (40.0%) reported always using clean water, 8 (40.0%) often used clean water, and 4 (20.0%) did so sometimes. Washing of work clothes was not routinely practiced, with 4 respondents (20.0%) doing so often, 4 (20.0%) sometimes, 8 (40.0%) rarely, and 4 (20.0%) never washing their work clothes regularly. The use of disinfectants when cleaning equipment and surfaces was particularly poor. Only 4 respondents (20.0%) reported using disinfectants sometimes, while 4 (20.0%) rarely used them and 12 (60.0%) never did. Appropriate disposal of slaughter waste was reported often by 4 respondents (20.0%), sometimes by 4 (20.0%), rarely by 8 (40.0%), and never by 4 (20.0%). Reporting hygiene or sanitation problems to supervisors was also uncommon, with 4 respondents (20.0%) indicating that they sometimes reported such problems, 8 (40.0%) rarely did so, and 8 (40.0%) never reported them. Risk factors associated with Salmonella occurrence and transmission

A higher proportion of Salmonella-positive samples was recorded among cattle held in lairage for more than 2 h (42.1%; 8/19) compared with those held for less than 2 h (17.4%; 4/23). Although the odds of Salmonella occurrence were higher among cattle with longer lairage periods (OR = 3.45; 95% CI: 1.33–5.79), the association was not statistically significant (p = 0.064). No Salmonella was detected in samples collected before evisceration (0.0%; 0/12), whereas 16.7% (5/30) of samples collected after evisceration were positive. Despite this difference, processing stage was not significantly associated with Salmonella occurrence (p = 0.298).

Discussion

This study evaluated the prevalence of presumptive Salmonella contamination, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and associated operational risk factors in Gombe Township Abattoir, Gombe State, Nigeria. The overall prevalence of 12.8% is consistent with the 13.4% reported in a recent Ethiopian abattoir study [26] but higher than the 3.4% documented in Abuja, Nigeria [16], and lower than the 33.5% recorded in Anambra State, Nigeria [27]. The similarity with the Ethiopian findings likely reflects comparable hygiene standards and operational conditions common in many low-resource slaughter settings across sub-Saharan Africa. In contrast, the lower prevalence in Abuja may indicate better regulatory oversight and infrastructure, while the higher rate in Anambra probably stems from more informal practices, poorer sanitation, and sampling of high-risk matrices. These variations highlight how abattoir-specific conditions strongly influence Salmonella contamination levels and underscore that meaningful reductions in slaughterhouse contamination remain elusive across much of the region.

Significant differences in Salmonella recovery across sample types emphasise the ecological complexity of contamination pathways in abattoirs. Higher isolation rates from effluents, slaughter floors, caecal contents, and carcass swabs align with multiple studies showing that faecal shedding and environmental persistence drive pathogen transmission. Similar patterns have been reported in Nigeria, where Salmonella was more frequently isolated from slaughter floors and intestinal contents than from live animals [27], and in abattoir wastewater acting as a reservoir for resistant strains [28]. Environmental surfaces such as floors, drains, and effluents provide moist, nutrient-rich niches that support Salmonella survival and recontamination of carcasses and equipment [29, 30]. Published molecular epidemiological studies from other settings have demonstrated genetic relatedness between Salmonella isolates from carcasses and the abattoir environment [31, 32], supporting the slaughterhouse as an active amplification site rather than a passive conduit. In the present study, however, genotyping was not undertaken; our recovery of phenotypically similar isolates across compartments therefore indicates co-occurrence rather than demonstrated clonal linkage. In well-regulated systems with effective zoning, wastewater management, and validated sanitation protocols, environmental recovery rates are substantially lower [33], reinforcing that infrastructural and operational deficiencies are primary drivers of contamination in this setting.

Phenotypic and biochemical characteristics of the isolates were consistent with standard descriptions of Salmonella spp. Culture-based and biochemical methods remain reliable frontline tools for surveillance in resource-limited settings [19, 20, 34], although whole-genome sequencing is increasingly used for high-resolution source tracking and resistance profiling. Molecular confirmation via 16 S rRNA PCR produced the expected ~ 1,500 bp amplicon consistent with bacterial genomic DNA of the expected size. This finding aligns with previous studies [35, 36] and demonstrates good concordance between conventional and molecular methods, validating the use of classical approaches where advanced diagnostics are unavailable [21]. Even where some of these presumptive isolates ultimately represent non-pathogenic Salmonella-like Enterobacteriaceae rather than confirmed pathogenic serovars, their recovery from carcasses, equipment, effluents, and worker swabs remains informative as an indicator of faecal contamination and of the broader hygienic conditions under which genuinely pathogenic and resistant strains could also persist and be selected for.

The antimicrobial resistance profile revealed a concerning pattern, with near-universal resistance to ampicillin. This reflects long-term selective pressure from widespread, often unregulated use of β-lactams in Nigerian livestock production [37–39]. Resistance is commonly mediated by plasmid-borne genes such as blaTEM-1, which are readily disseminated via horizontal gene transfer. High resistance to azithromycin is particularly worrisome given its increasing role in treating invasive non-typhoidal salmonellosis, especially where fluoroquinolone resistance is emerging. In contrast, high susceptibility to ciprofloxacin and ceftriaxone was observed. This pattern is consistent with several African studies where these critically important antimicrobials retain better efficacy in food-animal isolates due to relatively restricted use in livestock compared to older agents [10, 40]. Continued prudent use and surveillance are essential to preserve their effectiveness.

Multidrug resistance (MDR) was detected in 76.6% of isolates, with ampicillin resistance forming the core of most MDR profiles. This high MDR prevalence mirrors global trends in livestock-associated Salmonella driven by antimicrobial overuse and mobile genetic elements [41, 42]. Abattoir effluents and environmental samples served as notable reservoirs of MDR strains, consistent with their potential role as shared contamination points across animal, environmental, and human-occupational compartments of the abattoir [11, 28].

Overall knowledge was evenly divided, with 50.0% of workers classified as having good knowledge and 50.0% having poor knowledge. However, workers appeared to be more familiar with general food hygiene than with antimicrobial resistance. All respondents correctly recognised that contaminated knives and equipment can spread infection, while half correctly identified contaminated meat, poor hand hygiene, unsafe water, and inadequate cleaning as potential sources of contamination. In contrast, only 20.0% knew that inappropriate antibiotic use in animals contributes to antimicrobial resistance, and only 20.0% were aware that antimicrobial-resistant bacteria can be transmitted from animals to humans. These findings suggest that workers are generally aware of basic hygiene measures but have limited understanding of antimicrobial resistance and its implications for public health. Similar observations have been reported in a recent scoping review of KAP studies among abattoir workers in low- and middle-income countries, where awareness of food safety and zoonotic diseases was generally higher than awareness of antimicrobial use and resistance [43]. Comparable findings have also been reported among abattoir and butcher-shop workers in Ethiopia, where respondents demonstrated reasonable knowledge of hygiene practices but had limited awareness of specific foodborne hazards and antimicrobial resistance [44, 45]. In Lagos, Nigeria, meat handlers have been reported to possess relatively higher levels of hygiene knowledge [46]. The findings of the present study therefore suggest that improving antimicrobial resistance awareness should be a priority for future training programmes at Gombe Township Abattoir.

Most workers demonstrated a positive attitude toward food safety and hygiene. All respondents agreed that slaughter equipment should be cleaned after every operation and recognised the importance of safe water during meat processing. However, attitudes towards antimicrobial resistance were less favourable. Only 20.0% considered antimicrobial resistance to be a serious public health concern, while 40.0% were neutral and another 40.0% disagreed. This pattern suggests that workers readily appreciate visible hygiene-related risks but are less aware of the public health consequences of antimicrobial resistance. Similar findings have been reported among meat and food handlers in other parts of West Africa, where positive attitudes towards food hygiene were not accompanied by comparable concern about antimicrobial resistance and antimicrobial use [47]. Antimicrobial resistance is often less visible than the day-to-day hygiene challenges encountered in slaughter environments, which may explain the limited concern expressed by workers in the present study [43]. These findings highlight the need for targeted awareness programmes that specifically address antimicrobial resistance rather than relying solely on general food-safety education.

Although attitudes towards food safety were generally positive, hygiene practices remained poor among most workers. Handwashing after handling carcasses and the use of soap were reported as rare or absent by most respondents. Cleaning knives after processing each carcass and the use of disinfectants during equipment cleaning were also uncommon. These findings suggest that knowledge and positive attitudes were not consistently translated into safe hygienic practices. Similar gaps between knowledge and practice have been reported among meat handlers in Ethiopia, where workers demonstrated acceptable knowledge but poor adherence to recommended hygiene measures [45]. A comparable pattern has also been reported among food handlers in Ghana, where adequate food-safety knowledge did not always result in appropriate food-handling practices [48]. The poor practices observed in the present study may be partly explained by the limited availability of sanitation resources and the lack of formal training. Nearly all workers had not received hygiene training, and basic sanitation materials such as soap, disinfectants, and protective clothing were largely unavailable. Similar infrastructural challenges have been identified in other Nigerian abattoirs, where inadequate facilities and poor management practices were found to hinder the implementation of proper hygiene measures despite reasonable awareness among workers [49, 50]. These findings indicate that improving worker knowledge alone is unlikely to achieve substantial improvements in hygiene practices unless accompanied by investments in sanitation infrastructure, regular training, and effective supervision.

Among the factors examined, only standing wastewater was significantly associated with Salmonella occurrence. Samples collected from areas where standing wastewater was present had a substantially higher prevalence of Salmonella (37.1%) than those collected from areas without standing wastewater (10.5%). This finding suggests that stagnant wastewater may serve as an important environmental source of contamination within the abattoir. Wastewater provides favourable conditions for the persistence and spread of enteric pathogens and can facilitate contamination of carcasses, equipment, and surrounding surfaces through splashing, runoff, and worker movement [29].

The association between standing wastewater and Salmonella contamination observed in this study agrees with previous reports from Nigerian abattoirs where Salmonella has been isolated from wastewater channels and drainage systems [50]. Abattoir effluents have also been shown to contain multidrug-resistant Salmonella strains capable of contaminating surrounding environments and receiving water bodies [51]. The ability of Salmonella to survive for prolonged periods in aquatic environments further supports the role of wastewater as a reservoir for contamination within slaughter facilities [29]. These environmental findings are consistent with the poor cleaning and disinfection practices reported by abattoir workers in this study. The majority of workers reported infrequent or absent use of disinfectants (60.0% never disinfected equipment or surfaces) and inconsistent waste disposal (40.0% rarely or never disposed of slaughter waste appropriately), while only 20.0% demonstrated overall good hygiene practices (Table 10). The convergence of these self-reported practice deficits with the elevated Salmonella prevalence observed in effluents (22.5%) and slaughter floor swabs (21.9%); the two sample types with the highest contamination rates and with the significant association between standing wastewater and Salmonella detection (OR = 4.03), suggests a plausible operational pathway: inadequate cleaning and waste disposal practices allow organic matter and wastewater to accumulate on the slaughter floor and in drainage channels, creating conditions that favour Salmonella persistence and onward contamination of equipment, carcasses, and workers. Addressing this pathway would likely require concurrent improvements in waste disposal infrastructure, routine disinfection protocols, and worker training, rather than any single intervention in isolation.

Although not statistically significant, higher Salmonella prevalence was observed among cattle subjected to longer lairage periods, on wet or soiled surfaces, and on visibly contaminated equipment. Cattle held for more than two hours before slaughter showed a higher prevalence than those held for shorter periods. Likewise, wet or soiled surfaces and visibly contaminated equipment yielded higher recovery rates than clean surfaces and equipment. These findings are biologically plausible because moisture, organic matter, and prolonged animal holding periods may increase opportunities for pathogen survival and cross-contamination. Similar associations have been reported in previous studies, where contaminated surfaces and equipment contributed to the persistence and spread of Salmonella within slaughter environments [28, 50, 52].

No significant association was observed between personal protective equipment use and Salmonella occurrence. However, interpretation of this finding should be made with caution because only a small proportion of workers reported using PPE, resulting in limited statistical power. The lack of significance therefore does not necessarily indicate that PPE is ineffective, particularly given the established role of protective equipment in reducing occupational exposure to biological hazards. Overall, improving wastewater management should therefore be prioritised alongside measures aimed at strengthening equipment sanitation, surface hygiene, worker training, and lairage management.

From a One Health perspective, the principal value of this study lies in the simultaneous detection of presumptive Salmonella across the animal, environmental, and human-occupational compartments of a single slaughter interface, rather than in any compartment in isolation. The recovery of biochemically similar isolates sharing a dominant ampicillin-anchored multidrug-resistance pattern from caecal contents, carcasses, slaughter-floor and effluent samples, equipment, and worker swabs indicates a shared contamination pool at the human-animal-environment interface and identifies the abattoir as a plausible convergence point for cross-sectoral exposure. It must be emphasised, however, that these data establish co-occurrence and not directional transmission: in the absence of serotyping and discriminatory genotyping, isolates from different sources cannot be shown to be clonally related, nor can the direction of contamination be inferred. The findings therefore support the abattoir as a priority site for integrated One Health surveillance and intervention, while underscoring that molecular source-tracking is required to confirm the linkages implied by these co-occurrence patterns.

Conclusion

This study provides baseline evidence that Gombe Township Abattoir harbours presumptive Salmonella with a high prevalence of multidrug resistance across cattle, environmental, equipment, and worker samples, and identifies standing wastewater as a significant operational risk factor. While confirmation of pathogenic identity and any cross-compartment transmission would require serotyping and genotyping, the contamination and resistance patterns observed, together with the gaps identified between workers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices, point to the need for improved water quality management, enforced equipment sanitation, routine worker training, provision of basic hygiene infrastructure, and strengthened antimicrobial stewardship at this facility.

Limitations of the study

Findings were derived from one abattoir in Gombe State, which may limit the generalisability of results to other abattoirs with different operational practices and hygiene standards. Identification was to genus level using cultural and biochemical methods; serotyping, genus-specific molecular confirmation (e.g., invA), and whole-genome sequencing were not performed. Consequently, biochemically similar Enterobacteriaceae cannot be entirely excluded, serovars were not determined, and the clonal relatedness and directionality of contamination across compartments could not be established. Knowledge, attitudes, and practices of abattoir workers were self-reported and may be affected by recall and social desirability bias, influencing the accuracy of behavioural findings.
