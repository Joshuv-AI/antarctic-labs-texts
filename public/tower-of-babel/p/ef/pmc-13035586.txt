Cognitive Screening Variability by English Proficiency: A Multi-group Confirmatory Factor Analysis Using the National Health and Aging Trends Study Cognitive Screening Battery
Authors: Katherine Kitchens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective cognitive tasks in the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS) are used to classify probable dementia in Medicare beneficiaries. Whether these tasks measure the same latent construct with comparable precision across English-proficiency groups has not been established. While the NHATS offers English and Spanish versions, it remains unclear whether translation alone achieves measurement equivalence. This study tests measurement invariance of the NHATS cognitive screening battery across English-language-proficient (ELP) and limited English-proficient (LEP) groups to determine whether noninvariance produces measurement bias that could misclassify probable dementia and distort prevalence estimates. Methods round 12 (2021) of the NHATS (N = 5,628) data were analyzed using a multi-group confirmatory factor analysis that incorporated the complex survey design. Factor loadings were constrained equal across ELP and LEP groups, and differences in factor (latent) variance and item residual variances were used to assess measurement noninvariance. Results all standardized indicators loaded positively on the cognition factor (p < .001). The factor (latent) variance was larger in ELP (1.80) than LEP (1.49). Residual (error) variances were higher in LEP, especially for clock drawing (ELP 0.87 vs. LEP 1.38) and name recall (ELP 1.07 vs. LEP 2.63), indicating lower measurement precision in the LEP group. Discussion these findings indicate that the NHATS cognitive screening battery does not measure the same construct with equal precision across English-proficiency groups, suggesting that translation alone is insufficient to ensure measurement invariance. Larger factor (latent) variance in ELP and higher residual (error) variance in LEP suggest lower measurement reliability and possible cultural bias in item interpretation. Such differences may lead to misclassification of probable dementia and distort population estimates. These results highlight the need for culturally and linguistically adapted cognitive screening tools to improve the validity of dementia surveillance and research.Keywords: Cognitive screening, English proficiency, National Health and Aging Trends Study, Dementia

Cognitive health disparities among older adults in the United States are a growing concern (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2022), particularly in relation to immigration status. Evidence using the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS) suggests that individuals with immigration backgrounds may have an increased risk of developing or having undiagnosed dementia, with limited English proficiency—defined as a limited ability to understand, speak, or read English—serving as a critical mediator in this relationship (Franco & Choi, 2020). However, the precise mechanisms by which immigration status and English proficiency interact to influence cognitive health remain unclear, and other studies using NHATS data have yielded conflicting evidence and nuanced findings highlighting differences in cognitive performance among various racialized and ethnic groups with immigration backgrounds compared to individuals born in the United States (Moon et al., 2019). When addressing this conflicting evidence, it is crucial to recognize that assuming the validity of standardized cognitive screening tools, such as those used in the NHATS, across cultural and linguistic contexts may compromise the accuracy of determining dementia burden in diverse populations (Gjersing et al., 2010).

Many studies pointing to immigration background as a risk factor for dementia rely on secondary data derived from cognitive screening batteries that may not have been validated for use among diverse groups. Failing to question the cross-cultural validity of these tools can lead to misclassification, inaccurate incidence and prevalence rates, and the implementation of ineffective interventions (Fennig, 2024). Ensuring the cognitive measures are culturally congruent and contextually valid is critical. This study utilizes data from Round 12 of the NHATS (2021), a nationally representative longitudinal exploration of the health and well-being of older adults aged 65 and older with Medicare, to investigate the cross-cultural validity of the cognitive screening battery utilized in the NHATS dementia classification system (Kasper et al., 2013) across groups stratified by English proficiency. The analysis employs a multi-group confirmatory factor analysis to observe for differences in factor (latent) variance and residual (error) variances between the two groups. This study aims to underscore the complex dynamics between English proficiency and cognitive screening accuracy, contributing to the discourse on culturally and linguistically sensitive tools essential for accurate cognitive screening in diverse populations.

Background

Studies have explored the complex interplay between immigration status, English proficiency, and cognitive health among older adults in the United States, employing the NHATS dementia classification system (Kasper et al., 2013). Franco and Choi (2020) found that older adults with Medicare who were born outside the United States had significantly increased odds of developing dementia and having undiagnosed dementia after adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic status, and health factors. Further, they found that limited English proficiency was a critical mediator in this relationship. However, Moon et al. (2019) investigated the relationship between older adults born outside of the United States and dementia prevalence across various racialized and ethnic groups, revealing nuanced patterns. They found that while non-Hispanic white, Hispanic, and other immigrant groups exhibited higher rates of dementia compared to their counterparts born in the United States, participants who identified as non-Hispanic Black and were born outside the United States had lower rates of dementia than those born in the United States. These findings led the authors to conclude that attention should be paid to the cross-cultural validity of dementia screening across diverse populations.

Unraveling these contradictory findings and establishing the burden of probable dementia across diverse populations is crucial in public health research, as it enables cross-cultural comparisons in epidemiological research and informs the development and evaluation of effective interventions and policies (Fennig, 2024; Panter-Brick et al., 2018). However, methodological errors in measurement can lead to a myriad of issues, including misclassification, stigmatization, pathologizing culturally appropriate symptoms, discrepancies in incidence and prevalence rates, under-recognition, misidentification, and misleading generalizations and conclusions (Matos et al., 2022). These problems can have far-reaching consequences, such as implementing harmful programs and policies and the inability to evaluate interventions effectively (Fasfous et al., 2017).

Translating cognitive screening tools developed and validated in one population and assuming their validity in another ignores the complexity of the adaptation process as a highly nuanced task that significantly influences the validity of cross-cultural findings (Fennig, 2024; Gjersing et al., 2010). Translated versions developed initially for English speakers can be problematic (Charlson et al., 2019), as they may lack phrasing equivalence and fail to capture the cultural nuances and contextual factors that shape cognitive processes in populations for which they are translated (Weaver, 2016). Further, simple forward and back translation can result in poor translation quality (Kaiser et al., 2019) and often prioritizes the legitimacy of the source language while neglecting the worldview and context of the community for whom the tool is being translated (Barger et al., 2010; Kasujja et al., 2022).

The assumption that there are “culture-neutral” tests is challenged by Homeostatic Socio-cognitive Systems (HSCS) theory (Nisbett et al., 2001), which emphasizes the profound cognitive differences maintained between diverse cultures. HSCS theory highlights the integrated (i.e., holistic) cognitive processes prevalent in collective cultures, which focus on interconnectivity and relational aspects of the environment, contrasting sharply with the more analytic, salient object-focused cognitive processes that characterize Western thought. HSCS theory posits that these cognitive processes are in a state of homeostasis with the surrounding social practices, influencing and reinforcing each other within a given culture. This theory suggests that cognitive screening tools developed in one cultural context may not adequately capture the cognitive processes and abilities of individuals from different cultural backgrounds.

Given the complexity of cross-cultural cognitive screening and the potential for methodological errors, it is essential for researchers to adopt a culturally sensitive approach when translating and adapting instruments. This approach involves going beyond mere translation and back translation and, instead, considering the cultural, linguistic, and contextual factors that shape cognitive processes in diverse populations. By doing so, researchers can enhance the validity and reliability of their findings and contribute to a more accurate understanding of probable dementia across cultures.

The Current Study

This study aims to contribute toward a more culturally sensitive approach to analyzing the cognitive screening battery utilized in the NHATS dementia classification system (Kasper et al., 2013). Specifically, it investigates whether the factor (latent) variance and residual (error) variances of the factor structure of the NHATS cognitive screening battery vary by English proficiency among older adults with Medicare. It is hypothesized that the factor (latent) variance and residual (error) variances will vary between groups with different English proficiency levels. Specifically, it is expected that individuals with lower English proficiency will show a smaller factor (latent) variance and larger residual (error) variances, suggesting that language proficiency may significantly influence cognitive task performance. The implications of such findings suggest that language ability is a crucial factor influencing performance on the NHATS cognitive screening battery, potentially highlighting the need for culturally sensitive measures in the NHATS dementia classification system. Utilizing the most recent wave of data (National Health and Aging Trends Study [NHATS], 2021), the current study leverages multi-group confirmatory factor analysis to address this aim, seeking to clarify the role of English proficiency and offer insights into the potential implications for dementia classification and cognitive screening strategies.

Method

Data Source

The NHATS is a longitudinal panel study designed to investigate the health and well-being of older adults (aged 65 and older) across the United States who receive Medicare (Freedman et al., 2023). Round 12 of the NHATS, conducted in 2021, was utilized for this analysis (NHATS, 2021). This wave provides comprehensive data collected through in-person interviews, encompassing detailed information on physical, mental, and cognitive health, mobility, health care utilization, living arrangements, and social support, among other variables. The NHATS employs a complex survey design, including stratification, clustering, and weighting, to ensure the representativeness of the Medicare population. This design provides rich, nationally representative data, ensuring the inclusion of a diverse cohort of older adults, including those residing in community and institutional settings (Jiao et al., 2022).

Sample Population

This study’s sample was specifically chosen based on direct responses to a question about English language proficiency, totaling 5,628 unweighted individual observations (N = 49,485,443, weighted). The sample excluded data provided by proxy respondents or collected from institutions to enhance the reliability and accuracy of the analysis. This approach ensured that the dataset included directly assessed measures from the cognitive screening battery and self-reported measures of language proficiency, facilitating a comparative analysis of cognitive screening scores across these groups.

Measures

The cognitive screening battery employed by the NHATS measures cognitive functioning through tasks administered in either English or Spanish that include the immediate and delayed recall of 10 words, the clock drawing test, orientation to time, and name recall. Immediate word recall was measured on a scale of zero to 10, reflecting the count of words immediately repeated (in any order) from one of three fixed word lists. For example, Word List 1 consists of: hotel, river, tree, skin, gold, market, paper, child, king, book. Immediately after the first attempt, a standardized probe was administered to elicit any additional words. Delayed word recall was scored from zero to nine, based on the number of words from the 10-word list recalled in any order after two intervening cognitive tasks presented in a fixed order, which keeps the delay structure consistent across participants. Clock drawing accuracy was scored from zero to five, with higher scores denoting greater precision according to the NHATS scoring rubric. Orientation to time was evaluated on a scale of zero to four, reflecting the accurate recall of the current month, date, year, and day of the week. Name recall was scored from zero to four based on recall of the first and last names of the sitting U.S. President and Vice President in 2021, with the same rubric applied in English and Spanish. A graded scoring system was applied for both orientation to time and name recall, with points incrementally awarded for each correct response. The clock drawing variable had 1.89% missing data, and the delayed word recall had 0.25% missing. The remaining variables—immediate word recall, orientation to time, and name recall—had no missing data. Given the negligible missingness, listwise deletion was used for analyses.

Grouping Variable

English language proficiency was derived from a self-report response to the NHATS question, “How well would you say you understand English when someone is talking to you?” Responses to this question were coded from zero to four, with zero representing “not at all” and four indicating proficiency of a native English speaker. The original scale variable was recoded as dichotomous. Scores less than three were classified as indicating low English proficiency (LEP), and scores three or greater were classified as English language proficient (ELP). This dichotomization facilitated a focused comparative analysis between groups with differing levels of English proficiency (Franco & Choi, 2020).

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

Unweighted descriptive statistics were first computed to summarize the data; means and standard errors for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for dichotomous and categorical variables. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to test measurement invariance of the cognition factor across ELP and LEP groups, as operationalized through the five-test cognitive screening battery in the NHATS dementia classification system. The rationale for employing CFA centers on its precision in validating the hypothesis that observed variables accurately reflect the corresponding latent construct (factor). This step was crucial due to potential concerns regarding the cross-cultural validity of cognitive screening among diverse populations, where cultural and linguistic differences might impact the interpretation and performance of cognitive screening tools (Gjersing et al., 2010; Waheed et al., 2020). The analytic sample was divided into two groups based on English proficiency (ELP and LEP).

Data were analyzed considering the NHATS’s complex survey design, which includes stratification, clustering, and individual weighting. Survey weights were applied to produce nationally representative estimates, ensuring that the analysis reflects the population accurately. However, this method necessitated the selection of goodness-of-fit indices that are robust and interpretable in this framework. Due to the constraints imposed by Stata (Version 18 for Mac, College Station, TX) on analyzing data from complex survey designs, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) and the Coefficient of Determination (CD) were available as the primary indices for assessing model fit and explanatory power. These indices are particularly suited for use in Stata when dealing with weighted survey data, as they provide reliable measures of fit and model explanatory capacity despite the additional complexity. SRMR is advantageous for its interpretability, with values closer to zero indicating a better fit—typically, values below 0.08 are considered acceptable (Hu & Bentler, 1999). Similarly, CD, by indicating the proportion of variance the model explains, is crucial for assessing how effectively the model captures the latent construct, with values closer to one indicating stronger explanatory power.

Subsequently, a multi-group CFA was conducted to compare the factor structure between the ELP and LEP groups, essential for testing the hypothesis that measurement properties of the cognitive screening battery may differ by English proficiency. Factor loadings were constrained to be equal across groups to test metric (loading) invariance, enabling a focused examination of differences in factor (latent) variance and residual (error) variances. This approach was crucial for exploring potential biases in test performance attributed to language proficiency within a unified analytical framework. Given the constraints of Stata’s survey estimation capabilities, the analysis emphasized the statistical significance of these differences across groups. Although direct tests of scalar and residual invariance were not feasible, observing variations in factor (latent) variance and residual (error) variances provided indirect insights into potential measurement discrepancies. This method was chosen to evaluate model fit and the robustness of the latent construct (factor) across the subgroups under study. All analyses were performed in Stata (Version 18 for Mac) (2023).

Results

Table 1 provides descriptive statistics for all study variables for the sample population and English proficiency subgroups. The fit of the CFA model for the total sample (N = 5,628) was evaluated using two goodness-of-fit indicators: the SRMR value was 0.05, indicating a good model fit, and the CD was 0.84, suggesting that a substantial proportion of the variance in the observed variables was accounted for by the cognition factor. The factor (latent) variance was estimated at 1.81. The standardized factor loadings for the observed variables on the cognition factor were all statistically significant (p < .001). As shown in Fig. 1, the loadings were as follows: immediate word recall was fixed at 1, setting the metric of the latent variable (with a residual error variance of 0.77), delayed word recall = 1.23 (1.11), clock drawing = 0.29 (0.89), orientation to time = 0.18 (0.40), and name recall = 0.39 (1.12).

Multi-group Analysis

As shown in Fig. 2, the factor loadings for the multi-group CFA were constrained to be equal across the ELP and LEP groups as per the results provided above. The factor (latent) variance was quantitatively larger in the ELP group (1.80) compared to the LEP group (1.49). Additionally, the residual (error) variances for the observed variables were higher in the LEP group, with immediate word recall (0.77 vs. 0.78), delayed word recall (1.11 vs. 1.20), clock drawing (0.87 vs. 1.38), orientation to time (0.41 vs. 0.75), and name recall (1.07 vs. 2.63) for the ELP and LEP groups, respectively.

Discussion

The current study critically evaluated the measurement invariance of the cognitive screening battery used by the NHATS for dementia classification (Kasper et al., 2013), examining its application across varied English proficiency levels among older adults who receive Medicare. The primary question investigated whether the factor (latent) variance and residual (error) variances differed by English proficiency, essential for validating cognitive screening methods in populations with diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds. The hypothesis predicted variability in the factor (latent) variance and residual (error) variances across different English proficiency levels. It was predicted that the factor (latent) variance in the LEP group would be significantly smaller, while the residual (error) variances would be significantly larger than those of the ELP group, suggesting that lower English proficiency might influence cognitive screening outcomes.

The multi-group CFA results demonstrated different factor (latent) variance in the ELP (1.80) and LEP (1.49) groups. This difference suggests that the latent construct might capture the observed variables differently across groups, potentially affecting the accuracy and fairness of the cognitive screening battery. Particularly, the elevated residual (error) variances observed in the LEP group, especially in tasks such as clock drawing (0.87 vs. 1.38) and name recall (1.07 vs. 2.63), indicate that these test scores might be less reliable for individuals with limited English proficiency. This reliability concern, coupled with the differences in factor (latent) variance, suggests a potential method bias in test administration or cultural biases in item interpretation (Vijver, 2018).

Translation alone does not alter symbol familiarity or the learned analog-clock schema, which likely drives the higher residual (error) variance in LEP on clock drawing. Clock drawing likely shows elevated residual (error) variance in LEP because several task features introduce construct-irrelevant difficulty that translation cannot remove. First, the task relies on familiarity with analog clocks and the idiomatic “10 past 11” convention, which vary by schooling and daily exposure. Second, it requires symbolic switching between Arabic numerals and clock hands plus a visuoconstructional sequence (layout, spacing, hand length) that depends on learned conventions rather than cognitive ability alone. Third, comprehension of the instruction (e.g., tense and prepositions in “put the hands to…”) adds language load, even when presented in Spanish, and may interact with literacy and educational background. Together, these elements increase construct-irrelevant variance in LEP beyond what a direct translation can address, consistent with reports that clock drawing performance is sensitive to education and cultural context and may be a weaker predictor in multicultural samples (Maestri et al., 2023; Storey et al., 2002).

The findings of the current study align with existing literature that highlights the influence of education, literacy, and cultural background on cognitive test performance, particularly in tasks such as clock drawing and memory recall. Maestri et al. (2023) provide evidence of the profound impact these factors have on clock drawing performance, while Storey et al. (2002) have long suggested that clock drawing may not be a useful predictor of dementia in multicultural populations, especially among older adults from non-English speaking backgrounds. Furthermore, Nielsen and Waldemar (2022) argue that questions about national political leaders may be virtually unknown to people with immigration backgrounds and are considered culture-dependent test elements. These findings underscore the importance of considering the cultural and linguistic appropriateness of cognitive screening tools when evaluating diverse populations. The current study’s results, in conjunction with the existing literature, emphasize the need for further research and development of culturally sensitive cognitive screening methods to ensure accurate and fair dementia classification across diverse populations.

It is crucial to address these biases to ensure valid cognitive screening. The development of culturally and linguistically adapted cognitive screening tools is paramount for an accurate understanding of cognitive functioning and the prevention of disparities (Fennig, 2024; Waheed et al., 2020). Contrary to the notion that English proficiency may serve as a protective factor against dementia, these findings suggest that higher proficiency could potentially offer an advantage in cognitive screening, likely due to method biases particularly significant when screening tools developed for English-speaking populations are used among those from different linguistic and cultural backgrounds. Notably, most individuals in the LEP group (91.30%) identified as Hispanic. Although Spanish translation is available, the elevated residual (error) variance in LEP indicates persistence of noninvariance under minimal translation load; the likely sources are nonlinguistic—symbol familiarity, schooling in analog clocks and time conventions, and culture-bound name knowledge—rather than long statements or questionnaire interpretation. In this context, translation alone was insufficient to produce measurement equivalence, underscoring the need for task-level cultural adaptation (beyond forward/back translation) when screening diverse populations.

Implications

This study’s findings call for a critical shift towards incorporating cultural and linguistic informed adaptation in the development and validation of cognitive screening tools. The consideration of language proficiency and potential cultural bias in cognitive screening necessitates the development of culturally adapted cognitive tasks. These tools must respect and incorporate the varied linguistic and cultural backgrounds of individuals to avoid potential biases that may affect the accuracy of cognitive health evaluations.

Moving forward, public health strategies and cognitive screening tools must be culturally adapted, ensuring they are sensitive to the differences within diverse populations. Culturally and linguistically tailored cognitive tasks can prevent the misinterpretation of cognitive abilities, improve validity, and reduce misclassification. This approach not only avoids the misallocation of resources (Fennig, 2024) but also counters the perpetuation of stereotypes (Fasfous et al., 2017; Matos et al., 2022). Ultimately, research should progress toward screening tools that honor and represent the diverse cultural and linguistic backgrounds of the communities they aim to assess, promoting accuracy in cognitive health evaluations.

Strengths, Limitations, and Recommendations

The multi-group CFA was adapted to the NHATS’s complex survey design, applying survey weights to ensure representativeness. A key strength of this study is the identification of differences in residual (error) variances and factor (latent) variance across the two examined groups, highlighting potential biases in the measurement process and suggesting that the cognitive screening battery may not operate identically across diverse populations. These variations emphasize the complexity in how different groups may interpret or respond to the test items.

However, a limitation of this analysis is that metric invariance was assumed by holding factor loadings constant across groups, which does not provide insights into whether differences in item intercepts (scalar invariance) or residual (error) variances (strict invariance) are substantial enough to affect the comparability of screening scores across groups. This assumption is necessitated by the incorporation of survey weights, clustering, and stratification. Future research should aim to extend the scope of invariance testing, utilizing software capable of more comprehensively handling complex survey designs, to include tests of scalar (intercepts) and strict (residual) invariance to help determine whether observed biases stem from true differences in the underlying constructs or measurement artifacts.

Additionally, fit indices such as chi-square, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) are unavailable when working with complex survey data in Stata 18, due to the assumption of simple random sampling required for their calculation. This limitation, alongside the equal-loading constraints imposed in the metric invariance specification to maintain model identifiability and stability under complex survey design constraints, restricts deeper insights into model fit and potentially masks group-specific variations. Future research should explore the development and use of specialized statistical techniques or software that can incorporate the complex sampling design directly into the calculation of fit indices, thus allowing for more nuanced assessments of model quality and invariance.

Conclusion

This study underscores the importance of developing and employing culturally and linguistically sensitive cognitive screening tools. The findings reveal significant differences in factor (latent) variance and error variances between ELP and LEP groups, highlighting potential biases inherent in the NHATS cognitive screening battery. Notably, the smaller factor (latent) variance and higher error variances observed in the LEP group suggest that English proficiency exerts a substantial influence on cognitive screening outcomes, potentially resulting in misclassification and inaccurate estimations of probable dementia prevalence among diverse populations. These disparities underscore the critical need for the development and validation of cognitive screening tools that are culturally adapted and contextually valid, ensuring valid screening across diverse populations.

Future research endeavors should prioritize the extension of invariance testing to encompass scalar (intercepts) and strict (residual) invariance, employing specialized statistical techniques capable of accommodating complex survey designs. Moreover, public health strategies must emphasize the development of culturally adapted cognitive screening tools that respect and incorporate the diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds of individuals. By adopting such an approach, researchers and practitioners can enhance the validity and reliability of cognitive screening, ultimately contributing to the production of unbiased population estimates and valid screening and the accurate understanding of cognitive functioning in diverse populations.
