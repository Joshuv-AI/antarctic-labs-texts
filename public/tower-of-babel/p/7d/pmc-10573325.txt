Probing Energy-Level Alignment in Molecular Multilayers by Frequency-Modulation Electrostatic Force Microscopy under Tapping-Mode-Combined Fowler–Nordheim Tunneling Spectroscopy
Authors: Masahiro Nakayama, Kentaro Kajimoto, Tomoki Misaka, Naoya Mishima, Takashi Yamada, Hiroshi Ohoyama, Takuya Matsumoto
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe alignment of molecular electronic levels in a molecular multilayer is of crucial importance to realize desired functions for molecular devices. Amplitude-modulation-feedback frequency-modulation electrostatic force microscopy combined with Fowler–Nordheim tunneling spectroscopy is utilized as a probe for the energy-level alignment in an organic multilayer. Bias-dependent electrostatic force spectra were examined for bilayers including a Ru complex as a benchmark multilayer system. Electrostatic properties in the low-bias region were captured well by a single-capacitor model, which indicates weak coupling at the bilayer interface between the Ru complex and self-assembled monolayer. In contrast, in the high-bias region, significant disagreement with the expected electrostatic force was recognized for the bilayers and evaluated as the loss of electrostatic energy through the Fowler–Nordheim tunneling process. Alignment of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) level of the Ru complex was determined by Fowler–Nordheim emission through resonant tunneling. These results indicate an effective method to probe level alignment at interfaces inside multilayers and to provide the partition factor β that depicts a divided electric field.Keywords: Ru complex, molecular multilayer, electrostatic force microscopy, Fowler−Nordheim tunneling, molecular-level alignment

Introduction

Organic thin-film multilayers are extremely important as powerful strategic devices to tune interfacial properties and achieve the desired functionalities for applications such as organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs),1 organic field-effect transistors (OFETs),2,3 cellular imaging devices,4 and sensors5 because molecular–molecular and molecular–electrode junctions allow various combinations of properties. Despite such noteworthy applications of organic multilayer devices, there is difficulty in the exploration of their design and preparation because each organic layer has many different imperfections, including disorder, void formation, and unexpected doping. For this reason, organic electronics do not receive sufficient support from the knowledge of molecular electronics in which individual or ensembled molecules are utilized as active or passive elements in electronic devices, where the molecules serve as the current paths themselves or affect the charge transport properties from the connected electrodes. Therefore, bridge-building research between organic electronics and molecular electronics is necessary for the development of intrinsic device physics.

One of the key issues in the electron transport processes for organic multilayers is the relationship between molecular structure (or molecular–molecular junctions) and the electrical conduction mechanism, which is dependent on the energy-level alignment through the molecular junctions. The length of the bridge in the electron transport in donor-bridge-acceptor (DBA) molecular junctions is known to alter the conduction mechanism.6−8 The practical problem for the prediction of the energy-level alignment is the coupling effects due to molecule–electrode junctions and molecule–molecule junctions because the interactions at their interfaces affect the properties of the molecules themselves.

Considering the electronic structure of a bilayer molecular junction, as shown in Figure 1a, the vacuum-level shift of Δe1–m1, Δm1–m2, and Δm2–e2 appears at the electrode 1–molecule 1, molecule 1–molecule 2, and molecule 2–electrode 2 interfaces due to the charge transfer. Furthermore, the oriented molecular layers themselves have dipoles. These polarizations are affected by the external electric field, but are not estimated exactly due to the lack of an efficient model for theoretical calculation. As a result, it is difficult to obtain the partition factor β that determines the electrical potential at the molecule 1–molecule 2 interface and also to estimate the floating potentials of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels in both molecular layers.

(a) Energy diagram of an organic bilayer sandwiched between metal electrodes with applied bias. Level shifts Δe1–m1, Δm1–m2, and Δm2–m2 are induced by charge transfer at the interfaces. Each δqi(±) denotes the local charge at the respective interfaces induced by the interaction between the adjacent layers like sample–electrode and sample–sample. ε1 and ε2 are the permittivities of molecules 1 and 2, respectively, and β is the partition factor that indicates the division ratio of the external electric field for each layer. In the case of weak coupling between a molecule and electrode, molecular levels such as the HOMO and LUMO are floating and aligned by the externally applied electric field in accordance with the division by the partition factor β. Density functional theory (DFT) calculation in combination with the nonequilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) has recently proven to be a successful method to understand the electron transport properties of molecular junctions at the molecular level for a wide range of molecular systems.9,10 However, very little theory exists that can adequately predict how molecular orbital energy levels will align with the Fermi energy of an electrode because small changes in the energy levels can significantly affect junction conductance. Therefore, understanding of how the interface energy levels correlate with the junction is critical, and thus demands both theoretical and experimental study.

Based on the field emission mechanism, it was reported that the position of the nearest molecular energy level with respect to the Fermi level of the electrodes in molecular junctions can be determined from current–voltage (I–V) measurements, transition voltage spectroscopy (TVS).11−13 TVS is a useful method to evaluate the energy-level alignments of either HOMOs or LUMOs in a monolayer; however, for application to multilayer systems, a fairly high bias voltage must be applied to tune the Fermi level of the electrodes to the position of the molecular levels where the electrons can be resonantly transported. In this case, the most serious problem is the structural instability of the devices. In most cases, a high applied voltage leads to a high electronic field, which could break the junctions before the position of the molecular levels is reached. Therefore, a method that permits a high electric field is desired for electron transport measurements.

To address this requirement, we propose a new method of tapping-mode-combined amplitude-modulation-feedback frequency-modulation electrostatic force microscopy (AM-FM EFM)14,15 with Fowler–Nordheim tunneling spectroscopy to investigate the energy-level alignment in organic multilayers as shown in Figure 1b. In this method, the tip–sample distance is controlled in AM mode feedback and the electrostatic force is detected in FM mode, which enables measurement of the local electrostatic properties, such as the surface potential and dielectric polarization. This allows intermittent tip–sample contact in a very short time duration by tapping of the vibrating conductive probe with less damage to the sample, even at fairly high bias voltages. This method is a combination of EFM and point contact tunneling spectroscopy (PCTS). EFM probes local dielectric properties of a sample, such as the surface potential and dielectric polarization, and is often used as a nondestructive method to evaluate organic multilayer films with high spatial resolution. On the other hand, PCTS is the most commonly used in situ molecular break junction technique that has revealed various electron mechanisms,16−20 and many interesting phenomena in single-molecule junctions.21−27 As a result, the combination of EFM and PCTS using AM-FM EFM can realize sensitive measurements of the electron transport probability as a frequency shift change due to the loss of electrostatic energy through the Fowler–Nordheim tunneling process.

In the present study, we investigate the bilayers of a Ru complex on self-assembled monolayer (SAM) as a benchmark multilayer system. We have previously reported observations of temperature-insensitive and nonlinear resonant tunneling for a Au nanoparticle bridge junction of a Ru complex, supported by a SAM film attached to the Au electrode.28 In contrast to the characteristic electrical properties, the Ru complex/SAM bilayer shows considerable loss of electrostatic energy due to the Fowler–Nordheim tunneling process in the high-field region. This can be a fundamental approach to reveal level alignment with the partition factor β in multilayer metal complex materials and devices including coordination nanosheets,29−31 and their accumulated electrical devices,32,33 redox-active, and metal complex multilayer devices.34,35

Results

We focus on a Ru complex, di-tetrabutylammonium cis-bis(isothiocyanato)bis(2,2′-bipyridyl-4,4′-dicarboxylato)ruthenium(II) (N719) as an electron transfer molecule, and two aminoalkanethiols, 6-amino-1-hexanethiol (6-AHT) and 11-amino-1-undecanethiol (11-AUT), as inert buffer molecules, as shown in Figure 2a. These molecules were used to prepare four samples, as illustrated in Figure 2b, for comparison of the electronic properties. Two samples are SAMs of 6-AHT/Au and 11-AUT/Au and the other two samples are bilayers consisting of the Ru complex and the SAMs, N719/6-AHT/Au and N719/11-AUT/Au. All samples were prepared by self-assembly in solution, and tetrabutylammonium (TBA) cations of N719 are replaced by protons during sample preparation to yield the equivalent form of cis-bis(isothiocyanato)bis(2,2′-bipyridyl-4,4′-dicarboxylato) ruthenium(II) (N3). The SAM/Au and N719/SAM/Au samples were characterized using X-ray photoelectron (XPS), X-ray reflectivity (XRR) (Supporting Information 1-2, Figure S5), and atomic force microscopy (AFM).

Discussion

Electrostatic Properties of Ru/SAM Bilayers

Considering the electrostatic properties of a molecular bilayer, the most primitive model is a series of two parallel plate capacitors,44 where each capacitor is estimated according to the size, molecular density, orientation, dipole, and polarizability of each layer, and the partition factor β can be obtained circuit-wise (Supporting Information 3 and 5). However, the coverage of the SAM on Au(111) and N719 on the SAM is much less than dense packing and can only be obtained by experimental measurements. Figure 5a,b shows the coverages for 6-AHT and 11-AUT on Au(111) denoted as θ6-AHT/Au and θ11-AUT/Au, and those of N719 on 6-AHT and 11-AUT are represented as θN719/6-AHT and θN719/11-AUT. θ11-AUT/Au is reported to be 0.5 in the literature and θ6-AHT/Au is estimated to be 0.55 from this value of θ11-AUT/Au and the XPS intensity ratio of the N 1s peaks of 6-AHT to 11-AUT on Au(111) shown in Figure 2d and Supporting Information 1-1. As discussed in Supporting Information 5, the Δf–Vb curves are attributed to the time-averaged frequency shift due to the electrostatic force over the tip vibrational motion. As the parameters of representative tip distance to give the Δf–Vb curve (i.e., V0 and A), r0V and r0A were introduced. These two parameters were determined as the best fitting parameters from the simulation of Δf–Vb curves by using eqs S8 and S9 to reproduce all V0 and A for 6-AHT/Au, 11-AUT/Au, N719/6-AHT/Au, and N719/11-AUT/Au.

The r0V is a parameter for the V0 (CPD) related to the zero electrostatic energy averaged over the tip vibration motion which is dominantly affected at the closest distance between the tip and the surface due to the inverse distance dependence of the capacitor (see Figure 1b). This parameter was determined to be r0V = 0.075 nm as the parameter that best fits V0 by using eqs S8 and S9.

Based on the coverages of the SAMs, θN719/6-AHT and θN719/11-AUT can be estimated from V0-θN719 plots, as shown in Figure 5c. Simulation of A was conducted using the observed coverage of θ6-AHT = 0.55 and 11-AUT θ11-AUT = 0.5 from XPS data and a tip–sample distance of r0V = 0.075 nm with the single-capacitor model. When θN719 = 0, V0 has a positive value due to the forward directions of the dipole moment of the SAMs along the direction normal to the surface. Here, the V0 values of −0.5 and −0.6 V observed for 6-AHT/Au and 11-AUT/Au in Figure 4a,b were used for the V0 value at θN719 = 0, respectively. As the N719 coverage increases, the calculated V0 decreases due to the backward directions of the dipole moment of N719 along the direction normal to the surface (Supporting Information 3, Table S2). The calculated V0 curves for bilayers of N719/6-AHT and N719/11-AUT cross the observed V0 value, and thus θN719/6-AHT and θN719/11-AUT are estimated using the observed V0 values obtained by AM-FM EFM measurements. The solid lines indicate the results using a single-capacitor model, where the dipole moments calculated for the N719-SAM molecule were used, in which N719 is ionically bonded with 6-AHT or 11-AUT. As a reference, the hypothetical case was calculated as a combined two-capacitor model, where the dipole moments of N719-SAM are treated as the sum of the individual dipole moments for neutral amine and carboxylic acid. The single-capacitor model results in smaller coverages of N719 than that obtained by the two-capacitor model because N719 ionically bonded with the SAMs gives larger dipole moments compared with the sum of the individual dipole moments for neutral amine and carboxylic acid. As can be seen in the plots in Figure 5c, the observed V0 values for N719/6-AHT/Au and N719/11-AUT/Au intersect at the points corresponding to the estimated N719 coverage of θN719 = 0.095 and θN719 = 0.048, respectively, which are smaller than the two-capacitor model case.

The coverages determined from the observed V0 can be used to fit the quadratic curves of each sample in Figure 4 with curvature A depending on only one parameter, the tip–sample distance, r0A (Supporting Information 4-2). Since the r0A is related to the frequency shift averaged over the vibration motion of the tip which is induced by the time variation of electrostatic force due to tip vibration motion, the value of r0A is quite different from r0V because the A value reflects the long-range electrostatic interaction between the vibrating tip-cone and the sample surface (see Figure 1b). Figure 5d shows calculated A–r0A plots for each sample, where the solid and dashed lines indicate the calculated values for the single- and two-capacitor models, respectively. Each line crosses the experimentally obtained A values that correspond to each sample. While the r0A values do not match each other in the case of the two-capacitor model. In Figure 5d, all calculated solid lines using the single-capacitor model have an intersection with observed values of the dotted lines around r0A = 1.8 nm (vertical dotted line). Therefore, the single-capacitor model is suitable to consistently evaluate the electrostatic properties, including the V0 and A values for all samples, i.e., the estimated coverages of each layer are appropriate and AM-FM EFM reflects the electronic properties of the bilayers well as a benchmark.

Fowler–Nordheim Tunneling via Molecular Level with High-Bias Region

The Δf–V curves in Figure 4a,c show the deviation from quadratic curves (red line) in the high-bias region. This difference is expressed as ξ ≡ Δf + A(Vb – (−V0))2, and the ξ–Vb curve in Figure 6 indicates a sharp increase with thresholds in both the positive and negative high-bias regions. These disagreements are not attributed to charge accumulation as previously reported,45 because the electron lifetimes on the SAM layer are expected to be as short as 100 ps compared to the cantilever vibration frequency of 300 kHz.46 Accordingly, the electron conduction through the SAM layer is anticipated as the reason for the high-bias behavior. We have previously reported that the conduction through N719/6-AHT is temperature-independent, and hopping conduction is clearly excluded.28 Therefore, the loss of electrostatic energy through Fowler–Nordheim tunneling is the appropriate model for the ξ–Vb curves considering the distorted trapezoidal barrier model. The tunneling process at a high electric field was evaluated by using Simmon’s theory47 in Supporting Information 6. The ξ–Vb curve for 6-AHT/Au in Figure 6a shows asymmetric thresholds of +4.2 V in the positive-Vb region and −5.9 V in the negative-Vb region, which are attributed to the presence of a surface potential shift V0 that results in the variation of barrier height due to the surface dipole (Supporting Information 3). Fowler–Nordheim tunneling is generally the result of barrier suppression by an external electric field. No loss of electrostatic energy was observed for the thick samples of 11-AUT/Au and N719/11-AUT/Au, as shown in Figure 4b,d, respectively. However, N719/6-AHT, which is thicker than 11-AUT, exhibits significant energy loss, as shown in Figure 4c. Figure 6b shows that the ξ–Vb curve for N719/6-AHT/Au has a sharp increase with thresholds of +7.9 V in the positive-Vb region and −6.0 V in the negative-Vb region.

Observed ξ–Vb curves (gray lines) and fits based on the tunneling process (red and blue lines). (a) For 6-AHT/Au, the observed ξ–Vb is fitted using the calculation for Fowler–Nordheim tunneling using an asymmetric trapezoidal barrier model with φ6-AHT = 2.0 eV and σ6-AHT = 0.6 eV. (b) For N719/6-AHT/Au, the observed ξ–Vb is fitted only in the negative-bias region using the calculation for Fowler–Nordheim tunneling with an asymmetric trapezoidal barrier mode with β = 0.7, φ6-AHT = 2.0 eV, and σ6-AHT = 0.0 eV in the negative-Vb region (red line). Considering resonant tunneling via the LUMO of N719 enables reproduction of the positive region of the ξ–Vb curve (blue line) using the same β, φ6-AHT, and σ6-AHT parameters for the negative region.

The negative threshold is similar to that for 6-AHT, whereas the positive threshold is considerably higher than that for 6-AHT. This asymmetric characteristic for N719/6-AHT/Au is explained by the large permittivity difference between 6-AHT and N719, as shown in Figure 7, where the schematic illustration (a) and the energy diagrams (b–d) for N719/6-AHT/Au indicate the difference of the electric field conditions between (c) Vb < 0 and (d) Vb ≥ 0. The permittivity of 6-AHT is much smaller than that of N719; therefore, the external electric field applies mainly to the 6-AHT layer. As a result, Fowler–Nordheim tunneling occurs easily with effective barrier suppression by a highly negative Vb in comparison with a highly positive Vb. The calculation can well reproduce the observed ξ–Vb curve with an asymmetric structure, which strongly suggests that the ξ–Vb curve for 6-AHT/Au can be attributed to the loss of a substantial amount of electrostatic energy via the Fowler–Nordheim tunneling process in the high-Vb region.

For electrostatic analysis with dependence upon the quadrature Δf–Vb curves under the low Vb condition, the single-capacitor model is appropriate and β = 0.41 is estimated by the ratio of the molecular size of 6-AHT to that of N719. However, the β value gives an insufficient applied electric field, which results in a very low tunneling current (Supporting Information 6, Figure S16). Considering the large difference in permittivity between 6-AHT and N719, it is reasonable to use a different partition factor β that provides a sufficient voltage division ratio between 6-AHT and N719 to induce Fowler–Nordheim tunneling. The red curve in Figure 6b is the calculated result based on the trapezoidal barrier model with β = 0.7 and with an energy shift σN719 = 0.2 eV, and σ6-AHT = 0.0 eV at the interface. This result reproduces the experimental ξ–Vb curve well in the negative-Vb region, which suggests the loss of a substantial amount of electrostatic energy via the Fowler–Nordheim tunneling process; however, the calculated positive threshold is far higher than that observed. Accordingly, it is necessary to introduce a tunneling mechanism related to the molecular orbital (MO) of N719. By adopting β = 0.7, the threshold value of +7.9 V for a sharp increase in ξ–Vb curve at positive Vb corresponds to the floating MO location γ = 1.2 eV above the tip Fermi level; γ = (1 – β)/2 Vb = 1.2 eV. The blue curve in Figure 6b shows the calculation based on resonant tunneling through the MO of N719 at γ =1.2 eV and β = 0.7 can well reproduce the experimental ξ–Vb in the positive-Vb region. Assuming that the work function of the Pt-coated tip is close to that of Au(111), −5.3 eV from the vacuum level,48 the energy level of the MO associated with resonant tunneling is located at −4.1 eV from the vacuum level.

The energy level of the LUMO of N719 was reported to be −3.83 eV from the vacuum level by photoacoustic calorimetry (PAC),49 which is in good agreement with that by DFT calculation (−3.85 eV). Considering the surface potential of 0.2 eV observed from the EFM measurement, the corrected LUMO energy level (−4.05 eV) is in good agreement with the experimental value (−4.1 eV). Resonant tunneling through the LUMO of N719 was thus identified in the positive-Vb region as the frequency shift Δf. In addition, the partition factor (β = 0.7) obtained from this fitting was in agreement with a capacitor model using the calculated polarizabilities (Supporting Information 3 and 7). Therefore, the voltage applied to each layer in the multilayer can be approximately predicted using the ratio of dielectric constants calculated from the capacitor model, which indicates the weak coupling between N719 and 6-AHT in the N719/6-AHT bilayer. This result also strongly suggests that the ξ–Vb curve observed in N719/6-AHT can be attributed to the loss of a substantial amount of electrostatic energy via the Fowler–Nordheim tunneling process in the high-Vb region. It was thus concluded that the electrical conduction through the LUMO of N719 can be observed as a frequency shift. Conclusions

In conclusion, bias-dependent electrostatic force measurements for Ru complex/SAM/Au were performed using frequency-modulation AFM. The Δf–Vb curves were evaluated using a capacitor model for a molecular bilayer based on the dipole, polarizability, and the effect of partial coverage in combination with DFT calculations and XPS measurements. The loss of electrostatic energy through the Fowler–Nordheim tunneling process was found in the high electric field region. In particular, resonant tunneling through the LUMO of the N719 layer was confirmed in the positive-Vb region. Based on these results, the partition factor β was estimated for the bilayers from the simulation of the ξ–Vb curves, and the results were in good agreement with those estimated from the capacitor model using the calculated permittivity. This agreement reflects the weak coupling between N719 and 6-AHT in the N719/6-AHT layer. These results can provide a versatile means to determine the structure-energy level relationship and the interfacial energetics in multilayer systems. This work also provides insights into device physics with neuromorphic electronic properties, such as with organic memristors that involve voltage-dependent switching for charge accumulation and conduction.

Methods

Preparation of the N719/SAM/Au Sample

Polycrystalline Au(111) films were prepared by the vacuum evaporation deposition of Au on a mica substrate maintained at 833 K. The Au(111) surface was then processed by ozone/UV treatment (NL-UV253, Filgen, Japan). Two kinds of self-assembled monolayer (SAM) on the Au(111) surface were obtained by immersion in ethanol solutions (1 mM) of 6-amino-1-hexanethiol (6-AHT; Dojindo, Japan) and 11-amino-1-undecanethiol (11-AUT; Dojindo, Japan), respectively, at 40 °C for 2 h. The SAM samples were then soaked in a 1 mM N719 (Sigma-Aldrich, Japan) solution in ethanol for 24 h to accumulate the N719 layer.49 Finally, the samples were rinsed with ethanol and dried with nitrogen gas. The two TBA counter ions in N-719 ([RuL2(NCS)2]·2TBA) were replaced by protons during the above procedures resulting in protonated form, N3.

Apparatus for Obtaining Δf–Vb Curves

Amplitude-modulation-feedback frequency-modulation electrostatic force microscopy (AM-FM EFM) measurements were performed by a scanning probe microscope (JSPM-4200, JEOL, Japan) with self-excited cantilever oscillation at its resonance frequency under a pressure of 2.4 × 10–3 Pa at room temperature. A schematic of the experimental setup is shown in the Supporting Information 2, Figure S6. During the electrostatic force measurements, the tip–sample distance was kept constant with z-feedback using amplitude signal and electrostatic force was observed by frequency shift monitored by a phase-locked loop (OC4 station, Nanonis). Electrostatic force measurements were carried out using single-point measurements. To obtain Δf–Vb curves, DC bias voltage was applied by a signal generator (1946, NF, Japan) and scanned with a rate of 4 V/s. The signals of z-piezo, amplitude, frequency shift, and sample bias were acquired simultaneously into a 16-bit data recorder (DL950 Yokogawa, Japan). Details of AM-FM EFM have been described previously.14,15

A Pt/Ir-coated rectangle cantilever (PPP-NCHPt-50, Nanoworld) with a resonance frequency of 300 kHz and a spring constant of 30 N/m was used after cleaning with isopropyl alcohol, ethanol, and Milli-Q water to remove silicone-oil contaminants on the AFM tip derived from gel boxes.50,51

X-ray Photoemission Spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS measurements were performed at 298 K using a spectrometer (ESCA 5700, Ulvac-Phi) with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source.

X-ray Reflectivity (XRR)

The thickness of SAM was evaluated by XRR. The XRR measurements were performed at 298 K on a high-resolution X-ray diffractometer system (Rigaku: SmartLab). Cu Kα X-ray sources were collimated by a set of mirrors and a slit collimator (∼0.04°), and the reflected X-rays were detected by a scintillation counter via a parallel slit analyzer. The SAM sample was placed on a high-precision goniometer with a hemispherical dome stage (Anton Paar: DHS 900) where N2 gas was flown to avoid oxidation of the molecules during X-ray irradiation.
