Survival Outcomes of Liver Transplantation Amid Rising Recipient and Donor Risk Profiles
Authors: Eishan Ashwat, Francis J Spitz, Abiha Abdullah, Charbel Elias, Jason Mail‐Anthony, Sabin Subedi, Godwin Packiaraj, Timothy Fokken, Shwe Han, Michele Molinari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAdvances in liver transplantation (LT) have improved recipients’ short‐term survival. Whether similar gains extend to long‐term outcomes remains unclear.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of adult, first‐time LTs performed in the United States from 2002 to 2018, with follow‐up through December 31, 2023. Patients were grouped into three eras: 2002–2007, 2008–2013, and 2014–2018. Exclusions were ABO‐incompatible transplants, multivisceral or split grafts, and non‐hepatocellular carcinoma malignancies. Survival was estimated with Kaplan–Meier methods; Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for primary indication, sex, Liver Transplant Risk Score (LTRS), and Donor Risk Index (DRI).ResultsAmong 82,696 recipients, 23,656 underwent LT in 2002–2007, 29,355 in 2008–2013, and 29,685 in 2014–2018. Despite increasing LTRS and DRI over time, mortality was significantly lower in 2014–2018 than in earlier eras at 90 days, 1 year, and 5 years (all p < 0.001). Long‐term survival improved across all LTRS strata except LTRS ≥ 7. Relative to 2002–2007, adjusted hazards for death were 0.89 (95% CI, 0.87–0.92; p < 0.001) in 2008–2013 and 0.67 (95% CI, 0.64–0.69; p < 0.001) in 2014–2018.ConclusionsDespite rising recipient complexity and broader use of higher‐risk donors, both short‐ and long‐term LT survival improved in the United States over the past two decades.Keywords: liver transplantation, liver transplant risk score, long‐term outcomes, short‐term outcomes, trends post‐transplant survival

Introduction

Liver transplantation (LT) is the gold standard for the treatment of patients with end‐stage liver disease or unresectable tumors, offering both improved survival and quality of life [1]. Over the past few decades, two opposing trends have shaped the field of LT. On one hand, advances in surgical techniques, perioperative care, immunosuppression, organ preservation, bridging therapies for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and direct‐acting antivirals for hepatitis C have driven significant gains in outcomes [1, 2]. On the other hand, both recipients and donors are now older, with more comorbidities and lower‐quality organs than in earlier eras, complicating perioperative management and long‐term prognostication [3, 4, 5].

Amid these opposing shifts, two high‐impact studies from the United States and Canada reported no improvement in long‐term post‐LT survival over the last two decades [6, 7]. Given substantial changes in donor and recipient risk profiles, it remains uncertain whether this pattern is uniform or concentrated within higher‐risk groups [6, 7].

To clarify contemporary trends, we analyzed short‐ and long‐term survival among adults who underwent first‐time LT in the United States over the past two decades, adjusting for evolving donor and recipient characteristics [5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

Patients and Methods

Data analyzed in the current study were obtained from the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR), which includes comprehensive information on all donors, wait‐listed candidates, and transplant recipients in the United States. Data are submitted by members of the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) and overseen by the Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) of the US Department of Health and Human Services. As the data are anonymized, the institutional review board waived the requirement for individual recipient consent (study protocol number PRO 13060220). All procedures and methods used in this study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and Istanbul on ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects [13] and were reported following the Strengthening of the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [14].

Study Design, Setting, Inclusion, and Exclusion Criteria

Retrospective analyses were performed on consecutive adult recipients (aged ≥ 18 years) who underwent LT for the first time between January 1, 2002, and December 31, 2018, in the United States (n = 110,783). The last follow‐up date was December 31, 2023. Exclusion criteria were the use of split liver grafts from deceased or live donors (n = 5727), simultaneous multi‐visceral transplants (n = 9083), acute liver failure (n = 5005) or malignancies except for HCCs (n = 305), and ABO incompatibility (n = 0). Additionally, patients with missing data required for the Liver Transplant Risk Score (LTRS) calculation were excluded (n = 7967) first and then included during sensitivity analyses (Figure 1).

Flowchart illustrating the selection process of liver transplant recipients, including inclusion/exclusion criteria and final cohort stratification.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was 5‐year post‐LT survival across three distinct eras and stratified by the LTRS and Donor Risk Index (DRI) [5]. Secondary outcomes included 90‐day and 1‐year mortality rates for a comprehensive understanding of post‐transplant risks.

Data Collection and Model Calculations

The methodology for calculating the LTRS has been detailed in prior studies [8, 9, 10, 11]. Briefly, the LTRS was computed using parameters available at the time of transplantation using the following formula: LTRS = recipient age in years + model for end stage liver disease (MELD) score + recipient body mass index (BMI) + history of diabetes + history of dialysis. The values ranged from 0 (best prognosis) to 9 (worst prognosis):

Recipient age: 0 points for < 64.9 years; 1 point for 65–69.9 years; 2 points for 70–74.9 years; 3 points for ≥ 75 years.

Recipient age: 0 points for < 64.9 years; 1 point for 65–69.9 years; 2 points for 70–74.9 years; 3 points for ≥ 75 years.

MELD: 0 points for ≤ 25, 1 point for 26–29, 2 points for 30–34, and 3 points for ≥ 35.

MELD: 0 points for ≤ 25, 1 point for 26–29, 2 points for 30–34, and 3 points for ≥ 35.

BMI: 1 point for ≤ 18.5 or ≥ 40; 0 points for all other values.

BMI: 1 point for ≤ 18.5 or ≥ 40; 0 points for all other values.

History of diabetes: 1 point for patients with diabetes and 0 points for patients without diabetes.

History of diabetes: 1 point for patients with diabetes and 0 points for patients without diabetes.

Dialysis: 1 point for patients requiring renal replacement therapy and 0 points for others.

Dialysis: 1 point for patients requiring renal replacement therapy and 0 points for others.

MELD scores were calculated using the formula by Kamath et al. [15]: MELD = 3.78 × ln[serum bilirubin (mg/dL)] + 11.2 × ln[INR] + 9.57 × ln[serum creatinine (mg/dL)] + 6.43, excluding exception points for patients with hepatocellular carcinoma. The BMIs of recipients and donors were calculated using the World Health Organization formula: BMI = body weight (kg)/height2 (m2).

The DRI was calculated using the formula originally proposed by Feng and colleagues to quantify the relative risk of graft failure associated with donor and transplant characteristics in LT [5]. DRI = exp[0.154⋅(Age 40–49) + 0.274⋅(Age 50–59) + 0.424⋅(Age 60–69) + 0.501⋅(Age ≥ 70) + 0.179⋅(Black race) + 0.145⋅(Cause of death = CVA) + 0.184⋅(Donation after cardiac death) + 0.079⋅(Partial/split graft) + 0.244⋅(Cold ischemia time (h) > 12) + 0.302⋅(Regional share) + 0.411⋅(National share)].

Risk Groups

The study population was stratified into five LTRS categories based on definitions from previous studies [9, 10, 11], reflecting an increasing gradient of the predicted post‐LT mortality risk:

Low‐risk: LTRS ≤ 1.

Low‐risk: LTRS ≤ 1.

Low‐moderate risk: LTRS = 2.

Low‐moderate risk: LTRS = 2.

High‐moderate risk: LTRS = 3–4.

High‐moderate risk: LTRS = 3–4.

High risk: LTRS = 5–6.

High risk: LTRS = 5–6.

Futile: LTRS ≥ 7.

Futile: LTRS ≥ 7.

Patients in the low‐risk group were expected to have the most favorable outcomes with minimal perioperative risk and excellent long‐term survival. In contrast, those classified as futile had the highest perioperative mortality risk and poor survival prospects, despite transplantation.

Transplant Eras

Comparisons were conducted across three distinct transplant eras: Era I (January 1, 2002, to December 31, 2007; n = 23,656), Era II (January 1, 2008, to December 31, 2013; n = 29,355), and Era III (January 1, 2014, to December 31, 2018; n = 29,685).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed data and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, ensuring appropriate selection of statistical methods. The ANOVA test was applied for normally distributed continuous variables, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for non‐parametric data. Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test when the expected frequencies were low (< 5 per cell) to ensure statistical robustness.

Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method stratified by LTRS categories. Time‐dependent variables included the date of transplantation, re‐transplantation, last follow‐up, or death. The log‐rank test was used to compare the survival distributions between the groups. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models identified independent predictors of survival, with the proportional hazard assumption tested graphically using log‐minus‐log survival plots and statistically with Schoenfeld residuals. If violations were detected, time‐dependent covariates were incorporated to account for non‐proportional hazards. Patients who underwent redo transplantation, were lost to follow‐up, or remained alive at the conclusion of the study were censored at their last recorded follow‐up date.

To address potential biases, missing data were handled using multiple imputations for key covariates to ensure a more robust analysis. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results by comparing the complete‐case analyses with imputed datasets.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Bonferroni corrections were applied to adjust for multiple comparisons and to minimize the risk of type I errors. Statistical significance was defined as two‐tailed p values < 0.05, unless otherwise specified.

Risk Groups

The distribution of the LTRS shifted significantly over time (p for trend < 0.001; Figure 2). The proportion of low‐risk recipients declined steadily from 80.3% in Era I to 67.1% in Era III. Concurrently, the proportion of recipients classified as low to moderate risk increased substantially, reaching 45.3% and 14.6%, respectively, in Era III compared to 21.7% and 9.1% in Era I. The high‐risk category remained relatively small, and it increased modestly from 0.8% in Era I to 2.5% in Era III. The futile category remained rare throughout the study period, with only 0.1% of the recipients classified as futile in the most recent era.

90‐Day and One‐Year Mortality

A total of 3954 patients (4.8%) died within 90 days after LT. Ninety‐day mortality declined significantly over time, decreasing from 5.7% in Era I to 4.0% in Era III (p for trend < 0.001; Figure 3). Similarly, the 1‐year post‐LT mortality showed a downward trend over time. Overall, 9.2% of the patients died within the first year after transplantation, with mortality rates declining from 11.2% in Era I to 7.2% in Era III (p < 0.001, Figure 3). Survival Analysis

The median follow‐up was 15.6 years (95% CI, 15.3–15.8). Five‐year patient survival improved significantly from 75% in Era I to 81% in Era III (p < 0.001, Figure 4A). Similarly, among patients who survived at least 1 year post‐LT, 5‐year survival increased from 83% in Era I to 87% in Era III (p < 0.001, Figure 4B). Further analyses revealed survival improvements across all LTRS groups except for patients with LTRS ≥ 7 (futile transplant group). Owing to the limited sample size of patients belonging to this category, the statistical power was inadequate to draw definitive conclusions (Figure 4C–G).

Multivariate Analyses

Multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to test whether transplantation era was an independent prognostic factor after adjusting for LTRS, sex, race/ethnicity, primary indication for LT, donor sex, and DRI. Compared to patients transplanted in Era I (reference group), the adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) for death was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.84–0.91; p < 0.001) for Era II and 0.66 (95% CI: 0.63–0.68; p < 0.001) for Era III. Survival analysis restricted to patients who survived at least 1‐year post‐LT yielded similar findings. Compared to Era I (reference group), the aHR for death was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.87–0.94; p < 0.001) for Era II and 0.72 (95% CI: 0.68–0.77; p < 0.001) for Era III. Fully, aHRs are reported in Table 4.

Given the non‐random distribution of data that was missing for the calculation of the LTRS, sensitivity analyses were performed, including individuals without LTRS. The results confirmed that the era of transplantation remained an independent prognostic factor for post‐LT survival, even when analysis was restricted to individuals surviving at least 1 year after LT.

Discussion

Two highly cited studies have raised concerns regarding a lack of survival gains in LT recipients over the last two decades. Using data from the United States and Canada, respectively, Rana et al. [6] and Zhao et al. [7] have challenged the notion of continuous progress in post‐LT outcomes, suggesting that while early post‐transplant mortality may have declined, the gains in long‐term survival have not kept pace.

Contrary to those reports [6, 7], we observed improvements in 90‐day, 1‐year, and 5‐year survival rates among LT recipients over the past two decades. These results are particularly notable given the increasing clinical complexity of LT candidates, who are older and with a higher prevalence of comorbidities compared to previous eras [1]. Our intent was risk‐adjusted benchmarking rather than individual‐level prediction; therefore, we used LTRS (recipient risk) and DRI (donor risk) to adjust for changing case mix, not to forecast outcomes.

The divergence between our findings and previous studies [6, 7] is likely attributable to methodological differences. Our analysis incorporates more recent years, thereby capturing the impact of key advancements that may not have been fully reflected in earlier research. These include the widespread adoption of direct‐acting antivirals for hepatitis C, refined locoregional strategies for HCC, and improvements in organ procurement. The use of LTRS to stratify preoperative risks and the incorporation of the DRI [5] to account for donor characteristics also provided the opportunity to perform more comprehensive analyses of post‐LT survival trends [16]. Because composite scores have recognized limitations (and DRI may be outdated as a summary of donor quality), we conducted robustness checks, replacing LTRS/DRI with their component covariates (e.g., recipient age, MELD‐Na, BMI, diabetes, dialysis; donor age, cause of death), and stratified models by era; effect estimates and inferences were materially unchanged.

Over the past few years, significant advancements in organ preservation have emerged. Normothermic perfusion technology has shown considerable potential to enhance graft viability, mitigate ischemia‐reperfusion injury, and reduce early graft dysfunction, particularly in marginal donors [17]. However, perfusion use was limited during most of our study window, and it is not available in SRTR, and this technology received FDA approval only recently. As a result, our study establishes a benchmark for evaluating LT outcomes before the widespread adoption of pre‐implantation perfusion techniques and provides a reference for future assessments of these evolving preservation technologies.

Other emerging technologies, including structured decision‐support for donor recipient matching [18, 19, 20, 21, 22] and in vivo normothermic regional perfusion (NRP) [23, 24], are leading to increasing expanded criteria donor utilization and represent promising avenues that might affect the outcomes of LT recipients in the near future [18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. At the same time, we acknowledge that predictive tools, artificial intelligence‐based or otherwise, come with inherited limitations and should complement, rather than replace, clinical judgment. These changes, when integrated with existing practices driving recent survival gains, might further improve the outcomes of LT recipients [9, 11]. Specifically, for candidates at high perioperative risk, personalized approaches, including tailored donor‐recipient matching, enhanced perioperative monitoring, and biomarker‐guided therapies, may offer additional benefits [2, 16, 25].

One of the strengths of the current study is the large number of patients representing the entire cohort of adults undergoing LT in the United States during the study period and the extended follow‐up. The inclusion of data spanning transplant centers across diverse geographic regions minimizes single‐center biases and institutional variations, providing a more comprehensive assessment of survival trends. Additionally, the use of LTRS for perioperative risk stratification enhances clinical relevance by enabling the evaluation of outcomes across heterogeneous patient populations with varying disease severity and surgical complexity. We further strengthened internal validity by demonstrating that results were consistent in score‐agnostic and era‐stratified models.

Another strength of this analysis is the detailed characterization of causes of death among liver transplant recipients across different eras in the United States. The distribution of causes has shifted over time, with a relative decline in deaths attributed to malignancies, graft failure, renal failure, and infections, and a corresponding increase in deaths from cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease. Although the reasons for these trends cannot be established definitively, several factors may contribute. Improved surveillance for malignancies, more rigorous selection of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma, and wider implementation of screening programs for colorectal, breast, and skin cancers may have reduced cancer‐related mortality. Advances in immunosuppressive strategies may also have lessened the burden of post‐transplant renal failure and opportunistic infections. In contrast, the growing proportion of deaths from cardiovascular and cerebrovascular causes may reflect the changing demographics of transplant recipients, including older age at transplantation and greater prevalence of obesity, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome.

Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design, while mitigated by multivariable adjustments, may still introduce residual confounding due to unmeasured clinical and socioeconomic factors influencing post‐transplant outcomes. Additionally, the dataset lacks granular details on key perioperative and post‐transplant interventions, including specific donor optimization strategies, center‐level differences in immunosuppression protocols, and individualized therapeutic approaches for patients with hepatitis C cirrhosis or HCC. The absence of data on evolving treatment paradigms, such as the use of checkpoint inhibitors for HCC or biomarker‐driven immunosuppressive regimens, limits the ability to precisely delineate the contributions of these innovations to the observed survival improvements. Moreover, composite scores such as DRI and LTRS are imperfect proxies; however, sensitivity analyses using component covariates yielded similar results, supporting the robustness of our conclusions. Finally, SRTR does not capture perfusion use or functional graft assessments, and cause‐of‐death coding may introduce misclassification that warrants future analyses.

Furthermore, although this study accounts for major donor‐related factors through DRI, it does not capture emerging liver‐specific variability metrics derived from machine perfusion technologies, which are increasingly used to guide organ utilization decisions. The evolving landscape of transplantation underscores the need for prospective studies that incorporate more detailed clinical, biochemical, and immunological data to elucidate the determinants of long‐term survival. Periodic re‐evaluation with newer cohorts will be essential to determine whether survival gains persist or vary across risk strata as practice evolves.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that, despite increasing donor and recipient age and clinical complexity, both short‐ and long‐term outcomes following LT have improved in the United States over the past two decades. This study provides a contemporary survival benchmark just before the broad uptake of machine perfusion and other preservation technologies and before advanced, potentially AI‐enabled, organ allocation policies.
