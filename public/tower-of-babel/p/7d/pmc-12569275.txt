Exploring the correlation between eosinophils with clinical risk of severe pneumonia based on MIMIC-IV data
Authors: Jixiu Fan, Jin Huo, Jifang Liang, Xia Jing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Respiratory tract infections in pneumonia patients can rapidly progress to severe pneumonia (SP). However, the clinical relationship between eosinophils (EOS) and SP prognosis remains underexplored. This study aimed to assess this relationship to enhance prevention and treatment strategies for SP. A retrospective analysis was conducted on 325 SP patients from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database. The patients were divided into four groups (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4) based on ascending EOS levels. Baseline characteristics were analyzed to compare clinical indicators. Kaplan–Meier (K–M) survival analysis and weighted multivariate logistic regression models were employed to examine the association between EOS and SP. Additionally, univariate Cox regression analysis identified prognostic factors, and a nomogram based on these factors was validated. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to explore the relationship between EOS levels and SP. The analysis of patients in the Q1-Q4 groups revealed significant differences in platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) and SP (p < 0.05). K–M survival analysis showed significant differences in in-hospital mortality among the groups (Q1–Q4) (p < 0.05). Higher EOS levels were significantly associated with a reduced risk of SP (odds ratio (OR) = 0.3802, 95% confidence intervals (CI) = 0.1546–0.8928, p = 0.0295) in logistic regression analysis. Prognostic factors, including minimum mean blood pressure (MBP), atrial fibrillation (AF), and EOS group, were identified, with the nomogram demonstrating strong predictive accuracy. RCS models confirmed a linear relationship between EOS and SP. This study highlighted the critical role of EOS in SP prognosis. The nomogram proved to be a highly effective tool for predicting in-hospital mortality in SP patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21444-1.

Background

Severe pneumonia (SP) is a life-threatening condition primarily caused by localized or diffuse lung infections involving parenchymal and/or interstitial tissues1. It can progress to systemic complications, including respiratory failure, septic shock, or multiple organ dysfunction2, and is frequently associated with systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS). Clinically, SP is characterized by hypoxemia or hypotension and often evolves into acute lung injury (ALI) or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)3. According to the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) and the American Thoracic Society (ATS), SP is defined by the presence of one major criterion (either shock requiring vasopressor support or mechanical ventilation) or three or more minor criteria4. The core of SP pathophysiology is pathogen replication in the lower respiratory tract, which causes direct lung injury and triggers a robust immune response, typically in the form of a cytokine storm (such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), transforming growth factor-α (TNF-α), and IL-1β)5, leading to ARDS and potential progression to multi-organ failure6. Compared with mild pneumonia, SP causes higher mortality due to excessive inflammatory response and immune imbalance. Early and accurate assessment of the condition and predicted prognosis of patients with SP can guide the formulation of clinical treatment plans and have important implications for improving the clinical outcomes of patients. However, the immune markers regulating this process remain undefined.

In the complex pathological process of SP, the potential role of eosinophils (EOS), an important component of innate immunity, has not been fully explored. EOS are phagocytes with similar chemotactic, phagocytic, and antibacterial and antifungal killing capabilities as monocytes and polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNLs)7. EOS possess the ability to synthesize, store, and secrete multiple cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors. Additionally, they act as antigen-presenting cells to regulate the immunocompetence of T lymphocytes8. EOS combat various pathogens through mechanisms such as cytokine synthesis, classical degranulation, and the release of exosomes and EOS extracellular traps9. A previous study found that patients with lower respiratory tract infections and decreased EOS counts for COVID-19 had a longer hospital stay and a poorer prognosis10. Although the role of EOS has received some attention in some respiratory diseases11, the specific mechanism of EOS in the development of SP is not clear, and systematic research is lacking. Especially in the relationship between changes in EOS number and SP disease severity and prognosis, the existing research results are not sufficient and there are large knowledge gaps. Therefore, deep investigation of the relationship between EOS and SP will not only help to reveal the pathophysiological mechanisms of SP, but may also may provide an important basis for the development of new prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets to improve the clinical management of SP patients.

The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, supported by the Computational Physiology Laboratory at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT, Cambridge, MA, USA), Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC, Boston, MA, USA), and Philips Healthcare12, compiles data on vital signs, laboratory measurements, and treatments for over 40,000 patients admitted to BIDMC’s intensive care unit (ICU) since 200112,13. MIMIC is the largest and most widely used open-source clinical database in intensive care medicine14, providing valuable insights into intensive care outcomes, predictive modeling, and clinical decision support.

This study innovatively adopted MIMIC-IV data, and systematically explored the independent prognostic value and linear dose effect of EOS count in SP, through the retrospective cohort analysis, survival analysis and nomogram model construction, aiming to fill the gap in the systematic study of EOS in SP prognosis evaluation, deepen the understanding of SP immunopathology mechanism, provide scientific basis for future development of new therapeutic targets, and finally improve the clinical outcome of SP patients and reduce the public health burden.

Methods

Data sources and ethical standards

The MIMIC-IV is a widely used public database that contains extensive clinical data of ICU patients. Developed by the Laboratory of Computational Physiology at MIT, MIMIC-IV comprises data from 315,460 hospitalizations between 2008 and 201915. The creation and use of this database have been approved by the Institutional Review Board in the United States. All personal data within the database are de-identified using random codes to replace patient identifiers, thereby eliminating the need for ethical approval or informed consent for database access and ensuring patient privacy protection. The primary endpoint of this study was in-hospital mortality, while the secondary endpoint was ICU mortality.

Study population and data collection

This study included SP patients who experienced their first ICU admission, with disease codes from the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10: J120, B012, J95851, J159, J1289, J182; ICD-9: 4829, V1261, 48241, 48249, 99731). The dataset was re-examined to ensure the presence of necessary clinical criteria, such as pulmonary infiltrates on chest X-rays and symptoms of lower respiratory tract infections like cough, dyspnea, fever, and/or pleuritic chest pain. The inclusion criteria were patients aged ≥ 18 years meeting the diagnostic criteria for SP. Exclusion criteria included patients who did not stay in the ICU or stayed in the ICU for less than 24 h. Additionally, patients with a history of solid tumors, hematological malignancies, autoimmune or endocrine disorders, parasitic and fungal diseases, allergic conditions, or those who had received steroid treatment within the past 6 months, as well as patients on myelosuppressive therapy, were excluded due to their potential impact on peripheral EOS counts. Meanwhile, samples with missing covariate data were excluded. A total of 325 SP patients were enrolled and divided into four groups based on EOS quartiles (Q1-Q4) (Fig. 1). Specifically, the absolute EOS count (EOS abs) represented the number of EOS in the blood (×109/L). The quartiles were as follows: Q1: [0, 0]; Q2: [0, 0.05]; Q3: [0.05, 0.16]; Q4: [0.17, 2.21].

Flowchart of patient selection process.

This study selected several key clinical indicators for analysis: (1) Vital signs: height, weight, minimum heart rate, minimum mean blood pressure (MBP), minimum respiratory rate, minimum blood glucose, minimum systolic blood pressure (SBP), and minimum temperature; (2) Blood routine indicators: white blood cell count (WBC), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), and red blood cell distribution width (RDW); (3) Blood gas analysis: maximum arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PO2), minimum arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2), and maximum PCO2; (4) Comorbidities: atrial fibrillation (AF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pulmonary embolism (PE); (5) Therapeutic interventions: mechanical ventilation, hemodialysis (HD), elective surgery, antiplatelet agents, and vasoactive drugs; (6) Inflammatory biomarkers: platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR); (7) Length of hospital stay, survival status, and survival time during follow-up. Disease codes for these conditions are provided in Additional file 1.

Baseline characteristics, survival analysis, and association analysis

To explore the relationship between baseline characteristics and SP, all SP patients were categorized into four groups based on ascending EOS levels: Q1 (n = 82), Q2 (n = 81), Q3 (n = 81), and Q4 (n = 81). The weighted chi-square test or Kruskal-Wallis test was then applied to assess differences in baseline characteristics across the four groups (p < 0.05), using the tableone package (v 0.13.2)16.

Next, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (log-rank test) was conducted using the survminer package (v 0.4.9)17 to investigate survival differences among the four groups. In-hospital and ICU mortality rates were compared between groups (p < 0.05). The time from patients’ ICU admission to specific outcome events was used as the survival time variable, where the outcome events were explicitly defined as two categories: in-hospital death (cases of in-hospital death identified by the hospital_expire_flag) and censored cases (referring to cases where no death occurred but observation was terminated for other reasons). For censored cases, the calculation of survival time uniformly adopted the interval between the last observation date and the admission date, specifically including three scenarios: patients who survived and were discharged during hospitalization (survival time = discharge date - admission date); patients who remained alive and undischarged at the end of follow-up (survival time = last follow-up date - admission date); and patients who withdrew from the study due to non-SP-related reasons (e.g., transfer to another hospital, termination of treatment at the request of family members) (survival time = withdrawal date - admission date).

To assess the impact of covariates on this relationship, the connection between EOS and SP was examined using the nhanesA package (v 1.0)18 (p < 0.05). Using the first quartile range of EOS as the reference, weighted multivariate logistic regression models were applied to compute adjusted odds ratios (OR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Three models were constructed: Model 1 evaluated the association between SP and EOS without adjustment. Model 2 adjusted for 12 potential confounders, including height, minimum heart rate, minimum MBP, minimum respiratory rate, minimum blood glucose, minimum SBP, minimum temperature, WBC, MCH, MCHC, MCV, and RDW. Model 3, based on Model 2, further adjusted for additional confounding factors, including maximum PO2, minimum PCO2, maximum PCO2, AF, COPD, HD, PE, mechanical ventilation, elective surgery, antiplatelet agents, vasoactive drugs, PLR, length of hospital stay, follow-up survival status, and follow-up survival time.

Nomogram validation and nonlinear analyses

Univariate Cox regression analysis (hazard ratio (HR) ≠ 1 and p < 0.05) and the proportional hazards (PH) assumption test (p > 0.05) were performed on all variables to identify prognostic indicators using the survival package (v 3.5.2)19. To assess the predictive power of these indicators, a nomogram was constructed using the rms package (v 6.2.1)20. Points were assigned to each prognostic factor based on its impact on outcomes, as represented in the nomogram model. The total points were derived by summing the individual scores, and the 20-day, 40-day, and 60-day survival probabilities for SP patients were predicted based on the total score. To evaluate the reliability of the nomogram, calibration curves and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using the rms (v 6.2.1) and timeROC (v 0.4)21 packages, respectively. An area under the curve (AUC) greater than 0.6 indicated a high diagnostic value for the nomogram model. Additionally, the plotRCS package (v 0.1.4)22 was used to create restricted cubic spline (RCS) models to further validate the correlation between EOS levels and SP.

Statistical analysis

In this study, subjects were stratified into quartile groups (Q1–Q4) based on the absolute count of eosinophils (EOS abs, ×109/L). For the description of baseline characteristics, grouping variables were presented as percentages (quartiles). Categorical variables were analyzed using the weighted chi-square test; for continuous variables (e.g., height [cm], weight [kg]), the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for comparisons between two groups, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied for comparisons among multiple groups (the test statistic H was reported as “K–W” with a degree of freedom [df] of 3). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Dunn test, with Bonferroni correction applied to control for multiple testing errors. If the expected frequency of any cell in categorical variables was < 5, Fisher’s exact test (df = 3) was used instead. Effect sizes were reported to quantify inter-group differences (epsilon-squared [ε2] for continuous variables and Cramér’s V for categorical variables). All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (v 4.2.2), and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In this cohort, 245 SP patients survived, while 80 patients died during hospitalization, resulting in an overall in-hospital mortality rate of approximately 24.6%. Notably, as EOS levels increased from low to high (from Q1 to Q4), the number of surviving SP patients also increased. The influence of EOS on SP survival was highly significant (p = 0.012).

Survival analysis and association results between EOS and SP

To analyze SP incidence across EOS quartiles, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were utilized, as shown in Fig. 2. Patients in the lower quartiles of EOS experienced a significant increase in in-hospital mortality (p = 0.029) (Fig. 2a). However, no significant difference in ICU mortality was found among the four groups (p = 0.076) (Fig. 2b). The KM curves effectively identified patients with low and high EOS levels in SP. To determine whether the OR for EOS and SP changed with the inclusion of covariates, three multivariate logistic regression models were developed (Table 2). High EOS levels (Q4) emerged as a key predictive indicator. In Model 1, without any adjustments, a significant association was found between high EOS levels and reduced SP risk (OR = 0.2872, 95% CI = 0.1270–0.6120, p = 0.0017). In Model 2, after adjusting for height, minimum heart rate, minimum MBP, minimum respiratory rate, minimum blood glucose, minimum SBP, minimum temperature, WBC, MCH, MCHC, MCV, and RDW, the negative correlation remained statistically significant (OR = 0.3473, 95% CI = 0.1466–0.7819, p = 0.0126). Notably, in Model 3, which incorporated additional adjustments for variables such as maximum PO2, minimum PCO2, maximum PCO2, AF, COPD, HD, PE, mechanical ventilation, elective surgery, antiplatelet agents, vasoactive drugs, PLR, length of hospital stay, follow-up survival status, and follow-up survival time, high EOS levels remained a crucial protective factor for SP (OR = 0.3802, 95% CI = 0.1546–0.8928, p = 0.0295). After adjusting for various covariates, high EOS levels consistently remained a protective factor for SP, with all three models showing statistical significance (p < 0.05). This suggests that the effect of EOS levels on SP is robust and unaffected by other confounding variables.

Establishment and verification of a nomogram for SP patients

To further investigate whether clinical indicators could serve as prognostic factors for SP, univariate Cox regression analysis and the PH assumption test were conducted on data from SP patients. The results identified AF, MBP, and the EOS group as significant prognostic indicators for overall survival (OS) (Fig. 3a, Additional file 2).

A more practical and stable nomogram was constructed by integrating these prognostic indicators to quantify the predicted survival probabilities for patients at 20, 40, and 60 days. The nomogram indicated that higher total points corresponded to a greater likelihood of survival (Fig. 3b). The calibration curve of the nomogram showed strong concordance between the predicted survival probabilities and the reference line at 20, 40, and 60 days, reflecting the high predictive accuracy of the model (Fig. 3c). The linear relationship between EOS and SP in RCS analysis

To explore potential linear relationships, RCS models were employed. The analysis revealed that both in-hospital and ICU-related survival rates increased linearly with higher EOS levels (p = 0.0694, p = 0.0908) (Fig. 4a-b). These findings highlight linear associations between EOS levels and SP prognosis.

Discussion

SP as a global major health burden is prominently characterized by high mortality and lacks efficient prognostic assessment tools23. Based on this, this study first and deeply explored the association of EOS count and SP prognosis through the MIMIC-IV database. After cohort analysis, survival analysis and nomogram model construction, a higher EOS count was significantly associated with a reduced risk of death in SP patients, and there was a clear linear dose-effect relationship. Further observation showed that PLR was significantly different between different subgroups of EOS, which suggested that inflammation and immune status may be the key mechanism of EOS affecting the prognosis of SP. In addition, this study innovatively integrated AF, MBP and EOS into the nomogram model, which effectively improved the clinical application value of the model and provided a new tool for the prognostic evaluation of SP.

EOS is known to have anti-inflammatory and tissue repair functions24, which can inhibit the excessive activation of other inflammatory cells by releasing IL-10 and reduce the damage to lung tissue, thus preventing inflammation from spreading and deteriorating25,26. Simultaneous release of TGF- β to promote lung tissue repair and regeneration, which has positive implications for structural and functional recovery after lung injury caused by SP27. Animal models also confirm that EOS plays a critical role in controlling infection and inflammation28. And the protective role of EOS has been verified in various clinical situations of respiratory tract infections, including COPD, community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), and COVID-1929–32. It is worth noting that SP, as a serious infection, the intensity of inflammatory response (e.g., cytokine storm, risk of multiple organ dysfunction) and treatment strategies (e.g., ICU monitoring, invasive ventilation ratio) are significantly different from other types of pneumonia. Higher EOS levels observed in this study were associated with a significantly lower risk of death in SP patients (OR = 0.38, p = 0.03) and statistically different PLR between different EOS groups (p = 0.0444). This suggests that in SP, EOS may improve patient outcomes by alleviating the systemic inflammatory response and promoting tissue repair.

As common comorbidities of SP, AF is closely associated with severe hypoxia, inflammatory storm, hemodynamic disorders and metabolic imbalance associated with SP, and is a recognized adverse prognostic marker. Large cohort studies have confirmed that both pre-existing and new-onset AF significantly increase the risk of death in patients with pneumonia33–35. This study further identified AF as an independent adverse predictor of survival in SP and successfully integrated it into the predictive nomogram, emphasizing the importance of monitoring and actively managing cardiac complications in the management of SP. Similarly, hypotension (reflected as MBP) is a key pathological feature of CAP or sepsis severity and is included in all major severity and prognosis scoring systems (e.g., qSOFA, APACHE II)36. Hypotensive status or a significant decrease in blood pressure has been widely demonstrated to be significantly associated with increased mortality and increased need for organ support in critically ill patients, including pneumonia37,38. This study confirmed that the lowest MBP was an independent predictor of survival in SP patients, cooperating with EOS and AF to improve the predictive efficacy, reflecting the effect of circulatory function on SP outcome. Its application in the nomogram highlights the impact of maintaining adequate perfusion pressure on SP prognosis, which is consistent with the recognition of hypotension as a key indicator of poor prognosis in severe infections38, and again confirms the potential significance of maintaining hemodynamic stability for improving SP prognosis.

PLR refers to the ratio of platelet count to lymphocyte count in peripheral blood, which can be used to assess platelet activation caused by systemic inflammatory response39. Severe infections can suppress immune function, inhibit lymphocyte generation, induce lymphocyte apoptosis, and lead to the migration of lymphocytes to inflamed sites, thereby reducing peripheral blood lymphocyte counts. Increased PLR on admission has been linked to a higher risk of 90-day mortality in CAP inpatients40, serves as an independent predictor of pneumonia in patients with acute intracerebral hemorrhage41, and is an independent risk factor for the severity of pneumonia in stroke patients39. This study found that PLR levels were significantly reduced in the high EOS group (P = 0.0444), suggesting that EOS may inhibit excessive inflammatory response by inhibiting platelet hyperactivation and lymphocyte depletion.

Although this study revealed an association between EOS level and SP prognosis, several limitations remain. As a retrospective study based on the MIMIC-IV database, only using cohort stratification and basic regression model, lacking deep phenotyping analysis and time series study; excluding comorbid patients is although to reduce the interference of EOS count, but may limit the universality of the conclusions. Second, key confounders such as glucocorticoid use, secondary infections and burden of comorbidities were not adequately controlled and may affect the robustness of the results. Moreover, the study did not perform external validation or reproduced across datasets, reducing the universality and reliability of the study conclusions. Meanwhile, the association of dynamic changes of EOS in the course of SP was not analyzed, nor its interaction with treatment was explored, limiting its practical application value in clinical decision-making. Moreover, the lack of direct comparison with existing standard scoring systems limits the clinical significance of the study conclusions. In the future, we plan to track the dynamic change trajectory of EOS in patients with SP and to clarify its association with treatment response and prognosis. Deeply analyze the interaction of EOS and therapeutic means to evaluate the prognostic efficacy of EOS under different treatment regimens. At the same time, more optimization models of confounding factors were included to analyze the types of comorbidities and treatment plan to control the interference, so as to improve the reliability and transformation value of the research conclusions. In addition, multi-center external validation and comparison with the existing standard scoring system is planned to enhance its clinical utility.

This study was the first to systematically explore the prognostic value of EOS in SP, and found that low baseline EOS count was an independent risk factor for hospital death, and confirmed the linear association between EOS count and hospital death risk by RCS model, which provided a direct basis for using EOS as a continuous variable for prognostic stratification in SP. Meanwhile, this study constructed an effective prognostic prediction model including EOS grouping, which showed robust prediction performance for 20 days, 40 days, and 60 days. In clinical practice, doctors can simply calculate the total score and quickly assess the patient risk through this model, which provides an important reference for the formulation of individualized treatment strategies and helps to optimize the clinical management and prognosis improvement of patients with SP. Future exploration of EOS dynamic change trajectory and its interaction with treatment is needed to promote it as a practical biomarker for individualized treatment decisions in SP.
