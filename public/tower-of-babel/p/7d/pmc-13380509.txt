Water-seal duration and chest tube removal outcomes after traumatic pneumothorax: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Rahul Pawa, Joshua Gazzetta, Leo Andrew Benedict, Sean Nix, Philip G. Jones, Yoon J. Cho, Alyssa Fesmire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Water-seal duration was not associated with SWS failure.•Longer total chest tube duration was associated with SWS failure.•Higher BMI was unexpectedly associated with greater SWS success.•Longer water-seal duration did not appear to improve outcomes in this cohort.•Larger prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings.

Introduction

Thoracic trauma accounts for 35% of trauma-related deaths in the United States, with pneumothorax being a common complication requiring chest tube (CT) insertion to drain air or fluid from the pleural space [1,2]. CTs are managed using active suction, water-seal (WS), or a combination of both. The optimal duration of WS trials before safe removal remains uncertain. Lack of standardized CT management protocols often leads to prolonged tube duration, potentially increasing hospital length of stay, patient discomfort, and healthcare costs.

Active suction, typically a negative pressure of −10 to −20 cm H₂O, promotes lung expansion by facilitating drainage and pleural apposition [3]. However, excessive suction may exacerbate air leaks, potentially delaying healing [[4], [5], [6]]. In contrast, WS trials rely on gravity to drain air or fluid, offering a less invasive approach that may support spontaneous resolution of air leaks [5,7]. While WS trials are widely used to assess readiness for CT removal, no standardized duration exists, with protocols ranging from 1 to 36 h [7,8]. Longer WS trials aim to detect occult air leaks and reduce pneumothorax recurrence but may unnecessarily prolong hospitalization [9,10]. Conversely, shorter trials could streamline care but risk premature removal and WS failure, defined as CT reinsertion or return to suction [[11], [12], [13]].

The aim of this study was to evaluate whether there is an association between the duration of water seal time and safe chest tube removal. We hypothesized that WS duration would not be significantly associated with thoracostomy failure rates in patients with a traumatic pneumothorax.

Materials and methods

This was a retrospective study of patients treated at our urban American College of Surgeons verified level I trauma center with a traumatic pneumothorax requiring tube thoracostomy. This retrospective study was approved by the Saint Luke’s Health System Institutional Review Board (SLHS-23–013). All procedures were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient privacy rights were protected, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies. All patients over 17 years old between January 1, 2016, and October 1, 2022, who underwent a tube thoracostomy for a traumatic pneumothorax were included in the study. To ensure cohort homogeneity focused on pneumothorax management, patients that had chest tubes placed for a hemothorax were excluded as well as patients that required more than one chest tube simultaneously. Patients with a concomitant hemothorax in the setting of a primary traumatic pneumothorax were not excluded. Patients with missing chest tube data were excluded.

At our institution, chest tubes placed for traumatic pneumothorax were initially managed with continuous wall suction at −20 cm H2O. Patients were subsequently transitioned from suction to water seal as a trial, with the timing determined by the treating trauma team. If patients remained clinically stable after water seal, with no pneumothorax on chest X-ray, the chest tube was removed. If clinical criteria for removal were not met, suction was reapplied. Reapplication of suction and/or chest tube reinsertion after the initial water-seal trial was classified as water-seal failure in this study.

Patient demographics, comorbidities, traumatic mechanism, anticoagulation status, and Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) were collected using our trauma registry. Chart review was conducted to identify insertion details (image-guided vs open), chest tube duration, and water seal duration. The primary model examined the association between removal of the chest tube after a single water seal (SWS) course and the outcome (failure vs. success). The secondary model assessed the association between total chest tube duration and the same outcome. Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Multivariable logistic regression was used to analyze the primary and secondary models, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, associated air leak, and mechanism of injury; suction duration was included only in the primary model. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (64-bit, R Core Team, 2024) and generalized linear models were used to model binary outcomes, with a significance level set at 5% (with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 54 patients met inclusion criteria, of whom 33 (61.1%) achieved successful single water-seal (SWS) removal and 21 (38.9%) experienced SWS failure, defined as the need for repeat suction and/or chest-tube reinsertion following the initial water-seal trial. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were generally similar between groups (Table 1). The mean age of the cohort was 53.3 ± 20.8 years, and most patients were male (74.1%) and Caucasian (68.5%). There were no statistically significant differences between the SWS success and failure groups with respect to age, sex, race, ethnicity, smoking status, or respiratory comorbidities. Patients who failed SWS tended to have a lower mean body mass index (BMI) compared with those who achieved SWS success (23.7 ± 3.9 vs. 26.4 ± 5.5 kg/m²), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.06). Most injuries resulted from blunt trauma (59.3%), with falls representing the most common mechanism, followed by gunshot wounds, motor-vehicle collisions, and stabbings. Rib fractures were frequently observed (85.2%) and were more common among patients with SWS success compared with those who failed SWS (90.9%vs. 76.2%), though this difference was not statistically significant. Initial vital signs at presentation, including heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation, were comparable between groups (Supplementary Table 1). Chest tube and clinical course characteristics

Chest-tube characteristics and time-related variables are summarized in Table 2. The majority of chest tubes were placed using an open technique (83.3%), with similar distributions between SWS success and failure groups. Median chest-tube size did not differ significantly between groups. The mean duration of the water-seal course was 30.9 ± 23.1 h among patients with SWS success and 42.4 ± 56.7 h among those who failed SWS (p > 0.9). Similarly, the mean duration of suction prior to water seal did not differ between groups (58.3 ± 37.8 h in the success group vs. 46.3 ± 32.4 h in the failure group; p = 0.20). In contrast, total chest-tube duration was significantly longer among patients who failed SWS, with a mean duration of 137.7 ± 90.7 h compared with 89.2 ± 44.6 h in the SWS success group (p = 0.013). Primary model: water-seal duration and SWS failure

In the primary multivariable logistic regression model evaluating factors associated with single water-seal (SWS) failure, water-seal duration was not associated with failure (OR 1.22 per 12 h, 95% CI 0.89–1.67; p = 0.21) (Fig. 2). Body mass index (BMI) was the only variable associated with SWS failure. Higher BMI was associated with significantly lower odds of failure (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.71–0.99; p = 0.041), corresponding to a 16% reduction in the odds of failure for each one-unit increase in BMI. Other covariates—including age (per 10 years), sex, presence of an associated air leak, time to first suction (per 12 h), and mechanism of injury—were not significantly associated with SWS failure. Secondary model: total chest-tube duration and SWS failure

In the secondary multivariable model examining total chest-tube duration, longer tube duration remained associated with increased odds of SWS failure. Each additional 12 h of chest-tube time was associated with a 39% increase in the odds of failure (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.09–1.78; p = 0.008) (Fig. 2). Consistent with the primary model, higher BMI remained independently associated with lower odds of failure (OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.60–0.94; p = 0.011), corresponding to a 25% reduction in the odds of failure per one-unit increase in BMI. The presence of an associated air leak showed borderline statistical significance (OR 0.07, 95% CI 0–1; p = 0.050), suggesting a potential protective association, though this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the wide confidence interval. Age, sex, and mechanism of injury (blunt vs penetrating) were not associated with SWS failure in this model.

Discussion

In this retrospective study evaluating chest tube management following a single water-seal (SWS) trial, we found that longer total chest tube duration, rather than the duration of the water-seal trial itself, was significantly associated with decreased odds of SWS success. These findings suggest that prolonged chest tube duration may serve as a marker of increased tube thoracostomy failure risk.

The optimal duration of water-seal trials before safe removal remains uncertain. The lack of standardized chest-tube management protocols across institutions often leads to variability in practice, resulting in unnecessarily prolonged tube duration, which may increase hospital length of stay, patient discomfort, and healthcare costs. Our study supports the need for more consistent, evidence-based guidelines for managing chest tubes following trauma.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies advocating for the timely reassessment and removal of chest tubes to minimize morbidity. Platnick et al. demonstrated that prolonged chest tube duration was associated with an increased risk of complications, including infection and persistent pneumothorax [14]. Similarly, Bridges et al. found that shorter water-seal intervals (1–8 h) were not associated with higher rates of tube reinsertion compared to longer intervals (18–36 h), supporting a flexible and individualized approach to chest tube management [9]. Our findings are broadly consistent with prior work suggesting that longer water-seal trials are not clearly associated with improved outcomes. Although our small sample size limits definitive conclusions, we did not observe a measurable benefit to longer water-seal duration in this trauma-only cohort.

Due to our relatively small sample size and limited number of failure events, we were unable to stratify water-seal duration intervals in a manner comparable to Bridges et al. As an alternative, we categorized patients into four quartiles based on water-seal duration to descriptively illustrate how procedural success varied across ranges of time (Supplementary Figure 1). Failure proportions were broadly similar across quartiles, with no clear shift in outcomes above or below the mean duration. However, because these quartile boundaries are data-driven and do not necessarily correspond to clinically meaningful time thresholds, this categorization should be interpreted as descriptive rather than predictive. Additionally, dividing a continuous variable into quartiles may reduce statistical efficiency and obscure underlying trends. For this reason, the formal analysis evaluating the relationship between water-seal duration and procedural success was performed using a logistic regression model (a generalized linear model with logit link) with water-seal duration treated as a continuous variable, allowing preservation of the full information contained in the time variable. Most water-seal durations in our cohort exceeded 8 h, with an average duration of approximately 26 h, reflecting institutional practice patterns, provider preferences, and patient-specific clinical considerations. The wide range observed in the highest quartile reflects several prolonged water-seal trials in complex clinical cases. Based on the stratifications proposed by Bridges et al., our cohort would predominantly align with the longer water-seal duration group. These findings further highlight the heterogeneity in chest-tube management practices and the ongoing need for standardized protocols to guide optimal timing of chest-tube removal.

An unexpected finding was the protective association between higher body mass index (BMI) and greater odds of SWS success. Although higher BMI is traditionally linked with procedural difficulty and increased complication risk during tube thoracostomy, our data identified an association between higher BMI and SWS success, but this unexpected finding should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect residual confounding or unmeasured clinical differences rather than a true biologic effect. This contrasts with previous reports that obese patients experience higher technical complexity during tube placement but aligns with the physiologic rationale that increased thoracic pressure or decreased pleural space mobility may enhance sealing after trauma. For example, Brandenberger et al. analyzed 2802 trauma patients and found that although most patients were of normal weight (34%) or overweight (30%), both underweight (3%) and morbidly obese (6%) individuals experienced longer mean ventilator days compared to their normal-weight counterparts (10.3 and 7.4 days vs. 4.6 days, respectively) [15]. These findings highlight the complex and sometimes paradoxical relationship between body habitus and post-trauma recovery, suggesting that extremes of BMI may adversely impact clinical outcomes. In our study, higher BMI appeared protective rather than detrimental, a finding that warrants further investigation in larger cohorts. Future research should explore whether the observed effect reflects mechanical differences in pleural dynamics or confounding clinical factors such as injury severity or operator experience.

We also examined other procedural and clinical variables, including the presence of air leaks, placement technique, and associated rib fractures. Interestingly, the presence of an air leak was not associated with water-seal failure. Although air leaks have traditionally been associated with delayed tube removal, our findings suggest that isolated, clinically stable leaks may not necessarily predispose patients to SWS failure. This observation is consistent with findings from non-trauma populations. Cerfolio et al., studying patients with persistent air leaks following pulmonary resection, showed that safe chest tube removal was feasible even in the presence of a residual pneumothorax, provided specific clinical criteria were met—namely, absence of symptoms, no subcutaneous emphysema, and no radiographic progression of the pleural space deficit after 14 days on a portable drainage device [16]. While their study focused on a post-surgical population, it suggests that the presence of an air leak alone may not necessitate prolonged chest tube duration or extended water-seal trials. This insight raises the possibility of establishing similar objective criteria for patients with traumatic pneumothoraces, enabling safe and timely tube removal despite ongoing air leak. Similarly, we observed no significant difference in SWS outcomes between image-guided and open chest-tube placement, although the majority of tubes in both groups were placed surgically. However, our study was not powered to definitively exclude modest technique-specific effects, and selection of placement technique in clinical practice is often influenced by patient stability, injury characteristics, and operator preference. Future prospective studies with standardized placement and post-placement management protocols are needed to more formally evaluate the independent role of placement technique in SWS outcomes.

It is important to note that the association between air leak and SWS outcome varied depending on model specification. When adjusting for the individual components of chest tube management time (water-seal and suction duration), the association was attenuated and not statistically significant in the primary model. However, when adjusting for total chest tube duration only, air leak demonstrated a stronger inverse association with the outcome. Given that total chest tube time is the sum of its component durations, adjustment for individual timing components may partially account for the pathway through which air leak influences outcome. The overall effect of air leak suggests that part of its association with outcome operates through total management timing. Additionally, the first model could be over adjusted compared to the secondary model given our limited sample size.

The frequency of associated rib fractures differed between groups, occurring more often among patients with SWS success. While this initially seems counterintuitive, it may reflect differences in injury patterns rather than severity; patients with isolated rib fractures could have more localized injuries and therefore faster recovery compared with those sustaining more complex thoracic trauma. Further research is needed to determine whether rib fractures influence chest-tube duration or the likelihood of successful SWS removal.

Mechanism of injury—whether blunt or penetrating—was not significantly associated with SWS outcome, suggesting that water-seal trial success is likely multifactorial and influenced more by individual patient characteristics than by the type of trauma sustained. Nonetheless, our observation of a trend toward lower success among blunt-trauma patients aligns with findings from Helling et al., who reported that blunt thoracic trauma was associated with longer ICU stays, prolonged chest tube durations, and higher rates of recurrent pneumothorax and residual hemothorax [17]. Recognizing these trends may help guide clinical decision-making by identifying patient subgroups that could benefit from closer monitoring or earlier intervention.

Naturally, there are limitations to acknowledge in our study. First, this was a single-center, retrospective analysis, introducing inherent risks of selection bias, unmeasured confounding, and variation in documentation quality. Although a consistent protocol for chest tube management was followed, individual clinician judgment may have influenced the timing of water-seal initiation and removal. Second, despite collecting data over a six-year period, our overall sample size was relatively small, with a limited number of SWS failures. This inherently reduced the power to detect differences among subgroups and may have contributed to type II errors for associations that could be clinically relevant but statistically nonsignificant. A likely reason for the small sample size was the narrow inclusion criteria, focusing exclusively on patients with a traumatic pneumothorax as the primary indication for tube thoracostomy. This approach, while improving cohort homogeneity, limits generalizability, as it does not capture the broader trauma population in which chest tube placement often occurs for multifactorial reasons, including hemothorax and bilateral injuries. Finally, the observational design of the study precludes conclusions about causality. Although prolonged chest tube duration was associated with higher failure rates, it remains possible that more severe underlying injuries contributed to both longer chest tube need and higher risk of failure. As this was an observational study, causality cannot be inferred. Despite these limitations, we believe our study adds meaningful insight to an area of trauma care that remains highly variable and lacks standardized, evidence-based guidelines.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this single-center retrospective cohort of patients with traumatic pneumothorax, water-seal duration was not significantly associated with SWS outcome, whereas longer total chest-tube duration was associated with higher failure rates — a finding that may reflect greater underlying injury complexity and underscores the importance of ongoing clinical reassessment. These results suggest that water-seal duration alone may not be the primary determinant of tube thoracostomy failure in this population, although the small sample size and limited number of failure events preclude definitive conclusions. The unexpected association between higher BMI and SWS success is an intriguing observation that warrants further investigation, particularly regarding its potential implications for procedural planning in trauma populations. Larger prospective multicenter studies with standardized management protocols are needed to validate these findings and to determine whether individualized approaches to water-seal trial duration can safely optimize chest tube management after trauma.
