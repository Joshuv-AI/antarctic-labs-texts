Problems in the assembly and analysis of the kangaroopox virus-NSW isolate
Authors: Shin-Lin Tu, Chris Upton
Published: 2024

Introduction

arising from: S. Sarker et al.; Scientific Reports 10.1038/s41598-017-16775-7 (2017).

We compare the genome sequences of the New South Wales and Sunshine Coast isolates of the Eastern grey kangaroopox virus and find a large number of discrepancies in the manuscript describing the former. These appear to result from both the genomic sequence itself and the analysis performed on the sequence. We discuss 6 distinct areas of analysis that we believe to have serious errors.

Poxviruses are an important family of viruses, many of which cause significant diseases of humans, wild and farmed animals. There are at least 11 poxvirus genera (some viruses are unassigned) and the dsDNA genomes range in size from 133 to 360 kb, with an approximate coding capacity of 1 gene/kb. Approximately 81 genes are common to all chordopoxviruses (infecting vertebrate hosts)1. The largest poxvirus genomes appear to have grown large by the creation of multi-gene families2.

In 2017, both authors were associated with a publication3 describing the genome sequence and annotation of the Eastern grey kangaroopox virus strain Sunshine Coast (EKPV-SC) and Western grey kangaroopox virus strain Western Australia (WKPV-WA). Overall, these two genomes are syntenic and were found to be 96% identical (nucleotide alignment); based on previous poxvirus taxonomic assignments4, we suggested that EKPV-SC and WKPV-WA should be considered as separate species within a new genus (Thylacopoxvirus). Shortly afterwards, Sarker et al.5 reported the genome sequence of Eastern grey kangaroo poxvirus strain New South Wales (EKPV-NSW). However, significant differences were described between EKPV-NSW and EKPV-SC. These included: (1) only 91.5% and 87.9% nucleotide identity to EKPV-SC and WKPV-WA, respectively, (2) EKPV-NSW > 3% higher in G + C content than EKPV-SC, (3) very poor conservation of the DNA polymerase gene, (4) inconsistent phylogenetic trees over different genome regions, (5) 28 genes unique in EKPV-NSW, and (6) various recombination events. In view of these significant differences between what is described as two isolates of the same poxvirus species, we undertook our own analysis of these apparent problems.

Genome sequence similarity

For the first step in our analysis, using the default parameters of MAFFT, we aligned the EKPV-NSW, EKPV-SC and WKPV-WA genome sequences in the Base-By-Base (BBB)6 software package. Comparing EKPV-NSW to EKPV-SC and WKPV-WA, the tool calculated the percent nucleotide identities to be 98.8 and 96.0, respectively. Our values (1.2 and 4.0) for percent difference (EKPV-NSW to EKPV-SC and WKPV-WA) are much less than those calculated by Sarker et al. (8.5 and 12.1% difference). One possible source of this discrepancy (98.8% vs 91.5% identity) for the comparison of EKPV-NSW and EKPV-SC, may be that the tool used by Sarker et al. counted alignment columns containing a gap in the calculation of identity. Further evidence of problems with their calculations of sequence identity can be found in Sarker et al. Fig. 3B, in this table, many of the pairwise comparisons are < 15% nucleotide identity; random DNA sequences (4 nucleotides) should be approximately 25% identical.

As also identified by Sarker et al., we found that there were very significant differences at the termini of the EKPV-NSW and EKPV-SC genomes and within 8 blocks (Fig. 1) scattered along the genome. Most of these blocks result from large indels in one or other of the EKPV genomes, but 2 blocks are regions of almost no similarity (Fig. 1D, H). After we removed the terminal regions and these 2 unusual blocks, which account for only 5.1% of the genome lengths, from the multiple sequence alignment (MSA), we found that the EKPV-NSW and EKPV-SC genomes were actually 99.8% identical and these were 96.2 and 96.4% identical to the WKPV-WA genome sequence, respectively. Thus, our final results indicate that the EKPV-NSW and EKPV-SC genomes have diverged by only 0.2%, except for two relatively small misaligning regions, and not by 8.5% as reported by Sarker et al.

A review of the variable regions revealed simple explanations for their lack of similarity. In examining the relationships between the large indels and misalignments (Fig. 1A–H), it is important to note that for each of the differences between EKPV-SC and EKPV-NSW, EKPV-SC agrees with WKPV-WA, which is a different species of kangaroopox virus. Blocks A and B (Fig. 2, panel a): these blocks are essentially identical except the block is translocated in EKPV-NSW; Block C (Fig. 2, panel b): this block represents a repeat in EKPV-NSW that is made up of 2 other regions that are also present in the same position as EKPV-SC; Block D represents the sequence within the DNA polymerase gene that has been replaced by MOCV sequence (discussed below); Blocks E and F (Fig. 2, panel c): block F represents the translocation of block E from EKPV-SC and replacement of the block marked with an asterisk; Block G represents a region absent from EKPV-NSW; Block H (Fig. 2, panel d): this block represents a repeat in EKPV-NSW that is made up of 2 other regions that are also present in the same position as EKPV-SC, the translocated sequence replaces the block marked with an asterisk. Many of the genes associated with these regions are either truncated or missing in the EKPV-NSW annotation; these include the orthologs of F13L, E9L, G5R, G7L, L3L, H2R, H3L, A14.5L, A16L, and A18R (vaccinia virus Copenhagen nomenclature), which are conserved in all other chordopoxviruses. It is therefore most improbable that such gene losses are survivable for any chordopoxvirus.

Nucleotide composition

Sarker et al. reported the G + C composition as 57.7% for EKPV-NSW, which is considerably higher than the 54% we reported for EKPV-SC and would be very unusual for viruses within a species. When we recalculated the nucleotide composition values we found the EKPV-NSW and EKPV-SC genomes to be 53.96 and 53.94 G + C %, respectively. However, when we plotted G + C composition across these genomes, one small region (840 nucleotides) did have a significantly higher G + C content in EKPV-NSW (66.5% in EKPV-NSW vs 53.9% in EKPV-SC). Not surprisingly, this region also coincides with one of the “unusual” regions noted above with relatively low similarity; it maps within the DNA polymerase gene.

DNA polymerase gene

Sarker et al. suggested that multiple recombination events were responsible for the unusual nucleotide composition of the EKPV-NSW DNA polymerase gene. However, several lines of evidence point to a simpler explanation—errors in assembly of the EKPV-NSW genome. First, an alignment of the DNA polymerase gene sequences from the kangaroopox viruses reveals that the first 1000 nucleotides of this approximately 3 kb gene are 100% identical between EKPV-NSW and EKPV-SC; this is to be expected for a highly conserved gene in two viruses that are 99.8% identical (nucleotide). This region is followed by approximately 840 nucleotides that are > 99.5% identical to the MOCV genome, before returning to a normal > 99% identity between the EKPV-NSW and EKPV-SC sequences. Second, the MOCV derived region of the DNA polymerase gene is out of frame with respect to the first and third EKPV-like sections. Given that Sarker et al. reported that a MOCV genome was used as a reference for assembly of the EKPV-NSW genome, we believe the simplest explanation is that part of the reference sequence was somehow transferred to the new genome in silico.

Recombination

Table 1 of Sarker et al. lists 48 predicted recombination events. According to the RPD4 user manual, such predictions are likely to be flawed when alignments include sequences that have low DNA identity (< 60%) or, as expected, contain rearrangements of DNA sequences resulting in loss of synteny. Unfortunately, the genome sequences used by Sarker et al. suffer from both of these problems. Thus, given that the EKPV-SC and WKPV-WA genomes are almost completely syntenic, it is extremely unlikely that the EKPV-NSW genome, which is mostly 99.8 identical to EKPV-SC, has accumulated a large number of recombination events “between EKPV-NSW and other chordopoxviruses”.

Phylogenetic tree

Sarker et al. created 3 phylogenetic trees. The first (Sarker et al. Fig. 3) should be considered erroneous because it uses the complete genome of EKPV-NSW that we have shown (see above) to contain multiple blocks of rearranged sequences. Somehow in this analysis, lumpy skin disease virus (a Capripoxvirus that infects cattle) has been grouped within the Avipoxviruses that infect birds. The second (Sarker et al. Fig. 4) should be considered erroneous because it uses the DNA polymerase protein sequence that we have shown (see above) to be a mixture of 2/3 of the kangaroopox virus protein and 1/3 random sequence derived from an alternative ORF from the MOCV DNA polymerase gene. In the third tree (Sarker et al. Fig. 5), which uses the DNA topoisomerase protein sequences, the relationship between the three kangaroopox viruses is correct because the EKPV-NSW and EKPV-SC proteins are 100% identical and 96.8% identical to the WKPV-WA protein. This is exactly the same relationship that is displayed when a tree is generated for 95% of the length of the genomes (removing the misaligned blocks; see above). Thus, the naming of the virus as an Eastern kangaroopox virus by Sarker et al. is correct.

Gene annotation

Sarker et al. annotated the genome of EKPV-NSW with 169 genes, slightly more than the 162 we annotated for EKPV-SC. We have counted 28 unique gene annotations in their table; however, 21 ORFs overlap almost completely with other genes (contradicting Sarker’s own stated Method section), and 6 have poxviral orthologs with 100% aa/nt sequence identity. In reality, bona fide poxvirus genes rarely have significant overlap.

Additionally, there are a number of errors in the gene annotation table (Sarker et al. Supplementary Table 1). First, the lengths of the proteins are wrong; an extra amino acid has been added to the length, presumably by including the STOP codon in an “ORF length”. Second, the Supplementary Table does not distinguish which genes are encoded on the top and bottom strands of the genome (i.e. transcribed towards the right or left); the gene positions that are provided suggest all genes are transcribed left to right. Third, the gene numbering of a large block of the EKPV-NSW genes (ORF060–ORF081) in the Supplementary Table are shifted by one and linked to the wrong information. Fourth, EKPV-NSW-048, -052, -163 are described as having other cellular/viral homologs, however, all significantly overlap with known poxviral genes. Fifth, it is claimed that orthologs of conserved VACV-Cop orthologs F12L, E7R, A2.5L are missing from EKPV-NSW, but they are present as genes -027, -054 and -112, respectively. Sixth, 6 ORFs in EKPV-NSW (-028, -050, -051 -079, -131, and -108) are claimed to have arisen by horizontal gene transfer from other poxviruses, however, the first 5 actually encode conserved chordopoxvirus genes that clearly have complete or partial counterparts on EKPV-SC, and the last overlaps (opposite sense) with 2 well conserved poxvirus genes and is therefore very unlikely to be a real gene. Finally, the EKPV-NSW annotations by Sarker et al. claim that the EKPV-NSW genome lacks 30 EKPV-SC orthologs, but when we performed a manual analysis, 24 of these were identified. Those that are indeed absent, map to the regions of assembly errors.

Conclusions

Our analyses indicate that the unique portion of the EKPV-NSW genome has a significant series of errors due to assembly mistakes (we did not attempt to examine the terminal regions). Syntenic regions of 99.8% nucleotide identity make up the majority of the EKPV-NSW/ EKPV-SC genome alignment. With this degree of similarity, the 8 large non-syntenic misaligned blocks of sequence would be extremely unusual, especially given that WKPV-WA, which is 96% identical to EKPV-SC, is almost completely syntenic to EKPV-SC. Furthermore, a segment of MOCV sequence, a virus that infects only humans and that was used as the assembly reference, was incorporated, out of frame, into the EKPV-NSW DNA polymerase gene. The analyses by Sarker et al. also have serious problems. Errors are present in calculation of genome similarity, nucleotide composition, the generation of phylogenetic trees and the genome annotation.

Methods

Tools at the Viral Bioinformatics Resource (virology.uvic.ca) were used extensively. Genomes were aligned using MAFFT7 within BBB6, our multiple sequence alignment editor and analysis tool. BLAST8 searches were performed using tools at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) resource. Genome comparisons were visualized with JDotter9 and the Viral Genome Organizer10. Similarity plots were performed by utilities in BBB.
