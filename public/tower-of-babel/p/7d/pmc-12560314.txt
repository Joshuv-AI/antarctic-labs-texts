Background

Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) experience unique challenges, including comorbidities such as intellectual disability, epilepsy, enuresis, and ADHD, which affect approximately 86% of individuals with autism [1]. These conditions, combined with difficulties in communication, social interaction, and behavioral regulation, create complex caregiving demands [2]. As the global prevalence of ASD continues to rise [3], families are increasingly confronted with the physical, psychological, and financial strains of long-term caregiving [4]. Parents often face emotional distress, economic burdens [5], social stigma [6], and disruptions to family dynamics [2], leading to lower quality of life and increased vulnerability to health problems [7]. Given that ASD is typically diagnosed in early childhood and persists across the lifespan, families must adapt to ongoing care responsibilities [8] that differ significantly from those of families of children with other developmental conditions [9]. Therefore, there is a growing need to evaluate the impact of problems related to this disease on the families of these children [3].

While international research has highlighted the heavy caregiving burden associated with ASD [10–14], understanding these challenges requires sensitivity to cultural and social contexts. In Iran, studies have begun to shed light on the lived experiences of families raising children with ASD. For instance, Mohammadi et al. (2019) documented the national prevalence of autism and its comorbidities, underscoring the magnitude of the issue [1]. Other Iranian research highlights challenges such as family violence against children with autism [5], lower quality of life and health literacy among mothers [6], and predictors of parental well-being [7]. Heydari et al. (2015) explored the spiritual dimensions of caregiving, revealing how Iranian mothers interpret autism through religious frameworks of suffering, patience, and divine testing [15]. Similarly, Faraji-Khiavi et al. (2021) examined the lived experiences of families in Iran and emphasized themes of social isolation, stigma, and emotional strain [16]. In addition, Samadi, Ghanimi, and McConkey (2025) examined the quality of life of both Iranian mothers and fathers, revealing gendered caregiving roles and the disproportionate burden on mothers [17]. Complementing this, Asgari et al. (2025) tested mindfulness-based parenting and transdiagnostic interventions in Iranian mothers of autistic children, demonstrating that culturally sensitive approaches can enhance coping and reduce distress [18]. Furthermore, Imanpour et al. (2025) studied Iranian immigrant families and reported that cultural identity, stigma, and systemic barriers significantly shape caregiving experiences, even outside Iran [19]. These studies reflect both the universality of caregiving challenges and the unique ways Iranian families make sense of them.

Cultural values in Iran, including strong family ties, collectivist orientations, and the central role of religion, significantly shape caregiving experiences. Mothers, in particular, often assume the primary caregiving role, balancing expectations of self-sacrifice with limited institutional and social support. Stigma surrounding developmental disorders further exacerbates isolation, as families may avoid disclosing their child’s diagnosis due to fear of social judgment [5, 15, 16]. Although Iranian studies provide valuable insights, existing research tends to focus on quantitative measures of quality of life, mental health, or coping strategies, with fewer phenomenological inquiries into the lived, day-to-day realities of mothers raising autistic children. Moreover, the intersection of cultural values, gender roles, and caregiving stress remains underexplored.

This gap highlights the importance of conducting qualitative research that captures the subjective, context-bound experiences of mothers caring for children with ASD in Iran. By using a phenomenological hermeneutic method inspired by Ricoeur [20–22], the present study seeks to deepen understanding of how Iranian mothers interpret and negotiate the difficulties associated with autism within their cultural and social context. Unlike previous studies that emphasize prevalence, coping predictors, or spiritual dimensions in isolation, this research aims to provide a holistic exploration of maternal lived experience. In doing so, it contributes to bridging gaps in the Iranian literature, offering new insights into how caregiving is shaped not only by the demands of autism but also by cultural frameworks, religious meanings, and local social conditions. Ultimately, the study aspires to inform family-centered interventions and culturally sensitive support systems for Iranian families living with autism.

Methods

Study design and setting

This qualitative research was carried out using a phenomenological hermeneutic approach influenced by Ricoeur to discover the lived experiences of mothers. Phenomenology emphasizes the mental experiences of individuals and groups, aiming to reveal how people perceive the world through their life narratives [23]. A phenomenological hermeneutical interpretation aims to uncover truths about the essential meaning of being in the lifeworld. The process does not seek a single fundamental truth but rather explores possible meanings through a continuous process [23]. The study setting was the Imam Ali Centre of Autism in Kerman, the largest city in southeastern Iran.

Participants and sampling

Eight mothers with ASD Children with ASD were selected for interviews by using purposive sampling. The sampling period was from March 2024 to May 2024. Inclusion criteria focused on mothers with at least one child clinically diagnosed with autism, who also have satisfactory mental health and proficiency in Persian. Creswell (2013) emphasizes the necessity of reaching theoretical saturation and recommends an optimal sample size of 5 to 25 participants for phenomenological research. This range enables in-depth exploration of lived experiences while preventing data complexity that might arise from a larger sample [24]. Purposive sampling was used to select participants in this study, and sampling continued until data saturation, which meant that new data were unavailable [25]. Saturation was determined after the sixth interview, when no new codes, categories, or thematic insights emerged from the data. To confirm this, two additional interviews were conducted, which corroborated the absence of novel findings. This process ensured confidence that the final set of eight interviews adequately represented the studied phenomenon. Data collection procedure

The study collected data through semi-structured and in-depth interviews with open-ended questions. Throughout the study, researchers tried to select participants who had rich experiences. The corresponding author conducted interviews at a convenient time and location, and during the interviews, the researcher tried to strike a balance between the narratives and the aim of the research. Each interview lasted approximately 60 to 90 min and was digitally recorded and transcribed verbatim. Table 2 shows an example of an interview question.

Data analysis

The researcher collected and analyzed data simultaneously from March 2024 to May 2024. The data included interviews with 8 participants about their living experiences as mothers who have children with ASD. The second researcher transcribed all interviews verbatim. This study used Ricoeur’s thematic analysis approach to analyze data [21]. This process entailed a dialectical movement between understanding the whole and understanding the parts of the text across three methodological phases. In the first phase, the researchers read the interviews with an open mindset to gain a naive understanding of the meaning of having a child with ASD. They divided the text into meaning units, condensed them, and interpreted them against this initial naive understanding. In the second phase, the researchers sorted and condensed the meaning units to form subthemes and themes. Finally, in the third phase, the researchers formulated a comprehensive understanding by contextualizing the text. This approach was a more sophisticated mode of understanding, emerging from a critical dialectic between the first two phases [21, 22]. The comprehensive understanding involved a dialectical movement between understanding and explanation, between the whole and parts of the text, leading to an agreed-upon interpretation. We used MAXQDA version 10 to manage the data. MSN, ZER, and SBF analyzed the interviews. The authors checked and approved all categories and themes. MSN and ZER SBF contributed to the final written report’s composition, review, and correction. Table 3 presents the themes and subthemes identified in the structural analysis.

Trustworthiness

The trustworthiness of the study was assessed using five criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba (1994): credibility, confirmability, dependability, and transferability [26]. To achieve this, the researcher established a positive relationship with the participants during the interviews. In some cases, participants were consulted to clarify vague aspects of their responses and to develop a shared understanding of their experiences (member check). Additionally, two researchers who specialize in qualitative research assisted in interpreting the data, coding, and categorizing the results (peer check). A mother who had experience caring for a child with autism but was not part of our study was also invited to provide feedback on the findings. We aimed to thoroughly explain all stages of the research process in the study. To ensure authenticity, the experiences and perspectives of the study participants were accurately represented, and the data were compared to their actual views, which were ultimately confirmed.

In addition, inter-rater reliability was considered in the coding process. Two independent researchers coded 20% of the transcripts separately and then compared results. Agreement on coding categories reached 87%, exceeding the generally acceptable threshold of 80% for qualitative reliability. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, ensuring consistency in the interpretation of themes.

Findings

The participants’ mean age was 34 years (24–45 years). The mean age of the children was 7 years (4–11 years). Following the phenomenological hermeneutic approach, the results are presented across three stages: (1) Naïve understanding (2), Structural analysis, and (3) Comprehensive understanding.

Naive understanding

The experience of living with an autistic child seems to be like a journey from suffering to appreciation. Initially, the societal stigma surrounding autism caused significant suffering for mothers. People around them look at these children as “crazy,” “abnormal,” “strange,” and “unbearable,” and this perception is painful for mothers. This causes mothers to withdraw and hide their child’s condition from other people. However, through connecting with peer mothers who share similar challenges, a sense of peace and hope begins to emerge as the child starts to develop, even if it is just uttering a single word; the family’s outlook shifts, and joy returns to the home. The judgmental attitudes of others may persist, but what is important is the progress of the child, although this progress seems extremely slow in the eyes of others. Over time and adaptation, mothers accept autism as a truth and a divine test, leading them to embrace the situation with a sense of surrender.

Stage two: structural analysis

Three hundred fifty codes remained after constant comparison, compression, and merging. A total of three themes and nine subthemes were obtained. (1) Navigating social and emotional pressures alongside unconscious resistance to reality; this theme consisted of three subthemes: “Concerns about social commitments”, “Hiding the truth” and “Feeling exhausted from the overwhelming responsibilities”; (2) Illuminating darkness of neglect and rejection by supportive bonds and collective experiences: which consisted of three sub-themes: “Learning from peers with common pain”, “Being attended in supportive social organizations” and “Being supported by family and close friends” and; (3) Opening doors of perception to higher reality: expanding consciousness; which consisted of three sub-themes: “Learning to be authentic and self-respect”, " Learning to live in the present moment in the light of higher power” and “Learning to live with innate intuitive child”.

1- navigating social and emotional pressures alongside unconscious resistance to reality

The textual analysis indicates that the initial period following an ASD diagnosis is characterized by significant maternal distress, manifesting as profound grief, depressive symptoms, and acute isolation. Mothers’ narratives describe intense internal and spiritual conflict, often articulated through questions such as “Why me?” and feelings of being punished for an unknown transgression. This emotional turmoil frequently led to denial, exemplified by seeking second medical opinions or turning to religious intervention for a cure.

Societal factors, such as the pervasive stigma associated with the label of ‘autism’ and a general lack of public awareness, profoundly exacerbated this suffering. Mothers reported that people negatively judged their children for behaviors stemming from their neurodivergence, simply because they did not conform to established social norms. The constant pressure of these societal expectations and unflattering comparisons to neurotypical peers intensified their anguish, prompting many to conceal their child’s diagnosis and withdraw from social engagement, thereby compounding their isolation and caregiving burden.

A- concerns about social commitments

The text revealed that mothers almost always confront questions like what will happen to their children’s future. The worry comes from social commitment, including living like a normal person in society, achieving a sense of worth, being able to talk about their capability to relate to others, and especially if they can find a suitable person to get married to construct a usual family to strive in the society and protect themselves in the form of a family:

I asked myself these questions: Can she get married? Can she talk and handle her daily affairs on her own? (P1)

Mothers always experienced rumination. Rumination always comes from comparing their child with other children of the same age. This comparison itself amplified suffering. Society around expects a child to speak at a certain age and go to school at a certain age; meanwhile, the autistic child has a different process of development from other children. The difference seems to be neglected by mothers, and this neglect produces enormous suffering:

Children her age are talking, but my son does not even know simple things like going to the bathroom. (P2)

According to the text, mothers did not tolerate people pitying themselves. People’s questions about their children were not only pleasant but also caused them suffering.

I hate pitying myself and my daughter. They keep asking, “What is wrong with your child? God grant you patience.“(P5).

B- hiding the truth

Based on the text, since the mothers doubted the diagnosis and still did not believe in its correctness, they thought that it would be better to hide the issue. Hiding the truth seems to be a solution, or maybe a coping mechanism, to encountering their worries and suffering. The text revealed that it was painful to accept a child’s ASD. Mothers mentioned that for a long time, they did not accept that their child had any disorder. They were seeking someone to tell them that their child is normal. They feel satisfied with this neglect:

At that time, I was in a state of denial. I didn’t want to accept that; I wanted to find someone to tell me that my child is not autistic! (P2)

According to the stories, mothers avoid contact with relatives and acquaintances, even when the couple’s family tries to help them. This isolation continued until they realized that this issue was not temporary and may last for the rest of their lives. They finally found that hiding the truth and isolation are not logical solutions for their pain, which comes from not accepting the truth:

No matter who came to our house, I never opened the door. I had locked myself up like a prisoner. (P4)

C- feeling exhausted from overwhelming responsibilities

Mothers experienced tremendous fatigue because of bearing all the family’s burden on their shoulders. The sense of loneliness and lack of support from their husbands intensified their suffering. Such experiences caused exhaustion and boredom.

I am alone. In everything! My husband did not help me at all. I bear all the responsibility for my daughter. (P5)

2- illuminating darkness of neglect and rejection by supportive bonds and collective experiences

The analysis indicates that the chronic nature of the challenges associated with raising a child with ASD functioned as a catalyst for maternal adaptation. Over time, the persistent necessity to manage this reality prompted mothers to move beyond initial distress and actively develop mechanisms for coping. The data reveal that this enduring situation inherently motivated mothers to preserve their well-being and continuously devise strategies to navigate an evolving landscape of challenges.

A critical turning point was the emergence of hope, which participants described as gradually dispelling a mindset of resignation and silent endurance that had been formed by the burden of concealment and unacknowledged pain. This psychological shift was facilitated by several key support mechanisms: engaging with peer networks who shared similar experiences, accessing resources through supportive organizations, and receiving consistent backing from family and close friends.

Consequently, mothers came to highly value these communal support structures. Peer groups and specialized centers provided vital environments for the exchange of practical knowledge, empathic understanding, and adaptive strategies. Simultaneously, familial and social support offered crucial emotional and practical sustenance. Collectively, these supportive bonds were instrumental in mitigating feelings of isolation and social rejection, fostering resilience and acceptance.

A- learning from peers with common pain

Based on the text, one of the most suitable mothers’ strategies to cope with ASD was meeting mothers who had children with similar problems. Sharing the familiar stories of living with ASD helped them realize that they were not the only ones who suffered from having a child with it. Participation in groups with shared suffering created a supportive, warm, intimate atmosphere within which mothers were able to share their own stories without feeling judged or blamed or pitied. Some mothers shared stories that seemed much worse than their own experiences. The autism center also provided them with an opportunity to learn from each other. They also learned how to cope with their daily challenges by drawing on the experiences of other mothers. This coping helped reduce fatigue, loneliness, and isolation:

I used to think that this problem was only mine. I kept saying, “Why me?” However, since I met other moms who had worse problems than I did, I felt better. (P3)

B- being attended to supportive social organizations

Mothers reported that attending the autism center, which provides daily and ongoing education for children, significantly improved their children’s daily activities and capabilities, making them happier and more hopeful about their child’s future. With the staff’s efforts, these children became increasingly capable of doing previously impossible things. For example, they were able to say some words, do daily affairs, and even go to the bathroom alone. The social organizations also provided some educational sessions for mothers. Their independence provides them with opportunities to increase their knowledge about the nature of autism and how they cope with it.

Well, later, when I attended the classes at the autism center, I learned many things about my child that I did not know before. I have a deeper understanding of him now. (P2)

C- being supported by family and close friends

Mothers reported that the support of their close friends and family assisted them in coping with problems, bearing the burdens, and tolerating daily pressures and conflicts. Surrounding oneself with a supportive family and intimate friends significantly alleviated the mental and psychological tensions that arise from living with ASD. This seems to be one of the most valuable products of acceptance of the truth and not resisting the reality of ASD. This type of support was already there, but the resistance against the truth prevented them from seeing the support. The radiation of lights of acceptance caused them to be able to attend to the realities that were neglected before:

My parents supported us a lot. They always inspired us. Whenever I felt hopeless and worn out, I went to see my parents. (P6)

3- opening doors of perception to higher reality: expanding consciousness

The study revealed that raising a child with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), while undeniably challenging, also serves as a potent catalyst for profound personal growth in mothers. Participants described their children as embodying purity, honesty, and innocence. Coexisting with a child with these traits was reported to foster a unique environment that heightened maternal awareness of life’s valuable realities, which are often overlooked by parents of neurotypical children.

This personal evolution was not described in isolation but was situated within a complex framework of social and emotional pressures. For instance, the strains of societal stigma and fatigue were found to directly impact the mothers’ ability to seek support. Conversely, when established, supportive bonds acted as critical catalysts for acceptance and growth, thereby facilitating an expanded consciousness.

Ultimately, this transformative growth was attributed to learning directly from the child and engaging with their distinct and remarkable world. Mothers reflected that they were fundamentally changed from who they had been years prior. A key realization was the conscious decision to reject external, often limiting, perceptions of their child and instead embrace an internal, affirming perspective rooted in their direct experience.

A- learning to be authentic and self-respect

One of the lessons learned by mothers was self-respect. Living with autism made them face their true selves. Being your true self and expressing feelings as they are has given the participants pleasure and joy and made it possible to experience calm in a deep and real way. Mothers also learned from ASD not to judge other people and not to compare themselves with others. They learned to act according to their preferences. They reported that ASD children with ASD consistently choose the best options for themselves. They love themselves. For example, when they go shopping, they choose the most beautiful clothes to wear, the most delicious food to eat, and the most suitable environment to go to, such as beautiful parks:

My child always gets the best clothes, the best blanket, and the best food. I did not see anything like that in my other children. She seems to be saying, “Respect yourself, Mom!” (P5).

B- learning to live in the present moment in the light of a higher power

According to the text, living with ASD prompted mothers to re-evaluate their usual values and lifestyles so that they feel they get closer to a higher being. Mothers experienced that connecting to power beyond them has given them the power to confront challenges resulting from living with ASD and made them able to cope with it. This power is always and everywhere with them, making them happier and keeping them away from the paths that waste their energy and destroy their future. Mothers found peace and tranquility.

It happened many times that my son was in danger. Every moment, I feared that this child would suffer harm, yet God Himself shielded him. God gave me my child, and He takes care of it. (P7)

Mothers stated that ASD children taught them to live just now and here with purity and presence. This sense separates their minds from thoughts related to the past and future, thereby eliminating the fears and anxieties that result from them. The key idea is that every challenge or event occurs in the present moment, and its outcomes are unpredictable. In the light of the presence and connection to the divine infinite power, every single event in their life became meaningful and strengthened them mentally and physically.

I learned from my son to live in the present moment. He lives in this moment and does not contemplate the future. While I was always used to calculating and worrying about everything! (P1)

C- learning to live with the innate intuitive child

According to the text, mothers of children with ASD use their innate intuition. The skill is called remote viewing, or seeing from a distance. They spoke about precognition, seeing what lies ahead on the road, in life, the next day, or in the far future. They intuitively sensed something beyond factual evidence. Observing these children, they noticed an absence of fear towards the surrounding dangers—yet remarkably, none of these threats ever seemed to harm them. Intuition alerts children to danger, guides their decision-making, and aids in problem-solving. They also noted that there was a reason behind the child’s insistence to leave one place or go to another at a particular time, which they did not realize at the time. For example, participant number 8 said:

“My son woke up at 2 AM and took us outside. Behind a red light, we saw a man lying motionless in his car. We took him to the hospital. I said to myself that my son was the rescuer of that man, and his fate was beyond our understanding.”

Comprehensive understanding

Living life with ASD seems to be a journey from suffering to appreciation. Suffering and pain seem to come from unconscious resistance to reality, to what is. Unconscious resistance to reality resulted in a sense of separation, being alone and isolated from others. It is like the story of waves and their sense of separation from other waves and the whole tormented mother.

Meeting mothers with the same condition as they have provided them with an opportunity to see themselves. They saw their peers as a mirror. Being understood, being accepted, and receiving emotional support from a peer group, not an individual, has led to the elimination of isolation and finally receding into the universal whole of the ocean of oneness.

Using social organizational resources, including the child’s educational support and also education for the mother about how to live effectively with ASD, provided them opportunities to stop resistance against the truth and to stop constant complaining, comparing, and suffering. Through the journey, mothers learned to connect with the divine higher being. They experienced moments of oneness with others and learned to live in the moment with presence, peace, and tranquility. They were able to see the truths about ASD that they had never paid attention to before. Their consciousness returns to them, revealing the truth of the intuitive, innate child. Mothers perceived and found that ASD has a form of thinking they somehow cannot grasp or understand anymore. Therefore, appreciate them for who they are; do not expect them to be something they are not. They celebrated their child. Suffering invited mothers to open up the doors of their perception to higher realities.

This trajectory resonates with theories of resilience and post-traumatic growth, where adversity becomes a source of transformation. Caring for a child with autism was not merely framed as a burden but also as a catalyst for self-discovery, authenticity, and expanded awareness. The interplay between suffering and appreciation underscores the complexity of caregiving, where pain and growth are inseparably intertwined.

Discussion

The results of this study showed that at the beginning of diagnosis, mothers experienced suffering and pain due to unconscious resistance to reality. What is the root of this suffering? The answer can be found in Ego. It is the ego that has led humans to the abyss of isolation and loneliness, constant comparison, competition, anxiety, and pain [27, 28]. From the point of view of some existential philosophers such as Heidegger and Krishnamurti, some researchers such as Ventegodt, Braden, Lipton, and Tolle, and mystics such as Rumi, the inauthentic Self or Ego is the cause of all sufferings, failures, and negative feelings [28–35]. One of the important characteristics of the ego is the value-oriented quality of comparison. The ego arises out of the self during early development. It is a self-expression, but not the same as the self, which is much larger [27, 31]. Based on Jung’s theory, the persona is the part of the personality that we show to the world. The persona emerges from the need in childhood to adapt to the expectations of parents, teachers, and peers. This process may result in the persona carrying desirable personality traits while leaving undesirable ones to form part of the shadow [36]. Anyhow, a persona is formed by social commitments [27, 30, 31]. Social commitments cause people to conceal the truth and distance themselves from their surroundings [27]. Mothers’ stories in this study showed huge fatigue due to the overwhelming responsibilities. Mothers’ stories in this study showed enormous fatigue due to the overwhelming responsibilities. The same finding is reflected by Mazhari et al., who illuminated Iranian mothers’ experience of exhaustion because of living with husbands who are not responsible for the care of the child. They stated that one of the reasons could be related to fathers’ absence because of their jobs [37], lack of knowledge about the disease, and their fear of potential problems during interventions [38]. They also asserted that mothers appear to provide some form of protection for fathers. Protection of the father by Iranian mothers may be rooted in traditional cultural beliefs. They go on to say that the fear of Iranian men turning to addiction or remarrying, avoiding coming home, or even having a heart attack caused Iranian mothers to shoulder the burden of caring for a child with cancer alone [38, 39]. Avicenna’s (Ibn Sina’s) emphasis on the mother’s primary responsibility for childcare, including for children with disabilities, points to the deep-rooted historical and cultural origins of gendered caregiving roles in Iran. The persistence of this paradigm can be considered a reason for the continued disproportionate distribution of caregiving responsibilities to the detriment of mothers in the modern era [40].

There are studies available about the kinds of problems that parents suffer. It appears that most of the earlier studies focused on the negative side of having an autistic child. Earlier studies found that parents commonly suffered from financial conditions, occupational injuries [41], psychological problems such as stress and depression [16], problems in marital relations [42], and stigma and social barriers [43]. Some studies looked at ways to adapt to autism. An interpretive phenomenological approach in Karachi showed the mothers’ unconditional love for their children and provided lovely care from the start of the journey [44]. Ni’matuzahroh et al. identified positive reappraisal coping as a beneficial strategy for parents to adjust to stress, and it was discovered to potentially mediate the relationship between parental stress and quality of life [45].

According to Islamic teachings, individuals with disabilities are not to be marginalized; on the contrary, they possess inherent dignity, and supporting them is considered both a religious and social duty. This perspective stands in stark contrast to the social stigma and isolation often experienced by mothers, highlighting a transformative cultural and spiritual resource that could alleviate suffering rooted in social judgment [46].

According to the results, mothers who are suffering seek strategies to cope. The best coping method was joining meetings and group programs with other mothers who have the same child. Sharing experiences with peers provided a self-reflective learning opportunity for mothers, allowing them to expose their feelings, behaviors, perceptions, and practices about ASD, evaluate them, and learn from each other. Learning and change improved their approach to caring for children with ASD. Braden (2008) asserts that every individual in your life serves as your mirror. This means that others reflect aspects of your consciousness, enabling you to see yourself more clearly and ultimately grow [32]. Best (2016) declares that groups that have common pain and are an enduring and successful peer-based mutual aid group provide a practical and tangible case study through [47]. Using organizational support helped mothers tolerate daily challenges raised by living with ASD. Previous studies conducted in Iran [4, 6, 16, 48]. also bear this out. An autism center in Kerman provided almost free educational courses and classes for children with ASD and their mothers. Meanwhile, another study in Iran (Ahvaz City) used the phenomenological method to focus on the experiences of autistic parents and found that the high costs of the centers were one of their problems [16]. This data shows that we have not yet reached a final consensus on how to manage autism in Iran. Family and close friends also had a crucial role in assisting mothers to cope with complex problems that commonly emerge from living with ASD. This finding can be supported by previously studied conduct among families who live with ASD [49–51]. Clifford (2013) showed that parents who currently use parent support groups reported using more adaptive coping strategies than parents who had never used them [52]. Based on the findings, the acceptance of light radiation through coping strategies led mothers to expand their consciousness. Consciousness is the unconditioned presence in which all expressions of life can be fully met, understood, and allowed to be what they are. The famous Iranian poet Hafez Shirazi says, “The state of the times does not remain the same always; do not grieve” [53]. The phrase also means that everything in this world is temporary and transitory. What is important are the lessons that people learn from hardships. In Islam, which is the dominant religion of the people of Iran, there is a role model named Hadrat Zainab bint Ali, whose name is a symbol of nursing and also patience, who has a very famous saying: “I saw nothing but beauty” [54]. In this study, mothers’ stories of living with an ASD child reveal one of the beauties: they experience their child with ASD as an intuitive, innate child.

Intuition is the ability to understand something instinctively, without the need for conscious reasoning. An intuitive understanding is derived from instinctive feelings rather than conscious reasoning [55]. Intuition is not arrived at by thinking, but by logic. Intuition comes from within. It comes from stillness and presence. It is to reach a state of presence (become fully present) by tapping into our inner awareness (beyond the ego) to become fully conscious of the present moment. Therefore, intuition comes from stillness and presence [34]. Based on the findings, children with ASD are still present at the moment and therefore innately intuitive. Mothers also learned from ASD children to live in the moment, to live in oneness and joy in the light of a higher power’s presence. Knowing the Self as presence, without past and future thoughts (which are part of the ego), connects a person to the profound truth of their authentic self and fosters self-respect. Suffering is simply a rite of passage, not a test or a punishment, nor a signpost to something in the future or past, but a direct pointer to the mystery of existence itself, here and now [56].

Clinical implications

The results indicate an urgent need for implementing psychosocial interventions aimed at bolstering support structures for mothers of children with autism. The introduction of structured peer-support networks and culturally attuned group-based interventions presents a viable strategy to mitigate feelings of isolation and enhance psychological resilience. Considering the significant physical and emotional toll on these mothers, coupled with their heightened vulnerability to social isolation and depression, the establishment of dedicated female support groups is strongly indicated. Moreover, the establishment of respite care centers would greatly benefit mothers by offering them temporary relief and practical childcare assistance.

At a systemic level, healthcare policy in Iran could be advanced by initiatives that actively promote increased paternal involvement in caregiving duties, thereby challenging and gradually reshaping the deep-seated cultural norms that disproportionately burden mothers. Finally, the integration of spiritual and existential counseling into support frameworks is likely to hold particular resonance for Iranian families, given the central roles that religion and mysticism play in constructing personal and collective meaning.

Limitations and strengths of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The small sample size (n = 8) and recruitment from a single center in southern Iran limit the generalizability of findings. Given the cultural, ethnic, and religious diversity of Iran, mothers’ experiences in this study may not reflect those of families in other regions, particularly in northern or western provinces where cultural norms differ. Additionally, the exclusive focus on mothers leaves unexamined the perspectives of fathers, siblings, and extended family members, whose roles may significantly shape the caregiving journey. Despite these limitations, the study offers in-depth insights into the lived experiences of mothers and provides a novel theoretical contribution by linking existential philosophy, ego psychology, and Iranian cultural frameworks.

Future research

Future studies could expand the sample to include fathers, whose voices remain underrepresented in autism caregiving research, both in Iran and internationally. Investigations into the long-term trajectories of families caring for children with autism, including how coping strategies evolve, would provide valuable insights for policy and intervention design. Comparative studies across different cultural regions of Iran—or between Iranian families and those in other Middle Eastern contexts—could also deepen understanding of how religion, tradition, and social norms shape caregiving. Finally, mixed-methods research incorporating both qualitative narratives and quantitative measures of psychological health could further strengthen evidence for interventions tailored to Iranian families.

Conclusion

This study opened up a new horizon in living with ASD among mothers. Social pressure causes mothers to make unfavorable comparisons and experience emotional distress. When one accepts what is occurring, the pressure and suffering that come from it are released, and tension is broken. Mothers may still be subject to the questions and curiosity of others, but they are no longer the same person. Overall, there is almost always light at the end of the dark tunnel of all types of suffering for those who choose to change the course and ‘go to any length’ to seek recovery. In the following study, ongoing searches and efforts to receive assistance to cope with ASD led mothers to finally find the resources, learn, and go on positively. Accessing almost free support and services, such as education, along with support from family and close friends, was an important part of this passage through the tunnel of darkness. It seems necessary for researchers to have such a qualitative appreciative approach in their research pathways. Identifying positive and appreciative trends or pathways in the diverse life stories of participants can be highly beneficial, as it facilitates their transformational journey within a consistent and affirmative context.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The Ethics Committee of Kerman University of Medical Sciences (IR.KMU.REC.1402.138) authorized all study methods, which were carried out in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to participation, written informed consent was obtained, and participants were told verbally and in writing about the study’s goals, methods, and their rights. At the end of each session, participants were offered psychological support resources if the interview process evoked emotional discomfort. Interview times and locations were arranged according to participants’ preferences to maximize comfort and autonomy.
