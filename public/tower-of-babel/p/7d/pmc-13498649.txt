The Price of Speed: Estimating the Impact of List Prices on Time to Availability of New Medicines in 18 European Countries
Authors: Afschin Gandjour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo examine whether higher national list prices for new medicines are associated with faster patient access across 18 European countries.MethodsData were drawn from the 2023 European Federation of Pharmaceutical Industries & Associations (EFPIA) Patients Waiting to Access Innovative Therapies (WAIT) Indicator Survey and the Swedish TLV’s 2023 international price index. Time to availability was defined as the number of days between marketing authorisation and national reimbursement access. A multivariable linear regression model was applied, controlling for gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, population size, health technology assessment (HTA) duration, and the use of Managed Entry Agreements (MEAs). To address endogeneity, a two-stage least squares (2SLS) regression was conducted using a composite headquarters (HQ) Index as an instrumental variable for national list prices.ResultsIn the multivariable model, each one-point increase in the list price index (European average = 100) was associated with a 7.9-day reduction in time to availability (p = 0.091). A slow HTA/reimbursement process was significantly associated with longer delays (p = 0.022). Managed entry agreement intensity was positively associated with longer delays but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.103). In the 2SLS model, a one-point increase in the instrumented list price was associated with a 14.1-day reduction in access delays in the preferred parsimonious specification (p = 0.011). Sensitivity analyses showed that the negative association was generally preserved across alternative instrumental variable specifications and after exclusion of Poland, although effect sizes and first-stage strength varied.ConclusionsHigher national list prices may be associated with faster patient access to new medicines in European markets. Although the findings are consistent with a possible causal effect, they should be interpreted with caution given the sensitivity of the estimates to specification and sample composition, as well as concerns about instrument validity and strength. The results highlight a potential policy trade-off between affordability and timely availability in pharmaceutical pricing and access frameworks.

Key Points for Decision Makers

Across 18 European countries, higher national list prices for new medicines are associated with shorter delays from marketing authorisation to reimbursed patient access, with estimates around 8 fewer days per one-point increase in the price index.Using an instrumental variables approach, the manuscript estimates a larger association of about 14 fewer days per index point.For health policy, the results point to a trade-off where stronger price containment can slow availability, so pricing and reimbursement frameworks may need mechanisms that preserve timely access while managing affordability.

Introduction

Timely access to innovative medicines is a core objective of health systems worldwide but varies substantially across countries due to differences in pricing policies, regulatory processes, and health technology assessments (HTA). The trade-off between affordability and access has become particularly salient in Europe, where national governments attempt to contain pharmaceutical expenditure while ensuring patient access to effective treatments [1]. In this context, an important and widely debated issue is how expected prices influence launch order and strategic launch sequencing across countries, thereby shaping the timing of market availability.

Previous studies have shown that manufacturers tend to delay or forgo launching new drugs in countries with stricter price regulation or lower expected revenues. In a seminal cross-country analysis, Danzon et al. [2] used a Cox proportional hazards model to examine launch patterns of 85 new chemical entities across 25 countries, showing that countries with lower expected prices or smaller market sizes experienced significantly longer launch delays and fewer launches overall. Similarly, Vogler et al. [3] examined pricing dynamics in the European Union (EU) and identified a pattern of strategic launch sequencing, in which manufacturers prioritise early launches in high-price countries while delaying or avoiding entry in lower-price markets. This dynamic can distort access and equity across Europe and may unintentionally undermine policy objectives that rely on international reference pricing.

Despite the importance of this issue, there remains limited empirical evidence on whether, and to what extent, observed list price levels across countries are systematically associated with the speed of patient access. Few studies have attempted to address reverse causality directly in this context. An important exception is the findings of Danzon and Epstein [4], who jointly model launch timing and launch pricing. More generally, the association may not run only from prices to access delays; expected or realised delays in reimbursement and HTA decisions may also affect manufacturers’ launch sequencing and pricing strategies, thereby influencing the observed national price level.

The objective of this article is to test the hypothesis that countries with higher list prices experience shorter delays in access to new medicines, and to explore the strength and robustness of this relationship after accounting for confounding variables and reverse causality.

This study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, whereas previous studies have mainly examined launch probability or launch delay, the present analysis focuses on time to reimbursed patient access, an outcome more directly related to effective patient availability within European healthcare systems. Launch indicates that a product has entered a market, but it does not necessarily mean that patients can obtain it under routine reimbursement. By contrast, time to availability captures the period from marketing authorisation to reimbursed patient access and therefore more closely reflects actual access to treatment. Second, the analysis relates this outcome to observed cross-country differences in national list-price levels rather than relying primarily on indirect indicators of expected pricing incentives, such as price regulation, expected revenues, market size, or country attractiveness. List prices are publicly visible and may influence launch sequencing, external reference pricing, and reimbursement negotiations, even though they do not necessarily correspond to the confidential net prices ultimately paid by insurers. The external reference pricing (ERP) literature emphasises that regulators typically rely on publicly observable list prices rather than actual transaction prices, which can generate cross-country spillovers and strategic launch sequencing by manufacturers [5]. The analysis therefore speaks primarily to the role of the national list-price environment in shaping access, while partially accounting for confidential pricing arrangements through a control for Managed Entry Agreement (MEA) use. Third, the study goes beyond descriptive comparison by addressing potential endogeneity in the price-access relationship through an instrumental-variable approach. Taken together, these contributions provide new empirical evidence on how cross-country price variation may be linked to access timing in a setting shaped by international reference pricing and strategic launch sequencing, using recent and harmonised cross-country data.

The conceptual model underlying this study is that expected price levels affect manufacturer launch sequencing and reimbursement effort. In countries where higher prices can be obtained, firms have stronger incentives to prioritise rapid launch, submit evidence earlier, and engage more intensively in pricing and reimbursement negotiations. By contrast, in lower-price markets, manufacturers may delay launch or negotiation because expected returns are smaller and because low prices may spill over internationally through reference pricing. As a result, higher price levels are hypothesised to be associated with shorter waiting times to patient access.

Methods

This study investigates the relationship between national list prices of new medicines and the time to patient access (availability) in 18 European countries: Austria, Belgium, Czechia, Denmark, England, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Ireland, Italy, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, and Switzerland. To this end, the analysis combines ordinary least squares (OLS), robust regression, and instrumental variable (IV) techniques. The analytical sample was defined as the set of European countries for which both the 2023 European Federation of Pharmaceutical Industries & Associations (EFPIA) Patients Waiting to Access Innovative Therapies (WAIT) Indicator Survey [6] provided country-level data on time to availability and the Swedish TLV international price comparison provided a corresponding national list price index; countries with missing information in either source were excluded. This yielded a cross-sectional sample of 18 countries, corresponding to 18 observations in the main analysis. Unless otherwise stated, all regression models were estimated on this full sample; sensitivity analyses excluding influential observations are reported separately with the corresponding reduced sample size.

Data on time to availability were obtained from the 2023 EFPIA Patients WAIT Indicator Survey [6]. The dependent variable used here is the country-level median time to availability (days), defined as the median number of days between marketing authorisation and national patient access, typically corresponding to inclusion on the reimbursement list. For EU and European Economic Area (EEA) countries, the date of marketing authorisation refers to the European Commission’s decision, whereas for non-EU countries such as England, Switzerland, and Norway, national authorisation dates were used. List price levels were taken from the 2023 international pharmaceutical price comparison published by the Swedish Dental and Pharmaceutical Benefits Agency (TLV), which reports a relative country-level list price index (European average = 100) [7]. This measure is used here as an indicator of the national list-price environment rather than as a direct observation of product-specific launch prices for the medicines in the EFPIA Patients WAIT cohort. Additional control variables included population size (in millions), GDP per capita, a binary indicator for whether a country uses MEAs intensively, and a binary indicator for whether the country’s health technology assessment (HTA) process is classified as slow.

The MEA variable was operationalised as a binary indicator of intensive use (1 = yes, 0 = no). A country was classified as using MEAs intensively when the literature documented systematic and widespread application of financial and/or performance-based agreements to a substantial share of high-cost innovative medicines. Classification drew primarily on OECD Health Working Paper No. 115, particularly Tables 2.1 and 2.2, which provide harmonised survey data on the number and types of MEAs for 14 countries on a common sample of 104 high-cost product/indication pairs [8]. The seven countries meeting the intensity threshold—Belgium, France, Hungary, Italy, Portugal, Spain, and England—were coded as 1; all remaining countries, including the four countries not covered in the OECD paper, were coded as 0. Managed Entry Agreement use was included because such agreements may affect time to availability independently of observed list prices, for example by facilitating earlier reimbursement through confidential discounts, outcome-based arrangements, or other forms of pricing flexibility. Controlling for MEA intensity therefore helps account for cross-country differences in access pathways that might otherwise confound the association between list price levels and time to availability.

The binary indicator for a slow HTA/reimbursement process is not identical to the dependent variable, because time to availability reflects the full period from marketing authorisation to patient access and may also be shaped by launch sequencing, pricing negotiations, reimbursement procedures, and other institutional factors. The variable was coded as 1 for countries with a slow HTA/reimbursement process and 0 otherwise, based on time from pricing and reimbursement (P&R) filing to reimbursement decision, rather than as a continuous measure, because directly comparable post-filing decision-time data were not available for Switzerland. Countries were classified as slow when the average time from P&R filing to reimbursement decision exceeded 300 days in the EFPIA European Access Hurdles Portal [9]. Switzerland was coded 0 on the basis of available Swiss evidence: Interpharma reports a median of 189 days from marketing authorisation to inclusion in the Specialities List in 2024 [10]. This interval is broader than the post-filing reimbursement-decision measure used for other countries and therefore does not support classifying Switzerland as slow under a 300-day threshold.

The primary model used was a multivariable linear regression with time to availability as the dependent variable and the list price index as the main independent variable, controlling for the aforementioned covariates. To assess the robustness of the findings, we conducted several diagnostic tests: multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs), heteroskedasticity was tested using the Breusch-Pagan test, and the normality of residuals was assessed through visual inspection (histograms, Q–Q plots). Influential observations were identified using Cook’s Distance. A robust regression model using Huber’s M-estimator was also estimated to account for outliers.

To address the potential endogeneity of list prices—such as reverse causality, whereby slower access may lead to lower prices—we employed a two-stage least squares (2SLS) regression. The IV was a constructed headquarters (HQ) Index (ranging from 0 to 2; see Table 2), which captures the presence and concentration of global pharmaceutical company headquarters in each country. Countries with a stronger pharmaceutical footprint tend to exhibit higher launch prices due to several interrelated factors, including their prioritisation as strategic launch markets, pricing and reimbursement systems that support premium pricing, stronger manufacturer lobbying capacity, and the fact that their prices are more likely to serve as anchors in international reference pricing systems. In the first stage, the HQ Index was used to predict the national list price index. In the second stage, the instrumented list price was regressed on time to availability.

Given the small sample size, we estimated three IV specifications to assess the robustness of the results under different assumptions. The first was an unadjusted model without additional controls. The second was a parsimonious specification including only the binary indicator for a slow HTA/reimbursement process, which we treat as the preferred specification because it addresses the most important observable alternative pathway through which the instrument might affect time to availability independently of price while preserving first-stage relevance. The third was a fuller specification including the broader set of covariates used in the main model. Comparing these three specifications allows us to assess the trade-off between parsimony, instrument strength, and adjustment for observable confounders.

The exclusion restriction requires that the HQ Index affect time to availability only through its association with price. Because HQ presence could theoretically influence access timelines directly—for example through closer regulatory interaction, stronger local manufacturer engagement, or other institutional channels—we explicitly examined specifications controlling for HTA/reimbursement delay to mitigate this concern. The IV results should therefore be interpreted as evidence consistent with a causal effect, but not as definitive proof of a causal relationship.

Model fit statistics, residual diagnostics, and instrument strength (via first-stage F-statistics) were reported to ensure the reliability and validity of the IV estimates. All analyses were conducted using standard econometric procedures in Stata/SE 19.5.

Results

Figure 1 illustrates the negative association between national list price levels and time to availability across the 18 countries. The fitted line represents the unadjusted linear association between the list price index and time to availability. Consistent with this pattern, the multivariable regression model revealed a negative association between the list price index and time to availability: each one-point increase in the list price index (European average = 100) was associated with a 7.9-day reduction in time to patient access (p = 0.091, Table 3). A slow HTA/reimbursement process was associated with significantly longer access delays (p = 0.022), while MEA intensity was positively associated with delays but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.103). Population size and GDP per capita were not statistically significant.

Model diagnostics did not reveal major violations of the main regression assumptions (Table 4). These tests are relevant because several of the country-level covariates included in the model—such as GDP per capita, population size, MEA intensity, and HTA/reimbursement delay—could plausibly be correlated with one another in a small cross-country sample. For example, richer or larger countries may also have more developed reimbursement systems or greater use of managed entry arrangements, which could make it difficult to distinguish their separate associations with time to availability. However, multicollinearity was low (all VIFs < 2.5), indicating that the explanatory variables were not so strongly correlated as to make the coefficient estimates unstable or difficult to interpret. The Breusch–Pagan/Cook–Weisberg test did not reject homoskedasticity (p = 0.681), suggesting that the variance of the residuals was reasonably constant across observations and that the conventional standard errors are not obviously distorted by heteroskedasticity. Normality of residuals was assessed using visual inspection of the histogram and Q–Q plot, supplemented by the Shapiro–Wilk test, which did not reject normality (p = 0.941), although this test has limited power in small samples. At the same time, influence diagnostics indicated that several observations—Poland, Denmark, and Ireland—had relatively strong leverage on the fitted results. This matters because, in a small sample, a few countries can exert substantial influence on the estimated association. To examine this issue, a robust regression was estimated, which down-weights the influence of such observations. In that specification, the coefficient on the list price index remained negative, indicating that the direction of the relationship was unchanged, but it became smaller in magnitude and no longer statistically significant (β = − 3.47, p = 0.306). By contrast, the coefficient on slow HTA/reimbursement process remained positive and statistically significant. The coefficient on MEA intensity also remained positive and became statistically significant in the robust regression (p = 0.023), whereas it had not reached statistical significance in the original multivariable model (p = 0.103). Taken together, these diagnostic tests suggest that the negative association between list prices and time to availability observed in the multivariable regression is unlikely to be solely attributable to model misspecification. However, the results appear sensitive to influential observations and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Instrumental-variable analysis using the HQ Index as an instrument for list prices yielded results consistent with a negative effect of higher prices on access delays (Table 5). Across the three IV specifications, the first-stage relationship between the HQ Index and list prices was positive, although its strength varied with the control set. In the preferred parsimonious specification, which includes only the slow HTA/reimbursement indicator, the HQ Index significantly predicted list prices in the first stage (coefficient = 8.04; p = 0.024). In the second stage, a one-point increase in the instrumented list price index was associated with a 14.1-day reduction in time to availability (p = 0.011). In the fuller specification, the corresponding estimate was a 25.9-day reduction (p = 0.071), but the first-stage relationship was weaker. These findings strengthen the case for a negative effect of higher list prices on access delays, while also indicating that the causal interpretation remains sensitive to specification. At the same time, first-stage strength was modest by conventional rules of thumb, particularly in the parsimonious and full specifications, so the IV estimates should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive evidence.

When Poland was excluded from the parsimonious IV analysis, the sample size decreased from 18 to 17 observations. In this reduced sample, the estimated effect of list prices on access time remained negative and statistically significant: a one-point increase in the instrumented list price index was associated with a 13.9-day reduction in time to availability (p = 0.021). The first-stage coefficient on the HQ Index also remained statistically significant (p = 0.037), although the overall first-stage model was only marginally significant (p = 0.056), indicating somewhat weaker instrument support in the reduced sample. This suggests that the negative price–access relationship is not driven solely by a single influential observation. Nevertheless, because the effect size and precision vary across specifications and sample composition, the results should still be interpreted with appropriate caution. Discussion

This study provides empirical evidence consistent with a negative association between higher national list prices and faster patient access to new medicines across 18 European countries. Using multiple regression methods—including IV analysis to address potential reverse causality—the analyses generally indicate a negative relationship between the list price index and time to availability. Countries with higher list prices tended to experience shorter delays between marketing authorisation and patient access, even after controlling for factors such as HTA duration, GDP per capita, population size, and the use of MEAs.

These findings align with previous research suggesting that pharmaceutical manufacturers strategically prioritise early launches in high-price countries [2, 3, 11]. They are also consistent with the literature on international reference pricing, which suggests that manufacturers may delay or sequence launches strategically in lower-price countries in order to avoid downward spillovers on prices in other markets that use external price referencing [2, 12]. In contrast to earlier studies that relied on proxies for expected price or market attractiveness, the present analysis uses observed cross-country differences in national list prices and relates them to cross-country variation in time to reimbursed patient access. The instrumental-variable approach (HQ Index) strengthens inference by addressing potential endogeneity, supporting a plausibly causal interpretation.

A key strength of the study is the use of contemporaneous and comparable data sources, combining the EFPIA Patients WAIT Survey with the Swedish TLV international price index. The use of multivariable OLS alongside 2SLS and robust regression facilitates assessment of confounding, endogeneity, and outlier sensitivity. Sensitivity analyses, including the exclusion of influential observations, further support the stability of the estimated effects.

A few limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the price measure used in the analysis is a country-level list price index rather than observed net prices actually paid by insurers. This is not a weakness of the research question itself since list prices are publicly visible and policy-relevant for launch sequencing and external reference pricing. However, confidential discounts, rebates, and other pricing arrangements may substantially reduce net prices, and these differences are only imperfectly captured by the binary indicator for intensive MEA use. In addition, for countries not covered in the OECD survey, absence from the harmonised data source was coded as no evidence of intensive MEA use. This assumption may have introduced misclassification if some of these countries used MEAs intensively despite not being included in the OECD dataset. Such misclassification would most likely attenuate the estimated association between MEA intensity and time to availability, although the direction and magnitude of bias cannot be determined with certainty in a small cross-country sample. The results should therefore be interpreted as evidence on the role of the national list-price environment, not as estimates of the effect of net prices on access timing. Second, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to examine within-country trends over time or to assess the impact of policy reforms introduced during or after the study period. Third, although the instrumental-variable approach was used to reduce concerns about reverse causality, the validity of the instrument cannot be established conclusively. In particular, it cannot be excluded that the HQ Index is correlated with time to availability through channels other than price. Although the models adjust for HTA/reimbursement delay, this does not fully eliminate that concern. In addition, the statistical support for the strength of the HQ Index as an instrument was only modest in some specifications, raising the possibility of weak-instrument bias. The instrumental-variable estimates should therefore be interpreted with caution, as they strengthen the analysis but do not definitively rule out residual endogeneity, violations of the exclusion restriction, or biases arising from limited instrument strength. Fourth, time to availability was derived from the EFPIA Patients W.A.I.T. Indicator, which is designed to permit standardised comparison across European healthcare systems, but cross-country comparability is not perfect. In particular, availability may be captured through somewhat different reimbursement routes or access channels across countries, and alternative forms of access, such as named-patient schemes or restricted access programmes, may not be captured consistently across healthcare systems [13]. Accordingly, the measure should be interpreted as a harmonised indicator of access timing rather than a perfectly identical measure across all national systems. Fifth, the TLV index reflects the country-level list-price environment rather than observed product-specific launch prices for the medicines included in the EFPIA Patients WAIT cohort. Although the two sources refer to a similar period, the present analysis cannot verify exact product-level correspondence between them. The findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence on the association between national pricing environments and time to availability, rather than as product-level estimates of the effect of launch prices on access timing.

Despite these limitations, the results have important policy implications. Efforts to contain prices—particularly through strict forms of international or external reference pricing—may inadvertently contribute to slower access if they increase manufacturers’ incentives to sequence launches strategically or to delay entry in lower-price markets. At the same time, the findings do not imply that higher net prices or pharmaceutical expenditures are unambiguously desirable, because the analysis concerns publicly visible list-price environments rather than the confidential net prices ultimately paid by insurers. A more promising policy direction may therefore lie in pricing and reimbursement approaches that better align incentives for timely access with value and affordability, including transparent value-based pricing frameworks and well-designed managed entry agreements.

These issues may become even more salient in light of renewed US interest in most-favoured-nation (MFN) or international reference pricing. Although the original US MFN model was rescinded, the 2025 Executive Order on MFN pricing and subsequent Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) proposals for internationally benchmarked drug payment models suggest that US policy may again place greater weight on prices observed in other developed markets. The ERP literature has long emphasised that regulators typically rely on publicly observable list prices rather than confidential net prices, and that such systems can generate cross-country spillovers and strategic launch sequencing by manufacturers [5]. If US benchmarking policies increase the importance of visible foreign prices, manufacturers may face even stronger incentives to protect list prices in reference countries and to sequence launches strategically across Europe. In that case, the mechanisms examined in this paper could become more pronounced. This possibility is also consistent with recent discussion of the 2025 US drug-pricing order, which notes that wider use of external reference pricing may reinforce incentives for delayed launch or restricted access in lower-price markets and may encourage governments to rely more heavily on confidential rebates to shield net prices from international spillovers [14]. At the same time, these implications remain uncertain because the eventual design, scope, and enforcement of US benchmarking policies will determine how strongly European prices affect US reimbursement.

Future research should examine how confidential discounts, payer negotiation capacity, and therapeutic category affect the timing and value of market entry. In particular, analyses focusing on areas such as oncology and orphan drugs may help clarify whether the relationship observed in this study varies across product types with different market and reimbursement dynamics. It would also be valuable to distinguish more clearly between manufacturer-related delay before pricing and reimbursement submission and payer- or HTA-related delay after submission. More generally, study designs that improve causal identification, such as longitudinal analyses or evaluations of policy changes over time, could strengthen the evidence base.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence consistent with a negative association between higher national list prices and shorter time to availability for new medicines across European countries. Countries with higher list prices tended to experience shorter delays in patient access, although the strength of the relationship was sensitive to sample composition and model specification. The instrumental-variable results strengthen the analysis but should be interpreted cautiously, given potential weak-instrument concerns in some specifications and the possibility that the instrument may affect access through channels other than price. The findings therefore suggest that the national list-price environment may influence manufacturer launch strategies and access timelines, but they do not establish a definitive causal relationship or speak directly to the effect of confidential net prices. Policymakers should consider how visible list prices, confidential discounting, and pricing regulation interact in shaping timely access to medicines.
