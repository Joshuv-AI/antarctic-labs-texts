Enteral olive oil supplementation as a rescue strategy for caloric insufficiency in critically ill neonates: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ferid Aliyev, Ece Karatas, Damla Yıldırım, Gozdem Kayki, Sule Yigit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the feasibility and short-term growth outcomes associated with enteral olive oil supplementation in critically ill neonates who were unable to meet daily caloric requirements due to feeding intolerance or fluid restrictions.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 38 neonates admitted to a Level IV neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) between January 2018 and January 2025 who received enteral olive oil supplementation because of insufficient caloric intake despite standard nutritional interventions. Olive oil was started at 1 mL/kg/day and adjusted based on clinical tolerance. Pre- and post-supplementation comparisons were performed for caloric intake (kcal/kg/day), daily weight gain (g/day), weekly head circumference growth (cm/week), weight-for-age and head circumference-for-age Z-scores, and macronutrient energy distribution, as well as clinically documented complications, including gastrointestinal events, respiratory outcomes, and sepsis.ResultsThe mean gestational age was 36 ± 4 weeks and mean birth weight was 2291 ± 826 g. Following supplementation, median caloric intake increased significantly (98.4 to 119.4 kcal/kg/day, p < 0.001), along with daily weight gain (7.5 to 20 g/day, p < 0.001) and weekly head circumference growth (0.70 to 0.96 cm/week, p < 0.001). Weight-for-age and head circumference-for-age Z-scores showed modest but significant improvement. No increase in clinically documented gastrointestinal or respiratory complications was observed during the supplementation period, and sepsis rates remained unchanged.ConclusionIn this selected cohort of critically ill neonates, enteral olive oil supplementation was associated with increased caloric intake and improved short-term growth parameters. Given the retrospective design, small sample size, and absence of a comparator group, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01286-4.

Introduction

Critically ill neonatal populations, including both term neonates with complex conditions such as congenital anomalies or congenital heart disease and preterm infants, face substantial nutritional challenges [1]. Gastrointestinal immaturity, clinical instability, frequent comorbidities, and the need for fluid restriction often complicate the achievement of adequate enteral nutrition in these patients [2]. The metabolic response to critical illness in neonates is complex and dynamic. Although acute illness is accompanied by hormonal and inflammatory stress responses, accumulating evidence indicates that energy and metabolic requirements vary according to the phase and severity of illness and the recovery state, rather than uniformly reflecting a hypermetabolic condition. According to the ESPGHAN Committee on Nutrition, increased energy requirements are more consistently observed during the recovery phase, whereas energy expenditure may be reduced or unchanged during the acute phase of critical illness as part of adaptive metabolic responses [3]. These physiological complexities pose significant challenges in defining optimal nutritional targets in critically ill neonates.

In neonatal intensive care units, nutritional strategies primarily rely on a combination of parenteral and enteral nutrition. Although parenteral nutrition is essential during early stabilization, prolonged use is associated with risks such as hepatic dysfunction, catheter-related infections, and metabolic complications, including hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia [4]. Consequently, transition to enteral nutrition is encouraged whenever feasible. However, achieving adequate energy intake via enteral routes remains difficult in neonates with poor feeding tolerance, gastrointestinal immaturity, or strict fluid limitations [5, 6].

Failure to meet energy requirements in neonates inevitably results in postnatal growth restriction [6]. While human milk and standard enteral formulas are sufficient for healthy term infants, they may not provide adequate energy density for critically ill neonates who are unable to tolerate increased feeding volumes. To address this limitation, strategies such as human milk fortification, use of concentrated formulas, and lipid supplementation, including medium-chain triglycerides and long-chain triglycerides, have been employed to increase caloric density within restricted fluid volumes [7]. While MCT/LCT blends are widely used in developed countries due to their absorption advantages and energy density, their high cost and limited availability in resource-constrained settings present significant barriers [8].

This context highlights the need to explore alternative lipid sources to augment enteral energy intake. Olive oil emerges as a noteworthy option due to its high monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) content (55–83% oleic acid) and distinctive fatty acid profile. Although oleic acid is a long-chain fatty acid, it demonstrates relatively favorable absorption characteristics and β-oxidation efficiency compared to other long-chain fatty acids [9]. Furthermore, olive oil’s phenolic compounds (e.g., hydroxytyrosol, oleuropein) exhibit potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, offering potential benefits against the heightened oxidative stress and inflammation characteristic of critical illness. Nevertheless, data on enteral olive oil supplementation in neonates, particularly among critically ill infants, remain limited.

Existing studies evaluating enteral olive oil administration in neonates are few, and critical aspects such as optimal dosing, duration of supplementation, metabolic safety, and long-term outcomes have not been adequately characterized.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of enteral olive oil supplementation on caloric intake, weight gain, and selected growth parameters in a cohort of critically ill neonates who were unable to meet age- and condition-appropriate caloric targets due to clinical instability or feeding intolerance. This study aims to provide descriptive, hypothesis-generating data regarding the feasibility of olive oil supplementation as an adjunctive energy source in this high-risk population.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

A retrospective cohort study was conducted on patients admitted to the Level 4 neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) at Hacettepe University Ihsan Dogramaci Children’s Hospital between January 2018 and January 2025, providing specialized care for extremely preterm infants and newborns with complex birth defects, including congenital heart defects, malformations, and syndromes. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Hacettepe University Ethics Committee (Approval No: SBA 24/062, Date of Approval: January 9, 2024). The cohort comprised critically ill neonates in the NICU who failed to meet their daily caloric requirements during their hospitalization and therefore received enteral olive oil supplementation.

Determination of energy and protein requirements

Energy and protein requirements were guided by internationally accepted neonatal nutrition recommendations, primarily those of the ESPGHAN Committee on Nutrition, and applied in conjunction with the routine clinical protocols of our NICU. Nutritional targets were individualized based on gestational age, birth weight, underlying diagnosis, and clinical severity. In general, target energy intake ranged from 110 to 135 kcal/kg/day for preterm infants and ≥ 100–120 kcal/kg/day for term but critically ill neonates, with protein provision maintained within age-appropriate recommended ranges whenever enteral tolerance allowed [3].

Standard nutritional management and human milk fortification

Caloric inadequacy was defined as the documented inability to achieve age- and condition-appropriate caloric targets despite standard nutritional interventions, as determined by the treating clinical team. These interventions included optimization of enteral feeding volumes, use of standard or individualized human milk fortification when applicable, and selection or optimization of age-appropriate formula. Human milk was the preferred enteral feeding source whenever available. Standard and individualized human milk fortification practices were applied in accordance with international guidelines, including recommendations from the European Milk Bank Association (EMBA), as well as the routine clinical practice of our unit [10, 11].

In our unit, human milk fortification was initiated once enteral feeding volumes reached approximately 50–80 mL/kg/day. Standard fortification was applied to the following groups of infants:

Standard fortification consisted of the addition of 4 g of human milk fortifier per 100 mL of human milk, irrespective of baseline human milk composition. Individualized fortification strategies were subsequently considered based on clinical response, growth trajectory, and feeding tolerance.

In this study cohort, enteral olive oil supplementation was implemented as an adjunctive strategy to increase caloric intake in situations where conventional nutritional approaches - including standard or individualized fortification and/or formula optimization - were insufficient to meet age- and condition-appropriate caloric requirements, and where further increases in feeding volume or osmolality were not clinically feasible.

In Turkey, donor human milk banking is not available; therefore, donor human milk was not used during the study period.

Fortification intolerance was defined a priori using commonly accepted clinical criteria, including recurrent gastric residuals, vomiting, abdominal distension, feeding interruptions, or clinical concern for necrotizing enterocolitis. In infants who did not tolerate standard fortification or in whom adequate caloric intake could not be achieved despite fortification or formula optimization, fortification was discontinued or deemed insufficient. Parenteral nutrition was initiated according to routine NICU protocols in neonates who were unable to achieve adequate enteral intake, particularly during early stabilization or periods of clinical instability. Parenteral nutrition was gradually tapered as enteral feeding advanced. Enteral olive oil supplementation was not used as a substitute for parenteral nutrition or intravenous lipid provision. Neonates were excluded if they were not fully enterally fed.

Data collection and growth assessment

Demographic and nutritional data such as gestational age, gender, birth weight, diagnosis and feeding regimen (breast milk/formula) was obtained from the electronic database of the hospital and patient charts. The amount of calories taken by the patients before and after olive oil supplementation, daily weight gain, head circumference, gastrointestinal (GI) and respiratory complications that may develop after supplementation and sepsis were recorded.

Caloric intake was calculated using standard nutritional composition tables based on documented volumes of breast milk, formula, parenteral nutrition, and olive oil supplementation recorded in the medical charts. Direct biochemical analysis of nutrient content was not performed. Weight gain was calculated as mean daily weight change normalized to body weight. Daily weight gain (g/day) was determined by dividing the difference between final and baseline weight by the number of observation days. Mean body weight was calculated as the average of baseline and final weight, and growth velocity was expressed as grams per kilogram per day (g/kg/day). Anthropometric measurements were obtained as part of routine NICU care by trained personnel using standardized equipment. Body weight was measured daily using calibrated electronic scales, and head circumference was measured weekly using a non-stretchable measuring tape. Weekly head circumference growth velocity was calculated as the change in head circumference divided by the observation interval.

Feeding tolerance and safety assessment

In this study, feed intolerance was defined a priori using commonly accepted clinical criteria and was applied uniformly to both standard human milk fortification and olive oil supplementation, including recurrent gastric residuals, vomiting, abdominal distension, feeding interruptions, or clinical suspicion of necrotizing enterocolitis. These criteria are consistent with definitions used in studies evaluating tolerance to human milk fortifiers in preterm and critically ill neonates. Safety monitoring in this retrospective cohort was limited to routinely documented clinical outcomes available in the medical records. Feeding tolerance was assessed clinically based on gastrointestinal symptoms (vomiting, abdominal distension, feeding interruptions), respiratory stability, and the occurrence of sepsis or necrotizing enterocolitis. No predefined protocol for biochemical monitoring of lipid metabolism, including serum lipid profiles, liver function tests, fatty acid levels, or objective assessment of steatorrhea, was implemented as part of the study design. Accordingly, safety evaluation in this study reflects short-term clinical tolerability rather than comprehensive metabolic safety. In sepsis scoring, according to the Turkish Neonatal Society and European Medicines Agency, positivity in at least two of the six clinical categories and at least two of the six laboratory categories were evaluated as clinical sepsis [12].

Olive oil supplementation strategy

Enteral olive oil supplementation was introduced as an adjunctive caloric strategy in neonates who remained unable to meet age- and condition-appropriate caloric targets despite optimization of enteral nutrition, including standard or individualized human milk fortification and/or formula adjustment, and in whom further increases in feeding volume or osmolality were not clinically feasible.

Refined olive oil was used for enteral supplementation. The refined olive oil used was a commercially available product routinely used in clinical practice; detailed information on processing characteristics or phenolic content was not available. Olive oil was administered enterally via nasogastric tube in infants receiving intermittent bolus feeds and incorporated into the feeding regimen of infants receiving continuous enteral nutrition. The olive oil was mixed with breast milk or formula immediately prior to administration and gently agitated by trained personnel until a homogeneous lipid–milk mixture was achieved. Olive oil supplementation was initiated at a dose of 1 mL/kg/day and subsequently increased based on individual feeding tolerance and clinical assessment. Patients received comparable olive oil doses regardless of feeding type (human milk, mixed feeding, or formula). Due to the retrospective design, direct assessment of lipid loss related to feeding tube adhesion was not feasible. Accordingly, calculated caloric intake reflects the intended rather than the precisely delivered lipid dose, and this limitation was considered in the interpretation of the results. Tolerance to olive oil supplementation was assessed using predefined clinical criteria for feed intolerance, as described above.

Olive oil supplementation was not implemented as part of a protocolized comparative strategy but was introduced exclusively as a pragmatic rescue intervention in routine clinical care. Accordingly, infants who tolerated standard human milk fortification or achieved adequate caloric intake with conventional nutritional strategies were not eligible for olive oil supplementation and were therefore not included in the study cohort. As a result, a contemporaneous comparator group of infants with similar clinical indications who did not receive olive oil supplementation was not available, since, based on our clinical observations, withholding olive oil from infants who we believed could benefit from its addition to the diet solely for the purpose of creating a control group could have raised ethical concerns, and the study was therefore designed as a non-comparative, descriptive cohort.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data of the patients in the study group was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version v25.0 for mac (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic data and results. The conformity of the variables to normal distribution was examined using visual (histogram and probability plots) and analytical methods (Kolmogorov-Smirnov/Shapiro-Wilk tests). Descriptive analyses were given using mean and standard deviation for normally distributed variables and median and interquartile range for non-normally distributed variables. The effects of olive oil on daily calorie intake, weight gain and growth were examined using the Wilcoxon test. The total type-1 error level for statistical analysis was determined as 5%.

Results

A total of 38 neonates (20 females, 18 males) who met the study inclusion criteria were included in the analysis. The cohort consisted of critically ill neonates with heterogeneous diagnoses, including congenital heart diseases (e.g., hypoplastic left heart syndrome, total anomalous pulmonary venous return, aortic coarctation, tetralogy of Fallot, and critical pulmonary stenosis), prematurity, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, and postoperative duodenal atresia. The mean gestational age at birth was 36 ± 4 weeks, and the mean birth weight was 2291 ± 826 g.

Baseline demographic and nutritional characteristics are summarized in Table 1. At birth, 15 infants (39.5%) were classified as small for gestational age (SGA), 22 (57.9%) as appropriate for gestational age (AGA), and 1 (2.6%) as large for gestational age (LGA). Regarding feeding background, 20 infants (52.6%) received exclusive human milk, 10 (26.3%) received mixed human milk and formula, and 8 (21.1%) were fed formula only. Standard human milk fortification was applied in 30 infants (78.9%), while 8 infants (21.1%) did not receive fortification; fortification intolerance was observed in 4 infants (13.3%). Donor human milk was not used in this cohort.

The median duration of olive oil supplementation was 25 days (IQR: 4–98), and the median caloric contribution from olive oil was 54 kcal/kg/day (IQR: 36–90).

With regard to clinical tolerance, three infants required noninvasive respiratory support prior to initiation of olive oil supplementation. No patient demonstrated an increased need for respiratory support or developed new respiratory complications following supplementation. Additionally, no gastrointestinal adverse events were observed during the supplementation period, and there was no increase in the incidence of late-onset neonatal sepsis.

Discussion

While human milk remains the gold standard, it may be insufficient to meet the increased energy and nutrient requirements of these high-risk populations alone. Although human milk fortification (HMF) is standard practice for very low birth weight (VLBW) infants or preterm neonates requiring supplemental nutrition, it may sometimes fail to provide adequate caloric intake or be poorly tolerated by patients. In this retrospective study, we investigated the effects of enteral olive oil supplementation on growth parameters in critically ill neonates with suboptimal growth in whom standard or individualized fortification and/or formula optimization were insufficient to meet age- and condition-appropriate caloric targets, or where further increases in feeding volume or osmolality were not clinically feasible.

Our study observed a significant increase in short-termmean daily weight gain following enteral olive oil supplementation in neonates with inadequate caloric intake. To date, only two studies have evaluated olive oil supplementation in neonates, both conducted exclusively in preterm populations, reporting improved weight gain and reduced length of hospital stay without major adverse effects [13, 14]. In contrast, our cohort included a substantial proportion of critically ill neonates with complex conditions, including congenital heart disease and infants born small for gestational age (SGA), allowing a descriptive assessment of enteral olive oil supplementation in a broader neonatal population beyond preterm infants. The observed short-term increase in weight gain is most plausibly explained by the provision of additional calories from olive oil, delivered with a low osmotic load and minimal increase in feeding volume. However, the study was not designed to assess subgroup-specific efficacy, and the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of differential benefit according to gestational age or birth growth status.

Adequate protein intake is essential for linear growth and lean tissue accretion in neonates, as emphasized in international nutritional guidelines [15, 16]. Although olive oil provides energy without protein, in our cohort it was used strictly as an adjunctive measure while standard or individualized human milk fortification was continued and, in selected cases, additional protein supplementation was provided when clinically indicated. Consistent with this approach, the proportion of energy derived from protein did not change significantly after supplementation at the group level.

From a nutritional standpoint, increasing caloric density through lipid supplementation may alter the dietary energy–protein balance if protein provision is not maintained. In our cohort, olive oil supplementation was implemented only after standard or individualized human milk fortification had been applied and further increases in feeding volume or osmolality were not clinically feasible. Analysis of macronutrient energy distribution demonstrated that although lipid-derived energy increased, the proportion of energy derived from protein did not change significantly at the group level. Nevertheless, due to the retrospective design and heterogeneity of feeding practices, absolute protein intake expressed as g/kg/day and detailed longitudinal protein exposure could not be reliably reconstructed for all patients. Therefore, while short-term improvements in weight gain and head circumference growth were observed, the impact of olive oil supplementation on growth quality, body composition, and lean mass accretion cannot be directly assessed. Increasing caloric density without parallel increases in protein intake may theoretically favor fat mass deposition over proportional somatic growth, particularly in preterm and critically ill neonates, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation and for future prospective studies incorporating detailed macronutrient quantification and body composition assessment.

Within this context of short-term energy support, another notable finding was the significant acceleration in weekly head circumference (HC) growth velocity following supplementation. HC growth is commonly used as a surrogate marker of early brain growth, particularly in preterm and SGA infants [17]. The neonatal brain accounts for a disproportionately high proportion of total energy expenditure, and improved short-term energy availability may support cerebral growth during critical periods. Adequate energy intake is also necessary to enable effective utilization and metabolic conversion of essential fatty acid precursors. Although olive oil is not a direct source of long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (LCPUFAs), it contains linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid, which serve as substrates for endogenous LCPUFA synthesis [18, 19]. Adequate total fat and energy intake facilitates these conversion processes, and the observed short-term acceleration in head circumference growth velocity may be associated with improved short-term energy availability in this vulnerable population. However, head circumference growth alone cannot be considered a surrogate marker of neurodevelopmental outcomes. In the absence of standardized neurodevelopmental assessments or long-term follow-up data, the observed increase in head circumference velocity should be interpreted with caution and does not imply a direct neurodevelopmental benefit.

Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress associated with critical illness may further compromise growth and neurodevelopment. In neonates with critical congenital heart disease (CCHD), the risk of gastrointestinal ischemia due to splanchnic hypoperfusion makes olive oil’s low osmotic load and putative anti-inflammatory properties may be theoretically favorable for this population. In this context, olive oil’s low osmotic load and its content of monounsaturated fatty acids, phenolic compounds, and vitamin E may offer theoretical advantages by reducing gastrointestinal stress and modulating inflammatory pathways [20, 21]. These properties may complement caloric provision in selected high-risk patients.

Enteral olive oil supplementation was well-tolerated in our cohort, with no observed increase in major gastrointestinal adverse events, including feeding interruptions, gastric residuals, or necrotizing enterocolitis. These findings suggest acceptable short-term clinical tolerability of enteral olive oil supplementation in this cohort. However, no conclusions can be drawn regarding metabolic or long-term safety due to the absence of systematic biochemical monitoring. In addition, isolated cases of oily stools or steatorrhea have been reported in the literature, warranting caution in infants with severe cholestatic liver disease and limited bile acid pools [22].

From a practical perspective, olive oil differs from commercially available MCT/LCT formulations in several theoretical aspects. MCT-containing lipid preparations are designed to enhance fat absorption and provide readily available energy, particularly in preterm infants with limited bile acid pools. In contrast, olive oil is predominantly composed of long-chain monounsaturated fatty acids and does not provide medium-chain triglycerides. However, its low osmolality, wide availability, and familiarity in clinical nutrition make olive oil a pragmatic alternative in selected clinical settings where commercial MCT/LCT preparations are unavailable, not tolerated, or contraindicated. Importantly, olive oil supplementation in this study was used as an adjunct to support energy intake rather than as a substitute for balanced neonatal lipid formulations.

Given the retrospective design and the absence of a comparator group, the findings of this study should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of causality. Enteral olive oil supplementation appears to be a feasible strategy for providing additional energy in a selected population of critically ill neonates with limited nutritional options. As reliable serial length measurements and detailed protein intake data were not consistently available, the impact of this strategy on linear growth and growth quality cannot be assessed in the present study. Prospective, controlled studies are required to better delineate its comparative effectiveness and safety across different neonatal subgroups.

Study limitations

Our study has several important limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and limits control over confounding factors, including concomitant therapies and underlying clinical conditions. Second, the small sample size (n = 38) reduced statistical power and precluded meaningful subgroup analyses.

Although olive oil supplementation was associated with improvements in caloric intake and weight gain, linear growth could not be adequately evaluated due to the lack of reliable serial length measurements in this retrospective cohort.

Importantly, the absence of a comparator group reflects routine clinical practice rather than a deliberate study design choice. Olive oil supplementation was implemented exclusively as a rescue nutritional strategy in infants who failed to achieve adequate caloric intake despite individualized standard human milk fortification and/or formula optimization, and in whom further increases in feeding volume or osmolality were not clinically feasible. Consequently, a contemporaneous group of infants with comparable clinical indications who did not receive olive oil was not available, limiting direct comparisons with conventional fortification strategies.

Due to the retrospective nature of the study, objective metabolic biomarkers such as lipid profiles or serum fatty acid levels were not consistently available, and only short-term outcomes could be assessed. In addition, reliable serial length measurements were lacking, limiting evaluation of linear growth.

Finally, cohort heterogeneity prevented the application of a uniform caloric threshold, and therefore the findings should be interpreted as exploratory, without causal inference.

Conclusion

Enteral olive oil supplementation may represent a pragmatic adjunctive strategy to increase energy intake in selected critically ill neonates who are unable to meet caloric requirements due to feeding intolerance or fluid restriction, despite standard nutritional interventions. Owing to its high caloric density and low osmolarity, this approach was associated with improvements in short-term growth parameters, including weight gain and head circumference growth, in this retrospective cohort. Given the retrospective design, small sample size, absence of a comparator group, and limited safety monitoring, these findings should be interpreted with caution and considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Prospective, controlled studies incorporating detailed macronutrient assessment, comprehensive safety monitoring, and long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up are required to more clearly define the role of enteral olive oil supplementation in the nutritional management of critically ill neonates.
