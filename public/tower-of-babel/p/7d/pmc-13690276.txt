Menstrual cycle experiences and Ramadan fasting: a mixed-methods study among Lebanese women
Authors: Georges Yared, Jihad Al Hassan, Charlotte El Hajjar, Maysaa Traboulsi, Christopher Massaad, Kariman Ghazal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRamadan fasting is a central religious practice for Muslims, yet women are exempt during menstruation. Despite this exemption, many still face physical, emotional, and social challenges.ObjectiveTo explore the impact of Ramadan fasting on menstrual cycle patterns, women’s perceptions, and coping strategies.MethodsWe conducted a mixed-methods study using structured questionnaires that were circulated on social media platforms and in the clinic after Ramadan 2025. Convenience sampling was used as 154 Lebanese women aged 13–45 years took part in the study. Participants with regular cycles were included; those with amenorrhea or menstrual abnormalities were excluded. Both quantitative and qualitative data were analyzed to capture physical, psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions.ResultsOver 60% of participants reported fatigue and cramps during menstruation. More than half had cycles lasting 6–7 d, while 28% noticed irregularities during Ramadan. Emotionally, 70% expressed difficulty with not fasting. Social stigma was reported by about one-third, while 55% felt awareness around menstruation in Ramadan was lacking. Of 18% admitted using medication to delay menstruation, and 90% called for health education in religious spaces.ConclusionsRamadan fasting affects menstrual patterns and psychosocial wellbeing. Community-based awareness and culturally sensitive guidance are essential to support women’s health and spirituality.Keywords: Ramadan fasting, menstrual cycle, reproductive health, psychosocial wellbeing, menstruation stigma, endocrinology, pain

Introduction

During the Holy Month of Ramadan, fasting is required among Muslims as it one of one of the basic five pillars of Islam mentioned in the Qur’an [1]. The five pillars of Islam consist of Shahada (profession of faith), Salah (prayer), Zakah (almsgiving), Siyam (fasting), and Hajj (pilgrimage), with fasting being considered essential for all healthy adult Muslims [1,2]. Indeed, during this month Muslims commit time for devotion, solidarity on the community level along with spiritual reflection. The length of the days during this month can vary from year to year, but also from a country to another. Since it is based on the lunar calendar, it depends on the latitude, longitude, and season [3]. As a matter of fact and, Muslims restrain from eating and drinking in order to follow this obligation. For the duration of one month, they abstain from doing so from sunrise until sunset, with a fasting period which ranges from 11 to 20 h everyday [1–4]. This calendar is based on moon sighting so Ramadan happens at a different time each year. Depending on the time of year, Muslims fast anything between 12 and 18 or more hours each day for around 30 d after the sighting of the crescent moon [3,4].

Nonetheless, it is worth noting that the Holy month of Ramadan is not merely associated with the abstinence from eating and drinking. Indeed, Ramadan presents an opportunity to pare back to basics: to cleanse diet, house, and thoughts while increasing spiritual practice [2,5].

It is true that fasting during the Holy month of Ramadan is obligatory for all Muslims. Nonetheless, it is important to note that many individuals who are not mentally or physically capable of doing so, are exempted. This includes menstruating women and women in the post-partum period [6–8]. For centuries, this tradition has existed. Nevertheless, it is not frequently discussed that numerous Muslim women encounter challenges during their menstrual cycles. These factors further affect their lives and spirituality [6,9,10]. The menstrual cycle is typically characterized by the frequency of periods, the duration of flow, and the volume of blood loss [11]. In the mid-reproductive years, the average menstrual cycle length ranges from 28 to 30 d. Menstruation usually lasts about 5 d, with blood loss volumes ranging from 25 to 35 mL [11,12]. The regulation of menstruation is controlled by an endocrine axis through hormonal regulation and feedback loops, known as the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian (HPO) axis [13–15].

Irregular menstruation cycles are a common sign of many disorders affecting this axis. According to this viewpoint, the menstrual cycle is still a crucial sign of a woman’s reproductive health [16]. Menstrual cycle irregularities can be caused by a variety of factors, including body mass index, age of menarche, food, exercise, smoking, and psychosocial stressors. From this perspective, knowing about normal variability in menstrual cycle is important. Indeed, it may be the first sign and symptom of potential reproductive abnormalities among women [17,18].

For example, the month-long Ramadan fast in Lebanon lasts for 12 h every day. All daily activities, sleep patterns, and eating habits would change during Ramadan fasting. This, in turn, could have a significant impact on the menstrual cycle [19,20]. Specifically, nutritional consumption significantly varies throughout Ramadan, according to Shatila et al. In fact, the researchers showed that Lebanese people had changed their consumption habits in 12 out of 19 food groups. It includes consuming more veggies and sweets but less cereals and dairy [20].

It is a common misconception that the female body alone controls the cycle. However, the latter is governed by multiple neuroendocrine interactions which include autonomic nervous system, corticotropic, and thyrotropic factors, beyond the traditional gonadotropic hormones [21]. On a different level, women often suffer from a range of menstrual-related issues, such as dysmenorrhea, menorrhagia, irregular menstrual-related menses, discomfort, exhaustion, and mood swings [22].

Naturally, fasting significantly alters a person’s typical eating, sleeping, and behavioral habits. Nonetheless, women face additional challenges at times due to their menstrual cycle, during which neither fasting nor praying is obligatory, but they can engage in supplication and remembrance. For instance, during Ramadan, sleep-wake patterns are particularly disrupted, with delays in sleep cycles and reduced deep sleep [23]. In addition, women face additional challenges as Ramadan fasting significantly impacts menstrual cycles. According to Yavangi et al., 30% of women in the study experienced abnormal menstrual patterns during the fasting month, compared to 11.3% before Ramadan, particularly among those fasting more than 15 d [19].

Considering how the menstrual cycle plays a significant role in women’s health and disease, it remains of the utmost importance to evaluate how it is affected by Ramadan fast.

To our knowledge, this is the first study that investigates the multifaceted experiences of women in managing their menstrual cycles during Ramadan.

This area in the literature remains underexplored as limited number of studies study the intersection between reproductive health and religious obligations. Accordingly, this study contributes new insights into the existing body of knowledge, by exploring how menstruation affects spiritual practice, social perception, and overall wellness. It also provides insights on how to manage fasting and prayer when faced with irregular periods, as this often leads to uncertainty among muslin women: should you continue fasting and praying, or is the bleeding considered menstruation?

Material and methods

This observational study with a cohort retrospective approach aims to assess the relationship between Ramadan fasting and menstrual cycle among Lebanese women. We conducted a mixed-methods study using structured questionnaires that were circulated on social media platforms and in the clinic after Ramadan 2025. Convenience sampling was used as 154 Lebanese women aged 13–45 years took part in the study. No personal identifiers were collected. Indeed, quantitative findings were complemented by qualitative interpretations. In particular, statistical results did demonstrate measurable trends (psychological distress and perceived stigma). In parallel, qualitative narratives did provide a contextual insight into the lived experiences of participants.

The inclusion criteria of this study were female teenagers and women between 13 and 45 years old with menstrual cycle. The exclusion criteria were patient having amenorrhea or not having menstruation in 3 consecutive months; having one or more abnormalities in menstrual cycle (hypermenorrhea/menorrhagia, hypomenorrhea, oligomenorrhea, polymenorrhea, and/or metrorrhagia); irregular menstrual cycle in last 3 months dem (Table 1).

Quantitative data was gathered using Likert-scaled questions. It was analyzed descriptively. Irregular menstruation was self reported as per FIGO criteria. In addition, qualitative data was gathered through open-ended prompts in the online survey. It further underwent thematic analysis by using the Braun and Clarke framework.

Results

Physical impact of menstruation

A significant 60.9% of participants reported annoying physical symptoms during menstruation, primarily fatigue and cramps (Figure 1(A)). This indicates that for many, menstruation imposes not just a temporary physical inconvenience but also compromises their ability to perform daily tasks, including spiritual observance.

(A) Physical symptoms during menstruation. (B) Cycle duration. (C) Irregularity of cycles during Ramadan.

The majority of participants also had cycles lasting 6–7 d (54.3%), which overlaps with a substantial portion of Ramadan (Figure 1(B)).

Notably, 27.8% of women reported irregular cycles (Figure 1(C)), which reinforces the importance of education and support on how to adapt religious practices.

Emotional and spiritual conflict

An overwhelming 70.5% of participants felt psychological or spiritual difficulty due to not fasting during menstruation, despite being religiously exempt (Figure 2(A)). This internal conflict may stem from cultural expectations or feelings of guilt and exclusion from group worship.

(A) psychological or spiritual difficulty due to not fasting; (B) engagement in alternative forms of worship (dhikr, supplication, charity…).

Although 44.2% engage in alternative forms of worship like dhikr and charity (Figure 2(B)), many are unsure about their validity or effectiveness during menstruation. These insights highlight a disconnection between religious literacy and emotional needs.

Social awareness and menstrual stigma

Only 10.7% believed there is sufficient societal awareness regarding menstruation in Ramadan, while a majority of 55% claimed that the latter is far from being sufficient (Figure 3(A)). On another level, a combined 30% of respondents had experienced shyness or judgment when not fasting during menstruation, which reveals persistent social stigma (Figure 3(B)). Encouragingly, 67.5% are able to speak openly with family or friends about this topic, which points out to slow but emerging normalization in some circles (Figure 3(C)). Moreover, 89.9% aspire for greater awareness about women’s health in mosques and religious gathering which steers the wheel toward a community appealing for change (Figure 3(D)).

(A) Perception on societal awareness regarding menstruation in Ramadan. (B) Social stigma regarding abstinence from fasting. (C) Openness to discuss menstruation during Ramadan. (D) Need for greater awareness pertaining to women’s health.

Religious practices and coping strategies

While 89.3% fast Ramadan regularly (Figure 4(A)), 18.5% admitted using medications to delay menstruation for religious continuity. Though permissible under guidance, this suggests a deeper pressure to remain consistent in religious participation (Figure 4(B)). Furthermore, 47.3% said menstruation affects their energy levels even on fasting days. The decision to delay a natural process is often not rooted solely in faith, but also in social and emotional expectations (Figure 4(C)). A total of 54.1% expressed interest in workshops addressing women’s health and fasting, which reflects further readiness for institutional engagement (Figure 4(D)).

Discussion

With the arrival of Ramadan – the month of fasting and Qur’an – Muslim women worldwide reflect on their fast and its associated rewards. A common concern arises regarding the divine command to refrain from fasting and prayers during menstruation and postnatal bleeding.

Studies have shown that dietary changes, altered sleep cycles, and reduced caloric intake during Ramadan influence menstrual regularity and hormonal balance. These physiological effects mirror the lived experiences shared in this study.

Physiological effects of Ramadan on menstrual cycle

A substantial proportion of participants (60.9%) reported bothersome physical symptoms during menstruation. The latter included fatigue and cramps which subsequently had an impact on their everyday lives in addition to their spiritual involvement (Figure 1(A)). In addition, the majority of participants in this study reported cycle durations of 6–7 d which often overlap with Ramadan as it is depicted in Figure 1(B). Moreover, 27.8% of participants reported irregular cycles during Ramadan (Figure 1(C)), which is consistent with the findings of Yavangi et al. as previously stated. Indeed, according to the aforementioned study, approximately 30% of women experienced abnormal menstrual patterns during the fasting month compared to 11.3% before Ramadan, particularly among those fasting more than 15 d [19].

Although hormonal biomarkers were not measured in this study, existing literature does suggest that physiological pathways can help in interpreting these observations. From a biological point of view, these findings are highly plausible. Indeed, during the Holy month of Ramadan, individuals do experience repeated cycles of fasting and refeeding. Accordingly, significant changes can be noticed, especially when it comes to sleeping patterns. In fact, during this month, people tend to wake up at an earlier hour, before sunrise, for the first meal. In addition, many individuals tend to return to sleep at a later hour or add an afternoon nap. Accordingly, it can be inferred that these behavioral changes can subsequently cause alterations in hormonal secretions.

Of important notice, these disrupted feeding and sleep schedules during Ramadan abolish the normal circadian rhythm of cortisol. Previous literature has reported that disrupted circadian rhythms during Ramadan may alter cortisol secretion patterns [24]. Moreover, negative energy balance from fasting can impair the HPO axis. This is achieved by reducing leptin levels, which eventually leads to decreased gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pulsatility and reproductive dysfunction. After 6–8 h of fasting, a steady drop in leptin concentration suppresses the pulsatility of GnRH in the hypothalamus. Therefore, it subsequently reduces pituitary LH and FSH secretion. In turn, it can impair ovarian function via the HPO axis. This may contribute to menstrual irregularities observed during prolonged fasting during Ramadan [24].

Emotional and spiritual conflict

Perhaps one of the most striking findings in this study relates to the overwhelming psychological burden associated with menstruation during Ramadan. In this study, 70.5% of participants reported emotional or spiritual difficulty with not fasting despite being religiously exempt (Figure 2(A)). Accordingly, nearly half (44.2%) engaged in alternative forms of worship (such as dhikr or charity), however many remained uncertain of their religious validity (Figure 2(B)). These findings depict a flagrant gap between religious principles/exemptions and personal emotions. Indeed, this dissonance results in feelings of guilt despite being religiously exempted. This alone may act as a stressor, and as previously mentioned can ultimately affect the HPO axis function. From this perspective, one can infer that the stress during fasting is not merely physiologic but also has a psychosocial component. This is especially relevant in the Lebanese context whereby families tend to operate within a strongly collectivistic culture. Indeed, family/religious obligations are socially reinforced [24]. In this regard, the role of patrilineality cannot be overlooked as well. That being said, on a cultural level, Lebanese individuals tend to experience both shame and guilt when compared to individualistic cultures.

Social awareness and menstrual stigma

Our findings reveal persistent, though slowly improving, social stigma pertaining to menstruation in Ramadan. Only 10.7% of participants believed societal awareness about menstruation was sufficient (Figure 3(A)), while nearly one-third experienced shyness or judgment when not fasting (Figure 3(B)).

Another key finding which should not be overlooked is the fact that 67.5% of respondents felt able to discuss menstruation openly with family or friends (Figure 3(C)). This encouraging statistic showcases that intergenerational communication might be improving in this context. Furthermore, 89.9% of respondents expressed a deep desire for greater awareness about women’s health in mosques and religious gatherings (Figure 3(D)). This puts the steering wheel in the hands of public health authorities and stakeholders community-based health education.

This is in line with the existing body of literature whereby Muslim women were shown support mosque-based health interventions. This is especially relevant with imam-led sermons and peer-led educational classes by respected, religiously and health-educated women [25]. There is also growing enthusiasm in communities for discussing such topics. The seed of change has been planted and studies have shown that 88.5% of women believe menstruation should be openly discussed in schools and families. Another 70.5% shared their awareness of menstrual hygiene, especially among urban populations [26].

Mitigation strategies

In order to mitigate the effect of fasting on the menstrual cycle, women are encouraged to consume a nutrient-rich diet. As a matter of fact, during non-fasting periods, for instance, at suhoor, they can consume carbohydrate-rich meals (oats, whole grains), protein-rich food (eggs/yogurt…), healthy fats (nuts/avocado) in addition to spinach and bananas. This diet will help them stabilize leptin levels, which in turn would preserve the pulsatility of GnRH [27].

In addition, the current body of literature denotes that fasting during Ramadan can flatten cortisol rhythm [28], which is typical of chronic stress. Moreover, Zeiders et al. further demonstrate that consistent sleep can help regulate these levels [29]. Therefore, women are recommended to get between 7 and 8 h of sleep per night.

On a psychological level, it is also important to raise awareness pertaining to religious exemptions during Ramadan. The existing literature underlines that Muslim women do prefer sermons led by imams. They also prefer educational with peers in order to promote health discussions [25]. Furthermore, Bajaj et al. discuss that some women tend to hesitate to use religious exemptions. This mainly stems from familial and societal pressures [30]. From this perspective, one can infer that that is a growing need for spreading awareness on the topic.

Limitations

Despite our findings, it is also worth noting that our study is not without limitations. Indeed, our sample included 17 patients over the age of 45 (11.3%) which may confound our results to a certain extent. Moreover, irregular menstruation was self reported in this study which makes it prone to recall bias. The definition of irregular menstruation lies on the FIGO criteria; however, it lacks objective bleeding volumes. In addition, while our study proposed that hormonal mechanisms (leptin, cortisol) could explain menstrual changes, the study did not measure any biomarker to further support this claim. Future prospective studies which could incorporate endocrine measurements are warranted to validate these hypotheses. The latter would assess menstrual cycle changes during Ramadan fasting with more subjects and measuring hormones concentration that might contribute to menstrual cycle changes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, it remains undeniable that Ramadan fasting intersects with menstrual health. This is palpable on complex physical, emotional, and social levels. While many women experience these challenges successfully, it should not be overlooked that a substantial proportion experiences psychological strain and perceived stigmas. From this perspective, there is a growing need for culturally sensitive education and open dialogue on the matter. These initiatives are important in order to support the wellbeing of women during the Holy Month.

Recommendations

Mosque-based initiatives could address women’s wellness in the Holy Month of Ramadan. It would target Lebanese Muslim women aged 13–45 years (with n = 200, a pilot cohort). It would prioritize women living in Beirut/Mount Lebanon but can also extend to rural areas in the Bekaa. It would include weekly sessions and workshops 4 weeks prior to the Holy Month in 50 mosques. Sessions would tackle religious exemptions, nutrition for menstrual stability, stress management, and dialogue regarding stigma on the topic. This initiative would be led by imams and OBGYNS. Material of the sessions would be provided in Arabic.
