Topology-guided rotational dynamics of magnetic soliton assemblies under pulsed current
Authors: Zhuolin Li, Wataru Koshibae, Kosuke Karube, Yasujiro Taguchi, Yoshinori Tokura, Naoto Nagaosa, Xiuzhen Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emergent topological spin textures, such as nanometric skyrmions and antiskyrmions, not only exhibit a wealth of novel physical phenomena but also represent promising candidates for next-generation spintronic devices due to their topological stability and low-current-driven dynamics. Investigating their responses to electric currents is essential for uncovering unique electromagnetic properties and advancing their integration into electronic technologies. While considerable progress has been made through extensive research over the past decade, key challenges still persist. In this study, we demonstrate the novel, current-driven oppositely rotating dynamics of skyrmion and antiskyrmion assemblies in terms of Lorentz transmission electron microscopy. Their senses of rotation are strongly dictated by their inherent topological charges and remain largely unaffected by current direction, showing consistent behavior observed across various sample geometries. Further experimental analyses have revealed a reduction in angular velocity as (anti)skyrmions move away from the sample edge. The theoretical analyses, combined with micromagnetic simulations, highlight the critical roles of boundary-induced confining potentials and gyrotropic forces in steering the rotational dynamics of (anti)skyrmions. These findings offer new insights into current-driven (anti)skyrmion dynamics and open promising avenues for advancing topological concepts in confined spintronic systems.

Introduction

Magnetic skyrmions, characterized by their vortex-like noncollinear spin configurations, occupy an important position in contemporary condensed matter physics due to their unique topological properties and promising functional applications1,2. Skyrmions are found in diverse magnetic systems and exhibit sizes ranging from nanometers to micrometers3–5. Their topological charge Q is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q=\frac{1}{4\pi }\iint {{{\boldsymbol{m}}}}(r)\cdot ({\partial }_{x}{{{\bf{m}}}}({{{\rm{r}}}})\times {\partial }_{y}{{{\bf{m}}}}({{{\rm{r}}}})){{{{\rm{d}}}}}^{2}{{{\bf{r}}}}$$\end{document}Q=14π∬m(r)⋅(∂xm(r)×∂ym(r))d2r, where m(r) is the unit vector of magnetization. The Q typically takes integer values of ±1 for skyrmions, with the sign set by the core polarity1,2. Investigating the dynamics of skyrmions is essential for gaining insight into their intricate topological entities and extending their applicability6,7. The dynamics of skyrmions are driven by various external stimuli, including magnetic fields8, electric/thermal currents9–14, magnons15,16 and acoustic waves17. Among these, current-induced dynamics are particularly well-regarded and have been intensively studied due to their operational simplicity and superior energy efficiency. The dynamics of skyrmions under current flows can be described by the Thiele equation6,18,19, in which the gyrotropic vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G=4\pi Q\hat{z}$$\end{document}G=4πQz^ originates from the topological properties of skyrmions. As a consequence, current-driven skyrmions acquire a finite transverse velocity component, known as the skyrmion Hall effect1,6,18,19. This intriguing phenomenon has been observed in both multilayer systems12,13 and bulk samples14,20, emerging as a defining feature of skyrmions.

In realistic systems, skyrmions inevitably experience boundary-induced confinement, which can strongly modify their dynamics under electric current flows. The interplay between gyrotropic forces and confining potentials leads to rich transport behaviors distinct from those in unbounded media21,22, highlighting the importance of confinement effects for both fundamental studies and technological implementations.

In contrast to the extensive progress made in the study of skyrmions, the investigations of antiskyrmions remain relatively limited. Antiskyrmions are characterized by a topological charge of opposite sign23 to that of skyrmions and have been demonstrated to form in non-centrosymmetric magnetic systems with D2d or S4 symmetry24,25, where anisotropic Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interactions (DMI) give rise to complex spin textures26–28. Antiskyrmions have been observed in Heusler compounds26,29 and schreibersite-type materials, including (Fe₀.₆₃Ni₀.₃Pd₀.₀₇)₃P (FNPP)30–32, as well as in multilayer films33,34. While theoretical models predict that the dynamics of antiskyrmions should mirror those of skyrmions with reversed trajectories due to the sign reversal of the topological charge35, experimental validation is still scarce. To date, only experimentally confirmed current-driven motion of antiskyrmions is their constrained sliding along stripe domains32,36, offering limited insight into dynamics guided by topological properties. More complex behaviors—such as rotational motion—have neither been experimentally demonstrated nor systematically explored. This lack of experimental insight leaves a critical gap in understanding the topological properties of antiskyrmion dynamics, underscoring the need for further investigation.

In this study, through in-situ Lorentz transmission electron microscopy (L-TEM), we have identified novel current-induced rotations in skyrmion and antiskyrmion assemblies confined within finite geometries. By altering the direction of the pulse current, we have demonstrated that the consistent rotational dynamics of the assemblies are guided by their intrinsic topological charges, independent of the current-flow orientation. The first experimental observation of current-driven motion in antiskyrmion assemblies also validates the dynamic manifestation of its opposite topology with skyrmions. Additionally, experimental observations in samples with different geometries have confirmed that the rotation direction of (anti)skyrmions is independent of sample geometry. In the circular-shaped sample, the analyses of angular velocity across varying radial distances have revealed a decrease in skyrmion velocity as they approach the center of the sample, highlighting the significant impact of the boundary potential on rotational dynamics. Further validation through micromagnetic simulations aligns with theoretical analyses, underscoring the critical role of both the boundary and the gyrotropic force in governing skyrmion motion.

Results

Unidirectional rotation of (anti)skyrmion assemblies driven by pulse currents

Here, to highlight the differences in in-plane magnetization configurations between skyrmions and antiskyrmions, we describe skyrmions as vortex-like and antiskyrmions as antivortex-like magnetization textures, extending beyond the definition solely on topological charge. A comprehensive classification and detailed depiction of these unique spin configurations are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1.

The skyrmion assemblies (Fig. 1d) or antiskyrmion assemblies (Fig. 1e) are respectively stabilized under the external field. As defined in Supplementary Note 1, when the magnetic field B is applied along the −z axis, the created skyrmion assemblies possess a topological charge of Q = +1, while the antiskyrmion assemblies possess Q = −1. Both skyrmion and antiskyrmion assemblies display rotational dynamics under pulsed currents, yet rotating senses are opposite: counterclockwise (CCW) for skyrmions (Fig. 1d), while clockwise (CW) for antiskyrmions (Fig. 1e) when the external field is kept along the −z axis. Importantly, the current flows, whether along the +x or −x axis, do not affect the direction of skyrmion/antiskyrmion rotations, highlighting that the senses of rotation are highly related to the topological charge. Additionally, as shown in the schematics (Fig. 1), (anti)skyrmions located near the sample center show slower motions than those nearer the sample boundary, illustrating the significant impact of boundary effects on their rotational dynamics.

In addition, Supplementary Fig. S3 compares skyrmion assemblies stabilized under opposite perpendicular magnetic fields (along the +z direction). As a result of the reversed field, the skyrmions carry topological charges Q = −1, opposite to the case in Fig. 2d−f. Correspondingly, the direction of rotation under pulsed current excitation is reversed to CW (see also Supplementary video 4), confirming the critical role of topological charge. The striking contrast in rotational direction between skyrmions and antiskyrmions underscores a strong correlation between the rotational dynamics and the intrinsic topological properties of these spin textures. These results advance the understanding and exploitation of topologically guided rotational mechanisms, providing a novel pathway for current-driven spin textures without relying on thermal gradients or external magnetic fields. Meanwhile, this marks the first experimental observation of current-driven motion of antiskyrmions associated with their topological properties. The motion, which is opposite to that of skyrmions, reflects their contrasting topological intrinsics.

The effect of confinement potential on rotational dynamics

When pulsed currents were applied along the short axis of a rectangular sample (see Supplementary Fig. S4), rotational dynamics of skyrmions were observed. To further investigate the geometric effect on the observed dynamics, a circular sample was fabricated using FIB. The SEM image (Fig. 3a) illustrates the structure, which consists of two tungsten electrode pads connected to a central FNPP region encircled by a tungsten ring. A protective carbon layer was also deposited on top to prevent the curtaining effect during FIB thinning processes42. Due to geometric confinement, the skyrmions within the central FNPP region (outlined by a black dashed line) arrange into a circular pattern when stabilized under a perpendicular magnetic field (Fig. 3b). Upon subsequent application of a pulsed current through the tungsten pads, skyrmions exhibited CCW rotation (Supplementary Fig. S5, Supplementary Video 5), in accordance with observations described in Fig. 2d−f, thus confirming that the rotation direction of topological spin textures does not depend on sample geometry. COMSOL simulations, as detailed in the “Methods” section, indicate that the current inhomogeneity and temperature gradient under pulsed currents (Supplementary Fig. S6) are minimal and negligible for the rotational dynamics.

The angular velocity, ω, of skyrmions was further quantitatively estimated at four different radial distances (schematically depicted in Fig. 3c). For each radial position, angular velocities were extracted from three representative skyrmions. The results with varying current density in Fig. 3d demonstrate a decreasing trend in ω when the skyrmions approach the sample center, with ω finally dropping to 0. The error bars reflect the experimental uncertainties, such as statistical uncertainty and local pinning effects. Owing to the small sample tilting angle (~5 degrees) and minimal thickness variation (<50 nm) in our experiment, their influence on the extracted angular velocities is negligible. The representative skyrmion trajectories under the same current conditions are shown in Supplementary Fig. S7. Such a spatially correlated trend is difficult to reconcile with random pinning or homogeneous damping effects, which would not naturally produce a well-defined radial dependence. Due to the spin tilting at the sample boundaries in the DMI system, the skyrmions experience a repulsive confinement potential from the boundary1,21. Since the boundary potential energy experienced by skyrmions also exhibits a similar decreasing trend with reducing radial distance, this indicates that the rotational velocity is strongly influenced by the boundary confinement potential. Additionally, the current-density dependence of ω is also shown in Fig. 3e. The increase in ω with current density is consistent with previous current-driven experiments on skyrmions20,43,44, further highlighting the importance of the STT effect in initiating this rotational motion. The absence of measurable texture distortions and excitation timescales far exceeding intrinsic relaxation times suggests that effective inertia plays a negligible role in the present regime.

These results suggest that the continuous rotation of a skyrmion arises from repeated pulsed excitations followed by relaxation dynamics. During each pulse, the system is displaced from the equilibrium state, and in the subsequent relaxation state, boundary confinement and the gyrotropic term dominate, resulting in a finite angular displacement. Repetition of this excitation–relaxation cycle accumulates into a net rotational motion of a skyrmion.

The dynamics of skyrmion assemblies with a topological charge of Q = +1 under current excitation were further investigated through comprehensive simulations. Initially, the simulations under DC excitation (Supplementary Fig. S9, Supplementary Video 8) revealed that the skyrmion assembly firstly shifted to a new equilibrium position but subsequently ceased to move, indicating the absence of sustained rotational dynamics. In contrast, under the pulsed current with periodic excitation intervals (t1) followed by relaxation intervals (t2 = 2t1), the simulation results (Fig. 4e–g, Supplementary Video 9) consistently revealed a CCW rotation of skyrmions after each excitation-relaxation cycle, confirming the behavior discussed above. Conversely, when the system configuration was altered to antiskyrmions possessing a topological charge of Q = −1, as depicted in Fig. 4h−k, Supplementary Video 10, the direction of rotation changed to CW. This inversion aligns closely with our experimental observations in Fig. 2a−c, clearly demonstrating the critical dependence of the rotational dynamics on the topological charge. Similar results under current reversal are also shown in Supplementary Fig. S10, Supplementary Videos 11−13, which proves the dynamics are independent of the current direction. Furthermore, the theoretical analysis agrees well with experimental results, revealing the validity and effectiveness of the confinement-potential model adopted in this work.

To systematically examine the role of relaxation time, the pulse width was fixed at 500 ns, and the pulse interval was varied under continuous pulse trains. Representative frames extracted from the recorded videos are shown in Supplementary Fig. S11. When the interval is 5 μs, the rotational motions disappear, whereas the interval up to 10 μs restores pronounced rotational dynamics. The results demonstrate that an adequate relaxation time is essential for enabling the observed rotational motion, providing strong experimental support for our proposed excitation–relaxation mechanism and theoretical framework.

To clarify the experimental and simulation results regarding skyrmion-assembly motion, we provide a comprehensive theoretical derivation and validation based on the Thiele equation in Supplementary Note 2. Collectively, the theoretical analysis underscores three key conclusions: (i) the direction of skyrmion rotational dynamics depends solely on the sign of the topological charge Q, and is independent of current direction; (ii) the relaxation time between current pulses is important for the continuous rotation and the nonlinear boundary confinement potential constitutes the primary driving force underlying these rotational dynamics; and (iii) the equilibrium established by skyrmion-skyrmion interactions leads to the gradual reduction in angular velocity as skyrmion assemblies approach the sample center.

In conclusion, we have experimentally demonstrated novel current-induced rotational dynamics of skyrmion and antiskyrmion assemblies confined within samples with different geometries. The in-situ L-TEM results have revealed that the sense of rotation depends solely on the intrinsic topological charge. Notably, this first experimental observation of antiskyrmion assembly dynamics has directly confirmed their contrasting topological nature relative to skyrmions. Further analyses have identified clear evidence for a decrease in the angular velocity of spin textures toward the sample center, underscoring the crucial effect of confinement potentials caused by the sample boundary. Complementary micromagnetic simulations and theoretical modeling have emphasized the decisive roles of the nonlinear boundary potential and the gyrotropic force in driving these unique rotational dynamics of topological spin textures as a transient phenomenon. These results offer significant advances in exploring skyrmion dynamics.

Methods

Sample preparation

The single-crystalline bulk sample of (Fe0.63Ni0.3Pd0.07)3P (FNPP) was synthesized using the self-flux method, employing iron (Fe), nickel (Ni), palladium (Pd), and red phosphorus (P) sealed in an evacuated quartz tube. For the in-situ L-TEM observations, sample plates were fabricated from FNPP single crystals utilizing a FIB technique. The (001) plane was selected for the sample, with the edges aligned along the <110> direction to align with the spiral wave vector q, optimizing the observation of skyrmion dynamics. The dimensions of the rectangular samples were approximately 6 µm in length, 4 µm in width, and 160 nm in thickness. Additionally, a round-shaped sample plate was fabricated, featuring two tungsten electrode pads and a central FNPP circle encased by a tungsten ring. For the preparation of the round-shaped sample, part of the ring region was first removed by FIB milling, followed by tungsten deposition into the milled area. The remaining FNPP in the ring region was then removed, leaving only the tungsten in that area. The diameter of the inner FNPP circle was set to 6 µm, with a thickness of 160 nm.

L-TEM observations

The in-situ observations of skyrmions under pulsed current were conducted using JEM−2100 and JEM−2800 microscopes (JEOL, Japan), equipped with a double-tilt current-capable holder (Fusion Select, Protochips). In L-TEM, since the objective lens is essentially a strong electromagnetic lens composed of current-carrying coils, the application of vertical magnetic fields was controlled by adjusting the objective-lens current. For capturing L-TEM images with the magnetic field oriented along the +z direction, the JEM−2800 microscope was utilized. Conversely, images with the magnetic field applied along the −z direction were captured using the JEM−2100 microscope. Pulsed voltages were generated using an arbitrary function generator (AFG31252, Tektronix, USA), with each pulse manually triggered at intervals exceeding 0.1 s. To quantitatively analyze the in-plane magnetic induction fields, the under- and over-focused L-TEM images were processed using the QPt software package (HREM Co., Japan), based on the transport-of-intensity equation.

Micromagnetic simulation

For the micromagnetic simulations, the GPU-accelerated software MuMax345–47 was used to model the dynamic processes of skyrmions under pulsed current in a system featuring asymmetric DMI. The simulations were governed by solving the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equation, which was augmented to include both adiabatic and non-adiabatic spin-transfer torques. In the FNPP, Ni, Pd, and P do not contribute appreciably to the magnetic moment at room temperature30,31. Instead, the magnetism is dominated by the Fe atoms. Therefore, a single-sublattice LLG framework was used in the micromagnetic simulations to describe the system. The density of the pulse current was set to 7 × 1010 A/m2 and injected along the +x or −x direction, closely aligning with experimental values. COMSOL simulation

The current distribution and temperature variation in the samples under pulsed current were simulated using COMSOL Multiphysics. For these simulations, the “Joule Heating” module was employed, incorporating the “Electric Currents” and “Heat Transfer in Solids” interfaces, along with a Multiphysics node for “Electromagnetic Heating”. A time-dependent Solver was selected to meticulously track the temperature evolution throughout the pulsed current application. The geometrical dimensions of the simulated structures were meticulously matched to those of the actual experimental setup. Specifically, the two tungsten electrode pads were modeled with dimensions of 2 µm × 6 µm × 0.16 µm. The diameter of the inner FNPP circle (colored in Brown) was set to 4 µm, while the surrounding tungsten ring (colored in orange) was assigned a width of 1 µm. The sample thickness was consistently modeled at 160 nm. Given that FNPP is primarily iron-based, the material parameters employed in the COMSOL simulations were chiefly derived from those of iron.
