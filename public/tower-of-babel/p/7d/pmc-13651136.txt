Symptom-level analysis of problematic social media use and its associations with health, well-being, and lifestyle in Lithuanian adolescents: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Vladas Golambiauskas, Tomas Vaičiūnas, Justė Lukoševičiūtė-Barauskienė, Kastytis Šmigelskas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProblematic social media use among adolescents is increasingly recognized as a multidimensional phenomenon with distinct symptom profiles. While aggregate scoring approaches are common, examining individual symptoms may offer a more efficient entry point for identifying adolescents at higher risk. This study examined the prevalence of individual problematic social media use symptoms and their associations with health, mental well-being, and lifestyle indicators among Lithuanian adolescents.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted using data from the 2022 Health Behaviour in School-aged Children survey in Lithuania (n = 6628; aged 11–17 years). Problematic social media use was assessed using the nine-item Social Media Disorder scale with binary response options. Fifteen outcomes were examined across three domains: health (subjective health, somatic complaints), psychological well-being (life satisfaction, psychological complaints, psychological well-being, anxiety, loneliness, happiness), and lifestyle (daily physical activity, vigorous physical activity, cigarette smoking, e-cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep duration, sleep quality). Logistic regression analyses were performed for each symptom-outcome pair, adjusting for gender, age, and socioeconomic status.ResultsEscape-motivated use was the most prevalent symptom (51.2%), followed by loss of control (39.2%) and preoccupation (27.2%). Escape-motivated use was also the strongest predictor of adverse outcomes, showing strong evidence for associations across 14 out of 15 health and lifestyle indicators. Girls endorsed seven of nine symptoms at higher rates than boys, with the largest disparities observed for escape-motivated use and loss of control. An inverse age pattern was observed, with younger adolescents reporting higher symptom prevalence than older ones.ConclusionsSymptom-level analysis reveals meaningful variation in the predictive relevance of individual problematic social media use criteria, with escape-motivated use emerging as the symptom most consistently associated with adverse outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28593-6.

Background

The past two decades have witnessed an unprecedented integration of social media into the daily lives of adolescents worldwide. With over 87% of teenagers globally reporting regular social media use [1] and many engaging with these platforms almost constantly throughout the day, digital communication has fundamentally transformed how young people interact, communicate, and experience their social world [1]. The widespread adoption of smartphones has enabled continuous access to these digital environments, with adolescents spending an average of 3.5 to 6 h daily on social media platforms [2]. This pervasive digital engagement occurs during a critical developmental period characterized by rapid neurobiological, psychological, and social changes, making adolescents particularly vulnerable to both the beneficial and detrimental effects of technology use [3].

The COVID-19 pandemic markedly accelerated adolescent social media use, as lockdowns and remote learning drove a significant increase in screen time [4, 5]. While digital platforms helped adolescents maintain social connections during this period, increased engagement was also associated with higher rates of anxiety and depression, particularly among those using social media more than three hours daily [6–8]. Importantly, elevated psychological distress levels have persisted beyond the acute phase of the pandemic [9], and increased screen time has been linked to broader lifestyle disruptions including poorer sleep, reduced physical activity, and changes in dietary patterns [10].

On the one hand, social media platforms facilitate expanded social networks, enable maintenance of friendships across geographic distances, and provide venues for identity exploration and community belonging [11]. Adolescents utilize these platforms to strengthen existing relationships, coordinate social activities, and access peer support during challenging times [12]. On the other hand, concerns have emerged regarding potentially problematic social media use, with some adolescents developing patterns of excessive and compulsive use which interferes with daily functioning [13] and includes addiction-like symptoms. Understanding when normative social media use transitions into problematic, addiction-like engagement is thus central to assessing its impact on adolescent health and development.

Longitudinal studies indicate increasing rates of problematic and addiction‑type social media involvement among adolescents, with some cohorts showing increases during and after the COVID‑19 pandemic. These trends, observed across multiple countries and large samples, suggest that elevated engagement and impairment related to social platforms represent a growing public health concern for youth mental health and functioning [14–18]. It’s also important to note significant gender-specific differences. Recent global syntheses (2020–2025) indicate a significant gender gap, with problematic use affecting 13% of girls compared to 9% of boys [19, 20]. Gender also moderates the psychological impact of social media, with girls exhibiting a higher susceptibility to distress from frequent use. Persistent digital engagement among girls is significantly associated with diminished life satisfaction, reduced happiness, and elevated anxiety. While boys also experience adverse mental health outcomes, the magnitude and manifestation of these effects differ; notably, cyberbullying appears to mediate the relationship between social media exposure and psychological distress more substantially in girls [21].

Researching problematic social-media use in adolescents is especially important because adolescence involves rapid neurobiological and social development during which self-regulation and social identity are forming, making teens potentially more vulnerable to the effects of intensive or compulsive digital behaviour [22]. Empirical work also links high or problematic digital media use with poorer psychological well-being, disrupted sleep, and lower academic performance – outcomes that are particularly consequential during developmental windows [21, 23–26].

Beyond mental health symptoms, the social media use has been associated with increased somatic complaints, behavioural problems and physical health symptoms among adolescents. Research documents positive correlations between social media engagement and various physical complaints, including headaches, neck and back pain, eye strain, and fatigue [27] as well as eating disorders [28]. Studies utilizing objective biomarkers have identified associations between social media use and elevated markers of systemic inflammation, suggesting potential physiological pathways linking digital engagement to physical health [29].

Although the interest in adolescent social media harms has grown, most empirical work examines PSMU as a total score or diagnostic category, with relatively few studies investigating individual symptoms and their differential associations with health outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and predictive potential of separate PSMU symptoms in relation to health, mental well-being, and lifestyle outcomes of adolescents. We hypothesized that PSMU symptoms would differ substantially in their predictive strength.

Methods

Participants and procedure

The study used data from the Lithuanian Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) survey conducted in April–June 2022. Ethical approval was obtained from the Lithuanian University of Health Sciences Bioethics Centre (2022-03-17, Nr. BEC-M-05), the approvals were obtained from school administrations. To achieve a geographically representative sample, all Lithuanian general education and vocational schools were divided into 15 regions (5 largest cities and 10 counties), from which 142 schools were randomly selected. Of these, 124 agreed to participate (school response rate 87.3%). The total eligible sample across participating schools was 8541 students. However, some were absent on the day of data collection, declined to participate, or had parental refusal, resulting in a final sample of 6,628 adolescents (student response rate 77.6%). The survey was administered to students in grades 5, 7, 9, and 11, corresponding to the predominant ages of 11, 13, 15, and 17 years [30]. Paper-based anonymous questionnaires were used. Passive parental consent was obtained via school administrations, and verbal consent was obtained from all adolescent participants prior to data collection.

The study employed a cross-sectional design. The sample consisted of 6628 adolescents aged 11, 13, 15, and 17 years. Rates of missing gender data were low across age groups (< 1.5%). Overall, gender distribution was approximately balanced across all age groups (Table 1).

Measures

In total, 9 PSMU symptom predictors and 15 outcome variables (spanning subjective health, psychological well-being, and lifestyle domains) were used in this analysis, with age, gender, and socioeconomic status included as covariates. All outcome and predictor variables were dichotomized.

Socioeconomic status was assessed using the Family Affluence Scale, version III (FAS-III), a six-item measure of family material affluence developed and validated for the HBSC study [31, 32]. The FAS-III items are: (1) number of family cars (0/1/2 or more), (2) whether the adolescent has their own unshared bedroom (no/yes), (3) number of computers/tablets at home (0/1/2/more than 2), (4) number of bathrooms (0/1/2/3 or more), (5) whether the family has a dishwasher (no/yes), and (6) number of family holidays abroad in the past 12 months (0/1/2/3 or more). Item responses were summed to produce a total score ranging from 0 to 13, with higher scores indicating greater family affluence. Following the standard HBSC categorisation procedure [30], country-specific FAS scores were then used to classify adolescents into three affluence groups: low (lowest 20%), medium (middle 60%), and high (highest 20%).

PSMU symptoms

Problematic social media use was assessed using the nine-item Social Media Disorder (SMD) scale [13], with dichotomous (yes/no) response options. The scale covers nine symptom criteria: preoccupation, tolerance, withdrawal, loss of control, neglect of activities, interpersonal conflict, deception, escape-motivated use, and family conflict. Internal consistency in the current sample was α = 0.77.

Physical health

Subjective health was assessed with a single item asking adolescents to rate their health on a five-point scale from “excellent” to “poor”. Corresponding to the standard HBSC procedure, responses were dichotomised into fair or poor versus good, very good, or excellent [30]. Somatic complaints were assessed using the HBSC Symptom Checklist [30], which asks how often adolescents experienced four physical symptoms (headache, stomachache, backache, dizziness) in the past six months on a five-point scale ranging from 1 (“rarely/never”) to 5 (“almost every day”). Responses were dichotomised with adverse value defined as at least one symptom occurring at least once a week, with accordance to standard HBSC protocol [30].

Mental well-being

Life satisfaction was assessed using the Cantril Ladder [33], a single item on which adolescents rated their current life on a scale from 0 (“worst possible life”) to 10 (“best possible life”). Scores of 5 or below were classified as low life satisfaction, in line with the dichotomisation used in HBSC research [34]. Psychological complaints were assessed using the same HBSC Symptom Checklist described above, with at least one psychological symptom (feeling low, irritability, nervousness, sleeping difficulties) occurring more than once a week classified as the adverse outcome, in accordance to HBSC standard procedure [30]. Well-being was assessed using the WHO-5 Well-Being Index [35], a five-item scale with responses on a six-point scale from 0 (“none of the time”) to 5 (“all of the time”). Scores were averaged to produce a summary score ranging from 0 to 5, with scores below 2.5 (equivalent to < 50 on the 0–100 transformation) classified as low well-being, corresponding to the established threshold for screening for poor mental wellbeing in adolescent and adult populations [35]. To assess sensitivity to cutoff selection, supplementary logistic regression analyses were also conducted using a stricter, ≤ 1.8 threshold, corresponding to the adolescent validated optimal threshold for depression screening [36]. Anxiety was assessed using the Generalised Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7) [37], consisting of 7 items with responses on a 4-point scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”). Following established clinical cutoffs [37], GAD-7 scores can be classified into four severity categories: none/minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), and severe (15–21). For the primary analysis, scores of ≥ 11 were classified as moderate-to-severe anxiety, consistent with the cutoff validated for adolescent populations [38]. To assess sensitivity to cutoff selection, supplementary logistic regression analyses were also conducted using the three alternative dichotomous thresholds (≥ 5, ≥ 10, ≥15), corresponding to mild, moderate, and severe anxiety cutoffs. Loneliness was assessed with a single item asking how often adolescents felt lonely during the last year, rated on a five-point scale from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”). Responses of “quite often” or “always” were classified as lonely, consistent with previous HBSC studies. Happiness was assessed with a single item asking how happy adolescents felt, rated on a five-point scale ranging from 1 (“very happy”) to 5 (“not at all happy”). Responses of “not very happy” or “not at all happy” were classified as unhappy, consistent with HBSC standard categorization [30].

Lifestyle outcomes

Daily physical activity was assessed by asking how many days in the past week adolescents had been physically active for at least 60 min; fewer than 7 days was classified as insufficient, in accordance with WHO guidelines [39]. Vigorous physical activity was assessed by asking how many times per week adolescents typically engaged in vigorous leisure-time physical activity until out of breath or sweating; fewer than 4 times per week was classified as insufficient [40]. Smoking and e-smoking in the past 30 days were each assessed with separate items asking how many days in the past 30 days the adolescent had smoked conventional or electronic cigarettes, rated on a seven-point scale from 1 (“never smoked”) to 7 (“30 or more days”). Smoking on at least one day was classified as the adverse outcome, consistent with standard HBSC categorization [41]. Alcohol consumption in the past 30 days was assessed with a similar item and dichotomised in the same way [41]. Sleep duration was assessed by asking adolescents their usual weekday bedtime and wake time; sleep duration was calculated as the difference between the two. In accordance with National Sleep Foundation recommendations [42], fewer than 8 h was classified as insufficient for adolescents aged 13–17, and fewer than 9 h for those aged 11. Sleep quality was assessed using the short version of the ASWS (ASWS-10) [43], consisting of 10 items regarding sleep with responses on a 6-point scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 6 (“always”). Total ASWS-10 sum scores were dichotomized at 35, the midpoint of the possible range (10–60). This conceptual midpoint corresponds to the response boundary between ‘sometimes’ (response option 3) and ‘quite often’ (response option 4) averaged across the 10 items, distinguishing adolescents whose responses leaned toward less frequent sleep problems (> 35) from those whose responses leaned toward more frequent sleep problems (≤ 35). Because no validated clinical or population-specific dichotomization cutoff has been established for the ASWS or ASWS-10 [43], a sensitivity analysis was conducted using the continuous ASWS-10 sum score as the outcome.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and verified in MS Excel 2016 and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 30.0 [44]. The statistical significance threshold was set to p < 0.05. Missing data were handled by pairwise deletion. Sample sizes for individual logistic models ranged from 5,615 to 5,951, corresponding to 9.5%–15.3% missingness across outcomes. Missingness in predictors and covariates was minimal (< 2% for gender, age and FAS; <5% for SMD symptoms).

To examine co-occurrence among PSMU symptoms, pairwise associations were calculated using phi coefficients and binary logistic regression for all 36 symptom pairs. Binary logistic regression analyses were then conducted separately for each of the 15 adverse health outcomes, with all nine PSMU symptoms, age, gender, and socioeconomic status entered simultaneously as covariates.

Because the study protocol prioritised participant anonymity, no school-level identifiers were retained in the analytic dataset to ensure that responses could not be linked back to individual schools, classrooms, and participants. Therefore, design weights and cluster-robust standard errors at the school level could not be applied. Because of that, all logistic regression models assumed independent observations.

Given the large number of statistical tests conducted in the main symptom-by-outcome analyses (9 symptoms×15 outcomes = 135 comparisons), p-values from these models were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, controlling the expected proportion of false discoveries at 5% [45]. All 135 raw p-values were ranked in ascending order from smallest (i = 1) to largest (i = 135), and each was compared with its critical value (i/135 × 0.05). The largest raw p-value that did not exceed its critical value defined the FDR threshold for this set of tests at p = 0.022. Accordingly, associations with raw p-values ≤ 0.022 are reported as statistically significant after FDR correction, and associations with raw p-values > 0.022 are reported as non-significant after correction. The FDR-adjusted threshold of p ≤ 0.022 is applied consistently across the primary 135-test logistic regression analyses and is the criterion used to anchor evidence strength in Figs. 1 and 2 and in the primary results text. Descriptive analyses (Tables 3 and 4), sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Tables S1, S2), and LASSO results (Supplementary Tables S4, S5) report raw p-values or alternative metrics without FDR adjustment, as detailed in the corresponding table footnotes.

For each symptom, the number of statistically significant associations across the 15 models was calculated, and median odds ratios were computed across all models and across significant models only to summarise overall predictive strength for each symptom.

To account for multicollinearity among the nine SMD symptoms, and to assess the stability of the models, two additional analyses were conducted. First, multicollinearity diagnostics (variance inflation factors and tolerance values) were calculated for all predictors using linear regression with the same predictor set, which provides valid collinearity diagnostics for logistic models including those with dichotomous predictors [46]. Second, LASSO-penalised logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation was conducted for each of the 15 outcomes using the glmnet package in R [47]. LASSO performs variable selection by shrinking weak coefficients to zero, which yields stable selection even when predictors are correlated [48]. Coefficients were examined both at the optimal penalty (λ_min) and the penalty within one standard error (λ_1se) to assess whether the symptom ranking was robust to the strength of penalization.

Results

The prevalence of individual problematic social media use (PSMU) symptoms in the total sample ranged from 14.6% to 51.2% (Table 3). The most frequently reported symptoms were escape-motivated use (51.2%), followed by loss of control (39.2%) and preoccupation (27.2%). The least prevalent symptoms were deception (14.6%) and interpersonal conflict (17.2%). Girls reported higher prevalence than boys for seven of the nine symptoms, with odds ratios ranging from 1.39 to 2.68 (p < 0.05). The largest gender difference was observed for escape-motivated use (OR = 2.68, p < 0.001) and loss of control (OR = 2.40, p < 0.001). No/weak evidence was observed for gender differences for symptoms of interpersonal conflict and deception. Symptom prevalence in younger groups was higher than in older: for most symptoms, 11-year-olds reported the highest prevalence and 17-year-olds the lowest. For example, prevalence of preoccupation declined from 34.2% among 11-year-olds to 18.2% among 17-year-olds (OR = 0.73, p < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed for all other symptoms (OR = 0.72–0.83), excluding escape-motivated use, which was the only symptom showing an increase in prevalence in older age groups, when compared to 11-year-olds (OR = 1.10, p < 0.001). Overall, girls and younger adolescents reported higher levels of problematic social media use symptoms (Table 3).

To examine the extent to which PSMU symptoms co-occur, pairwise association analyses using phi coefficients and binary logistic regressions (in terms of odds ratios) were conducted for all nine symptoms.

All symptom pairs were positively associated (all p < 0.001), indicating systematic clustering of PSMU symptoms within individuals (Table 4). Phi coefficients ranged from 0.17 to 0.48 while ORs ranged from 2.56 to 10.71. The strongest association was observed between preoccupation and tolerance (φ = 0.48; OR = 10.71), suggesting substantial overlap between these symptoms. Similarly strong associations were found between tolerance and withdrawal (φ = 0.46; OR = 10.10), and between preoccupation and withdrawal (φ = 0.45; OR = 9.69).

Several other symptom pairs demonstrated moderate associations, including interpersonal conflict and family conflict (φ = 0.33; OR = 5.89), and deception and family conflict (φ = 0.38; OR = 8.18). Most remaining coefficients fell within the 0.21–0.33 range, with corresponding ORs falling between 2.56 and 5.82, indicating consistent clustering across symptoms. Notably, even the smallest observed odds ratios indicated more than a twofold increase in the likelihood of reporting a second symptom when the first was present.

To assess how each of the symptoms associates with health and lifestyle, 15 variables were chosen as outcomes. Each of them was dichotomised – the outcome variables were coded to indicate the presence (1) versus absence (0) of the adverse condition (Table 2).

Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted separately for each of the 15 outcomes. All models included the nine PSMU symptoms, age, gender, and socioeconomic status (12 predictors entered simultaneously). For each symptom, the number of associations showing strong evidence after FDR correction (p ≤ 0.022) across models was calculated. Regression coefficients were then converted to ORs, and the median ORs across the 15 models were computed to provide a summary estimate of overall predictive strength (Fig. 1). In the analysis escape-motivated use emerged as the most prevalent predictor of adverse outcomes with strong evidence of association across 14 of 15 outcomes after FDR correction, followed by withdrawal with strong evidence across 10 outcomes. Median OR for escape-motivated use was 1.75, almost 0.5 points larger than the second best predictor – family conflict (median OR = 1.29).

Because dichotomization of continuous outcomes can affect prevalence estimates and effect-size magnitudes, and because the choice of cutoff for some outcomes (WHO-5, GAD-7, ASWS-10) is subject to ongoing methodological debate or, in the case of ASWS-10, lacks a validated clinical threshold, sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative dichotomous cutoffs for WHO-5 and GAD-7 and the continuous score in linear regression for ASWS-10.

For WHO-5 and GAD-7, alternative cutoffs did not meaningfully impact the symptom-level findings: escape-motivated use symptom remained the strongest predictor across all five outcome cutoffs and consistently exceeded the next-strongest symptom by a factor of 1.5–2 (Table S1).

For ASWS-10, linear regression analysis on the continuous sum score (Table S2) yielded the same pattern: escape-motivated use was the strongest predictor of poorer sleep quality (standardized β=−0.22, p < 0.001), with an effect almost 3 times the magnitude of the next-strongest symptom (deception, β=−0.08, p < 0.001).

Together, these sensitivity analyses indicate that the symptom-level findings are robust to dichotomization choices and do not reflect an artifact of any particular cutoff.

To account for multicollinearity among the nine SMD symptoms multicollinearity diagnostics (variance inflation factors and tolerance values) were calculated for all predictors using linear regression and LASSO-penalised logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation was conducted for each of the 15 outcomes at the optimal (λ_min) and one standard error (λ_1se) penalty.

Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated no concerning collinearity among the predictors (Table S3). Variance inflation factors for the nine SMD symptoms ranged from 1.22 to 1.56 (tolerance values 0.64–0.82), all well below conventional thresholds (VIF < 10) [49].

Under LASSO-penalised logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation, escape-motivated use emerged as the top-ranked SMD symptom in 11 of 15 outcomes at λ_min (Table S4) and 12 of 15 at λ_1se (Supplementary Table S5). It dominated in mental health, wellbeing, and sleep outcomes, with LASSO-shrunk ORs exceeding the next-strongest SMD symptom by a factor of approximately 1.5–2. In lifestyle outcomes (substance use and physical activity), no single SMD symptom dominated, and effect sizes were considerably smaller across all symptoms, although escape-motivated use nevertheless remained among the top-ranked predictors.

Figure 2 displays the predictive power of escape-motivated use across the 15 outcomes. The strongest evidence for associations was observed with psychological well-being indicators, particularly psychological complaints (OR = 2.71), low well-being (OR = 2.57), and loneliness (OR = 2.81) as well as low sleep quality (OR = 2.29). Although strong evidence was also observed for associations with lifestyle outcomes, they were generally weaker in magnitude, with smoking, e-smoking, and alcohol consumption showing the smallest effects.

To sum up the results, individual PSMU symptoms varied considerably in prevalence, with escape-motivated use being the most common (51.2%) and deception the least (14.6%). Girls and younger adolescents reported higher symptom prevalence across most indicators. Escape-motivated use emerged as the strongest and most consistent predictor as it was associated with 14 out of 15 possible adverse outcomes in logistic regression models and remained stable when performing regularized LASSO regression. Escape-motivated use showed the strongest evidence for associations with mental well-being and sleep quality outcomes while associations with lifestyle outcomes were generally weaker.

Discussion

Problematic social media use among adolescents has emerged as a salient public health and developmental concern, as social media are now central to young people’s social lives, identity processes, and emotion regulation strategies. Most previous research addresses the social media use from overall perspective rather than looking to specific symptoms. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the prevalence and predictive potential of individual PSMU symptoms for health, mental well-being, and lifestyle outcomes among Lithuanian adolescents.

The present study found notable variation in the prevalence of individual PSMU symptoms among Lithuanian school-aged children, with rates ranging from 15% to 51%. Cross-national validation studies using the same nine-item SMD scale have confirmed its structural validity and strong psychometric properties across 44 countries [50], reinforcing the reliability of the present findings within an international context.

Escape-motivated use of social media emerged as both the most prevalent symptom (51%) and the dominant predictor of adverse outcomes across 14 out of 15 measured health and lifestyle indicators, with only withdrawal approaching similar predictive relevance. This is consistent with a substantial body of literature identifying emotion regulation difficulties as a key driver of problematic social media engagement – adolescents with poorer regulation skills are more likely to turn to social media as a form of emotional avoidance or relief [51–54]. Mood regulation has been further identified as a key trigger for compulsive social media use, with escape from negative affect functioning as both a primary motivation and a reinforced behavioural pattern [55]. Meanwhile, PSMU itself operates as a dimension-specific mediating factor between emotion dysregulation and psychopathology – distinct from broader transdiagnostic factors such as boredom [56]. In the present study, escape-motivated use showed the strongest associations with mental well-being and sleep quality, though associations with lifestyle outcomes were comparatively weaker.

Clear gender differences were also observed. Girls reported higher prevalence rates for seven of the nine PSMU symptoms, with strong evidence for association and odds ratios ranging from 1.39 to 2.68. The largest disparities were found for escape-motivated use and loss of control symptoms, indicating that girls were more than twice as likely as boys to have these features of problematic use. These findings align with evidence from large-scale representative studies showing stronger associations between moderate-to-heavy digital media use and reduced psychological well-being among girls compared to boys [57]. Research on gender differences in social media motivations provides further explanatory context. Girls tend to use social media primarily for social connection, emotional support, and compensation for offline relational difficulties, whereas boys more often report entertainment-driven and competitive motivations [58, 59]. This gender-differentiated motivation structure renders girls’ social media use more vulnerable to problematic use, as social media becomes possibly integrated into their primary emotion regulation frame.

A notable finding of the present study was the inverted developmental trajectory of PSMU symptom prevalence, with 11-year-olds reporting the highest rates and 17-year-olds the lowest. This pattern diverges from the typical developmental course observed in many behavioural health conditions, which often increases in prevalence across adolescence. It also contrasts with broader research on problematic internet use (PIU), where prevalence generally rises with age: for example, a large-scale study found that PIU prevalence increased from 2.7% in early adolescence to 6.0% in late adolescence [60], indicating a progressive rise over time. This pattern may reflect the fact that younger adolescents, still developing self-regulatory skills, are more susceptible to the engaging features of social media platforms – such as infinite scroll, notifications, and algorithm-driven content – that are specifically designed to maximize time spent online [61]. As adolescents mature, they may gradually develop greater capacity to resist these mechanisms. On the other hand, given that the study was cross-sectional, the younger participants may represent more affected digital natives rather than different developmental stage.

The present findings carry meaningful implications for research and practice concerning adolescents in school, counselling, and general health settings. Given that escape-motivated social media use emerged as the symptom most consistently and strongly associated with adverse health and wellbeing outcomes, it merits particular attention when investigating PSMU in adolescents. When researching screening or intervention efforts of PSMU, it would be worth to consider targeting this particular symptom in order to assess whether the discovered associations hold true in different settings; and whether focusing on reducing emotional avoidance while promoting healthy coping strategies may serve as a valid strategy to diminish the risk of developing broader PSMU or adverse outcomes. Even though the present cross-sectional design could not address these questions, it could be a target for future longitudinal studies. Additionally, the results indicated that younger adolescents and girls more frequently endorsed PSMU symptoms, suggesting that research efforts may benefit from gender- and age-sensitive approaches.

The present study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow for causal conclusions about the relationships between PSMU symptoms and the measured outcomes, and longitudinal studies would be needed to establish directionality and track symptom development over time.

Second, all data were collected through self-report, which may introduce biases such as inaccurate recall, particularly among younger adolescents who may have limited awareness of their own behavioural patterns and emotional states.

Third, the sample was limited to Lithuanian school-aged children, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to adolescents in other cultural or socioeconomic contexts.

Fourth, the present study did not account for family dynamics and peer network factors, which are recognized as important contextual influences on adolescent PSMU and its health consequences [62, 63]. Furthermore, several important confounding factors were not considered in this paper, such as school-related stress, migration background, or other digital behaviours (such as screen time or gaming). Therefore, it is possible that the observed associations between SMD symptoms and adverse health outcomes are partially confounded by broader psychosocial or behavioural factors. Future research should integrate these variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the associations investigated in this paper.

Fifth, beyond unmeasured confounding, the cross-sectional design also precludes inference about directionality: adolescents experiencing emotional distress, sleep difficulties, or social isolation may turn to social media as a coping strategy, thereby developing PSMU symptoms – particularly escape-motivated use, which, by definition, reflects a coping response to negative emotional states.

The final limitation concerns the clustered HBSC sampling design. Adolescents were nested within schools, but the analytic dataset did not include school identifiers, as these were omitted to protect participant anonymity and ensure that individual responses could not be linked back to specific schools, classrooms, or peer groups, given the sensitive questions in the questionnaire. Design weights and cluster-robust standard errors could therefore not be applied. This may have resulted in narrower confidence intervals than a design-corrected analysis would yield, although the large sample size and substantial effect sizes for escape-motivated use make a meaningful change in the symptom ranking unlikely.

Taken together, the present study findings underscore the value of symptom-level analysis over aggregate scoring, revealing that not all PSMU symptoms carry equal weight in associating PSMU with adverse health and wellbeing outcomes. Escape-motivated use in particular stands out for its prevalence and its predictive breadth across multiple outcomes. Whether it would also represent an effective intervention or screening target remains to be determined by longitudinal and experimental research. Future research should also examine whether symptom-specific patterns observed in Lithuanian adolescents replicate across different cultural and socioeconomic contexts, and whether brief symptom-based tools can match the predictive utility of full-scale measures.

Conclusions

The findings in this study demonstrate that not all symptoms carry equal weight: escape-motivated use emerged as both the most prevalent symptom and the strongest predictor of adverse health and lifestyle outcomes, showing strong evidence for associations across 14 out of 15 measured indicators. Withdrawal followed as the next most predictively relevant symptoms, while deception and interpersonal conflict showed comparatively limited associations.

These results have several important implications. First, they underscore the value of symptom-level analysis in capturing meaningful variation that aggregate approaches may miss. Second, the prominence of escape-motivated use aligns with a growing body of evidence implicating emotion dysregulation – particularly the use of social media as a coping mechanism for negative emotions – as a central driver of problematic engagement. However, the present cross-sectional study design cannot answer whether targeting escape-motivated use for screening or intervention purposes is a valid and fruitful strategy – that is a question for future longitudinal and experimental research. Third, the pronounced gender and age differences, with girls and younger adolescents endorsing most symptoms at higher rates, suggest the need for gender- and age-sensitive approaches in both research and practice.

Taken together, the present study results suggest that examining individual PSMU symptoms alongside aggregate scores may reveal variation in predictive relevance that total scores alone do not capture. The consistent prominence of escape-motivated use across outcomes raises the merit for additional attention given to the symptom when researching PSMU.
