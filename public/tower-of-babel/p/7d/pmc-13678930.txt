One-pot LAMP-CRISPR/Cas12b coupled with nanoparticle-based biosensor for point-of-care test of Neisseria gonorrhoeae
Authors: Ling Xie, Yan Tan, Yanyan Qin, Cencen Jia, Ya Li, Yanan Liu, Xu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGonorrhea, caused by the human obligate pathogen Neisseria gonorrhoeae, is the second most common bacterial sexually transmitted infection worldwide. In the absence of an effective gonococcal vaccine, convenient and accurate diagnosis of N. gonorrhoeae infection is essential for optimal patient management and for interrupting ongoing transmission.MethodsA custom-designed gold nanoparticle-based lateral flow biosensor (AuNP-LFB) was employed. The unique LAMP primers and a specific single-guide RNA (sgRNA) were designed to target the orf1 gene of N. gonorrhoeae. The key diagnostic conditions, including detection temperature and time, were optimized. The sensitivity and specificity of the assay were validated, and its clinical feasibility was validated using seventy-eight genital secretion samples from patients suspected of N. gonorrhoeae infection.ResultsThe custom-designed AuNP-LFB was successfully fabricated. The Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay achieved a limit of detection (LoD) as low as 7 copies per test and exhibits no cross-reactivity with other tested microbial species. The entire detection workflow, including nucleic acid extraction (10 min), LAMP-CRISPR/Cas12b detection (56 °C, 50 min), and AuNP-LFB visual readout (< 2 min), can be completed within 65 min without requiring specialized instrumentation. Compared with a reference N. gonorrhoeae-qPCR method, our assay achieved 100% sensitivity (95% CI: 93.94–100.00%) and 100% specificity (95% CI: 82.35–100.00%).ConclusionsCollectively, these preliminary results indicate that the Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay holds promise as a practical point-of-care (POC) diagnostic tool, enabling accurate and convenient detection of N. gonorrhoeae, particularly in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05404-x.

Introduction

Gonorrhea, a sexually transmitted disease caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae, is a major global public health challenge, with an estimated annual incidence of 87 million cases worldwide [1–3]. Even worse, the highest prevalence rates of gonorrhea are observed in low-income countries and regions according to the World Health Organization (WHO) investigation [2, 4]. N. gonorrhoeae infection is often asymptomatic, leading to underdiagnosis and inadequate treatment in a substantial proportion of cases [5, 6]. If left untreated, persistent infection can result in serious complications. In men, ascending infection can cause epididymitis, orchitis, prostatitis, even result in infertility [7, 8]. Among women, infection can cause salpingitis and pelvic inflammatory disease, tubal infection can give rise to ectopic pregnancy and infertility [8]. Maternal infection during pregnancy is strongly associated with a range of adverse obstetric outcomes, encompassing spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, preterm birth, and low birth weight [8]. In neonates, N. gonorrhoeae infection represents a significant cause of ophthalmia neonatorum and purulent conjunctivitis that can lead to blindness [8]. To date, an effective vaccine against gonorrhea remains elusive, posing a significant challenge to global public health [9, 10]. In addition, N. gonorrhoeae infection acts as a significant co-factor for increasing susceptibility to human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) transmission [11]. Aligned with the WHO’s “Global Health Sector Strategy on Sexually Transmitted Infections,” which targets a 90% reduction in gonorrhea incidence by 2030 [2], the development of convenient and visual point-of-care (POC) diagnostic platforms for the ultrasensitive and specific detection of N. gonorrhoeae is urgently needed.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based technologies remain the gold standard for nucleic acid detection, yet they are often unsuitable for deployment in remote areas owing to their demands for sophisticated instrumentation and skilled technicians [12, 13]. Prokaryote-derived clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat and CRISPR-associated protein (CRISPR/Cas) systems hold promises for next-generation POC diagnosis of infectious diseases and cancer [14, 15]. A key advantage of these systems is their design and implementation versatility, which is fundamentally enabled by the programmable guide RNA sequence [14]. Leveraging the collateral trans-cleavage property, many Cas effectors have been utilized for nucleic acid detection, such as CRISPR/Cas12a-based DETECTR (DNA Endonuclease-Targeted CRISPR Trans Reporter), CRISPR/Cas13a-based SHERLOCK (Specific High Sensitivity Enzymatic Reporter UnLOCKing), CRISPR/Cas12b-based HOLMESv2 (One-Hour Low-Cost Multipurpose Highly Efficient System Version 2) [16, 17]. Many of these detection strategies commonly employ a two-pot assay, in which a pre-amplification step such as LAMP (loop-mediated isothermal amplification) or RPA (recombinase polymerase amplification) is required prior to CRISPR/Cas detection [18]. This two-step workflow increases the risk of carryover contamination and prolongs the detection time.

Cas12b (C2c1), a thermophilic RNA-directed endonuclease, is mediated by a specific single-guide RNA (sgRNA) to active its collateral single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) trans-cleavage activity after cleaving target DNA [19]. The optimal reaction temperature of CRISPR/Cas12b (approximately 45–65℃) is compatible with that of LAMP amplification, thus enabling the development of streamlined, one-pot, LAMP-CRISPR/Cas12b diagnostic system for POC testing in practical application [20]. Gold nanoparticle-based lateral flow biosensor (AuNP-LFB) is widely employed in POC detection due to their high accuracy, reliability, ease of use, and stability [21, 22]. The mechanism relies on a target-induced aggregation of AuNP, which generates a visual colorimetric change for instrument-free result interpretation [23]. Inspired by the basic mechanisms of one-pot LAMP-CRISPR/Cas12b detection and AuNP-LFB visual interpretation, for the first time, we herein presented a new N. gonorrhoeae diagnostic platform, termed the Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay, which integrates one-step LAMP-CRISPR/Cas12b-based detection with a visual AuNP-LFB platform, enabling ultrasensitive, specific, and naked-eye identification of N. gonorrhoeae strain by targeting the orf1 gene [24]. The feasibility and diagnostic potential of our system were validated using genital secretion samples from patients with suspected N. gonorrhoeae infection.

Materials and methods

Reagents and instruments

AapCas12b (C2c1) endonuclease was purchased from Tolo Biotech (Shanghai, China). BstXT DNA polymerase, MgSO4 and deoxynucleotide (dNTP) mix were obtained from New England Biolabs (Ipswich, USA). Glycine was obtained from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). RNase-free water was obtained from TaKaRa Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). AuNP-LFB was sourced from HuiDeXin Biotechnology (Tianjin, China) per our design specifications. Streptavidin-coated AuNPs (SA-AuNP; crimson red) (size, 40 ± 5 nm; extinction coefficient, 6.0 × 109 M− 1 cm− 1 at 506 nm) were obtained from Bangs Laboratories, Inc. (Indiana, USA). Rabbit anti-fluorescein antibody (anti-FAM) and biotinylated bovine serum albumin (biotin-BSA) were purchased from Abcam. Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Real-time turbidimeter (Loopamp EXIA) was sourced from Eiken Chemical Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). The PCR thermal cycler and ChemiDoc MP imaging system was obtained from Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc. (California, USA). The biosafety cabinet (MSC-Advantage) and benchtop high-speed refrigerated centrifuge were provided by Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Genomic templates and clinical samples preparation

N. gonorrhoeae reference strain (American Tissue Culture Collection, ATCC 49926) was used as a positive control.

Seventy-eight genital secretion samples were collected from patients with suspected N. gonorrhoeae infection at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine between December 2024 and August 2025. The genital secretion samples (500 µL each) were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and then the resulting pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of nuclease-free water, incubated in a heating block at 100 °C for 10 min, and centrifuged briefly. The supernatant-containing genomic DNA was then collected and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis. The concentrations of genomic DNA were measured with a Nano Drop ND-2000 (Thermo, USA) at A260/280 nm. The corresponding genome copy number was calculated from the weight of the N. gonorrhoeae genome, One N. gonorrhoeae genome is 2.45 fg (2.2 × 106 bp×665 Da/bp×1.67 × 10− 24 g/Da) [24].

N. gonorrhoeae LAMP primer and sgRNA design

The LAMP primers for N. gonorrhoeae were designed using the online platform Primer Explorer v5.0 (https://primerexplorer.eiken.co,jp/lampv5/index.html) and Primer Premier 5.0 software, based on the orf1 gene (GenBank accession no. M84113.1), which is conserved and specific for N. gonorrhoeae species [25, 26]. Primer specificity was verified using the NCBI BLAST tool to ensure target selectivity. The sgRNA for the N. gonorrhoeae orf1 was designed according to the Cas12b endonuclease mechanism. The conserved Cas12b scaffold sequence (5′-GUCUAGAGGACAGAAUUUUUCAACGGGUGUGCCAAUGGCCACUUUCCAGGUGGCAAAGCCCGUUGAGCUUCUCAAAUCUGAGAAGUGGCAC-3′) was fused to the N. gonorrhoeae orf1 specific targeting sequence (5′-UCCGAUACCGCGCUCUACUA-3′), yielding the full-length sgRNA sequence. The schematic positions of N. gonorrhoeae LAMP primers and sgRNA are presented in Fig. 1A, with their corresponding nucleotide sequences detailed in Table 1. All oligonucleotides were synthesized and purified by GenScript Biotech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China) to high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade. The principle of LAMP pre-amplification and CRISPR/Cas12b-based detection is shown in Fig. 1B.

AuNP-LFB preparation and visual readout

The schematic of the AuNP-LFB is presented in Fig. 2. Briefly, the AuNP-LFB comprises four main functional components: a sample pad, a conjugate pad, a nitrocellulose (NC) membrane, and an absorbent pad. Crimson-red streptavidin-conjugated gold nanoparticle (SA-AuNP) were pre-immobilized on the conjugate pad. The NC membrane was anchored with two spatially distinct lines: a control line (CL) coated with anti-FAM antibody (4 mg/mL) and a test line (TL) functionalized with biotin-BSA (0.2 mg/mL), positioned 5 mm apart. Simultaneous appearance of both lines (CL and TL) on the biosensor indicates a positive Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot outcome, while only the CL appears for a negative result. Biosensors were stored at room temperature prior to use.

For AuNP-LFB readout, the Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot reaction products (2.0 µL) and running buffer (50 µL; 100 mM PBS, pH 7.4) were simultaneously applied to the sample pad of the AuNP-LFB (Fig. 2-①). The mixture migrated along the strip via capillary action, rehydrating the lyophilized streptavidin-conjugated gold nanoparticles (SA-AuNP) on the conjugate pad (Fig. 2-②). In the event of a positive reaction, the ssDNA probes (10 µM of 5′-FAM-TTTATTTATTTAT-Biotin-3′) were cleaved by Cas12b endonuclease, resulting in the separation of the FAM and biotin moieties. Consequently, the biotin-SA-AuNP complexes were captured by biotin-BSA at the test line (TL) (Fig. 2-③). In a negative outcome, the ssDNA probes remained intact with both FAM and biotin, and the resulting FAM-biotin-SA-AuNP complexes were specifically arrested by anti-FAM at the control line (CL) (Fig. 2-③). Visual interpretation of the Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay results using the AuNP-LFB is illustrated in Fig. 2-④.

Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot reaction condition

The Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay was performed in a 25 µL mixture, containing 2.5 µL of 10× LAMP buffer (Tolo Biotech, Shanghai, China), 1 µL of BstXT DNA polymerase (8U), 0.4 µM of F3 and B3, 1.6 µM of FIP and BIP, 0.8 µM of LF and LB, 3.5 µL of dNTP mix (10 mM), 1.5 µL of MgSO₄ (100 mM), 6 µL of glycine (2 M), 4.0 µl of AapCas12b-gRNA complex, 1µL of reporter (10 µM of 5′-FAM-TTTATTTATTTAT-Biotin-3′ for AuNP-LFB visual readout or 10 µM of 5′-FAM-TTTATTTATTTAT-BHQ1-3′ for real-time fluorescence assay), 1 µL of nucleic acid template, the final volume was adjusted to 25 µL with nuclease-free double-distilled water (ddH2O). The one-pot Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot reactions were carried out at 50/52/54/56//58/60/62℃ for 10/20/30/40/50/60 min.

The AapCas12b-gRNA complex was prepared using 2 µL of AapCas12b (10 µM), 2 µL of gRNA (10 µM), 5 µL of HOLMES buffer (TOLO Biotech. Shanghai, China), the final volume was adjusted to 50 µL with nuclease-free ddH2O. The mixture was preincubated at 37℃ for 10 min and utilized immediately or maintained at 4℃ for no more than 24 h.

Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay sensitivity and specificity

The sensitivity of Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay was evaluated using serially diluted genomic DNA (ATCC 49926) ranging from 1.0 × 10⁴ down to 1.0 copy per reaction under optimized conditions. The results were analyzed in parallel via visual AuNP-LFB, real-time fluorescence, and under blue light. Each experiment was performed in at least three independent replicates at differently days. The limit of detection (LoD) was defined as the lowest DNA concentration at which all three replicates produced positive results.

Analytical specificity was assessed using nucleic acids derived from a range of bacterial, viral, and fungal organisms (Table 2). The templates were extracted with Bacterial-Genomic-DNA or Virus-DNA/RNA Extraction Kits (Xi’an Tianlong Technology Co., Ltd., China) and quantified using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). All template concentrations were validated to be > 1.0 × 10⁴ copies/mL based on A260/280 measurements. Following the optimized protocol, assay outputs were subsequently assessed in parallel through visual AuNP-LFB, real-time fluorescence analysis, and under blue light. Experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates at differently days.

Evaluation of the feasibility of Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay through clinical samples

To validate the clinical feasibility of the optimized Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay, a comparative analysis was performed using 78 genital secretion samples from patients suspected of N. gonorrhoeae infection. These samples were tested in parallel with our developed assay and a reference quantitative PCR (qPCR) method. The qPCR detection was conducted using a commercial real-time TaqMan PCR kit (DaAn Gene Co., Ltd., China; Cat. #DA-D053; Approved by the National Medical Products Administration) on an Applied Biosystems™ 7500 Real-Time PCR System, with an N. gonorrhoeae concentration > 500 copies/mL defined as a positive outcome. The Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay was performed following the established protocol. Finally, the results from both methods were statistically compared using the online diagnostic test calculator on the MedCalc platform (http://www.medcalc.org/calc/diagnostic_test.php) [27].

Statistics and analysis

All experiments included at least three independent replicates, as detailed in the respective figure legends. Figures and schematic illustrations were generated using Adobe Illustrator 6, with data visualization performed in GraphPad Prism 8. Data processing and statistical analysis were conducted using Microsoft Excel 2016 and GraphPad Prism 8, respectively.

Overview of the Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay system

Figure 3 illustrated the workflow and fundamental principles of the Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot diagnostic system. In summary, genomic nucleic acid from N. gonorrhoeae was extracted using a boiling method (Fig. 3A and B, step 1). Subsequently, the target gene orf1 of N. gonorrhoeae was amplified and specifically identified through the LAMP-CRISPR/Cas12b process (Fig. 3A and B, step 2). Finally, the signal detection was performed using visual AuNP-LFB with probe 5′-FAM-TTTATTTATTTAT-Biotin-3′ (Fig. 3A, step 3), real-time fluorescence analysis or and blue light through Flu-probe 5′-FAM-TTTATTTATTTAT-BHQ1-3’ (Fig. 3B, step 2).

Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay sensitivity and specificity

The LoD of Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay was evaluated using serially diluted genomic DNA from N. gonorrhoeae ATCC 49,926 (1.0 × 10⁴ to 1.0 copy per reaction) under optimal conditions (56 °C, 50 min). Assay outputs were simultaneously assessed via real-time fluorescence intensity, blue light observation, and AuNP-LFB visualization. The LoD was determined to be 7 copies per test (Fig. 6). Notably, the visual AuNP-LFB results showed excellent concordance with both real-time fluorescence intensity and blue light detection (Fig. 6A–C).

Evaluating Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay feasibility

The diagnostic feasibility of Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay for N. gonorrhoeae detection was assessed using 78 genital secretion samples from individuals with suspected gonorrhea infection. All clinical specimens were tested in parallel by qPCR and the Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay. Discussion

Herein, we report a new N. gonorrhoeae diagnostic platform Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot, which integrates CRISPR-Cas12b-based detection with LAMP pre-amplification in a single, one-step reaction. This platform enables specific, ultrasensitive, convenient, and visual identification of N. gonorrhoeae. The clinical feasibility of our assay was validated using genital secretion specimens obtained from patients with suspected N. gonorrhoeae infection, and the results were compared with those obtained by qPCR for N. gonorrhoeae species.

Gonococcus is a gram-negative, non-encapsulated, obligate human pathogen.] In the absence of a gonococcal vaccine to prevent N. gonorrhoeae infection, the timely and accurate diagnosis of gonorrhea is essential for optimal patient management and the interruption of further transmission. Conventional culture has long been regarded as the “gold standard” for the detection of N. gonorrhoeae. However, the cultivation of this fastidious pathogen remains technically demanding and time-intensive, with success rates that vary considerably across specimen types [13, 28]. In symptomatic males, the presence of intracellular Gram-negative diplococci on urethral Gram stain is considered diagnostic for N. gonorrhoeae infection [29]. However, owing to its low sensitivity, Gram staining of endocervical, rectal, and pharyngeal specimens is insufficiently sensitive and is therefore not recommended [29, 30]. PCR-based techniques are recommended for detection of N. gonorrhoeae infection owing to their increased specificity and sensitivity and ease of specimen collect and transference. Chaudhry et al. utilized PCR approach and detected 25 fg per assay for N. gonorrhoeae agent [25]. Zhou et al. applied a droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) method and detected 5.8pg/µL for gonococcus species [31]. Our previous study reported a LAMP-based technology for detection of N. gonorrhoeae, and achieved an LoD of 50 copies/test [32]. Moreover, the Pyrococcus furiosus argonaute integrated LAMP pre-amplification was applied for gonococcus, and achieves an LoD of 5 copies/test [33]. Low et al. reported an RPA-Cas13a-based method for the detection of N. gonorrhoeae, and achieved an LoD of 3.9 copies/µL [34]. Zhao et al. designed an RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a-based strategy for the detection of N. gonorrhoeae and identified as few as 10–20 copies/test [35]. Here, we integrated CRISPR-Cas12b-mediated identification with LAMP pre-amplification within a one-pot reaction, achieving an LoD as low as 7 copies per test and exhibiting no cross-reactivity with other microbial species tested. To ensure a more accurate statistical evaluation of analytical sensitivity in future studies, each concentration level could be tested with a sufficient number of independent replicates. The discovery of CRISPR/Cas systems have revolutionized biomedicine and is driving health care systems toward precision molecular medicine. Within these systems,, a CRISPR RNA (crRNA) or an engineered sgRNA functions as a programmable guide that directs Cas effector nucleases to specific target sequences [14, 15]. This RNA-guided targeting enables highly efficient on-target cleavage and substantially enhances target recognition specificity. The first application of CRISPR/Cas systems for nucleic acid detection was reported in 2016 [36], and then has spurred considerable interest in the development of diverse diagnostic platforms. These systems capitalize on key features of various Cas effectors, including sequence-specific recognition, targeted nucleic acid endonuclease activity, and trans-cleavage activity of Cas12, Cas13, and Cas14 [16, 17]. Utilizing the inherent diversity of CRISPR-Cas architectures to engineer tailored guide RNA sequences enables specific, cost-effective detection of a broad range of targets. In this study, a specific sgRNA was designed to target the orf1 gene of N. gonorrhoeae, thereby enabling precise guidance of the Cas12b effector to the intended genomic locus. LAMP is a transformative molecular diagnostic method that employs four to six primers targeting six to eight distinct regions of the target gene, together with Bst DNA polymerase, enabling robust nucleic acid amplification under 58–69 °C constant temperature. Several integrated LAMP-CRISPR/Cas approaches typically require separate reaction steps for LAMP amplification and CRISPR/Cas-based specific detection, which increases the risk of cross-contamination and operational complexity [37, 38]. In contrast, our assay integrates LAMP with CRISPR/Cas12b in a single one-pot reaction, thereby eliminating the risk of cross-contamination and rendering it more suitable for clinical applications.

To enable visual, instrument-free readout of the Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot results, the gold nanoparticle-based biosensor was incorporated into our diagnostic platform. The AuNP-LFB, a paper-based biosensor, is well-suited for POC testing due to its high specificity and sensitivity, straightforward fabrication, low cost, user-friendly operation, and direct visual readout by the naked eye [21, 39]. In our diagnostic design, the AuNP-LFB enables visual readout of the Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay using the probe 5′-FAM-TTTATTTATTTAT-Biotin-3′ (Fig. 2). Herein, real-time fluorescence detection and blue light observation using the Flu-probe (5′-FAM-TTTATTTATTTAT-BHQ1-3′) were also employed to analyze the Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay results. Unfortunately, both methods require specialized instrumentation, and not suit for POC detection. The approximate total cost of a single Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay with AuNP-LFB visual interpretation, including LAMP isothermal reagent (approximately US $0.1), CRISPR/Cas12b endonuclease (approximately US $0.2), and AuNP-LFB (approximately US $0.5), was not exceed US $1.0 per reaction, which is cheaper than that of traditional real-time PCR testing (approximately US $7.0 per reaction). The entire workflow, from N. gonorrhoeae DNA extraction to result interpretation, is completed within 65 min, comprising sequential steps of DNA extraction (10 min), LAMP-CRISPR/Cas12b detection (50 min), and visual readout (2 min). Collectively, these preliminary results establish the Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay as an advanced nucleic acid-based diagnostic platform for gonorrhea detection.Our study also has certain limitations. First, The Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay developed herein was primarily designed for the qualitative identification of N. gonorrhoeae. Nevertheless, real-time fluorescence data suggest its potential for semi-quantitative applications, as a positive correlation was observed between fluorescence intensity and N. gonorrhoeae concentration (Fig. 6A). Accordingly, developing a robust, quantitative CRISPR-based method for N. gonorrhoeae detection represents a valuable and important direction for future research. Second, the clinical specimens used in our study were collected from a mixed cohort of patients with suspected N. gonorrhoeae infection. For more comprehensive evaluation of our assay, it needs to test more samples across clinically distinct patient subgroups (e.g., symptomatic versus asymptomatic individuals, and male versus female cohorts).

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully established a novel diagnostic platform that integrates one-step LAMP-CRISPR/Cas12b-based detection with a visual AuNP-based biosensor for the ultrasensitive, specific, and naked-eye identification of Neisseria gonorrhoeae. Our Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay achieved a LOD as low as 7 copies per test and exhibited no cross-reactivity with other microbial species tested. The entire detection procedure, comprising nucleic acid extraction (10 min), LAMP-CRISPR/Cas12b detection (50 min), and visual readout using the AuNP-LFB (< 2 min), can be completed within 65 min without the need for specialized instrumentation. Collectively, these results indicate that the Ng-CRISPR-One-Pot assay holds promise as a practical POC diagnostic tool, enabling more accurate and convenient detection of N. gonorrhoeae infections, particularly in resource-limited settings.
