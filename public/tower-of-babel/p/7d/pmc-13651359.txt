Rural-urban divide in internet usage among the women of reproductive age group: a non-linear Fairlie decomposition analysis in Indian context
Authors: Tamal Basu Roy, Priya Das, Subhadeep Saha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite rapid digitization in India, internet usage of rural women still remains lagged behind. The rural-urban divide in internet usage particularly among the women of reproductive age (15–49 years) though being a key concern to resolve, yet limited evidence exists on the factors driving this disparity. This study addresses the research gap by examining the magnitude of the rural-urban gap and identifying the underlying contributors to this gap.MethodsThis study utilized information collected from 105,373 women aged 15 to 49 years, (Rural-79,012 and urban-26,361) from the fifth round of of National family Health survey (NFHS-5), conducted in India during 2019–2021. Initially, descriptive statistics, bivariate analysis and logistic regression were performed. To quantify the factors contributing to the rural-urban gap in internet usage among women, non-linear Fairlie decomposition analysis was applied.ResultsA substantial and persistent rural-urban gap in internet use was observed among the women of reproductive age. The largest share of the gap was explained by household wealth status (45.75%), followed by household ownership of a mobile phone (27.28%) and women’s educational attainment (21.61%).ConclusionAddressing rural-urban divide in internet use requires a holistic and intersectional approach. Policies must extend beyond improving connectivity to actively dismantling the social and economic barriers that inhibit women’s digital participation irrespective of the place of residence.Keywords: Internet usage, Rural-urban divide, Fairlie Decomposition Analysis, Women of reproductive age group, India

Introduction

Internet usage has now become essential for every individual, acting as a gateway to participate in modern society [3]. The transformation of modern society has made possible to a large extent by digital revolution which contributes unprecedented access to information, communication, services and most importantly prospects for empowerment [5, 42]. Nevertheless, this transformation has not been experienced uniformly everywhere, especially in those countries with deep-rooted socio-economic and geographic disparities like India [41, 50]. One of the most obvious manifestations of this disparity is the rural-urban divide in internet usage which is found much more noticeable when combined age with gender [15]. Women of reproductive age (15–49 years) are the most vulnerable and as well as underserved groups of population nowadays in the society, and they continue to face several socio-cultural and structural barriers that limit their access to and use of different digital technologies like computer, smartphone, internet etc. [43].

Although India has shown a notable rise in internet usage over the last few years, remains still unequally distributed concrening the place of residence [34, 35]. According to the recent report published by National Family Health Survey in 2019-21 (NFHS-5), there are continuing substantial differences in internet usage rates in-between rural and urban areas; the rate of internet usage was found significantly lower in rural areas as compared to urban areas, not only that, NFHS-5 further reported that stark differences persist in internet usage across gender also; About 51% of men reported using the internet while it was 33% for women. The disparity is much more noticeable in rural areas where only 25% rural women used the internet compared to 43% of rural men [39]. Women education and employment, their financial inclusion, reproductive health and above all women’s overall empowerment are severely impacted by these disparities.

A complex interplay of different factors play significant role in widening the rural-urban gap in internet usage among women [9, 11]. Technological factors include device access, internet availability, connection speed and digital literacy [52, 55]. Economic factors most probably play the major role in shaping the internet use of women; in this case, hosehold income level, household infrastructure availability, employment status of women, financial independene, their occupation type, level of economic development of a region, cost of internet access are some of the prime economic factors influencing women’s internet use [20, 31, 32]. Educational attainment is also widely recognized as a key determinant of digital access and internet use. Individuals with higher levels of education are more likely to possess the necessary skills, awareness, and confidence required to effectively engage with digital technologies, thereby enhancing their ability to access online information and services. Education also plays a crucial role in shaping digital literacy, which in turn shows an association with patterns of internet adoption and usage [4] &. In the Indian context, evidence from the International Institute for Population Sciences (NFHS-5) [39] highlights substantial disparities in internet use across different educational levels, particularly among women, with higher usage observed among those with secondary and higher education. These disparities are further pronounced across rural and urban areas, reflecting the intersection of educational inequality with spatial and socio-economic factors. Socio-economic status of women, age of the individual, their marital status, entrenched social norms, cultural attitudes, women autonomy etc. are some of socio-cultural factors affecting women’s internet use [16, 33, 47]. Household size, caste, religion, mass media exposure sometimes can also play significant role in widening or narrowing the gap [60]. The physical infrastructure required for using reliable internet services such as electricity, mobile towers and reasonably priced data services are often lacking in rural areas [12, 59]. Furthermore, gender norms are generally more restrictive for rural women which may impede their freedom to use internet. Conversely, women in towns and cities typically have greater autonomy, higher educational possiblitites and more exposed to outside world which all are positively associated with internet use of urban women [22, 23].

The rural-urban digital divide in internet usage has been widely recognized as an emerging development challenge globally [30, 58]. In the Indian context, national survey evidence has also highlighted substantial disparities in women’s access to and use of the internet across rural and urban areas [39]. Although a growing body of research has examined digital inequality, relatively fewer studies have systematically analysed the underlying factors contributing to the rural-urban divide in internet use among women. A key strength of this present study lies in the application of the Fairlie decomposition technique to examine the rural-urban gap in internet use among women in India. Unlike conventional regression-based approaches that only identify associations, this method enables a decomposition of the observed gap into the relative contributions of various factors. Through this approach, the study provides deeper insight into the extent to which differences in characteristics such as education, household wealth and others explain the digital divide. It also highlights the presence of an unexplained component, suggesting the role of structural and contextual factors beyond measured variables. This decomposition-based evidence offers a more nuanced and policy-relevant understanding of the drivers of rural–urban inequality in internet use among women of reproductive age. Though we found a prior study [49] has attempted to decompose this divide in India, this present study has made several important meaningful extensions and refinement to the prior one. While the existing study primarily focuses on broad socio-economic determinants such as education, wealth, and social group, the present study advances the literature by incorporating a more comprehensive set of household-level infrastructural and digital access variables. In addition to conventional predictors, this study explicitly integrates factors such as mobile phone ownership, access to electricity, banking inclusion, media assets, and household characteristics. So, beyond identifying socio-economic inequalities to uncover the factors through which digital exclusion operates at the micro level, this study findings further highlights the substantial contribution of mobile phone ownership to the rural–urban gap, obviously an important aspect not explicitly quantified in earlier work. This provides a more direct explanation of how access barriers can act as a driver of unequal internet use. By integrating both socio-economic and infrastructural determinants together, the present study offers a more comprehensive analytical framework, enabling a clearer understanding of with economic disadvantage how technological accessibility constraints also create this rural-urban gap. This triggers to more actionable policy implications, suggesting that bridging the digital divide requires not only improvements in education and wealth but also targeted interventions in device access and basic infrastructure. Furthermore, most existing studies focus on the general population or broader gender gaps, with comparatively little attention given specifically to women in the reproductive age group (15–49 years), despite the significant implications of digital access for health, autonomy, and overall well-being [54]. For reproductive age group women, use of internet can facilitate them for increased access to crucial reproductive health information, improved reproductive knowledge and attitude, strengthened social support networks, increased awareness regarding adequate maternity care and family planning services etc. [13, 14, 46, 61]. Besides, the women of this age group are basically central to household well-being, having crucial role in child development and improving overall socio-economic outcomes. Therefore, expanding their internet access and usage is not only to resolve the issue of gender inequality but also a tactical investment in national development also. By concentrating exclusively on women of reproductive age group, this study offers valuable insights at the nexus of gender, age, location and digital inclusion which are crucial for accomplishing a number of Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), related to achieveinng SDG 5 which is gender equality, SDG 10 which is aimed to reduced inequalities and SDG 3 also have focused on to improve health and well-being. Moreover, this study provides a profound understanding of not only measuring how large the gap is but also the underlying factors exacerbating it. Identifying and understanding those factors that drives this gap is indispensable for targeted interventions and policy-making. Overall, by providing a nuanced analysis of why rural women are lagged behind than their urban counterparts in internet use, this study contributes to the scientific research, thereby helps policymakers and development professionals by furnishing useful information to bridge this rural-urban gap.

Materials & methods

Data source

This current study utilized secondary data taken from the fifth round of National family Health survey (NFHS-5) which was conducted in India during 2019–2021. NFHS-5 is a nationally representative cross-sectional surveys that collect comprehensive data and information regarding various socio-economic, demographic, reproductive and child health, other health issues and family welfare indicators at the national, state and district levels.The entire survey for data collection in NFHS-5 has been framed using a stratified two-stage sampling approach, encircling a vast number of populations, approximately 636,699 households, 724,115 women aged 15–49 years and 101,839 men aged 15–54 years distributed across the 707 districts spanning 28 states and 8 union territories. NFHS-5 also provides information about several vital characteristics of the respondents including media exposure and internet usage. The survey guide was adapted from the women’s questionnaire of the National Family Health Survey-5 (NFHS-5), conducted by the International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS) and ICF (International Institute for Population Sciences [IIPS] & ICF, 2021).

Study population

Out of a total sample of 724,115 women of reproductive age group (15–49 years) in NFHS-5, the present study was purposefully constricted to particularly those women of reproductive age group for those the information regarding internet usage was available. Besides, we omitted all the missing values and uncategorized responses from each predictor and outcome variables. Hence, maintaining all these, the study included a total of 105,373 participants aged 15 to 49 years, including 79,012 women from rural areas and 26,361 women from urban residential areas. Study variables

Outcome variable

Internet usage among the women of reproductive age group 15–49 years) was the primary outcome of interest of this present study. This variable was derived from the NFHS-5 survey question asking whether the respondent had ever used the internet. Responses were recorded in a dichotomous format and coded as ‘Yes’ and ‘No’.

Explanatory variables

Women’s age and education level, their marital status, religion, caste, household wealth quintile, number of household members, access to bank account, household has electricity, sources of drinking water, households owns a television and household owns a mobile phone were selected as key explanatory variables of this present study. All the explanatory variables were chosen on the basis of earlier literature [10, 45, 50, 62]. Statistical methods

Initially a descriptive analysis was performed to describe (frequency and percentage distribution) different background characteristics of sample population. After that a bivariate analysis was carried out to present the percentage prevalence of women’s internet usage in rural and urban areas by selected predictor variables and rural-urban difference in usage of internet was also calculated. Additionally, Karl Pearson’s chi-squared test statistics of association was conducted to examine the nature of bivariate associations where p-value shows the significance level. Furthermore, a binary logistic regression analysis was carried out to estimate the likelihood of internet usage and its precision among rural and urban women. The model estimates the probability of the outcome as a function of selected explanatory variables. The estimated coefficients from the logistic regression were then used to compute predicted probabilities for each individual. These predicted probabilities form the basis for the Fairlie decomposition analysis. The non-linear decomposition technique that is Fairlie decomposition analysis, generally suitable for binary outcome, was applied here to decompose the existing gap in the probability of internet usage between rural and urban women and the contribution of its underlying predictors. Unlike linear decomposition methods, the Fairlie approach decomposes the gap in mean predicted probabilities rather than raw coefficients. We have also checked multillinearity through Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) before conducting binary logistic regression and Fairlie decomposition technique.

Fairlie decomposition analysis was proposed by professor Robert Fairlie in the year 1995. Fairlie decomposition analysis estimates the contribution of each explanatory factors to explain the existing differences across groups by calculating the average predicted probability change resulting from replacing one independent variable at a time for one group, holding all other variables constant for the other group (regression coefficient). This model splits the study results into two distinct segments: explained and unexplained part. The equation used in Fairlie decomposition analysis can be expressed as follows:

The first half of the equation above represents the explained part which tells about the contribution of entire set of observed variables included in the study expressing the gap in terms of internet use between rural and urban women which is attributed in the form of estimated coefficients whereas the second half represents the unexplained part, caused by the group differences in unmeasurable or unobserved endowments or due to exogenous covariates [19].

Discussion

Using data from the most recent nationally representative sample survey, NFHS-5 (2019-21), the current study examines the rural-urban gap in internet usage among women in India and quantifies the contribution of key predictors to this disparity. The decomposition results indicate that 85.34% of the observed gap is explained by differences in characteristics, while 14.66% remains unexplained, suggesting the role of unobserved factors. These findings can be interpreted through the lens of the digital divide theory, which emphasizes inequalities not only in access but also in the skills and effective use of digital technologies, as articulated by Jan van Dijk [18]. Conforming to recurring patterns found in earlier studies, internet usage was substantially higher among urban women than the women residing in rural counterparts [6, 7, 37]. While earlier studies have documented the existence of such disparities across different contexts [8, 21, 24, 35], many have not quantified the relative contribution of underlying factors [25, 26, 57]. By applying a decomposition approach, the present study extends this literature by identifying the specific drivers of the rural-urban divide.

Findings revealed the fact that there was a significant rural-urban gap regarding internet usage observed among Indian women which was mostly associated with three major contributory factors such as household wealth quintile, household owns mobile phone and educational level of women and notably household wealth quintile was the most significant contributory factor largely explaining this gap; in particular, 45.75% variation in internet usage was detected between rural and urban women in terms of their household wealth status. This finding aligns with the digital divide perspective, where economic resources shape access to digital infrastructure and services. From a capability approach standpoint proposed by Amartya Sen, material access alone is insufficient; rather, individuals must possess the means and opportunities to convert resources into meaningful use [2]. Urban households in India generally tend to be wealthier and wealth enables these households to be better connected with easier and greater access to digital devices like smart phones, computers, laptops and other gadgets and as a result women in these households can readily benefit from using internet services [27, 31]. On the other hand, rural households, particularly the poorest and most impoverished ones may not have these gadgets which may prevent rural women from utilizing the internet. Most of the rural households don’t have the access of stable electricity and using inverter is out of their reach. Furthermore, cost continues to be a barrier even when infrastructure is accessible. The majority of low-income and impoverished families in rural India can’t afford the expensive data plans needed to access the internet. Thus although being essential in many facets of modern life, the rural women are continuously being deprived from using internet services [38]. Moreover, wealthier households are generally common in urban areas, are more likely to send girls to school and provide digital education which increases their familiarity and usage of the internet from an early age whereas poorer rural families often have lower female educational attainment and the dropout rate among girls are also high which restricts their exposure to digital world. In addition to wealth, social and cultural norms also play an important role in widening the rural-urban gap of internet use among women. In rural and lower-income areas, patriarchal family norms are generally stronger, limiting women’s mobility and internet access [7, 40]. On the other side, affluent families of urban areas are typically more liberal or at least permissive, allowing women into greater freedom to explore and utilize internet services. While rural impoverished women are normally engaged in unpaid domestic work or informal labor and have minimal need to access the internet, urban women from wealthy households generally participate more in economic activities, creating a functional need for internet use [10, 36].

The major findings of the study further identified that after household wealth status, household owns mobile phone has emerged as a significant predictor for creating gap of using internet between rural and urban women in India. Due to a variety of interconnected socio-economic, cultural and infrastructure issues, household mobile phone ownership accounts for a considerable portion of the disparities in internet usage between rural and urban women. Every household in urban areas are more likely to own mobile phones, especially smart phones which are essential for internet access while uses of mobile phone are less common in rural areas, and even if a household owns a phone, it might not be a smart phone or it might be shared with other family members who are mostly men. Due to not having any internet-enabled devices in their possession, women in rural areas are therefore less likely to utilize the internet. Women’s ownership and use of mobile phones can occasionally still be stigmatized and discouraging in rural areas due to low educational attainment and patriarchal family norms; this pattern can be understood through a gendered perspective on technology, as discussed by Judy Wajcman, where social norms and intra-household power relations influence women’s interaction with digital resources [56]. Urban households typically belong from higher income groups, enabling women to possess smart phones for their personal use. Rural women specifically the poorer ones constantly faces financial restraints so that the rural households may prioritize phone-ownership especially for work-related purposes by men only [1, 28, 47]. In order to bridge the gender digital divide in contemporary India, it is imperative to have mobile phone ownership among all rural households, especially by making sure that every women must own mobile phone and have direct internet access [44, 51].

Educational status of women also plays a significant role generating the rural-urban divide of internet usage among Indian women because education has an enormous impact on digital access, abilities and confidence of using technology. Women with greater education are more likely to be digitally literate, especially in urban areas; they can utilize digital technologies for communication, education and employments as well as they know the technical knowhow to operate smart phones, navigate apps and browse the internet. Some rural women’s ability and confidence to use internet services are hampered by their lower educational attainment. Educated women are more aware of advantages of internet by knowing how internet can benefit an individual in the field of education, employment, health facilities, financial services and as well as social interaction. In India, although the availability of digital content in regional languages has increased in recent years, a substantial proportion of online information particularly educational, administrative, and health-related content continues to be concentrated in English and Hindi [48]. This may create differential ease of access, as women with higher levels of education, who are more likely to be proficient in these languages, can navigate and utilize such content more effectively. Conversely, women with lower levels of education, especially in rural areas, may face linguistic and comprehension barriers that can limit meaningful engagement with the internet [29]. At the same time, the relationship between internet access and content use is likely to be bidirectional. While the availability of relevant and comprehensible content may motivate individuals to adopt internet services, having access to the internet itself can also gradually expose users to diverse content and enhance digital familiarity over time [17]. Furthermore, disparities in early exposure to digital technologies may also play a role. Urban educational institutions are more likely to integrate information and communication technologies into teaching-learning processes, providing students with hands-on experience. In contrast, many rural schools continue to face infrastructural constraints, such as limited access to computers and internet facilities, which may restrict early digital exposure among girls and contribute to the persistence of the digital divide [53]. Last but not the least, educated women mostly from urban settings are more likely to pursue their higher education and engage in work, both of which necessitate regular internet use for taking and joining in online classes, sending emails and job applications etc. In rural areas, women with lower educational attainment don’t generally require that kind of digital needs that prevents themselves from using the internet services [10, 16, 33].

Limitations

This present study faced a few limitations. Though this study identified several significant contributory factors explaining the rural-urban divide of internet use among women in India, 14.66% of the inequality still remained unexplained which indicates there might be some unidentified factors that could be responsible for making this variations. This might warrant a need for further investigation in future research to explore all the unidentified factors contributing to the internet use inequality between rural and urban women. Depending on cross-sectional data (NFHS-5), the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between explanatory variables and internet use. The possibility of omitted variable bias cannot be ruled out, as certain relevant factors such as digital literacy, quality of internet access, intra-household dynamics, and local infrastructural availability were not captured in the dataset. As NFHS-5 provides only quantitative data, some qualitative aspects related to internet usage such as personal beliefs and attitudes, habits, social and cultural norms were missed out from this current study. As NFHS-5 provides the outcome variable as a binary measure, which does not capture the frequency, purpose and intensity of usage. This limits a more comprehensive understanding of digital engagement and may mask important variations between rural and urban women. The responses in NFHS-5 regarding internet usage were self-reported which might introduce the chances of recall biases and social desirability biases, compromising the absolute reliability and accuracy of the findings. Lastly, although sampling weights were applied, the analysis did not fully account for the complex survey design (such as clustering and stratification), which may influence the precision of estimates and standard errors.

Conclusion

This study has concentrated on ongoing and complex rural-urban divide in internet usage among reproductive age group women in India, reflecting light on the socio-economic and infrastructural factors underlying this digital divide. Through decomposition analysis, we find that much of the disparity is driven by household wealth, access to enabling infrastructure and differences in educational attainment-all of which are significantly skewed in favor of women living in urban areas. From a policy perspective, bridging this gap requires a multi-dimensional approach. While initiatives such as Digital India Mission and BharatNet Project have improved connectivity, targeted interventions focusing on female education, digital literacy, and affordability are essential to enhance meaningful internet use among rural women. Addressing socio-cultural barriers is equally important to ensure equitable digital inclusion. Closing the rural-urban divide in internet usage among women is a vital step toward empowering countries, reducing inequality and ensuring that no woman is left behind in this digital era.
