Platelet-derived exosomes regulate endothelial cell inflammation and M1 macrophage polarization in coronary artery thrombosis via modulating miR-34a-5p expression
Authors: Kangkang Wei, Lintong Yu, Jinming Li, Jie Gao, Li Chen, Min Liu, Xiaohan Zhao, Min Li, Dazhuo Shi, Xiaojuan Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As the important factors in coronary artery thrombosis, endothelial injury and M1 macrophage polarization are closely related to the expression of miR-34a-5p. Exosomes in plasma are mainly derived from platelets and play an important role in thrombosis. Based on these facts, this study was conducted to investigate the acting mechanism of platelet-derived exosomes (PLT-exo) in the effects of endothelial injury and M1 macrophage polarization on coronary artery thrombosis. Firstly, rats were divided into the sham-operated group and the coronary microembolization (CME) group, and their plasma-derived exosomes were extracted to detect the expression of miR-34a-5p. Next, the PLT-exo were extracted from healthy volunteers and then co-cultured with ox-LDL-induced endothelial cells and LPS-induced macrophages, respectively. Subsequently, the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and ICAM-1 in endothelial cells was measured, and the level of markers related to M1 macrophage polarization and Sirt1/NF-κB pathway was detected. Finally, the above indicators were examined again after PLT-exo combined with miR-34a-5p mimic were co-cultured with endothelial cells and macrophages, respectively. The results demonstrated that the expression of miR-34a-5p in the CME group was up-regulated compared with the sham-operated group. In cell experiments, PLT-exo modulated the Sirt1/NF-κB pathway by inhibiting the expression of intracellular miR-34a-5p and down-regulated the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and ICAM-1 in endothelial cells and M1 macrophage polarization. After the transfection with miR-34a-5p mimic, endothelial cell inflammatory injury and M1 macrophage polarization increased to varying degrees. In conclusion, PLT-exo can alleviate coronary artery thrombosis by reducing endothelial cell inflammation and M1 macrophage polarization via inhibiting miR-34a-5p expression. In contrast, miR-34a-5p overexpression in PLT-exo may exacerbate these pathological injuries in coronary artery thrombosis.

Introduction

Coronary artery thrombi can be induced on eroded atherosclerotic plaques, and they are closely related to endothelial injury and inflammatory responses1,2. Coagulation activation due to coronary artery endothelial cell injury is the most important and common cause of thrombosis3. Endothelial cell dysfunction is mainly caused by the activation of endothelial cells, especially type II activation, accompanied by the up-regulated expression of interleukin 1 (IL-1), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), which leads to chronic inflammation of the endothelium4. In atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases, macrophages (mø) play a central role in the development of plaques. Classically activated M1 macrophages are implicated in initiating and sustaining inflammation, and they may secrete numerous inflammatory factors such as IL-1, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-12 (IL-12) to promote coronary artery thrombosis after plaque rupture5,6. Therefore, inflammatory injury of endothelial cells and M1 macrophage activation should be highlighted in the exploration of coronary artery thrombosis.

Exosomes are small extracellular vesicles (30–150 nm in diameter) originating from different cell types, and they can modulate the behavior of recipient cells by transferring their bioactive cargos (DNA, chemokines, messenger RNAs, microRNAs, and proteins)7–9. As exosomal release can be triggered by several stimuli associated with cellular stress, exosome secretion may be modulated in several pathological conditions, including myocardial infarction and stroke10–12. In general, more than 75% of exosomes in the blood are derived from platelets, which play an important role in resisting inflammation and atherosclerosis13–15. In coronary artery thrombosis, more platelet-derived exosomes (PLT-exo) are released with platelet activation. However, it remains unclear about the role of these PLT-exo in thrombosis. It has been corroborated in our previous study that PLT-exo may be involved in the process of coronary artery thrombosis by modulating endothelial cell injury and inflammatory cell activation, but relevant mechanisms have not been clarified16. Therefore, this study was designed to explore the mechanism of PLT-exo in coronary artery thrombosis based on their effects on endothelial cell inflammation and M1 macrophage polarization.

Since the active substances in exosomes are derived from secretory cells, specific microRNAs tend to increase in platelets with their elevated levels in corresponding exosomes during thrombosis16. In our previous study, after microRNA detection and screening of patients with coronary artery prethrombotic states, miR-34a-5p was identified as a regulatory gene for coronary artery thrombosis17. Therefore, it was predicted that as miR-34a-5p increased in platelets during thrombosis, the level of miR-34a-5p may also be elevated in PLT-exo and it may regulate other cellular functions through intercellular communication. As an important microRNA in cardiovascular diseases, miR-34a-5p regulates various pathological processes, such as cardiomyocyte injury18, ischemia–reperfusion injury19, inflammation response20, and endothelial cell dysfunction21. Sirt1 is an important target of miR-34a-5p, and it can protect endothelial cell functions, attenuate macrophage inflammation, and inhibit platelet activation22. In previous studies, it was confirmed that miR-34a-5p inhibited the expression of Sirt1 in coronary microembolization (CME) rats, thus activating the NF-κB pathway and exacerbating the inflammatory response23. Based on these findings, the mechanism that PLT-exo influenced coronary artery thrombosis by regulating endothelial cell inflammation and M1 macrophage polarization via mediating miR-34a-5p expression was investigated in this study.

Results

The expression of miR-34a-5p in plasma-derived exosomes was up-regulated in CME rats

In this study, the CME rats were used to investigate the changes in miR-34a-5p in plasma-derived exosomes during thrombosis. To clarify the effects of modeling, the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was measured using echocardiograms 24 h after surgery. The results showed that the cardiac function of the rats in the CME group was significantly reduced compared with the sham group (Fig. 1A and B). HE staining was performed on cardiac tissues to observe the CME incidence and inflammation in the myocardium, and HBFP staining was used to evaluate myocardial damage. The results showed that the CME incidence, inflammatory infiltration, and myocardial injury increased significantly in the CME group compared with the sham group (Fig. 1C–G). Furthermore, the exosomes were extracted and characterized. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that the exosomes had round and cup-shaped vesicles with a bilayer structure (Fig. 1H). The main particle size peak was 119.2 nm, accounting for 95% of the total, with a mean concentration of approximatelycx 1.6 × 1011 particles/ml, which was consistent with the characteristics of exosomes (Fig. 1). The equivalent protein concentration was approximately (2.1 ± 0.45) × 103 μg/ml, as determined by the BCA kit. The purity of proteins was about (8.1 ± 1.62) × 107 particles/μg. Furthermore, Western blotting (WB) was performed to assess the protein expression of exosome markers (TSG101, HSP70, and CD63). The results demonstrated that TSG101, HSP70, and CD63 displayed positive expression in PLT-exo; while calnexin exhibited negative expression in PLT-exo (Fig. 1J). Finally, plasma-derived exosomes were extracted, and the miR-34a-5p level was detected. The results showed that compared with the sham group, the level of miR-34a-5p in plasma-derived exosomes in the CME group increased significantly, while there was no significant difference in external controls (Fig. 1K and Supplementary Fig. S1). It has been corroborated that 75% of exosomes in plasma are derived from platelets, and platelet activation during thrombosis leads to a large amount of exosome secretion. HUVECs and macrophages endocytosed PLT-exo to down-regulate miR-34-5p

In addition, the RT-qPCR results revealed that HUVECs or macrophagocytes treated with PLT-exo displayed decreased miR-34a-5p expression (Fig. 2C and D). PLT-exo inhibited ox-LDL-induced HUVEC inflammation via down-regulating miR-34-5p expression

Based on the CCK8 and ELISA (IL-6) results, 100ug/ml ox-LDL was used to induce HUVEC inflammation, with 5ug/ml PLT-exo for pretreatment (Supplementary Figs. S2A-C). RT-qPCR was used to detect the expression of miR-34a-5p in HUVECs after the intervention of ox-LDL and PLT-exo. The results showed that compared with the control group, the expression of miR-34a-5p in the model group was significantly up-regulated, and this phenomenon was improved after the addition of PLT-exo (Fig. 3A). Besides, WB was used to detect the expression of the miR-34a-5p downstream protein (Sirt1). The results showed that compared with the control group, the expression of Sirt1 was inhibited in the model group, and the addition of PLT-exo promoted the expression of Sirt1 (Fig. 3B and C). It has been validated that in the downstream pathway of miR-34a-5p/Sirt1, inflammatory pathways play an important role in thrombosis23. Additionally, ELISA was conducted to determine the levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) in the supernatant of the culture medium. The results showed that the level of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α was elevated in the model group compared with the control group, and the level of inflammatory factors was reduced after PLT-Exo treatment (Fig. 3E–G). Moreover, Immunofluorescence and WB were used to detect ICAM-1 expression, and the results showed that PLT-Exo treatment could ameliorate the up-regulated expression of ICAM-1 induced by ox-LDL in endothelial cells (Fig. 3B, D, and H). The overexpression of miR-34a-5p promoted HUVEC inflammation

According to the above results, PLT-exo can inhibit miR-34a-5p to mitigate endothelial inflammation. However, it remains undefined whether the overexpression of miR-34a-5p in PLT-exo could aggravate endothelial inflammation during thrombosis. In this study, PLT-exo combined with miR-34a-5p mimic transfection were co-cultured with HUVECs to explore the effect of overexpressing miR-34a-5p in PLT-exo. The co-culture of miR-34a-5p labeled by green fluorescence with HUVECs showed high transfection efficiency (Fig. 4A). The RT-qPCR results showed that the expression of miR-34a-5p was significantly up-regulated after transfection (Fig. 4B). HUVECs were divided into five groups according to experimental requirements: the control group, the ox-LDL + NC mimic group, the ox-LDL + miR-34a-5p mimic group, the ox-LDL + NC mimic + PLT-Exo group, the ox-LDL + miR-34a-5p mimic + PLT-Exo group. Besides, WB was used to detect the expression of the miR-34a-5p downstream protein (Sirt1). Compared with the ox-LDL + NC mimic group, Sirt1 expression was down-regulated after the transfection of miR-34a-5p mimic. Compared with the use of PLT-exo alone (ox-LDL + NC mimic + PLT-Exo group), the overexpression of miR-34a-5p mimic (ox-LDL + miR-34a-5p mimic + PLT-Exo group) inhibited the expression of Sirt1 in endothelial cells (Fig. 4C and D). Furthermore, the downstream inflammatory factors of Sirt1 were also examined. Compared with the ox-LDL + NC mimic group, the ox-LDL + miR-34a-5p mimic group had higher levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. After the overexpression of miR-34a-5p, the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α increased significantly, which reduced the anti-inflammatory effect of PLT-exo (Fig. 4F–H). Finally, immunofluorescence and WB were used to detect ICAM-1. The results showed that the expression of ICAM-1 was up-regulated in the ox-LDL + miR-34a-5p mimic group compared with the ox-LDL + NC mimic group. Compared with the treatment with PLT-exo alone, the expression of ICAM-1 was also up-regulated after miR-34a-5p mimic transfection (Fig. 4E) and I). MiR-34a-5p aggravated HUVEC inflammation by mediating the NF-κB signaling pathway

To explore the effect of miR-34a-5p on the inflammation reaction of HUVECs, HUVECs were transfected with miR-34a-5p mimic or PLT-exo based on ox-LDL treatment. As the core protein of miR-34a-5p regulation, Sirt1 is a deacetylated protein that regulates the acetylation of downstream NF-κB P65. Therefore, the degree of P65 acetylation detected by WB could reflect the regulatory effect of miR-34a-5p on the NF-κB pathway. The results showed that P65 acetylation increased in the transfected miR-34a-5p mimic group compared with the NC mimic group and decreased in the PLT-Exo group compared with the model group (Figs. 5A and B). THP-1 cells were treated with PMA (25 nM, 50 nM, 100 nM, and 150 nM) for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, respectively, to induce M0 macrophages. A better induction effect was observed under the treatment of 100 nM PMA. After 24 h of intervention, the THP-1 cells attached to the wall, with an increased volume and nucleus, showing the morphology of M0 macrophages. Maintaining the concentration of PMA, the cells exhibited obvious cell elongation or pancake-like changes after 72 h. Therefore, the most suitable period for the experiment was determined to be 24–72 h (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, 1ug/ml LPS was added to M0 macrophages to induce M1 macrophages for 16 h based on CCK8 results (Supplementary Fig. S3). RT-qPCR was used to detect the expression of miR-34a-5p in M0 macrophages after LPS and PLT-exo intervention. The results showed that compared with the control group, the expression of miR-34a-5p in the model group was up-regulated, and this phenomenon was improved after the addition of PLT-exo (Fig. 6B). Besides, the expression of Sirt1 was detected by WB. Compared with the control group, the expression of Sirt1 in the model group was inhibited, and the addition of PLT-exo promoted the expression of Sirt1 (Figs. 6C and D). Moreover, INOS was detected by immunofluorescence, and the results showed that PLT-Exo treatment improved LPS-induced polarization of M1 macrophages (Fig. 6H). Discussion

In this study, the mechanism of PLT-exo in the regulation of coronary artery thrombosis was investigated. The results corroborated that PLT-exo could alleviate endothelial cell inflammatory injury, inhibit M1 macrophage polarization, and prevent thrombosis by down-regulating miR-34a-5p expression. These results also showed that miR-34a-5p expression was up-regulated in PLT-exo during coronary artery thrombosis, which aggravated endothelial inflammation and M1 macrophage polarization. These data highlighted the important role of PLT-exo in coronary artery thrombosis.

Coronary artery thrombosis is induced by the rupture of atherosclerotic plaques, and both have similar pathological mechanisms. As revealed in previous studies, PLT-exo can regulate miR-25-3p and miR-223 to mitigate endothelial cell injury and inhibit atherosclerosis14,15. However, the role of PLT-exo in regulating coronary artery thrombosis has not been clarified. Our study not only identified a new target of PLT-exo to protect endothelial cells but also demonstrated for the first time the role of PLT-exo in regulating M1 macrophage polarization. It has been reported that the expression of miR-34a-5p was up-regulated in atherosclerosis in ApoE-deficient mice, which may be related to endothelial cell senescence24. The results of this study may also provide novel insights into the exploration of atherosclerosis.

The function of exosomes is mainly reflected in intercellular communication. The mother cell encapsulates nucleic acids, proteins, and cytokines into the exosomes, and then the exosomes enter the target cells to regulate their functions25. The function of exosomes often depends on their contents. In this study, healthy PLT-exo were innovatively compared with thrombosis-induced PLT-exo, and the results confirmed that miR-34a-5p was specifically expressed in thrombosis-induced PLT-exo. Moreover, these results also verified our hypothesis that miR-34a-5p increased in PLT-exo along with the increase of miR-34a-5p in platelets, thus promoting endothelial cell inflammation and macrophage polarization through intercellular communication.

Nevertheless, this study also has some limitations. Currently, there is no optimal method for the isolation of exosomes with high recovery and high specificity. Although the ultracentrifugation method can balance the exosome recovery and specificity, it is still difficult to avoid the residual small amount of non-vesicular entities26. In particular, 'soluble' proteins such as serum albumin and lipoproteins in blood cannot be completely isolated, which is a potential confounding factor27. In addition to PLT-exo, other cell-derived exosomes in the blood may also affect the purity of PLT-exo. Although these extraneous cells were centrifuged and discarded, the exosomes secreted from them may still affect downstream experiments. The exosomes from patients with coronary artery thrombosis were not used for the comparison in this study because age, gender, disease, and drugs may affect PLT-Exo contents, including the expression of miR-34a-5p. In addition, it can be found that PLT-exo can suppress miR-34a-5p expression, but it is difficult to determine the specific exosomal content that can be responsible for this regulatory mechanism, which may be explored in subsequent studies.

In conclusion, PLT-exo can inhibit the expression of miR-34a-5p to improve endothelial cell inflammation and M1 macrophage polarization, thereby inhibiting coronary artery thrombosis. This may involve a protective mechanism in the human body, and hence PLT-exo are expected to be used in the prevention and treatment of coronary artery thrombosis. In the progression of coronary thrombosis, with the increase of miR-34a-5p in PLT-exo, endothelial cell inflammation and M1 macrophage polarization are also aggravated, which further promotes thrombosis. This may be considered a new target for the treatment of coronary artery thrombosis.

Materials and methods

Isolation and identification of PLT-exo

It has been confirmed that more than 75% of exosomes in plasma are derived from platelets. In this study, to maintain the original survival environment of platelets and reduce confounding factors, PLT-exo were extracted from platelet-rich plasma (PRP). Blood samples were collected from healthy volunteers recruited from Xiyuan Hospital of China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences. Informed consent was obtained from each patient, and all experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study design and methods were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xiyuan Hospital of China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (2021XLA089-2). The blood samples were collected in a 3.8% sodium citrate anticoagulation tube and subjected to treatment immediately. The initial procedure was performed at room temperature (23 °C) to avoid artifactual platelet activation. Then, the PRP was isolated from other blood cells using centrifugation at 100×g for 10 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 1U/ml of thrombin (T8021, Solebo, Beijing, China) was added to the PRP for reactions at 37 °C for 1 h, and then the platelets were aggregated into clumps. Next, the platelets were isolated using centrifugation at 1500×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was extracted and centrifuged at 3000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. Then, the supernatant was transferred to an ultracentrifugal tube, diluted to an appropriate volume, and centrifuged at 13,000×g for 30 min to remove large particles. Next, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22um filter membrane and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g (P50A72-986 rotor, CP100NX, Hitachi, Tokyo, JPN) for 2 h at 4 °C to precipitate the exosomes, followed by the re-suspension of the precipitate in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifugation at 100,000×g for 2 h. After the supernatant was removed, suspended using 100ul PBS, and then dispensed for freezing, the plasma-derived exosomes of the rats were extracted using similar methods.

The ultrastructure and the size distribution of the exosomes were analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (H-7650, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and ZetaView (PMX 110Particle Metrix, Meerbusch, Germany), respectively. The exosome-positive markers (Tsg101, HSP70, and CD63) and the exosome-negative marker (calnexin) were detected by WB.

Animal model

Adult male specific-pathogen-free (SPF) Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (200 ± 20 g) were procured from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (certificate No. SCXK (Jing) 2021-0006). After 7-day acclimation under standard animal care conditions, 30 SD rats were randomly divided into the sham group and the model group. All animals received humane care, and the study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xiyuan Hospital of China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (2023XLC004-2). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All investigators complied with the ARRIVE guidelines.

CME was induced by the injection of sodium laurate into the left ventricle (LV) according to a previous study28. The formation mechanism of CME is similar to that of human coronary artery thrombosis, and there are also some pathological changes, such as platelet activation, endothelial cell injury, and inflammatory cell aggregation. Specifically, the SD rats were intraperitoneally anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg) and ventilated with a small animal ventilator (HX-300S; Taimeng Software Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China). Then, the heart and ascending aorta were exposed by local thoracotomy. Subsequently, 0.2 ml of sodium laurate (2 g/L; L9755, Sigma-Aldrich, Mo, USA; for the CME model) or physiological saline (for the sham group) was injected into the LV using a syringe during the 30-s occlusion of the aorta arch. After the syringe was removed, the heart was put back into the thorax, and the surgical incision was sutured layer by layer. Finally, the rats were placed in incubators and transferred to their cages when they were awake.

Echocardiogram

These rats underwent cardiac ultrasound examinations to evaluate their cardiac functions 24 h after the establishment of the CME model. Specifically, the anterior chest fur of the rats was scraped to perform inhalation anesthesia using isoflurane (Hebei Jindafu Pharmaceutical Co., LTD., Xingtai, China). Then, the rats were fixed in a supine position on a small animal ECG monitoring platform under continuous inhalation anesthesia, and the next step was performed after the heart rate of the rats became stable. To calculate the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of the rats, the B-mode and M-mode echocardiograms of left ventricular long-axis views and short-axis papillary muscle views were performed using a small-animal echocardiograph device.

Tissue treatment and histopathological changes

After echocardiogram measurements, the rats were sacrificed after anesthesia using sodium pentobarbital. The heart samples of the rats were collected and washed in 50 ml of saline and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. After the heart samples were dehydrated by gradient ethanol elution, they were embedded in paraffin and cut into 4-μm slices perpendicularly to the interventricular septum. All slices were stained using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and observed under a light microscope at a magnification of × 200 to evaluate CME and myocardial inflammation. The incidence of CME was calculated as a percentage of the number of coronary arterioles with embolization to the total number of coronary arterioles, and the inflammatory cells per square millimeter (mm2) under the microscope were also counted. The myocardial microinfarct area was identified with hematoxylin-basic fuchsin-picric acid (HBFP) staining (200 × magnification), and the percentage of the microinfarct area was calculated using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 according to the formula: microinfarct area/total observed area × 100%.

Cell culture and protocol

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, 10HU-012, iXcells, CA, USA), after 2–5 passages, were cultured in the endothelial cell medium (ECM, 1001, Sciencell, CA, USA), containing 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% antibiotics, and 1% cell growth supplement (CGS). The HUVECs were randomly assigned to 3 groups: the group treated with PBS (control group), the group treated with the exosomes (5 μg/ml) for 24 h and stimulated with ox-LDL (100ug/ml, YB-002, Yiyuan Biotech, Guangdong, China) for 16 h (PLT-Exo group), and the group only treated with ox-LDL (100ug/ml) for 16 h (model group).

The HUVECs were transfected in accordance with the instructions of the riboFECTTMCP Transfection Kit (C10511-05, RiboBio, Guangdong, China). MiR-34a-5p mimic and miR-34a-5p negative controls (NCs) were provided by RiboBio Co., Ltd. The cells were randomly assigned to 5 groups: the PBS + miR-34a-5p NC group (control group); the PBS + ox-LDL + miR-34a-5p NC group (ox-LDL + NC mimic group); the PBS + ox-LDL + miR-34a-5p mimic group (ox-LDL + miR-34a-5p mimic group); the ox-LDL + miR-34a-5p NC + PLT-Exo group (ox-LDL + NC mimic + PLT-Exo group); the ox-LDL + miR-34a-5p mimic + PLT-Exo group (ox-LDL + miR-34a-5p mimic + PLT-Exo group).

The macrophages were obtained by inducing THP-1 with Phorbol 12-Myristate 13-Acetate (PMA). The THP-1 cell lines were obtained from MeisenCTCC Co., Ltd. and incubated in the THP-1 specific medium containing RPMI-1640, 10% FBS, 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 1% P/S. Then, these cells were primed to M0 macrophages by PMA (100 nM, P1585-1MG, Sigma-Aldrich, Mo, USA) for 24 h. These macrophages were randomly assigned to 3 groups: the group treated with PBS (control group), the group treated with the exosomes (5 μg/ml) for 24 h and stimulated with LPS (1ug/ml, L4391-1MG, Sigma-Aldrich, Mo, USA) for 16 h (PLT-Exo group), and the group only treated with LPS (1ug/ml) for 16 h (model group).

The THP-1 cells were transfected in accordance with the instructions of lentiviruses (HanBio, Shanghai, China). These lentiviruses included miR-34a-5p mimic and miR-34a-5p negative controls (NCs). After transfection, THP-1 was induced into M0 macrophages. These macrophages were randomly assigned to 5 groups: the PBS + miR-34a-5p NC group (control group); the PBS + LPS + miR-34a-5p NC group (LPS + NC mimic group); the PBS + LPS + miR-34a-5p mimic group (LPS + miR-34a-5p mimic group); the LPS + miR-34a-5p NC + PLT-Exo group (LPS mimic + PLT-Exo group); the LPS + miR-34a-5p mimic + PLT-Exo group (LPS + miR-34a-5p mimic + PLT-Exo group).

Uptake of exosomes by endothelial cells and macrophages

To trace the exosomes in the HUVECs and macrophages, the exosomes were isolated and stained with a PKH26 dyestuff (UR52302, Umibio, Shanghai, China) and centrifuged at 100,000 × g. The PKH26-labeled exosomes with a concentration of 5 μg/mL were incubated with the HUVECs and macrophages on 24-well plates at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 24 h. Then, these cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 min at room temperature. Finally, these cells were washed and analyzed under a fluorescence microscope (IX83, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluorescence

The cells were washed with 1 × PBS three times and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Then, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (only Sirt1) and blocked by 5%BSA for 2 h. Subsequently, anti-ICAM-1(10,831–1, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-Sirt1 (ab189494, Abcam, MA, USA), anti-INOS (ab283655, Abcam, MA, USA), and anti-CD206 (24595S, CST, BOS, USA) were added to conduct overnight incubation at 4 °C. After being washed with 1 × PBS three times, the secondary antibody labeled with DyLight 488 or 594 was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. After being washed with 1 × PBS three times, the cells were stained with DAPI for 5 min. Finally, the samples were imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

CCK8 was used to measure the concentration of ox-LDL and LPS. At the end of the cell intervention, the CCK8 reagent was added according to the manufacturer's instructions and incubated at 37 °C for 2.5 h. Next, the optical density (OD) value was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader (Synergytm, BioTek, VT, USA). The supernatant was collected from the cell culture medium to examine the expression of IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-αas per the instructions of the ELISA kits (R2906/ R8160/ R6180, YAD, Beijing, China). The blank and standard wells were set as the controls for the reaction wells. Finally, the OD value was measured using the same method.

Western blotting

The total proteins of cells were extracted with the RIPA buffer (9806, CST, BOS, USA). Then, the supernatant was extracted using the BCA Kit (P0010, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to determine the protein concentration of each sample. Next, the samples were collected and subjected to 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and then they were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, followed by blocking with 5% fat-free milk in the tris buffered saline with Tween (TBST). Subsequently, membranes were conjugated with primary antibodies targeting rabbit monoclonal anti-Sirt1(ab189494, Abcam, MA, USA), rabbit monoclonal anti-NF-kappaB P65 (ab76311, Abcam, MA, USA), rabbit polyclonal Acetyl-NF-kappaB P65 (Affinity Biosciences, AF1017, OH, USA), rabbit monoclonal anti-ICAM-1 (10831-1, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), mouse monoclonal anti-β-tubulin (YM3030, Immunoway, TX, USA), and mouse monoclonal anti-GAPDH (200306-7E4, Zenbio, Chengdu, China) on a table concentrator at 4 °C overnight. Afterwards, the membrane was rinsed by TBST three times and incubated with diluted horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) (CW0103S, CwBio, Beijing, China) or goat anti-mouse (SE131, Solarbio, Beijing, China) at room temperature for 1 h. The stripping buffer (CWBIO, CW0056M, Taizhou, China) was used to remove antibodies from the membrane for repeated measurements. After the ECL kit (Millipore, WBKLS0500) was added, the chemiluminescence instrument was used for automatic exposure. The blots were quantified by Image-Pro Plus 6.0.

Statistical analysis

The analysis method was selected based on whether the data met a normal distribution and the homogeneity of variance. In multiple data sets, the one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test was selected for the inter-group comparison; the independent sample t-test, t' test, or Mann–Whitney U test was selected for the comparative analysis between the two groups. All these statistical analyses were performed by IBM SPSS 20.0, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (two-sided tests). The figure was processed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.
