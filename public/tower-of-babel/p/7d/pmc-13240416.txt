Eligibility for anti‐amyloid treatment in a multiethnic community‐based study
Authors: Lydia Trudel, Joseph Therriault, Arthur C. Macedo, Karin L. Meeker, Meredith N. Braskie, Arthur W. Toga, Serge Gauthier, Paolo Vitali, Suzanne E. Schindler, Sid E. O'Bryant, Pedro Rosa‐Neto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe approval of anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), including lecanemab and donanemab, represents a significant advance in disease‐modifying therapies (DMTs) for early Alzheimer's disease (AD). While appropriate use recommendations (AURs) have been established to guide clinical decision‐making, the proportion of individuals with cognitive impairment in real‐world, multiethnic populations meeting eligibility criteria remains unknown, as do potential differences in treatment‐related risks across ethnic groups.MethodsWe included 513 cognitively impaired individuals from the Health and Aging Brain Study–Health Disparities study, a multiethnic community‐based cohort. Eligibility for lecanemab and donanemab was determined using published AUR criteria. Counts of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities were estimated based on apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 genotype and ethnicity using published incidence rates.ResultsOnly 15% of participants met eligibility criteria for lecanemab or donanemab. Black individuals had a numerically higher estimated ARIA burden, though differences were not statistically significant.DiscussionFew individuals in this community‐based, multiethnic cohort met eligibility for anti‐amyloid therapy, highlighting limited real‐world applicability of current AURs.Highlights Only 15% of community‐based individuals with MCI or dementia met eligibility criteria for lecanemab and donanemab.Black participants had numerically higher estimated ARIA cases, though not statistically significant.Current AUR criteria have limited real‐world applicability across multiethnic populations.Broader inclusion criteria and real‐world safety data are needed to ensure equitable, safe implementation.

INTRODUCTION

The recent approval of anti‐amyloid beta (Aβ) monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) as disease‐modifying therapies (DMTs) such as lecanemab and donanemab 1 , 2 for early symptomatic Alzheimer's disease (AD) marks a critical turning point in the treatment of early symptomatic AD. These therapies hold significant interest, yet it remains unclear how many individuals in the broader population, particularly outside of highly screened clinical trial settings, would meet the eligibility criteria to receive them.

Recent work has examined the implications of these criteria in specialty clinic settings and among patients with access to AD biomarker assessments. 3 However, the generalizability of trial eligibility remains largely untested in diverse, community‐based populations, particularly in cohorts with greater racial and ethnic diversity, where health disparities and differential access to care may further restrict eligibility. 4 Furthermore, the extent to which the risks and benefits of these therapies across groups remain uncertain, given the limited representation of diverse populations in clinical trials.

In this study, we assess the proportion of individuals with cognitive impairment who would qualify for lecanemab and/or donanemab in an ethno‐racially diverse, community‐based cohort. We compare eligibility based on the appropriate use recommendations (AURs) 5 , 6 for each medication, which were developed to guide treatment decisions in real‐world settings while maintaining a conservative approach aligned with clinical trial criteria.

METHODS

Participants

The Health and Aging Brain Study–Health Disparities (HABS‐HD) study is a single‐site cohort study conducted at the University of North Texas Health Science Center in Fort Worth, Texas. The HABS‐HD cohort is a community‐based study of aging and dementia focused on the diverse recruitment of Black, Hispanic, and non‐Hispanic White participants. Inclusion/exclusion criteria for the HABS‐HD study have been described elsewhere. 7 Briefly, exclusion criteria included recent/current diagnosis of cancer, current severe mental illness, recent traumatic brain injury with loss of consciousness, current or recent alcohol or substance abuse, or contraindications to positron emission tomography (PET) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Ethnicity and race are self‐identified, and participants may choose to conduct their interview in English or Spanish. The HABS‐HD study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of North Texas Health Science Center. All participants or their caregivers provided informed written consent to participate in the respective studies.

Neuroimaging

MRI acquisition

Imaging data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Skyra or Vida MRI scanner. High‐resolution structural T1‐weighted images were obtained using a magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters on the Skyra: repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.93 ms, matrix size = 256, field of view = 270 mm, and voxel dimensions of 1.1 × 1.1 × 1.2 mm or on the Vida: TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.98 ms, matrix size = 256, field of view = 256 mm, and voxel dimensions of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm. 8 Additional MRI sequences collected in the study were previously described. 9 A gradient echo (GRE) sequence was used to assess cerebral microhemorrhages. Lacunar infarcts, cerebral microhemorrhages, and white matter hyperintensity (WMH) burden were visually assessed by a trained radiologist, with WMH severity classified according to the Fazekas scale.

Amyloid‐PET acquisition

Amyloid‐PET was acquired using [18F]florbetaben normalized by the whole cerebellum on one of two identical Siemens Biograph Vision 450 PET/CT scanner or MCT20 scanner. Scans were acquired using 8.1 mCi (± 10%) of 1 8F‐florbetaben (Neuraceq). Emission data were collected over 20 min, starting 90 min after injection, and divided into four 5‐min frames. Images were reconstructed using a protocol with eight iterations and five subsets, incorporating time of flight, a 440 × 440 matrix, and an all‐pass filter. 10 A composite amyloid‐PET standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) was calculated by averaging the SUVR from the anterior/posterior cingulate, frontal, lateral parietal, and lateral temporal cortices. Amyloid‐PET scans were considered positive if the SUVR was equal or greater than a previously validated threshold of 1.08. 10 , 11 This threshold aligns with the [18F]florbetaben SUVR cutoff established by the ADNI3 protocol for [18F]florbetaben‐PET. 12 Imaging processing information, as well as details of imaging acquisition parameters, have been described elsewhere for the HABS‐HD cohort. 10 , 13

Clinical assessment

Participants completed a standardized neuropsychological battery aligned with major Alzheimer's studies and commonly used in practice: Mini‐Mental Status Examination (MMSE), Wechsler Memory Scale‐Third Edition (WMS‐III), Digit Span and Logical Memory I and II, Trail Making Test Parts A and B, FAS (phonemic fluency), Animal Naming (semantic fluency), Spanish‐English Verbal Learning Test (SEVLT), Digit Symbol Substitution, Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), the American National Adult Reading Test (English speakers), and Word Accentuation Test (Spanish speakers). Cognitive diagnoses were assigned using an algorithm (Figure S1) based on test performance, Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scores, and expert consensus. Norms were stratified by education, age, and primary language. Participants were classified as cognitively unimpaired, MCI, or dementia based on CDR scores and test performance relative to these norms, with neuropsychologist review in cases of diagnostic mismatch.

APOE genotyping

DNA was isolated from peripheral blood buffy coat samples (n = 1635) using the Mag‐Bind Blood & Tissue DNA HDQ 96 Kit (Omega Bio‐tek) and processed on a Hamilton Microlab STARlet liquid handling system (Hamilton Company). Genotyping followed the Illumina Global Screening Array (GSA) protocol based on Infinium HTS chemistry and was analyzed using Genome Studio 2.0. Samples with a call rate below 98% were either reprocessed or excluded. Quality control procedures were consistent with published guidelines and the established protocols used in prior work by the original investigators. 14 APOE genotype (rs429358 and rs7412) was imputed from single nucleotide polymorphism array data using Impute2 with 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 as the reference panel.

Statistical methods

We applied the AUR's eligibility criteria to the study sample for both lecanemab and donanemab. We then derived sample characteristics in eligible subsamples. We estimated the number of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA)‐E and ARIA‐H cases by ethnicity for both lecanemab and donanemab by multiplying published ARIA incidence rates for each APOE ε4 genotype from either open‐label addendum (OLA) 15 for donanemab or the Phase 3 CLARITY‐AD trial of lecanemab 1 , 16 by the number of participants within each APOE ε4 group across ethnicities. This provided an expected count of ARIA events, stratified by ethnicity and based on APOE ε4 genotype. To evaluate differences in ARIA occurrence across ethnicities, we used Fisher's exact test. For our study, this test was more suitable and reliable than the χ2 test, given the small sample sizes and low expected cell counts in some ethnic groups.

RESULTS

Participants

Out of the 3838 participants from the HABS‐HD cohort, we included 513 participants with MCI or dementia who had available amyloid‐PET imaging, MRI assessment, and APOE genotyping (Figure 1). Inclusion and exclusion criteria for lecanemab and donanemab were applied based on AURs. 5 , 6 All participants included in the study had the necessary data available to apply both criteria. Lecanemab eligibility – AUR inclusion criteria

Applying the lecanemab AUR inclusion criteria reduced the eligible study population from 513 to 78 participants (i.e., 15% of the study sample was eligible; Figure 2A). For the clinical diagnosis of MCI or mild AD dementia, 55 participants were excluded due to a CDR global score other than 0.5 or 1.0. Thirty‐nine participants were excluded due to their MMSE score (<22), and 341 individuals were excluded due to a negative amyloid‐PET scan. As part of the study design, all HABS‐HD participants are required to have a designated study partner and to provide written consent, thereby meeting this inclusion criterion. Note that in the AUR, age and BMI criteria, as well as clinical diagnosis of MCI or mild dementia, are applied based on physician judgment, which may introduce some variability but likely reflects a more flexible and inclusive approach compared to strict clinical trial protocols.

Lecanemab eligibility – AUR exclusion criteria

When applying the lecanemab AUR exclusion criteria, we excluded nine individuals from the previously included 78 participants; 69 individuals were still eligible (Figure 3A). No individual had four or more microhemorrhages, five individuals had a Fazekas score of 3, one individual had more than two lacunar infarcts, one individual had a stroke in the last 12 months prior to study assessment, and no individuals were on anticoagulants. One individual was excluded due to a platelet count lower than 50,000. One individual presenting with an immunologic disease (in this case, rheumatoid arthritis) was excluded. We inferred that no participants met exclusion criteria related to severe psychiatric illness (e.g., psychosis) that would impair comprehension of study procedures or treatment implications, as enrollment in the HABS‐HD cohort requires the capacity to understand and comply with study demands.

Exclusion criteria based on Appropriate Use Recommendations for (A) lecanemab and (B) donanemab.

The final eligible sample (n = 69) had a mean age of 71.4 ± 6.79 years; 37 participants were male, and 32 participants were female, with an average of 14.5 ± 2.78 years of education. Further, 31 participants were Black, 25 were non‐Hispanic White, and 13 were Hispanic. Mean global amyloid‐PET burden was 60.9 ± 25.7 Centiloids (CL).

Estimated risk of ARIA by APOE ε4 genotype and ethnicity

We assessed the risk of ARIA‐E and ARIA‐H in eligible participants across ethnic groups based on APOE ε4 genotype.

Among eligible individuals for lecanemab, a little over half of White and non‐Hispanic White participants were APOE ε4 non‐carriers, while only 26% of Black individuals were APOE ε4 non‐carriers. Black individuals also had the highest prevalence of APOE ε4 homozygosity (19%), compared to 0% in Hispanic participants and only 4% in non‐Hispanic White participants. Further, 55% of Black individuals, 53% of Hispanic, and 40% of non‐Hispanic White individuals were APOE ε4 heterozygotes (Figure 4A). Using published ARIA prevalence rates associated with APOE ε4 allele number for the Phase 3 CLARITY‐AD trial of lecanemab, we estimated six ARIA‐E events in Black individuals, two in Hispanic individuals, and three in non‐Hispanic White participants in the eligible sample for lecanemab (Figure 4B). For ARIA‐H, we estimated six events in Black individuals, two in Hispanic individuals, and four in non‐Hispanic White individuals. Although a higher estimated number of ARIA events was observed in Black individuals, Fisher's exact test did not yield statistically significant differences across ethnic groups (ARIA‐E: p = 0.39; ARIA‐H: p = 0.18).

Among eligible individuals for donanemab, approximately half of White and non‐Hispanic White participants were APOE ε4 non‐carriers, while only 29% of Black individuals were APOE ε4 non‐carriers. Black individuals also had the highest prevalence of APOE ε4 homozygosity (21%), compared to 0% in Hispanic participants and only 4% in non‐Hispanic Whites in the eligible sample for donanemab (Figure 4C). Using published ARIA prevalence rates in OLA associated with APOE ε4 allele number, we estimated eight ARIA‐E events in Black individuals, three in Hispanic individuals, and five in non‐Hispanic White participants (Figure 4D). For ARIA‐H, we estimated 11 events in Black individuals, four in Hispanic individuals, and seven in non‐Hispanic White individuals. Again, despite higher estimated ARIA risks in Black individuals, these differences were not statistically significant (ARIA‐E: p = 0.19, ARIA‐H: p = 0.07).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we estimated eligibility for anti‐amyloid treatments in a community‐based multiethnic cohort study. We found that applying the AUR criteria for both lecanemab and donanemab to individuals presenting with cognitive impairment reduced the eligible sample by ∼85%. Although not statistically significant, a higher number of estimated ARIA events was observed among Black individuals based on published ARIA incidence rates. Taken together, these results reveal limited eligibility for treatment and disparities in potential safety concerns within real‐world diverse populations.

Our findings add to growing real‐world evidence that a relatively small proportion of individuals with cognitive impairment meet the eligibility criteria for anti‐amyloid treatments. 3 , 18 Large clinical trials have faced high rates of ineligibility due to their inclusion/exclusion criteria. For example, the Phase 3 lecanemab trial reported a screen failure rate of approximately 70%. 1 Screening challenges are also observed in clinical settings: Recent reports from memory clinics using anti‐amyloid treatment AURs found that only 10.7% of patients were eligible for lecanemab, largely because most did not meet the basic clinical criteria for early symptomatic AD, such as a CDR of 0.5 or 1 and MMSE ≥ 22, and biomarker evidence of amyloid‐beta pathology. 18 Similarly, we found that only 15% of our community‐based cohort was eligible under the AURs for either lecanemab or donanemab. In our study, the primary factor contributing to participant ineligibility was an amyloid‐negative PET scan. Our findings emphasize the importance of biomarker confirmation and highlight how real‐world populations often include individuals whose cognitive impairment is not driven by AD pathology. Furthermore, recent evidence suggests that a subset of individuals with MCI who are amyloid‐positive do not exhibit detectable tau pathology, raising uncertainty about the biological drivers of their symptoms. 19 As a result, future treatment eligibility criteria may become increasingly stringent, incorporating additional markers such as tau pathology, to better ensure that AD pathology is the primary contributor to clinical impairment.

The issue of accessibility to biomarker confirmation of Aβ status is critical. In our study, eligibility was determined based on amyloid‐PET status, which, while accurate, remains costly and inaccessible, especially outside urban or research settings. Plasma biomarkers such as tau phosphorylated at threonine 217 have shown promise in predicting amyloid‐PET positivity among cognitively impaired individuals and may facilitate broader access to diagnosis and treatment in the near future. 20 The integration of plasma testing into routine clinical practice could increase accessibility by eliminating the need for advanced imaging or lumbar punctures as a prerequisite for diagnosis and treatment. 21

Beyond clinical criteria, real‐world barriers may limit treatment access even among patients who are otherwise eligible. Some individuals may lack adequate social support, such as family members or caregivers who can reliably accompany them to infusion appointments or monitor for treatment‐related side effects like ARIA. Others may face financial concerns, including uncertainties around insurance coverage, out‐of‐pocket costs for biomarker testing or monitoring MRIs, and indirect expenses such as missed work or transportation. 22 Furthermore, the decision to initiate treatment introduces another layer of complexity. Many ultimately choose not to initiate treatment due to a variety of concerns, including perceived risks, time commitment, or the physical and emotional burden of treatment. As such, non‐biological, real‐world determinants have an influence on who ultimately receives DMTs, and addressing these barriers will be essential to ensure equitable implementation of anti‐aymloid therapiess. Importantly, these barriers are not experienced equally across populations and may be further exacerbated among racial and ethnic minoritized groups due to structural racism, differential access to healthcare, a higher burden of comorbidities, and other social determinants of health. 23 , 24 , 25 In fact, many inequities arise before treatment, including disparities in diagnosis, referral, and access to cognitive evaluation or biomarker testing, which may limit eligibility from the outset. As a result, inequities in treatment initiation and monitoring may persist or widen as anti‐amyloid therapies are implemented in routine clinical practice. Addressing these disparities will require targeted strategies to improve access to timely and culturally appropriate diagnostic services, educate healthcare providers about how social and structural factors affect patients’ health and access to care, and implement community‐based outreach programs to ensure equitable identification and care for all individuals at risk of AD.

ARIA are MRI‐detectable adverse events associated with anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibody therapy for AD. 26 ARIA‐E refers to edema or effusion (vasogenic edema, sulcal effusion), and ARIA‐H refers to hemorrhagic findings (microhemorrhages, superficial siderosis, or rarely larger intracerebral hemorrhage), which are more likely to be associated with clinical symptoms. 27 , 28 Given the association between APOE ε4 genotype and ARIA risk, 29 it is also important to consider the rates of ARIA‐E and ARIA‐H. When compared with results from recent clinical trials, estimated ARIA events in our community‐based sample were broadly consistent. For lecanemab, estimated ARIA‐H rates (17.4%) were nearly identical to those observed in the CLARITY‐AD Phase 3 trial (17.3%), while ARIA‐E was modestly higher (15.9% vs 12.6%). 1 Similarly, for donanemab, the estimated frequency of ARIA‐H (29.7%) and ARIA‐E (21.6%) closely resembled that reported in the TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ Phase 2 trial (ARIA‐H: 27.2%; ARIA‐E: 19.8). 15 These small differences may reflect variation in Aβ burden, APOE ε4 carriership, and other comorbidities between community‐based cohorts and highly selected clinical trial samples. For example, the mean global amyloid‐PET burden in our participants eligible for donanemab was lower than that reported in the donanemab trial (61.7 ± 25.8 CL vs 82.5 ± 37.2 CL). Similarly, the mean burden in our participants eligible for lecanemab was lower than for those in the CLARITY‐AD lecanemab trial (60.9 ± 25.7 CL vs 77.9 ± 44.8 CL). Thus, the lower Aβ burden in our sample may partly explain why our estimated ARIA‐E frequencies were slightly different than observed in trials. Another possibility is differences in the severity of cognitive impairment, as recent observational studies have reported higher rates of ARIA in individuals with dementia versus individuals with MCI. 18

Despite evidence suggesting Black individuals may be at increased risk of AD and tend to present with more severe dementia, 30 , 31 , 32 they have been historically under included in AD research. 3 , 30 , 33 As a result, treatment eligibility and associated risks in this population remain poorly characterized. In our study, Black individuals made up a large proportion of those who met eligibility criteria, but they may also be disproportionately excluded from treatment due to their higher proportion of APOE ε4 homozygotes. In our study, we found that 19% of Black individuals had two APOE ε4 alleles, compared to 0% in Hispanic Whites and 4% in non‐Hispanic Whites. Based on our estimations, both ARIA‐E and ARIA‐H showed similar patterns across racial and ethnic groups, though Black individuals had numerically higher rates for both types. However, the effect of APOE ε4 on AD risk may vary depending on the local ancestry around the APOE gene, which raises the question of whether ARIA risk might also differ accordingly based on ethnicity. 34 , 35 Overall, these results highlight the need for multiethnic data in evaluating both eligibility and safety, given that the prevalence and impact of medical comorbidities may vary across ethnically diverse and underserved populations. 36 Applying these guidelines in real‐world, multiethnic contexts requires careful consideration to ensure equitable and inclusive care for all individuals affected by AD.

The delivery of these treatments presents additional challenges. Anti‐amyloid therapies require substantial infrastructure and staffing. 18 For instance, both AURs detail the extensive clinical resources required for safe and effective use, emphasizing the need for careful implementation planning before large‐scale implementation. 6 These requirements may further exacerbate health inequities, as not all health systems, particularly those serving racially and economically marginalized communities, are equipped to meet these demands. 37 , 38

Ongoing prevention trials such as the TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ3 of donanemab, AHEAD 3‐45, and the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network (DIAN) study are evaluating whether early intervention in asymptomatic individuals can modify disease progression. 39 , 40 While these studies offer potential for expanding treatment indications, the generalizability of their findings to broader clinical practice will depend on the representativeness of their study population. Many of these trials recruit highly selected participants 41 using specific biomarkers, which may limit applicability to the wider asymptomatic population.

This study has several limitations. First, we lacked access to complete medical records, so our eligibility estimates may be liberal; more exclusions might have occurred with full data on comorbidities. Second, we did not have access to MoCA scores, which prevented us from performing sensitivity analyses to see how different cognitive screening tools might affect treatment eligibility. In real‐world settings, the choice of cognitive test may vary across clinics, making it important to understand whether different tools yield consistent outcomes. Another limitation of our study is that we described eligibility based on the AURs for each medication, which are more restrictive than the US Food and Drug Administration label. Correspondingly, our estimates of eligibility may be more modest than what would be observed in real‐world clinical settings. Lastly, the population of the HABS‐HD cohort consists of younger and healthier individuals who are motivated to participate in a study of brain aging and AD and, hence, may not be fully representative of patients in memory clinic settings.
