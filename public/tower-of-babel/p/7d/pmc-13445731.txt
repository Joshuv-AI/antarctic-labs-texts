Building resilient and response-ready influenza vaccination programs for pandemic preparedness: a comparative analysis from five middle-income countries
Authors: Marissa Malchione, Theresa Sommers, Meredith Dockery, Vishakh Unnikrishnan, Alison Mack, Kathryn L Hopkins, Angus Thomson, Ben McCormick, Joel Tinkler, Cassidy Howell, Nick Boehman, Danielle Countryman, Marie Mazur, Malembe Ebama, Stacey Knobler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractInfluenza vaccination programs are critical in reducing the morbidity and mortality from seasonal influenza and bolstering pandemic preparedness. However, there is a notable lack of information about the factors contributing to resilient and response-ready influenza vaccination programs in middle-income countries (MICs). This study set out to identify determinants of resilient and response-ready seasonal influenza vaccination programs in MICs.MethodsThis study used a collective case study design to analyze structural and programmatic elements of resiliency and response-readiness within MIC influenza vaccination programs. A multi-step process was used to select suitable countries for the case study analysis, including an initial literature landscaping of MICs, stakeholder mapping, and an online scoping survey. A conceptual framework was developed to guide analysis across six determinants of resilient and response-ready programs: political, economic, structural, communication & education, socio-behavioral, and COVID-19 response. Data for the selected case studies were gathered via a narrative literature review (initial search conducted January−September 2022) and stakeholder consultations with 27 stakeholders (held between June and September 2022). Findings from the literature review and stakeholder consultations were analyzed using the framework to identify key themes related to influenza vaccination program resiliency and response-readiness.ResultsFive countries – Albania, Bolivia, Brazil, South Africa, and Thailand – met the study selection criteria and were selected as geographically diverse case studies. Five key themes emerged as consistent indicators of resilient and response-ready influenza vaccination programs: 1) building research capacity and a clear pathway from evidence to policy creates resilient and responsive influenza programs; 2) diversified procurement mechanisms help ensure a reliable supply of influenza vaccines; 3) healthcare workers are essential partners for implementing resilient and response-ready influenza vaccination programs; 4) demand generation is key to driving and maintaining influenza vaccine uptake; and 5) building and maintaining seasonal influenza vaccine infrastructure contributes to pandemic preparedness.ConclusionsThe findings offer valuable insights and actionable guidance to improve influenza vaccination program resiliency and response-readiness in MICs and more broadly. Strengthening areas highlighted in this evaluation can help countries improve influenza vaccination coverage while building greater program resilience and readiness to respond to future public health threats, including influenza pandemics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14811-9.Keywords: Influenza vaccination, Middle-income-countries, Vaccination program, Resiliency, COVID-19, Policy

Background

The influenza virus causes substantial morbidity and mortality worldwide, with an estimated one billion influenza cases each year resulting in up to 650,000 respiratory deaths annually [1, 2]. Reducing the seasonal burden of influenza is among the goals set by the World Health Organization (WHO) in their Immunization Agenda 2030 [3, 4]. Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) bear the greatest burden of influenza, experiencing substantial social and economic impacts from the disease [1, 5]. Influenza also remains an ongoing pandemic threat, due to its ability to rapidly mutate and jump between species, creating a risk that a new highly transmissible and pathogenic strain could emerge [6, 7]. In recent years, there has been a growing threat from outbreaks of highly pathogenic H5N1 avian influenza A virus, with spillover into humans [8].

Vaccination is the most effective way to prevent influenza and its complications [2]. WHO recommends that every country establishes an annual influenza vaccination program that can respond to seasonal epidemics and novel influenza strains with pandemic potential [5, 9]. In 2003, WHO Member States endorsed the target from the 56th World Health Assembly to improve influenza vaccination coverage among high-risk groups and achieve 75% coverage among older people by 2010 [10]. However, most countries, particularly LMICs, have yet to achieve these targets [10–12]. Risk groups for influenza, as defined by WHO, include groups at particular risk of developing severe disease (e.g. pregnant women, older adults, and people with chronic conditions), as well as those more likely to be exposed to or transmit the influenza virus (e.g., health and care workers) [9].

Studies have found that a minority of low- and lower-middle income countries have influenza vaccine policies or vaccines available [13]. Only 46% of low- and lower-middle income reported having vaccines available in the public and/or private sector in 2022 [13]. Meanwhile, only 34% of low- and lower-middle income countries reported having a policy for seasonal influenza vaccination in 2023 [14]. Further, despite experiencing a high burden of influenza, low- and lower-middle-income countries receive few vaccine doses. In 2022, high-income and upper-middle income countries purchased 97% of seasonal influenza vaccines, with limited amounts procured by low- and lower-middle-income countries [14]. Decision-makers in LMICs are often faced with multiple competing health priorities, such as HIV, tuberculosis, malaria, and others, which can compete for resources with the annual influenza vaccination program [5, 15]. Where countries are reliant on external funding, influenza programming may also be dictated by funder priorities. Furthermore, in some regions, influenza vaccination is part of the routine immunization program, while in others it is conducted as a standalone program. Affordability at the national level and access to influenza vaccines are also repeatedly documented as key constraints to vaccine uptake [5, 16]. These challenges, among others, continue to hamper progress with increasing vaccine uptake in countries with a high burden of influenza [10].

Previous research has identified policy and programmatic characteristics that support improved and sustained influenza vaccine coverage in certain high- and low-income countries [5, 16]. However, there is a paucity of related literature for middle-income countries (MICs), which collectively represent three-quarters of the world’s population and one-third of global gross domestic product [17]. MICs are also home to over half (62%) of the world’s population who live in poverty [17]. Additionally, there is limited information on features of resilient influenza vaccination programs in MICs, in terms of the health system’s ability to maintain function during stresses and respond to crises. Vaccine resiliency has been defined as immunization systems that are able to withstand major disruptions (e.g., as a result of an outbreak or pandemic) and rapidly adapt to changing circumstances to maintain high vaccine uptake and acceptance over time [18]. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed the fragility of immunization systems and highlighted the importance of vaccine resiliency, as countries experienced significant disruptions to routine immunization, as well as challenges to instating COVID-19 vaccination programs [19].

This study examined the determinants of resilient and response-ready seasonal influenza vaccination programs in MICs, recognizing their critical role in broader pandemic preparedness efforts. We identified five MICs – Albania, Bolivia, Brazil, South Africa, and Thailand – with well-performing influenza vaccination programs to serve as case studies. The project identified structural and programmatic elements of resiliency and response-readiness within the influenza vaccination programs of the case study countries. We also considered how these factors contributed to the country’s COVID-19 response, particularly the implementation of the COVID-19 vaccination program. Findings from the evaluation provide practical, evidence-based insights to increase vaccine coverage and improve the overall preparedness of MICs for influenza outbreaks and future pandemics.

Methods

This study used a collective case study design to analyze structural and programmatic determinants of resiliency and response-readiness across MIC influenza vaccination programs. We followed the collective case study approach by Merriam et al., which involves examining several related cases to generate insights into a broader issue through both within-case and cross-case analysis [20–22]. The study followed a structured approach that included: a multi-step process to select case study countries; developing a framework to evaluate influenza vaccination program resiliency and response-readiness; and conducting a narrative review and stakeholder consultations to collect information on the case study countries, followed by data analysis and synthesis. The study was conducted between January 2022 and September 2022.

Case study selection

A multi-step process was used to select suitable countries for case study analysis. An initial landscaping of MIC influenza programs was conducted through an extensive literature review of published and grey literature to identify an initial set of candidate countries. From these, we selected those that met the following criteria: a seasonal influenza vaccination program in place for five years or more, indicating an established program; a program wholly or partially funded by the national government, indicating country ownership; and no active conflict in the country (as this could disrupt healthcare infrastructure and services).

We then conducted stakeholder mapping for these countries to identify representatives of ministries, national advisory bodies (e.g., National Immunization Technical Advisory Groups [NITAG]), healthcare providers, academics, and other immunization professionals. Following stakeholder mapping, stakeholders from this second list of candidate countries were invited by email to complete an online scoping survey, to gather further information on the vaccination program to support case study selection and identify potential stakeholders for consultation.

Countries were subsequently selected based on the following final selection criteria:

Narrative review

A narrative literature review for case study countries was conducted via searches of PubMed, Google Scholar, Google News, and social media (X [formerly Twitter], Facebook, and LinkedIn). Searches were structured as follows: [Country name] + influenza-specific terms associated with each of the determinant categories. Separate search terms were used to gather information on COVID-19 vaccination rates in case study countries, using the format: [Country name] + COVID-19-specific terms. The full list of search terms is provided in Additional file 1. This research informed the development of questions posed to stakeholders, which further probed how influenza vaccination infrastructure contributed to COVID-19 response efforts. The inclusion criteria were as follows: each source had to include information on an MIC (with MICs defined by the World Bank criteria [17]), and information relating to the key determinant categories listed in Table 1. The initial narrative review was conducted between January and September 2022, with additional references identified during the data synthesis process to support the findings.

Stakeholder consultations

Insights were primarily gathered via key stakeholder consultations, conducted between June and September 2022. Recruitment of stakeholders drew on the selected country’s stakeholder mapping and snowball sampling through existing contacts. Honoraria were offered to participants in compensation for their time and high level of expertise. Drawing on the literature review, a semi-structured guide was developed for each country in alignment with the six determinant categories. The guide is provided in Additional file 2. We conducted a total of 26 consultations with 27 stakeholders (one consultation was conducted with two stakeholders at the same time), with 24 in English and two in Spanish. The stakeholders included both country-based and regional subject matter experts representing a range of sectors and disciplines, including – but not limited to – government agencies, national immunization advisory committees, multilateral organizations, academia, non-profit organizations, and healthcare providers. All consultations were conducted over Zoom and recorded following a verbal informed consent process. Consultations were transcribed verbatim into English.

Data analysis and synthesis

Analysis teams, comprising groups of 2–3 authors, led the development of each case study using the framework method to analyze the literature review and qualitative codes [23]. Using Dedoose (version 0.9.54), a codebook was developed by seven authors (CH, DC, MD, MMal, NB, TS, and VU) based on the six determinant categories. Each transcript was then coded deductively by a primary coder and a reviewer. Each team member reviewed the coded transcripts, the results of the narrative literature review, and landscaping survey results, with results organized and inputted into an Excel sheet. Based on this information, each team member independently identified key themes related to influenza program resilience and response-readiness, with findings charted by determinant category. Case study country teams subsequently met virtually to compare matrices and reach consensus on the key themes associated with each country’s influenza vaccination program and response to COVID-19. Teams also sought to identify emergent patterns within and across determinant categories. One additional team member attended each analysis team meeting as an observer, to identify cross-cutting themes among all case studies.

Positionality and reflexivity

The study was conducted in close collaboration with stakeholders from the case study countries and was intentionally designed to elicit and reflect their professional experiences, perspectives and insights. Program leaders and stakeholders in LMICs played a central role in shaping the aims and direction of this work, ensuring that the findings reflected their priorities and contexts. Throughout the study, the team sought to identify potential assumptions and biases, and ensured that emerging themes were grounded in the stakeholders’ insights and findings from the literature, rather than the team’s expectations.

Ethics statement

This work was conducted as a public health program evaluation and health systems analysis, drawing on publicly available and secondary data sources and structured consultations with professionals engaged in influenza vaccination programs in their institutional or technical roles. In accordance with standard definitions of human subjects research, this activity did not involve the collection of data about identifiable individuals or private information and therefore did not meet the criteria for human subjects research. As such, institutional review board or research ethics committee approval was not required.

No personal, sensitive, or identifiable information was collected, recorded, or included in this study. Stakeholder consultations were conducted solely to obtain expert perspectives on programmatic and policy considerations rather than personal experiences.

Participation in consultations was voluntary. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to each consultation using a standardized script. Participants were informed of the purpose of the activity, how the information would be used, and their rights to decline participation, withdraw at any time, or refrain from answering specific questions.

To ensure confidentiality and data protection, no names or direct identifiers were recorded, and all findings are presented in aggregate, non-attributable form. Notes from consultations were securely stored and accessible only to the study team.

The project adhered to established ethical principles, including respect for persons, voluntary participation, minimization of risk, and protection of confidentiality.

Results

Case study selection

The first step of the case study selection identified 60 MICs with a seasonal influenza vaccination program. From these, we identified 15 countries that met the criteria of having a seasonal influenza vaccination program in place for five years or more, having a program wholly or partially funded by the national government, and not being engaged in active conflict. From these, countries were selected based on the final selection criteria, informed by findings from the stakeholder mapping and scoping survey (completed by 10 respondents). Five countries, Albania, Bolivia, Brazil, South Africa, and Thailand, were subsequently selected to achieve geographic diversity across regions.

Building research capacity and a clear pathway from evidence to policy creates resilient and responsive influenza programs

All five countries had a national scientific advisory body focused on immunization – the equivalent of a NITAG. These advisory groups are made up of multidisciplinary experts on immunization and act as a bridge between research and policy, advising their national decision-making bodies on vaccines and vaccine introduction, immunization programming and implementation, and infectious diseases. In this analysis, these bodies emerged as a key driver of policy change for influenza. For example, the Ministry of Health (MOH) in Thailand routinely accepts and acts on recommendations set forth by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices of Thailand [25, 26]. All countries in this review have expanded their risk group categories for influenza vaccination or made other program design changes based on evidence that was brought forth to decision-makers through national scientific advisory bodies for immunization.

Monitoring the burden of influenza is foundational to program design, development, and evaluation

All countries have strong surveillance capabilities, connected to either global (e.g., WHO) and/or regional (Pan American Health Organization [PAHO], South-eastern Europe Health Network [SEEHN]) influenza surveillance programs. Surveillance is foundational to the development of any vaccination program. For influenza, surveillance is vital to identify priority risk groups, inform program design, determine seasonality, and guide vaccine formulations [27]. While influenza-specific surveillance in most case study countries is limited by a lack of laboratory testing and diagnostics for influenza, sentinel surveillance for influenza-like illness (ILI), acute respiratory infection (ARI), and severe acute respiratory infection (SARI) takes place across various clinical settings, both public and private.

In South Africa, influenza is not a nationally reported disease, but surveillance nevertheless occurs through four different programs that draw on data from both public and private sectors. This includes data via the earliest known program among the five countries: Viral Watch, a prospective, sentinel surveillance program that began in the early 1980s. Viral Watch collects data on ILI in public and private clinics across the country [28]. Additionally, the South African National Institute for Communicable Disease (NICD) leads syndromic surveillance efforts, including ILI in both public clinics and private outpatient centers across the country. SARI surveillance also occurs in hospitalized patients in public hospitals in four provinces [28], as well as limited private tertiary care settings around the country. Both programs report to the NICD, allowing for data aggregation and integration at the national level.

Thailand runs 40 sentinel surveillance sites through its National Institutes of Health, in collaboration with the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [29, 30], monitoring for ILI in outpatients and SARI in hospitalized patients. Bolivia’s influenza surveillance program began in 2011 and comprises one ILI surveillance site and nine SARI sentinel surveillance sites. The SARI sites are based in hospitals, mainly in urban La Paz and Santa Cruz [31]. Since 1999, Albania has conducted universal ARI syndromic surveillance based on primary care reporting from all general practice, pediatric, and polyclinic facilities, as well as emergency departments. Additionally, the ALERT system monitors ILI in outpatients at two health facilities in urban Tirana during the influenza season. Albania also established SARI surveillance in 2009 in 11 regional and university hospitals, which sample all SARI cases year-round [32]. All countries also report various types of epidemiological data to either regional or global organizations.

The design of country health systems can influence the types of surveillance data that are and can be collected throughout the country to estimate disease burden. Both Brazil and South Africa have health systems that include both public and private sectors. In South Africa, where roughly 80% of the population accesses care through the public sector [33], there are some differences in data collection capabilities that affect the surveillance infrastructure, with stakeholders reporting that testing may be more frequent in the private sector due to its greater resources compared with the public sector. Overall, the countries in this review were found to use sentinel surveillance with a mix of ILI, ARI, SARI, and influenza-specific surveillance to fully estimate the burden of disease.

Data on vaccine acceptance and demand, coverage, and cost-effectiveness provide vital insights to inform risk group prioritization and design resilient influenza vaccination programs

In addition to assessing disease burden, the ongoing collection of data on acceptance and demand, coverage, and cost-effectiveness is vital for effective and responsive influenza systems. These data are important to support prioritization activities, inform communication activities for targeted risk groups, and advocate for policy changes and increased investment in influenza programs. In particular, monitoring vaccination coverage rates in the public and private sectors and setting clear uptake targets can serve as powerful motivators for countries to achieve high coverage rates [16].

As an example, stakeholders noted that Albania’s investment in data collection and evaluation was able to provide the evidence needed to persuade decision-makers to develop more comprehensive influenza policies and increase procurement. Additionally, stakeholders shared that Albania expanded its influenza risk groups from only frontline health workers to include all health workers after WHO’s Flutool revealed the cost-effectiveness of vaccinating every health worker for influenza [34]. A knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) study among pregnant women and their healthcare workers was also used to support the expansion of the influenza program and increased procurement of vaccines [35]. Furthermore, in South Africa, pregnant women and people living with HIV were further prioritized after research indicated that influenza vaccination is especially effective in preventing severe disease among these populations [15].

Diversified procurement enhances program resilience

Insights from the literature review and stakeholder consultations identified that how countries procure vaccine doses has implications for program design, resiliency, and overall population confidence in influenza vaccination. The insights highlight that vaccine confidence and uptake can be negatively impacted by delays in vaccine delivery, and that regional manufacturing and diversified procurement mechanisms help create resilient influenza vaccination programs. In this review, three of the five countries – Albania, Brazil, and Bolivia – regularly procure sufficient influenza vaccines to vaccinate their identified risk group populations. Brazil and Bolivia are both member states of PAHO and can participate in the PAHO Revolving Fund although Brazil’s National Immunization Program purchases influenza vaccines exclusively through the Instituto Butantan. Similarly, Albania has been a part of the Partnership for Influenza Vaccine Introduction (PIVI) since 2016, which assisted with procurement in the beginning of their program, and the country now also procures seasonal influenza vaccines through UNICEF Supply Division’s pooled procurement process. Thailand and South Africa participated in the WHO Global Action Plan on Influenza Vaccines (2006−2016) to develop influenza vaccine production capacity.

Vaccine confidence and uptake can be negatively impacted by delays in vaccine delivery

From the perspective of stakeholders, timely vaccine delivery is essential for maintaining confidence in influenza vaccination. Efforts to build demand for influenza vaccination lead to increased public interest, but if people seek the vaccine and find it unavailable, it can undermine motivation and erode trust in the program. To mitigate this, a stakeholder from Bolivia noted that their promotion of the influenza vaccine each year only begins once they have received vaccine doses and can confirm the vaccine is available.

Several stakeholders raised concerns about delays in receiving influenza vaccine doses. Stakeholders from South Africa, Albania, and Thailand discussed delays in the delivery of vaccine doses. Two stakeholders from South Africa described the administrative and bureaucratic system for importing doses, which regularly causes delays in the delivery of vaccine doses. As noted by a stakeholder, seasonal influenza vaccines are considered new products each year as their composition changes annually. As a result, South Africa must go through a lengthy regulatory process to launch the vaccine as a new product each year, with the stakeholder describing it as a “very stressful and very difficult market”.

Another stakeholder from South Africa described some of the challenges faced when procuring vaccines from manufacturers in the Northern Hemisphere. The stakeholder noted that once all the paperwork is complete for the importation and delivery of vaccines into the Southern Hemisphere, the arrival of vaccine doses is often delayed, because of the time to release and deliver the vaccines: “So it’s all got to do with the paperwork of the importation, the release and the steps to get Northern Hemisphere, Southern Hemisphere vaccines released to the north, to the south and in time. So we had delays all the time in getting the final vaccines in country”.

Similarly, a stakeholder from Albania commented on how this delay is “demotivating” for the population: “They are asking all the time since mid-September and then we postpone and postpone”. Together, these insights underline how procurement processes and activities to generate demand for vaccination (i.e., demand generation) must work hand-in-hand to maintain confidence in the influenza vaccination program.

Regional manufacturing capacity and diversified procurement systems, including pooled procurement, may safeguard programs from external market pressures and emergencies

Countries that successfully secure sufficient vaccine supply tend to procure from a diversified set of manufacturers, including public-private partnerships and local or regional manufacturing schemes. For example, Albania procures doses through UNICEF Supply Division, previously received donations from PIVI, and has influenza vaccines also available in the private market (via private pharmacies and clinics). Accessing influenza vaccines through three different sources gives Albania the flexibility to meet its needs during unexpected delays in any supplier. As one stakeholder noted: “In PIVI in 2018, we had a very unfortunate … vaccine importing hurdle that just our vaccines got stuck in the wrong place and the manufacturer was not able to ship … It was the primary impetus around the shift in our PIVI strategy to focus more on meeting our annual needs with UNICEF’s [vaccine doses] rather than directly with manufacturers, because that was such a blow to the program.” Similar sentiments were found in Brazil, with one stakeholder commenting on Brazil’s domestically manufactured influenza doses: “And it’s important for the Brazilian government to have this auto sufficient model … If you depend on another supplier, you may be in trouble like it happened with COVID-19 vaccine.” The stakeholder also noted that depending on “only the private sector” could be dangerous. Establishing domestic manufacturing capacity would reduce this risk.

In Bolivia, all influenza vaccines are procured through a PAHO pooled procurement scheme known as the Revolving Fund. The Bolivian MOH requests the number of needed influenza doses from the Revolving Fund, which in turn, presents the MOH with options for purchase. Such regional procurement systems can shorten the ‘distance’ between manufacture and dose delivery, which can provide some safeguards against emergencies or other market pressures that could cause delivery delays. These vaccines, from different manufacturers, are distributed free of charge through the public sector to members of designated risk groups.

Regional manufacturing capacity also plays a vital role in enhancing supply chain resilience and response-readiness. Brazil’s Instituto Butantan, one of the largest influenza vaccine manufacturers in the Southern Hemisphere, can currently produce up to 140 million trivalent influenza vaccines annually, addressing both the Southern and Northern Hemisphere influenza seasons [36]. The trivalent influenza vaccine manufactured by Instituto Butantan has received WHO prequalification and is exported to countries in Latin America via a PAHO tender [37]. Brazil currently produces all the trivalent influenza vaccines its public system requires and maintains sufficient capacity to meet increased demand in the event of an influenza pandemic [38]. The country is also producing A(H5) influenza vaccines as part of pandemic preparedness. In response to the 2009 H1N1 pandemic, Brazil produced and administered over 89 million doses of influenza vaccine, enabling the country to surpass immunization targets among several priority groups, including health workers [39].

Healthcare workers are key program implementation partners

Across the five countries, healthcare workers emerged as essential partners for implementing resilient influenza vaccination programs, across the following subthemes.

Community trust in healthcare workers is foundational to successful influenza vaccination programs and pandemic preparedness

Strong trust in healthcare workers was reported in all five countries from the stakeholders and literature. Not only do healthcare workers play a crucial role in driving influenza vaccine demand through their contributions to immunization campaigns and one-on-one clinical encounters, they are also widely considered the most trusted source of reliable vaccine information within communities across the board. A stakeholder from South Africa described the ways in which community members perceive recommendations from healthcare workers on vaccination: “Yeah, especially in the rural areas or in the township, the healthcare professional is trusted. You’re unlikely to get hesitancy or questions around, “Do I need this?”. Community health workers have historically played an important role in South Africa’s health system, serving as a bridge between communities and the health system [40]. High levels of interpersonal trust have been noted between community health workers and their patients in the country [41].

Further, both the literature review and stakeholder consultations suggest that risk groups are more likely to accept vaccination if recommended by a health worker [42]. For example, two stakeholders, from South Africa and Albania respectively, describe this trust dynamic: “when you advise them [on vaccination], they will not say no, as long as their doctor says, ‘You need influenza vaccine. I’m writing it up as part of your prescription to go out, someone will give it to you,’ they will give it to them”. A second stakeholder shared ““We did a KAP survey … And within the KAP survey, the most trusted source were healthcare workers. The stakeholder noted that this indicates people have trust in government services. Health workers’ perceptions of the severity of a vaccine-preventable disease can also influence recommendations. A study from Thailand found that physicians who recognized the increased risk of severe influenza in pregnant women were significantly more likely to recommend vaccination for pregnant patients [43]. Thailand’s Ministry of Public Health has recommended influenza vaccination for pregnant women during the second and third trimesters, with pregnant women included in the highest priority group for influenza vaccination [43]. Despite this, only 25% of surveyed physicians practicing at antenatal care clinics reported routinely recommending influenza vaccine to pregnant women [43]. Trust in health workers and other professionals who support and implement the vaccination program therefore appears to be a critical asset for program success.

Health workers are resilient and committed, but need continuous training and support

We found numerous examples of healthcare workers displaying resiliency in their contributions to the implementation of seasonal influenza vaccination programs. In every case study country, we heard examples of health worker resiliency in delivering influenza vaccination and other life-saving care in the face of challenges. For example, Brazil’s national vaccination campaign was abruptly defunded in 2016 [44], which stripped all national funding to support demand generation activities for influenza vaccines. We heard from several stakeholders that despite this, health workers would lead their own campaigns in their constituencies to encourage influenza vaccination. One stakeholder discussed some of the strategies various close-to-community professionals used to ensure that communications campaigns continued for the influenza program, describing them as “secret vaccine campaigns”. This same stakeholder an academic physician, also noted that relevant professional societies, for healthcare professionals in pediatrics and gerontology, have filled the gap left by this programmatic change by conducting communication strategies. These findings highlight the deep commitment of many health workers and clinicians to protecting their patients.

Nevertheless, health workers also require support and continuous training to successfully contribute to program implementation. Case study countries offered various examples of health worker training. Albania, for example, has developed a program of ‘influenza vaccination champions’ that takes advantage of health workers who were already making strides in promoting influenza vaccination among patients: “We monitored the coverage, but also the work done in different districts or hospitals and we have seen differences. So some people were more motivated to distribute information materials, to work with them, to promote, to have other materials other than we provided … we started to think about [how] to make some champions … we identified nurses or doctors that were promoting influenza vaccination … And it was like a competition among healthcare workers.” Such programs serve to ensure continuous health worker engagement in program implementation. Offering incentives is another tactic countries use to support health workers, as seen in Bolivia, where a stakeholder noted that nurses and nursing assistants were given bonuses for involvement in the vaccination program.

MOH and other government entities were found to organize and implement various types of health worker training, with many of them responding to previously identified health worker needs. For example, Albania conducts training sessions for health workers in effectively engaging with anti-vaccine activists [45], while Bolivia trains health workers to administer vaccines in rural and underserved populations. In South Africa, limited funding for the NICD prevents broad influenza vaccination training programs for health workers. However, stakeholders noted that the NICD manages a large and growing listserv (electronic mailing list) of roughly 8000 health workers that regularly pushes out influenza campaign materials, program updates, and external training opportunities.

All case study countries also reported efforts to train additional qualified vaccinators, including pharmacists and community health workers, to increase physical access to vaccines and vaccinate hard-to-reach populations. In Bolivia, a stakeholder noted how trained vaccinators are important to provide vaccine access for rural populations. The stakeholder also shared that health workers receive training in vaccine storage and preservation, thus empowering them to effectively administer and safely transport vaccines to the hardest to reach communities. However, some countries may require new laws to allow influenza vaccination in pharmacies and administered by different cadres of health workers. Of the study countries, only Brazil and South Africa are noted as having clear legal frameworks in place for pharmacists to administer vaccines [46].

Sustained investment in demand generation is vital to drive and maintain vaccine uptake

Demand generation refers to activities and strategies to increase people’s motivation to seek, support, and/or advocate for vaccines and immunization services [47]. The five countries have adopted a variety of methods towards their demand generation activities, from employing celebrities and social media influencers, to utilizing mascots and slogans to encourage vaccination. We found that collaborating and partnering with community leaders and disseminating messages in local languages can be effective strategies in reaching remote or minority populations. WHO recommends that countries use the behavioral and social drivers of vaccination tools and framework to understand drivers of vaccine uptake and design tailored demand generation activities and interventions [48].

Investment in demand generation at the national level can help build trust in vaccination

All case study countries invested in demand generation activities at the national level as part of their overall influenza vaccination program. Implementation of these activities include a large variety of methods that seek to build influenza vaccine confidence and demand among prioritized risk groups. For example, Brazil has long implemented successful and far-reaching communication and outreach campaigns to build confidence in vaccination. Included in this has been specific programs targeted to remote and otherwise marginalized populations, as reported by a stakeholder: “… an outreach program to reach the hard-to-reach populations that means indigenous communities in the middle of the Amazon Forest … it used to be one program that reached all places of Brazil, the whole population and the trust was built”. As noted, efforts to reach the hardest-to-reach communities with outreach and demand generation activities also built trust. Furthermore, the Brazilian Government has employed celebrities as part of their campaigns, in addition to television commercials, social media, radio announcements, and even created a national vaccination mascot that gained popularity among children [49]. Brazil’s National Immunization Program is notable in that it is conducted over a tight timeframe (4 weeks).

In South Africa, the private and public sectors run their own independent demand generation campaigns. In the private sector, Clicks and Dis-Chem, two large pharmacy chains that distribute influenza vaccine, sponsor annual influenza vaccination campaigns to encourage people to get vaccinated. In the country’s public sector, NICD uses both social media and traditional media—broadcast news, radio, and print—to raise awareness and drive influenza vaccine uptake. Stakeholders noted that the overall campaign is evaluated at the national level each year, using traditional media monitoring, social media monitoring, as well as through analyzing the positive or negative sentiments towards the materials expressed in text.

Thailand boasts a particularly innovative strategy for demand generation. While only nurses and physicians are allowed to administer vaccines, a network of 1 million village health volunteers are trained to increase awareness, especially during outbreak response [50, 51]. Albania’s approach aims to encourage health workers to set immunization targets and communicate influenza success stories to the public. The government also runs promotional campaigns on social media and through TV advertisements as described by one stakeholder: “And thanks to these promotional campaigns that went for months with … appearances of minister of health, and also of director and the specialist of Public Health Institute in all TV channels”.

Bolivia’s influenza campaign focuses on radio and television to inform the public about the availability and benefits of influenza vaccination. Radio adverts are widely used to target commuters to and within urban settings. Local health facilities display banners and posters, distribute brochures, and send letters to spread the word on when and where people can get vaccines.

Demand generation activities should be linked with procurement to ensure uptake

Our analysis of case study literature and stakeholder consultations indicates that linking demand generation activities to procurement is critical to ensure adequate vaccine coverage, maintain and improve vaccine confidence, and avoid dose wastage. The various demand generation methods adopted by countries require financial and technical support to execute and as a result, the cost of running seasonal campaigns (operational costs) and promoting influenza vaccine must be included as part of the influenza program budget.

There is compelling evidence from literature and stakeholder consultations supporting the integration of demand generation activities with supply. For example, stakeholders noted that Albania factored in demand among risk groups when determining how many vaccines to procure. In Brazil, the influenza immunization budget comprised two-line items: procurement and vaccination support. Brazil’s prediction of influenza vaccine demand is based on estimates of the number of each risk group in municipalities. These estimates are then shared with the MOH and Instituto Butantan to guide procurement decisions. A drawback of this model is the potential for vaccine dose wastage if uptake among members of the targeted risk groups is low. Brazil faced additional hurdles with a new government administration implementing substantial cuts in funding for communication and awareness campaigns, with MOH immunization campaigns replaced with anti-vaccine and COVID-era misinformation. A stakeholder explained: “So, it’s before 2016, but until then we still had a media campaign every year when we launched the influenza vaccine campaign, it was on TV, in social media, and so people were all the time reminded that we need to vaccinate, and then they stopped doing that in 2016. We don’t have media campaigns within the vaccine campaigns anymore, so that disappeared, and the anti-vax messages increased”.

Cross-cutting collaboration between institutions, regional departments, and governments at the national level can help connect demand generation activities with supply. In Bolivia, the MOH collaborates with regional department-level governments to decide on procurement rates and vaccine mandates, but regional departments independently determine policies for campaigns and mobilization. Each department-level government manages its own health service, called the Servicio Departamental de Salud. This led to disparities in awareness and immunization activities across departments and in the resources required to run campaigns or mass vaccination events. Stakeholders noted that districts with wealthy urban centers or with greater tourism income hosted vaccination campaigns and events, while rural districts and those with limited resources were less likely to support influenza programming. A stakeholder noted “.some departments such as Santa Cruz, La Paz, Cochabamba, that are the best known in Bolivia, they are the departments that get the highest financing because of their own resources coming from tourism, etc. But if you think of Pando, Beni, and Tarija, those are departments that have fewer resources available, less access to communities for communities, and fewer resources to be able to identify uptake or also to implement mass vaccination against one or other diseases.” However, the Servicio Departamental de Salud districts arrange to share vaccine doses among themselves, a practice that reduces wastage and supports districts where vaccine demand exceeds forecasts.

In Thailand, the influenza vaccination system has historically provided vaccines via a walk-in, first-come-first-served model [52]. In 2020, the walk-in system was replaced by an online registration system in the Bangkok health region, which resulted in increased influenza vaccine coverage in the area [52]. The increase in seasonal influenza vaccine uptake with the online system was especially evident among primary level health facilities and in high-risk groups of individuals with obesity and the elderly [52]. The authors suggest that the online system may improve access to vaccination services for high-risk individuals who do not visit the health system regularly [52]. In South Africa, a study of public influenza vaccination in antenatal clinics identified vaccine stock-outs as a major factor behind non-vaccination [53]. While the campaign helped vaccinate three quarters of all pregnant women presenting at the selected clinics between 2015 and 2018, half of the unvaccinated pregnant women reported lack of availability and vaccine stock-outs at the clinic as reasons behind not being vaccinated [53]. Additionally, several stakeholders shared how health centers received influenza vaccine late in the season, reducing demand for and potentially the utility of immunization. “And then another challenge that we have been experiencing, and it’s not only this year, but historically, is that we receive the vaccines late. We receive them far into May, almost June … and then there’s the perception that it’s now too late to get vaccinated” a stakeholder shared.

Stakeholders also noted that South Africa’s low level of procurement compared to the size of its risk-group population causes challenges throughout its influenza immunization program. On the contrary, in Brazil, manufacturing capacity over the years has expanded to accommodate ongoing increases in demand for influenza vaccines and for pandemic preparedness resulting in historically high coverage rates up until 2019 [54].

Seasonal influenza vaccination infrastructure lays the foundation for pandemic preparedness and response

Stakeholders from all five case study countries noted that their influenza vaccination programs, including regional partnerships and existing infrastructure, helped them respond more quickly to the COVID-19 pandemic. For example, in Albania, South Africa, Brazil, and Thailand, the existing surveillance and data sharing infrastructure for influenza provided the foundation for developing COVID-19 surveillance capacity. Surveillance data are vital for immunization programs, to evaluate vaccine effectiveness and inform vaccine composition, policy and delivery [55].

In Albania, investment in the Southeast European Center for Surveillance and Control of Infectious Diseases (SECID) during the 10 years prior to the COVID-19 pandemic has strengthened sentinel surveillance and standard operating procedures, increased laboratory capacity and expanded human resources [56]. SECID was established in 2013 as a key component of the Regional Health Development Center of Surveillance and Control of Infectious Diseases in Tirana, Albania, under its parent organization the Southeast European Health Network [57]. These enhancements were crucial to Albania’s pandemic response in 2020 and beyond, as noted by a stakeholder: “We are very proud to say that during this year [2022], the surveillance system for flu has been strengthened and enforced and actually it helped a lot during the pandemic because it was the same infrastructure in terms of laboratory people and the flow of work. And so many things that … have been done through the year, this infrastructure and human resources served to work with the COVID pandemic as well.” As this Albanian example shows, sustainably investing in and maintaining the seasonal influenza laboratory infrastructure, supply chains and human resource expertise is also an investment in pandemic preparedness. Seasonal influenza vaccination program resources and expertise can be leveraged quickly and effectively to respond to emerging or re-emerging infectious diseases. In a qualitative study of 11 countries, including Albania, stakeholders described how key components of seasonal influenza vaccination programs were leveraged for the emergency vaccination of health workers during the COVID-19 pandemic [58].

A key component of successful COVID-19 mitigation was the real-time sharing of information, especially among neighboring countries. Before the pandemic, Albania shared surveillance data and hosted meetings to collaborate with neighboring countries, including North Macedonia, Kosovo, Montenegro, Serbia and Croatia. Building these networks during ‘peacetime’ can help establish and sustain trust among neighboring countries, which is essential to effectively addressing regional needs during a crisis like the COVID-19 pandemic. Similarly, Thailand integrated SARS-CoV-2 testing into existing respiratory syndromic surveillance (ARI/ILI and influenza sentinel systems) and used this to adapt their COVID-19 vaccine delivery strategy, leveraging established national immunization program infrastructure for rollout. A stakeholder shared that “… because of the surveillance system and EPI infrastructure established before the pandemic, it was easy to provide COVID vaccines during the pandemic”.

Influenza has been part of Thailand’s EPI program since 2004, which has also provided an effective platform for influenza awareness and educational campaigns, as shared by stakeholders. In fact, Thailand views investment in influenza vaccines as a part of their overall national security, locating the management of their program in the National Health Security Office. One stakeholder stated that “Investing in influenza vaccines is something like buying state-of-the-art arms or submarines … for the security preparedness in relation to health security”. Existing influenza vaccine management strategies and structures helped the country rapidly achieve high COVID-19 vaccination coverage once sufficient doses became available [59].

Similar to Albania, South Africa has made investments over the years to strengthen their surveillance capacity, particularly their genomic sequencing technology, and surveillance for emerging or re-emerging infectious diseases such as influenza. In South Africa, a strong and well-supported genomic surveillance program, in place since 2007, contributed to the early identification of the Delta and Omicron variants of SARS-CoV-2 [60].

Discussion

This study revealed key factors contributing to seasonal influenza vaccination program resiliency and response-readiness across five middle-income case study countries. The findings provide valuable insights into the attributes underpinning resilient and response-ready influenza vaccination programs in MIC settings, which can be used to guide further efforts to strengthen influenza programs. Five key themes emerged as consistent indicators of resilient and response-ready influenza vaccination programs. Firstly, building research capacity and a clear pathway from evidence to policy helps create influenza vaccination programs that are resilient and responsive to the needs of the population and health system. As a result, countries need to strengthen surveillance and research capacity, as well as a pathway to connect evidence to policy, to build influenza vaccination programs that are efficient and responsive to country demands and emerging threats. Within this, systems to monitor the burden of influenza are vital for well-designed and resilient programs. Data on cost-effectiveness as well as vaccine acceptance and demand by health workers and vaccine recipients are also important to inform risk group decisions and advocate for policy changes to support influenza vaccination programs.

Secondly, the evidence indicates that regional manufacturing and diversified procurement mechanisms help ensure a reliable supply of influenza vaccines, which in turn supports a resilient vaccination program. In addition, multi-year contracts and pooled procurement (e.g., participating in the UNICEF Supply Division’s procurement of influenza vaccines) can greatly reduce the cost of influenza vaccines. Findings across the case study countries highlighted that the way in which countries manufacture and/or procure vaccine doses has implications for program design, resiliency, and the overall population confidence in influenza vaccination. While in-country manufacturing may not be feasible for most countries, regional manufacturing capacity can bolster supply security and enhance regional ability to meet demand during emerging outbreaks and pandemics – as seen with Brazil’s Instituto Butantan. Stakeholders noted that insufficient or delayed vaccine procurement could impact vaccine confidence and uptake in their countries. As a result, procurement processes and demand generation efforts must be closely coordinated to sustain confidence in the influenza vaccination program. Thirdly, health workers emerged as essential partners for implementing resilient influenza vaccination programs, as trusted sources of reliable vaccine information, and critical assets to drive demand and uptake of influenza vaccination. However, healthcare workers require ongoing training and support to provide them with the skills and resources to effectively communicate with and engage the public on influenza vaccination.

Fourthly, demand generation is key to driving and maintaining influenza vaccine uptake. A resilient seasonal influenza vaccination program is only effective when there is robust demand for and subsequent uptake of vaccines. Across country case studies, national-level investment in demand generation emerged as a common activity to build trust in vaccination and higher immunization rates. Countries employed diverse demand generation activities, with a mix of both national and locally targeted communication and outreach strategies. Finally, building and maintaining seasonal influenza vaccine infrastructure contributes to pandemic preparedness, and countries noted how investments in the seasonal influenza vaccination program and surveillance capacity enabled a more rapid response to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Together, the evaluation highlights cross-cutting themes that underpin resilient and response-ready seasonal influenza vaccination programs in MICs, as well as the context-specific factors and challenges faced by diverse MICs. Our findings add to the body of literature on factors contributing to robust and resilient influenza vaccination programs in low- and high-income countries [5, 16]. Some of the themes elucidated in this review overlap with those identified in other studies, for example, on the importance of health workers as key drivers of vaccine demand [16]. Our findings also align with broader research showing that seasonal influenza vaccination programs can strengthen the acceptance, uptake and delivery of other vaccination programs, and improve pandemic preparedness by leveraging existing infrastructure, resources and expertise [61–63]. One analysis found that the presence of a mature influenza vaccination program was significantly associated with greater national COVID-19 vaccination capacities and higher COVID-19 vaccination coverage [64].

Our review also identified factors that may be unique to MIC settings and/or the targeted countries. For example, MICs often rely on a mix of public, private and donor funding to support their national immunization programs, whereas high-income countries typically have more robust domestic budgets for these programs. In contrast, low-income countries benefit from Gavi support, which is not available to MICs. Additionally, some MICs may have only recently transitioned out of Gavi support to other vaccination programs, and as a result, may lack domestic resource mobilization or the strategic planning needed to ensure the sustainability of certain health programs, particularly for adult vaccines. Nevertheless, the findings provide insights into the ways in which countries implement and deliver influenza vaccination programming.

Beyond the cross-cutting themes, the findings reveal the complexities that arise when influenza vaccination is delivered through both private and public programs. These parallel systems can in some cases create disparities in access (depending on ability to pay and resources) and fragmentation of efforts. In a review of 194 WHO Member States in 2022, 68 countries reported having an influenza vaccination policy for the public sector, 53 for the public and private sectors, and two exclusively for the private sector [13]. Given the prevalence of mixed public and private influenza vaccination programs, future efforts to strengthen influenza vaccination should seek to coordinate and harmonize activities and data between sectors. Findings from this study also emphasize the critical role of political will in shaping influenza vaccination programming and broader health systems. Insights from several countries demonstrated how changes in government and/or priorities can lead to backsliding in influenza vaccination demand, coverage and system improvements. Consequently, the insights underscore the importance of capitalizing upon periods of stability to invest in influenza vaccination programming, so the system and people’s trust and demand for vaccination remains robust, even in times of global health crises or political upheaval.

Altogether, insights from this review can be instructive for MICs in developing vaccination programs across the life course and building vaccination systems capable of swiftly responding to emerging health threats. Strengthening influenza vaccination is a key component of the Global Influenza Strategy 2019 − 2030 and the Immunization Agenda 2030, which aims to ensure everyone, everywhere has access to vaccines [3]. Key priorities of the Immunization Agenda also include integrating health service delivery and strengthening immunization systems for outbreak response and public health emergencies. To this aim, countries can enhance vaccine access and coverage while achieving cost-savings by leveraging shared human and financial resources to deliver influenza as part of a unified immunization program alongside other seasonal and life-course immunizations. Importantly, as well as strengthening immunization program delivery, integrating influenza as part of life-course immunizations can improve overall resiliency and readiness to respond to outbreaks and pandemic threats. Insights from the review highlighted how investments in influenza immunization delivery and surveillance aided a rapid response to the COVID-19 pandemic. Before the next serious outbreak or pandemic emerges, it is vital that countries invest in robust vaccine delivery and surveillance systems capable of responding quickly and efficiently.

Some limitations in this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, stakeholder mapping outreach was limited to those with publicly available or otherwise known email addresses. Secondly, the literature review was conducted with English-language sources only, excluding literature in local languages. In addition, although efforts were made to select geographically diverse countries, the nature of a case study evaluation may limit the generalizability of insights. Although this study engaged stakeholders from diverse sources (including non-governmental bodies), some degree of reporting bias may have occurred. Additionally, while our evaluation was designed to assess program resiliency under routine conditions, examining influenza vaccination program performance during periods of conflict is an important direction for future research. Nevertheless, the case study design provides insights across diverse MICs, highlighting common themes and context-specific factors that impact influenza vaccination program resilience and response-readiness.

Conclusions

This study examined the structural and programmatic factors underpinning the resiliency and response-readiness of influenza vaccination programs across five MIC case studies: Albania, Bolivia, Brazil, South Africa, and Thailand. From this analysis, five key themes emerged as consistent indicators of resilient and response-ready influenza vaccination programs. These were that: 1) building research capacity and a clear pathway from evidence to policy creates resilient and responsive influenza programs; 2) diversified procurement mechanisms help ensure a reliable supply of influenza vaccines; 3) healthcare workers are essential partners for implementing resilient influenza vaccination programs; 4) demand generation is key to driving and maintaining influenza vaccine uptake; and 5) building and maintaining seasonal influenza vaccine infrastructure contributes to pandemic preparedness and response. These findings provide timely, actionable guidance for countries with similar governance and health systems contexts to improve influenza vaccination. As countries consistently grapple with constrained resources and shifting health priorities, investments and commitments to these programmatic constructs can strengthen service delivery and build resiliency and readiness to respond to future outbreaks and pandemics.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This work was conducted as a public health program evaluation and health systems analysis, drawing on publicly available and secondary data sources and structured consultations with professionals engaged in influenza vaccination programs in their institutional or technical roles. In accordance with standard definitions of human subjects research, this activity did not involve the collection of data about identifiable individuals or private information and therefore did not meet the criteria for human subjects research. As such, institutional review board or research ethics committee approval was not required. No personal, sensitive, or identifiable information was collected, recorded, or included in this study. Stakeholder consultations were conducted solely to obtain expert perspectives on programmatic and policy considerations rather than personal experiences. Participation in consultations was voluntary. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to each consultation using a standardized script. Participants were informed of the purpose of the activity, how the information would be used, and their rights to decline participation, withdraw at any time, or refrain from answering specific questions. To ensure confidentiality and data protection, no names or direct identifiers were recorded, and all findings are presented in aggregate, non-attributable form. Notes from consultations were securely stored and accessible only to the study team. The project adhered to established ethical principles, including respect for persons, voluntary participation, minimization of risk, and protection of confidentiality.
