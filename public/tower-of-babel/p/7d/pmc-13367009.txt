Chemoproteomic profiling of adaptive ATP-binding protein activity in Enterococcus faecium in response to host-derived cues
Authors: Jeanette S Grunnvåg, Kristin Hegstad, Stephen Dela Ahator, Christian S Lentz, Breck Duerkop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Enterococcus faecium has become a significant nosocomial and opportunistic pathogen, increasingly implicated in severe infections such as urinary tract infections, bacteraemia, and endocarditis. Due to acquired resistance to vancomycin and ampicillin and high intrinsic resistance to other antibiotics, E. faecium infections are often challenging to treat. However, relatively little is known about the molecular factors used by E. faecium during infection. Here, we employed desthiobiotin-ATP as an activity-based probe to investigate the activity profile of ATP-binding proteins in E. faecium upon growth in standard rich laboratory media and in response to host-derived cues. We present a chemoproteomic dataset containing 230 selectively enriched putative ATP-binding proteins. Thirty-one proteins were specifically activated in response to human colonic organoid extract, 19 in response to human serum including the ABC transporter, TcyA, and a sensor histidine kinase, ArlS. Our findings suggest that E. faecium adapts its metabolic and regulatory pathways in response to host-derived signals from human serum and organoid extract, which could be crucial for its survival and pathogenicity in various host tissues. Our study highlights the potential relevance of TcyA and ArlS, that have been implicated in virulence in other pathogens, for further functional characterization in E. faecium.Keywords: desthiobiotin-ATP, activity-based protein profiling (ABPP), kinase, ABC-transporter, two-component system

Introduction

Enterococcus faecium has emerged as a prominent nosocomial and opportunistic pathogen, increasingly associated with infections such as urinary tract infections, bacteraemia, and endocarditis (Johnson 1994, Thurlow et al. 2010, Heikens et al. 2011, Pericás et al. 2015, Paganelli et al. 2016). This organism exhibits intrinsic resistance to numerous antibiotics and demonstrates a remarkable capacity for acquiring additional resistance mechanisms (Palmer et al. 2010, Lebreton et al. 2017). Historically, accountable for merely 5% of enterococcal infections, E. faecium now constitutes over 30% of such cases (Top et al. 2007). In contrast to E. faecalis, which has traditionally been the predominant agent of enterococcal infections, E. faecium is more adept at acquiring new resistance determinants, including resistance to vancomycin (Kempf et al. 2008, Ubeda et al. 2010, Cattoir and Leclercq 2012, Mutters et al. 2013) and ampicillin (Rice et al. 2001, Galloway-Pena et al. 2011, Zhang et al. 2012, Pietta et al. 2014, Novais et al. 2016), thereby complicating therapeutic interventions.

Targeting virulence factors has gained increased focus as an alternative means of mitigating the pathogenicity of bacteria, without the added selection pressure imposed by bactericidal and bacteriostatic antibiotics. Given the adaptability of E. faecium, which has evolved from a harmless commensal organism to a priority pathogen (Van Tyne and Gilmore 2014, Guzman Prieto et al. 2016), mechanisms underlying the ability of E. faecium to alter virulence and resistance characteristics are particularly interesting. However, in E. faecium there is limited knowledge on virulence factors beyond adhesion (Heikens et al. 2011, Somarajan et al. 2015, Montealegre et al. 2016), bacterial proliferation (Wagner et al. 2018), carbohydrate metabolism (Paganelli et al. 2016) and biofilm formation (Di Rosa et al. 2006, Mohamed and Huang 2007, Thurlow et al. 2010, Almohamad et al. 2014, Paganelli et al. 2016). Other virulence factors have largely been inferred from genomic studies indicating the presence of potential virulence genes in clinical strains compared to commensal strains.

Among bacterial proteins that may serve as promising anti-virulence targets are the ATP-binding proteins, which are essential for processes such as transport, signal transduction and DNA repair. A good example is the histidine kinases of two-component systems (TCSs) that are central in bacterial signal transduction (Olivera et al. 2010). Many pathogens rely on TCSs for the regulation of genes involved in virulence (Beier and Gross 2006), biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance, making them proteins of interest in anti-virulence research or for studying bacterial adaptation to antibiotics (Arthur et al. 1992, Guzmán Prieto et al. 2017, Kellogg et al. 2017, Guffey and Loll 2021, Ahator et al. 2024b). Importantly, since humans lack analogous systems, selective inhibition is more likely (Capra and Laub 2012). Another relevant group are the ATP-Binding Cassette (ABC) transporters, which utilize the energy from ATP to transport various molecules across the cell membrane and play crucial parts in for instance nutrient uptake, toxin export, and have been shown to facilitate antibiotic resistance (Burnie et al. 2002, Rees et al. 2009, Orelle et al. 2019). A number of these ATP-binding proteins, given their importance for bacterial survival and adaptation, could be interesting for further study in bacterial physiology, adaptation and potential antibacterial strategies.

To further explore the activity of ATP-binding proteins in E. faecium we here present a chemoproteomic study identifying previously uncharacterized or underexplored E. faecium enzymes through activity-based protein profiling (ABPP). ABPP is a chemoproteomic method that utilizes electrophilic small molecule probes, known as activity-based probes (ABPs), which specifically and covalently bind to enzymes when catalytically active (Fig. 1a), allowing for quantification and identification of their activity profiles (Jessani and Cravatt 2004, Barglow and Cravatt 2007, Speers and Cravatt 2009, Lentz et al. 2018, Uddin et al. 2025). Recently, we have performed ABPP with fluorophosphonate-probes to identify novel enzymes of the family of serine hydrolases across E. faecium and E. lactis strains. (Grunnvåg et al. 2024)

We specifically targeted active ATP-binding proteins in E. faecium using the ABP desthiobiotin-ATP within a chemical proteomics framework. This study was designed to assess the activity response of ATP-binding proteins in E. faecium cultured in Brain–Heart-Infusion (BHI) media supplemented with human serum (HS) and organoid extract (OE) derived from a colon organoid model. The objective was to identify ATP-binding proteins and characterize their adaptive responses in E. faecium, providing a basis for future studies to investigate their role in bacterial fitness and virulence.

Materials and methods

Growth conditions and bacterial strain

For this study, three independent chemoproteomic experiments were performed. In each experiment, the clinical vancomycin-resistant E. faecium strain E745 was plated onto BHI agar plates and grown overnight (18–20 h) at 37°C. On the following day, three biological replicates per experiment were prepared in parallel tubes by resuspending a single colony in 5 ml liquid culture media, growing overnight (18–20 h) at 37°C, shaking at 200 rpm.

The liquid culture media differed between experiments and consisted of either (i) pure BHI alone, (ii) BHI with 20% human serum (human male AB plasma, sterile-filtered, Sigma–Aldrich) or (iii) with BHI with 10% supernatant from the apical side of a human colonic organoid culture at 100% differentiation of cells, and 70%–100% confluency (kindly provided by Marco Viveen, Rob Willems, UMC Utrecht, The Netherlands). For these experiments the clonal human-derived colonic organoid cell line Pt15-70206 was used to grow colonic epithelium, following described protocols (Vonk et al. 2020). Cells used for the organoid extract preparation were differentiated in differentiation medium (F12 glutamax, HEPES final conc. 15%, R-spondin-1 conditioned medium final conc. 25%, Noggin conditioned medium at final conc. 10%, B27 final conc. 2%, N-acetylcysteine final conc. 1.25 mM, mEGF final conc. 50 ng/ml, A83-01 final conc. 500 nM, DMEM final conc. 50%). The culture supernatant was filter-sterilized prior to use using a Steriflip® vacuum tube-top filter (0.22 µm pore size; Millipore). The use of human serum and organoid extract was intended to introduce host-associated molecular cues, representing circulating and intestinal environments, respectively, while maintaining sufficient nutrient availability for bacterial growth.

Desthiobiotin-ATP labelling and sample preparation for mass spectrometry

Buffers, reagents and samples were kept ice cold. The bacterial overnight cultures of strain E745 had an OD600 of 3.2, 2.9, and 3.0 in BHI, HS and OE, respectively, were spun down (10 000 g, 5 min) and resuspended in 1 ml Pierce IP Lysis buffer (with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Pierce Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Mini Tablets, EDTA-free, Thermo Fisher). The experimental design included six samples, divided equally into three samples for labelling and three control samples. The bacterial suspension was transferred into 2-ml screw-cap tubes containing 100 µl of 0.1-mm glass beads and lysed by bead-beating (10 000 rpm, 9 cycles of 30 s, 1 min pause between each cycle). Samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 4°C and 16 000 g. The supernatant was transferred to new tubes.

The lysis buffer was exchanged for the reaction buffer (4 M Urea/IP lysis buffer) using the Zeba Spin Desalting Columns (Thermo Fisher). Following buffer exchange, the lysate protein concentration was measured using the Qubit protein assay kit. The protein concentration was usually in the range of 0.65–0.7 mg/ml. For all samples 500 µl was transferred to fresh tubes. Each sample was mixed with 10 µl of 1 M MgCl2 and incubated for 1 min at room temperature. Prior to labelling, control samples were treated with 10 µl of 1 mM ATP (final conc. 20 µM) to block the active sites of potential targets, while an equivalent volume of water was added to the labelling samples. All samples were incubated for 10 min at room temperature.

The ActivX desthiobiotin-ATP probe (Thermo Fisher) was prepared in ultrapure water to a concentration of 1 mM, and 10 µl was added to each sample (final conc. 20 µM) followed by incubation for 10 min at room temperature. Following labelling, 50 ml of 50% high-capacity streptavidin agarose resin slurry was added to each sample and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with constant mixing on a rotator. The samples were centrifuged at 1000 g for 1 min to pellet the resin. The resins were washed two times with 500 µl of the reaction buffer, and the bound protein was eluted by adding 2× Laemmli reducing sample buffer and boiled for 5 min. Samples were centrifuged for 1 min at 1000 g and subsequently run 10 mM into the resolving part of an SDS-PAGE gel before being stained with SimplyBlue™ SafeStain (Invitrogen). The stained area was cut from the gel and transferred to clean tubes.

Gel pieces were subjected to standard in-gel digestion (Shevchenko et al. 2006) including in-gel reduction with DTT with iodoacetamide, and overnight digestion using 6 ng/μl trypsin (Promega). Eluted peptides were purified using an Omix C18 tip (Agilent) and the eluate was evaporated to dryness, then samples were re-dissolved in 15 µl 0.1% Formic acid. Peptide concentration in samples was measured on a Nanodrop ONE spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific™).

Liquid-chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis

A total of 0.24 µg peptide from the BHI and HS samples, and 0.39 µg were loaded onto a ThermoFisher Scientific EASY-nLC1200 system and EASY-Spray column (C18, 2 µm, 100 Å, 50 µm, 50 cm). Peptides were fractionated using a 5%–80% acetonitrile gradient in 0.1% formic acid over 60 min at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. The separated peptides were analyzed using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). Data was collected in data dependent mode using a Top40 method. Data was searched against a non-redundant protein sequence database of E. faecium E745 (obtained from NCBI bioproject PRJNA295268) using the Chimerys search engine in Proteome Discoverer 3.0. The search criteria included a peptide mass tolerance of 10 ppm and a fragment mass tolerance of 0.02 Da. A rigorous false detection rate (FDR) threshold of 5% was applied for peptide identification. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE (Perez-Riverol et al. 2025) partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD064189. In addition, any protein identified based on less than 2 unique peptides across all experimental groups were not considered. An abundance ratio was calculated based on the average abundance across the replicates of the Desthiobiotin-ATP enriched samples against the abundance in the controls. The proteins with abundance ratio >1.5 in at least one of the three experimental groups were kept.

RT-qPCR analysis

For RT-qPCR analysis, E. faecium E745 was cultured under the same growth conditions as described above for the chemoproteomic experiments, using either BHI or BHI + 20% human serum (Sigma–Aldrich) in biological triplicate. The organoid supernatant condition was omitted due to insufficient availability of organoid material. One milliliter of the culture was pelleted, resuspended in RNAprotect Bacteria Reagent (Qiagen), and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The RNAprotect was removed, and the bacterial pellets were resuspended in 100 µl of TE buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) containing lysozyme (1 mg/ml) and mutanolysin and incubated at 37°C for 60 min.

Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was eluted in 40 µl of nuclease-free water (Cytiva). RNA quantity and integrity were measured using the Nanodrop1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). To prevent genomic DNA contamination, an equal amount of RNA from each sample was treated with DNase I (Sigma) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The effectiveness of the DNase treatment was assessed for all the samples by running RT-negative samples.

The DNase-treated RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After synthesis, cDNA was diluted 10-fold with nuclease-free water. The cDNA was stored at −20°C for future use.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the PowerTrack SYBR green master mix (Applied Biosystem) and the primers tcyA F-R, ftsH F-R, and arlS F-R (Table 1). PCR efficiency was determined by generating a standard curve using a 10-fold serial dilution. Primer efficiencies for tcyA, ftsH, and arlS were 102%, 101%, and 103%, respectively. Each qPCR reaction was carried out in a final volume of 20 µl using the LightCycler® 96 Instrument (Roche). All qPCR experiments included three biological replicates and three technical replicates. RT and PCR Negative control samples were also included. Quantification cycle values (Cq) were determined using the LightCycler® 96 Instrument Software 1.1. Relative fold differences were calculated by the Delta–Delta Cq method with ftsH serving as the reference gene. The hypothesis that tcyA and arlS expression levels are increased in BHI supplemented with 20% HS compared with BHI alone was evaluated using an unpaired one-tailed Welch’s t-test.

Principal component analysis

A principal component analysis (PCA) of the enriched proteins was performed using the ggplot2 (Wickham 2016) package in R Statistical Software v.4.2.2 for the visualization of influential proteins within the three experimental conditions.

Classification of ATP-interacting proteins

Protein sequences from E. faecium of a comprehensive set of known ATP-interacting proteins were retrieved from the Uniprot database, including ATPases, AAA-domain containing proteins, GTPases, ABC transporters, RNA polymerases, sensor proteins, kinases, heat shock proteins, chaperones, DNA helicases, ATP synthases, and nucleotide-binding proteins. These protein datasets were used to construct a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) using the HMMER v.3.4 (Eddy 2011) software suit, and enriched proteins from the experiments were search against the constructed HMM profiles to match amino acids sequences to the known ATP-binding HMM profiles.

Functional annotation and pathway analysis

Enriched proteins from the three experimental groups were functionally annotated using the EggNOG-mapper v2, which assigns orthology-based annotations to sequence data and categorizes proteins into “clusters of orthologous groups” (COG) (Cantalapiedra et al. 2021). Assignment of K number (KO) and KEGG mapping of metabolic and signalling roles was performed with BlastKOALA (Kanehisa et al. 2016). Involvement of proteins in KEGG pathways were integrated and visualized using the Pathview package for R (Bioconductor) (Luo and Brouwer 2013).

Structural analysis of ATP-binding region

Domain architecture analysis was performed using the SMART database. Multiple sequence alignments were generated using ClustalW. Structural models were predicted using AlphaFold2, and structural superposition with homologous proteins was performed using the PDB structures for MalK (PDB: 1Q12), BtuD (PDB:1L7V) and EnvZ (PDB:1XBD) from E. coli, and HisP (PDB: 1B0U) from Salmonella enterica.

Results

Profiling of activity of ATP-binding protein in E. faecium

To characterize condition-dependent ATP-binding protein activity in E. faecium, we employed the Desthiobiotin-ATP (Fig. 1b) probe coupled to LC-MS/MS analysis (Fig. 1a). Subsequent data filtration across the three experimental conditions yielded a dataset comprising of 230 proteins, each exhibiting an abundance greater than 1.5-fold compared to the ATP treated negative control in at least one condition (Supplementary Dataset 1). As an example, multiple pyruvate dehydrogenase subunits (pdhA, pdhB, pdhD) exhibited elevated activity in OE and reduced activity in HS, whereas ldhB, encoding a subunit of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), showed increased activity in HS.

Enterococcus faecium displayed distinct protein activation profiles when the standard growth medium BHI was supplemented with either 20% human serum (HS) or 10% organoid extract (OE), derived from the apical side of a human-rectal colon organoid model. A subset of proteins showed enhanced activity in HS relative to both BHI and OE, indicating a serum-specific activation (Fig. 1c). Conversely, another subset demonstrated increased activity in OE-supplemented samples compared to the other conditions (Fig. 1c), suggesting a unique bacterial response to this medium. These findings indicate the presence of condition-specific regulation of protein activity within the bacteria.

Further analysis was conducted using principal component analysis (PCA) to explore the variance within the dataset and to identify proteins that significantly influenced the variance among the treatment groups. The PCA plot revealed distinct clustering of proteins based on the experimental conditions (BHI, HS, and OE), while some proteins showed minimal influence on the variance between groups (Fig. 1d). A deeper examination revealed that 54 of the 230 proteins were pivotal in differentiating the treatment groups. Specifically, 19 proteins were unique to the HS sample, 31 to the OE sample and 4 to the BHI samples (Fig. 1d). Notably, the 4 proteins clustering with the BHI condition were CtsR, HolA, and WP_002311547.1 (Fig. 1d). Given that both HS and OE supplementation were done in BHI, this pattern likely reflects a relative decrease in activity under host-mimicking conditions rather than exclusive upregulation in BHI alone.

To categorize the differentially activated proteins within the dataset, a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) was developed using HMMER (v.3.4) (Eddy 2011) and UniProt database information of known ATP interacting types of proteins, such as ATP synthases, ATPases, RNA polymerases, ABC transporters, kinases, DNA helicases, motor proteins, GTPases, AAA-domains, heat shock proteins, and chaperones or nucleotide binding interactions. Proteins were then classified into categories of types of ATP-interacting proteins based on the models (Fig. 2). Overlap in the classification of ATP-interacting proteins across certain proteins may be attributed to the presence of multiple domains within these proteins (Fig. 2). Among the ATP-binding protein categories represented in the HMM, ATPases were the most abundant group, followed by AAA-domain-containing proteins and GTPases. In contrast, DNA helicases and ATP synthases were the least abundant. There were many proteins within the dataset lacking alignment with established ATP- or nucleotide-binding domains. The interaction mechanism of these “Unassigned” proteins with the ATP probe remains unknown. The possibility of non-specific interactions contributing to the enrichment of certain targets cannot be discounted. Moreover, some proteins may be enriched indirectly due to their association within complexes labelled by the ATP probe. The probe could also interact with allosteric sites where ATP functions as an allosteric regulator. Novel ATP-binding allosteric sites have been identified in previous studies with nucleoside phosphate probes (Okerberg et al. 2019) and could potentially represent a novel function for unassigned proteins in this dataset.

Overall, the differential responses of E. faecium to OE and HS did not only highlight the functional adaptability of E. faecium but also raised important questions regarding the functions of these proteins. Understanding these functions could provide insights into the adaptive mechanisms of E. faecium and reveal potential targets for therapeutic intervention. To enhance our understanding of the functional roles of these proteins and their potential impact on bacterial adaptation to different environmental conditions, we employed bioinformatic tools for further investigation.

Functional analysis of proteins

To elucidate the functional roles of the identified proteins, a comprehensive analysis was conducted utilizing EggNOG-mapper and BlastKOALA for functional annotation. EggNOG-mapper employs the evolutionary gene orthology information based on conserved orthologous groups. BlastKOALA was employed to assign KEGG Orthology (KO) numbers to the protein sequences, facilitating the exploration of associated biological systems, functions, and networks. This dual-annotation approach adds detailed functional insights to our dataset, facilitating a more comprehensive understanding of the biological roles of these proteins.

The COG analysis distributed the proteins across 18 distinct categories, encompassing a broad spectrum of biological functions (Fig. 3a). For comparative analysis, proteins exhibiting abundance ratios greater than 1.5 in each experimental group were selected to assess the enrichment of COG categories under different growth conditions (Fig. 3a). This analysis revealed differential activation of specific COG categories depending on the supplementation of the growth medium. Notably, categories such as defence mechanisms and signal transduction mechanisms showed increased activity in samples supplemented with both HS and OE. In the OE-treated samples, there was a notable increase in activity within the categories of energy production and conversion, carbohydrate transport and metabolism, and translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis. Conversely, the HS-supplemented samples uniquely exhibited heightened activity in Inorganic ion transport and metabolism, along with increased numbers of active proteins in the categories of replication, recombination and repair, transcription, and function unknown (Fig. 3a).

Further examination of the COG categories for the proteins influencing the variance among the treatment groups in the PCA revealed distinct profiles between samples (Fig. 3b and Supplementary dataset 2). The OE sample exhibited a unique composition of COG categories, including those involved in nucleotide and lipid transport and metabolism, energy production and conversion, intracellular trafficking, secretion and vesicular transport, cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis, posttranslational modification, protein turnover, chaperones, and secondary metabolites biosynthesis, transport, and catabolism (Fig. 3b). The HS sample displayed a more limited array of COG categories, with predominant representation in categories associated with unknown functions, transcription, signal transduction mechanisms, replication, recombination and repair, and amino acid transport and metabolism (Fig. 3b).

This comparative COG category analysis underscores the distinct biochemical pathways and cellular processes that are modulated in response to different environmental conditions, providing insights into the adaptive mechanisms of E. faecium.

Metabolic activities

Protein function analysis revealed a pronounced enrichment of proteins associated with amino acid transport and metabolism, as well as carbohydrate transport and metabolism, in samples supplemented with both HS and OE. Utilizing the Pathview package in R studio, these metabolic pathways were visualized, facilitating the integration and detailed examination of protein activity responses across the three experimental conditions, including BHI media.

Despite notable activity in amino acid and carbohydrate metabolism as categorized within the COG framework, the analysis using Pathview for glycolysis and gluconeogenesis pathways indicated minimal activity across all conditions (data not shown). This discrepancy may stem from a significant proportion of enzymes not being assigned a KEGG pathway due to limitations in database annotations, rather than an absence of these proteins. Notably, the cysteine and methionine metabolic pathways exhibited the most significant activity. Specifically, CysK, an enzyme involved in cysteine biosynthesis, showed activity exclusively in the OE-treated samples, suggesting a condition-specific metabolic adaptation in response to the OE-supplemented environment. Conversely, a cysteine-S-conjugate beta-lyase (CBL), which plays a crucial role in cysteine degradation, was active only in samples supplemented with human serum. These findings highlight distinct metabolic adaptations of bacteria in response to different biological environments.

Bacterial ABC transporters and two-component systems

Pathway analysis was also conducted to map the two-component systems (TCS) and ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, given their established roles in bacterial virulence across various species. The PCA analysis revealed significant activation of a protein called YckB (Fig. 1d) in the HS samples. This protein is also known as an ABC transporter, annotated as TcyA in the KEGG analysis and henceforth called TcyA in this paper. TcyA, an amino acid transporter, is crucial for the uptake of cystine, the oxidized dimer form of cysteine. The activation of TcyA in the HS condition suggests an adaptive response, enhancing the bacterium’s ability to import cystine for reduction to cysteine and subsequent utilization within cellular processes.

The histidine kinase, ArlS, showed notable activation exclusively in the HS-supplemented samples, whereas it was not detected in pure BHI media or upon OE-treatment. ArlS is known to play a significant role in bacterial pathogenicity in other organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus, potentially influencing processes such as adhesion (Fournier and Hooper 2000), autolysis (Fournier and Hooper 2000), and multidrug resistance (Kwiecinski et al. 2022). The specific activation of ArlS in the presence of HS underscores its possible involvement in stress response mechanisms that contribute to bacterial virulence. Another protein that was enriched predominantly in the OE sample is the dehydrogenase VanH, which is regulated by the VanRS TCS. Additionally, the analysis of TCSs highlighted the activity of VanH. VanH is a component of the vancomycin-resistance VanA operon, is instrumental in producing D-lactate as an alternative to D-alanine, a modification that contributes to vancomycin resistance. However, in contrast to the sensor kinase ArlS, VanH is not known to bind or require ATP for its function and the molecular basis for enrichment of this target is unclear.

Structural and domain analysis of ArlS and TcyABC transporter system

To further investigate the structural basis of selected ATP-binding proteins identified in our dataset, we performed domain architecture, sequence conservation, and structural modelling analyses of ArlS and the TcyABC cystine transporter system. Domain prediction using SMART revealed that ArlS exhibits a canonical histidine kinase architecture, including transmembrane regions and conserved intracellular domains associated with signal transduction. These include the HAMP domain, involved in dimerization and signal relay, a PAS domain associated with sensory functions, and the conserved HisKA and HATPase_c domains required for autophosphorylation activity (Fig. 4a).

Sequence alignment of the HATPase_c domain of ArlS with homologous domains from well-characterized histidine kinases, including YycG from Staphylococcus epidermidis and EnvZ from E. coli, demonstrated strong conservation of key motifs, including the G1, G2, F, and N boxes, which are characteristic of ATP-binding histidine kinases (Fig. 4b). Structural modelling of the ArlS HATPase_c domain using AlphaFold further revealed a fold consistent with known histidine kinase ATPase domains (Fig. 4c). Structural superposition with the ligand-bound HATPase domain of EnvZ (PDB: 1BXD) showed a high degree of structural similarity, with conserved motifs forming a cleft corresponding to the ATP-binding site (Fig. 4d–e).

To further characterize the cystine transport system, domain analysis of the TcyABC components showed that TcyA contains a substrate-binding protein domain typical of periplasmic components of ABC transporters, while TcyC harbours a conserved AAA ATPase (ABC_tran) domain (Fig. 5a). Sequence alignment of TcyC with well-characterized ABC transporter ATPases, including MalK and BtuD from E. coli and HisP from S. enterica, revealed the presence of conserved ATP-binding motifs, including the Walker A and Walker B motifs as well as the ABC signature (C-loop), supporting its functional annotation as an ATP-binding and hydrolysing component of the transporter (Fig. 5b).

TcyA contains a periplasmic substrate-binding protein domain, whereas TcyC harbors a canonical AAA ATPase (ABC_tran) domain. (b) Sequence alignment of TcyC with well-characterized AAA ATPase domain-containing proteins, including MalK (PDB: 1Q12) and BtuD (PDB:1L7V) from E. coli and HisP (PDB: 1B0U) from Salmonella enterica, reveals the presence of conserved ATP-binding motifs in TcyC, namely the Walker A, Walker B, and ABC signature (C-loop) motifs.

Transcriptional response of tcyA and arlS to human serum

To investigate transcriptional responses of tcyA and arlS in E. faecium E745 under conditions used for ABPP, gene expression was quantified by qRT-PCR following growth in BHI and BHI+20% HS. Unfortunately, the same analysis could not be performed for OE, due to the amount of available OE being limited to the ABPP experiments. Expression of tcyA was strongly induced in E745 upon exposure to HS compared to growth in BHI medium (Fig. 6a). Normalization to the reference gene FtsH revealed low baseline expression in BHI-grown cultures (1.07 ± 0.19), whereas exposure to human serum resulted in a marked increase in transcript levels (104.08 ± 3.25-fold; n = 3). Statistical analysis using an unpaired one-tailed Welch’s t-test confirmed that this difference was highly significant (P = 0.000159).

Expression was normalized to ftsH and calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCq method. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three biological replicates. In contrast, expression of the target gene arlS showed only minor variation between conditions (Fig. 6b). A slight increase in expression was observed in HS compared to BHI (2.42 ± 0.18 vs. 1.56 ± 0.54), this difference was statistically significant under an unpaired one-tailed Welch’s t-test (P = 0.0479).

Together, these results demonstrate strong and statistically supported induction of tcyA in HS, while arlS shows only a modest but significant increase in HS, indicating limited transcriptional responsiveness under these experimental conditions.

Discussion

In this study, we employed a chemoproteomic approach using the ABP desthiobiotin-ATP to profile the activity of ATP-binding proteins in E. faecium under three distinct growth conditions. Our findings reveal a complex landscape of putative ATP-binding protein activities that was distinctly modulated by the environmental conditions. These adaptations likely reflect bacterial responses to differences in host-associated stressors, highlighting enzymes that may be relevant for survival and persistence in host environments.

The application of desthiobiotin-ATP labelling coupled with mass spectrometry provides a detailed and sensitive approach to identify ATP-interacting proteins. To control for the specificity of enriched targets, a competitive ABPP setup was chosen. Samples were pretreated with unlabelled ATP which will compete with desthiobiotin-ATP for specific binding sites and is therefore expected to reduce the abundance among enriched, genuine ATP-binding proteins. Despite this attempt to reduce the noise from non-specific background labelling, there were proteins identified as enriched in the probe-labelled compared to control dataset where the molecular foundation for binding and thus specificity for probe-binding remains unclear. In fact, the dataset included many proteins not assigned to any known ATP-interacting types in the HMM analysis, suggesting potential unexplored ATP-interacting roles that could illuminate their functions in metabolism, stress responses, and host-pathogen interactions. The presence of hypothetical proteins lacking functional annotation could indicate that these possess possible ATP-interacting domains, though further studies are required for confirmation. Other proteins such as the dehydrogenase VanH are functionally described but are not known to bind ATP. We cannot exclude that this or other proteins may have been indirectly enriched due to interactions with other ATP-binding proteins or that allosteric effects within protein complexes with ATP-binding proteins lead to differences resulting in non-specific enrichment. In this study, equimolar concentrations of ATP and desthiobiotin-ATP were used for the competitive labelling setup. It is possible that a higher molar excess of ATP could lead to a more pronounced competition and a more reliable distinction of specific and non-specific binders. However, it is also plausible that VanH possesses an as-of-yet unidentified ATP-binding domain (Okerberg et al. 2019). It is intriguing to speculate that the abundance or activity of VanH may be positively regulated in response to host-factors from the human gut in the absence of antibiotic pressure and could indicate a previously unrecognized physiological role of D-lactate beyond cell wall remodelling related to vancomycin resistance.

We profiled the activities of ATP-binding proteins under simple in vitro conditions modelling environments relevant for colonization and infection. In addition to BHI, as standard growth medium, we also tested BHI supplemented with human serum (HS) and organoid extract (OE) which is culture supernatant from differentiated human colonic organoid line. These supplements were added to a final concentration of 20% and 10%, respectively, which we hypothesized would allow for investigations of bacterial responses to host factors present in HS and OE but otherwise maintain consistency in the overall nutrient profiles across all samples since the samples are based on BHI. Due to the presumed limited permeability of cells to the desthiobiotin-ATP probe, labelling was performed in lysates as opposed to live cells (Grunnvåg et al. 2024). As samples were harvested after overnight growth into stationary phase, the resulting profiles likely reflect integrated adaptive states shaped by both early and sustained responses to the supplemented conditions. Consequently, highly responsive regulatory proteins may not be captured at their transient activation peak, but rather as part of a broader physiological state in which signalling systems are maintained, attenuated, or reprogrammed following prolonged exposure.

We observed distinct protein activation patterns in both HS and OE conditions, compared to the BHI condition indicating that E. faecium responds differentially to host-derived signals. The PCA analysis further illustrates that numerous ATP-binding proteins display differential activity across the three growth conditions, highlighting a complex and condition-dependent adaptive landscape. Specifically, the reduced activity of CtsR, HolA and WP_002311547.1 under host-mimicking conditions may be explained by regulatory adaptations to host-derived signals present in HS and OE, which can modulate bacterial protein activity. Functionally, CtsR is associated with stress response regulation, while HolA is involved in DNA replication. The reduced activity of HolA may reflect decreased replication-associated processes, whereas changes in CtsR suggest a shift in stress response regulation, potentially indicating activation of alternative stress pathways under HS and OE conditions.

The colon organoid model comprises epithelial cells and goblet cells secreting mucus, and Paneth cells producing antimicrobial peptides (Uddin et al. 2024). Here, we used cell-free organoid culture supernatant that is expected to contain secretions of these cells to stimulate E. faecium. One limitation regarding the interpretation of functional responses of E. faecium to OE is that it is not possible to distinguish effects triggered by organoid-derived components from effects induced by factors introduced with the organoid medium, which includes e.g. growth factors and antioxidants. HS, where blood cells and clotting factors have been removed, may contain antibodies, hormones, electrolytes, growth factors, and proteins from the complement system, all crucial for immune responses. These factors may trigger adaptive responses from the bacteria, including stress-response pathways that may enhance protection against complement activity in HS or antimicrobial peptides in OE.

Functional annotation using EggNOG-mapper and BlastKOALA support this interpretation by revealing differential activation of different COG categories across conditions, consistent with environment-specific adaptation. For instance, the increased activity of defence mechanisms and signal transduction in HS and OE suggests a heightened responsiveness and adaptation to external stressors. OE further showed enrichment of proteins involved in macromolecule transport and metabolism, suggesting a metabolic adjustment tailored to the nutrient profile available in the organoid environment.

At the pathway level, central carbon metabolism was differentially regulated between conditions. Multiple pdh subunits (pdhA, pdhB, pdhD) were more active in OE and reduced in HS. PDH, which converts pyruvate into acetyl-CoA, plays a central role in carbohydrate metabolism and has been implicated in virulence (Wang et al. 2019, Echlin et al. 2020). Conversely, in HS, ldhB—encoding a subunit of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)—was more active. LDH mediates the anaerobic conversion of lactate to pyruvate and has also been associated with virulence, including in E. faecalis (Rana et al. 2013).

While PDH activity in OE is consistent with increased carbon flux towards energy metabolism and biosynthesis, the higher activity of LDH in HS than OE is initially surprising given its classical association with anaerobic metabolism. We speculate that this activity could reflect a requirement for redox balancing rather than growth under oxygen limitation. In host environments, bacteria encounter oxidative stress and fluctuating metabolic constraints, which can disrupt intracellular redox homeostasis. Under these conditions LDH could function to regenerate NAD+ by converting pyruvate to lactate, thereby maintaining essential metabolic processes. These findings suggest that E. faecium adopts distinct metabolic strategies in response to the environments of OE and HS.

Pathway-level analysis highlighted a significant increase in the activity-based enrichment of proteins involved in amino acid transport and metabolism across both conditions. Further examination of these pathways revealed that, specifically in HS, the ABC transporter component TcyA, which transports cystine into the cell, was highly active. This activation suggests a shift in bacterial metabolism toward utilization of cystine, a prevalent sulphur-containing metabolite in blood, potentially as a preferred sulphur source over cysteine. As cystine represents the oxidized form of cysteine and predominates under extracellular, oxidizing conditions, bacteria may rely on transport systems such as TcyA to import and subsequently reduce it to meet their sulphur requirements. Supporting this observation, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that tcyA was strongly upregulated in HS compared to BHI, with and approximately 100-fold increase in transcript levels. The concordance between increased tcyA transcript levels and activity-based enrichment suggests that the observed increase in TcyA activity is likely driven, at least in part, by increased protein abundance resulting from transcriptional upregulation under serum conditions. While normalization was performed using FtsH, which displayed some variation between conditions the magnitude of tcyA induction indicates that this conclusion is robust to potential normalization effects.

While we detected the TcyA component in our ABPP dataset, it is important to note that it is a part of a complex in which TcyB and TcyC also take part, and where TcyC is the ATPase component. Structural analysis confirmed that TcyC contains conserved Walker A, Walker B, and ABC signature motifs characteristic of ATP-binding. The enrichment of TcyA therefore likely reflects its association to the active TcyABC transporter complex rather than direct interaction with the ATP-based probe. Such indirect enrichment is consistent with chemoproteomic approaches, where proteins in stable or transient complexes with ATP-binding proteins can be co-enriched. In this context, the increased activity of TcyA in HS may indicate enhanced activity of the cystine transport system under serum conditions, consistent with the need for uptake of oxidized sulphur sources in a host-associated, oxidizing environment. In S. aureus TcyA has been reported to facilitate sulphur acquisition during infection and was crucial for fitness in a murine heart infection (Lensmire et al. 2020). TcyA is also required for optimal growth in oral biofilms of the dental pathogen, Streptococcus mutans (Kim et al. 2012). The activation of TcyA in HS supplemented with BHI, but not in pure BHI or OE-supplemented BHI, could indicate the need for it as a survival strategy, optimizing growth and persistence in blood. The potential inhibition of TcyA, as demonstrated in studies with the plant pathogen Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (Lonare et al. 2023), suggests a viable avenue for impairing bacterial fitness and virulence.

There were also other cysteine-related targets enriched. Specifically, CysK and cysteine S-conjugate beta-lyase (CBL) were notably active in OE and HS, respectively. CysK catalyses the conversion of O-acetylserine to cysteine, a critical step not only for protein synthesis but also for sulphur metabolism and antioxidant defence (Joshi et al. 2019, Brenner et al. 2022). Since the organoid differentiation media (that makes up 10% of OE samples), includes N-acetylcysteine, it appears likely that this antioxidant triggers increased CysK activity in OE-conditions.

CBL is often associated with oral and gut bacteria, but CBL’s role in HS could be twofold: facilitating the breakdown of cysteine conjugates to liberate cysteine for metabolic use and potentially participating in hydrogen sulphide production. Hydrogen sulphide has been implicated in modulating the host immune response (Sun et al. 2021, Pozzi et al. 2022), which could aid in bacterial evasion from immune response and contribute to virulence (Peng et al. 2017). The absence of CBL activity in OE suggests that under conditions resembling its native intestinal environment, E. faecium does not strongly rely on cysteine conjugate degradation, while in HS the activity of CBL is likely driven by the need for detoxification, survival against host defences, and nutrient scavenging in a nutrient-limited and hostile environment.

In addition to metabolic adaptations observed in transport and amino acid pathways, regulatory proteins such as the histidine kinase ArlS exhibited pronounced activity in the HS-supplemented samples, whereas it remained inactive in both OE and BHI conditions. ArlS is a part of a two-component regulatory system. Structural analysis of ArlS further supports its functional classification as an ATP-binding histidine kinase, as modelling of the HATPase_c domain revealed conserved ATP-binding motifs and a canonical kinase fold. Structural similarity to the ligand-bound HATPase domain of EnvZ indicates the presence of a functional ATP-binding pocket, consistent with its identification in the activity-based profiling dataset.

qPCR analysis suggests that arlS expression increased only very modestly in HS compared to BHI. This finding contrasts with the strong HS-specific activation observed in the ABPP dataset and highlights one of the methodological strengths of the chemoproteomic ABPP strategy: It allows to dissect differences in protein ACTIVITY across conditions that might go unnoticed by the mere assessment of protein abundance or gene expression analysis. As a component of a two-component regulatory system, the (ATP-binding) sensor histidine kinase activity of ArlS is expected to be conditionally activated in response to environmental cues, but inactive in their absence. It is likely that HS contains such activating cues and contains host-derived ligands that induce conformational changes leading to an increase in autophosphorylation activity of the catalytic domain and thus increased ATP-probe binding.

While FtsH was selected as a reference gene based on its apparent stability in prior ABPP datasets and its essentiality, its expression showed variation between pure BHI and BHI+20% HS in the qRT-PCR analysis, highlighting the limitations of single-reference normalization in bacteria exposed to host-like stress conditions. Although this may introduce some variability in absolute fold-change estimates, the magnitude of the observed differences (particularly for tcyA) indicates that the main biological conclusions remain unaffected.

Interestingly, previous chemoproteomic studies have identified ArlS in a similar ABPP experiment in S. aureus, where an ArlS-deficient mutant displayed reduced lethality in the G. mellonella infection model and exhibited a reduced capacity for infection (Ahator et al. 2024a). Furthermore, deletion of the ArlRS two-component system in S. aureus has been shown to increase susceptibility to oxacillin (Bai et al. 2019), and the inhibition of ArlS resulted in the loss of several virulence traits, including clumping, adherence to host ligands, and evasion of the immune response (Bai et al. 2019). These inhibitors were also effective in reducing the severity of MRSA infections in murine models, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting this enzyme (Kwiecinski et al. 2022).

Although the function of ArlS in E. faecium has not yet been characterized, its activation in response to human serum suggests that it may contribute to regulatory responses required for survival under host-associated stress conditions. Further investigation, for example through the development of an ArlS-deficient mutant, would help clarify its functional role in E. faecium and determine whether it contributes to host adaptation and pathogenicity. Considering the rising issue of antibiotic resistance, understanding and potentially targeting ArlS in E. faecium could lead to novel therapeutic strategies.

In conclusion, this study provides a condition-dependent view of the adaptive landscape of ATP-binding proteins in E. faecium across different environmental conditions. The insights could enhance our understanding of bacterial adaptability and further aid investigations into the specific roles and interactions of these proteins that could be crucial in developing targeted strategies to inhibit E. faecium pathogenicity and resilience in host environments.
