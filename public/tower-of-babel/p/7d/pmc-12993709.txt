Underrated, Unvaccinated, Untreated: A Multinational Registry Analysis of Human Metapneumovirus in Hematological Malignancy, Insights From EPICOVIDEHA/EPIRESEHA Registry
Authors: Jon Salmanton-García, Francesco Marchesi, Erica Mackenzie, Inmaculada Heras-Fernando, Iker Falces-Romero, Antonio Vena, Carolina Garcia-Vidal, Barbora Weinbergerová, Nicola Sgherza, Klára Piukovics, Gustavo Adolfo Méndez, Dario Leotta, Alberto López-García, Francesca Farina, Nurettin Erben, Yavuz M Bilgin, Monika M Biernat, Verena Petzer, Gabriele Magliano, Ľuboš Drgoňa, Benjamín Víšek, Andrés Soto-Silva, Michail Samarkos, Milan Navrátil, Tobias Lahmer, Nicola S Fracchiolla, Donald C Vinh, Lucia Prezioso, Nikola Pantić, Sofya Khostelidi, Reham Abdelaziz Khedr, Noha Eisa, Michelina Dargenio, Caterina Buquicchio, László Imre Pinczés, Mario Virgilio Papa, Patricia García-Ramírez, Anna Dąbrowska-Iwanicka, Annarosa Cuccaro, Natasa Čolović

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman metapneumovirus (hMPV) is an increasingly recognized respiratory pathogen in immunocompromised patients, but its impact in haematological malignancies is poorly defined. Unlike influenza or SARS-CoV-2, hMPV has no specific treatment or vaccine, contributing to underdiagnosis and underestimation of its clinical relevance.MethodWe performed a multicentre retrospective cohort study within the Epidemiology of COVID-19 in patients with hematological malignancies: A European Haematology Association Survey/ Epidemiology of respiratory viral infections in patients with hematological malignancies: A European Haematology Association Survey registry of haematological patients with hMPV (January 2023–December 2024), comparing clinical features and outcomes with matched influenza and SARS-CoV-2 cohorts.ResultsThe study included 130 patients with hMPV. Median age was 58.5 years; 57% were male. Plasma cell neoplasms (25%), lymphoma (23%), and acute myeloid leukemia (20%) were the most common hematological malignancies. Hospitalization occurred in 64%, intensive care unit (ICU) admission in 19%, and 30-day mortality was 8%. Most cases (73%) received supportive care only. Secondary infections were common (24%). Chronic renal disease significantly increased mortality risk (hazard ratio 20.9 11.05, P = .014). Compared with influenza and SARS-CoV-2, hMPV patients had comparable severity and outcomes, with 18.5% ICU admission rates versus 25.9% for influenza (P = .316) and 20.9% versus 4.7% for SARS-CoV-2 (P = .006), and 30-day mortality of 5.6% versus 11.1% for influenza (P = .489) and 7.0% versus 2.3% for SARS-CoV-2 (P = .277), yet received fewer targeted interventions.ConclusionshMPV causes clinically significant disease in patients with hematological malignancy, often necessitating hospital and ICU care, and leading to mortality. In the absence of specific treatments or vaccines, this virus remains an underrecognized pathogen in patients with hematological malignancy. Enhanced clinical awareness and investment in diagnostics, prevention, and therapeutics are needed.

Acute respiratory viral infections remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality among patients with hematological malignancies (HMs) [1]. While significant attention has been dedicated to common respiratory viruses such as influenza [2] and, more recently, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [3], the clinical impact of other viral pathogens, including human metapneumovirus (hMPV), has received comparatively little attention in the hematology community. hMPV, first described in 2001, is a ribonucleic acid (RNA) virus from the Pneumoviridae family [4], closely related to respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). hMPV is now recognized as a significant contributor to seasonal respiratory infections in both pediatric and adult populations worldwide [5]. In the general population, hMPV typically causes mild upper respiratory tract infections; however, in immunocompromised individuals, particularly those patients with hematological malignancy, hMPV can lead to severe lower respiratory tract infections, respiratory failure, prolonged hospitalization, and even death [5]. The absence of specific antiviral therapies or licensed vaccines for hMPV further compounds the risks for this group [6].

Despite its clinical relevance, hMPV remains underdiagnosed and underreported in patients with HM. Several factors contribute to this, including its nonspecific presentation, overlap with other respiratory viruses, limited routine testing, and the general lack of clinician awareness [6]. Moreover, there is a lack of large-scale, multicenter studies characterizing the clinical course and outcomes of hMPV infection in HM [7]. As a result, the epidemiology, clinical presentation, disease severity, risk factors for poor outcomes, and burden on healthcare resources associated with hMPV infection in patients with hematological malignancy remain poorly understood. In contrast, progress has been made in understanding and managing other respiratory viruses, especially influenza and SARS-CoV-2, in immunocompromised hosts [1]. These viruses are subject to routine surveillance, have clearly defined diagnostic and therapeutic pathways, and benefit from the availability of antivirals and, in most cases, vaccines. Understanding how hMPV compares with these better-characterized viruses may help delineate its relative importance, inform infection control strategies, and guide the prioritization of research and drug development efforts.

In this study, we present a large, multicenter cohort of patients with hematological malignancy diagnosed with hMPV, collected through the Epidemiology of COVID-19 in patients with hematological malignancies: A European Haematology Association Survey/ Epidemiology of respiratory viral infections in patients with hematological malignancies: A European Haematology Association Survey (EPICOVIDEHA/EPIRESEHA) registry. This study aims to fill critical knowledge gaps by describing the epidemiological, clinical, and outcome-related features of hMPV infection in this population. Additionally, we provide a comparative clinical context by matching this cohort to patients with influenza and SARS-CoV-2 infection, thereby offering insights into the unique and shared challenges posed by these 3 respiratory pathogens in immunocompromised individuals. Our findings seek to inform clinical awareness, diagnostic vigilance, and future development of targeted preventive and therapeutic strategies for hMPV in patients with hematological malignancy.

METHODS

This study analyzed adult patients with hematological malignancy who were diagnosed with hMPV infection between 1 January 2023, and 31 December 2024 (Figure 1). All reported cases were recorded in the EPICOVIDEHA/EPIRESEHA registry [8, 9], a secure, web-based international database hosted on http://www.clinicalsurveys.net (EFS, TIVIAN, Cologne, Germany), and officially endorsed by the European Hematology Association's Specialized Working Group on Infections in Hematology. The registry operates under the clinical trial identifier NCT04733729.

Flow chart of patients included in the analysis. CARV, community-acquired respiratory virus; hMPV, human metapneumovirus; SARS-CoV-2, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2.

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 18 years or older, had a documented diagnosis of active antineoplastic treatment for hematological malignancy within the preceding 5 years, and had a laboratory-confirmed diagnosis of hMPV within the study period. Laboratory-confirmed diagnosis of hMPV infection was established according to local standard-of-care diagnostic practices at each participating center. In all included cases, hMPV detection was based on laboratory confirmation using nucleic acid amplification techniques performed on nasopharyngeal swabs or bronchoalveolar lavage samples. In some centers, testing was performed as part of multiplex respiratory viral panels, while in others hMPV-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays were used. Given the multinational nature of the registry, detailed information on PCR platforms, assay manufacturers, and cycle threshold values was not collected systematically and was therefore not available for analysis. For influenza and SARS-CoV-2, rapid antigen detection tests were also accepted as valid diagnostic methods. Exclusion criteria included nonmalignant hematologic conditions, solid tumors, age below 18 years, being free of HM or off HM treatment for more than 5 years, or diagnosis of hMPV based solely on imaging without confirmative PCR testing. Only patients with ante-mortem diagnoses of hMPV infection were included.

For each patient (Supplementary Table 1), data collected included demographic characteristics (age and sex), detailed information on the underlying hematological malignancy, hematological malignancy status at the time of infection, the nature of the last antineoplastic treatment received, and virological diagnostic method. Clinical parameters included symptomatology (ie, no symptoms, extrapulmonary [cough, fever, and rhinorrea], or pulmonary [lung impairment and respiratory distress]) at hMPV episode onset, infection severity, antiviral treatment strategies, hospitalization details, and outcomes, including mortality. Hematological malignancy status at the time of infection was classified as either controlled (complete or partial remission) or active (including stable, refractory, or newly diagnosed with hematological malignancy). Infection severity was categorized according to previously published EPICOVIDEHA/EPIRESEHA criteria as asymptomatic, mild, severe, or critical [10]. Asymptomatic cases involved patients managed at home with no symptoms. Mild disease included patients at home with only nonpulmonary symptoms or hospitalized patients with no or mild symptoms. Severe disease included patients at home with pulmonary symptoms or hospitalized patients with nonpulmonary and/or pulmonary symptoms. Critical disease was defined as admission to the intensive care unit, regardless of symptoms.

Data validation was performed by experts in hematology and infectious diseases, with active resolution of data queries in collaboration with participating centers. Cases missing critical data points necessary for regression modeling were excluded from the final analysis.

Patients were matched in 2 separate comparisons: hMPV versus influenza and hMPV versus SARS-CoV-2. To enable meaningful comparison between patients with hMPV infection and those with influenza or SARS-CoV-2 infection, a matching strategy was employed to minimize confounding by key demographic, temporal, and disease-related factors. Matching variables were selected a priori based on their known or plausible association with infection risk, disease severity, and outcomes in patients with hematological malignancies [1, 3, 10–12]. Age (±10 years) and sex were included to account for baseline demographic differences known to influence immune function and respiratory infection outcomes. Season of diagnosis was used to control for temporal variation in circulating respiratory viruses, healthcare system strain, and testing practices. Underlying hematological malignancy type and disease status (controlled vs active) were selected to address heterogeneity in immune competence, treatment intensity, and baseline mortality risk across malignancy subgroups. Matching was performed using nearest-neighbour matching without replacement. After matching, balance between cohorts was assessed by comparing baseline characteristics using standardized mean differences. Residual imbalance was considered acceptable when standardized mean differences were <0.1; variables exceeding this threshold were interpreted cautiously in subsequent comparative analyses.

Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and absolute ranges. Fisher's exact test was used for categorical comparisons when expected frequencies were fewer than or equal to 5; otherwise, the χ2 test was applied. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous variable comparisons. Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan–Meier curves, with group differences evaluated by the log-rank test. Univariable Cox proportional hazards models were employed in patients with single hMPV infections to explore potential risk factors for mortality. Variables with clinical relevance or a significance threshold of P ≤ .1 were included in multivariable Cox regression models, using the Wald backward elimination method. Results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Cox proportional hazards regression was performed using a backward elimination approach to derive a parsimonious model while retaining covariates independently associated with the outcome. This method was selected given the exploratory nature of the analysis and the number of candidate variables relative to the number of observed events. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors and correlation matrices, and no evidence of significant multicollinearity was identified. The multivariable model included a comprehensive set of clinical variables, such as patient sex and age, hematological malignancy type (lymphoid, myeloid, or plasma cell neoplasms), hematological malignancy status at infection onset (active or controlled), number and type of comorbidities (eg, chronic cardiopulmonary disease, diabetes, liver disease, renal disease, obesity, smoking history), neutrophil and lymphocyte counts at diagnosis, recent hematological malignancy treatments (chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation [HSCT], chimeric antigen receptor T-cell [CAR-T] cell therapy, or no hematological malignancy treatment), presence of coinfections with other viruses, symptom presentation, infection severity, antiviral strategies, occurrence of secondary infections (bacterial, fungal, or viral), and care setting (home, hospital, or intensive care unit [ICU]). Missing data were handled using a complete-case (list wise) approach. Observations with missing values for variables included in the regression models were excluded from the corresponding analyses, and no imputation was performed. The proportion of missing data was limited and was not considered likely to materially affect the results (Supplementary Table 1).

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY), and P-values ≤.05 were considered statistically significant.

The study protocol received ethical clearance from the central review board at Fondazione Policlinico Universitario Agostino Gemelli IRCCS in Rome (Study ID 3226), alongside approvals from local ethics committees when required. As all patient data were fully anonymized, informed consent was not mandated, in line with the regulations and policies of each contributing center.

RESULTS

This multinational cohort included 130 patients diagnosed with hMPV infection between January 2023 and December 2024. The cohort showed a slight male predominance (n = 74, 56.9%) and a median age of 58.5 years (IQR 43–69; range 18–95). Most cases were reported from Italy (n = 26, 20.0%), Spain (n = 25, 19.2%), the United States (n = 24, 18.5%), and Germany (n = 13, 10.0%) (Table 1; Supplementary Table 2).

The most common underlying hematological malignancies were plasma cell disorders (n = 33, 25.4%), lymphoma (n = 30, 23.1%), and acute myeloid leukemia (n = 26, 20.0%). Over half of the individuals (n = 72, 55.4%) had controlled malignancy at the time of hMPV infection. Most patients (n = 91, 70.0%) had 0–1 additional comorbidity, with chronic cardiac (n = 43, 33.1%) and pulmonary conditions (n = 22, 16.9%) being the most frequent. Neutrophil counts ≥1000/μL and lymphocyte counts ≥500/μL were recorded in 68.5% (n = 89) and 57.7% (n = 75) of cases, respectively. Recent hematological malignancy therapies included immunochemotherapy (n = 36, 27.7%) and allogeneic HSCT (n = 36, 27.7%) (Table 1).

hMPV was the sole detected virus in 86.9% (n = 113), while coinfections were reported mostly with rhinovirus (n = 6, 4.6%) or SARS-CoV-2 (n = 4, 3.1%). At onset, 80 patients (61.5%) exhibited nonpulmonary symptoms; only 5 (3.8%) remained asymptomatic. More than half of the cases were classified as severe (n = 68, 52.3%), with 24 (18.5%) reaching critical severity, prompting hospitalization in 63.8% (n = 83). Among these, 24 patients (18.5%) required ICU admission and 14 (10.8%) received invasive mechanical ventilation. The median hospital stay was 13 days (IQR 7–28.5; range 1–140). Most patients (n = 95, 73.1%) received supportive care only. Secondary infections were reported mainly involving bacterial pathogens (n = 31, 23.8%) (Table 1).

The 30-day mortality rate was 7.7% (n = 10), with deaths primarily linked to combined effects of the underlying malignancy and hMPV (n = 4, 3.1%) or to hMPV alone (n = 2, 1.5%). Chronic renal disease significantly increased the risk of mortality (HR 11.054; 95% CI: 1.639–74.569; P = .014) (Table 2).

In the matched comparison of hMPV and influenza patients (n = 54 each), baseline characteristics were similar, with 2 significant differences. Firstly, antiviral usage was greater among influenza cases (n = 40, 74.1%) compared with hMPV (n = 1, 1.9%; P < .001), while the majority of hMPV patients (n = 40, 74.1%) received no antiviral treatment. Additionally, secondary bacterial infections were more frequent in the hMPV group (n = 15, 27.8%) than in the influenza group (n = 5, 9.3%; P = .024) (Table 1).

When comparing hMPV and SARS-CoV-2 patients (n = 86 each), several differences emerged. A significantly lower proportion of hMPV patients were asymptomatic at diagnosis (n = 3, 3.5% vs n = 6, 7.0%; P = .003), while a higher proportion developed severe disease (n = 41, 47.7% vs n = 38, 44.2%; P = .003). Antiviral treatment was less common among hMPV patients (n = 1, 1.2% received antivirals) than among SARS-CoV-2 cases (n = 56, 65.1%; P < .001). Bacterial secondary infections were also more prevalent in the hMPV group (n = 22, 25.6%) compared with the SARS-CoV-2 group (n = 8, 9.3%; P = .008) (Table 1).

No significant differences in survival probability were observed among patients with hMPV, influenza, and SARS-CoV-2 at 30 days following infection onset (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

This real-world cohort analysis highlights the clinical and public health burden posed by hMPV in patients with hematological malignancy. hMPV was linked to high rates of severe infection, hospital admission, and ICU utilization, comparable with or exceeding, effects seen in matched cohorts of influenza and SARS-CoV-2. These findings challenge the perception of hMPV as a mild pathogen, instead presenting it as a serious opportunistic threat in immunocompromised individuals [13].

Patients with hMPV had predominantly plasma cell disorders, lymphoma, or acute myeloid leukemia. Importantly, most were in remission and not overtly neutropenic or lymphopenic, suggesting that severe hMPV disease can occur even without profound cytopenias. Therefore, pathogenesis may be driven more by functional immune deficits, due to prior immunochemotherapy, alloHSCT, or chronic T- or B-cell dysfunction, rather than by absolute cell counts [14, 15]. Traditional markers like neutropenia may thus underestimate risk; broader consideration of functional immunosuppression is needed when evaluating susceptibility to respiratory viruses.

No approved vaccine is currently available for hMPV, though several candidates are in early-stage development [16, 17], including bivalent formulations targeting both RSV and hMPV. mRNA vaccines have demonstrated acceptable safety and robust neutralizing antibody responses in phase 1/2 studies in healthy adults children [18]. In addition, an RSV–hMPV mRNA combination is being evaluated in adults aged ≥60 years, while a bivalent virus-like particle vaccine has shown strong immunogenicity and tolerability in older adults. Other approaches, including subunit vaccines, as well as vectored and live-attenuated constructs, have shown promising preclinical and early clinical results. Notably, immunocompromised patients, including those with hematological malignancies, have largely been excluded from these studies. Experience with influenza and SARS-CoV-2 vaccination indicates that even partial immune responses can reduce disease severity and hospitalization [19]. Inclusion of these high-risk populations in future trials will be essential to determine safety, immunogenicity, and optimal vaccination strategies, while passive immunization with broadly neutralizing monoclonal antibodies may offer an interim protective option. In contrast, influenza [20] and SARS-CoV-2 vaccines [11, 12] were more widely accessible during the study period, likely contributing to their comparatively lower associated mortality. The absence of hMPV prophylaxis leaves vulnerable patients with hematological malignancy unprotected. Notably, some vaccine candidates targeting both RSV and hMPV, such as the mRNA-based bivalent formulations and recombinant protein subunit vaccines, are currently in Phase II or III trials, with preliminary data indicating acceptable safety and immunogenicity in healthy adults. However, immunocompromised patients have largely been excluded from these studies, leaving critical gaps in understanding efficacy and tolerability in this population. Our data underscore the need to accelerate the development of hMPV-specific preventive and therapeutic strategies. Regarding antiviral therapy, ribavirin and other investigational agents (eg, fusion inhibitors and nucleoside analogs) have been tested in small case series or compassionate use settings, but evidence of clinical benefit is limited. Moreover, ribavirin carries risks of hemolytic anemia and liver toxicity, which may be particularly problematic in patients with hematological malignancies undergoing intensive therapy. Clinical trials must include immunocompromised groups to assess safety, immunogenicity, and real-world effectiveness. Taken together, these findings highlight the dual need to advance both prophylactic vaccines and carefully monitored antiviral treatments to reduce morbidity and mortality from hMPV in high-risk hematological populations.

Coinfections with other respiratory viruses were rare, possibly due to viral competition or limited concurrent testing [21, 22]. Clinical symptoms were nearly universal [23], with almost absent asymptomatic infection episodes, which may imply diagnostic delays, especially for hMPV, where testing is less routine and clinical suspicion remains low. From a diagnostic perspective, nucleic acid amplification techniques, particularly RT-PCR, represent the current gold standard for hMPV detection. In contrast to influenza and SARS-CoV-2 [24, 25], for which rapid antigen assays are widely available and routinely used, no validated point-of-care antigen tests are commonly implemented for hMPV in adult clinical practice. Consequently, the diagnostic heterogeneity related to antigen versus molecular testing that affects other respiratory viruses is largely not applicable to hMPV. In the present cohort, all hMPV infections were confirmed using PCR-based methods, either through single plex assays or multiplex respiratory viral panels, thereby limiting intracohort variability in analytical sensitivity. Nevertheless, differences in testing platforms, specimen types (nasopharyngeal swab vs bronchoalveolar lavage), laboratory thresholds, and local workflows may have introduced intercenter variability in case detection. Despite technical availability in many tertiary centers, routine testing for hMPV remains inconsistent and is often deprioritized in favor of influenza, RSV, and SARS-CoV-2, particularly outside peak respiratory virus seasons. Even among immunocompromised patients, extended respiratory viral panels are not universally performed and are frequently reserved for hospitalized or clinically severe cases. As a result, hMPV infections may have been missed in several scenarios, including mild or outpatient respiratory illness, empiric testing limited to high-priority viruses, delayed sampling after symptom onset, resource-limited settings without multiplex PCR access, and cases in which detection of another respiratory pathogen precluded further viral investigation. These diagnostic patterns likely introduced a degree of selection bias, with preferential identification of more severe and hospitalized cases. Consequently, the true incidence of hMPV infection in patients with hematological malignancies is probably underestimated, while the observed severity, hospitalization rates, and ICU utilization may be overestimated. Furthermore, differential diagnostic intensity compared with influenza and SARS-CoV-2, both of which benefit from routine screening and rapid testing, may have influenced comparative analyses by increasing the likelihood of detecting milder disease in those cohorts. Finally, geographic variability in diagnostic infrastructure across participating countries may have contributed to heterogeneity in case capture. These limitations highlight the need for more standardized respiratory virus testing strategies that systematically include hMPV, particularly in high-risk immunocompromised populations.

Hospitalization was frequent (64% for hMPV), with ICU admission rates notably high (18.3%), similar to other studies [26], reflecting more advanced infection. Invasive mechanical ventilation was required in a subset of cases, underscoring the potential for severe pulmonary complications. Antiviral treatment was uncommon, as there are no approved antivirals for hMPV, although ribavirin is occasionally used [27]. This therapeutic gap underscores the need for timely diagnosis and close monitoring to prevent complications.

Thirty-day all-cause mortality was significant (7.7%), similar to historical influenza data and higher than rates for recent SARS-CoV-2 infections in patients with hematological malignancy cohorts [1] and lower than in previously reported populations [26]. Multivariate analysis identified chronic renal disease as an independent predictor of mortality, in line with previous reports in the literature [28, 29]. The strong association between chronic renal disease and 30-day mortality deserves careful interpretation. In this registry, chronic renal disease reflects preexisting baseline kidney dysfunction rather than acute kidney injury related to hMPV infection. Patients with chronic renal impairment often represent a particularly vulnerable subgroup characterized by impaired immune function, reduced physiological reserve, higher inflammatory burden, and altered pharmacokinetics of antimicrobial and supportive therapies. In addition, renal dysfunction may limit the use or dosing of potentially beneficial treatments and increase susceptibility to drug-related toxicity. Although we adjusted for major clinical confounders such as hematological malignancy status, recent intensive therapies (including HSCT and CAR-T), and infection severity, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. The registry does not capture granular information on chronic kidney disease stage, dialysis status, full cumulative exposure to nephrotoxic agents, or dynamic renal function changes during hospitalization. Therefore, the observed association likely reflects a combination of baseline vulnerability, comorbidity burden, and treatment-related complexity rather than a direct causal effect of renal disease alone. This finding should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and highlights the need for closer monitoring and early supportive interventions in hematological malignancy patients with preexisting renal impairment who develop hMPV infection.

Notably, neutropenia and underlying hematological malignancy type were not independently associated with death. These findings might suggest that organ dysfunction and infection complications, not virologic or hematological malignancy factors alone, are the primary drivers of poor outcomes. These variables may prove useful in risk stratification and early intervention strategies. While traditional markers of immunosuppression, such as neutropenia and lymphopenia, are often used to predict infection risk, our data suggest that severe hMPV disease can occur even in patients with near-normal cell counts. This points to the role of functional immune deficits rather than absolute cytopenias. Prior immunochemotherapy, allogeneic HSCT, CAR-T therapy, or underlying hematologic malignancy itself can impair T- and B-cell function, disrupt cytokine signaling, and compromise antiviral responses. Dysfunctional mucosal immunity and impaired interferon-mediated antiviral defense may also contribute to uncontrolled viral replication in the respiratory tract. Additionally, chronic comorbidities, including renal disease, may exacerbate immune dysregulation through systemic inflammation, altered pharmacokinetics of supportive therapies, or increased susceptibility to secondary infections. The combination of these factors likely explains why hMPV can cause severe lower respiratory tract infections, prolonged hospitalization, and higher ICU utilization even in patients without profound cytopenias. Understanding these mechanisms is critical for identifying high-risk patients, guiding early supportive interventions, and designing targeted prophylactic or therapeutic strategies, including vaccines and antiviral agents, that account for functional rather than purely numeric immune deficits.

Previous studies in patients with hematological malignancy have reported similar morbidity and mortality from hMPV but were often limited to single centers or small sample sizes. Our multicenter, real-world cohort reinforces and extends these findings. By directly comparing outcomes to those of influenza and SARS-CoV-2, we provide compelling evidence that hMPV deserves equal clinical and public health attention. This virus represents a burden and must be recognized as a major pathogen in patients with hematological malignancies. Notably, nearly 1 in 5 hMPV patients required ICU admission, underscoring the pressure these infections place on critical care resources. The scientific community has already raised concerns regarding the economic impact of respiratory viral infections, and our findings further emphasize the need for targeted strategies to mitigate these costs [30, 31].

Compared with influenza, hMPV caused similarly severe illness but was associated with no vaccination and lower antiviral use. This likely contributed to worse outcomes, despite influenza patients being older and having more comorbidities. Notably, hMPV had a higher superinfection rate than both influenza and SARS-CoV-2. In contrast, antibiotics are usually recommended in influenza cases, especially in pneumonia or immunosuppressed patients, due to the high risk of bacterial superinfection. Compared with SARS-CoV-2, hMPV was more likely to cause symptomatic infection, hospitalization, and clinical severity, suggesting that, in the current era of vaccines and antiviral access, SARS-CoV-2 may now pose a relatively lower threat to hematologic patients [30].

Our study underscores the need to reconsider current approaches to hMPV management in patients with hematological malignancy. hMPV should no longer be viewed as a rare or benign pathogen, given its frequent underdiagnosis and the absence of available prophylactic or therapeutic options, which place vulnerable patients at considerable risk. To improve early detection and patient outcomes, hMPV should be systematically included in routine diagnostic panels during respiratory virus seasons. Finally, healthcare systems must anticipate and address the clinical and economic burden this infection poses in immunocompromised populations.

Several limitations must be acknowledged in this study. First, the retrospective design introduces potential biases in diagnosis and management, despite some aspects of the data collection being prospective. Additionally, diagnostic inconsistency, due to variability in testing methods (eg, PCR vs antigen testing), may have impacted the accuracy of pathogen detection. In fact, as a registry-based retrospective study, reporting bias is a recognized limitation. Variability in diagnostic intensity, PCR platforms, and local testing practices across centers and countries may have preferentially identified more severe or hospitalized hMPV cases, while milder infections were likely underrepresented. These factors should be considered when interpreting the epidemiological and comparative outcomes presented here. Additionally, due to the limited number of patients with specific hematologic malignancies, we were unable to assess the impact of particular antineoplastic agents, such as anti-CD20 antibodies, proteasome inhibitors, certain cytostatics, corticosteroids, or Bruton's kinase inhibitors, on clinical outcomes. The study also faced challenges with antiviral treatment heterogeneity, as we lacked detailed data on the timing, duration, and choice of treatment, particularly regarding antiviral use, which could influence outcomes.

Emerging data from the 2024–2025 respiratory virus season show that hMPV is an important cause of respiratory illness worldwide, including in high-risk populations. In East Asia, seasonal increases in respiratory infections were observed in late 2024, with hMPV detected in about 6.2% of positive respiratory tests and 5.4% of hospital admissions in China during December, higher than several other non-SARS-CoV-2 viruses. These trends were seen in both children and adults, although authorities considered them within normal seasonal variation. At the same time, studies of overall respiratory virus burden highlight the continued impact of vaccine-preventable viruses such as influenza, RSV, and SARS-CoV-2 [13]. In Europe, influenza and RSV caused the highest hospitalization rates during this season, while SARS-CoV-2 contributed less. Together, these findings show that seasonal respiratory viruses remain a leading cause of hospitalizations, ICU use, and deaths among older adults and other high-risk groups [32].

Our findings highlight the need for structured, prospective surveillance of hMPV in high-risk populations, particularly patients with hematological malignancies. One way could be the implementation of multicenter, standardized respiratory virus panels that routinely include hMPV, with consistent data collection on clinical presentation, laboratory parameters, comorbidities, and outcomes. Such surveillance would allow for early detection of outbreaks, better characterization of disease burden, and identification of risk factors for severe outcomes. In parallel, interventional studies are urgently needed to evaluate preventive and therapeutic strategies for hMPV. Vaccine trials should prioritize immunocompromised populations, assessing safety, immunogenicity, and efficacy in reducing severe infection, hospitalization, and ICU admission. Similarly, antiviral trials should be designed to test efficacy and tolerability in patients with hematological malignancies, with careful monitoring for adverse events and organ-specific toxicity. Finally, integrating prospective surveillance with interventional studies would provide a comprehensive framework to guide clinical management, inform infection control strategies, and accelerate the development of effective preventive and therapeutic options for this vulnerable patient population.

In conclusion, infections with hMPV in patients with hematological malignancy are associated with significant morbidity, mortality, and healthcare utilization, comparable with or exceeding that of influenza and SARS-CoV-2, but remain underrecognized and underresourced. This study identifies critical gaps in prevention, diagnosis, and antiviral treatment and calls for action to address these needs in one of the most vulnerable patient populations.
