Nanoparticle-based serine replenishment rescues SP1-BNIP3 mitophagy and ameliorates preeclampsia in preclinical models
Authors: Yinan Wang, Qiongjie Zhou, Huanqiang Zhao, Zhilin Liu, Wei Li, Meng Xu, Jiangyuan Du, Yinfei Chen, Wei Cheng, Xinping Ng, Wenhan Zhou, Xinyan Zhang, Yan Lin, Wei Xu, Shimin Zhao, Yiyuan Yuan, Xiaotian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPreeclampsia (PE) is a devastating hypertensive disorder affecting pregnant women worldwide. Disrupted metabolic reprogramming is recognized as a key feature of placental dysfunction in PE, yet the abnormal metabolic adaption and underlying mechanisms remain largely unknown. In this study, we perform targeted metabolomic profiling and identify placental serine deficiency as a hallmark metabolic alteration in PE, which favors PE occurrence. Serine-deficient chow exacerbates PE-like symptoms, such as hypertension and proteinuria, in mice. Mechanistically, serine deficiency attenuates SAM-dependent methylation, decreasing SP1 levels and impairing SP1-BNIP3-mediated mitophagy, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress to cause placental dysfunction. Notably, targeting serine to the placenta using mPEG5k-poly(D/L-serine) effectively relieves PE-like symptoms in the mouse model. Our findings elucidate an unknown serine-deficiency-mediated metabolic reprogramming in PE and suggest manipulating serine supplementation as a promising translational strategy for PE treatment.Keywords: preeclampsia, serine deficiency, mitophagy, mPEG5k-Poly(D/L-serine)

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE), one of the most severe complications of pregnancy, has emerged as a leading cause of maternal and perinatal mortality globally and profoundly impacts the short- and long-term health of both mother and offspring.1,2,3 Maternal systemic endothelial dysregulation induced by causative factors originating from a malfunctioning placenta, which exacerbates oxidative stress or abnormal metabolic reprogramming across trophoblast cells, is a hallmark of PE. Consequently, systemic endothelial damage puts tremendous stress on organs, causing serious complications, such as hypertension, proteinuria, fetal growth restriction (FGR), and preterm birth. However, despite more than 150 years of research on the pathogenesis of PE, the factors driving placental disorders remain largely unknown.

A successful pregnancy necessitates the proper allocation of limited resources between the mother and fetus.4,5 Unlike other placental mammals that undergo superficial blastocyst implantation, the human blastocyst is entirely embedded within the endometrium. This is accompanied by trophoblast invasion deep into the uterus and remodeling of the spiral artery, facilitating optimal nutrient extraction from the mother to support fetal development.6,7 However, maternal resources must be conserved for survival, leading to a conflict over nutrient allocation between mother and fetus. This conflict may be resolved when resources are ample; however, when specific nutrients are limited, adverse outcomes such as PE may occur.8,9 PE incidence is reportedly seven times higher in underdeveloped countries than in developed nations,10,11 and multiple pregnancies significantly increase PE rates, further indicating that nutrient deficiency may heighten susceptibility to PE onset.12

As a transient yet metabolically active organ, the placenta dynamically adapts its metabolic status during gestation to navigate the resource allocation tug-of-war between the mother and fetus.13,14 Various metabolites undergo intricate changes in the placenta throughout pregnancy, acting as a critical source of energy and essential precursors to support rapid fetal growth.15,16 These metabolites also serve as key regulatory signals in the maternal-fetal dialogue, optimizing pregnancy outcomes, particularly under adverse conditions such as malnutrition and hypoxia.17,18 Furthermore, intermediates, cofactors, and substrates generated by metabolic reactions play a vital role in maintaining placental homeostasis, such as balancing redox equivalents and regulating gene expression.19,20,21 Thus, precise metabolic reprogramming is essential for effective nutrient allocation and harmonious fetal growth, often at the expense of the maternal metabolic profile. Disturbances in placental metabolic adaptation, even minor deficits stemming from metabolite availability, may exacerbate adverse outcomes and contribute to PE pathogenesis. Therefore, identifying metabolites and metabolic pathways that altered in the PE pathogenesis may help decipher the etiology of PE and suggest potential therapies.

Results

Serine deficiency in the placenta of individuals with PE

To explore metabolic alterations contributing to PE pathogenesis, we performed a targeted metabolic survey combined with bioinformatics analysis, comparing placentas from healthy individuals to those diagnosed with PE (Figure 1A). To minimize potential confounding factors, we matched our samples by maternal age and gestational age at delivery (Table S1). Analysis via partial least squares-discriminant analysis revealed distinct metabolite profile separations between healthy and PE groups, confirming significant metabolic differences (Figure S1A). Among the identified candidate metabolites, 79 (19.75%) exhibited considerable changes (Figure 1B). Remarkably, about one-third (29 out of 79) of these altered metabolites were linked to amino acid metabolism pathways, exceeding the total number of metabolites related to carbohydrate and fatty acid metabolism (20 out of 79). Notably, pathway analysis utilizing the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) highlighted serine, glycine, and threonine metabolism (KEGG 00260) (Figure 1C). Within this pathway, serine showcased the most pronounced reduction (Figure S1B), indicating an association between serine deficiency and the presence of PE, which was further verified by comparing serine concentrations in placentas from normotensive pregnancies (34 samples) against those from preeclamptic pregnancies (53 samples; Table S2), no matter early-onset (with gestational age adjustment) or later-onset PE (Figures 1D and S1C). Furthermore, there also has a negative correlation between decreased serine levels and both systolic and diastolic blood pressure in pregnant women across both healthy and preeclamptic groups (Figures 1E and 1F). In summary, our targeted metabolomics analysis and clinical data revealed a notable decrease of serine levels in preeclamptic placentas, suggesting that serine deficiency may have critical roles in the pathogenesis of PE.

Decreased “one-way” serine transportation leads to preeclamptic serine deficiency

Serine in the placenta is derived mostly from maternal transport rather than de novo synthesis.22,23 To address the primary cause of serine reduction in the placenta during preeclampsia, we measured the concentrations of serine and downstream glycine in maternal blood and umbilical cord blood. Interestingly, serine levels were comparable in maternal serum but significantly lower in umbilical serum of preeclampsia (Figures 1G and 1H; Tables S3 and S4), indicating that the serine reduction in the placenta is caused by the defects of placenta itself but not by the decreased serine in maternal circulation. Similarly, glycine, which was mainly synthesized from serine in the placenta and released into fetal circulation for reconversion to serine in the fetal liver, exhibited comparable patterns in both maternal and fetal circulation (Figure S1D). To further investigate whether the serine reduction in the placenta is caused by transportation or de novo synthesis, we first quantified the expression levels of the serine transporters and enzymes involved in serine synthesis in the placenta (Figures S1E and S1F). SLC38A1, a symporter responsible for the “one-way” transfer of short-chain amino acids24 into placental trophoblast cells, was significantly decreased in the placenta of patients with PE at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figures 1I, 1J, and S1G), which was positively correlated with placental serine concentration but negatively with systolic and diastolic blood pressure (Figures S1H and S1I). Consistently, further data mining analysis of GES75010 (the largest RNA sequencing [RNA-seq] of preeclampsia within the GEO datasets) confirmed that the relative expression of SLC38A1, but not that of other serine transporters or enzymes responsible for de novo serine synthesis, was negatively correlated with systolic and diastolic blood pressure (Table S5). Additionally, single-cell RNA-seq analysis revealed that SLC38A1 was predominantly expressed in trophoblast cells (Figures 1K, S1J, and S1K) and exhibited decreased expression specifically in the subpopulations of trophoblasts, including extravillous trophoblasts (EVT) and villous cytotrophoblasts (VCT) in preeclamptic placentas (Figures 1L, 1M, and S1L), which were corroborated by GSE173193 and our research team.25

Taken together, these findings provide compelling evidence that serine deficiency in preeclamptic placentas stems from impaired placental serine transport, primarily driven by decreased expression of SLC38A1 in trophoblasts. This highlights a potential mechanism through which placental dysfunction in PE could adversely affect fetal nutrient supply.

Serine deficiency exacerbates oxidative stress in trophoblasts

To probe the impact of serine deficiency on trophoblast function and its potential contribution to PE, we cultured HTR8/SVneo trophoblast cells in serine-deficient media, which leads to intracellular serine deficiency (Figure S2A). The above-treated cells were subjected to RNA-seq, and 303 differentially expressed transcripts were identified (Figure 2A). Notably, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) underscored significant enrichment of transcripts related to oxidative stress (Figures 2B and S2B),26,27,28,29,30 suggesting that serine may play roles in regulating oxidative stress. To probe this idea, we cultured trophoblast cells in serine-deficient medium under mild oxidative stress, which refers to the physiological condition in placenta. Interestingly, while serine deprivation alone did not elevate reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, extra low doses of H2O2 significantly increased ROS levels in trophoblasts, and this hypersensitivity to oxidative stress could be mitigated by the addition of serine (Figures 2C and 2D). Superoxide dismutase (SOD), which is essential for reducing oxidative stress, was significantly decreased in preeclamptic placentae and exhibited a strong positive correlation with serine concentration (Figures 2E and 2F). Furthermore, siRNA-mediated SLC38A1 knockdown similarly increased ROS levels in HTR8/SVneo cells subjected to low H2O2 amounts (Figures S2C and S2D). In conclusion, these observations indicated that serine deficiency impairs the ability of ROS removal and antioxidant production in trophoblasts, which may exacerbate oxidative stress and consequently the damage of trophoblast cells.

Serine deficiency induces PE-like symptoms in mice

To establish the role of serine deficiency in exacerbating oxidative stress and mimicking PE symptoms in vivo, we fed pregnant mice a serine-deficient diet (SerD) and assessed its effects on maternal blood and placental serine levels, comparing these to control mice fed a normal chow diet: SerN (A10021B, 0.5% Serine31) (Figures S2E and S2F). Additionally, both groups received a low dose of N(ω)-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME, 100 mg/kg/d) to induce mild oxidative stress in the placenta. We noticed that SerD mice had slightly increased blood pressure than the SerN mice in the later gestational time. However, upon L-NAME administration, SerD-LN mice demonstrated a significant elevation in blood pressure relative to controls (Figures 2G and S2G). This increase in blood pressure correlated with noteworthy proteinuria (Figure 2H) at embryonic day 17.5. Histopathological analysis of the SerD-LN mice revealed abnormal stromal collagen deposition in the placental labyrinth layer, glomerular endotheliosis, and compromised kidney capillaries (Figure 2I). The lowest placental weight and fetal weight also indicate that the SerD-LN group exhibits the most severe preeclampsia-like phenotype (Figures 2J–2L). Additionally, SerD-LN mice exhibited increased levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and decreased superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels in the placenta (Figures 2M and 2N), indicating heightened oxidative stress. sFlt-1, a clinical biomarker of preeclampsia, was also significantly elevated in the serum of SerD-LN group (Figure S2H). These findings collectively suggest that the SerD-LN group exhibited the most pronounced preeclampsia-like symptoms.

In addition to serine-deficient diet, we also aimed to determine whether blocking “one-way” serine transportation to the placenta would induce PE-like symptoms in mice. To achieve this goal, we administered pregnant mice with daily intraperitoneal injections of MeAIB (an inhibitor of SLC38A1, 3 mg/d), with or without a low dose of L-NAME (100 mg/kg/d). Expectedly, the serine concentrations in the placenta were significantly reduced in the MeAIB group (Figures S2I and S2J), and the MeAIB+LN mice demonstrated a significant elevation in blood pressure relative to other groups (Figure S2K). This increase in blood pressure correlated with noteworthy proteinuria (Figure S2L) at embryonic day 17.5. Histopathological analysis of the MeAIB+LN group also revealed abnormal stromal collagen deposition in the placental labyrinth layer, glomerular endotheliosis, and compromised kidney capillaries (Figure S2M). Additionally, the MeAIB+LN mice exhibit similar PE-like symptoms as SerD-LN mice, such as lower placental weight and fetal weight (Figures S2N and S2O), as well as altered levels of SOD in the placenta (Figures S2P) and sFlt-1 in the serum (Figures S2Q).

Collectively, the above in vivo experiments convincingly demonstrated that placental serine deficiency, caused either by feeding serine-deficient chow or by blocking the “one-way” serine transportation to the placenta via inhibiting SLC38A1, impairs the ability of reducing oxidative stress in the trophoblast cells, which leads to an exacerbation of placental oxidative stress and severe PE-like symptoms consequently.

Serine deficiency impairs mitochondrial functions through downregulating BNIP3-mediated mitophagy

To further investigate the mechanisms by which serine deficiency exacerbates oxidative stress in trophoblasts, we conducted Gene Ontology-cellular component (GO-C) analysis on differentially expressed transcripts. This analysis revealed a substantial number of these transcripts localized to the mitochondria (Figure 3A). Given the pivotal role of mitochondrial dysfunction in PE pathogenesis, we hypothesized that serine deficiency might impair mitochondrial function, thereby increasing oxidative stress to induce the onset of PE.

Then, we first examined mitochondrial morphological integrity through transmission electron microscopy. HTR-8/SVneo cells cultured in serine-deficient medium and exposed to low doses of H2O2 exhibited classic signs of mitochondrial damage, including swelling, outer membrane rupture, and distorted cristae (Figure 3B). Further functional analyses revealed that serine deprivation impairs mitochondrial respiration function (Figures 3C and 3D), which led to significantly reduced ATP production (Figure 3E), diminished mitochondrial membrane potential (Figures 3F and S3A), and impaired mitochondrial permeability transition (Figures 3G and S3A), all pointing to compromised mitochondrial function and heightened oxidative stress in serine starvation trophoblasts.

Subsequently, we performed protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis based on our RNA-seq data, which identified 13 differentially expressed mitochondrial pathway genes that were highly interconnected (Figures 3H and S3B). Notably, BNIP3 emerged as the most downregulated mitophagy protein in placentas from patients with PE, a finding that was robustly recapitulated in trophoblast cells subjected to serine deprivation (Figures 3I–3K; Figures S3C and S3D). Intriguingly, BNIP3 expression levels showed a strong positive correlation with serine levels (Figure 3L; Table S2). Immunofluorescence revealed significant downregulation of BNIP3 in trophoblasts of PE placentas (Figure S3E), consistent with reduced BNIP3 expression in placentas from SerD-LN mice compared to SerN-LN mice (Figures S3F and S3G). Moreover, both serine deprivation and SLC38A1 knockdown resulted in decreased BNIP3 transcription (Figure 3M), accompanied by reduction of downstream molecules involved in BNIP3-mediated mitophagy, such as LC3B-II/LC3B-I in HTR8/SVneo (Figures 3N and 3O). In contrast, proteins related to mitophagy via alternative pathways, including PINK1, ATF4, and NIX, did not change significantly (Figure S3H). Notably, serine deprivation led to the inhibition of mitophagic flux (Figure 3P), while serine supplementation can alleviate the inhibition of mitophagic flux caused by BNIP3 downregulation (Figure S3I). Furthermore, both serine deprivation and inhibition of SLC38A1 knockdown corresponded with lower LC3B levels in the mitochondria of HTR8/SVneo cells, indicating impaired mitophagy; conversely, BNIP3 overexpression alleviated the LC3B level reduction caused by serine deficiency (Figures S3J–S3L), which suggested the indispensable role of serine in sustaining mitochondrial function through modulating BNIP3-mediated mitophagy.

In conclusion, these insights indicated that serine deficiency specifically hampered mitochondrial functions through the downregulation of BNIP3-mediated mitophagy. Understanding BNIP3’s role in serine deficiency, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress in trophoblasts could reveal therapeutic targets for intervention in PE.

Serine deficiency reduces S-adenosylmethionine level to indirectly decrease BNIP3 transcription

To investigate how serine deficiency affects BNIP3 expression, we performed pathway analyses that revealed an enrichment of differentially expressed enzymes involved in one-carbon metabolism and glycolysis (Figures S4A and S4B), which corresponds with targeted metabolomic profiling of placental tissues from patients with PE (Figure S4C). To delineate the metabolic adaptations to serine starvation, we performed stable isotope tracing with U-13C glucose in HTR8/SVneo cells cultured under serine-replete or serine-deficient conditions. Integrated Mass Isotopomer Distribution and Relative Quantification (Figures 4A and 4B) revealed that serine deprivation suppresses global glycolytic, TCA cycle, and one-carbon metabolite levels. Despite this overall metabolic downregulation, isotopic tracing demonstrated a compensatory rerouting of glucose-derived carbon toward serine synthesis, as indicated by increased m+3 3-phosphoglycerate and diminished glucose entry into the TCA cycle (evidenced by predominant m+0 malate). However, this adaptation failed to maintain one-carbon metabolism, resulting in impaired de novo synthesis of S-adenosylmethionine (accumulation of m+0 SAM). Thus, serine starvation triggers a prioritized yet insufficient metabolic rewiring that accentuates serine synthesis at the cost of mitochondrial metabolism but ultimately compromises one-carbon cycle fidelity. Remarkably, enzymes associated with one-carbon metabolism, specifically SHMT2, PHGDH, and CBS, were significantly upregulated, potentially compensating for decreased serine availability through enhanced de novo serine synthesis. Conversely, enzymes involved in glycolysis, such as IDH2, ALDOA, and LDHA, were downregulated, a trend corroborated in serine-starved HTR8/SVneo cells and in mice fed with serine-deficient chow (SerD-LN mice) (Figures S4D–S4F).

SAM, a crucial metabolite derived from serine that acts as a methyl donor, was found to be significantly reduced in the preeclamptic placenta. We observed a similar decrease in SAM levels in serine-starved HTR8/SVneo cells (Figure 4C), in the placentas of SerD-LN mice (Figure 4D), and most importantly, in the placental samples from patients with PE (Figure 4E). Notably, SAM levels positively correlated with serine and BNIP3 concentrations (Figures 4F and 4G). Exogenous SAM supplementation partially restored BNIP3 expression at both transcriptional and translational levels compromised by serine deficiency in trophoblast cells (Figures 4H and 4I), improved LC3B fluorescence intensity (Figures 4J and 4K, S5A), and mitigated the excess ROS levels induced by serine starvation or SLC38A1 knockdown (Figures S5B and S5C), pointing to its protective role against mitophagy impairment arising from serine deficiency.

Correspondingly, dot blot experiments demonstrated that serine deficiency reduced overall methylation levels in HTR8/SVneo cells (Figure 4L). To determine whether decreased BNIP3 expression was caused by its methylation alterations, we first analyzed whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) data from eight preeclamptic and nine healthy control samples (GEO: GSE154348) and then performed pyrosequencing validation in serine-deficient HTR8/SVneo cells. Surprisingly, no significant differences in differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were detected between con and PE groups, indicating that serine deficiency did not influence BNIP3 methylation levels in either the promoter or gene body regions in HTR8/SVneo cells (Figure S6A), which suggested an indirect role of serine in regulating BNIP3 at the transcriptional level.

Serine deficiency decreases BNIP3 through the transcriptional inhibition of SP1

To further investigate the mechanism by which serine regulates BNIP3 at the transcriptional level, we predicted 81 potential transcription factors of BNIP3 using PROMO. The GSE75196 dataset (450K array), which contains 8 preeclamptic and 16 normal samples, was selected to identify abnormally methylated genes in preeclamptic placentae. A total of 6,822 abnormally methylated genes were screened by GEO2R. Eight of the eleven intersection genes obtained from these two datasets displayed abnormal methylation levels, and USF132 and SP1,33,34,35 two transcription factors that have never been linked to PE, had the top scores based on JASPAR (Figure 5A). Decreased SP1 and increased USF1 levels were detected in serine-starved HTR8/SVneo cells, which could be reversed by SAM supplementation (Figure 5B), suggesting that SP1 and USF1 could be the downstream targets of SAM in trophoblasts. Interestingly, although WGBS analysis of preeclamptic placental tissue (GEO: GSE154348) revealed no significant differences in the methylation sites of USF1 (Figure S6B), hypomethylated regions were characterized in the SP1 gene body (Figure 5C), which were further verified by both pyrosequencing and targeted DNA methylation sequencing in cells cultured under serine-deficient conditions (Figure S6C, data not shown). Since increasement of methylation in the gene body region commonly increase the gene expression,36 SAM may positively regulate SP1 level using the same mechanism. As we expected, exogenous SAM restored cellular SP1 levels, which were decreased by SP1 siRNA (Figure 5D; Figures S7A and S7B), serine deprivation (Figures 5E and 5F), and SLC38A1 knockdown (Figure S7C). Consistently, SP1 downregulation was observed in both preeclamptic placentae (Figures 5G and 5H, S7D) and SerD-LN mouse placentae (Figure 5I), and more importantly, SP1 was positively correlated with serine levels in the preeclamptic placenta (Figure 5J). These results suggested that serine deficiency decreases the methylation level of the SP1 gene body in preeclamptic placentae by downregulating SAM, thereby leading to a decrease in cellular SP1 levels.

To validate whether SP1 is a transcription factor that directly regulates BNIP3, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP-qPCR) using primers specific for the SP1 motif (Figure 5K), which revealed that SP1 directly binds to the promoter region of BNIP3 (Figures 5L and S7E). Further reduction of SP1 through siSP1 inhibited BNIP3 at the transcriptional and translational levels in HTR8/SVneo cells (Figures 5M, S7F and S7G), and the dual-luciferase reporter assay further confirmed that SP1 is a key factor that transcriptionally regulates BNIP3 (Figure 5N). Moreover, the reduction of SP1 by siSP1 inhibited the fluorescence intensity of LC3B, indicating the mitigation of mitophagy damage (Figures 5O and S7H). In contrast, SP1 overexpression alleviated the reduction in BNIP3 expression and mitophagy caused by serine deficiency (Figures S7I–S7K). Furthermore, SP1 overexpression or SAM supplementation rescued the reduction in SP1 induced by serine deprivation or SP1 knockdown (Figures S7L). Hence, these results provide sufficient evidences for the significant role of serine in mitophagy during oxidative stress in the placenta; that is, decreased SAM levels caused by serine deficiency in the placenta reduce methylation in the gene body of SP1, thus inhibiting mitophagy by downregulating SP1 and BNIP3 consequently.

Serine supplementation alleviates oxidative stress and PE symptoms in vivo

Recognizing the role of serine deficiency in exacerbating oxidative stress and facilitating PE development, we questioned whether serine supplementation could alleviate PE-like symptoms in vivo. Considering that placental serine deficiency in preeclampsia is caused by abnormalities in serine transporters, simply oral serine supplementation may not achieve satisfactory therapeutic effects for preeclampsia. Therefore, we developed a serine-based nanodrug [mPEG5k-poly(D/L-serine), PS] by nanoprecipitation.37 This nano-formulated serine can leverage the ability of nanomedicines to cross the placental barrier, enabling its entry into the placenta independent of transporter activity (Figure 6A). Successful synthesis of the PS was verified by 1H NMR and gel permeation chromatography (Figures 6B and S8A). PS self-assembled into nanomicelles in aqueous solution, confirmed by Tyndall effect and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging (Figure S8B). PS exhibited a zeta potential of approximately −1.5 mV (Figure S8C) and a critical micelle concentration of 22.4 μg/mL (Figures S8D and S8E), demonstrating exceptional dilution resistance suitable for sustained systemic delivery. In vitro, PS showed sustained release, with 40% serine released over 72 h (Figure S8F). Pharmacokinetic evaluation in C57BL/6 male mice revealed significantly elevated plasma serine levels in the PS-treated group compared to both PBS controls and free oral serine (Figures S8G and S8H). These results establish PS as a robust long-circulating nanoplatform with enhanced bioavailability and therapeutic potential.

Based on the established principle that nanoparticle size governs placental penetration, we engineered ∼170 nm PS to passive targeting (Figure S8I). Using near-infrared cyanine 7 (Cy-7) labeling, we tracked PS accumulation specifically in placental tissue 10 h post-injection in pregnant mice (Figures S8J and S8K). Histological analysis revealed diffuse Cy-7 signal enrichment within the placental labyrinth, a key site in preeclampsia pathogenesis (Figure 6C). After dose screening, 10 mg PS was selected for in vivo administration (Figure S8L). Unlike orally administered serine, which distributed broadly across multiple organs, intravenously delivered PS exhibited prolonged circulation and delayed placental passive targeting (Figures S8M and S8N). These findings provide a mechanistic basis for targeted serine delivery and support the therapeutic potential of nanodrug strategies for preeclampsia management.

In vivo, pregnant mice were subjected to PE induction via a high dose of L-NAME (185 mg/kg/day) and received either 2% serine in drinking water or intravenous injection of PS. As we expected, serine supplementation markedly increased placental serine levels (Figure 6D) without significantly elevating serum serine levels (Figures S8O). Reduced blood pressure and urine protein (Figures 6E and 6F) indicated an alleviation of preeclampsia-like symptoms. Critically, treatment with PS substantially alleviated abnormal collagen deposition in the placenta, glomerular endotheliosis, and capillary occlusion in the kidneys of preeclamptic mice (Figure 6G). Although the intervention had a negligible impact on placental weight (Figure 6H), PS administration effectively elevated fetal weight (Figure 6I). Moreover, a reduction in malondialdehyde (MDA) was detected (Figure 6J), indicating a decline of oxidative stress. Concurrently, elevated SAM levels were recorded in the mouse placenta (Figure 6K), correlating with the upregulation of SP1 and BNIP3 (Figure 6L) and the restoration of mitophagy (Figures 6M, S8P, and S8Q). As anticipated, sFlt-1 levels were significantly reduced in the PS group compared to the LN group (Figure S8R). Collectively, these findings indicated the potential therapeutic value of PS for PE.

Finally, to remove the concerns of potential toxicity of PS, we further evaluated the acute toxicity of PS 24 h after a single administration. It is pleasing that there has no significant alterations in liver function, renal function, myocardial injury markers, or complete blood count (Figures S9A–S9D) and has no notable abnormalities in the placenta (Figure S9E), which demonstrated that PS administration induces no significant acute toxicity in pregnant mice.

In conclusion, these in vivo findings illustrated that serine administration mitigated oxidative stress and alleviated PE-like symptoms by enhancing SP1-BNIP3-mediated mitophagy, indicating that serine supplementation may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for preeclampsia management.

Discussion

Serine is one of the most challenging amino acids to obtain from dietary proteins and requires a range of transporters or de novo synthesis to meet its supply needs.38,39 However, serine levels are remarkably high and exhibit a metabolic paradigm in the uteroplacental circulation,40,41,42 highlighting its vital role for healthy pregnancy. In this study, we presented compelling evidence that placental serine deficiency, caused by decreased “one-way” serine transporter SLC38A1, is closely associated with the pathogenesis of PE. Following mechanism experiments further elucidated how inadequate serine availability damaged mitophagy, leading to excessive cellular oxidative stress, a critical factor in PE development. Besides, thinking of the importance of serine for neuronal development,43,44 our findings perfectly aligned with clinical observations that infants exposed to maternal PE are at a heightened risk for neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders.45,46 Thus, our work not only highlights serine metabolism, rather than lipid or carbohydrate metabolism, as a pivotal player in the etiology of preeclampsia, but also positions it as a potential metabolic target for intervention.

Mitophagy, which removes and recycles dysfunctional mitochondria to maintain cellular functions, contributes to diverse aspects of human health, such as aging and diseases.47,48,49 During pregnancy, the placenta endures continuous oxidative stress,50 and mitophagy is triggered to remove dysfunctional mitochondria.51 Although the underlying mechanisms remain largely unexplored, altered mitophagy and elevated ROS levels are commonly reported in the placentas of patients with PE.47,48 Utilizing RNA-seq analysis and bioinformatics, we uncovered a previously unrecognized mechanism of serine in regulating BNIP3-mediated mitophagy to sustain optimal placental oxidative stress for healthy pregnancy. Mechanistically, adequate serine-derived SAM in the trophoblasts upregulates SP1 at the transcriptional level via elevating methylation in the gene body region of SP1, which in turn increases BNIP3 transcription to counteract oxidative stress by facilitating the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria. On the contrary, serine deficiency inhibits BNIP3-mediated mitophagy, thereby accumulating defective mitochondria and exacerbating oxidative stress to trigger pathogenesis of PE. These findings highlighted an unrecognized role of serine metabolism in sustaining placental homeostasis and revealed SP1 as a transcription factor that regulates BNIP3-mediated autophagy to reduce oxidative damage.

Our findings propose serine could serve as an innovative therapeutic agent for PE. Although dietary interventions can be both preventive and therapeutic in animal models, it is worth noting that leveraging passive targeting, PS can effectively traverse the placental barrier52,53,54 and showed diffused distribution throughout the placenta, which perfectly bypass the defects of SLC38A1 that causes serine deficiency in placenta. Upon arriving at the placenta, the PS undergo degradation into its constituent components, PEG and serine, which are safe materials and do not have any acute toxicity to the animal. Moreover, this targeted approach using our nanoparticles aligns seamlessly with the principles of precision medicine and presents a promising avenue for the treatment of not only PE but also other placental-derived disorders in the future.

In summary, our study establishes a mechanistic connection between serine deficiency and PE pathogenesis, highlighting intensified placental oxidative stress exacerbated by downregulated SP1-BNIP3-mediated mitophagy. This underscores the pivotal role of serine metabolism in PE, and administration of PS offers a low-risk yet promising therapeutic avenue for PE management.

Limitations of the study

Although our study identifies serine metabolism as a key driver in preeclampsia pathogenesis with translational therapeutic potential, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, whether serine deficiency interacts with other pathways beyond impaired mitophagy to trigger PE remains unclear and requires further exploration. Second, although serine supplementation shows beneficial effects in mice, optimal dosage, timing, and delivery routes must be defined for clinical use. Large-scale clinical trials are warranted to validate its safety and efficacy. Additionally, as PE is a multifactorial disorder, serine supplementation may not benefit all patients, especially those with immune-related etiologies. Thus, stratifying patients by clinical and metabolic features could enable more personalized therapeutic strategies for PE.

Experimental model and study participant details

In vivo animal studies

The C57BL/6J mice, aged 6 to 8 weeks, weighing approximately 18–20 g, were purchased from Jicui Laboratory (Shanghai, China), and all animal procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines set forth by the Fudan University for the care and use of laboratory animals. The mice were maintained in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment with unrestricted access to standard chow and water. The lighting conditions were automatically regulated to achieve 12-h light-dark cycles. All animals were acclimatized to the new environment for 1 week before commencing the experiments. All animal experiments were performed by two independent investigators in a blinded manner. The experimental unit is a single animal.

Human clinical studies

Human placental tissues, maternal serum and umbilical cord serum were obtained from the tissue bank and preeclampsia-specific disease database of the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital, Fudan University. All participants are Chinese. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital, Fudan University. Samples of placental tissue, maternal serum, and umbilical cord blood were categorized into three groups. The diagnosis of preeclampsia was made according to the guidelines set forth by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) Hypertension. Informed written consents were obtained from all participants.

Cell lines and primary cultures

The HTR-8/SVneo cells were generously provided by the Key Laboratory of Reproductive Endocrine- Related Diseases, Shanghai. The Bewo and JAR cells were purchased from Wuhan SUNNCELL Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The HTR-8/SVneo and JAR cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Biological Industries) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin (Invitrogen). The Bewo cell was cultured in F12K medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin. The cells were trypsinized and passaged when they achieved 80–90% confluence. The serine-free medium was purchased from Shanghai Harmonious One Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The cells were cultured with medium for 24 h. Then the medium was replaced with serine-free medium and cultured continuously for 48 h. Serine was added to the serine-free medium as the control group for the serine deficiency experiment. The above-mentioned cell lines were authenticated and tested for mycoplasma contamination upon purchase. After use, no further mycoplasma testing was performed because the cells remained in good condition.

Method details

Clinical sample preparation and analysis

Clinical specimens Human placental tissues, maternal serum and umbilical cord serum were obtained from the tissue bank and preeclampsia-specific disease database of the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital, Fudan University. All participants are Chinese. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital, Fudan University. Samples of placental tissue, maternal serum, and umbilical cord blood were categorized into three groups. The diagnosis of preeclampsia was made according to the guidelines set forth by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) Hypertension. Informed written consents were obtained from all participants. PE was diagnosed as the presence of persistent, new onset hypertension after 20 weeks of gestation (systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg) with proteinuria (urine protein quantification ≥0.3 g/24 h or urine protein/creatinine ratio ≥0.3 or Dipstick reading of 2+). PE can also be diagnosed in the absence of proteinuria if one or more of the following organ dysfunctions are present: thrombocytopenia (platelet count 1.1 mg/dL); elevated liver transaminases (>2-fold higher than normal); pulmonary; or other organ dysfunction: such as neurological symptoms, pulmonary edema, visual disturbances, etc. after 20 weeks of gestation. Exclusion criteria for both patients with PE and normal pregnant women were (1) diabetes mellitus, (2) chronic hypertension, (3) renal diseases, (4) autoimmune diseases, (5) cardiovascular diseases, and (6) chronic hypertension complicating pregnancy. Age and gestational age were matched between the groups.

Clinical sample collection:Trained study staff collected placentas within 30 min of delivery using a standardized protocol. In brief, a 1 cm3 placental tissue sample was collected from the maternal-facing side (within a 5 cm radius of the corresponding location of cord insertion on the other side). The villous placental biopsy samples were collected, excluding decidual tissue. These placental samples were stored in liquid nitrogen for 15 min, then stored in a −80°C freezer for later use. Approximately 5 mL of venous blood from pregnant women and umbilical cord were collected using a standardized protocol. Blood samples were drawn into coagulation-promoting tubes and stored for 3 min, then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Upper layer was stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Correlation analysis between the transporter and maternal diastolic or systolic blood pressure:A correlation analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between the relative expression levels of transporters and metabolic enzymes and maternal maximum systolic and diastolic blood pressures using data from GSE75010, the largest RNA-seq database for preeclampsia. GraphPad software was used to plot the results.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA):Bioassay Technology Laboratory ELISA kits (China) were used to assess the levels of serine (μmol/L), glycine (pg/mL), BNIP3 (pg/mL), SLC38A1 (ng/mL), SAM (ng/mL), SP1 (ng/mL), SOD (U/mL, pg/mL), MDA (nmol/mL) and sFlt-1 (pg/mL).

ANCOVA of serine levels between EOPE and control groups: A linear regression model was used to assess the effect of early-onset preeclampsia (EOPE) on serine concentrations, with gestational age included as a confounding factor. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test and the Breusch-Pagan test. The presence of an interaction between group and gestational age was examined by comparing the model with an interaction term to the main effects model. Type II analysis of variance was performed to assess the contribution of each variable, and least-squares means were calculated to estimate and compare serine concentrations between the two groups after adjustment for gestational age. Model robustness was evaluated by examining the normality of residuals using the Shapiro-Wilk test and by inspecting residual plots.

Immunofluorescence:Normal and preeclamptic placental tissue sections were deparaffinized and blocked, then incubated with a mixture of anti-CK7 and anti-SLC38A1 antibodies overnight at 4°C. After PBS washing, a mixture of fluorescence-conjugated goat anti-rabbit and anti-mouse secondary antibodies was applied and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Following thorough PBS washing, sections were incubated with Vector dye for 5 min, washed again with PBS, and then counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Finally, the sections were mounted with mounting medium and visualized/captured using a Thunder imaging system (Leica).

Omics analysis

Metabolomics:For metabolomics analysis, about 75 mg placental sample were homogenized and extracted with 200 μL extraction solution (acetonitrile: methanol: water = 2:2:1), followed by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube and stored in a −80°C freezer overnight to allow for complete sedimentation of insoluble materials. Analysis of serum metabolites was performed on a Shimadzu Prominence HPLC system (ExionLC AD) interfaced with a QTRAP 6500+ system (AB SCIEX). The sample injection volume was 5 μL. Metabolites were separated through an iHILIC-(P) Classic HPLC HILIC column (150 × 2.1 mm with 5 μm particle size, HILICON) with column temperature maintained at 25°C. Mobile phases consisted of 20 mM ammonium acetate in 25 mM ammonia water (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile (mobile phase B) and was run at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The gradient was as follows: 0–2 min, 85% B; 7 min, 60% B; 12 min, 35% B; 12.1–15.9 min, 20% B; 16–23 min, 85% B. The mass spectrometer was with multiple reactions monitoring (MRM). The ESI source parameters: source temperature of 500°C, the ion source gas 1 and 2 at 60 psi, the curtain gas at 35 psi, the ion spray voltage floating at 5.5 kV or −4.5 kV for positive or negative modes. Data was processed and analyzed using SCIEX OS software (AB SCIEX). Software OS was used to establish methods based on the parent ion/product ion (MRM) of metabolites, and we compared the identified peaks with the retention times of standard substances for identification. This falled under level 1 identification. Metabolite profiles were analyzed using MetaboAnalyst, version 4.0. Furthermore, enrichment analysis was performed to assess whether the genes or metabolites observed in specific pathways were detected more frequently than expected by random chance.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and data processing:Raw data from GSE173193 and the dataset of this study were processed using the Seurat R package (version 4.0). A generalized linear model was used to filter delocalized and low-quality cells. The harmony function was used to integrate the datasets. Principal component analysis was performed for linear dimensionality reduction based on gene expression. t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding for nonlinear dimensionality reduction was performed to further visualize the results in two-dimensional space. Seurat was used to identify marker genes, defined as genes exhibiting upregulated expression in specific cell classifications compared with that of other cell groups. vinPlot and feature-plot functions were used to visualize the identified marker genes.

Transcriptomics sequencing and analysis:The HTR-8/SVneo cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium or serine-deficient RPMI 1640 media for 48 h. The cells were harvested and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA). Total RNA was qualified and quantified using a Fragment Analyzer or Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, CA, USA), or Qseq-400 (Bioptic, Taiwan, China).

The library preparation was carried out using the Optimal Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit (BGI-Shenzhen, China). In brief, an appropriate amount of total RNA was denatured at an optimal temperature to break secondary structures, then mRNA was enriched using magnetic beads coated with oligo (dT), and was fragmented using standard procedure. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer-primed reverse transcription, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis. After end repair for the newly synthesized double-stranded cDNA, which add a single 'A' nucleotide to the 3′ ends of the blunt-ended fragments, the adaptors were then ligated to the cDNA fragments in a configured ligation reaction system, and the library products were amplified by PCR and subjected to quality control. The library products were denatured and circularized to produce single-stranded cyclized DNA products, with any remaining uncyclized single-stranded linear DNA molecules being digested. The final single-stranded circularized library undergoes amplification via phi29 and rolling circle amplification (RCA) to form DNA nanoballs (DNBs), which containing over 300 copies of the original single-stranded circularized library molecule. The DNBs were loaded onto a patterned nanoarray, and single end reads of 50 bp were generated on the mgiseq2000 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China). Subsequent data analyses were performed using the BGI Dr.Tom Analysis Platform. An absolute log2 fold change of >0.5 indicated the presence of DEGs and a false discovery rate-adjusted p value of <0.05. These DEGs were further analyzed using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis to identify the metabolites associated with metabolic pathways. Gene Ontology-cellular component (GO-C) analysis was performed to determine the cellular localization of clustered DEGs. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis was performed using mitochondria-related DEGs to identify the key genes involved in mitochondrial function.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to interrogate the enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in predefined biological gene sets using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.14.6). The analysis incorporated five gene sets: autophagy-related genes (n = 232), the hallmark of angiogenesis (n = 36), immune-related genes (n = 2483), oxidative stress-related genes (n = 1399), and senescence-related genes (n = 125). All gene sets were curated from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) and relevant literature. The enrichment score (ES) and normalized enrichment score (NES) were computed to quantify the degree of enrichment for each set. Gene sets with an adjusted p-value <0.05 and |NES| > 1 were considered statistically significant. The results were visualized using enrichment plots and ridge plots.

Methylome analysis:All the WGBS data were selected from GEO dataset (accession: GSE154348). 9 normal samples (GSM488404, GSM4669399, GSM4669394, GSM4669389, GSM4669351, GSM 4669346, GSM4669424, GSM4669419, GSM4669414), and 8 preeclamptic samples (GSM4669409, GSM4669341, GSM4669336, GSM4669331, GSM4669326, GSM4669321, GSM4669316, GSM4669311). All the raw data were filtered by the software of FastQC, removing the low-quality sequencing reads. Then the clean data were mapped to human genome (hg38) by Bsmap (version 2.74). Later, 10-folds covered regions were kept in the final. Different methylation regions (DMR) were recognized if the DMR more than 50% between the normal and preeclampsia sample group. Therefore, the DMR regions in SP1, USF1 and BINP3 both promoter regions and genebody ones were chosen in the following test.

Targeted DNA methylation sequencing: Using the Qiagen, DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit, genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from the cell line. The quantity and quality of gDNA was measured using Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States). The DNA is then converted to bisulfite using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Based on our analysis, the two most promising candidate genes—SP1 and USF1—were identified. Optimized primers and probes compatible with DNA methylation sequencing were subsequently designed. Following this, targeted DNA methylation sequencing was performed to evaluate the methylation status of these three genes. Targeted PCR selects Takara Epi Taq HS purchased by Takara manufacturer (DNA for Bisulite treatment). The Epi Taq HS of each sample well was 0.25 μL, the PCR Buffer was 5 μL, and the MgCl2 was 5 μL. The dNTP mixture is 6 μL, the DNA template after bisulfite conversion is 100 ng, 1μL of forward primer and 1μL of reverse primer are added respectively, and 50 μL of sterile water is added. The PCR amplification steps are as follows: The first step is 5 min at 95°C; Step 2:95°C for 15 s, 53°C for 30 s, 72°C for 30 s, 40 cycles; Step 3: 72°C for 3 min. The resulting libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform (NovaSeq 6000) with paired-end reads. Methylation levels at each CpG site were quantified by calculating the ratio of methylated cytosine reads to the total number of reads covering that position.

Animal experiments

Animal Experiment Design: The C57BL/6J mice, aged 6 to 8 weeks, weighing approximately 18–20 g, were purchased from Jicui Laboratory (Shanghai, China), and all animal procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines set forth by the Fudan University for the care and use of laboratory animals. The mice were maintained in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment with unrestricted access to standard chow and water. The lighting conditions were automatically regulated to achieve 12-h light-dark cycles. All animals were acclimatized to the new environment for 1 week before commencing the experiments. All animal experiments were performed by two independent investigators in a blinded manner. To determine sample size, power calculations were performed based on smaller cohorts of mice to determine mean values of parameters to be analyzed and standard variations. The experimental unit is a single animal.

To investigate the effect of serine deficiency diet on the blood pressure of pregnant mice, a total of 24 C57BL/6J female were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6), and additional 12 C57BL/6J male were used for the mating purpose. The mice in the SerD and SerD-LN groups were fed a serine-deficient diet, whereas the mice in the SerN and SerN-LN groups were fed a normal diet for 2 months. Blood samples were collected from the cheek, and the serum serine levels were measured using ELISA kits in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Bioassay Technology Laboratory). Then, the virgin female mice were mated with stud male mice overnight at a 2:1 ratio, and the presence of a copulation plug the following morning was confirmed to designate the day as embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5). The pregnant mice in the SerN group and SerD group received subcutaneous injections of 0.9% saline, whereas the mice in the SerN-LN group and SerD-LN group received low-dose N(ω)-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME) (100 mg/kg/day) from E7.5 to GD E15.5. The key indicators of the occurrence and development of preeclampsia, including blood pressure, urinary protein, placentae and fetal weight, were measured and analyzed at gestational day 17.5 (E17.5).

To determine the placental targeting of mPEG5k-Poly(D/L-serine) (PS), a total of 18 female and 9 male C57BL/6J mice were used as described above. The pregnant female mice were divided into 6 groups using simple randomization (n = 3). On gestation day 12.5 (E12.5), mice were administrated of 80 mg of serine with oral gavage, or 10 mg or 20 mg of PS via tail vein injection, respectively. Mice were euthanized after 1 h or 10 h later, and the concentrations of serine in the liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and placentae were measured using the ELISA method.

To investigate whether oral serine and PS could alleviate preeclampsia-like symptoms in pregnant mice. A total of 24 female and 10 male C57BL/6J mice were used as described above, and pregnant mice were randomly divided into four groups: CON group (n = 6); LN-Con group (n = 6); LN-Ser Group (n = 6) and LN-PS group (n = 6). A higher dose of L-NAME (185 mg/kg body weight/day) was administered to induce severe symptoms of preeclampsia in pregnant mice (LN-Con group) in the reconstitution experiment. L-serine was supplemented in drinking water (2%) for the LN-Ser group, whereas Poly (ethylene glycol)-Serine (PS) was administered via the intravenous route to the LN-PS group to increase the serine levels in the placenta. The key indicators of the incidence and development of preeclampsia, including blood pressure, urinary protein, placentae and fetal weight, were measured and analyzed at gestation day 17.5 (E17.5).

To investigate the effect of downregulated SLC38A1 on blood pressure in pregnant mice, a total of 24 C57BL/6J female were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6), and additional 12 C57BL/6J male were used for the mating purpose. Virgin females were then housed overnight with stud males at a 2:1 ratio. The following morning, the presence of a vaginal plug was confirmed to designate the day as embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5). From E7.5 to E15.5, mice in the MeAIB and MeAIB-LN groups received daily intraperitoneal injections of the SLC38A1 functional inhibitor MeAIB (3 mg/150 μL), while those in the Control and MeAIB groups were administered subcutaneous injections of PBS. In contrast, the LN and MeAIB-LN groups received subcutaneous injections of low-dose N(ω)-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME; 100 mg/kg/day). Key indicators of preeclampsia progression, including blood pressure, urinary protein levels, placental weight, and fetal weight, were measured at gestational day 17.5 (E17.5). Serine and SLC38A1 levels in the placenta were quantified by ELISA.

Measurements of Blood Pressure and Urinary Protein in Mice: The blood pressure and urinary protein levels were measured to assess the symptoms of preeclampsia in the mice. The blood pressure in conscious mice was determined indirectly by tail cuff plethysmography with a Visitech System BP2000 (Apex, NC, USA). A habituation period was observed before formal evaluation, during which they were subjected to the measurement procedure eight times daily from E4.5. Formal blood pressure assessments were conducted when the mice were in stable condition only. At least six consecutive measurements were recorded. Blood pressure was monitored at six time points during pregnancy (E5.5, E8.5, E10.5, E12.5, and E15.5).

A 24-h urine sample was collected from the mice housed in metabolic cages between E16.5 and E17.5 to analyze urinary protein levels. The urine samples were stored at −80°C until further analysis. An enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Catalog #: P0010) was used in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions to quantify the urinary protein levels.

Histopathology and Immunofluorescence: Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed for the histological analysis of mouse kidney and placental changes. The mice were euthanized on E17.5 after urine collection was completed, and the kidney and placental samples were harvested. A portion of each sample was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 12 h. The tissue samples were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm, and stained using standard H&E staining protocols.

Cell culture

The HTR-8/SVneo cells were generously provided by the Key Laboratory of Reproductive Endocrine-Related Diseases, Shanghai. The Bewo and JAR cells were purchased from Wuhan SUNNCELL Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The HTR-8/SVneo and JAR cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Biological Industries) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin (Invitrogen). The Bewo cell was cultured in F12K medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin. The cells were cultured with medium for 24 h. Then the medium was replaced with serine-free medium and cultured continuously for 48 h. Serine was added to the serine-free medium as the control group for the serine deficiency experiment.

Experiments related to ROS and mitochondria

Intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species Measurement:A 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) cellular ROS assay kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Catalog#: S0033M) was used to measure the intracellular ROS levels. The HTR-8/SVneo cells were stained with 10 μM DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37°C. An Olympus fluorescence microscope and Microplate Reader were used to acquire fluorescence images.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay:JC-1 dye (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Catalog#: C2003S), which exhibits green fluorescence in its monomeric form and red fluorescence when aggregated, was used to evaluate the mitochondrial membrane potential. The HTR-8/SVneo cells were cultured in six-well plates and subjected to various treatments. CCCP (10 mM) was added to the cell culture medium at a ratio of 1:1000, diluted to 10 μM, and retained for 20 min to treat the cells. JC-1 (1 mL) was added to the medium, incubated for 30 min, and washed twice with phosphate buffer saline. The cells were treated with an anti-fade reagent and observed under an Olympus fluorescence microscope.

Mitochondrial permeability transition pore assay:The mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening in the cultured HTR-8/SVneo cells was assessed using the Calcein/Co2+ quenching method, as outlined in the) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, the HTR-8/SVneo cells were incubated with 1 mL of Calcein-AM (green) and 10 μL of CoCl2 at 37°C for 20 min in phenol red-free Hank’s Buffered Salt Solution after treatment. Olympus fluorescence microscope was used to image the live cells after washing.

Transmission Electron Microscopy: The HTR-8/SVneo cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium or serine-deficient RPMI 1640 media for 48 h. Cells were harvested and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at room temperature for 6 h, then were gradient dehydrated using ethanol at 30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%. The samples were dried using the CO2 critical point dryer, and were infiltrated with resin and polymerized it at 60°C overnight to obtain a solid block. The ultramicrotome (EM UC7, Leica, Germany) was used to cut thin sections (50–100 nm) from the resin-embedded sample and placed the sections onto copper grids. 120kV Transmission Electron Microscope (Talos L120C G2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to observe the sample at the desired magnification.

Seahorse assay: Cells were seeded into the XFe96 Microplates (Seahorse Bioscience) to measure the oxygen consumption rate (OCR: an indicator of mitochondrial respiration). OCR were measured under basal conditions and after the addition of mitochondrial inhibitors (oligomycin, carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP) and rotenone/antimycin A). Seahorse datasets were normalised to cell number using Hoechst 33258 fluorescent dye signal measured immediately after Seahorse analysis.

Mitophagy Assay using pCMV-mt-mKeima plasmid: The pCMV-mt-mKeima plasmid was utilized for the detection of mitophagy. HTR-8/SVneo cells were cultured in 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Cells were seeded onto glass-bottom dishes and transfected at 50–70% confluence with the pCMV-mt-mKeima plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The medium was replaced with normal or serine-deficient RPMI-1640 at 24 h post-transfection. The cells were subsequently cultured for an additional 48 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and then stained with Hoechst 33258 for 20 min. For Mito-Keima, fluorescence was sequentially acquired using 440-nm and 560-nm excitation, with emission collected between 700 nm by thunder imaging systems.

Total RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) was used in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions to extract total RNA from the placental tissue samples. A NanoDrop spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to measure the RNA quantity. The Primeript RT Reagent Kit (Yeasen) was used in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol to reverse-transcribe the extracted RNA into cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the SYBR Green assay (Yeasen) with gene-specific primers, with β-actin as the normalization control. The 2−ΔCt and 2−ΔΔCt methods were used to calculate the relative gene expression levels.

Western blotting

The cells were lysed, and the total protein was extracted using a radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis was performed to separate proteins, which were subsequently transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. β-actin was used as the loading control.

[U-13C]-glucose tracing assessment

HTR-8/SVNEO cells were cultured for 48 h in either standard or serine-depleted RPMI-1640 medium. For isotopic tracing, the culture medium was replaced at the 36-h time point with the respective medium containing 17.3 mM [U-13C]-glucose (Sigma). Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 10 min. The samples were then stored at −80°C until further testing and analysis at Shanghai ProfLeader Biotech Co., Ltd. Cell pellets were then extracted in 500 μL of ice-cold acetonitrile/methanol/water (4:4:2, v/v/v) containing internal standards by ultrasonication in an ice-water bath for 5 min. The extracts were centrifuged at 16000 rcf and 4°C for 15 min. The supernatant was evaporated to dryness under a gentle nitrogen and reconstituted in 50 μL of 50% acetonitrile. Sample analysis was performed on an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The redissolved extracts (2 μL) were injected into an Acquity UPLC BEH Amide column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters Corporation) at a flow rate of 0.30 mL/min with mobile phase (A) 90% acetonitrile, and (B) water, both with 15 mM ammonium acetate. A linear gradient elution was started from 10% B and held for1 min, increased to 25% B at 9 min, to 50% B at 10.2 min and held to 12 min, finally returned to 10% B at 12.1 min and equilibrated to 14 min. The separation of SAM was achieved using a linear gradient as follows: 5% B was held for 1 min, increased to 25% B over 8 min, ramped to 70% B over 1 min and held for 2 min, then returned to 5% B in 0.2 min, with a final re-equilibration for 2 min. The eluted metabolites were ionized in positive mode and negative mode of Heated Electrospray Ionization. Spray voltage was set to 4000 V. Capillary and Probe Heater Temperature were 320°C. Sheath gas flow rate was 35 (Arb, arbitrary unit), and Aux gas flow rate was 10 (Arb). S-Lens RF Level was 50 (Arb). The full scan was operated at a high-resolution of 70000 FWHM (m/z = 200) at a range of 50–1000 m/z with AGC Target setting at 3e6. Simultaneously, the fragment ions information of top 8 precursors each scan was acquired by Data-dependant acquisition (DDA) with HCD energy at 15, 30, and 45 eV, mass resolution of 17500 FWHM, and AGC Target of 1e5. Mass isotopomer distributions (MID) were corrected for natural isotope abundance using theoretical calculations on peak areas, and expressed as a percentage of the total pool.

Immunofluorescence

The cells were cultured in six-well plates until the desired density was achieved. The cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10-20 min to preserve their structures. The membranes were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5-10 min to enable the entry of antibodies. Non-specific block binding was performed with 1% BSA at room temperature for 40 min. The cells were incubated with LC3B antibody at 4°C overnight. The cells were washed thrice with PBS for 5 min each, and incubated with a green fluorescent secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h in the dark. The nuclei were stained with Hoechst (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Catalog #: C1018) for 5–10 min at room temperature. The cells were mounted in anti-fade medium and observed under a laser confocal microscope. Images of the fluorescent signals were captured.

Dual luciferase assay

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay (Promega) was performed in accordance with manufacturer’s to detect luciferase reporter activity. The antibodies were sourced from Qihe Biotech Co., Ltd. A Modulus Microplate Multimode Reader (BioTek) was used to record the luminescence intensity. Luminometric data were normalized to Renilla luciferase activity in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines, and further normalized to a control transfection sample specified for each experimental condition.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays were performed in accordance with the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads) protocol (CST, Catalog #:9003). The cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min, and the reaction was quenched with 125 mM glycine. Immunoprecipitation was performed using antibody after sonication. The SP1 antibody (1:100, CST, Catalog#: 9389) was used. The NCBI database provided the FASTA sequence of the BNIP3 promoter region, and the JASPAR database was used to predict the SP1 binding motif within the BNIP3 promoter. Target gene promoter sequences in the recovered DNA immunocomplexes and DNA inputs were detected by qPCR. Data were normalized to the corresponding DNA input controls.

Dot blotting analysis

Total DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA concentration in each tube was adjusted to 700 ng/μL using a Buffer AE. A 5-μL aliquot was taken from each tube and diluted to 1:2 concentration using Buffer AE, resulting in three tubes of gradient-diluted samples. The samples were applied to nitrocellulose membranes for dot blotting. The membrane was subjected to UV crosslinking for 1 h after air drying at room temperature. The membrane was incubated overnight with the primary antibody (5-Methylcytosine, 1:1000, CST, Catalog# 28692S), followed by incubation with the secondary antibody for 1 h. The blots were developed using a low-sensitivity developer.

Pyrosequencing

DNA extraction and bisulfite-PCR pyrosequencing Genomic DNA from primary tissue samples was extracted with phenol-chloroform. Genomic DNA from FFPE samples was extracted with the Qiagen DNA Extraction Kit (Qiagen, catalog no. 51404). Next, 20–200 ng genomic DNA was taken for bisulfite treatment (ZYMO Research, catalog no. D5006), and the recovered bisulfite-treated DNA was used as the subsequent PCR template. Two rounds of seminested PCR were performed to produce the single-band biotin-modified PCR products. The pyrosequencing assay was performed on a PyroMark Q96 ID Instrument (Qiagen). The final methylation value of each sample was calculated by the average of all the probes in the target region.

Preparation and characterization of nanocarriers

Synthesis of PS: Preparation and characterization of nanocarriers Synthesis of PS: In brief, L-Ser(bn)-NCA and D-Ser(bn)-NCA were dissolved in anhydrous Dimethylformamide (DMF) at a monomer/initiator molar ratio of 20:1 using amino-terminated polyethylene glycol monomethyl ether (mPEG5k-NH2, Mn = 5000 Da) as the initiator. The following procedure was used to prepare poly(D/L-serine) (2:8 D/L). D-Ser(bn)-NCA (2 g) and L-Ser(bn)-NCA (8.0 g) were separately dissolved in 200 mL of DMF under anhydrous and oxygen-free conditions. Subsequently, a DMF solution containing 11.3 g of mPEG5k-NH2 was added in a nitrogen environment. The mixture was stirred at room temperature after 72 h. The reaction was continued for 12 h until the end cap after adding 1.0 g of acetic anhydride. The crude product was added dropwise to 2 L of anhydrous ether for precipitation, filtered, and vacuum-dried at room temperature to obtain white, solid mPEG5k-Poly(D/L-serine benzyl ester). Deprotection of mPEG5k-Poly(D/L-serine benzyl ester): mPEG5k-Poly(D/L-serine benzyl ester) (4.5 g) was dissolved in 60 mL of trifluoroacetic acid and HBr/acetic acid (20.0 mL, 33 wt %) was added subsequently. The resulting solution was stirred at 30°C for 1 h. The polymer was then precipitated by pouring the solution into 800 mL of excess diethyl ether. The obtained precipitate was collected and washed twice with fresh diethyl ether. Subsequently, the crude product was redissolved in a minimal amount of DMF. Purification was performed by dialyzing this solution against pure water using a regenerated cellulose dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 3500 Da. Finally, the purified mPEG5k-Poly(D/L-serine) (PS) was obtained as a white solid by freeze-drying the dialyzed solution. Characterization of PS: A Bruker spectrometer with D2O as the solvent (Bruker AV-300, Germany) was used to obtain 1H nuclear magnetic resonance spectra. A Malvern Zetasizer (Nano-ZS90, UK) was used to measure the sizes and zeta potentials of nanoparticles.

Synthesis of Cy7-Labeled mPEG5k-Poly(D/L-serine) (PS-Cy7): The Cyanine 7 (Cy-7)-labeled PS conjugate was synthesized via Steglich esterification. Briefly, 300.0 mg of PS was dissolved in 10.0 mL of anhydrous N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF). To this solution, 3.0 mg of Cy7-COOH, 0.6 mg of N, N′-diisopropylcarbodiimide (DIC), and a catalytic amount of 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) were added. The reaction was allowed to proceed at 30°C for 72 h in the dark. After the reaction, the product was isolated by precipitation in 100 mL of cold diethyl ether. The precipitate was collected and washed twice with diethyl ether. The crude product was then redissolved in DMF and purified by dialysis against ultrapure water using a regenerated cellulose membrane (MWCO = 3500 Da) for 36 h. The final product, PS-Cy7, was obtained as a blue solid after lyophilization.

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) Analysis: Size characterization was performed by dynamic light scattering using a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument. Nanoparticle samples were diluted in ultrapure water to an appropriate concentration for measurement. Three sequential measurements of 10–15 runs each were carried out at 25°C. The data were analyzed using the instrument’s built-in software, and data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation from three independently prepared samples.

Zeta Potential Measurement: The zeta potential of nanoparticles was determined by laser Doppler micro-electrophoresis using a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument. Briefly, the samples were appropriately diluted in 1 mM KCl solution to ensure a consistent ionic strength. Measurements were performed in triplicate at 25°C using a disposable folded capillary cell. Data are represented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments.

Optical Properties Characterization: The absorption spectrum of the nanoparticles was recorded using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (e.g., Shimadzu UV-2600). Samples were appropriately diluted in a clear solvent [e.g., phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or water] and placed in a quartz cuvette. The absorption was measured across a wavelength range of 200–800 nm, with the specific detection wavelength corresponding to the maximum absorption (λmax) identified and reported. For fluorescence characterization, the emission spectrum was acquired using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (e.g., Hitachi F-7100) at the predetermined optimal excitation wavelength.

Critical Micelle Concentration (CMC) Determination: The critical micelle concentration (CMC) of the polymer PS was determined using a pyrene (Py) fluorescent probe. The specific steps are as follows: First, Py was dissolved in acetone and prepared into a 0.5 mg/mL solution. Then, 20.0 μL of the Py solution was added to a brown vial, which was placed in a 37°C constant-temperature shaking incubator to evaporate the solvent to dryness overnight. After that, 1.50 mL of PS solutions with different concentrations were added to the brown vials respectively, and the vials were placed back in the 37°C constant-temperature shaking incubator and shaken overnight. Finally, the fluorescence excitation spectra (λ em = 390.00 nm) of the solutions were measured using an F-320 fluorescence spectrophotometer produced by Tianjin Gangdong Technology.

Morphological Characterization by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM): The morphology of the PS nanoparticles was examined using a JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope. Briefly, a 10 μL aliquot of the nanoparticle suspension, diluted to a concentration of 0.5 mg mL−1, was deposited onto a 400-mesh copper grid pre-coated with a carbon film. The grid was incubated at room temperature for 12 h to allow the nanoparticles to adhere. The sample was then allowed to dry completely at ambient temperature before being transferred to the TEM instrument for observation.

Enzymatic Degradation of PS: PS (containing 0.5 mg, dissolved in 5 mL buffer) were incubated in 50 mM PBS solution (pH 7.4) containing 3.5 units/mL of Pronase E (1 mg/mL). Then they were added to a dialysis bag (MWCO = 3500 Da), and immersed in 45 mL buffer under shaking (90 rpm) at 37°C. At each desired time point (1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 10 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h), 150 μL of degradation samples was withdrawn into vials. To inactivate the enzyme, the vials were closed, heated for 5 min at 100°C, and stored at −20°C until analysis. 150 μL of the same fresh buffer was replaced. The release test was repeated intriplicate under the same conditions.

In vivo fluorescence imaging analysis of PS-Cy7: Pregnant C57BL/6J female mice at gestational day (GD) 16.5 were intravenously injected via the tail vein with 4 μg of PS-Cy7. At 10 h post-injection, the mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal administration of tribromoethanol (250 mg/kg). After achieving full anesthesia, the abdominal fur was removed to minimize signal attenuation. The mice were then subjected to in vivo fluorescence imaging using In Vivo Animal Imaging system (BioLight Biotechnology). Following in vivo imaging, the mice were euthanized, and maternal organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) along with the fetuses and placentas were harvested. The ex vivo fluorescence of all collected tissues was immediately imaged using the same system.

Cryosectioning and Immunofluorescence Imaging of Mouse Placenta: Fresh placentas were harvested from pregnant mice and immediately placed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After rinsing, the tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 4–6 h at 4°C on a rocker. Fixed placentas were cryoprotected by sequential incubation in 15% and 30% sucrose solutions until they sank. Each placenta was then embedded in O.C.T. compound, frozen in a slurry of dry ice and isopentane, and stored at −80°C until sectioning. Serial sections of 8 μm thickness were obtained using a cryostat with the chamber temperature set to −22°C. Sections were transferred onto Superfrost Plus glass slides and allowed to air-dry for 30 min before proceeding to staining or storage at −80°C. High-resolution images were acquired using a Thunder Imaging System with a 20x or 40× objective lens.

Blood Distribution of PS: C57BL/6J male were intravenously injected via the tail vein with 10 mg of PS dissolved in 200 μL of PBS. The control group received an equal volume (200 μL) of PBS. Blood samples were collected via retro-orbital bleeding at 0.5 h, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h post-injection. The blood was allowed to clot at room temperature for 15 min and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was collected for the measurement of serine concentration.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses of clinical samples and experimental data were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine data distribution normality. For 2-group comparisons, unparied t test (equal variances) or unparied t test with Welch’s correction (unequal variances) for normally distributed variables, or the Mann‒Whitney U test for variables not normally distributed were performed. In the case of multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test for normally distributed variables, or Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn’s multiple comparisons test for variables not normally distributed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In vitro experiments were performed in triplicates or more. No animals were excluded in the current study. Data are presented as Mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise specified. Significance levels are indicated as follows: ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ns: not significant.
