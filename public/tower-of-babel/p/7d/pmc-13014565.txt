Effective Partnerships With Parents of Adults With Mild Intellectual Disabilities in Residential Care: Experiences of Support Staff
Authors: Maria C den Boer, Sanne A H Giesbers, Noud Frielink, Petri J C M Embregts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEffective parent‐staff partnerships are essential for facilitating adequate service delivery in residential care for adults with (mild) intellectual disabilities. However, this partnership is often evaluated as being suboptimal. Whilst previous research has focused on parental perspectives, the present study explores support staff's experiences regarding effective partnerships.MethodEleven support staff members were interviewed using a semi‐structured format. The results were analysed using thematic analysis.ResultsFour themes were identified: (1) Acknowledging the value of parents, (2) Deliberating over what is possible and allowed given that it is service users who set the framework for the partnership, (3) Experiencing different perspectives on what constitutes high‐quality care and (4) Establishing a connection with parents.ConclusionsThis study provides concrete insights into how to improve parent‐staff partnerships, including recognising the paramount role of service users, enhancing informal parent‐staff interactions, and providing support for staff such as ethics consultations and peer support.

Introduction

Parents are vital resources for adults with intellectual disabilities, insofar as they provide instrumental and emotional support, guidance and advocacy throughout their lives (Giesbers et al. 2020; Sanderson et al. 2017; Zambrino and Hedderich 2021). The relationship between adults with intellectual disabilities and their parents has unique characteristics, including, amongst other things, emotional closeness, unconditional love and support, a long‐term perspective (Bigby and Fyffe 2012) and providing a sense of meaning in life (Krause 2007; Lambert et al. 2010). Even in instances in which people with intellectual disabilities move out of their family home to live in a residential facility, many parents continue to be involved in the life and care of their offspring (Zambrino and Hedderich 2021).

When residing in a residential facility, support staff also play a key role in terms of care provision for adults with intellectual disabilities (Friedman 2018; Giesbers et al. 2019). Hence, given the central role that both parents and support staff play in residential care for people with intellectual disabilities, developing a good collaboration between both parties is essential for enabling effective service delivery within residential care and, moreover, positively contributes towards the quality of life of the person with intellectual disabilities (Hastings 2016; Petry et al. 2007; Steel et al. 2011). Within extant literature, the collaboration between parents and support staff is assumed to best be achieved through building effective partnerships (Morrow and Malin 2004). Effective partnerships can also empower parents and other family members as well as improve their level of satisfaction with their family's quality of life, including parenting, family interaction and well‐being (Dunst and Dempsey 2007; Summers et al. 2007). However, it is known from both research and clinical practice that effective partnerships between parents and support staff in residential care are not straightforwardly achieved (John 2020; Zambrino and Hedderich 2021), not to mention that there is limited evidence on best practices with respect to effective partnerships (Bigby 2022).

The partnership between parents and support staff has unique characteristics, which distinguishes it from, for example, personal partnerships such as in a marriage. Most notably, parents and support staff ordinarily have no choice over who they are partnering with, certainly not on an individual basis and they also have different roles and responsibilities within the partnership (Murray 2000). The key characteristics of effective partnerships between parents and support staff that have been identified in extant literature are mutual respect, trust and honesty, mutually agreed‐upon goals and shared planning and decision‐making (Keen 2007). However, these characteristics are not always reflected in practice (Keen 2007); for example, communication breakdowns, broken promises, and a lack of communication by support staff have been found to lead to a loss of trust or a general mistrust in staff by parents (Zambrino and Hedderich 2021). Moreover, in order to set goals that both parties are in agreement over, there must first be a common understanding of these goals (Rodger and Keen 2017). However, goals in the relationship between parents and support staff are frequently defined in broad terms, which, in turn, can result in parents and support staff interpreting them differently (Murray 2000). Finally, research has shown that parents do not always perceive themselves as being full partners when it comes to planning and decision‐making over their offspring with intellectual disabilities in residential care (Schwartz 2005). Notably, no active role for people with intellectual disabilities has been ascribed within the definition of effective partnership. However, in line with the United Nations' Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRDP; United Nations 2006), people with intellectual disabilities should be supported in their own decision‐making (Devi 2014), and, indeed, their perspective has subsequently become a central component of support provision. In other words, people with intellectual disabilities should determine, as far as possible, their own support needs and have a say in how this support is provided (Embregts 2011).

Research has hitherto primarily focused on the parental perspective of what constitutes an effective partnership within care provision for people with intellectual disabilities, and, as such, there is a relative dearth of research focusing on the perspectives of support staff (John 2020). The limited research that is available suggests that support staff face various barriers to establishing effective partnerships with parents, including both over and under involvement from parents as well as difficulties in parents transitioning from being an active advocate to adopting a relatively passive role as their offspring grow older. Moreover, they report other barriers, such as, for example, the care being provided by family members not adequately aligning with the care being provided by the support staff of the residential facility, and unrealistic parental expectations (e.g., overly high expectations regarding support that support staff simply cannot live up to; Francis et al. 2017; John 2020). Furthermore, research on staff's perspectives has as yet not differentiated by level of intellectual disability, despite indications that the level of parental involvement in residential care can vary across different levels of intellectual disabilities (Schwartz and Tsumi 2003), which potentially influences parent‐staff partnerships. Finally, the roles within the partnership may differ for those parents and staff who are supporting people who are able to express themselves verbally compared to those supporting people who cannot do so, thereby requiring the latter to (collaboratively) read and interpret the person's signals and communication (Nieuwenhuijse et al. 2024; Casey et al. 2023). To improve the partnership between parents and support staff in residential care for adults with intellectual disabilities, it is important to gain greater insight into how support staff experience these partnerships, how they establish effective partnerships in practice, what challenges they face and what they require to engage in successful partnerships (John 2020). Therefore, the present study aimed to explore these experiences of support staff. In addition, as experiences may differ between levels of intellectual disability, this study aims to provide insights within a homogeneous sample of support staff, that is, support staff working in residential facilities who provide 24‐h care to adults with mild intellectual disabilities. The study was guided by the following research questions: (I) How do support staff experience the partnership with parents, and what challenges do they encounter in these collaborations? (II) How do they try to establish and maintain effective partnerships in practice, and what do they need in order to do so successfully?

Method

Participants

In total, 11 support staff members from four intellectual disability service providers in the Netherlands participated in this study. In order to be eligible for the study, the support staff had to meet several inclusion criteria: they had to have worked for at least 6 months at an accommodation in which they support people who (I) have a mild intellectual disability (IQ 50–70), (II) are aged 18–40 years, (III) receive 24‐h support under the Dutch Long‐term Care Act (in Dutch: Wet langdurige zorg; Wlz), and (IV) do not display severe challenging behaviour. The second criterion was determined by the fact that it might be more difficult for parents of older individuals with intellectual disabilities to support their offspring living in a residential facility due to their advanced years (Grey et al. 2015), which, in turn, potentially impacts partnerships with support staff. People with mild intellectual disabilities and challenging behaviour were not included as this subgroup often grows up in less stable or unsafe family environments (Nouwens et al. 2017), and the role of and relationships with family members may be more complex. This is likely to influence the parent–staff partnership. The support staff who participated in the study were predominantly women (9 out of 11) and had a mean age of 45.8 years (range 27–59). On average, they worked with people with intellectual disabilities for 21.5 years (range 6–39), with a mean age of 7.6 years (range 0.5–22) at the current accommodation.

Procedure

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Review Board of Tilburg University (RP589). A purposive sampling procedure was used. Eight intellectual disability service providers affiliated with the Academic Collaborative Centre Living with an intellectual disability of Tilburg University were invited to participate in this study. The contact persons from these service providers were informed about the aim and nature of the study, and four of the eight contact persons opted to invite service providers who expressed interest in participating in the study and subsequently identified, informed, and recruited potential participants to take part in the study. With their consent, 13 potential participants were contacted by the first author via either Microsoft Teams or telephone to inform them further about the aim and procedures of the study as well as to check that they met the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 11 potential participants met the inclusion criteria and expressed interest in participating in the study. Subsequently, an information letter and informed consent form was sent to them via email. Furthermore, an appointment for a videoconferencing interview was planned at a time that was convenient for the participants. All the support staff voluntarily agreed to participate in this study and returned their signed informed consent form via email. The participants received no incentive for participating in the study.

Interviews

Using an interview guide developed for this study, semi‐structured interviews were conducted with the participants. The interview guide is available from the corresponding author upon request. The interviews began with questioning the support staff about their current partnership practices and experiences with collaborating with parents. Subsequently, support staff were asked about what challenges they face and what they require to engage in successful partnerships. They were also asked about the specific characteristics of effective partnerships that were identified in extant literature, namely mutual respect, trust, and honesty, mutually agreed‐upon goals, and shared planning and decision‐making. A dialogue with support staff was established by using multiple open‐ended questions. Meanwhile, the interviewer remained open to other topics raised by the support staff and gave them ample opportunity to raise additional topics that had not yet been discussed but that they felt were significant in their partnerships with parents.

The interviews were conducted by the first author between July and September 2022 via videoconferencing using Microsoft Teams, because previous studies conducted both prior to and during the COVID‐19 pandemic indicated that videoconference interviews can be a useful supplement or replacement for face‐to‐face interviews (e.g., Khan and MacEachen 2022; Krouwel et al. 2019; Saarijärvi and Bratt 2021; Vereijken et al. 2022). With the consent of the participants, the interviews were recorded using the record function in Microsoft Teams and transcribed verbatim. The mean length of the interviews was 51.3 min (range 33–67).

Data Analysis

An inductive reflexive thematic analysis framework was used to identify, analyse, and report patterns within the data (Braun and Clarke 2021). Data analysis was carried out by the first author and comprised six consecutive steps. The qualitative data analysis software Atlas.ti was used to analyse the data. The analysis began with reading the verbatim transcripts in detail to become familiar with the data (step 1). In the second step, the first author generated initial codes for around 20% of the transcripts (step 2). That is to say, each segment of the transcripts that was deemed to be relevant to the research question was coded using open coding; we did not make use of pre‐set codes, but rather developed the codes as we worked our way through the coding process. The initial codes were then discussed with a second researcher as a kind of audit in order to sense‐check ideas and explore multiple interpretations of the data. After the audit had been performed, the first author then generated codes for the remaining transcripts. Next, the first and second author then systematically grouped and collated the final set of codes based on shared similarities in order to begin identifying potential overarching themes and subthemes (step 3). Subsequently (step 4), the coherence and consistency of these (sub)themes were evaluated internally (within each (sub)theme) and externally (between different (sub)themes) by all members of the research team. The first and second author repeatedly moved back and forth between the (sub)themes and the associated coded data, critically examining whether the themes accurately represented the data. These individual reflections were followed by in‐depth discussions with the research team to address discrepancies, refine (sub)themes, and ensure that each (sub)theme was both internally coherent and clearly distinct from the others. This iterative process facilitated the refinement of the thematic structure in order to ensure distinct, robust themes that were grounded in the dataset. The themes and accompanying subthemes were then named and extensively discussed by the entire research team (step 5). As the original verbatim transcripts were in Dutch, both the coding and collating of codes into themes and subthemes was deployed in Dutch. Finally, a narrative structure was established in Dutch and then critically reviewed and discussed by the research team. Subsequently, a scientific report in English was produced. To preserve the original meaning of the conversations during the translation process, the findings and the final paper were reviewed by an English native speaker fluent in Dutch (step 6).

Results

Four overarching themes were identified: (1) Acknowledging the value of parents, (2) Deliberating over what is possible and allowed given that it is service users who set the framework for the partnership, (3) Experiencing different perspectives of what constitutes high‐quality care, and (4) Establishing a connection with parents. The last of these themes has four related subthemes.

Theme 1: Acknowledging the Value of Parents

Generally speaking, the support staff underscored the importance of communicating with parents, keeping them informed, and collaborating with them. Indeed, several support staff members felt that it is beneficial for parents to be involved in the care of their offspring (and that this care is consistently followed up by the parents when service users spend time at their family home). Moreover, the support staff also talked about how they value parents as being the experts in what their offspring need, due to the fact that they know them best. Some support staff reported that without parental knowledge, they would not be able to provide the best possible care to their service users:

Because there's quite a lot going on, in fact new things come up every week and it would be good just to share them with someone who isn't directly involved [in the support team] but who still knows the service user. Because, yes, sometimes the issues are kind of sensitive and so I can't just ask the service user themselves. Or issues about which the service user doesn't have the ability to give a proper answer (…) But then… How can I put it? We know the service user well, but we don't know them as well as a mother knows her child (Support staff member 6).

Several support staff also stressed the value of parents as interlocutors when sharing and discussing concerns about their service users:

That's a very good thing, especially when you share the same concerns. It's very nice to discuss these things with each other, so that it's not just you against the service user, alone with those concerns (Support staff member 2).

Furthermore, the support staff stressed the importance of good relationships between service users and parents for the wellbeing of their service users. Therefore, they reported how they attempt to prevent conflicts between parents and support staff that could negatively impact on the relationship between service users and parents, such as conflicts that cause service users to feel caught in the middle between parents and support staff.

Even though support staff clearly acknowledged the value of partnering with parents, they also elaborated extensively about how effective partnership is not always simple and self‐evident. This will be addressed in the second, third, and fourth theme.

Theme 2: Deliberating Over What Is Possible and Allowed Given That It Is Service Users Who Determine the Framework for the Partnership

The support staff expressed that they prioritise the wishes and needs of service users in their partnerships with parents, not only from an ideological vantage point (i.e., the service user is paramount), but also from a legal perspective (i.e., the service user as a legal entity). Therefore, they adapt their approach to partnering with parents based on the individual needs of each service user.

Many support staff recounted situations in which service users preferred not to involve or inform their parents in their care at all, which prevented support staff from partnering with parents. According to the support staff, this often stemmed from service users' desires to be independent and engage in self‐discovery. In specific situations, being unable to partner with parents was not experienced as being difficult for the support staff; in such situations, they inform the parents (either without or together with the service user) that they cannot and are not allowed to inform or involve them:

To say, even to relatives, that well, your child, grandchild, son, doesn't want us to discuss that incident last week with you. If you want to know more, ask them yourselves (Support staff member 8).

In other scenarios, however, support staff reported that they found it more challenging when they were unable to inform or involve parents in the care of service users. This was especially the case if the safety or well‐being of service users was at risk or if problems would have persisted without the involvement of parents and other family members:

I don't always find it easy. No, I don't always find it easy. No, I really don't. Because, you know what it is? Problems can sometimes persist because… And involving the network, you know, whether that's her parents or brother or sister, can sometimes be a solution. Can sometimes make the case easier for me. So the service user restricts my options by not allowing me to contact them [the network], because [that means] they don't know exactly what's going on (Support staff member 4).

To develop partnerships with parents in these situations, the support staff talked about how they engage in dialogue with service users to try to gain their consent to inform or involve parents. They also informed us that they try to convince service users to inform or involve parents themselves. Finally, support staff reported that they consult colleagues, such as other support staff members, managers, or psychologists, to collectively determine the best course of action to take.

Theme 3: Experiencing Different Perspectives on What Constitutes High‐Quality Care

According to the support staff, the partnerships between parents and support staff can be hampered when they have different perspectives on what constitutes high‐quality care. For instance, when a service user moves out of their family home to live at an accommodation of a service provider, the support staff indicated that parents need to begin the process of letting go. However, the support staff expressed that some parents can find it difficult to relinquish control and want the care to proceed in a way that resembles the care they provided at their family home. This in turn can lead to conflicts between parents and support staff, insofar as some parents attempt to maintain control by closely monitoring the care staff provide to their offspring. According to the support staff, this can result in frequent disputes over relatively minor issues, such as, for example, room cleaning. Moreover, some of the support staff experienced that parents can have expectations for their offspring's care that support staff are unable to meet, such as providing one‐on‐one support, which is challenging due to limited staff and time and the responsibility they have to other service users. Some support staff even reported experiencing that parents sometimes expect support staff to provide care in such a way that is detrimental to their own offspring's independence and self‐reliance.

Furthermore, the support staff indicated that parents do not always have a clear understanding about their offspring's disability and abilities, which, in turn, can lead to setting unattainable goals for them or having unrealistic expectations about their care. According to various support staff members, conflicts about appropriate care are both more likely to occur and more challenging when parents have an intellectual disability or mental illness themselves. Indeed, one support staff member reported how in such cases they prefer parents not to be involved:

Let me put it this way: we have a number of parents who have a disability themselves, who simply take a very different view of what's good for their child or what we see. (…) Sometimes we have parents who demand things of us which the service user either doesn't need or which will do the service user no good at all, so that can be very difficult indeed. To be perfectly frank, in my experience, so over the past five years, it's better if the parents are less involved, though of course that depends on the type of service user and the type of parents too. But that often works better than them being very closely involved (Support staff member 2).

According to the support staff, partnerships with parents were also routinely hindered by differing views over service users' autonomy. Whilst the support staff reported that they strive to promote service users' autonomy, they sometimes felt that parents make decisions for their offspring that impede their autonomy. The support staff also noted that parents often aim to protect their offspring against potential harm or mistakes when they do things on their own. However, the support staff outlined the importance of allowing service users to explore things independently and learn from their own mistakes, with the support staff being present to help them if needed:

But I think that's because they are so on top of everything, that it all has to be done in line with their values. And so I sometimes think, okay but where is your child in all of this? When do you give them the trust to go ahead and do things their own way? (…) I find that difficult sometimes, for the service users themselves too. (…) While I sometimes think, just let them discover the world for themselves a little and let them make mistakes, that's okay. And that's why we're here, to jump in [if needed] (Support staff member 11).

Theme 4: Establishing a Connection With Parents

To effectively partner with parents, support staff emphasised the importance of building and maintaining a good connection with them. This involves establishing trust, honesty and mutual respect, but these concepts are fragile. To address this, four subthemes were identified: (1) Building trust in the knowledge, skills, and involvement of the support staff, (2) Difficulties with being honest, (3) Stressing the importance of respect, even if it is not mutual and (4) The importance of informal contact for strengthening the connection with parents.

Subtheme 1 Building Trust in the Knowledge, Skills, and Involvement of the Support Staff

Trust was acknowledged by the support staff as being a crucial component in developing and maintaining effective partnerships with parents. More specifically, they noted that trust can manifest in multiple ways, with having confidence in the knowledge and skills of the support staff and acknowledging their commitment to the best interests of service users being the most frequently cited trust‐related factors. In accordance with Theme 3, where the support staff observed that some parents can struggle to relinquish control, the support staff also stressed the importance of parents having trust in them, insofar as this can help parents to feel more comfortable with handing over the care for their offspring. In relation to this, some support staff members noted that building trust with siblings regarding the quality of care provision is often less challenging than it is with parents:

But I do notice that we have… now our target group is getting older, we have far more dealings with sisters and brothers these days, instead of parents. I see a real difference there. After all, it makes a difference whether you have to let go a little more as a brother or sister, or as a parent (…) And a brother or a sister are already slightly more removed from their little brother or sister (…) And so they do tend to say: well, you know, if you think that's what needs to be done then you should go ahead and do it, you know. Whereas parents can still be much more inclined to make that decision their own (Support staff member 7).

According to the support staff, building trust with parents can be a difficult process. Parents may enter the partnership with skepticism or mistrust towards professionals, often stemming from prior negative experiences. As such, building trust was viewed by the support staff as a critical task in developing effective partnerships with parents. There was a clear recognition that this process requires time, effort, and dedication and also that trust can be easily damaged. Therefore, the support staff stressed the need to continuously invest in developing and maintaining a bond of trust with parents. The support staff identified several factors that can help to facilitate the development of this bond of trust, including, amongst other things, actively seeking connection with parents, maintaining ongoing communication, and ensuring that one's actions align with one's words.

Subtheme 2 Difficulties With Being Honest

Honesty was also flagged up as an important aspect of developing effective partnerships with parents. Indeed, some of the support staff indicated that honesty is a fundamental precondition for building effective partnerships with parents, insofar as it helps to foster trust. In their estimation, honesty is thus a vitally important aspect of being a professional:

I really think that's very important, certainly from the point of view of my professionalism, that you are honest and frank with them, and don't do anything behind their back (Support staff member 3).

Despite the importance placed on honesty, the support staff acknowledged that it was a multi‐layered concept that poses challenges in practice. Some support staff underscored the necessity of being forthright with parents about actions that have been taken and incidents that have occurred, particularly when errors or missteps have been made. However, other support staff members reported finding it difficult to be honest with certain parents:

Truth is, you're honest with parents you're on the same wavelength as, let's say. You can spar with them and get things done. But you leave things out with those parents you know there might be a clash with or something (Support staff member 9).

The support staff also highlighted service users' role in parent‐staff partnerships with respect to honesty and, consequently, fostering or hindering the trust between all parties. They felt that if service users are not honest with their parents about support staff, then this can in turn damage parents' trust in staff. Moreover, when service users request that support staff withhold information from their parents, then they must comply in order to maintain service users' trust in them. However, withholding information can also damage parents' trust in the support staff. As a result, striking a balance between fostering trust with service users and their parents, whilst simultaneously being honest in the process can pose challenges for support staff.

Subtheme 3 Emphasising the Importance of Respect, Even if It Is Not Mutual

Although all the support staff members reported being committed to showing respect towards parents, several felt they did not always receive respect in turn from parents.

You know, just like very often I can understand parents when I think how I would feel if I were them. And sometimes it would be nice if they did the same the other way around. But that doesn't always happen. And sometimes I get it, because [for them] it's about their child and not the other seven living here in the same group. But I am responsible for seven service users including their child (Support staff member 5).

In the event that parents failed to show them respect, the support staff stressed how important it was to nevertheless show empathy towards the parents, based on the fact that their behaviour was likely influenced by their own personal circumstances. Rather than labelling these parents as ‘difficult’, the support staff stressed the importance of understanding their perspectives.

Some of the support staff members felt that mutual respect in their partnerships with parents could be influenced by their age or stage of life. For example, some noted that they received more respect from parents after they became mothers themselves, particularly if they had children the same age as their service users. Moreover, some of the support members talked about how being parents themselves made it easier for them to empathise with ‘difficult parents’ and understand their perspectives. However, they noted that this empathy can also sometimes complicate situations. Specifically, some staff members indicated that they sometimes prioritise the wishes of parents over the wishes of their service users, even if these two sets of wishes are in conflict with one another, out of a strong sense of empathy for the parents.

The support staff also outlined the importance of maintaining respect towards parents, even if they felt that parents did not show the same level of respect towards them. Although they acknowledged that this can be challenging in practice, they nevertheless stressed the need to set boundaries in a respectful manner, such as, for example, politely ending a conversation and revisiting it at a later time. Despite their efforts to remain professionally respectful, some of the support staff members admitted that they found such situations difficult and unpleasant.

Subtheme 4 the Importance of Informal Contact for Strengthening the Connection With Parents

Several support staff members underscored the importance of engaging in informal interactions with parents for establishing and maintaining effective partnerships. In fact, they explicated that such moments can be more effective than only having formal meetings or contact, especially with respect to addressing issues. They proceeded to note that contact with parents should also not be limited strictly to telephone or digital conversations but rather should include face‐to‐face contact, as this afforded greater opportunities for informal interactions. However, some of the support staff members reported that there was a lack of opportunities to engage in such informal face‐to‐face interactions, due to, for example, a lack of resources. However, support staff argued that it is the service provider's responsibility to facilitate such contact:

Well, you know, the things I need in order to have good contact with parents (…) Informal activities should be organised here at the group. A barbecue for example (…) And I think that, as a provider or as an organisation, it's your duty to invest in that kind of thing” (Support staff member 4).

In addition, the support staff acknowledged the importance of finding opportunities to connect with parents during their existing interactions. For instance, they suggested using humour, speaking in the dialect that the parents themselves speak, or asking how they are doing over a cup of coffee. These small actions can help build rapport and strengthen the partnership between parents and the support staff.

Discussion

According to previous research, collaboration between parents and support staff is best achieved through developing an effective partnership (Morrow and Malin 2004). To date, research has predominantly concentrated on the parental perspective of what constitutes effective partnerships within care provision for people with intellectual disabilities, with scarce attention given to the views of support staff (John 2020). Consequently, the present study aimed to explore the experiences of staff in residential facilities who provide 24‐h care to adults with mild intellectual disabilities. The findings of this study show that even though support staff acknowledge the value of effective partnerships with parents, achieving this with parents can be challenging within their daily practice.

First, the results of the present study show that the role of service users with mild intellectual disabilities is paramount in parent‐staff partnerships. The reason for this, according to the support staff, is that parent‐staff partnerships are guided by the wishes and needs of service users themselves. The extent to which support staff respect the wishes of service users and support their autonomy above all is illustrated by the fact that if service users choose to not include their parents in their care, then support staff will support this decision. This testifies to how the partnership between parents and support staff can be affected by service users' needs and behaviour. However, to the best of our knowledge, empirical findings on the different ways in which service users' behaviour impacts effective partnerships between parents and support staff remain scarce. In the present study, the support staff also mentioned that service users were sometimes dishonest with parents, or asked staff to be dishonest on their behalf with their parents, which could spark conflicts between parents and support staff. Therefore, the support staff stressed the importance of both honest communication between service users and their parents and getting service users' approval to engage in honest communication with their parents. It would be interesting for future research to further explore how service users' needs and behaviour can either hinder or foster effective partnerships between support staff and parents.

Second, alongside the aforementioned influence of service users' needs and behaviour on establishing effective partnerships, the present study also sheds light on support staff's experiences and views regarding parental behaviour. For example, the support staff felt that parents did not always show them the same respect as they felt to show to the parents. They expressed a strong need for parental respect, honest communication, and trust in their ability to provide the best care for service users. These findings expand upon earlier studies that focused on parental perspectives towards professional behaviours, such as, for example, keeping promises, taking family members seriously, keeping family members informed (e.g., Zambrino and Hedderich 2021), by illustrating how parental behaviours can also play an important role in establishing effective parent‐staff partnerships. Furthermore, our study indicates that the significance placed upon professional behaviours (such as honesty) is not solely emphasised by parents, as seen in previous research (e.g., Vereijken et al. 2022), but is also recognised by professionals themselves. Furthermore, the support staff expressed the need for more opportunities to engage in informal and/or face‐to‐face contact with parents to help build partnerships based on mutual respect, trust, and honesty. In contrast with prior findings (John 2020), the participants in the present study drew explicit connections between effective partnerships and the organisational context of service providers, insofar as the support staff argued that it is the service provider's responsibility to provide resources for informal and/or face‐to‐face contact with parents. As such, organisational support and the provision of resources seem essential to create an environment where partnership building is encouraged. Although participants did not explicitly address this issue, available resources in disability care are under increasing pressure due to budget cuts and staff shortages (VGN 2023; WfZ 2024), while at the same time, expectations for partnering with parents and other family members are rising, creating a paradoxical situation. Moreover, support staff emphasised their own responsibilities for finding opportunities to connect with parents during their existing interactions. Informal contact between parents and support staff may be effectively stimulated through spontaneous interactions, such as brief conversations during the drop‐off or pick‐up of service users at the residential facility (Zambrino and Hedderich 2021). Also, facilitating the use of communication tools like shared diaries can support ongoing informal contact by keeping parents informed and engaged in their offspring's everyday experiences. It may also be beneficial to foster an open and flexible environment in which parents feel welcome to visit the residential facility without requiring scheduled appointments (Giesbers et al. 2024). In addition, being receptive to parental suggestions for organising joint activities themselves, such as gardening with other family members at the residential facility, could help facilitate informal interactions and strengthen the relationships between support staff and parents (Giesbers et al. 2024). Third, according to the support staff, differences of opinion between parents and support staff over what constitutes good quality care and, more specifically, the issue of ensuring service users' autonomy, can hinder shared decision‐making and planning around mutually agreed‐upon goals. The United Nations (2006) Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities underscores the importance of autonomy and independence for individuals with intellectual disabilities. In line with previous studies (Casey et al. 2023; Saaltink et al. 2012), the support staff in this study felt that service users' choices and decision‐making could be hindered when parents struggled to find the right balance between promoting autonomy and protecting their offspring from the risk of harm. They experienced that in situations in which there were divergent opinions over service users' autonomy, conflicts, and tensions can arise between parents and support staff, which, in turn, subsequently affected their partnership. In line with our findings, previous research has shown that support staff may favour a more neutral approach than family members, who sometimes believe that their deep understanding of their relatives makes them best placed to decide what is best for them (Casey et al. 2023). Research has also shown that parents struggle with balancing considerations over their offspring's autonomy with their own wishes and ideas, which is grounded in a strong desire to protect their offspring and a sense that they are primarily responsible for their well‐being (Vereijken et al. 2022; Casey et al. 2023). Additionally, support staff can also encounter challenges when attempting to promote the autonomy of service users themselves (Petner‐Arrey and Copeland 2014), whilst people with intellectual disabilities have reported feeling that both family members and support staff can sometimes restrict their autonomy (Casey et al. 2023). A possible solution for addressing these aforementioned issues related to the autonomy and independence of adults, meanwhile enhancing the parent‐staff partnership, could be to organise moral case deliberations in which professionals, parents and service users could participate. In recent years, the awareness of partnering with both service users and parents in addressing ethical questions related to the care for people with intellectual disabilities has been growing, yet, doing so can be challenging (Hermsen et al. 2024). Future research should further explore opportunities, including inclusive moral case deliberations, for effective partnerships in light of issues related to the autonomy and independence of adults with mild intellectual disabilities.

Notably, the present study sheds light on the various challenges that support staff can face in their partnerships with parents, which can sometimes lead to distress. This distress may derive from the inability of support staff to fulfil their caring commitment to their service users, whilst simultaneously maintaining an understanding of family members' perspectives and needs, a dilemma which is referred to as moral distress (Walsh 2018). Experiencing moral distress has been shown to be an important factor in the onset of burnout (Dzeng and Wachter 2020), and thus profoundly impact the wellbeing of support staff in this sector. In order to enable support staff to fulfil their care commitments and manage moral distress in their partnerships with parents, future initiatives could explore options for additional support, such as, for example, peer support or ethics support. In the current study, support staff described receiving support and consultation from colleagues, including fellow support staff, managers, and psychologists. Previous research suggests that facilitating short lines of communication between support staff and managers, particularly when managers are regularly present within the residential facility and thereby more accessible and approachable for both support staff and parents, could support the development of effective partnerships between parents and support staff (Giesbers et al. 2024).

The findings of the present study should be interpreted in the context of some limitations. Given that this study is a qualitative study that focuses on the experiences of purposively sampled support staff working for a small number of service providers in the centre and south of the Netherlands, one must exercise caution regarding generalising the findings to a wider population. Furthermore, only support staff working in residential facilities for adults with mild intellectual disabilities were interviewed. Various of the findings, for example the paramount role of service users in the parent‐staff partnership and the issues related to the autonomy and independence of adults with mild intellectual disabilities, may be specific for individuals with mild intellectual disabilities. Moreover, previous research has indicated that parental involvement in the residential care of their offspring differs for different levels of intellectual disabilities (Schwartz 2005). It would therefore be interesting for future research to explore the perspectives of support staff working at residential facilities that provide care to individuals with other levels of intellectual disabilities, who both exhibit and do not exhibit challenging behaviour. Moreover, given the key role played by service users in effective partnerships between parents and support staff, it is crucial to explore service users' perspectives and needs regarding what constitutes an effective partnership between themselves, their parents and support staff. In future research, the perspectives and voices of self‐advocates should also be included, thereby ensuring the centrality of people with intellectual disabilities (Embregts and Frielink 2023). In addition, given that the findings of the present study are solely based on the participants' responses, future research should use (participant) observations of actual partnerships between parents and support staff to better assess what effective partnerships look like within daily practice.

Conclusion

This study highlights the complex dynamics involved in establishing effective partnerships between support staff and parents of adults with mild intellectual disabilities in residential care. While support staff recognise the value of such partnerships, challenges arise from the central role of service users' autonomy, differing views on what constitutes good quality care, differences in expectations—such as support staff expecting respect and trust from parents, which is not always reciprocated—and a lack of opportunities to connect with parents through informal contact. Addressing these challenges requires attention to the needs and behaviours of all parties involved and organisational support to facilitate effective partnership. Future research should continue to explore these dynamics from multiple perspectives, including service users themselves, and investigate practical interventions—such as moral case deliberations and (organisational) support and consultation—to strengthen parent‐staff partnerships and enhance the quality of care.
