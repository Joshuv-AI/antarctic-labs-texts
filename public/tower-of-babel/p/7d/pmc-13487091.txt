Arthroscopic vs Percutaneous Minimally Invasive Debridement and Reinsertion for the Treatment of Insertional Achilles Tendinosis: A Retrospective Analysis of Patient-Reported Outcomes
Authors: Sofia Rivera, Jorge I Acevedo, Eric H Durudogan, Kevin D Martin, Anish R Kadakia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) for insertional Achilles tendinopathy (IAT) has been adopted to reduce soft tissue trauma, accelerate recovery, and minimize complications associated with open procedures. Both percutaneous and arthroscopic repairs have demonstrated favorable outcomes, but direct comparison is limited. This study compares complication rates and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) between the two techniques. We hypothesized that both approaches would achieve postoperative scores comparable to population means.Methods:This retrospective nonrandomized cohort study included patients undergoing percutaneous or arthroscopic IAT repair with minimum 1-year follow-up. PROs including Foot and Ankle Ability Measure Activities of Daily Living subscale (FAAM ADL), Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System for Physical Function (PROMIS PF), and visual analog scale (VAS) scores were analyzed using Bonferroni adjusted significance thresholds (α = 0.05/3 = 0.0167). Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) achievement was compared using χ2 tests and sensitivity analyses were performed to account for baseline differences (α = 0.05).Results:Fifty patients (25 arthroscopic, 25 percutaneous) were included. The arthroscopic group had significantly longer follow-up (P < .001), and a significantly higher prevalence of diabetes (P < .01). There were no complications. All PROs improved significantly from baseline (P < .001 or P < .01). Postoperative VAS scores were significantly lower in the percutaneous group (0.3 ± 0.5 vs 1.6 ± 1.7, P < .01), averaging 2.2 points lower after adjustment (β = −2.22, 95% CI −3.33 to −1.12; P < .001). Postoperative FAAM ADL scores were higher in the percutaneous group (92.4 ± 15.5 vs 82.5 ± 15.5), although this did not meet the pre-specified significance threshold (P = .03; α = 0.0167); after adjustment, scores averaged 13 points higher in the percutaneous group (β = 12.99, 95% CI 1.29-24.69, P < .05).Conclusion:Both percutaneous and arthroscopic MIS approaches were associated with significant improvements in pain and function postoperatively. The percutaneous approach was associated with significantly lower postoperative pain scores after adjustment, and may also be associated with higher FAAM ADL scores, although this finding did not meet the pre-specified significance threshold and requires confirmation in larger prospective studies.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective comparative study.Keywords: insertional Achilles tendinopathy, Haglund deformity, minimally invasive surgery, Achilles repair, arthroscopy, patient-reported outcomes

Introduction

Traditional open surgical techniques for insertional Achilles tendinopathy (IAT) involve a central tendon-splitting incision, open debridement, resection of Haglund deformity (HD), and tendon reattachment using suture anchors.1-3 Although effective, this approach carries risks of delayed wound healing, nerve injury, infection, painful scarring, and prolonged rehabilitation. Minimally invasive surgical (MIS) approaches have expanded to reduce soft tissue trauma, accelerate recovery, and minimize complications.4,5

Historically, MIS approaches had been indicated only in patients with mild IAT requiring only a Haglund resection. However, recent literature combining Haglund resection with percutaneous suture augmentation of the Achilles tendon have demonstrated faster recovery and high patient satisfaction in cases of IAT with HD.4,6-13 Further, a double-row suture repair technique has been shown to significantly increase load to failure, enhancing the strength of the repair and accelerating recovery. 14

A percutaneous approach uses fluoroscopic guidance for Haglund resection, debridement, and tendon reinsertion. An arthroscopic approach uses direct visualization at the stage of Haglund resection and retrocalcaneal debridement. 4 Both approaches allow for thorough debridement of any potential sources of impingement including soft tissue and tendon calcification prior to Achilles tendon reattachment.

Both arthroscopic and percutaneous MIS approaches have demonstrated favorable outcomes compared with the open approach, including shorter recovery periods, fewer complications, and high patient satisfaction.7,8,10,12 However, there is a scarcity of literature directly comparing the two approaches. It is possible that the direct visualization of the arthroscopic approach may lead to better functional recovery through more complete decompression. On the other hand, the percutaneous approach may allow for less soft tissue disruption, potentially leading to lower postoperative pain and faster rehabilitation. This study employed an exploratory approach to compare patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and complication rates between percutaneous and arthroscopic debridement and reinsertion for IAT. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the complication profiles and patient-reported postoperative pain and functional outcomes of the two approaches. We hypothesized that both cohorts would achieve postoperative scores similar to population means.

Methods

Patient Data

This was a retrospective cohort study of patients undergoing minimally invasive debridement and reinsertion for IAT between December 2021 and October 2024. Percutaneous procedures were performed by 2 board-certified, fellowship-trained surgeons from 2 separate large, academic, tertiary medical centers. Arthroscopic procedures were performed by a single board-certified, fellowship-trained surgeon in a large private practice setting. Approval for the retrospective review of electronic medical records (EMRs) and patient surveys was obtained by each provider from the institutional review board of their respective organization.

Patient Selection

Patients aged ≥18 years were included in the study if they had clinical evidence of IAT on diagnostic imaging (ie, magnetic resonance imaging) and physical examination, had failed at least 6 months of conservative therapy, and underwent primary percutaneous or arthroscopic surgery with at least 1 year of follow-up. Patients were excluded if they had previous Achilles tendon surgery or if they required adjunct procedures such as a flexor hallucis longus tendon transfer that would be indicative of insufficient tendon tissue. Patients were also excluded if they had incomplete medical records or follow-up data. Only patients with preoperative and at least 1-year postoperative data, of at least 1 form of PRO, were included in this study. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all study participants before their inclusion in the research.

Surgical Techniques

Under spinal or general anesthesia with regional block, all patients were positioned prone, and the position for 4 stab incisions were drawn at the 2-, 5-, 7-, and 10-o’clock positions when viewing the tendon posteriorly (Figure 1). A minimally invasive burr was employed through the distal portals to resect the Haglund prominence and debride any tendon calcifications as needed (Figure 2). For percutaneous patients, burr positioning, debridement of bone, and resection of tendon calcification were performed under fluoroscopic guidance. A sharp elevator was used to elevate the Achilles tendon from the calcaneus, inferior to superior, avoiding tendon transection. For arthroscopic patients, a posterior scope and the burr were employed through the distal portals to visualize the resection of Haglund prominence and calcaneal spur, as well as the debridement of soft tissue and Achilles tendon calcifications, as needed (Figure 3). For both approaches, knotless suture anchors were then placed in a double-row configuration under fluoroscopic guidance in order to reattach the Achilles tendon to the calcaneus (Figure 4). This technique involves passing fiber tapes from the proximal portals through the medial Achilles tendon and up through the skin, then retrieving it back through the proximal portals and shuttling it diagonally to the contralateral distal portal where it was secured with a knotless technique (Figure 5). A negative Thompson test was confirmed in all patients after fixation. Surgical sites were irrigated, closed using 3-0 nylon suture, and covered with sterile xeroform gauze and a well-padded dressing.

Postoperative Protocols

Percutaneous patients were placed into a tall CAM boot with 2 Achilles wedges directly following surgical intervention and advised to be weight bearing as tolerated. At their 2-week postoperative appointment, sutures were removed, patients removed 1 Achilles wedge, and they began physical therapy. Patients progressed to full-weight bearing in a tall CAM boot with no wedges by week 4 postoperative, and transitioned to a stiff-soled shoe with no heel lift by week 6.

Arthroscopic patients were placed in a short-leg splint following surgical intervention. At their 2-week postoperative appointment, sutures were removed and patients were advised to remain non-weightbearing in a tall CAM boot with 2 Achilles wedges. Patients were advised to transition to weight bearing as tolerated at week 3 postoperative, and began physical therapy at week 4 postoperative. Patients transitioned to stiff-soled shoes with ½-in. heel lifts by week 6 to 8, and to stiff-soled shoes with no lift by week 12.

Data Collection

The EMRs and outpatient notes of all patients were reviewed to extract data on preoperative information and perioperative complications. Patient demographics included patient age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). Comorbidities evaluated were diabetes mellitus (DM) and tobacco use history, which included never, former, or current tobacco use. Perioperative complication data included wound complications, infection, hardware failure (including anchor pullout or suture breakage), revision, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and pulmonary embolism (PE). All patients were contacted preoperatively and postoperatively to assess levels of pain and function. Foot and Ankle Ability Measure Activities of Daily Living subscale (FAAM ADL), Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System for Physical Function (PROMIS PF), and visual analog scale (VAS) scores were collected for all arthroscopic patients. Each percutaneous surgeon collected FAAM ADL and either VAS or PROMIS PF, which created sample size discrepancies.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcomes of this study were the mean postoperative FAAM ADL, PROMIS PF, and VAS scores as reported by patients in both groups at least 1 year after surgery. Secondary outcomes were the mean preoperative to postoperative change in PROs. FAAM ADL is recorded as a 0% to 100% score, with higher scores indicating improved function in activities of daily living. PROMIS PF was developed for a US population mean score of 50, with higher scores also indicating better physical function. The VAS scale is a continuous scale (0-10) with lower scores indicating decreased pain.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for preoperative patient information. Exploratory-driven testing was performed with Microsoft Excel and Social Science Statistics. Pearson χ2 testing was used to compare categorical data between the 2 groups, including patient sex, tobacco use history, and DM. Mean score and SD for FAAM ADL, PROMIS PF, and VAS were calculated. Paired t tests were performed to analyze preoperative and postoperative scores within groups. Unpaired t tests were performed to compare the preoperative, postoperative, and change in FAAM ADL, PROMIS PF, and VAS between groups. Statistical significance was set with a Bonferroni adjustment for multiple outcomes (α = 0.05/3 = 0.0167). Hedges g was chosen as a bias-corrected effect size measure optimized for unequal and/or small sample sizes. Secondarily, minimal clinically important difference (MCID) scores were calculated using a distribution-based method (0.5 × SD of ∆ in PROs). 15 The proportion of each cohort that met the MCID was calculated and compared via χ2 tests (α = 0.05).

Because of significant baseline differences between groups, multivariable linear regressions were performed in Jamovi as sensitivity analyses in order to assess whether cohort membership independently predicted postoperative outcomes. Postoperative scores were the dependent variable, and preoperative scores, follow-up duration, cohort assignment, and diabetes status were the independent variables.

Results

Patient Demographics

A total of 111 patients received minimally invasive operative management for IAT between December 2021 and October 2024. Fifty-three patients underwent percutaneous MIS Achilles repair, but only 25 of these patients were eligible for inclusion (26 patients were excluded for not having completed 1-year follow-up, and 2 patients were excluded for having prior ipsilateral Achilles surgery or concomitant FHL transfer). Fifty-eight patients underwent arthroscopic MIS Achilles repair, but only 25 of these patients were eligible for inclusion (33 patients were excluded for not having completed 1 year follow-up).

After accounting for inclusion and exclusion criteria, 50 patients were included in final analysis. Twenty-five of these procedures were performed percutaneously, and 25 procedures were performed arthroscopically. Table 1 displays patient demographic and comorbidity characteristics. The percutaneous group included 9 male and 16 female participants, with a mean age of 54.9 years, ranging from 37 to 74 years, and a mean BMI of 39.2, ranging from 26 to 57. The arthroscopic group included 4 males and 21 females, with a mean age of 58.8 years, ranging from 45 to 78 years, and a mean BMI of 36.5, ranging from 25 to 49. One percutaneous patient and 9 arthroscopic patients had DM at the time of surgery (P < .01). The percutaneous group had a mean follow-up time of 1.1 years (range = 1.0-1.8), and the arthroscopic group had a mean follow-up time of 1.8 years (range = 1.0-3.1) (P < .001).

In both cohorts, all postoperative PRO scores were found to be significantly improved (P < .001 or P < .01) compared with preoperative scores (Table 2).

Postoperative VAS scores were significantly lower in the percutaneous group compared with the arthroscopic group (0.3 ± 0.5 vs 1.6 ± 1.7, P < .01) (Figure 6C) while preoperative scores were comparable (6.9 ± 1.1 vs 7.4 ± 1.7, P = .278) (Table 2). Sensitivity analysis confirmed that patients in the percutaneous cohort had postoperative VAS scores that were, on average, 2.2 points lower than patients in the MIS cohort after controlling for preoperative score, follow-up duration, and diabetes status (β = −2.22, 95% CI −3.33 to −1.12, P < .001). Our multivariate linear regression model also showed that higher preoperative VAS scores were found to be significantly associated with higher postoperative VAS scores (β = 0.408, 95% CI 0.097-0.719, P < .05), longer follow-up duration was found to be significantly associated with lower postoperative pain levels (β = −1.23, 95% CI −2.06 to −4.0, P < .01), and no significant difference in postoperative VAS scores was observed between patients with and without diabetes (β = −0.354, 95% CI −1.38 to 0.68, P = .490).

Postoperative FAAM ADL scores were higher in the percutaneous group, although no statistical difference could be detected with the numbers available (92.4 ± 15.5 vs 82.5 ± 15.5, P = .03) (Figure 6A), and preoperative FAAM ADL scores were also significantly higher in the percutaneous group (56.0 ± 13.9 vs 40.8 ± 20.6, P < .01). Sensitivity analysis revealed, however, that patients in the percutaneous cohort had postoperative FAAM ADL scores that were, on average, 13 points higher than patients in the MIS cohort after controlling for preoperative score, follow-up duration, and diabetes status (β = 12.99, 95% CI 1.29-24.69, P < .05). Our multivariate linear regression model also showed that higher preoperative FAAM ADL scores were found to be significantly associated with higher postoperative FAAM ADL scores (β = 0.313, 95% CI 0.02-0.61, P < .05), longer follow-up duration tended to be associated with higher postoperative FAAM ADL scores (β = 9.38, 95% CI −0.24 to 19.0, P = .056), and no significant difference in postoperative FAAM ADL scores was observed between patients with and without diabetes (β = 3.20, 95% CI −8.93 to 15.33, P = .597).

Postoperative and preoperative PROMIS PF scores were comparable between groups (47.7 ± 6.9 vs 51.8 ± 9.4, P = .263, and 36.9 ± 4.5 vs 31.1 ± 12.4, P = .209, respectively) (Figure 6B). Sensitivity analysis confirmed that there was no statistically significant difference in postoperative PROMIS PF scores between the percutaneous and the MIS cohorts after controlling for preoperative score, follow-up duration, and diabetes status (β = 3.55, 95% CI −12.3 to 5.2, P = .413). Our multivariate linear regression model also showed that preoperative PROMIS PF scores did not predict postoperative PROMIS PF scores in this data set (β = −0.034, 95% CI −0.35 to 0.28, P = .826), follow-up duration was not associated with higher postoperative PROMIS PF scores (β = 2.72, 95% CI −2.49 to 7.93, P = .294), and there was no evidence that diabetes status is associated with postoperative PROMIS PF (β = −3.03, 95% CI −11.0 to 4.95, P = .443).

The arthroscopic group had higher mean changes in FAAM ADL and PROMIS PF scores, and the percutaneous group had a higher mean change in VAS scores, but with the numbers available, no statistical difference between groups could be detected (Figure 7). There were also no significant differences detected in the proportion of patients whose change in PROs met the MCID between groups (Table 2).

Complications

None of the 50 total patients experienced perioperative wound complications, infection, hardware failure, revision, or postoperative DVT or PE.

Discussion

Hall et al 4 presented a comprehensive review of both percutaneous and arthroscopic strategies for Haglund syndrome and IAT, highlighting reduced wound complication rates, faster recovery, and better cosmetic outcomes compared with open techniques. Notably, they emphasized the utility of these techniques in patients with comorbidities such as DM or smoking, where traditional open approaches carry elevated risks for wound breakdown. Ferranti et al 6 reported excellent outcomes with percutaneous calcaneoplasty in patients with HD and mild to moderate IAT. They found significant improvement in both VAS and Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment-Achilles (VISA-A) scores and reported no wound complications, with 84.5% of patients expressing complete satisfaction with their outcomes. Although their study did not include double-row reattachment, it supports the trend toward lower complication rates and improved early recovery associated with the percutaneous approach. Miller et al 9 employed a technique similar to that used in our arthroscopic cohort. In their case series of >75 patients, there were no instances of wound complications, and patients returned to protected weightbearing by postoperative day 10. The literature consistently reports reduced wound complications, faster recovery, and high patient satisfaction with MIS over open techniques.4,6-10,12

Our study builds on the present literature by directly comparing the specific MIS techniques that can be used for the surgical management of IAT; offering a direct comparison of arthroscopic vs percutaneous approaches. Importantly, both the arthroscopic and percutaneous groups reported significantly improved function and pain postoperatively compared to preoperatively. Patients in the percutaneous cohort had postoperative VAS scores that were, on average, 2.2 points lower than patients in the MIS cohort after controlling for preoperative score, follow-up duration, and diabetes status (P < .001). This difference is both statistically significant and clinically meaningful, as it exceeds the reported MCID value of 1 to 2 points for this PRO. 16 Patients in the percutaneous cohort also had postoperative FAAM ADL scores that were, on average, 13 points higher than patients in the MIS cohort after controlling for preoperative score, follow-up duration, and diabetes status (P < .05). This difference is both statistically significant and clinically meaningful as well, as it exceeds commonly the reported MCID value of 8 to 10 points for this PRO. 17 Our findings suggest that although both approaches were associated with PRO scores at population means, the percutaneous approach may be associated with lower postoperative pain and higher postoperative FAAM ADL scores, possibly related to decreased soft tissue disruption as compared to the arthroscopic approach. It is important to note that the absence of complications in both groups may be due to the limited sample size rather than the safety of the surgical techniques.

Limitations of this study include its nonrandomized design and a relatively small sample size lacking a priori sample size calculation. Patients were assigned to treatment groups based on their treating physician, introducing differences in surgical techniques and also causing concern for selection or confounding bias. It is also important to point out the differences in postoperative protocol between groups, specifically earlier weightbearing and initiation of physical therapy in the percutaneous group compared with the arthroscopic group. Given that each percutaneous surgeon collected either VAS or PROMIS PF, we had an especially small sample size for PROMIS PF. Our sensitivity analysis showed a wide CI for PROMIS PF, indicating that our data may be underpowered for this PRO. Further, our use of a distribution-based method for the MCID analysis defines only “the minimum value below which a change in pain score is hopefully not due to measurement error.” 18 Future prospective, randomized studies with larger cohorts and longer-term follow-up are necessary to validate our findings. The authors of this study are currently performing a multicenter prospective investigation of the clinical outcomes and safety profiles of arthroscopic and percutaneous approaches.

Conclusion

This study provides a direct comparison of patients undergoing percutaneous or arthroscopic surgical debridement and reattachment for the treatment of IAT. Both approaches were associated with significant improvements in patient-reported pain and function, with postoperative scores reaching population means in each cohort; however, no complications were observed in either group, which may reflect the limited sample size rather than established safety. The percutaneous approach may be associated with lower postoperative pain scores and higher FAAM ADL scores, but future prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate our findings. These results build on the current literature and may also aid in counseling patients about potential postoperative improvements in pain and function following MIS surgical intervention for IAT.
