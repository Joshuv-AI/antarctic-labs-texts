Upper extremity lacrosse injuries presenting to United States emergency departments – 10 year review
Authors: Coen R Zediker, Abhishek Srivastava, Rami Taha, Karina Brouse, Hagop Klachian, Michael N Megafu, Robert L Parisien, Xinning Li, Antonio Cusano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLacrosse is a team-based recreational sport that has experienced a marked increase in popularity over the past 2null decades. Despite this growth, limited epidemiologic data currently exist regarding upper extremity (UE) injuries in this athletic population. The current study characterizes national U.S. trends and diagnoses of orthopedic-related UE injuries that presented to the emergency department from 2014 to 2023.MethodsData were collected from the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System using specific inclusion criteria to identify lacrosse-related UE injuries. Collected data were categorized by anatomical location and diagnosis, and national estimates were calculated by applying statistical weights assigned by National Electronic Injury Surveillance System coders at participating emergency departments.ResultsAn estimated 38,731 lacrosse-related UE injuries occurred nationally during the study period. Of the 1,415 individual analyzed cases, the most commonly injured sites were the shoulder (n = 363, 25.6%), fingers (n = 289, 20.4%), lower arm (n = 263, 18.6%), wrist (n = 241, 17%), hand (n = 133, 9.4%), elbow (n = 90, 6.4%), and upper arm (n = 36, 2.5%). Fractures accounted for the majority of diagnoses (n = 621, 43.9%), with the lower arm (eg, forearm) being the most frequent fracture location.ConclusionThe overall incidence of injuries remained stable across this 10-year period, with a statistically significant decrease in 2020 (P < .05), likely due to the global COVID-19 pandemic and imposed contact restrictions. Mechanistically, the high frequency of lower arm injuries is presumed to be secondary to direct trauma from “body checks” and/or “stick checks” as the lower arm is often unprotected, rendering it more susceptible to injury.Keywords: Lacrosse, NEISS, Emergency department, Orthopedic, Trauma, Upper extremity

Lacrosse is a team-based sport that originated among the Indigenous peoples of North America in the 12th century and has traditionally held significant cultural and spiritual meaning, often played ritualistically and in preparation for war.6 In recent decades, the sport has experienced significant growth in popularity, evolving into a mainstream competitive sport with widespread participation at the youth, collegiate, and professional levels throughout the United States.1 As of 2021, an estimated 1.89 million individuals were actively playing lacrosse nationwide. Still, despite this rise, there is a paucity of data specifically pertaining to this athletic cohort. Like other high-impact contact sports such as American football, hockey, and rugby, lacrosse carries an elevated risk for injury. Protective equipment and contact regulations differ significantly between male and female athletes, which in turn influences injury patterns. For example, while male athletes are required to wear a helmet with an attached faceguard, their female counterparts are mandated only to wear goggles, cleats, and a mouthguard.1 (Table I) These equipment disparities reflect differences in the rules—stick and body checking is permitted in the men's game, but is restricted or limited in the women's game.12 This places male athletes at a higher risk of contact-related orthopedic injuries.

Several studies have evaluated general injury patterns in lacrosse. An epidemiologic study conducted by Foss et al in 2017 found that injury incidence ranged from 0.095 to 12.98 per 1,000 lacrosse athletes, with sprains, strains, concussions, and contusions being the most commonly reported injuries.3 This study showed that males were far more susceptible to immediate injury from player-to-player contact. In this regard, the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System (NEISS) has served as a valuable tool for investigating trends in orthopedic injuries presenting to United States Emergency Departments (US EDs). Diamond and Gale used NEISS to analyze men's and women's lacrosse head injuries over a 10-year span, noting that males contributed to 80.5% of all cases of injuries, and 20.4% of injuries were either facial or cranial in etiology.5 They found that 24% of these injuries were classified as closed head injuries or concussions. Noncranial injuries most commonly involved the fingers (12.8%), shoulder (11.5%), and ankle (11.1%). In 98% of cases, treatment and discharge occurred in the ED.

Despite the dramatic rise in popularity, there remains a paucity of literature specifically focused on upper extremity (UE) orthopedic injuries in lacrosse athletes. The current study investigates national trends in lacrosse-related UE injuries from 2014 to 2023 using NEISS data. The authors hypothesized that fractures would be the most common diagnosis, particularly involving the distal upper extremity (eg, forearm), and that these injuries would predominantly affect male athletes due to mechanisms such as body and stick checking.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Data were obtained from the Consumer Product Safety Commission's NEISS, a database that collects injury information from a stratified probability sample over 100 U S. hospital EDs, all of which operate 24-hour services. NEISS utilizes trained coders within each ED to input detailed injury information, including diagnosis, anatomical location, patient demographic, and a brief narrative of the event. For the current study, lacrosse-related UE injuries were identified using the activity-specific product code for lacrosse (1215: lacrosse activity, apparel, or equipment) in conjunction with body part codes corresponding to upper extremity sites: shoulder (30), elbow (32), lower arm (33), wrist (34), upper arm (80), hand (82), and finger (92). Cases that did not involve these specified body parts were excluded. Individuals aged 1 year and older were included in the study. The dataset spanned 10 years, from January 2014 through December 2023. The final dataset included the following variables: treatment date, age, gender, race, body part, diagnosis, disposition, and a brief descriptive narrative provided by medical providers that described the nature of the injury. Narratives were not reviewed individually, but irrelevant injury locations outside the scope of the paper's focus were excluded by the first author on a case-by-case basis. The mechanism of injury was not explicitly coded in the NEISS dataset and therefore, could not be analyzed in this study. Further research incorporating detailed injury mechanisms is warranted.

Statistical analysis

Data were obtained from the NEISS database after querying the above-mentioned parameters. Data from 2014 to 2023 were sorted to calculate annual case counts, categorized by UE injury site, diagnosis, and fracture location. National estimates (NEs) were calculated by multiplying each data point by its associated weight. Weighted percentages were calculated by dividing the category-specific case count (eg, body part, fracture site, diagnosis) by the total number of cases and then multiplying by the national estimate for that respective category. A simple linear regression analysis was performed, with the independent variable the year of the injury and the dependent variable the number of injuries that year. Weighted percentages were rounded to the nearest tenth. Statistical significance was set to a P < .05.

Results

A total of 1,415 lacrosse-related UE injuries were identified in the NEISS database from January 2014 to December 2023, corresponding to a national estimate of 38,730 ED presentations (Table II). Seven cases were excluded as the associated body part location code was not consistent with that of the UE. Among included cases, 1,184 were male (NE = 32,407; 83.7%) and 231 were female (NE 6,323; 16.3%). The mean age of all patients was 15.6 years (range: 5 to 79 years). Race/ethnicity was not analyzed due to incomplete data. The most frequently injured anatomical sites were the shoulder (n = 363; NE = 9,936; 25.6%), finger (n = 289; NE = 7,910; 20.4%), lower arm (n = 263; NE = 7,199; 18.6%), wrist (n = 241; NE = 6,597; 17.0%), and hand (n = 133; NE = 3,640; 9.4%) (Table II). Fracture was the most common diagnosis (n = 621; NE = 16,998; 43.9%), followed by contusions/abrasions (n = 296; NE = 8,102; 20.9%), and strains/sprains (n = 210; NE = 5,748; 14.8%). Less frequent diagnoses included dislocations (n = 53; NE = 1,415; 3.8%), avulsions (n = 27; NE = 739; 1.9%), lacerations (n = 26; NE = 712; 1.8%), hematomas (n = 17; NE = 465; 1.2%), dermatologic conditions (n = 4; NE = 109; 0.3%), amputations (n = 2; NE = 55; 0.1%), crushing injuries (n = 1; NE = 27; 0.1%), and puncture wounds (n = 1; NE = 27; 0.1%) (Table III). Among the 621 fracture cases, the most frequently involved anatomical regions were the lower arm (n = 155; NE = 4,250; 26.5%), wrist (n = 130; NE = 3,553; 22.2%), shoulder (n = 129; NE = 3,536; 22.1%), and finger (n = 121; NE = 3,315; 20.7%), (Table III). A linear regression analysis was utilized to evaluate trends in the national estimates of UE over time. The incidence remained relatively stable over the 10-year period, except for a statistically significant decrease in 2020 (P < .001), likely secondary to reduced athletic activity and contact restrictions imposed during the COVID-19 pandemic (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The current study analyzed trends in UE lacrosse injuries presenting to US EDs over a 10-year period from 2014 to 2023. Our findings demonstrate that lacrosse-related UE injuries remained relatively stable across the study period, except for a statistically significant decrease in 2020, likely reflective of the widespread cancellations and restrictions on youth sports during the COVID-19 pandemic. The most frequently injured UE site was the shoulder, followed by the finger and lower arm. Fractures represented the majority of all injuries, with the lower arm being the most common fracture site. Despite the increase in injury prevention arising from the rapid growth of the sport, injuries to the upper extremity in male and female lacrosse athletes remained relatively constant between 2014 and 2023, based on the study results. As lacrosse gains significant popularity nationwide, understanding the mechanisms and distribution of injuries is essential for improving player safety and developing effective interventions.9

As of 2017, approximately 447,213 youth athletes – accounting for 54% of all lacrosse participants—were involved in the sport. Given this large population, a thorough understanding of injury patterns in young athletes is critical for ensuring long-term health and safety. Thus, athletes are particularly vulnerable given their immature musculoskeletal systems. A study by Kerr et al reported injury rates of 10.3 per 1,000 athletic exposures among youth athletes, compared to 5.3 and 4.7 per 1,000 among high school and NCAA players, respectively.7 In a separate study, the same authors found that youth boys sustained the majority of their injuries during games rather than practices (60%).8 Further, youth athletes tend to be less mature and have increased risk-taking tendencies, which can contribute to increased risk of traumatic sports-related injuries. These findings underscore the importance of developing sport-specific and age-specific injury prevention strategies to promote safe participation.

Although UE injury rates remained stable over time, small, nonsignificant fluctuations in the number of injuries were present during the 10-year period. This may be due to several factors, including variations in sport participation, athlete exposure, and the implementation of protective measures. For instance, structured injury-prevention programs, such as FIFA 11+ Kids, which have been successfully implemented in youth soccer, have the potential to reduce the injury burden in lacrosse.14 Additionally, evidence supporting the effectiveness of protective equipment in lacrosse may also contribute to subtle decreases in injury incidence.4,11

Male athletes represented the majority of injury cases (83.7%), which may be attributed to several sex-specific differences in game rules and play styles. Although the mechanism of injury could not be directly assessed in this study, male athletes are exposed to more aggressive play because body and stick checks are permitted.4,11 In contrast, girls' lacrosse imposes stricter limitations on physical contact, which may reduce the risk of high-energy trauma. The most common injury sites in our dataset—shoulder, finger, and lower arm—correspond well with areas frequently exposed during defensive maneuvers in boys' lacrosse. The predominance of fractures in these regions (notably, lower-arm fractures accounting for 25.6% of all fractures) supports the hypothesis that unprotected areas are particularly vulnerable to high-velocity impacts. These findings are consistent with prior research demonstrating a high incidence of injury to the hand, wrist, and forearm in lacrosse.13 Unfortunately, the mechanism of injury is not analyzed for individual injuries obtained from NEISS, but it may offer direction for future investigative efforts.

A study by Bano et al found that lacrosse-related injury rates presenting to U.S. EDs from 2000 to 2016 increased from 1.9 per 10,000 athletes in 2000 to 5.3 in 2012, followed by a decline to 3.4 in 2016.2 Boys were 1.62 times more likely to be injured via player-to-player contact, while girls were 2.21 times more likely to sustain non-contact injuries. With age, injuries from stick contact decreased, whereas player collisions became more common. These findings align with our data, which suggests that male athletes are disproportionately affected by high-impact trauma, as evidenced by the observed elevated fracture rate. Although this study did not specifically investigate concussion or head trauma, previous work by Macknofsky et al found 37,974 lacrosse-related concussions between 2006 and 2019, with a mean athlete age of 14.5 ± 3.5 years.10 Most concussions in boys were caused by player-to-ground contact, whereas girls more commonly sustained concussions from stick or ball impacts. This again highlights differences in injury mechanism and reinforces the vulnerability of youth athletes to traumatic injury. Similarly, Xiang et al found that the most frequently injured body part during gameplay was the head/face (32.0%), while lower extremity injuries predominated in practice settings.15 Notably, among boys, contact with another player was the most common mechanism of injury. Future studies should aim to differentiate between injuries sustained during competition versus practice in UE injuries to better inform targeted prevention efforts.

Based on the current study findings, the authors recommend implementing injury prevention programs to better educate players, coaches, and parents about the potential for injury in lacrosse-related activities, including the increased use of protective equipment—particularly for the lower arm. Additionally, further consideration could be given to modifying defensive techniques, such as stick and body checks, in youth leagues to help reduce preventable high-energy contact injuries in this setting.

Limitations

This study has several inherent limitations through its use of NEISS data. First, the mechanism of injury was not reliably reported and relied heavily on the narrative provided by ED coders. Many narrative entries lacked sufficient detail and varied in the amount of information provided. Additionally, injury severity was inconsistently documented, preventing stratification by grade or complexity. Another important limitation was the lack of specific diagnostic information on injury and fracture patterns, as well as on other potential imaging findings. As a result, fractures were broadly categorized by anatomical region rather than radiographic characteristics. It was also unclear in some instances whether multiple injuries were present in a single patient encounter, such as concurrent fractures in different regions of the upper extremity. This could potentially inflate the total number of injuries recorded and may limit the interpretation of injury counts per athlete.

Furthermore, the NEISS data likely underrepresent the true incidence of lacrosse-related UE injuries, as the dataset captures only ED visits and therefore omits injuries treated in outpatient clinic settings such as urgent care clinics or by athletic trainers. This is particularly true for pediatric and adolescent athletes who sustain less severe injuries, such as sprains, contusions, and nondisplaced fractures. Accordingly, the NEISS sample may disproportionately reflect more severe injuries requiring immediate medical attention, which may explain the high proportion of fractures observed in our study. The true burden of injury among all lacrosse athletes nationwide during the study period is therefore likely underestimated.

Conclusion

Between 2014 and 2023, lacrosse-related UE injuries presenting to U.S. EDs showed no significant change in annual incidence, except for a marked decline in 2020, likely secondary to restrictions imposed during the COVID-19 pandemic. Across the study period, the shoulder was the most commonly injured site, accounting for 25.6% of cases, while fractures represented the most frequent diagnosis (43.9%). Lower arm fractures were the most prevalent. These findings underscore the ongoing need for targeted injury prevention efforts, particularly in youth lacrosse. We recommend increased use of protective equipment designed to shield exposed areas of the upper extremity, particularly the forearm. Additionally, implementing structured injury prevention programs and regulating defensive techniques—such as stick and body checks—in youth leagues may help reduce the incidence of high-energy contact injuries. Continued surveillance and further research are warranted to better characterize injury mechanisms and to inform sport-specific safety strategies.
