Efficacy of Aspirin Usage in Free Flap Reconstruction of the Head and Neck
Authors: Robert E. Africa, Brian J. McKinnon, Felix Maldonado‐Chapa, Viran J. Ranasinghe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo compare head and neck free flap outcomes and complications following aspirin administration.MethodsThis is a multicenter, retrospective cohort study using data from the 107 healthcare organizations in the United States. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with a history of head and neck cancer who received non‐bony or bony free flap reconstruction were included. They were grouped by receiving 81 mg of aspirin (ASA81), 325 mg of aspirin (ASA325), or no aspirin on the same day of surgery or postoperatively. Primary outcomes included free flap failure, blood vessel repair, and need for flap revision. Relative risks (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were evaluated. Subgroup analysis was conducted between ASA81 and ASA325.ResultsNo differences in primary outcomes were observed for same day administration of aspirin. Postoperatively, ASA81 reduced the risk of flap failure and needing a blood vessel repair procedure (RR: 0.65[0.49–0.86]; 0.52[0.42–0.65]). ASA325 was also associated with a decreased risk of flap failure, need for blood vessel repair, and the need for flap revision procedures (RR: 0.10[0.08–0.13]; 0.57[0.46–0.71]; 0.37[0.32–0.41]) for all flap types. For non‐bony reconstruction, all primary outcomes were lower in the ASA81 group (RR: 0.72[0.53–0.98]; 0.53[0.41–0.68]; 0.73[0.59–0.89]). ASA325 for non‐bony flaps was also associated with lower rates of all three primary outcomes (RR: 0.47[0.34–0.64]; 0.63[0.49–0.80]; 0.80[0.68–0.94]). Aspirin use in bony flaps was associated with similar outcomes. ASA81 compared to ASA325 for non‐bony reconstruction was associated with higher flap failure (RR: 1.69[1.16–2.46]).ConclusionFor non‐bony and bony free flap reconstruction, aspirin was associated with decreased risks of free flap failure, blood vessel repair, and flap revision.

Introduction

Following ablation of head and neck malignancies, free tissue transfer to a surgical site is often performed to restore form and function after extensive resection. While transferring tissue from a donor site to a recipient site is a critical portion of the procedure following large ablative procedures in the head and neck, another equally important portion of the procedure is the microvascular anastomosis. It is known that the most common reason for flap failure is related to complications at the anastomotic site [1]. Vascular occlusion from thrombosis after reconstruction is a well‐recognized reason for flap failure and loss [2, 3]. Technique of the anastomosis aside, perioperative management with antithrombotic therapy has been highly considered and is currently being implemented by many reconstructive surgeons. However, there are no standardized guidelines emphasizing practice. Currently, no antithrombotic prophylactic measures have been shown to reduce free flap anastomotic thrombosis or flap loss [4, 5]. However, given cancer and surgery being known risk factors for venous thromboembolism, pharmacologic deep vein thromboprophylaxis has been considered, and the risks of bleeding must be weighed against the benefits [5, 6]. Aspirin has been commonly used following free flap reconstructive surgery given its ability to act as an antiplatelet agent through inhibiting cyclooxygenase‐1 (COX‐1) [7]. However, it has not been shown to improve flap survival and reduce thrombosis in human subjects despite frequent use [5, 8]. Given this discrepancy, this present study sought to evaluate the efficacy of aspirin usage following head and neck free flap reconstruction with clinical data from a large database.

While there are previous studies that evaluate free flap outcomes following antithrombotic treatment, many do not designate whether the flap type was non‐bony or bony. Both flap types demonstrate different physiology. Non‐bony flaps such as the radial forearm or anterolateral thigh develop neovascular connections in addition to receiving oxygenated blood from the pedicle [9, 10]. Bony flaps such as the fibula or scapula rely on oxygenated blood perfusing through the periosteum by medullary blood flow [11]. Given the differences in physiology, the present study separates the free flap types by non‐bony and bony to evaluate differences in free flap outcomes following administration of aspirin.

To contribute to the dearth of literature, this retrospective cohort study provides insight into the usage of aspirin following free flap reconstruction of the head and neck. Moreover, the present study compares different aspirin dosages, which have previously not been well evaluated.

Methods

Study Design

De‐identified patient data were collected from 107 participating healthcare institutions in the United States from January 1, 2010 to July 31, 2025. The TriNetX database was utilized to collect, organize, and analyze the data. The database sorts data and information by categorizing medical codes including the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes for diagnoses or relevant symptoms, the Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes for procedures, and RxNorm codes and Veteran Affairs (VA) medication classification codes for prescribed or administered medications. The detailed list of codes used to collect and analyze patient data is highlighted in the Tables S1 and S2. The VA medication classification codes are used within the database to categorize prescribed medication and are not limited to VA healthcare. With these specified codes, patient cohorts were created to evaluate the impact of aspirin usage on head and neck free flap reconstruction outcomes. Usage of the database including the analytical functions, statistical interrogation, and propensity score matching has been previously validated [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. The database protects patient privacy by adhering to the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) standards. There are 24 publicly known healthcare organizations that provide data to the TriNetX database in the United States, but others may be kept incognito to maintain privacy [17]. The University of Texas Medical Branch is a known contributor. This research was supported by the UTMB Institute for Translational Sciences, supported in part by a Clinical and Translational Science Award (UL1 TR001439) from the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences at the National Institutes of Health (NIH). The content is solely the responsibility of the authors and does not necessarily represent the official views of the NIH. The University of Texas Medical Branch Institutional Review Board granted Institutional Review Board (IRB) exemption.

Specific inclusion criteria were implemented utilizing the previously mentioned medical coding systems. All patients in the study were adult patients aged 18 years or older with a history of head and neck malignant neoplasms of the lip, oral cavity, pharynx, nasal cavity, middle ear, larynx, or accessory sinuses. All patients were treatment naïve, and all patients had free flap reconstruction following head and neck surgery to address malignancy. Patients were stratified into groups that either received non‐bony or bony free flap reconstruction. All cohorts were further stratified into groups prescribed either 81 mg of aspirin (ASA81), 325 mg of aspirin (ASA325), or no aspirin on the same day of surgery or postoperatively.

Statistical Evaluation

Statistical interrogation and analysis were performed within the TriNetX database. Prior to evaluation, propensity score matching was utilized to control for covariates. This was performed with logistic regression for 1:1 matching and building matrices from the covariates. Matching uses the greedy nearest‐neighbor matching algorithm with a tolerance level of 0.1 and a difference of ≤ 0.1 between propensity scores. The covariates used for propensity score matching included tobacco use (Z72.0), hypertensive diseases (I10‐I1A), chronic ischemic heart disease (I25), ischemic heart diseases (I20‐I25), other forms of heart disease (I30‐I5A), diabetes mellitus (E08‐E13), glomerular diseases (N00‐N08), kidney disease (N17‐N19), obesity (E65‐E68), nutritional deficiencies (E50‐E64), factors influencing health status and contact with health services (socioeconomic status) (Z00‐Z99), and any anticoagulants including heparin. Outcomes were analyzed by determining the relative risk (RR) with 95% confidence intervals and p values of less than 0.05 highlighting statistical significance. Prior studies validated the analysis and statistical methods utilized in the present study [18, 19]. Statistical analyses conducted within TriNetX use JAVA, R, and Python programming languages. Primary outcomes evaluated included flap failure (need for secondary free flap procedures), blood vessel repair (microvascular anastomosis revision), and free flap revision. Secondary outcomes assessed included deep vein thrombosis (DVT), hematoma/seroma, cardiovascular complications, pneumonia, sepsis, surgical site infections, and fistula formation. This study assessed all outcomes up to 30 days after surgery.

Results

Propensity score matching successfully matched cohorts for the statistical analysis and created control groups with an equal number of patients. After matching, there were a total of 13,726 patients who received non‐bony reconstructive surgery, while 5508 patients were treated with bony reconstruction. Among patients who underwent non‐bony reconstruction, 502 patients received ASA81 on the same day of surgery while 1157 patients received ASA325. Postoperatively, 2304 patients received ASA81, while 2900 patients received ASA325. In the group of patients who underwent bony reconstructive surgery, 172 received ASA81 on the same day, while 415 received ASA325. Postoperatively, 792 received ASA81, and 1150 received ASA325.

Postoperative Administration of ASA81 or ASA325 for All Free Flap Types

When ASA was administered in the postoperative period after reconstructive surgery, the results differed compared to same‐day administration. Table 2 details the findings (Table 2). The rate of free flap failure was higher among patients who did not receive ASA compared to those who received ASA81 (RR: 0.65[0.49–0.96]). Patients who did not receive ASA had a significantly higher risk of free flap failure and the need for blood vessel repair compared to patients who received ASA325 (RR: 0.10[0.08–0.13]; 0.57[0.46–0.71]). Additionally, the need for blood vessel repair was higher among patients who did not receive ASA compared to those who were treated with ASA81 (RR: 0.52[0.42–0.65]). Among patients who received ASA325, the need for flap revision procedures was higher among those who did not receive ASA (RR: 0.37[0.32–0.41]). For secondary outcomes, the ASA81 group did not have a higher rate of hematoma/seroma, but the ASA325 group had a significantly higher rate compared to the non‐ASA group (RR: 2.07[1.12–3.82]). The rate of DVT was found to be lower in those who did not receive ASA325 (RR: 0.69[0.50–0.95]). Surgical site infection and fistula were also found to be higher among patients who received ASA325 compared to no ASA (RR: 2.24[1.61–3.12]; 2.00[1.14–3.51]). No primary outcomes including flap failure and the need for blood vessel repair were higher in one group when comparing ASA81 to ASA325 (RR: 1.34[0.98–1.85]; 0.95[0.73–1.24]), but the rate of DVT was higher in patients who received ASA81 compared to ASA325 (RR: 1.56[1.12–2.18]).

Postoperative Administration of ASA81 or ASA325 for Bony Free Flaps

Table 6 highlights the results for the efficacy of ASA81 and ASA325 for bony free flap reconstruction (Table 6). When compared to patients that received ASA81, patients that did not receive ASA were at higher risk for free flap failure, while the need for blood vessel repair and flap revision rates remained the same (RR: 0.37[0.22–0.62]; 0.73[0.46–1.15]; 1.03[0.81–1.30]). Discussion

Perioperative management of antithrombotic regimens remains a debated topic in microvascular reconstructive surgery, and many surgeons continue to utilize some form of antithrombotic agent [4, 20]. There does not appear to be a consensus among usage of different types including heparins, dextran, and aspirin [21, 22]. Though a perioperative regimen may be implemented, variability exists even with dosing, and many often rely on prior experience and training without a specific guideline practice [8]. Prior studies suggest that routine use of antithrombotic agents may not provide benefit in reducing flap loss and instead increase risk of bleeding and hematoma, and aspirin has been previously demonstrated to provide no benefit for protection from pedicle thrombosis and flap failure [1, 4]. Despite many reconstructive surgeons commonly using antithrombotic agents in the postoperative period free flap surgery, there is currently no pharmacologic measure or regimen that has been proven to reduce free flap anastomotic thrombosis and necrosis [5]. To address and contribute to the discussion regarding usage of antithrombotic agents in free flap reconstructive surgery, the present study utilized a large, multicenter database to assess the risks, outcomes, and complications following aspirin administration after free flap reconstructive surgery in the head and neck. Usage of a multicenter, population‐based database allows for comprehensive evaluation of complications and outcomes between patients that did or did not receive aspirin. Another important topic addressed by the present study is the comparison between usage of ASA81 and ASA325 related to free flap complications, and no prior studies have demonstrated a difference in outcomes between the two dosages [23, 24].

For same day administration of aspirin, the data from the present study suggest no associated increased risk of free flap complications including free flap failure, repair of the recipient site blood vessel by microvascular anastomosis, and the need for free flap revision procedures with administration of ASA81, ASA325, or no aspirin. Moreover, the risks of free flap complications between patients who received either ASA81 or ASA325 were not significantly higher in one patient group. However, there were significant differences in outcomes identified in the postoperative period when comparing different dosages of ASA to no aspirin administration. For all free flaps, administration of ASA81 when compared to no usage of aspirin was associated with a decreased risk of free flap failure and needing blood vessel repair after reconstruction. Despite this association, one prior randomized controlled trial demonstrated no improvement in flap survival and microvascular thrombosis despite aspirin administration [25]. Though involving lower extremity free flaps, one study with 117 patients found that preoperative aspirin was associated with lower rates of arterial thrombosis and flap failure [26]. From a safety standpoint, there was no increased risk of hematoma/seroma in patients who were treated with ASA81, which is different from the previously mentioned randomized controlled trial that identified an increased risk of hematoma without improvement in flap survival [25].

Additionally, usage of ASA325 was associated with a significantly decreased risk of free flap failure and rate of free flap revision procedures when compared to no aspirin. Though this relationship was observed, prior studies suggest no benefit to administration of ASA325. One prior study found no benefit compared to ASA325, identifying no significant effect on the rates of flap loss [27]. Despite this finding, the previously mentioned study is limited to a single institution and 3 reconstructive surgeons and lacks a robust patient cohort. Though the present study found an associated decreased risk of flap complications, the rate of hematoma/seroma was higher among patients that received ASA325 compared to no aspirin, which also differs from the previous study that found no increased risk of hematoma [27]. Another prior study found no flap survival benefit or increased rate of thrombosis, but the study did identify a similarly higher rate of hematoma formation. However, a weakness of the prior study is the inability to identify a specific dosage of aspirin used after free flap reconstruction related to the rate of hematoma [24]. The present study also found a higher rate of fistula formation in patients given ASA325, but there do not appear to be prior studies that examine the relationship between antithrombotic agents and fistulas. The relationship between administration of ASA325 and fistula formation may be due to bleeding and hematoma formation at the flap recipient site, given the present data demonstrates an increased risk of hematoma [28, 29, 30, 31]. Similarly, the hematoma may become an infective source, and this may be related to the significant rate of surgical site infections in the group of patients who received ASA325 [32, 33].

For non‐bony free flap reconstruction, usage of aspirin with different dosages was also associated with decreased risk of the major primary outcomes. Patients who received ASA81 had decreased risks of flap failure, needing a free flap revision procedure, or repair of blood vessel compared to patients who did not get aspirin. Patients that received ASA325 postoperatively also had a decreased risk of the previously mentioned outcomes. While the previous studies investigate the relationship between aspirin and free flap reconstruction outcomes, there is a lack of specificity in evaluation of non‐bony and bony free flaps. This makes comparisons between previous studies and the present study difficult, and it is important to separate the non‐bony and bony free flap types given different physiologic behavior after free tissue transfer [34]. In general, one prior study mostly evaluated non‐bony free flap reconstruction and evaluated the efficacy of postoperative administration of aspirin with subcutaneous heparin [35]. The previously reported flap survival rate with aspirin was 97.2% with flap failures at 2.8% [35]. The present study controls for heparin and other anticoagulant administration, and the findings suggest a higher incidence of flap failure among patients who did not receive ASA. The prior study also identified no increased risk of hematoma, which is also like the present study regardless of aspirin dosage for non‐bony free flaps. In contrast, a different study with mostly non‐bony free flaps found no benefit to free flap survival or flap loss from thrombosis of the microvascular anastomoses [8]. Though an important finding, the study acknowledges that the small population utilized in the study is a limitation to the findings. As with other previous studies, other weaknesses are the lack of identification of the dosage of aspirin and the mix of flaps with non‐bony and bony types. Assessing aspirin dosage may help identify differences in outcomes and complications, like in the present study where administration of ASA325 was associated with a reduction in free flap failure. Additionally, the rate of DVT was higher in patients who did not receive aspirin compared to ASA325. For bony free flap reconstruction, free flap failure and the need for blood vessel repair procedures were lower in patients who received ASA81 compared to no aspirin. Similarly, administration of ASA325 was associated with decreased risk of both previously mentioned free flap complications in addition to a reduced risk of free flap revision procedures for bony flaps.

This comprehensive evaluation of the efficacy of aspirin usage in head and neck free flap reconstructive surgery included analysis of the outcomes and complications between usage of ASA81 and ASA325. For all free flap types, no differences in risks were noted between the ASA81 and ASA325 groups. However, patients who received ASA81 had a higher risk of DVT compared to ASA325. In the non‐bony free flap group, patients who received ASA81 had a higher risk of free flap failure and DVT compared to ASA325. This relationship was not observed for bony free flap reconstruction. In contrast, a previous study by Karamanos et al. evaluated the differences between administration of ASA81 and ASA325 and found no differences in free flap outcomes including revision of arterial anastomosis, complete flap loss, and wound dehiscence [36]. The results of the previous study suggested that ASA325 is not superior to ASA81 for free flap reconstruction. These findings are similar to the lack of increased risk of free flap failure for all flap types in the present study, but they differ when flaps are stratified by non‐bony and bony types. Like other previous studies, the one by Karamanos et al. is limited in population size, which may prevent determination of a difference that may exist. This previous study also appears to be the only other study that compares ASA81 with ASA325, and there are no other studies specific for head and neck reconstructive surgery. Given the absence of data and dearth of literature for reconstruction of the head and neck, more large population studies are necessary to evaluate the differences in aspirin dosages on free flap outcomes.

Though this study effectively evaluated head and neck free flap outcomes in a large patient population in the context of perioperative administration of aspirin, there are limitations that prevent determination of cause‐and‐effect relationships. Inherent to all data derived from any database, medical records and codes used to describe a patient's status are subject to the individual entering the medical codes. There is potential bias in utilization of CPT, ICD‐10, and other coding systems. To limit bias, the TriNetX database frequently performs data quality checks to maintain accuracy of the medical codes used. Regarding potential errors, the moderators of the database communicate with healthcare institutions that contribute data to improve accuracy in the data. Additionally, the timing of aspirin can be specified on the same day of surgery, but when it is administered cannot be determined. This is also a known limitation of studies utilizing large databases, but these prior studies are still valid [14, 37, 38]. The complexity of free flap reconstruction for head and neck defects also cannot be determined, and large defects or more complex disease may be more predisposed to flap failure and require a secondary procedure. Moreover, secondary flap procedures including blood vessel repair and flap revision may be recorded as flap failure within the 30‐day window. The present study also does not account for other cases that underwent regional or pedicled flap procedures. The number of days patients were treated with the different aspirin dosages also cannot be specified. There is also the possibility of confounding given the indication for ASA325 in the postoperative period as these patients may have pre‐existing cardiovascular disease including history of coronary artery disease, treatment with coronary stenting, atherosclerotic disease, and atrial fibrillation. These forms of heart diseases among many others were applied in the propensity score matching process, but the matching process may not be completely adequate to control for confounding variables.

Conclusion

Regardless of free flap type, many of the primary outcomes including free flap failure, the need for blood vessel repair, and the need for free flap revision were decreased in patients who received aspirin in the postoperative period. While there was an increased risk of hematoma in patients who received ASA325, the risk of needing to repair the flap's blood vessel and flap failure was not elevated. It also should be noted that the risks of surgical site infection and fistula formation were found to be higher among those who received ASA325, and these are clinically consequential findings in the head and neck surgery population. These complications can have significant functional and oncologic implications. For non‐bony free flaps and bony free flaps, there were associated lower rates of the primary outcomes without increasing the risk of hematoma. Between administration of ASA81 and ASA325 after reconstruction, the risk of free flap failure was significantly higher in patients who received ASA81. Though the results of this study contribute to the discourse of utilization of aspirin in the perioperative period for head and neck free flap reconstruction, similarly sized prospective studies specifying the type of reconstruction and aspirin dosage are necessary. Moreover, large studies comparing the usage of ASA81 with ASA325 specifically after head and neck free flap reconstruction are indicated.
