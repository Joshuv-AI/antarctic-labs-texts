Characterising disability in patients with long COVID–does gender matter? an analytic cross-sectional study
Authors: Cecilia Hee Laursen, Trine Brøns Nielsen, Steffen Leth, Berit Schiøttz-Christensen, Claus Vinther Nielsen, Vivian Langagergaard, Lotte Sørensen, Dorthe Sørensen, Lisa Gregersen Oestergaard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong COVID affects patients’ daily functioning and activity participation. However, gender-specific differences remain insufficiently explored despite well-documented effects of gender on health outcomes. This study examines gender differences in sociodemographic characteristics, employment status, sick leave, and mental fatigue among individuals with long COVID. Furthermore, this study explores self-reported and prioritised problems with activities of daily living (ADL) across genders and compares patterns between women and men. We hypothesised that traditional gender roles would manifest in distinct challenges for women and men.MethodsWe included 780 individuals (567 women and 213 men) diagnosed with long COVID who were referred to occupational therapy at a Danish outpatient clinic for long COVID. Sociodemographic characteristics, employment status, and sick leave were self-reported. Mental fatigue was assessed using the Mental Fatigue Scale, and ADL problems using the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure. A qualitative deductive content analysis was conducted to further categorise prioritised ADL problems.ResultsA higher proportion of women than men had higher education (55% vs. 37%). No statistically significant gender difference was seen in the prevalence of sick leave (57% vs. 50%). Moderate to severe mental fatigue was reported by 78% of women and 68% of men, with women reporting significantly higher fatigue (p < 0.001). Minor gender differences in ADL problems were observed, with more women reporting difficulties in household management, quiet recreation, and social interaction. Both women and men prioritised similar ADL problems, such as paid work, physical activity, social interaction, and fulfilling caregiving roles.ConclusionADL challenges reported by women and men were largely similar and had a significant impact on their daily lives. Identifying key activity limitations is essential to inform effective rehabilitation strategies.Keywords: Long COVID; Persistent COVID-19 symptoms; Gender differences; Activities of daily living; Rehabilitation; Work ability, mental fatigue

Background

On 11 March 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), a pandemic [1]. This global health crisis has affected countless individuals and societies worldwide. Men and women appear to have similar infection rates [2]. While most individuals recover from COVID-19 without requiring medical intervention and return to their daily lives within a few weeks, some report persistent symptoms lasting several months after the acute phase of infection. Symptoms persisting beyond 12 weeks are classified as long COVID and affect approximately 5–10% of all COVID-19 survivors, regardless of initial illness severity [3, 4]. Long COVID predominantly affects otherwise healthy individuals with active lifestyles, including involvement in family, occupational, and social activities [5].

Long COVID affects multiple organ systems [3, 6, 7]. Notably, fatigue and reduced energy levels are particularly disruptive to daily functioning, significantly limiting participation in work and household activities [8–10]. Long COVID often results in full- or part-time sick leave, reduced work capacity, and modifications to work tasks [5, 6, 11]. In a recent study of 448 individuals with long COVID [12], we found that everyday life was substantially affected and 94% required rehabilitation. Mental fatigue was highly prevalent, with 75% reporting severe mental fatigue. Most participants were women (72.5%), and 56% were on sick leave, primarily due to long COVID (96%) [12]. Globally, the female sex is considered a risk factor for long COVID, with more than 60% of cases occurring in women [3, 13, 14]. A systematic review identified being a woman as a risk factor for developing psychiatric and neuropsychiatric complications following COVID-19, including mental fatigue [15]. Gender disparities are evident in the types of daily activities individuals prioritise and undertake, with women and men facing different challenges. A large European study among individuals aged 50 years and older found that women reported more limitations in instrumental ADL than men across all age groups [16]. Similar patterns have been observed across Europe, North America, and Asia, where women were twice as likely as men to report difficulties with instrumental ADL [17]. A recent study on 307 individuals with long COVID similarly found that women were more restricted in activities such as household tasks, caregiving, and community mobility [18]. Gender disparities in daily activities are also reported more broadly, irrespective of health status and functional ability [19, 20]. For instance, men with advanced cancer, brain injury, and elderly men are more likely to prioritise work-related activities, while reporting difficulties with household chores such as cooking and doing laundry. In contrast, women tend to spend more time cooking and report household tasks as less challenging [19–21]. These findings suggest that traditional gender roles and the division of household responsibilities may partially explain differences in activity-related difficulties. The extent of gender-related differences in individuals experiencing long COVID remains insufficiently explored.

Methods

Aim

The aim of the present study was to examine gender differences in sociodemographic characteristics, employment status, sick leave, and mental fatigue. Furthermore, the study aimed to explore self-reported and prioritised ADL problems across genders, and to assess gender differences by comparing ADL patterns between women and men. We hypothesised that women and men would report different challenges in ADL, and that their prioritisation of activities would vary accordingly. We expected differences in ADL problems related to household tasks, caregiving roles, and paid work, primarily reflecting traditional gender roles and stereotypes. Based on this, we also expected a higher proportion of women to be on sick leave.

Study design

The analytical cross-sectional study recruited individuals diagnosed with long COVID, referred from a Danish outpatient clinic for long COVID, at Aarhus University Hospital in the Central Denmark Region, to occupational therapy for clinical evaluation. The recruitment period was between 15 July 2020 and 1 February 2023.

Study population

Individuals were referred to the long COVID outpatient clinic by their general practitioner. Referral criteria included age ≥ 18 years, persistent or newly developed symptoms suggestive of long COVID affecting at least two organ systems for a minimum of three months after the acute COVID-19 phase, and a positive test for SARS-CoV-2 confirmed by a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or an antigen test. In a few cases, where confirmatory testing was not available, long COVID was diagnosed based on medical history, symptoms, and clinical evaluation. Where clinically indicated, individuals were referred to occupational therapy for rehabilitation planning. Referral to occupational therapy was primarily due to mental fatigue and cognitive deficits. Individuals were excluded if language barriers prevented them from completing the study questionnaires or understanding the written and verbal study information in Danish, including the study purpose and participant rights.

Data collection and procedure

Data were collected by occupational therapists at the clinic. Data were collected systematically as part of routine clinical assessment, including patient-reported outcomes and clinically obtained information. Data were managed using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure web-based software platform for data management [22, 23].

Sociodemographic variables, including gender, age, cohabitation status, educational level, employment status, information on sick leave, main symptoms and time of SARS-CoV-2 infection, were self-reported via an electronic questionnaire. The questionnaire included only binary gender response options: woman or man. In this study, the term ‘gender’ is used to refer to these categories, recognising that it may encompass both biological and sociocultural aspects.

Mental fatigue were assessed using the Mental Fatigue Scale (MFS) and the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure (COPM), respectively.

Individuals scheduled for assessment at the occupational therapy clinic received a link to the electronic questionnaires. If the questionnaire was not completed in advance, a paper version was provided for completion before or during the clinic appointment.

The MFS was used to measure the extent of mental fatigue. The scale consists of 15 items assessing cognitive, affective, and sensory symptoms, sleep duration, and diurnal variation in symptom severity [24]. Each item is accompanied by examples of everyday activities, with responses scored from 0 (normal function) to 3 (severe symptoms) [24, 25]. A cut-off score of 10.5 is recommended for identifying clinically significant mental fatigue [24]. The total score reflects the severity of mental fatigue. Scores of 10.5–14 indicate slight mental fatigue, 15–20 moderate mental fatigue, and >20 severe fatigue. The scale has demonstrated good internal consistency and is considered useful for detecting mental fatigue from various aetiologies [24, 26]. A Danish version of the MFS was used at the Post COVID-19 Clinic, although its measurement properties have not been formally evaluated.

The COPM was used to assess the impact of long COVID on the ability to perform ADL and to identify associated difficulties. The COPM is a semi-structured interview that supports healthcare professionals in helping patients identify and prioritise ADL-related problems across the domains of self-care, productivity, and leisure. During the interview, a ten-point visual rating scale is used to rate the importance of each problem, where 1 indicates ‘not important’ and 10 indicates ’extremely important’. Patients are encouraged to select up to five of their most important ADL problems and then rate their performance and satisfaction with performance using a similar ten-point visual rating scale [27, 28]. The Danish version of the 5th edition of the COPM was used by the occupational therapists. The Danish version has demonstrated moderate reliability across various settings [28] and shows acceptable face and content validity [29].

Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA 18 [30]. Categorical data were presented as frequencies (n) and relative frequencies (%). Ordinal scale variables and non-normally distributed continuous variables were depicted as medians and percentiles (25th; 75th). Normally distributed continuous variables were characterised by means and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Gender differences were analysed using the chi-squared test, the Wilcoxon rank sum test, or the unpaired t-test.

The categorisation of ADL problems identified by the patients during the COPM semi-structured interviews was done manually, using the Taxonomic Code for Occupational Performance [29]. To ensure consistent categorisation, examples of common activity problems were provided under each subcategory. The Taxonomic Code for Occupational Performance encompasses diverse categories addressing various activity problems. To enhance our comprehension of the most critical ADL problems, a qualitative deductive content analysis was conducted to develop tertiary categories of the prioritised activities [31]. To inform this tertiary categorisation, the content of the identified ADL problems was examined iteratively to define more specific tertiary categories and better capture the nuances of the patients’ experiences (Table 1). This analysis was performed manually for all participants. When an ADL problem could fit multiple categories, classification was resolved by consensus within the author group. Given the data-driven nature of the tertiary categorisation results are presented descriptively.

Results

Inclusion

As shown in Fig. 1, 1,293 individuals were assessed at the long COVID outpatient clinic during the inclusion period, of whom 1,012 were referred for clinical evaluation by occupational therapists. Of these, 87 patients missed their appointments, and 35 patients were excluded due to language barriers, leaving 890 patients eligible for participation. However, 110 individuals were not included. Sixteen actively declined participation, while the remaining 94 were not included for unknown reasons. Some individuals may have declined participation without this being recorded, and others were excluded due to missing written consent. In total, 780 individuals were included: 567 women (73%) and 213 men (27%).

Sociodemographic, work status, sick leave

Table 2 presents the sociodemographic characteristics of the study population along with data on employment status, sick leave, hospitalisation due to acute COVID-19, self-reported most severe long COVID symptoms, and time since infection. Women had a statistically significantly lower mean age than men (45.6 vs. 49.2 years), and fewer women were living with a spouse (71% vs. 81%). Educational attainment also differed significantly by gender, with a greater proportion of women than men having completed medium- or long-cycle higher education (55% vs. 37%). Conversely, fewer women than men reported no completed education or primary or lower secondary school as their highest level of education (4% vs. 12%). Most participants were either employed (84% of women and 85% of men) or enrolled in education (7% vs. 3%, respectively) prior to SARS-CoV-2 infection. Among these, a high proportion reported being on sick leave, with no statistically significant difference between women and men (57% vs. 50%). A slightly higher proportion of women than men were on full-time sick leave (36 vs. 26%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p 0.135).

Mental fatigue scale

A total of 777 (99.6%) individuals completed the MFS (Table 3). Mental fatigue was reported by both women and men. However, women reported statistically significantly higher mental fatigue scores than men, with a mean score of 19 compared to 17.3 for men. In total, 78% of women and 68% of men reported moderate to severe mental fatigue, and 40% of women experienced severe mental fatigue compared to 25% of men (p < 0.001). Diurnal variation in symptom severity was reported by 65% of women and 54% of men. However, these differences were not statistically significant.

Overall, the prioritised ADL problems were similar across genders. Specific prioritised ADL problems included paid work (66% vs. 68%), physical activity or exercise (47% vs. 45%), and social interaction (60% vs. 53%). Fulfilling caregiving roles seems to be important for both women and men, with 25% vs. 18% of participants prioritising ADL problems in this aspect.

Discussion

Discussion of the results

The aim of this study was to examine gender differences in sociodemographic characteristics, employment status, sick leave, mental fatigue, and activity-related problems in a cohort of individuals with long COVID diagnosed at a long COVID outpatient clinic and examined by occupational therapists at a Danish University Hospital. We hypothesised that women and men would face different challenges in ADL, and that their priorities would differ accordingly. We anticipated differences in reported ADL problems, primarily attributable to traditional gender roles and stereotypes, and consequently expected a higher proportion of women to be on sick leave.

Our main findings suggest only minor gender differences in the types of ADL problems reported and prioritised. Both women and men reported substantial mental fatigue, and women were more prone to experience severe symptoms. Women also had higher educational levels than men, and women were less likely to live with a spouse. Overall, women and men were largely comparable within this cohort of individuals with long COVID.

Looking at the sociodemographic characteristics, a greater proportion of men than women were living with a spouse (82.2% vs. 71.4%). It is widely known that men are less likely than women to seek medical help, a pattern often attributed to traditional gender roles and stereotypes [32, 33]. Having a spouse may provide both emotional and practical support, and spouses often play a role in encouraging their partner to prioritise their health and seek medical help when needed [34]. This may help explain the higher proportion of men than women living with a spouse in our study, as these men represent a group of men who have sought medical help.

Another notable gender difference related to educational attainment: 44% of women had a medium-cycle higher education compared with 25% of men. In the broader population of the Central Denmark Region (ages 25–69), a similar gender difference is evident, with 29% of women and 16% of men holding a medium-cycle higher education [35]. Thus, our findings are consistent with regional patterns, although both women and men with medium-cycle higher education appear to be overrepresented in our study population.

Although women reported statistically significantly higher mental fatigue scores than men, both groups exhibited high mean scores (19 for women and 17 for men), with 20 representing the threshold for severe mental fatigue. A statistically significantly greater proportion of women (40%) than men (25%) experienced severe mental fatigue. Previous studies suggest that women are at greater risk of fatigue and neurological symptoms following COVID-19 infection [15, 36, 37]; however, to our knowledge, sex differences in the severity of fatigue have not previously been reported.

It is well documented that a high proportion of individuals with long COVID are on sick leave. Reported prevalence rates range from 13% to 65%, depending on study context and setting [6, 11, 12, 38, 39]. Our study contributes to this understanding, showing that 57% of women and 51% of men in our cohort were on sick leave due to long COVID at the time of assessment. Similar results were reported by Frisk et al. [39], who found that 63% of individuals with long COVID were on sick leave at baseline, suggesting that our findings are consistent with those from other populations.

Evidence on gender differences in sick leave among individuals with long COVID is sparse, but a recent study by Jakobsen et al. [40] found that being female was associated with an increased risk of long-term sick leave. In our study, a higher proportion of women than men were on sick leave, although the difference was not statistically significant. Higher sick leave rates among women than among men have been observed across countries and health conditions, including in Denmark [41–43]. One possible explanation is persistent gender imbalances in domestic and family responsibilities, which may increase women’s total workload and reduce their ability to remain in paid employment during illness [42, 44, 45]. This may partly explain the observed tendency for more women than men to be on sick leave in our cohort.

In this study, we expected to find gender differences in identified and prioritised ADL problems related to household tasks, caregiving roles, and paid work. However, our findings revealed more similarities than differences, with both men and women reporting substantial ADL problems across all categories. The literature on ADL problems among individuals with long COVID is limited, and few studies have examined gender differences in this context. In accordance with our study, previous studies have indicated that individuals with long COVID frequently experience limitations in ADL [5, 12, 18, 46, 47]. However, a detailed, gender-specific analysis of these ADL problems has, to our knowledge, not been conducted to the same extent as in our study. In our cohort, women reported a statistically significantly higher number of ADL problems than men, although the difference was minor. This finding aligns with a study by Cohen et al. [47] on individuals with self-reported long COVID, and with general population studies [16, 17]. In our cohort, instrumental ADL problems were the most frequently reported, while personal care issues were less common among both women and men. This pattern corresponds with previous findings reported by both Scheel Hincke et al. [16] and Crimmins et al. [17], as also mentioned in the background section.

We found that women were more likely than men to report issues within the category of household management (78% vs. 64%). This aligns with findings by Rosenstein et al. [18], who also reported greater restrictions in household activities among women with long COVID, although their study did not specify which tasks were affected or the underlying reasons. Similarly, Wæhrens et al. [19] examined ADL problems in a cohort of patients with advanced cancer and observed only minor differences. They found that women spent more time cooking, whereas men devoted more time to work. Regarding prioritised activities, the only notable difference was that men were more likely to prioritise home maintenance and furnishings [19]. In the present study, no gender differences were observed in the types of activities prioritised. Further tertiary categorisation revealed no gender-related differences.

In our cohort, women and men experienced the impact of long COVID on their ADL and daily lives in similar ways. This suggests that traditional gender role expectations may not apply to this specific target group, possibly due to their high socioeconomic status and relatively young age. These characteristics may reflect a greater gender equality in this cohort, which could help explain the lack of marked gender differences in reported ADL problems. In dual-earner couples with high levels of education and resources, more egalitarian divisions of labour and responsibilities are often observed [48], supporting the notion that traditional gender roles may exert less influence on ADL experiences in this group. Still, our results reveal important activities prioritised by both women and men, such as paid work, physical activities or exercise, social interaction, and caregiving roles.

Strengths and limitations

The study has both strengths and limitations. Strengths include the high participation rate of 88% and the large sample size of 780 individuals. Also, the high completion rates for the outcome measures (99.7% for the MFS and 97% for the COPM) further strengthen the validity of the findings. Despite being a single-centre study, we consider the participant group largely representative of individuals treated in the long COVID outpatient clinic, given the wide geographic catchment area, which includes both urban and rural areas. However, the study population should be considered a selected sample due to the multi-step referral. Individuals must actively seek help from their general practitioner, who will refer them to the long COVID outpatient clinic and, if necessary, to occupational therapy. This referral pathway implies that participants experienced considerable daily life impairment, with symptoms and functional impairments severe enough to warrant specialised care. The sociodemographic characteristics of our study population also suggest that our cohort is a selected population characterised by higher educational attainment than the general population [35]. This contrasts with findings by Cohen et al. [47], who reported that lower educational levels were associated with long COVID. However, their study recruited participants through a general health questionnaire not specifically targeting long COVID. The higher educational level in our cohort suggests a more resourceful population, with several possible explanations for their inclusion. One explanation may be health inequity, whereby individuals with lower socioeconomic status and education may have reduced health literacy and face greater barriers to seeking care for long COVID [49]. This could suggest that the true prevalence of long COVID may be underestimated in groups with lower socioeconomic status. Another possible explanation is that individuals with higher education are more likely to hold jobs requiring advanced cognitive and mental functioning. In our cohort, mental fatigue was a prominent symptom, potentially impairing such functions. As a result, individuals with higher educational levels may perceive a greater impact from long COVID and be more inclined to seek medical treatment. Furthermore, it is well established that people with higher educational levels are generally more likely to participate in research studies [50, 51], which may further explain the educational profile observed in our cohort. In our study, women constituted 73% of participants, compared with 27% men. This aligns with international and national data, indicating a higher prevalence of long COVID among women (approximately 60%) [13, 14, 52]. The overrepresentation of women in the present study may be attributed to the increased risk of mental fatigue associated with female sex. Mental fatigue and cognitive deficits were the primary reasons for referral to occupational therapy. Although individuals referred to occupational therapy represented 78% of all individuals assessed in the long COVID outpatient clinic, the study’s inclusion process may still limit the generalisability of findings. Another limitation is that gender was recorded as a binary variable (‘woman’ or ‘man’), which may not capture the full spectrum of gender identities, and it is possible that some individuals may have felt compelled to choose either ‘female’ or ‘male’. If some individuals choose not to participate because of this, it could have introduced selection bias. Despite these limitations, we consider our study population a broad representation of individuals seeking help for long COVID.

The COPM was one of the essential outcome measures in this study. Participation rate were high: 98% for women and 94% for men. Almost all individuals completed the COPM, including ratings of both performance and satisfaction related to their reported ADL problems. We consider this a strength of the study. The primary reasons for not administering the COPM were time constraints and patient-related considerations, such as high symptom burden, which could limit participation in the interview. The broad structure of the COPM limited the ability to capture specific ADL problems reported by individuals with long COVID. However, additional tertiary categorisation allowed us to uncover more nuanced and the most important symptoms and aspects of their reported ADL problems. A limitation of this study is that we did not investigate associations between, e.g., sociodemographic factors, symptom severity, or time since infection and prioritised ADL problems or the extent of sick leave (part-time or full-time). The analyses also did not adjust for potential confounders, such as educational level or time since infection. Future studies would benefit from stratified or adjusted analyses to clarify how such factors influence individuals’ prioritisation of activities and the impact of long COVID across genders.

Conclusions

Our study found that the challenges in ADL encountered by women and men were generally very similar in this cohort of individuals with long COVID. Both genders reported high levels of mental fatigue with a substantial impact on their daily lives, as reflected in the reported ADL problems. Contrary to our expectations, we observed no significant differences in ADL problems, nor did we find a higher prevalence of sick leave among women. These findings suggest that traditional gender roles may be less applicable to this specific cohort, thereby challenging our initial assumptions.

This research contributes to a better understanding of key activity limitations experienced by this population, including challenges related to paid work, physical activity and exercise, social interaction, and caregiving roles. Recognising these key activity limitations is essential for developing targeted and effective rehabilitation interventions for this population.
