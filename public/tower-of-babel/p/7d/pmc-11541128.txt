Comparative Study of Preoperative Sagittal Spinal Pelvic Alignment in Patients with Cervical Spondylotic Radiculopathy, Ossification of the Posterior Longitudinal Ligament, and Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy
Authors: Shuo Wang, Suomao Yuan, Ping Liu, Feng Qi, Yonghao Tian, Yuchen Zhang, Changzhen Li, Jiale Li, Xinyu Liu, Lianlei Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCervical spondylosis may lead to changes in the sagittal parameters of the neck and trigger compensatory alterations in systemic sagittal parameters. However, there is currently a dearth of comparative research on the changes and compensatory alterations to sagittal parameters resulting from different types of cervical spondylosis. This study compared the preoperative sagittal alignment sequences among patients with cervical spondylotic radiculopathy (CSR), ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL), and cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) caused by factors resulting from non‐OPLL factors.Materials and MethodsFull length lateral X‐ray of the spine and cervical computed tomography (CT) of 256 patients (134 men, 122 women; mean age, 56.9 ± 9.5 years) were analyzed retrospectively. A total of 4096 radiomics features were measured through the lateral X‐ray by two spinal surgeons with extensive experience. The clinical symptoms measures were the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score, number of hand actions in 10 s, hand‐grip strength, visual analog scale (VAS) score. Normally distributed data was compared using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for parametric variables and χ 2 test were used to analyze the categorical data.ResultsIn the OPLL group, the C2‐C7 Cobb angle was greater than in the CSR and CSM groups (19.8 ± 10.4°, 13.3 ± 10.3°, and 13.9 ± 9.9°, respectively, p < 0.001). Additionally, the C7‐S1 SVA measure was found to be situated in the anterior portion with regards to the CSM and CSR groups (19.7 ± 58.4°, −6.3 ± 34.3° and −26.3 ± 32.9°, p < 0.001). Moreover, the number of individuals with C7‐S1 SVA >50 mm was significantly larger than the CSM group (26/69, 11/83, p < 0.001). In the CSR group, the TPA demonstrated smaller values compared to the OPLL group (8.8 ± 8.5°, 12.7 ± 10.2°, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the SSA was comparatively smaller as opposed to both the OPLL and CSM groups (49.6 ± 11.2°, 54.2 ± 10.8° and 54.3 ± 9.3°, p < 0.05).ConclusionPatients with OPLL exhibit greater cervical lordosis than those with CSR and CSM. However, OPLL is more likely to result in spinal imbalance when compared to the CSM group. Furthermore, OPLL and CSM patients exhibit anterior trunk inclination and worse global spine sagittal parameters in comparison to CSR patients.Keywords: Cervical Spondylosis, Sagittal Position Parameters, Spine, X‐ray

Introduction

Cervical spondylotic radiculopathy (CSR), ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL), and cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) are compression lesions caused by degenerative spondylotic changes. 1 CSR is due to nerve root compression and inflammation in or near the cervical neural foramen. OPLL and CSM result from spinal cord compression caused by ossification or intervertebral disc herniation. 2 , 3

Sagittal spinal pelvic alignment is crucial in the development of spinal deformities and degenerative diseases, as well as in preoperative planning, postoperative clinical care, and functional rehabilitation. 4 , 5 An accurate cervical lordosis is crucial for shock absorption, balancing structural stresses, maintaining biomechanical balance, and preventing nerve root and spinal cord compression. 6 Age‐related cervical degeneration can lead to changes in sagittal cervical alignment, loss of cervical lordosis, straightening of physiological curvature, and potentially kyphotic changes. 7 , 8 It was confirmed that there were compensatory changes in the adjacent and distal segments in adults with alteration of cervical alignment. 9 Paying attention to the patient's preoperative sagittal alignment, especially the reciprocal compensatory mechanisms in cervical alignment, is necessary to establish appropriate surgical indications, guide postoperative rehabilitation exercises, and improve sagittal alignment. We found that few studies have focused on compensatory changes in sagittal spinal‐pelvic alignment in patients with cervical spine degeneration. The aim of this study is to investigate the following three aspects: (i) comparison of preoperative cervical sagittal parameters and clinical symptoms among patients with CSR, OPLL, and CSM; (ii) comparison of preoperative sagittal spinal pelvic alignment compensation among the three groups of patients; (iii) providing a more theoretical basis for preoperative planning and postoperative rehabilitation guidance for patients with the three types of cervical spondylosis.

Materials and Methods

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Institutional review board approval was obtained before commencing this study, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment (KYLL‐2021(KS)‐055).

Inclusion criteria: (i) ineffective conservative treatment for a duration of 3 months; (ii) confirmation of CSR, OPLL, or CSM through computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging.

Exclusion criteria: (i) history of prior cervical surgery; (ii) presence of tumors, trauma, infection, or rheumatoid arthritis; (iii) incomplete radiological data.

General Patient Information

In total, this study comprised 256 patients with degenerative CSR, OPLL, or CSM who were enrolled between September 2013 and July 2023. Among them, the CSR group consisted of 104 cases, comprising 53 males and 51 females and the mean age of the group was 55.4 ± 8.6 years. The OPLL group comprised 69 cases, including 38 males and 31 females, with an average age of 58.4 ± 9.6 years. The CSM group consisted of 83 cases, comprising 43 males and 40 females, with an average age of 57.6 ± 10.4 years.

Research Methods and Evaluation Indices

Evaluation of Clinical Symptoms

The neurological function of patients within each group was assessed prior to surgery using the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score, hand‐grip strength test, and the number of hand actions within a 10 s window. Visual analog scale (VAS) score was also used to assess neck and shoulder pain.

For the radiographic evaluation, assessment was conducted independently by two experienced orthopaedic spine surgeons who were blinded to patient data. Any differences in assessments were resolved through discussion and consensus. Classification reproducibility was evaluated by determining intra‐ and interobserver correlation coefficients.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS 20.0 statistical software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically and p‐value <0.001 was considered statistically significant. Normally distributed data was compared using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for parametric variables and χ 2 test were used to analyze the categorical data.

There were 124 cases with lordotic type, 86 with straight type, 27 with sigmoid type, and 19 with kyphotic type. Comparison of Preoperative Sagittal Sequence Parameters among CSR, OPLL, and CSM Groups

For the preoperative sagittal spinal‐pelvic alignment, the OPLL group exhibited a C2‐C7 Cobb angle of 19.8 ± 10.4°, which was greater than those in the CSR and CSM groups (13.3 ± 10.3° and 13.9 ± 9.9°) (p < 0.001). Additionally, the C2‐4 Cobb angle in the OPLL group was 10.3 ± 7.5°, which was also greater than in the CSM group (7.4 ± 5.6°) (p < 0.05). Furthermore, T1 Slope and PI‐LL resulted in measurements of 27.1 ± 8.4° and 3.1 ± 13.4°, respectively, both exceeding those obtained from the CSR group (22.7 ± 8.1° and −5.2 ± 13.9°) (p < 0.001). Lastly, the LL measurement was taken at 49.7 ± 14.6°, proving to be lesser than that of the CSR group (55.1 ± 13.4°) (p < 0.05). The C7‐S1 SVA in the OPLL group was 19.7 ± 58.4°, located anterior to the posterosuperior corner of S1. In contrast, the CSR and CSM groups showed of −26.3 ± 32.9° and −6.3 ± 34.3°, respectively, located posterior to the posterosuperior corner of S1. The three groups exhibited significant differences (p < 0.001). Simultaneously, we discovered that the number of individuals with C7‐S1 SVA >50 mm in the three groups were 25, 26, and 11, respectively. Notably, there was a significant difference between the OPLL group (26 cases) and the CSM group (11 cases) (Table 1). The CSR group exhibited a TPA of 8.8 ± 8.5°, which was smaller than those in the OPLL group (12.7 ± 10.2°) (p < 0.001). Additionally, the SSA in the CSR group was 49.6 ± 11.2°, which was smaller than in the OPLL and CSM groups (54.2 ± 10.8° and 54.3 ± 9.3°) (p < 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

The management of cervical spine disease through surgery presents significant challenges due to its complex structure, and a comprehensive understanding of cervical alignment and its effect on spinal‐pelvic alignment is essential to deal with this challenge. 11 An imbalance in cervical sagittal alignment can result in neck pain, neurological deficit or functional disability, and a lower quality of life. 12 , 13 Additionally, this imbalance induces compensatory alteration in the sagittal spino‐pelvic alignment. 14 Examining the preoperatively sagittal alignment of patients can help assess disease severity, formulate an appropriate preoperative plan, predict prognosis, and guide postoperative rehabilitation exercises. This study represents the first comparison of preoperative cervical sagittal parameters, clinical symptoms, and spinal pelvic sagittal compensation among three types of cervical spondylosis patients. The study revealed that OPLL patients exhibited a greater cervical anterior convexity angle than CSR and CSM patients, potentially providing a degree of cervical protection, but they are more susceptible to spinal imbalance than CSM patients. Additionally, this study revealed that, in comparison to CSR patients, OPLL and CSM patients exhibit forward trunk tilt and worse spinal sagittal position parameters.

Comparison of Preoperative Cervical Sagittal Position Parameters among CSR, OPLL, and CSR Groups

Sagittal alignment alters with age in the general population. Machino et al. 15 discovered that the sagittal sequence of the spine changes progressively with age by segregating populations into various age categories. When degenerative lesions arise, their sagittal sequence is more susceptible to alteration or even causing imbalance in contrast to typical individuals. Xing et al. 16 discovered a reduction in C2‐C7 lordosis and the T1 slope among patients with cervical degenerative conditions. Li et al. 17 discovered by comparative analysis of CSM and OPLL patients that OPLL patients have a milder degree of degeneration and a better cervical lordosis angle to compensate for spinal cord compression caused by bone compression. In this study, the OPLL C2‐7 Cobb angle was 19.7 ± 10.7°, which was larger to the CSM group (13.9 ± 9.9°) (p < 0.001); similarly, the C2‐4 Cobb angle was 10.3 ± 7.7°, which was larger than the CSM group (7.4 ± 5.6°) (p < 0.05). This is in agreement with previous studies, indicating that OPLL patients have a high spinal canal occupancy rate due to good anterior convex angle compensation ossification, which also provides a theoretical basis for OPLL patients to choose posterior surgery for decompression treatment. Meanwhile, the C2‐7 Cobb angle in the CSR group was 13.3 ± 10.4°, which was smaller than that in the OPLL group (19.7 ± 10.7°) (p < 0.001). This may be a contributing factor to the pain and exacerbation experienced by CSR patients: cervical lordosis reduction results in increased disc load and excessive fatigue of the neck muscles, which aggravates neck pain symptoms and impairs cervical spine function. Simultaneously, the heightened load on the intervertebral disc will hasten its degeneration, resulting in a reduction of the space occupied by the vertebral body and a decrease in the upper and lower diameters of the intervertebral foramen. When the intervertebral disc becomes degenerated and loses its supporting force, the upper vertebral body's lower joint can follow the slope by sliding backward and shifting. This results in the superior articular process of the lower vertebral body protruding into the intervertebral foramen, ultimately leading to narrowing of its anteroposterior diameter. The percentage of cervical lordosis in the OPLL and CSM groups (60.9% and 51.2%) is higher than that in the CSR group (37.9%). Although the difference is not significant, it still indicates that measures should be taken to restore cervical lordosis in individuals with a disappearance of cervical lordosis to avoid potential nerve root compression symptoms in the future.

The findings of Kim et al. 18 indicated that patients with higher preoperative T1 slope had more lordotic preoperative cervical alignment and more kyphotic alignment changes after laminoplasty. Liu et al. 19 observed a positive correlation between C2‐7 SVA and T1 slope in a study comparing patients with neck pain, CSR, and CSM. Kim et al. 20 verified that patients with a high T1 slope exhibited a smaller increase in the JOA score, indicating poorer neurological function recovery. In the OPLL group, the T1 Slope was 27.1 ± 9.3°, which was larger than that of the CSR group (22.7 ± 8.2°) (p < 0.05). As previously stated, the C2‐7 Cobb in the OPLL group with a greater T1 slope was also greater than that in the CSR group (19.7 ± 10.7 and 13.3 ± 10.4, p < 0.001). Additionally, the C2‐7 SVA in the OPLL group was greater than that in the CSR group, although there was no significant difference (20.8 ± 13.9 and 17.7 ± 11.5, p > 0.05). These two outcomes are consistent with those of previous studies. However, it is important to consider the possibility of a reduction in lordosis or cervical kyphosis and the challenges associated with the recovery of neurological function after laminoplasty in OPLL patients, due to the significant lordotic and high T1 slope in the cervical spine.

Comparison of the Compensation for Preoperative Sagittal Spinal Pelvic Parameters Caused by Changes in Cervical Sagittal Parameters among CSR, OPLL, and CSR Groups

Changes in cervical sagittal position parameters can also affect spinal‐pelvic parameters. Yoshida et al. 21 conducted a prospective radiometric analysis of patients with CSM and discovered that cervical sagittal abnormalities possess the capacity to alter the pelvis position and affect spinal balance. Truumes et al. 22 compared CSM and CSR and found that CSR patients usually only have symptoms caused by nerve compression, whereas CSM patients often have gait changes. Le Huec et al. 23 examined the compensation and balance of sagittal position and concluded that reducing LL is necessary to offset spinal anterior tilt caused by degeneration. The LL values in the OPLL group was 49.7 ± 14.6°, which was smaller than in the CSR group (55.1 ± 13.4°) (p < 0.05). This suggests that patients with OPLL exhibit a forward‐leaning posture in comparison to those with CSR resulting in a lowered LL to regulate their center of gravity; the body compensates for forward tilt by the mechanism of backward pelvic tilt, which can cause knee flexion when it reaches its limit.

Yuk et al. 24 proposed that normative values of SVA have been established as being less than 50 mm. Protopsaltis et al. 25 classified TPA and found that their angle changes were significantly correlated with SVA, PT, and PI‐LL. Roussouly et al. 26 suggest that kyphosis severity increases as SSA decreases. Patients diagnosed with OPLL and CSM display a more anterior curvature of the spine in comparison to individuals with CSR. Additionally, OPLL patients exhibit a larger cervical lordosis angle when compared to CSM patients. This results in a significant difference in SVA of 19.7 ± 58.4 mm, −6.3 ± 34.3 mm, and −26.3 ± 32.9 mm. Meanwhile, among patients with SVA >50 mm, the OPLL group (26 cases) had a significantly higher proportion compared to the CSM group (11 cases), indicating a higher likelihood of preoperative overall sagittal imbalance caused by OPLL. It is worth noting that among the 26 patients with OPLL, seven had SVA >100 mm, which was not observed in the other two groups. This indicates that OPLL causes progressive and severe spinal imbalance. Further follow‐up is required to determine which surgical method is more effective in restoring balance, especially for patients with combined OPLL and spinal imbalance. It also reminds us that for patients undergoing lumbar spine surgery, it is important to determine whether the imbalance is caused by lumbar spine disease or a combined cervical spine disease. At the similar time, the forward leaning posture also resulted in a larger PI‐LL and TPA in the OPLL group compared to the CSR group (3.1 ± 13.4° and 12.7 ± 10.2°, −5.2 ± 13.9° and 8.8 ± 8.5°) (p < 0.05). These three parameters align with the research of Protopsaltis. 25 Finally, the SSA values of 54.2 ± 10.8° and 54.3 ± 9.3° in the OPLL and CSM groups were higher than those in the CSR group (49.6 ± 11.2°) (p < 0.05); this is consistent with the cervical classification in this study.

Comparison of Preoperative Sagittal Position Parameters in Patients with Cervical Lordosis Subtype among CSR, OPLL, and CSR Groups

In the lordotic type of cervical spinal, the C2‐7 SVA was found to be significantly smaller in the CSR group (17.0 ± 11.7 mm) when compared to the OPLL group (24.6 ± 14.6 mm). The importance of C2‐C7 SVA as a predictive parameter for clinical efficacy has been reported by previous studies. 23 , 24 In a recent study, Thomas et al. 27 examined the relationship between cervical sagittal parameters and neck muscles. Their findings indicated that a greater C2‐7 SVA was significantly associated with a greater percentage of fat infiltration of the anterior group. The study conducted by Neda et al. 28 demonstrated a positive correlation between a reduction in fat infiltration and the efficacy of postoperative outcomes. In patients with anterior type OPLL, the C2‐7 SVA is larger. In the OPLL group, patients with C2‐7 SVA >40 mm are all of the lordotic type, and their postoperative recovery may be relatively poor. Consequently, it is of paramount importance to measure C2‐7 SVA prior to surgery, particularly in patients presenting with anterior convexity OPLL. It is of paramount importance to select an appropriate surgical method, to minimize damage to the cervical muscle group during the operation, and to reinforce neck muscle exercise in the postoperative period in order to achieve optimal surgical outcomes.

Limitations and Prospect

It is also worth noting that the study has certain limitations. Due to the relatively small sample size, the number of patients with straight, sigmoid, and kyphotic types is insufficient to permit the identification of differences between the three groups. Additionally, this study only collected and compared preoperative spinal and pelvic sagittal parameters and clinical symptom evaluations of patients, without any postoperative changes or comparisons. In the future, follow‐up will be conducted to collect changes in sagittal parameters and clinical symptom improvements of various types of patients. In‐depth research will also be conducted on the spinal and pelvic sagittal parameters of three types of cervical spondylosis.

Conclusion

OPLL patients exhibit better cervical lordosis when compared to CSR and CSM patients. OPLL is more likely to cause spinal imbalance when compared to CSM patients. In comparison to the CSR group, OPLL and CSM groups display anterior trunk inclination and worse global spine sagittal parameters.
