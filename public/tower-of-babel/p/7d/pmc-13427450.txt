Safety and efficacy of decompressed vertebroplasty in reducing pulmonary fat embolism: a proof-of-mechanism study in a sheep model
Authors: William Chu, Yu-Chuan Tsuei, Shih-Hao Cheng, Hsin-Tzu Lu, An-Shun Liu, Yung-Fu Hsu, Yin-Jiun Tseng, Hanna S Yuan, Chuan Li, Woei-Chyn Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercutaneous vertebroplasty (PV) is a minimally invasive procedure for vertebral compression fractures. However, elevated intravertebral pressure during bone cement injection may increase the risk of pulmonary fat embolism (PFE). To address this issue, a decompressed vertebroplasty technique is introduced by incorporating a contralateral aspiration to establish a pressure gradient inside the vertebral body to guide cement flow. Such a mechanism can facilitate the removal of fat droplets. This study, therefore, assesses the effect of decompression PV on pulmonary fat droplet burden to evaluate its preliminary proof-of-mechanism potential.MethodsEight skeletally mature male Corriedale sheep (Ovis aries) were randomly allocated into 2 experimental groups (n = 4 per group). Bilateral PV was performed at 3 nonadjacent lumbar vertebral levels in each animal. Group A (control) received conventional PV procedures with 0.8 mL of polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) cement injected per vertebral body. Group B (treatment) underwent decompressed vertebroplasty, consisting of simultaneous 1.2 mL PMMA injection through the primary trocar while maintaining continuous −600 mmHg suction via the contralateral access cannula. Following a 7-day postoperative observation period, all subjects were euthanized, spinal cord and lung tissues were harvested. Spinal nerve sections were stained with Luxol Fast Blue (LFB) and Fluorojade C (FJC) for nerve damage assessment, while lung tissues were stained with osmium tetroxide (OsO₄) to detect fat droplets.ResultsCT analysis demonstrated that lung tissues in group B had significantly fewer OsO₄-positive fat droplets than those in group A (p = .034). This difference occurred despite comparable cement distribution between groups (Group A: 0.59 ± 0.33 mL, Group B: 0.71 ± 0.45 mL, p = .42) and no significant difference in cement extravasation rates. Histopathological evaluation revealed no spinal cord damage.ConclusionsIn a healthy ovine model, decompressed vertebroplasty demonstrated preliminary proof-of-mechanism potential in reducing histological pulmonary fat droplet burden under the applied negative pressure gradient. However, further studies are required to determine whether these surrogate findings translate into a reduction in clinically meaningful embolic events or confirmed procedural safety in a clinical setting.Keywords: Osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture, Percutaneous vertebroplasty, Intravertebral decompression, Surgical safety, Pulmonary fat embolism

Introduction

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs), affecting approximately 50% of individuals over 80 in the US [1], result in chronic pain, kyphosis, and functional limitations. While initial conservative management (eg, bed rest, analgesics, bracing) may sometimes prove insufficient [2,3] for a subset of patients, percutaneous vertebroplasty (PV), involving polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) injection to restore vertebral integrity and reduce pain [[4], [5], [6]], is a standard intervention. PV offers rapid analgesia, improved mobility, and enhanced quality of life, especially for elderly or comorbid patients unsuitable for surgery [4,7,8], with a minimally invasive nature, reducing recovery time and complications like deep vein thrombosis and pneumonia [9].

Despite its advantages, PV poses significant risks [10], notably cement leakage and embolic complications [7,11,12]. Cement leakage, observed in 56%–87.5% of procedures [[13], [14], [15]], is often asymptomatic but can lead to serious outcomes, particularly in patients with cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction [16,17]. Fat and cement embolism have gained attention for their cardiopulmonary risks. Cement embolism, potentially fatal, may occur via venous plexus entry, cortical breaches, or fracture clefts [15,18,19]. Fat embolism arises when marrow fat enters the venous circulation during vertebral manipulation, causing pulmonary obstruction and systemic inflammation [20,21]. Fat embolism syndrome has been reported with a mortality rate of 30.2% [22]. While typically linked to long bone trauma [23], fat embolism also occurs in spinal procedures [22,24,25], contributing to postoperative respiratory issues. The incidence of subclinical fat embolism during PV is reported to be as high as 6.8%–10%, with high-risk groups including those with multiple-level fractures or pre-existing pulmonary compromise [26]. Prevention focuses on techniques that reduce intravertebral pressure and marrow disruption [27,28]. The incidence of pulmonary embolism after kyphoplasty ranges from 2.1% to 26% [29]. Vertebral augmentation procedures have also been associated with the occurrence of fat embolism, with heightened risk observed in vulnerable populations, including elderly individuals with obesity, osteoporosis, cardiovascular comorbidities, or prolonged immobilization [30]. Managing fat embolism risk is crucial in vulnerable populations, highlighting the need for safer vertebral augmentation methods.

Clinically, PV using an aspiration technique can be performed through either a unilateral [31,32] or bilateral approach [33,34]. Prior to cement injection, the procedure typically involves vacuum suction and often incorporates vertebral lavage techniques [35,36]. In contrast, decompressed vertebroplasty [37,38] involves simultaneous cement injection on 1 side and suction on the other, requiring bilateral cannula access and the establishment of a continuous intravertebral channel to ensure proper flow communication. Accordingly, to investigate decompressed vertebroplasty techniques further, in vivo studies using large animal models—particularly sheep, due to their anatomical and biomechanical similarity to humans [39,40]—are essential. Established sheep PV protocols [41,42] support consistent procedures and comprehensive evaluation of safety, cement spread, and complications.

In this study, we demonstrate that the decompressed vertebroplasty technique provides a promising treatment approach while also reducing histological fat droplet migration. Using a sheep model, we specifically evaluated the technique's potential mechanistic effect in mitigating pulmonary fat droplet deposition, hypothesizing that controlled intravertebral pressure modulation could help limit fat droplet migration into the pulmonary vasculature.

Material and method

Study subject

Eight skeletally mature male Corriedale sheep (Ovis aries; age: 29 ± 2 months; weight: 41.0 ± 3.21 kg) were acquired from Qingjing Farm, Taiwan, ROC. Upon arrival, animals were group-housed in a spacious enclosure with a 2-tier wire-mesh floor, stainless steel feeders, and water troughs. Following a 26–33-day quarantine under veterinary supervision, sheep were maintained in a controlled environment (16°C–27°C, 30°C–70% humidity; 12-hour light/dark) with ad libitum water and a standardized diet (alfalfa/oat hay plus Laboratory Rabbit Diet HF 5326 at 3% body weight). Eligible sheep (36.0–47.0 kg at surgery) underwent procedures at the Taiwan National Center for Biomodels, an AAALACi-accredited, OECD GLP-compliant facility. Weekly body condition was logged in “Animal Medical Records” (TNSY14-01). Study protocols were approved by the IACUC (NLAC(TN)-111-M-008-R1).

Surgical procedures

All animals underwent standardized anesthetic management prior to surgery, beginning with subcutaneous premedication (metoclopramide 0.4 mg/kg and meloxicam 0.5 mg/kg), followed by intravenous induction with diazepam (0.3 mg/kg) and ketamine (4 mg/kg). Anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane (0.2%–6.0%) via endotracheal intubation, with continuous monitoring of hemodynamic parameters (heart rate, blood pressure, SpO₂), respiratory function (end-tidal CO₂, fractional-inspired CO₂), and core temperature. Intraoperative analgesia was provided by fentanyl infusion (1–30 µg/kg/min) at the attending veterinarian’s discretion. Fluoroscopic guidance (Shimadzu OPESCOPE ACTENO C-arm) was used throughout the procedure, including cement deposition, and real-time detection of any extravasation. Wounds were closed with 2-0 nylon sutures using standard aseptic techniques. All procedures were performed in a standardized NCB operating room between 10:00 AM and 4:00 PM, with 1 control and 1 treatment animal randomly selected each day.

Sheep underwent vertebroplasty in a prone position on a radiolucent table following aseptic preparation with beta-iodine. Bilateral 8-gauge trocars were inserted into 3 randomly selected vertebrae between L3 and L7 via a standardized pedicular approach [28,41]. As shown in Fig. 1, animals were randomly allocated without stratification to either: (1) Control group (Group A, n = 4) receiving bilateral cement injection (0.4 mL per side of Howmedica Simplex bone cement containing barium contrast); or (2) Treatment group (Group B, n = 4) undergoing unilateral cement injection (1.2 mL) with simultaneous contralateral suction (−600 mmHg) maintained via a regulated vacuum system with real-time pressure monitoring. The higher cement volume in the treatment group was selected because contralateral decompression was expected to create additional intravertebral capacity and facilitate cement distribution under fluoroscopic guidance while avoiding cement leakage or over-pressurization. The −600 mmHg decompression pressure was selected based on preliminary feasibility testing and the operational range of standard clinical suction systems, with the aim of generating a stable and reproducible decompression gradient during cement injection. Before cement injection, a 15 mL saline lavage combined with aspiration was performed to confirm bilateral patency.

Schematic of the decompressed vertebroplasty experimental design in an in vivo sheep model: (A) bilateral trocar placement in 3 nonadjacent lumbar vertebrae (L3–L7) per animal, (B) control group with bilateral cement injection (0.4 mL/side), and (C) treatment group with unilateral cement injection (1.2 mL) and contralateral suction (−600 mmHg) for controlled pressure differentials. Fluoroscopic guidance ensured precise targeting throughout.

This exploratory study adhered to ISO 10993-6 and employed a repeated-measures design, analyzing 3 vertebrae per animal to enhance statistical power through increased within-subject comparisons. The sample size and design were informed by prior validated ovine studies [43], demonstrating statistically significant physiological and histological differences using a similar framework, thereby optimizing sensitivity while minimizing animal use in line with ethical and regulatory principles. To account for intra-animal correlation arising from the use of multiple vertebrae (L3–L7) per sheep, statistical analysis was conducted using a Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE), with significance set at p < .05.

Postoperative care

After surgery, animals were individually housed for a 7-day observation period under veterinary supervision prior to euthanasia and tissue harvest, ensuring adequate recovery while minimizing stress interactions that could confound histological outcomes. During this period, all animals received subcutaneous administration of enrofloxacin (5 mg/kg) and meloxicam (0.5 mg/kg) once daily for 3 consecutive days. In addition, daily veterinary assessments were performed to monitor for complications, including signs of neurological impairment, respiratory distress, or wound infection. Locomotor activity was monitored daily from admission to the day of termination and documented using the Tarlov scoring system.

On day 8 (D8), euthanasia was performed via intravenous potassium chloride (20 mEq/kg) under deep anesthesia, confirmed by absence of corneal reflex and pedal withdrawal, followed by spinal cord and lung tissues harvest for histopathological analysis.

Quantitative evaluation

The primary outcomes of this study were: (1) evaluation of pulmonary fat droplet deposition through histological staining of lipid droplets in the lungs, and (2) assessment of procedural and device safety, including analysis of cement leakage and potential tissue or spinal cord injury via histopathological staining.

CT image analysis

Cement distribution was quantified using Dragonfly software (v2022.1, ORS Inc.) on postprocedural CT scans. Polymethylmethacrylate cement (>900 HU) and trabecular bone (<900 HU) were differentiated. Automated segmentation excluded cortical bone to isolate cement volumes, and cement extravasation was calculated as the ratio of extravertebral to total cement volume.

The scoring of white and gray matter reactivity (on a 0–15 scale) was evaluated according to Table E4 of the ISO 10993-6:2016 standard. Results

All subjects survived the 7-day observation without paraplegia or complications requiring early euthanasia. On necropsy day (D8), body weights ranged from 35.5 kg to 47.5 kg (41.4 ± 4.45 kg), with no significant difference from preoperative values. Hematology and biochemistry showed lower hemoglobin and hematocrit on D8 in animals S02, S03, S06, and S08, indicating incomplete recovery, with no other significant changes in CBC or biochemistry.

Cement distribution analysis

Quantitative CT analysis revealed comparable cement volumes between groups, with control animals receiving 0.59 ± 0.33 mL per vertebra versus 0.71 ± 0.45 mL in the treatment group (p > .05). Cement extravasation rates were similarly nonsignificant (control: 6.8% vs. treatment: 8.1%, p > .05), indicating the decompression technique did not increase leakage risk.

Histopathological findings

Pulmonary fat droplet deposition

OsO₄ staining demonstrated significantly reduced histological fat droplet deposition in treatment animals (Table 1). Control specimens exhibited abundant black particulate deposits along alveolar walls and pulmonary vasculature (Fig. 2A), whereas treatment group lungs showed sparse fat droplet presence. Quantitative analysis confirmed this reduction across all pulmonary lobes (RUL, RML, RLL, LUL, LLL), with the exception of 1 treatment-group outlier (S07) that displayed values within control ranges.

Spinal histopathology

Histological assessment of spinal cord segments (Fig. 2B and C) revealed minimal tissue reactivity, meeting ISO 10993-6:2016 “no reaction” criteria. Both groups exhibited mild axonal dystrophy, likely due to trocar insertion rather than the decompression technique. Luxol Fast Blue and Fluorojade C staining confirmed preserved myelin and neuronal integrity. No inflammatory infiltrates, hemorrhage, necrosis, or infections were observed. Histopathological comparison of white and gray matter (Table 3) revealed no significant differences between groups (white matter pathology, p = .4377), indicating that decompressed vertebroplasty does not appear to compromise spinal cord integrity under the conditions evaluated. Observed changes reflect expected surgical effects rather than apparent technique-related complications (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, all procedures were performed under standardized surgical protocols with veterinary oversight, and postoperative care was optimized to minimize pain and distress. Each sheep underwent 3-level vertebral interventions with paired group comparisons conducted on the same day to minimize animal usage. All animals survived the 7-day postoperative period without major complications, indicating good tolerance and minimal physiological burden.

In the Treatment group, a higher volume of cement (1.2 mL) was injected unilaterally because contralateral decompression was expected to create additional intravertebral capacity and facilitate cement distribution. Despite the 50% increase in cement volume compared to the control group (0.8 mL), the suction was associated with a significant reduction in histological pulmonary fat droplet burden. This supports the hypothesis that modulation of intravertebral pressure and removal of marrow fat may help reduce migration of fat droplets into the pulmonary circulation. The mechanical theory suggests that trauma or bone fractures disrupt bone marrow fat and rupture intramedullary vessels. In the absence of adequate decompression, cement injection may induce a sharp pressure spike, potentially driving fat or marrow elements into the vasculature [44,45]. Aspiration combined with lavage has been reported to reduce intravertebral resistance and injection forces [35,36], which in turn may lower the risk of fat embolism, without inducing any cardiovascular responses during or after the procedure [33,46]. Additionally, 2-way ANOVA demonstrated no significant difference in fat droplet counts among lung lobes, indicating uniform distribution and supporting the validity of total droplet counts as a robust endpoint, minimizing bias from lobar anatomical variability.

With respect to cement leakage, no significant difference in incidence was observed between groups, suggesting that our approach was not associated with an observed increase in leakage despite the higher volume of injected cement. Cement extravasation is influenced by factors such as impairment of bone mineral density, cortical disruption, grade of vertebral compression, and surgical approach employed [47]. However, the use of healthy sheep vertebrae may not fully replicate the compromised conditions of osteoporotic fractures, potentially underestimating clinical leakage rates. Moreover, clinical studies have reported that applying aspiration techniques before cement injection and during the procedure can help reduce cement leakage rates [37,38] and improve the distribution of cement fill [37,48].

In terms of tissue and spinal cord safety, our approach did not alter the core PV technique and was applied externally. Consequently, decompressed vertebroplasty was not associated with procedural complications. While 4 animals exhibited decreases in hemoglobin and hematocrit, the incidence was evenly distributed across groups (2/4 in each), consistent with the physiological demands of 3-level interventions. One study [49] reported an average hidden perioperative blood loss of 293.82 mL, far exceeding surgical blood loss (11.89 mL) during PV, but without major complications. Lavage and suction during PVP [35] have been associated with up to 80 mL of additional blood loss per level, although no cases of postoperative anemia were reported. While this technique may theoretically increase bleeding risk, particularly in multi-level procedures, it warrants further investigation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study utilized a healthy, nonfractured sheep model, which limits direct clinical translatability to geriatric patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures. In clinical settings, such patients already present with trauma-related marrow disruption and disrupted intramedullary vessels, which may alter pressure dynamics, cement dispersion, and embolism risk [42,43]. Second, it remains unclear whether the observed reduction in OsO₄-stained pulmonary fat droplets directly correlates with clinically meaningful pulmonary embolic events, as these histological findings serve as surrogate endpoints. Third, while the establishment of a negative pressure gradient was verified by monitoring the suction-line vacuum, intravertebral pressure within the trabecular bone marrow cavity was not universally monitored across both groups during cement injection. Therefore, the absolute intravertebral pressure profiles and the specific mitigation of pressure surges should be interpreted cautiously when comparing groups. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as preliminary preclinical proof-of-mechanism evidence rather than confirmation of clinical embolic risk reduction or procedural safety. The observed reduction of embolic load in this high-resistance model suggests that the technique warrants further validation to better determine its clinical translational potential.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that maintaining a continuous differential pressure via a contralateral trocar may establish an intravertebral pressure gradient that could help limit histological fat droplet mobilization into the pulmonary circulation. Histopathological analysis revealed substantially fewer OsO₄-positive fat droplets, with no observed increase in cement leakage or adverse spinal reactions. These findings support further investigation of decompressed vertebroplasty in fractured or osteoporotic models and clinical studies evaluating embolic outcomes and procedural performance.
