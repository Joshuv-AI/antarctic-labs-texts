Geological Storage Potential and Stability of CO2 in Abandoned Deep Coal Seams: A Case Study of the Wanglou Mine, China
Authors: Lvhan Yang, Zeming Shi, Meng Wu, Xinyue Liao, Rui Liu, Xiaoping Zhou, Fei Shen, Zhiqiang Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study assesses the CO2 storage stability and potential of M3 and M4 deep coal seams in the Wanglou closed coal mine in northern Jiangsu, China. Based on eight primary indicators (coal seam cover performance, pressure-to-temperature ratio, depth-to-thickness ratio, groundwater pH value, structural complexity, permeability, degree of collapse in mined-out areas, and other geological factors) and two secondary indicators (fault intensity and density), we applied fuzzy comprehensive evaluation and theoretical modeling. Key findings indicate that the stability scores of the M3 and M4 seams are 86.621 and 89.268, respectively, both classified as “relatively stable.” The theoretical CO2 storage capacities are 80.842 and 65.439 Mt. Nonminable areas are categorized into favorable, moderately favorable, and unfavorable zones. These outcomes offer crucial theoretical support for site selection in deep coal seam CO2 geological storage projects, facilitating the green transition of the coal industry by efficiently utilizing abandoned resources.

Introduction

Global warming has led to accelerated glacier melting, rising sea levels, increased ultraviolet radiation, rampant infectious diseases, and frequent extreme weather events; which have seriously affected the natural ecological balance and human survival and development. Continuous and substantial emissions of CO2 are the primary contributing factor to global warming. In recent years, some coal mines in China have closed due to the depletion of medium and shallow coal resources or policies aimed at reducing capacity, leaving behind a large amount of unmined deep coal resources. , Study found that the coal seam’s capacity to adsorb CO2 is 2–3 times greater than its capacity to adsorb CH4 and a significant amount of CH4 can be displaced from the coal seam after CO2 injection into deep coal seams, which offers dual benefits of economic and environmental nature. − After initiating pilot projects for carbon dioxide-enhanced coalbed methane recovery (CO2-ECBM), the United States, Canada, China, and other countries have consistently found this technology to be viable. − Deep coal seams are located in a complex geological environment characterized by high geothermal temperatures, high stress, high reservoir pressure, and low permeability, while the injected CO2 is in a supercritical state (pressure above 7.38 MPa and temperature above 304.13 K). , Consequently, effective assessment of the CO2 storage capacity and stability in deep coal seams is crucial for this technology. , Some studies have not conducted case analyses, − or have performed case analyses but have not further classified the sealing area into grades, , or have not introduced the ″one-vote veto″ system based on faults, particularly positive faults with significant displacement (the main factor affecting sealing stability), into the classification of grades. Therefore, there is an urgent need to establish an assessment system specifically targeting the structural characteristics of abandoned deep coal seams and their multifactor coupled stability. −

In order to provide theoretical support for CO2 geological storage projects in abandoned deep coal seams, this Review examines the coal-rock and coal quality features of deep coal seams M3 and M4 within the Lower Shihezi Formation in the Wanglou mining area of Tongshan County, Northern Jiangsu Province, China. We propose an fuzzy comprehensive evaluation model incorporating eight primary indicators and two secondary indicators based on brainstorming, combined with theoretical storage capacity calculations. By categorizing zones into favorable, moderately favorable, and unfavorable areas, this research not only provides a quantitative decision-making framework for site selection in deep coal seam CO2 geological storage projects but also promotes the synergistic integration of green resource transition in mining regions with China’s “Dual Carbon” goals. The specific objectives include establishing a hierarchical evaluation index system tailored to deep coal seams, quantitatively assessing the storage stability and potential of the M3 and M4 seams, and proposing a zoning scheme based on a “one ticket veto” mechanism. Methodologically, this study integrates field geological surveys, laboratory experiments, fuzzy comprehensive evaluation, and theoretical modeling, aiming to deliver theoretical foundations and technical benchmarks for implementing deep coal seam carbon storage projects.

Geology and Characteristics of Coal Seams

Geological Background

The Wanglou mining area extends 4.16–6.93 km from north to south and 3.07–5.74 km from east to west, covering an area of 29.04 km2. As shown in Figure , the Wanglou mining area is with an overall structural form characterized by an “S”-shaped syncline (Qiaotazi syncline) slightly protruding northwestward. In the central-northern part of the eastern wing of this syncline lies an anticline (Shilou anticline), forming a T-shape. The intraregional folding is relatively simple, while faulting is well developed. The stratigraphic trend is north–south in the south and bends to nearly east–west in the north. The axis of the Shilou anticline trends NWW, with the hinge generally dipping westward. It intersects with the Qiaotaizi syncline near Shilou, forming a saddle. The core of the Shilou anticline is intersected by faults FD13, FD15, and FD18, but its morphology remains intact. The two limbs of the anticline are basically symmetrical, with gentle dips of approximately 5–6°. The axis of the syncline trends NNE in its southern segment, transitions to NNW in the middle segment, and shifts toward NE in the northern segment, with the axis slightly protruding northwestward in an arc-like manner. The hinge intersects with the Shilou anticline near Shilou, forming a saddle. The southern segment of the syncline has nearly symmetrical wings, with dip angles of approximately 7–13°. Due to folding, the exploitable coal seams in the southern part of the area are relatively deep.

Structural outline of the Wanglou mining area.

The intra-area fault structures are well developed, with 17 combined normal faults and one isolated normal fault break point. The predominant fault orientation within the area is northeast to north–northeast (10 faults), primarily distributed in the eastern part of the mining district. The northwest-oriented group follows (7 faults) and is mainly located in the western part of the mining district. The two elements located far apart to the east and west complement each other, forming an “X” shaped structure. There are 2 faults with offsets ≥100 m, 4 faults with offsets between 50 and 100 m, 5 faults with offsets between 30 and 50 m, and 6 faults with offsets <30 m within the area.

Characteristics of Coal Seam Occurrence

As shown in Figure , the Wanglou coalfield is located within the rich coal-bearing zone of the Jiulishan coalfield in the Xuzhou coal basin. This coal-rich zone is characterized by the balanced development and relative enrichment of the M5 and M6 coal seams from the Shanxi Formation and the M3 and M4 coal seams from the Xiashihezi Formation. The Shanxi Group’s M5 and M6 coal seams were identified as unstable coal seams with low exploitable indices during exploration, making them unsuitable for deep coal seam CO2 geological sequestration projects. Therefore, this study prioritizes the M3 and M4 coal seams as carbon sequestration reservoirs.

Schematic diagram of lithologic and facies cyclical structure of the Xiashihezi Formation.

The Xiashihezi Formation has a thickness ranging from 199.10 to 269.50 m, with an average of 237.48 m. It represents coal-bearing continental clastic deposits formed in an inland alluvial plain environment. This unit is in conformable contact with the Shanxi Formation beneath. From bottom to top, the Xiashihezi Formation can be divided into five cycles, including five coal seams numbered 1–5, among which M3 and M4 are the primary exploitable coal seams. It can be divided into two segments based on its coal-bearing characteristics. The upper section is approximately 140 m thick and mainly consists of heterogeneous mudstone, heterogeneous and dark gray siltstone, light greenish-gray fine sandstone, and light gray medium to coarse sandstone. The lower section (coal-bearing segment) is approximately 97 m thick, primarily composed of light gray to gray-green fine to coarse sandstone, multicolored dark gray mudstone or siltstone, and five layers of medium coal. The base consists of thick-bedded, gray-green conglomeratic medium to coarse sandstone, marking the boundary between the Lower Shihhezi Formation and the Shanxi Formation, which serves as a regional marker horizon for correlation.

M3 coal seam is located at the lower part of the Xiashihezi Formation, 140.00–212.05 m above the Kuishan Sandstone with an average distance of 175.98 m, and 49.09–71.52 m below the Fenjie Sandstone with an average depth of 59.29 m. The thickness of the M3 coal seam ranges from 0.56 to 3.08 m, with an average of 1.72 m. The exploitable index is 0.82. The pure coal thickness at exploitable points ranges from 0.92 to 2.04 m, with an average thickness of 1.42 m. The minable area of the M3 coal seam is 2731.00 × 104 m2, with a permeability of 4.66 × 10–15 m2. The M3 coal seam is buried at depths ranging from 916 to 1345 m, with a relatively simple overall structure. The coal seam is stable, although it contains interbedded mudstone in certain locations, with interbed thicknesses of 0.32–1.16 m. The roof of the M3 coal seam is typically composed of shale or siltstone with some points being sandstone, while the floor consists of either shale or siltstone.

M4 coal seam is located at the lower part of the Xiashihezi Formation, 0–11.14 m above the M3 coal seam with an average distance of 4.08 m, and a distance of 46.20 to 67.18 m below the boundary sandstone with an average distance of 53.88 m. The coal seam thickness ranges from 0.37 to 2.17 m, with an average of 1.10 m. The exploitable index is 0.82, and the pure coal thickness at exploitable points ranges from 0.70 to 1.62 m, with an average distance of 1.17 m. The exploitable area of coal seam M4 is 2712.50 × 104 m2, with a permeability of 4.71 × 10–15 m2. The M4 coal seam is buried at depths ranging from 921 to 1313.47 m, with a generally stable and simple structure. In certain locations, there is a 0.55 m intercalation of mudstone. The roof of the M4 coal seam is primarily composed of shale and siltstone with local occurrences of carbonaceous shale. The floor is mainly shale, followed by siltstone.

Among them, the anticlinal fold of Shilou is wide and gentle, with a fold amplitude of about 100 m, which has little influence on the coal seam. The syncline fold of Qiaotai is also wide and gentle, with a fold amplitude of about 150 m, resulting in the main coal seam in the southern part of the mining area being deeper. Simultaneously, the main fault in the mining area is deep and cuts across the main coal seam in the area.

Remaining Resource Quantity

The coal resource amount of the M3 coal seam at the Wanglou Coal Mine is 38.257 million tons, and that of the M4 coal seam is 38.395 million tons. Furthermore, in accordance with the requirements of Jiangsu Province’s Notice on Strengthening the Source Control of Coal Mine Impact Ground Pressure, new mining activities at depths exceeding one km are strictly prohibited, resulting in most of the coal in the M3 and M4 seams becoming inaccessible resources. According to the investigation and statistics, the unminable coal reserves of the M3 and M4 deep coal seams amount to 69.35 million tons. The proven reserves of coal seam M3 amount to 7.653 million tons, with inferred reserves totaling 26.671 million tons, summing up to a combined total of 34.324 million tons (Figure a). The proven coal resources of the M4 coal seam amount to 7.476 million tons, with inferred resources totaling 27.550 million tons, summing up to a combined total of 35.026 million tons (Figure b). Therefore, the coal resources in the M3 and M4 seams buried deeper than 1000 m are abundant, and the potential for carbon storage is significant.

Site Selection Indicators of Domestic Coalbed CO2 Sequestration

The evaluation metrics are determined as follows: Eight primary indicators include coal seam cover performance (A 1), pressure-to-temperature ratio (A 2), depth-to-thickness ratio (A 3), groundwater pH level (A 4), structural complexity (A 5), permeability (A 6), subsidence due to mining collapse (A 7), and other geological factors (A 8). The complexity of structure (A5) is further divided into two secondary indicators: fault intensity (B 1) and fault density (B 2).

Coal seam cover performance (A 1): The performance of caprock primarily affects its ability to seal the storage of CO2 in the overlying rock layers. The stronger the sealing capacity of the overlying rock layers, the better the storage effect. Shale has the best sealing effect, followed by limestone, while sandstone has relatively poor sealing properties.

Pressure-to-temperature ratio (A 2): The greater the coal seam pressure, the higher the amount of CO2 stored and the more stable the storage effect. Additionally, lower geothermal temperatures help to maintain the stability of the phase of stored CO2.

Depth-to-thickness ratio (A 3): The thicker the coal seam, the greater the space for CO2 migration, and the better the stability of CO2 storage. Similarly, the deeper the storage depth, the better the storage stability. Therefore, it is advisible for the ratio of the depth to thickness of sealed coal seams to be relatively large.

Groundwater pH level (A 4): Excessively acidic groundwater can corrode coal and surrounding rocks, increasing the risk of CO2 leakage. Conversely, overly alkaline groundwater reacts with CO2 in the water to form precipitates that block CO2 migration pathways. , Therefore, it is advisible for groundwater pH levels to be around neutral.

Structural complexity (A 5): The complexity of geological structures is primarily influenced by fault strength and fault density. Fault strength (B 1) generally refers to the ability of a fault to resist shear sliding and serves as a critical parameter for evaluating the fault stability. The higher the fault strength, the greater the stress required for fault slippage, thereby reducing the likelihood of earthquakes or fault displacement. Consequently, CO2 stored in coal seams remains more stable. Fault density (B 2) typically denotes the number, total length, or concentration of faults within a unit geological body. Regions with high fault density are prone to forming migration pathways for CO2, thereby affecting the stability of stored CO2.

Permeability (A 6): If the seam’s fracture rate or porosity is too low, it will be compressed and sealed due to adsorption expansion when carbon dioxide is injected into coal seams. Conversely, if the permeability of the coal seam is too high, then the CO2 adsorbed by the coal seam becomes unstable and is easily released under external forces.

Subsidence due to mining collapse (A 7): Subsidence of mined-out areas can lead to groundwater seepage from the upper aquifer, which may compress storage space and block migration pathways. Therefore, the lower the degree of subsidence in mined-out areas, the better.

Other geological factors (A 8): The activity of these geological factors, such as earthquakes and volcanic activity, should be as minimal as possible.

Qualitative Evaluation of CO2 Sequestration Stability in Coal Seams

According to relevant data, the roof strata of the M3 and M4 coal seams are generally composed of mudstone and siltstone, exhibiting favorable sealing properties. The deep coal seams are buried at greater depths with higher pressure/temperature ratios and depth/thickness ratios, which are conducive to carbon dioxide storage. The coal seam permeability and groundwater pH values are moderate, exerting minimal impact on the stability of CO2 storage. The fault strength in the region is relatively high with a medium fault density, resulting in moderate regional structural complexity. No goaf collapse zones currently exist in the M3 and M4 coal seams, and other regional geological activities are underdeveloped. Therefore, the carbon storage environments of the M3 and M4 coal seams remain relatively stable.

Monitoring of CO2 Geological Sequestration in Coal Seam

External earthquakes, excessive injection pressure, coal seam adsorption expansion, or strong internal acid corrosion can compromise the integrity of geological formations or gas injection wells, potentially leading to underground CO2 leakage. Consequently, CO2 sequestration projects in coal seams must establish an information reporting system, implement emergency and financial safeguard mechanisms, and enhance research on monitoring techniques and methodologies to ensure real-time dynamic observation of the CO2 sequestration process and prevent major leakage incidents. In July 2016, China issued the “Technical Guidelines for Environmental Risk Assessment of CO2 Capture, Utilization, and Storage (Trial).” These guidelines stipulate that environmental monitoring for geological utilization and storage should include four phases: baseline environmental monitoring, injection operation period monitoring, site closure monitoring, and long-term postclosure monitoring. The monitoring targets encompass groundwater, surface water, the atmosphere, and other elements.

There are various common monitoring methods, among which 4D seismic methods, InSAR technology, and hyperspectral imaging can be applied to large-scale geological formations or surface environment monitoring. Infrared gas analyzers, AC/EC, portable water quality detectors, and soil gas sampling analysis methods are suitable for simple and rapid routine inspections. Corrosion coupon methods and ultrasonic imaging logging are applicable for monitoring various wellbores and casings. Tracer methods (using isotopes such as 13C, Kr, SF6, and C2H2F4) can also distinguish CO2 sources, but may exhibit certain lag effects.

Conclusions

(1)Using fuzzy comprehensive evaluation method, eight primary indicators and two secondary indicators were selected for assessing the stability of M3 and M4 deep coal seams in Wanglou coal mine, with evaluation results of 86.621 and 89.268 respectively. Therefore, the M3 and M4 deep coal seams have a relatively stable storage grade, making them suitable for CO2 geological sequestration of deep coal seam.(2)Based on the theoretical storage capacity calculation model for CO2 in deep coal seams, the calculated theoretical storage capacities for the nonminable parts of the M3 and M4 deep coal seams in Wanglou coal mine are 80.842 Mt and 65.439 Mt, respectively. Combined with the evaluation results of geological storage stability, the nonminable parts of the M3 and M4 deep coal seams are further classified into three grades areas: favorable areas, relatively favorable areas, and unfavorable areas. The high density of faults (the primary factor affecting storage stability) reduces the area of sealable zones, resulting in a significantly lower actual carbon sequestration capacity compared to theoretical estimates. Therefore, during site selection, enhanced fault investigation and survey should be conducted to ensure the stability and safety of deep coal seam carbon sequestration.(3)It is advisible to prioritize pilot projects in favorable sealing zones to establish demonstration projects for CO2 storage in deep, unminable coal seams. This approach would verify the alignment between theoretical models and actual storage efficiency while also monitoring long-term storage safety. Concurrently, CO2 storage technology for deep, unminable coal seams should be integrated into the national CCUS technology development plan, accompanied by financial subsidies, tax incentives, or carbon quota rewards. Efforts should be made to deepen industry-academia-research integration and continuously promote the development of low-cost deep coal seam carbon storage technologies, such as efficient injection processes and intelligent monitoring systems.

(2)Based on the theoretical storage capacity calculation model for CO2 in deep coal seams, the calculated theoretical storage capacities for the nonminable parts of the M3 and M4 deep coal seams in Wanglou coal mine are 80.842 Mt and 65.439 Mt, respectively. Combined with the evaluation results of geological storage stability, the nonminable parts of the M3 and M4 deep coal seams are further classified into three grades areas: favorable areas, relatively favorable areas, and unfavorable areas. The high density of faults (the primary factor affecting storage stability) reduces the area of sealable zones, resulting in a significantly lower actual carbon sequestration capacity compared to theoretical estimates. Therefore, during site selection, enhanced fault investigation and survey should be conducted to ensure the stability and safety of deep coal seam carbon sequestration.

(3)It is advisible to prioritize pilot projects in favorable sealing zones to establish demonstration projects for CO2 storage in deep, unminable coal seams. This approach would verify the alignment between theoretical models and actual storage efficiency while also monitoring long-term storage safety. Concurrently, CO2 storage technology for deep, unminable coal seams should be integrated into the national CCUS technology development plan, accompanied by financial subsidies, tax incentives, or carbon quota rewards. Efforts should be made to deepen industry-academia-research integration and continuously promote the development of low-cost deep coal seam carbon storage technologies, such as efficient injection processes and intelligent monitoring systems.
