Acral melanoma: new insights into the immune and genomic landscape
Authors: Larissa Anastacio DaCosta Carvalho, Flavia C Aguiar, Keiran SM Smalley, Patricia A Possik
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcral melanoma is a rare subtype of melanoma that arises on the non-hair bearing skin of the nail bed, palms of the hand and soles of the feet. It is unique among melanomas in not being linked to ultraviolet radiation (UVR) exposure from the sun, and, as such, its incidence is similar across populations who are of Asian, Hispanic, African and European origin. Although research into acral melanoma has lagged behind that of sun-exposed cutaneous melanoma, recent studies have begun to address the unique genetics and immune features of acral melanoma. In this review we will discuss the latest progress in understanding the biology of acral melanoma across different ethnic populations and will outline how these new discoveries can help to guide the therapeutic management of this rare tumor.

Introduction

Acral melanoma (AM) is a rare subtype of melanomas that arises on the glabrous skin (palms of the hands and soles of the feet) and in the subungual regions (nail beds) [1], [2], [3]. Risk factors that are common for other cutaneous melanoma (CM) subtypes, including sun exposure, fair skin type, family history of melanoma, and pre-existing melanocytic nevi, are not applicable to AM [1,3]. As AM development is not linked to exposure to ultraviolet radiation (UVR) it has a lower tumor mutational burden (TMB) than CM and shows a different pattern of oncogenic driver mutation expression. AM is also characterized by unique genetic features, such as large-scale gene amplifications and re-arrangements [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]. These differences in genetic makeup are also associated with an altered tumor environment, and less immune infiltrate compared to CM [11], [12], [13]. Although it has been suggested that mechanical stress is a causative factor for AM, the evidence for this remains conflicting [14], [15], [16], [17], [18].

In European/Western populations, acral melanoma accounts for only 2–3 % of all melanomas [19]. However, among other groups including populations of African, Hispanic/Latin American or Asian origin, AM represents the dominant melanoma subtype [3,[20], [21], [22]]. In Asian countries such as China, Japan, Korea and Taiwan, and in Latin American countries such as Mexico and Peru, AM represents >50 % of all recorded melanomas [14,17,21,23]. In South-Africa, the incidence of AM among Black individuals is 65 % whereas in the total population it is 11 % [24].

Prognosis is worse for AM than CM which may be due in part to the later clinical presentation of AM compared to CM. At initial presentation >62 % of AM are stage II or higher, compared to 32 % of CM. The reasons underlying this are multi-factorial and can include a lack of awareness about melanoma among different racial/demographic groups and the anatomical site [25]. Patients with AM suffer worse 5 and 10-year melanoma-specific survival rates relative to patients with other forms of CM, making it one the deadliest subtypes of melanoma [11,25,26].

The past 10 years have seen incredible progress in the development new systemic targeted therapies and immunotherapies for the treatment of CM [27,28]. These new therapeutic modalities have revolutionized clinical practice to the extent that the death rate for advanced CM dropped by 30 % between 2016 and 2019 [29]. These promising developments have not translated to AM with multiple studies suggesting AM patients have lower rates of response to both immunotherapies and targeted therapies [3].

It is likely that AM will require different therapeutic strategies from CM, which are yet to be defined. Recent work has shed new light on the transcriptional and mutational profiles that underlie the oncogenic behavior of AM. New technologies such as single cell (sc)RNA-seq have provided novel insights into the microenvironmental landscape of AM and have provided critical new information about how the immune infiltrate differs from that of CM. It is now clear that AM is a heterogeneous disease, with distinct differences between racial groups. In this article, we provide an update on the latest research into AM and review how a greater understanding of the unique genomic and immune features of this rare melanoma can help guide future therapy development.

Genetic and signaling characteristics of acral melanoma

Early studies, comparing the mutational profiles of melanomas arising on sun-exposed and non-sun exposed skin (including both mucosal melanoma (MM) and acral melanoma), demonstrated key genetic differences between melanomas at the different anatomical sites. AM was characterized by large scale genomic chromosomal abnormalities associated with increased copy number gains and losses [6]. It was further noted that AM had a lower frequency of activating mutations in BRAF, and an increased likelihood of CDK4 amplifications [6]. Further characterization, using array CGH, identified a narrow, amplified band in 4q12 that corresponded to c-KIT [7]. Mutations/amplifications in c-KIT were identified in 36 % of AM, and it was proposed that KIT antagonists, such as imatinib could be used to treat KIT-mutant AM [7].

Subsequent whole genome sequencing (WGS) and targeted exome sequencing studies from multiple groups directly compared the genomes of AM, CM and MM [4,5,[8], [9], [10],30,31]. These analyses demonstrated significant differences in the numbers of single nucleotide variants (SNV), deletions and structural variations between the melanoma three subtypes. AM is characterized by the highest number of chromosomal structural variations, the largest percentage of whole genome doubling (71 %), and much lower numbers of SNVs and indels compared to CM and MM [10].

The development of new computational approaches to map the topology of cancer genomes have also identified unique structural genomic features in AM that are not shared with CM. This investigation, which characterized the topographical features of junction copy number (JCN) alterations across 2,788 cancer genomes, identified three new complex rearrangement phenomena [32]. Among these were Tyfonas – “typhoons” of high-JCN junctions and fold-back inversions associated with expressed protein-coding fusion and break-end hypermutations. These large amplicons were comprised of webs of elevated JCN junctions with copy numbers of up to 50 [32]. Tyfonas were observed in >40 % of AM specimens, while being mostly absent from CM (found in <2 % of samples). It remains to be determined why Tyfonas are a feature of AM rather than other melanoma subtypes.

It has long been postulated that mechanical stress/injury is a causative factor for AM development, although the evidence remains discordant across different populations [14,18]. There is preclinical evidence that mechanical stress can cause DNA damage [33]. Studies have shown that the implantation of melanoma cells into the foot pads of mice induces a DNA damage response, disruption of the nuclear membrane and activation of the YAP signaling pathway, which was not observed when the melanoma cells were implanted at other anatomical sites [33]. Although deep sequencing revealed the presence of new SNVs in the footpad-implanted melanoma cells, there was no evidence of the large-scale genomic amplifications or Tyfonas that are characteristic of AM [33].

An analysis of recurrent mutational signatures also identified key differences between AM, CM and MM [5,9,10,34]. CM was characterized by UVR mutational signatures, such as the COSMIC signatures SBS7a-d, that were mostly lacking in AM and MM [10]. Despite this, UVR signatures were found in a small subset of AMs arising from more sun-exposed primary tumor sites, such as the hands. AMs that lacked the UVR signatures were frequently enriched for the SBS38 mutational signature [10]. This signature is of unknown etiology but is known to be melanoma-associated [10,35]. Other mutational signatures found in AM were SBS40 (correlated with age), SBS2/13 (APOBEC associated) and SBS1/5 (correlated with age) [10]. In an Asian AM patient cohort, the commonly identified COSMIC mutational signatures included SBS3, 5, 9, 12, 16 and 20 [36]. Of note, signatures 9, 12, 16 and 20 have not been previously identified in melanoma [36]. In Taiwanese patients, mutational signature 12 was the most frequent, accounting for 45 % of the mutation profiles observed [37].

AM also harbors the genomic signatures of Chromothripsis - a process in which hundreds to thousands of clustered chromosomal rearrangements occur in a single event in a confined genomic region [10]. Specific amplifications identified in AM were noted in genomic regions containing MAP2K1, CCND1, CDK4, GAB2, PAK1, EP300 and MDM2, with deletions in CDK2NA and NF1 also observed [5,8,10] (Fig. 1). The identification of MDM2 and CDK4 amplifications is of particular interest given that these genes are frequently associated with Tyfonas [32]. Up to 46 % of AM were found to have TERT alterations. Among these, the frequency of TERT promoter mutations were much lower in AM compared to CM, with AM instead having a higher frequency of TERT amplifications [[8], [9], [10],31].

In Asian AM patient samples, 14.2 % of the genome was affected by CNVs, with major gains noted in KIT, PDGFRA, KDR, CCND1, GAB2, YAP1, CDK4, EP300 and MDM2. Co-amplification in CDK4 and MDM2 was noted in 57.1 % of samples [36] (Fig. 1). Integration of the SNVs and CNVs from this patient cohort identified the most impacted signaling pathways to be MAPK, cell-cycle progression and histone modification [36]. In preclinical cell line models, amplification of the transcriptional co-regulator EP300 was associated with sensitivity to an EP300 inhibitor. A negative correlation was noted between EP300 copy number gain and a smoldering immune environment, pointing to a potential mechanism of immune escape in AM [36].

Acral melanomas have a different profile of driver mutations from CM. In AM, 21.8 % are BRAF mutant, 10 % have NRAS mutations and 26 % have NF1 mutations, with 32.2 % of AM being triple wild-type [10] (Fig. 1). For comparison the mutational frequency of these genes in CM was 41.6 % BRAF, 27.7 % in NRAS, 23.1 % in NF1 and 7.6 % triple wild-type. Other significant mutations identified in AM included TYRP1, PTEN and c-KIT [10]. Difference driver mutation profiles were seen across different racial groups of AM patients. In an Asian cohort of AM patients, the mutational frequencies were BRAF (15 %), NRAS (15 %), BRAF (15 %) and KIT (16.7 %). NF1 mutations were much less common at a frequency of 6.7 % [36].

Another pooled analysis of 11 published studies (containing 147 acral melanoma samples) identified PRPRJ as a potential tumor suppressor gene and FER and SKP2 as potential oncogenes [38]. This study also identified a minor sub-set of BRAF mutant AM samples with very few amplifications/structural alterations. It was suggested that these AM (which arose at more sun-exposed sites) might be more genetically related to CM [38]. It is known that subungual AMs (developing in the nail beds) have UVR- mutational signatures and higher numbers of mutations per megabase [9]. It thus seems that AM from sun-exposed sites may share important genetic features with CM.

Data from integrated transcriptomic analyses revealed that the major pathways associated with AM were the MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways in 66 % of cases, CDK4/CDKN2A in 51 %, TERT in 37 %, and MDM2/TP53 in 17 % [31]. Interestingly, AM, MM and CM rely upon a shared set of signaling pathways (MAPK, PI3K, p16, p53, and telomere maintenance), suggesting the existence of melanocyte lineage-specific requirements for oncogenic transformation.

More recent work has shed light upon potential drivers of AM metastasis. An analysis of 104 AM specimens from both the USA and China identified a recurrent amplification in 22q11.21 that arose late in tumor progression and was associated with poor overall survival, a downregulation in genes associated with response to immunotherapy and risk of metastasis development [39]. One gene identified in this cytoband was the tumor suppressor LZTR1, with functional studies demonstrating an association between LZTR1 and anchorage-independent growth, spheroid formation and increased oncogenic signaling through Src and MAPK [39].

It is likely that preference for different oncogenic drivers in AM vs. CM are related to the anatomical site. A recent integrated DNA sequencing/RNA-seq study identified amplification of GAB2 and CRKL, two adaptor proteins that amplify receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling, in AM vs CM [40]. The introduction/silencing of the identified AM genes in a zebrafish model led to the development of melanomas in the fins, whereas expression of CM-associated genes led to skin melanomas [40]. Mechanistic studies showed that HOX13 was instrumental in regulating IGF signaling in fin melanocytes and that CRKL amplification served to amplify both HOX13 and IGFR signaling through the PI3K pathway [40]. This work demonstrated for the first time that microenvironment of the anatomical site dictated the underlying transcriptional state of the melanocytes, conferring susceptibility to distinct oncogenes. In support of this idea, nevi arising on acral skin have a similar BRAF mutational frequency to those arising on sun-exposed skin, despite AM having a much lower incidence of BRAF mutations [41].

Heterogeneity and TME landscape: recent advances in high-dimensional analysis

One major recent technological breakthrough has been the development of approaches to analyze tumor microenvironments at a single cell level. Initial work using these methods focused upon defining the transcriptional heterogeneity of the tumor compartment in CM, characterizing multiple melanoma cell states, as well as the immune infiltrate [42], [43], [44]. Improved computational approaches have allowed the landscapes of the entire tumor microenvironment to be defined, leading to the identification of unique populations of cells. One of the first of such studies in AM was a scRNA seq analysis of 9 specimens (5 primary AM, 4 metastases, 1 matched pair) from a white patient cohort [11]. This showed the immune environment of AM to be heterogeneous, with some specimens having very little immune infiltrate and others having a high level of lymphocytes (NK cells, B cells, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells) and myeloid cells (NK cells, macrophages, dendritic cells etc) [11] (Fig. 2). When the AM samples were compared to scRNA-seq data from CM samples, AM was found to have a suppressed immune environment, characterized by decreased numbers of NK cells, CD8+ tissue effector memory (TEM) T cells and gamma delta T cells [11]. Significant changes were also noted in immune checkpoint expression between the AM and CM cohorts, with the AM samples having decreased levels of TIM-3 and increased levels of ADORA2 and VISTA. Expression of PD-1, CTLA-4, TIGIT and LAG3 were noted to be similar between AM and CM specimens [11].

Another study undertook scRNA-seq analyses on a cohort of Chinese melanoma patients (5 AM and 3 CM as the discovery set) [13]. It was again noted that AM had a more immune suppressed tumor microenvironment than CM, characterized by increased numbers of regulatory CD4+ T cells (Tregs), decreased numbers of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and an increase in exhausted CD8+ T cells that expressed PD-1 and TIM-3 (Fig. 2). It was speculated that the increased Treg levels observed were associated with the immunotherapy resistance [13]. The authors also undertook co-culture studies with patient-matched AM cells and CD8+ T cells and showed that treatment with the combination of anti-PD-1 and anti-TIM3 increased tumor cell apoptosis [13].

One further scRNA-seq study profiled AM specimens from 6 Chinese patients. Here, the AM samples were found to have higher numbers of cancer associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and less lymphocytes than CM samples [12]. Analysis of cell-cell signaling pathways between the tumor and adjacent tissues identified an increase in KIT signaling from endothelial cells and fibroblasts and WNT signals from adjacent keratinocytes [12]. The authors further identified both M1 and M2 macrophages in the AM lesions, with the pro-tumorigenic M2 being the dominant subtype identified. Detailed curation of the CAFs identified three potential subtypes including known subsets that were RGS4+ and PDGFRA+ and a new type that expressed CTGF. Although the authors did not observe high levels of PD-1 and CTLA-4 expressed in the cytotoxic T cells, they instead identified TNFRSF9 as a possible AM-specific immune checkpoint [12] (Fig. 2). There is already some evidence that agonists of TNFRSF9 can be effective against some cancers and that the combination of anti-PD-1 + TNFRSF9 agonists leads to the expansion and activation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells in cancer models [45].

A recurrent observation across all of the scRNA-seq studies to date is the reduced level of immune infiltration in AM samples compared to CM. A recent analysis of over 892 bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq acral melanoma profiles demonstrated that >70 % of samples had a low expression of the T cell-inflamed gene expression signature, with 24 % of samples characterized as non-T cell-inflamed [46]. Patients with low levels of tumor-associated CD8+ PD-1+ T-cell infiltrate had a poor prognosis. Eleven oncogenic pathways, including MYC, HGF, MITF, VEGF, EGFR, SP1. EBB2, SREBF1, SOX and CCND1, were associated with the non-T cell-inflamed- relative to the T cell-inflamed immune microenvironment. Many of these pathways converged on CXCL8 (or IL-8), an inflammatory cytokine that is known to recruit suppressive myeloid cells and is associated with poor responses to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy [46]. These pathways were found to be enriched in patients non-responsive to ICI therapy across multiple cancer types, including AM.

Clinical management of advanced AM: current and emerging strategies

As AM is a rare cancer, there have been few clinical trials exploring therapeutic targets that are AM-specific. Some large-scale clinical trials of targeted therapies and immunotherapies have included AM patients in their sub-group analyses, although frequently in combination with patients with MM. At this time, there are no specific treatment guidelines for the treatment of AM, and patients are typically treated according to protocols developed for patients with advanced CM (Fig. 3).

One of the major therapeutic breakthroughs for CM has been the development of immunotherapies that restore CD8+ T cell function by relieving the inhibitory immune checkpoints (such as anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4 and anti-LAG3) [27,47]. Several studies have indicated that AM respond less favorably to immunotherapy than CM, with evidence of fewer complete responses and worse overall survival [[48], [49], [50]]. However, there are discrepancies in the literature. A recent Japanese study demonstrated improved response in melanoma patients undergoing ICI treatment, with differences being observed across subtypes [51]. Moreover, a further study from China reported comparable ICI efficacy across melanoma subtypes [52]. In all cases, the cohort sizes for these clinical studies were very small, making interpretation difficult.

The efficacy of ICI in acral melanoma may be influenced by several factors, including the mutational landscape. Studies have found a link between lower TMB in advanced melanomas from sun-protected regions, including acral sites, and reduced ICI responsiveness compared to their sun-exposed counterparts [53]. This association was similarly noted in both AM and MM cases [54]. Interestingly, the relationship between TMB and response to anti-PD-1 can be confounded by the melanoma subtypes: some AM patients with lower TMB exhibit better responses than CM patients with higher TMB [55]. It is also not clear whether the genomic changes that characterize many AM, such as Tyfonas, also generate tumor neoantigens. Moreover, subungual acral melanomas, known for their elevated TMB compared to other acral sites [9,10], demonstrated lower survival rates with anti-PD-1 treatment compared to melanomas located on the palms and soles among Japanese patients [56]. Yet, a multicenter study conducted in Australia, Asia, Europe, and the USA found no outcome differences among AM anatomical sites [57].

Another factor that may influence responses to ICI in AM is the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, as demonstrated by multiple recent scRNA-seq analyses of AM samples [11], [12], [13]. Additional studies have independently reported reduced levels of tumor infiltrating CD8+ lymphocytes in AM compared to CM [58]. Moreover, resistance to ICI has been associated with lower levels of CD8+, CD11+ and Treg+ T cells in both AM and MM patients. Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and PD-L1 expression have been suggested as a prognostic marker to ICI in AM [48]. Some studies have noted that PD-L1 expression was lower compared to CM [48].

Most of the ongoing clinical trials for AM involve immunotherapies (Table 1 and Fig. 3), including Tebentafusp, initially approved for the treatment of uveal melanoma [59]. Tebentafusp is a bi-specific antibody that combines a T cell receptor that recognizes the melanoma-specific antigen gp100 and an anti-CD3 single-chain variable fragment capable of recruiting and activating T cells. Tebentafusp has demonstrated increased overall survival compared to standard-of-care ICI therapy in uveal melanoma [59] and clinical trials assessing its combination with ICI in AM, CM and MM are ongoing. However, Tebentafusp's efficacy is limited to HLA-A02:01-positive individuals, more prevalent in European-descent populations [60], posing a challenge for its application to other demographic groups.

Many studies are investigating ICI combinations with LAG3–blocking antibodies (Fig. 3), which are showing promise in multiple sub-types of melanoma, including AM [47,61]. Given the similar expression LAG3 levels between AM and CM (at least at the mRNA level) [11], further studies are needed to evaluate its potential in AM.

Additional ongoing research encompasses a range of ICI options, including combinations with RTK inhibitors, such as VEGFR. Encouragingly, evidence from other cancer types supports the dual approach of ICI and angiogenesis targeting [62,63], with promising results for these combinations in melanoma mouse models [64]. Trials in melanoma, including AM, are still at an early stage, with results pending.

As there no AM-specific targeted therapies available, patients with BRAF-mutant AM are treated with BRAF inhibitors and the BRAF-MEK inhibitor combination (Fig. 2). Here, the responses appear to be similar to those observed for CM patients [65,66], which is not surprising given the mutational similarities between BRAF-mutant AM and BRAF-mutant-CM [9,10]. A number of targeted therapy clinical trials are currently ongoing that specifically include AM patients (Table 1). Among these, several studies are focused upon RTKs that target mutant/amplified c-KIT, which shows a higher rate of alteration in AM vs. CM (Fig. 2). Despite this, c-KIT inhibition has not shown encouraging results in most studies [67,68] with responses only being associated with mutations in KIT exon 11 and 13, but not with gene amplification [69,70]. Inhibition of CDK4/6 with Palbociclib is also being explored for AM patients with CDK4 or/and CCND1 gain or/and CDKN2A loss. Preliminary data showed some efficacy and identified an association of MCM7 expression and clinical benefit [71]. While further data is required for a comprehensive understanding of CDK4/6 inhibitor benefits in AM patients, combining these inhibitors with other strategies is showing some promise. Intriguingly, CCND1 amplifications, common in AM, were linked to poor responses to anti-PD-1 treatment, suggesting potential benefits from combining CDK4/6 inhibitors with anti-PD-1 therapy, especially for CCND1-amplified AMs [48].

The ongoing characterization of AM's genomic and immune landscape is revealing novel potential targets. Potential new targets include EP300, PAK1, PDGFRA, and IGFR/PI3K (Fig. 2). Additionally, immune checkpoint receptors such as TIM3 and TNFRSF9 are also potential candidates to enhance the effectiveness of ICI (Figs. 2, 3). It is our hope that these targets, along with those discovered through ongoing global genomic studies, will be explored, contributing to improved treatment outcomes for AM patients. It remains to be determined whether AM across different populations will require similar or different therapeutic strategies, as the genomic and immune profiling data thus far have identified key differences between Asian and white patients.

Ethnic disparities in survival and therapeutic outcomes for AM patients

Ethnic disparities in AM patient treatment and survival are evident, with a higher incidence of AM among non-European descent. Analysis of the SEER database reveals that underrepresented ethnic groups in the US, including Black, Asians, Hispanics, and Pacific Islanders, experience lower melanoma-specific survival rates compared to white patients [72]. This trend persists among AM patients, particularly Hispanics and Black individuals, even following the introduction of advanced therapies [72].

Late diagnosis plays a significant role in survival disparities, with underrepresented ethnic groups often facing a delayed diagnosis compared to white individuals. Non-white melanoma patients frequently present with thicker tumors and a higher frequency of ulcerated tumors at diagnosis [20,73,74]. Unlike white patients, where over 75 % are diagnosed at Stage I melanoma, non-white individuals, who comprise a substantial portion of AM cases, experience far fewer Stage I diagnoses and a notable increase in Stage IV diagnoses [73]. Additionally, African American, Hispanic, and Asian patients exhibit lower survival rates, even at earlier disease stages [20,73,74]. Notably, Chinese patients, despite being diagnosed at later stages, show no difference in stage-stratified survival compared to US patients [75].

Treatment response variability across populations further influences survival disparities. A multicenter study involving patients from the USA, Australia, and China showed that white patients exhibit a more favorable response to ICI and longer progression-free survival compared to East Asian, Hispanic, and African patients across all melanoma subtypes, including AM [76].

Socioeconomic status (SES) disparities also contribute to unequal melanoma care and outcomes. Higher SES is associated with early diagnosis, surgical treatment, and improved survival among melanoma patients. In a Brazilian AM cohort, higher SES correlates with improved survival [77]. In the US, lower SES is observed among Black AM patients, and a greater proportion of non-white patients lack health insurance [78]. Patients with private health insurance have greater access to immunotherapies, but even among them, Black patients are less likely to receive this treatment [79]. In countries with limited access to immunotherapy and targeted therapy, this situation can be even more challenging [80]. It is clear that further, large-scale, studies are needed to identify whether the differences in survival and outcomes are more reflective of biology (which may influence treatment selection) or demographic factors (that can be addressed through patient education and improved access to care).

Future perspectives

AM is different from CM not only in clinical and epidemiological aspects but also in its genomic landscape and immune microenvironment. In addition, there seems to be role for ethnicity and demographic characteristics in different aspects of the disease. Unfortunately, data on AM is still limited. Although several studies have recently shed light on the genomic and immune profile of AM in Asian and European-descent populations, very few studies have reported comprehensive data on patients from Africa and Latin America. The same is true for clinical studies, of which the majority involved Asian, American, Australian and European cohorts. An effort to include African and Latin America populations in genomic and clinical studies is therefore much needed. There are thousands of clinical trials for the treatment of melanoma currently registered at Clinicaltrials.gov, but only a small number specifically include AM (Table 1). Moreover, the reference studies that contributed to the approval of targeted and ICI therapies for advanced melanoma do not specify the inclusion or exclusion of AM, making it a challenge to assess treatment efficacy for this subtype.

To account for the clinical and genetic variation observed among diverse populations, it is important that clinical studies include patients from different ethnicities and countries. This approach ensures an adequate representation of cases and encompasses the rich diversity of global populations. Current data suggests that >97 % of participants in key clinical trials that have led to the regulatory approval of targeted therapies and immunotherapies in advanced melanoma are white. It is therefore crucial to encourage and promote the reporting of demographic information, as nearly half of the clinical studies do not provide comprehensive cohort characterization based on race and ethnicity.
