Development and validation of a clinical prediction model for neonatal seizures among neonates admitted to public health hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia: retrospective follow-up study
Authors: Tilahun Degu Tsega, Almaw Genet Yeshiwas, Wolde Melese Ayele, Chalachew Abiyu Ayalew, Assefa Andargie Kassa, Abathun Temesgen, Gashaw Melkie Bayeh, Chalachew Yenew, Getaneh Atikilt Yemata, Tesfaneh Shimels, Rahel Mulatie Anteneh, Getasew Yirdaw, Habitamu Mekonen, Berhanu Abebaw Mekonnen, Meron Asmamaw Alemayehu, Sintayehu Simie Tsega, Zeamanuel Anteneh Yigzaw, Amare Genetu Ejigu, Wondimnew Desalegn Addis, Birhanemaskal Malkamu, Abraham Teym, Kalaab Esubalew Sharew, Tamiru Alene, Daniel Adane, Ahmed Fentaw Ahmed

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeonatal seizures are sudden, abnormal brain activities that occur during the neonatal period, are the most common neurological emergencies in newborns, and are associated with high morbidity and mortality rates. Approximately 2–20% of neonates admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) worldwide experience neonatal seizures. Although established guidelines for managing neonatal seizures are available, no risk prediction models exist to help the clinical decision-making process; thus, the practices remain inconsistent across local facilities and resources. This study aims to develop and validate a neonatal risk prediction model for seizures among neonates admitted to NICUs at comprehensive specialized hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia.MethodsA retrospective follow-up study was conducted among 907 neonates admitted to the NICU in comprehensive specialized hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia. A systematic random sampling technique was employed to select the neonates’ records. Predictors for multivariable binary logistic regression analysis were selected via penalized LASSO for the variable selection method. A risk prediction nomogram was constructed from the simplified model. The discriminatory, prediction and calibration power of the risk prediction model was evaluated. Using the Youden index-identified cutoff point criterion, the level of risk of neonates for seizures was determined. Finally, decision curve analysis was performed to assess its clinical utility. For analysis purposes, Stata 17 and R.4.4.0 were used.ResultsThe incidence of neonatal seizures among neonates admitted to the NICU was 21.39% (95% CI: 18.83, 24.18%). The neonatal risk prediction nomogram was constructed using preterm birth, subgaleal hemorrhage, perinatal asphyxia, maternal history of seizure, hypoglycemia, and phototherapy as independent predictors. The internally validated discriminatory power of the model was 77.735% (95% CI: 73.912%, 81.634%), with a calibration plot test value of 0.056. The optimism coefficient of the simplified model was 0.0197. Decision curve analysis revealed a greater net benefit of the model in clinical practice than the “treat all” or “treat none” strategies did.ConclusionsThe incidence of neonatal seizures among neonates admitted to the NICU was relatively high. The predictive power of the constructed nomogram was good. Despite some limitations, the model offers superior net clinical benefit compared with the “treat all” or “treat none” strategies; however, further external validation is recommended for its wider application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-026-02246-1.

Introduction

Neonatal seizures are sudden and abnormal brain activity changes that may present with clinical signs or only as electrographic changes in neonates [1, 2]. Unlike seizures in older children and adults, seizures can occur because of the sensitive excitability of the developing brain [2]. The International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) classifies neonatal seizures into two types: electroclinical seizures, which have both clinical and electrographic features, and electrographic-only seizures, which appear solely on electroencephalograms (EEGs) without visible symptoms [1].

Neonatal seizures are the most common neurological emergencies during the newborn period and are linked to considerable rates of morbidity and mortality [3–5]. Its global incidence varies significantly, ranging from approximately 1–5 cases per 1,000 live births in high-income countries (HICs) to as many as 36–90 cases per 1,000 live births in low-income countries (LICs) [3–8]. This variation is largely attributed to disparities in healthcare systems, the prevalence of perinatal risk factors, and access to diagnostic resources [5, 7]. The primary etiologies of neonatal seizures include hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy [9–11], intracranial hemorrhage, central nervous system (CNS) infections, and metabolic disturbances [3, 10, 11]. The incidence of neonatal seizures varies among preterm and full-term neonates. In particular, preterm neonates have a greater risk of neonatal seizures than do full-term neonates. It frequently arises from the susceptibility of the preterm brain to harm, which is worsened by variables such as low birth weight and gestational age [3, 12]. Although there are many different causes of symptomatic seizures in preterm neonates, the most frequent causes include infections, periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), and intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), which are slightly different from the causes of neonatal seizures among full-term neonates [3, 11, 12]. Notably, neonatal seizures are often subtle and may be subclinical, necessitating EEG monitoring for accurate diagnosis [11, 13, 14].

Globally, the incidence of neonatal seizures among admitted neonates in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) varies widely. Studies suggest that approximately 2–20% of neonates admitted to NICUs experience seizures, depending on the population and diagnostic methods used [4, 7]. In Africa, the incidence is notably greater due to factors such as limited resources, higher rates of perinatal asphyxia, and infections [4, 5, 7, 15]. For example, in a rural Kenyan district hospital, 9% of total neonatal admissions were reported to have neonatal seizures [16]. In Ethiopia, specific studies have revealed the burden of neonatal seizures in NICUs [6, 16–19]. For example, research conducted at the University of Gondar Hospital revealed that 19.7% of neonates admitted to the NICU experienced neonatal seizures, with perinatal asphyxia being the leading cause [18].

The occurrence of neonatal seizures among neonates admitted to NICUs with poor outcomes is particularly high during development [4, 5, 19, 20]. In addition to the obstetric factors that increase the risk of neonatal seizures, the NICU setting up and poor management practices further increase the risk of neonatal seizures [21, 22]. Neonatal seizures are more prevalent in LICs such as Ethiopia, where the setups for NICUs and delivery and obstetric wards are poorly adequate [6, 10, 18, 19, 22].

In Ethiopia, neonatal seizures are a significant clinical and public health concern, reflecting the broader challenges faced by LICs. A study conducted at the University of Gondar Hospital reported that subtle seizures were the most common type, accounting for 60.7% of cases [18]. The study also highlighted that neonatal seizures were associated with increased mortality and adverse short-term neurological outcomes [18]. Another study from Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital in Mekelle, Tigray, Ethiopia, emphasized the need for early recognition and management to improve outcomes [19]. A systematic review of neonatal mortality in Ethiopia among neonates admitted to NICUs revealed that neonatal seizures were among the leading contributors to neonatal death, emphasizing the critical need for targeted interventions [23].

The high incidence of neonatal seizures in Ethiopia is often linked to perinatal asphyxia, subgaleal hemorrhages, CNS infections, and metabolic disturbances [4, 15]. Limited access to advanced neonatal care, delayed diagnosis, and inadequate management contribute to the elevated morbidity and mortality associated with neonatal seizures in the country [4, 17–19].

Neonatal seizures are a serious neurological condition that necessitates prompt and accurate diagnosis to prevent long-term complications [1–3, 9, 17–19, 23]. Although the ILAE has established guidelines for managing neonatal seizures, practices still vary across local facilities [4, 15]. While prediction models have proven to be valuable tools for identifying at-risk neonates, facilitating early intervention and improving outcomes, no such models have been developed in Ethiopia to assist clinicians in the decision-making process and diagnosis of neonatal seizures among neonates admitted to the NICU. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate a neonatal seizure risk prediction model for neonates admitted to the NICU at comprehensive specialized hospitals in the Amhara region, Northwest Ethiopia.

Methods

Study settings

A retrospective follow-up study was conducted in the Amhara region, Northwest Ethiopia, from January 1, 2024, to December 31, 2024. The study was conducted at comprehensive specialized public hospitals, namely, Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DBTCSH), Tibebe Ghion Comprehensive Specialized and Teaching Hospital (TGCSTH), Gondar Comprehensive Specialized and Teaching Hospital (GCSTH), Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DMCSH), and Felege-Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FHCSH).

These hospitals serve, on average, a total of 1,246 patients, 329 per year. Each of these hospitals has NICUs with similar set-ups and facilities and admits approximately 10,012 neonates per year. This follow-up study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening of the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [24].

Source and study population

The source population for this study was records of all neonates admitted to comprehensive specialized public hospitals in the Amhara Region, Northwest Ethiopia. The study population, on the other hand, included all neonates admitted to these hospitals from January 1, 2024, to December 31, 2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included all neonates admitted to the NICU whose medical records were complete at comprehensive specialized public hospitals. For neonates admitted multiple times, only the most recent admission was evaluated. Records with missing or incomplete information, such as seizure status, admission date, or discharge details, and uncertain clinical manifestations, such as jitteriness, tetanic spasms, complex malformations, and seizures during admission, were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated via Epi-info 7.2 software on the basis of the assumption of equal proportions between the two groups (exposed and unexposed) according to a rule of thumb. Hence, evidence suggests that the event-per-variable criterion (the most common sample size determination technique in clinical prediction modeling) has a weak relationship with predictive performance metrics and is not a suitable criterion for binary prediction model development studies [25]. Therefore, it was assumed that the exposed group had a 50% risk of developing neonatal seizures among neonates admitted to the NICU, whereas the risk was 40% in the nonexposed group. On the basis of these assumptions, the minimum adequate sample size required to represent the admitted neonates in these comprehensive specialized hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia for the risk of neonatal seizures was determined to be 938, including a 15% allowance for incomplete charts. The calculated sample size also met the assumption that the number of events per variable was at least ten [26].

Sampling technique

Initially, the data of 30% of the yearly admitted neonates were checked for eligibility. The calculated sample was then selected via a systematic random sampling method from each of the comprehensive specialized hospitals in the Amhara Region, Northwest Ethiopia. The Northwest Ethiopia part of the Amhara region comprises five such hospitals, and the sample size was allocated proportionally across these hospitals. Using the medical registration numbers from the NICU admission log books, a systematic random sampling technique was applied to identify the neonates. The medical charts of the selected neonates were subsequently retrieved from the hospital records room for data collection. The chart-associated records were subsequently retrieved from the selected hospitals (Fig. 1).

Variables of the study

Dependent variable: neonatal seizure (yes/no).

Independent variables included age of the neonate, sex of the neonate, residence, maternal age, neonatal sepsis status, maternal history of seizures, prolonged labor, APGAR score, perinatal asphyxia, acute respiratory distress syndrome, hypothermia, hypoglycemia, subgaleal hemorrhage, phototherapy, preterm birth, low birth weight and parity of the mother.

Operational definitions

Neonatal seizure

A neonate who has been diagnosed with neonatal seizure by the assigned physician according to the WHO criteria.

All predictors, including neonatal sepsis, maternal history of seizures, prolonged labor, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and low birth weight (which may require an operational definition), were obtained from the medical charts of neonates in the respective comprehensive specialized hospitals on the basis of physicians’ decisions.

APGAR score

In this study, we took the average of the first- and five-minute values recorded by the health professionals working in the delivery ward or the referral slip and recorded them in the neonates’ charts.

Data collection tools and procedures

A structured data extraction tool (checklist) was developed on the basis of information from the NICU admission logbook, neonatal charts, and various literature sources. Data related to the variables were gathered via this predefined checklist. The data collection was carried out by 12 trained neonatal nurses or other nurses with experience in NICU settings who were not actively working during the data collection period at the NICU. Six health professionals with master’s degrees were enlisted to oversee the data collection process. Data quality was ensured through training and proper supervision of the data collectors, with overall supervision provided by the supervisors and the principal investigator. The collected data were reviewed for completeness, clarity, and accuracy. Quality checks were conducted daily after data collection, and any necessary corrections were made before proceeding with further data collection.

Data processing and analysis

Data collection was conducted via the Kobo collection toolbox, after which the data were transferred to STATA version 17 and RStudio version 4.4.2 for analysis. Categorical variables were summarized via descriptive statistics, frequencies, and percentages. Furthermore, the incidence rate of neonatal seizures among admitted neonates was calculated.

The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm was employed to identify the most significant predictors. A penalized regression approach was chosen for variable selection to create an unbiased and highly efficient neonatal seizure risk prediction model by effectively reducing the risk of overfitting [27].

Variables were selected for inclusion in the development of the multivariable neonatal seizure prediction model on the basis of their biologically plausible connection to the outcome and straightforward interpretation in clinical settings. The LASSO model, optimized with the ideal shrinkage factor and the lowest cross-validation mean deviance, was chosen to identify predictors with nonzero coefficients, which were then used for subsequent multivariable logistic regression analysis.

The most significant predictors identified through LASSO regression were included in the multivariable analysis. The variables were then systematically removed from the model in a stepwise manner to create a simplified model, with a significance threshold set at < 0.05. The final simplified risk prediction model was presented as a nomogram, and its performance was evaluated by assessing its discriminatory power and calibration. Discriminatory power was measured via the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, which ranges from 0.5 (indicating no predictive ability) to 1 (representing perfect discrimination) [28, 29].

The model’s calibration was visually represented via a calibration plot and statistically evaluated via the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. A p value greater than 0.05 indicated good model calibration. Additionally, the model’s predictive performance was assessed through a prediction density plot. Internal validation of the model was conducted via bootstrap resampling, generating 1,000 random samples with replacement from the original dataset to calculate a bias-corrected AUC of the ROC curve. Neonates were categorized into high- and low-risk groups for developing neonatal seizures on the basis of the optimal cutoff point identified by the Youden index.

While the model’s performance measures, such as discrimination and calibration, provided insights into its accuracy, they did not address its practical utility in clinical settings. To bridge this gap, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical and public health impact of the model. The DCA plots the net benefit of implementing interventions via the risk prediction model against the threshold probabilities, with the net benefit on the y-axis and threshold probabilities on the x-axis. This analysis compared the outcomes of using the model to intervene in all patients, none, or a selected group on the basis of the model’s predictions. The study adhered to the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines to ensure transparent and comprehensive reporting of the prediction model [30, 31].

Incidence and outcomes of neonatal seizures

The overall incidence of neonatal seizures among admitted neonates in the NICU at comprehensive specialized hospitals in the Amhara region, Northwest Ethiopia, was 21.39% (95% CI: 18.83, 24.18%). The most common seizure in this study was a tonic seizure (53.09%), followed by a subtle seizure (31.96%). Clonic and myoclonic neonatal seizures accounted for 8.76% and 6.19%, respectively, of all seizures. The outcome status of the seizure-positive neonates was 24 (12.37%) who died, 97 (50.00%) who recovered, and 73 (37.63%) who were transferred.

Model diagnostics for variable selection

Using LASSO regression, 64 models were generated through a 10-fold cross-validation LASSO estimator variable selection method. Among these, the 40th model was selected by the LASSO estimator with an optimal penalty factor (lambda) of 0.0032053 and the lowest cross-validation mean deviance of 0.8163182 (Table 2). All the variables included in the LASSO regression analysis were selected as potential predictors for the development of the full multivariable logistic regression model.

Nomogram and its performance

The predictors included and statistically significant in the simplified model were used for the construction of the risk prediction nomogram. These predictors were preterm neonates, phototherapy, subgaleal hemorrhage, perinatal asphyxia, maternal history of seizure and neonatal hypoglycemia. For example, if a neonate was admitted with all the features of these predictors and had a total risk score of 52.2, the probability of developing neonatal seizures was 0.90 according to the nomogram (Fig. 2). The performance of the nomogram was evaluated via the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) of the simplified model, which was found to be 77.736%, 95% CI: 73.7%, 81.4% (Fig. 3).

The overall simplified neonatal seizure risk prediction model’s fitness was also evaluated via the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with a p value of 0.3785. On the basis of the area under the curve (AUC) of each of the variables, the predictive performance of preterm birth, phototherapy, neonatal hypoglycemia, maternal history of seizure, perinatal asphyxia, and subgaleal hemorrhage for neonatal seizures was 62.1%, 60.4%, 56.6%, 61.8%, 62.2%, and 62.4%, respectively. In addition, the calibration plot is almost at a 45-degree angle, indicating that there is no significant difference between the predicted probabilities and the observed probabilities (Fig. 4). Similarly, the probability density plot of the simplified neonatal seizure prediction model of this study assigned probability scores in a definitive manner to patients with and without neonatal seizures. High overlap of the plot of the neonatal seizures and nonseizures means that the model was not perfectly able to distinguish between seizure and nonseizure cases, potentially leading to some false positives or false negatives. A well-separated density plot would indicate a model that assigned high probabilities to true neonatal seizure cases and low probabilities to nonseizure cases (Fig. 5).

Neonatal seizure prediction model validation

The neonatal seizure prediction model was internally validated via bootstrap techniques and had a corrected discriminatory power of 77.735% (95% CI; 73.912%, 81.634%), with an optimism coefficient of 0.0197. A Brier score of 0.1316 suggested that the neonatal seizure prediction model has reasonably good calibration, indicating that its probability estimates are fairly accurate. The calibration plot of the internally validated model revealed that the bias-corrected curve (solid line) closely follows the ideal diagonal line, indicating that the model’s predicted probabilities align well with the actual probabilities. However, there were minor deviations from the ideal line, which was small, suggesting that the model does not significantly overestimate or underestimate risk. The mean absolute error (0.018) was very low, meaning that the model’s probability estimates are highly accurate on average (Fig. 6). The model’s overall accuracy was also explained on the basis of different cutoff point criteria (Table 4). The model accuracy based on the Youden index revealed that the optimal cutoff point criterion was 83.13% (95% CI: 80.53%, 85.51%). This accuracy had a discriminatory power of 77.735% (95% CI: 73.9%, 81.6%) (Fig. 7).

Decision curve analysis

The blue line indicates the risk prediction nomogram developed for assessing neonatal seizure risk, while the three thin gray lines indicate the assumption that all neonates are at risk, and the thick black line suggests that none are at risk. The net benefit was calculated by subtracting the proportion of false positives from the proportion of true positives, considering the relative harm of not intervening versus the negative effects of unnecessary interventions. The accompanying DCA plot demonstrated that utilizing the model for specific interventions, on the basis of the clinical context, yields a greater net benefit than does intervening in all or none of the neonates. The DCA indicated that when the threshold probability lies between 5% and 60%, employing the developed nomogram for clinical decision-making provides more advantages than a universal intervention approach (Fig. 8). Therefore, this DCA suggested that the use of a model in clinical practice could improve neonatal seizure management.

Discussion

This study revealed that the incidence of neonatal seizures among neonates admitted to the NICU at a comprehensive specialized hospital in Northwest Ethiopia was 21.39% (95% CI: 18.83, 24.18%). The most common type of seizure was tonic seizures (53.09%), followed by subtle seizures (31.96%). Additionally, the study identified six key predictors that can be assessed at the time of admission or during hospitalization for neonatal seizure prediction. These factors include preterm birth, maternal history of seizures, subgaleal hemorrhage, perinatal asphyxia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and phototherapy while in the NICU.

The incidence of neonatal seizures in this study was greater than that reported in previous studies conducted at Gondar (5.45%), Ayder (9.6%), and Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals (12.78%) in Ethiopia [17–19]. This variation may be attributed to differences in the study settings. While earlier studies were conducted at single centers, the present study was a multicenter investigation. Although sample size alone may not fully explain such a significant difference, it is worth noting that the sample size in this study was nearly twice as large as that of each of the previous studies. The study’s results suggest that neonatal seizures in Ethiopia occur at a significantly high rate, considering the limitations and potential weaknesses in our diagnostic and screening procedures. Furthermore, this finding exceeds the 16.9% prevalence of neonatal seizures reported in a study from China [32]. The difference can be attributed to variations in study design and settings. While the Chinese study reported the prevalence, our study focused on the incidence rate. Despite this distinction, the greater finding in our study suggests a greater impact of neonatal seizures in our research setting.

However, the findings of this study are lower than those of studies conducted in Nigeria and Kenya, which reported 60% and 39.5% prevalence rates of neonatal seizures, respectively [16, 33]. This significant difference may be attributed to variations in the timing of the studies. At the time of the Nigerian study, there were limited diagnostic and screening resources, facilities, and professional expertise. Overall, both studies revealed a significant prevalence of neonatal seizures in LICs.

The predictive discriminatory power of neonatal seizures found in this study was good, with an AUC of 77.735% after internal validation. This was further supported by an optimism coefficient of 0.0197 and a Brier score of 0.1316. An AUC of 77.735% indicated that the model is able to correctly discriminate between neonatal seizures and those without neonatal seizures with a reasonable level of accuracy. The internal validation results with the optimism coefficient and the Brier score suggest that it is both reliable and effective for predicting neonatal seizures with the predefined level of risk prediction threshold identified by the Youden index [34].

The neonatal seizure prediction nomogram was developed via six predictors (displayed in the nomogram) that can be collected from each neonate admitted to the NICU of comprehensive specialized hospitals in Ethiopia and other low-income countries. Using these predictors, the risk of neonatal seizures can be predicted with up to 83% accuracy on the basis of the neonate’s presentation and follow-up with these indicators, given that the mean absolute error of the model was low (0.018). Similarly, these predictors have been identified in previous studies as risk factors or associations for neonatal seizures [2, 3, 6, 7, 11, 17–19, 21, 32]. Therefore, on the basis of this up-to-date information, clinicians can identify neonates with a higher or lower risk of neonatal seizures on the basis of their clinical presentation. Additionally, when this nomogram is used, the risk of neonatal seizures can be identified as low as 10% to as high as 90% of the threshold probability of risk. The sensitivity and specificity of the nomogram, which are reasonably good, were determined via the optimal cutoff point criterion identified by the Youden index. The high- and low-risk levels of neonatal seizures were accurately determined via the constructed nomogram. Furthermore, the calibration and prediction density plots of the nomogram demonstrated strong alignment between the predicted and observed outcomes.

However, external validation of this clinical prediction model for neonatal seizures is necessary. This involves evaluating the model via data that are independent of the dataset used during model development [35]. This could be done at different times with the same population, place (other low-income countries of the same set up) and different subsets of the population in these LICs. This should include internal validation, model calibration, discrimination, and overall performance via the TRIPOD guidelines [31].

The clinical utility of the nomogram, which was constructed with six predictors, was assessed via DCA. The nomogram’s probability of risk prediction ranges from 5% to 90%, with a total risk score of 52.2, which is derived from the sum of each of the predictors included in the model. The DCA evaluates the net benefit of using the nomogram for clinical intervention compared with the “treat all” or “treat none” strategies. The results from the DCA revealed that the nomogram provides the highest net benefit at the threshold probability of risk (10% to 90%) compared with both low (< 10%) and high (> 90%) thresholds of probability. Therefore, applying this neonatal risk prediction nomogram in the NICU of comprehensive specialized hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia could provide significant net benefits for neonates with seizure risk scores between 0.1 and 0.9. The use of DCA to assess the clinical utility of prediction models is more important than the use of sensitivity and specificity metrics, as it reveals the net benefits of applying clinical interventions for true positives and avoiding interventions for false positives across various threshold probabilities.

Limitations of the study

This prediction modeling study has several limitations. First, it utilized secondary data, which do not include certain personal-level predictors, such as parents’ sociodemographic status, preconception care services, and variables related to healthcare professionals in the NICU. These elements may significantly affect individual risk prediction for neonatal seizures. Second, the ascertainment of the outcome variable was based solely on the physician’s judgment, without the support of EEG data. Additionally, there is a need for external validation to evaluate the study’s generalizability and clinical utility in similar and different healthcare settings. Furthermore, owing to the scarcity of prior research in this field, the consistency of the findings was not thoroughly analyzed. Instead, the study primarily emphasized the clinical implications in a descriptive manner.

Conclusion and implications

The incidence of neonatal seizures among neonates admitted to the NICU in comprehensive specialized hospitals in the Amhara region, Northwest Ethiopia, was relatively high. The predictive power of the nomogram, which is based on predictors such as preterm birth, maternal history of seizures, subgaleal hemorrhage, perinatal asphyxia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and phototherapy, was good, with reasonable accuracy, an optimism coefficient, and the Brier score. Despite the limitations specified, its net clinical benefit exceeds that of the “treat all” and “treat none” strategies. Therefore, we recommend that clinicians exercise caution in clinical practice and that further external validation of the prediction model be pursued by researchers.
