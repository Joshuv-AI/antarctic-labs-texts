Surgical repair of a ventricular septal defect following low-velocity blunt chest trauma
Authors: Yuanqi Yang, Xuan Zhou, Hong Tang, Xin Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVentricular septal defect (VSD) after blunt chest trauma is a rare form of heart damage.Case presentationWe present the case of a 14-year-old male student who developed a VSD after low-velocity blunt chest trauma. The defect was successfully closed via elective surgical repair. Two months post-surgery, echocardiography revealed no residual shunt, and no obvious abnormalities in pulmonary artery pressure or biventricular function.ConclusionEven in cases of low-velocity chest trauma, significant injuries such as traumatic VSD should be considered. Close interpretation of atypical physiological findings should prompt a thorough diagnostic evaluation. Factors such as the timing of surgery and the choice of surgical method are crucial in planning operative interventions.

Background

Ventricular septal defects (VSDs) are commonly known as congenital heart malformations. However, VSDs can be caused not only by sharp injuries, but also by blunt chest trauma, deceleration injury, ischemic necrosis of the ventricular septum due to coronary artery injury or acute thrombosis, and so on [1]. Among these, VSDs caused by blunt chest trauma are particularly rare.

Main treatment methods include surgical repair and percutaneous interventional closure. However, closures of smaller VSDs are often delayed to reduce operative time, decrease operative mortality risk, and allow for natural repair [2]. We present the case of a 14-year-old male with a large muscular VSD secondary to blunt chest trauma, who subsequently underwent elective surgical repair. This case highlights an optimal surgical strategy according to the patient’s situations.

Case

A 14-year-old male with no history of any cardiovascular disease accidentally collided with a door, resulting in chest pain and syncope. Shortly thereafter, he regained consciousness, experiencing dizziness without other symptoms such as chest tightness, shortness of breath, visual impairment, abdominal pain, or diarrhea. He was transferred from his local hospital to our hospital for treatment. The patient and his parents denied any medical, family or psycho-social history. The physical examination showed that the left heart border was enlarged, and the rhythm was regular. A grade 4/6 systolic murmur was heard in the 3rd/4th intercostal space of the left sternal border, which was transmitted toward the apex with a frontal shock. Other physical examination findings were normal. A 12- lead ECG showed sinus rhythm without abnormalities. Emergency chest CT scan revealed slightly lower transparency, diffuse distribution of blurred plaques, thickening of some interlobular septa in both lungs, slight thickening of some bronchial walls, and significant enlargement of the left ventricle. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) indicated enlargement of the left heart, progressive thinning of the anterior septum (about 4 mm thick), 10 mm below the aortic valve and about 13 mm in the weak area. In the weak area, the echo of the lower part (middle and lower part, not reaching the apex) was interrupted by about 23 mm. There were several incomplete residual ventricular septum on the right ventricular side. The upper ventricular septum and apical thickness were normal, and the posterior wall of the left ventricle was mildly thickened. Except for the weak area of the ventricular septum, the amplitude of left ventricular wall was normal. No pericardial effusion was detected. Moreover, there were no obvious abnormalities in the proximal segment of the coronary arteries. Doppler examination showed multiple left-to-right septal shunts in the muscular ventricular septal defect, with Vmax = 4.0 m/s and PG = 64 mmHg, and mild tricuspid regurgitation with Vmax = 3.3 m/s and PG = 42 mmHg. Therefore, the diagnosis was muscular VSD due to blunt trauma with a left-to-right shunt at the ventricular level and tricuspid regurgitation (mild). Meanwhile, left ventricular systolic function was normal with LVEF = 65% (Fig. 1, Video 1–3). Coronary CT confirmed normal left and right coronary arteries. One month later, the patient returned for surgical repair. 3D TTE showed an enlarged left heart, an irregular VSD with 26 × 16 mm, and elevated pulmonary artery systolic pressure (The Vmax and PG of tricuspid regurgitation were 4.3 m/s and 73 mmHg, respectively) (Fig. 2, Video 4). The left ventricular systolic function was normal, with an LVEF of 58%. The subsequent day, surgical repair was performed. The ventricular defect was closed using continuous + intermittent suture with Dacron polyester patches, and we used autologous pericardial pledgets to support the sutures in such oedematous myocardium. Scattered defects were identified in the inferior aspect of the ventricular septum, located 10 mm below the aortic valve. The largest defect measured 20 × 16 mm, while the smallest was 0.5 mm in diameter. Intraoperative TEE showed tno residual shunt in the ventricular septal defect. The following day, bedside TTE showed that the left heart was smaller than that before surgery and the pulmonary artery systolic pressure was significantly decreased (The Vmax of tricuspid regurgitation was 2.0 m/s, and the PG was 16 mmHg). Two months post-operation, the patient remained asymptomatic. TTE demonstrated that the ventricular septum was continuous, no significant residual shunt was observed at the ventricular level and no pulmonary artery pressure was detected (The Vmax of tricuspid regurgitation was 2.2 m/s, and the PG was 19 mmHg) (Fig. 3). Left ventricular systolic function was slightly decreased with LVEF = 48%. Discussion

This case illustrates a 14-year-old male with moderate to large muscular VSD secondary to blunt chest trauma, which is a notable rare occurrence. Blunt chest trauma may result in a variety of cardiac injuries, including stress cardiomyopathy, myocardial infarction, valvular lesions, cardiac contusion, free wall rupture, and VSD, some of which are self-limited while others have high mortality rates. Most previously reported cases are mainly related with high-speed incidents such as traffic accidents. In contrast, our case may broaden the understanding of the range of trauma-related VSD, suggesting that even less forceful impacts can result in severe cardiac injuries.

The mechanism of traumatic VSD may be related to mechanical compression of the heart between the sternum and spine, leading to a concomitant increase in end-diastolic/early systolic intrathoracic pressure. This may prompt myocardial contusion, coronary dissection, or adventitial hemorrhage. Given the absence of ischemic changes on the ECG, myocardial contusion seems to be the probable cause of the ventricular septal rupture. Additionally, in our case, the pathophysiology of how low-velocity trauma can lead to such critical injuries as VSD may involve pre-existing weaknesses in the septal wall or the presence of unexpectedly high impact pressure, even at low velocity.

In this case, the echocardiographic findings of traumatic VSD are different from VSD with ventricular septal perforation after myocardial infarction (Table 2). Some reports document spontaneous closure of small traumatic VSDs that do not lead to heart failure [4]. Although small VSDs can close spontaneously, large VSDs pose a high mortality risk and potential for late cardiovascular complications including pulmonary hypertension if not timely repaired [5]. Some previous studies indicate better outcomes following surgical intervention compared to non-surgical managements [6, 7]. Most patients opt for elective surgery rather than emergency procedures, suggesting more favorable timing for intervention. In this case, initial echocardiography revealed significant myocardial edema, with the patient hemodynamically stable, hence elective surgical repair was chosen. In patients with hemodynamic stability, delayed surgical repair is usually recommended to allow development of fibrous margins around the VSD [8]. In cases where myocardial edema or tricuspid valve structure obscures VSD margins, specific surgical techniques may enhance exposure [9, 10].

Conclusion

The VSD after blunt chest trauma is a rare form of cardiac injury. Major injuries such as traumatic VSD should be considered following low-velocity blunt trauma. Attention to atypical physiological findings should prompt comprehensive diagnostic evaluations. Clinicians should recognize the importance of careful monitoring and follow-up diagnostics even if initial trauma seems low-impact. Timing and surgical methods are also important factors for ensuring cardiac repair.
