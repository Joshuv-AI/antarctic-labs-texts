Authors: Amal M T Ababneh, Ruqayya Zeilani
Published: 2025

Introduction

People who work in caregiving professions—especially nursing—are susceptible to stress known as compassion fatigue (CF); to be specific, CF includes burnout and secondary traumatic stress.1 2 CF can result from prolonged exposure to patients’ suffering as well as from the demanding nature of the healthcare environment. Nurses are particularly susceptible to CF because they often have strong emotional ties with their patients.3 As a consequence of CF, turnover rates among nursing staff may rise and job satisfaction may decline due to CF. Furthermore, CF causes emotional exhaustion, diminished empathy and a compromised ability to provide compassionate care.3 4

Addressing the issue of CF is of utmost importance for several reasons. First, it safeguards the mental health of nurses and improves their job performance; this, in turn, ensures a high standard of the care and outcomes that patients expect. Moreover, communicating about CF among nurses can reduce their burnout and turnover rates; in other words, job satisfaction may enhance and the issue of nursing staff shortage may be resolved.5,8 Furthermore, CF might hinder nurses’ ability to advocate for patients; eventually, this can result in health inequity.9 In other words, nurses’ emotional exhaustion can lead to less communication with patients, which disproportionately affects underprivileged patients.10 11 To reiterate, emotional exhaustion might lead nurses to unconsciously use cognitive shortcuts, and to rely on patient-related stereotypes, which can contribute to inequities in care.12,14 Finally, CF can result in emotional exhaustion, which can impair nurses’ capacity to interact empathetically with patients; once again, this affects the quality of care that patients need.12,15

As mentioned in the preceding paragraphs, nurses typically provide compassionate care since they frequently witness illness, suffering and death in the course of their work in the medical field. To be specific, compassionate care entails having an inner sense of another person’s suffering and being motivated to alleviate that suffering.16 Usually, nurses establish therapeutic connections with patients and their families in various settings, including dialysis, medical-surgical, home health, oncology, palliative and intensive care units. In addition to providing skillful and competent care, nurses also provide compassion, spiritual support and emotional support, all of which are based on decades of nursing science and evidence-based practices.17

It became clear that the involved stakeholders must act to support nurses’ well-being, as nurses play a key role in promoting health equity and ensuring the quality of care provided.18 Nurses’ well-being may be improved by a number of interventions. Particularly, these interventions assist nurses in managing their stress and improving their professional lives. This, in turn, enhances the quality of patient care and health outcomes.9

All of the theories that address needs, personality, values, emotions, cognition, behaviour, environment and environmentalism are incorporated into the framework of contemporary research on work motivation.19 Motivation is effectively defined by Moody and Pesut20 as a “values-based, psycho-biologically stimulus-driven inner urge that activates and guides human behavior in response to self, other, and environment, supporting intrinsic satisfaction and leading to the intentional fulfilment of basic human drives, perceived needs, and desired goals”.20

Employee motivation has been at the forefront of management theory and practice since the 20th century. The mid-1960s marked the start of the ‘golden age’ of work motivation theory and research.21 For instance, McCloskey22 and Meir23brought attention to the issue of nurses’ motivation in the 1970s because these researchers were interested in predicting the persistence and sustainability of nurses in their jobs. These two studies were based on the idea that people have needs and that meeting those needs is the purpose of life. It is worth noting that Maslow first proposed the work motivation theory in the 1950s, and it was further developed by other theorists.24,26 This theory is deemed crucial; particularly, finding the factors influencing nurses’ motivation is believed to help prevent nurses’ burnout and dissatisfaction,27 and prevent their intentions to quit.28 Practically speaking, it has been demonstrated that nurses’ motivation is essential for their intent to work29 and essential for their job satisfaction.30,32

In order to boost nurses’ motivation, various approaches were suggested in the literature. The present study highlights the approach of motivational messages (MMs), which usually include brief and positive statements. MMs are claimed to inspire people and motivate them to remain focused and dedicated to their goals. In addition, MMs are claimed to give the emotional support needed to overcome obstacles, and also give high spirits and strengthen confidence.33 In short, MMs are intended to boost spirits, provide emotional support and reinforce the value and importance of the nursing profession. Practically speaking, MMs were found to lessen the effects of CF and help nurses manage the emotional demands of their career by creating a sense of purpose and providing consistent support and encouragement.34 35 Therefore, the present systematic literature review aims to assess the efficacy of MMs on nurses’ professional quality of life and well-being.

Purpose

The present systematic review has three main purposes. First, it aims to determine MMs and protocols that have been demonstrated to improve healthcare professionals’ work capacity, compassion satisfaction, well-being, perceived overall health, productivity and job satisfaction. Second, this review aims to determine whether MMs—that are directed to healthcare professionals—have the ability to lower CF, lower secondary traumatic stress, lower psychosocial stresses and lower job burnout. Third and finally, the present review aims to collect the existing MM texts and evaluate the extent to which they can be used to support and motivate nurses.

Review question

How successfully do the MMs affect the well-being, CF and compassion satisfaction of nurses and other healthcare professionals?

Literature methodology

Following PROSPERO registration (CRD42024576029), pertinent studies were collected from five databases. Particularly, all of the studies that discussed MM protocols and how MMs affect nurses were included. These studies were then reviewed for eligibility and data were retrieved. The researchers in this study adhered to the standards of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA). PRISMA is specifically used for the systematic review of randomised controlled trials (RCTs).36 Furthermore, the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) approach was employed to conduct the systematic review and synthesise the evidence, thereby ensuring the study’s rigour and quality.37

Search strategy

An electronic search was conducted—between 1 June and 25 July 2024—to collect primary and peer-reviewed research publications. Search filters were used to restrict the results to peer-reviewed papers published in English during the previous 10 years. The researchers used certain keywords and made use of five databases, namely, CINAHL, SCOPUS, PubMed, Google Scholar and the Cochrane Library. Next, a manual search was conducted in the reference lists of included studies to find other pertinent research publications.

A comprehensive search strategy was employed to identify relevant studies on the effectiveness of MMs in managing CF among nurses. Particularly, combinations of keywords were simultaneously used; these keywords were “empathy”, “job satisfaction”, “compassion fatigue”, “nurses”, “nursing staff”, “motivational messages”, “motivational interventions”, “emotional exhaustion” and “well-being”. Finally, the search was refined using Boolean operators like AND, OR and NOT (online supplemental appendix A).

Eligibility criteria

The final selection of this systematic review articles was carried out using the following criteria: (i) the articles should be published in peer-reviewed journals; (ii) the articles should be published in the English language; (iii) the articles should target nurses or healthcare professionals; (iv) the articles should examine the effectiveness of using MMs in managing CF, well-being, burnout, secondary traumatic stress and job satisfaction and (v) the articles should be published between the years 2014 and 2024. On the other hand, the exclusion criteria ruled out (i) preprints, theoretical papers, opinion pieces or editorials and also ruled out (ii) studies that did not include full text.

Data extraction and management

A detailed and systematic approach was used to select studies to ensure the inclusion of relevant and high-quality research. The authors’ initial task was screening titles and abstracts of all retrieved studies against the stated inclusion and exclusion criteria. Then, as a preliminary screening step, the full texts of possibly relevant research were retrieved and evaluated for ultimate inclusion. The studies found, screened, assessed for eligibility and included in the review, as well as the reasons for exclusion at each stage, were all documented in detail using a PRISMA flow diagram. At crucial stages of the review process, the authors asked faculty members to engage in peer-review or consult with an adviser in order to add further rigour.

To systematically extract relevant data from all included research, the authors used a standardised form. This section covered in detail the study’s characteristics (eg, author, publication year, country, study design), participant details (eg, sample size, demographic factors), intervention specifics (eg, type and content of MMs, duration, frequency), comparison groups (if applicable), outcome measures related to CF (eg, emotional exhaustion, empathy, job satisfaction) and the main findings. The authors tracked and arranged the retrieved data using data management tools such as EndNote or Excel to guarantee correctness and consistency. A comprehensive synthesis of the evidence was facilitated by this structured approach, which enabled the authors to systematically capture and analyse all relevant information. The authors periodically examined and double-checked the extracted data to guarantee accuracy and to lower the possibility of mistakes.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias in the included studies in this systematic review were assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal tools. Moreover, the researchers evaluated the reliability, practicability, relevance and outcomes of each included study. Particularly, the researchers deployed a number of essential elements like the suitability of the study design, the coherence of the research questions, the reliability of the data collection techniques and the transparency of the data analysis, all of which served as a structured and comprehensive approach.38 Furthermore, the researchers conducted independent evaluations for each included publication, focusing on possible sources of bias such as performance, detection, attrition and selection bias.

In this review, the researchers made use of JBI Critical Appraisal tools, as recommended by the JBI manual. Such tools are demonstrated to assist in assessing the trustworthiness, relevance, results and risk of bias of published papers.38 39 To be specific, the researchers used two appraisal tools; namely, RCTs and analytical cross-sectional studies. It is worth noting that each JBI Critical Appraisal checklist has a series of items intended to assess key aspects of study quality.38 39 In accordance with JBI guidelines, the authors independently evaluated each group of study items separately and marked responses as ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘unclear’ or ‘not applicable (N/A)’; the ‘N/A’ was used when an item was not relevant to the specific design or context of the study. Then, the findings were interpreted and informed based on the appraisal outcomes. It is worth noting that discrepancies in ratings were resolved through discussion between the reviewers. Finally, if consensus could not be reached, advice was sought from a faculty member experienced in systematic reviews to ensure further methodological rigour (table 1).

Study synthesis

In order to synthesise and evaluate the data, the authors created a narrative-descriptive synthesis of the evidence, including the main findings and consistency of findings across studies. The synthesis process of the extracted data involved collecting and summarising the findings of the included studies. Finally, several tables were created to present the results.

Results

Study synthesis and data extraction

The search process yielded 275 published studies. After excluding duplicate studies, 136 studies remained for screening. Abstract screening identified 53 eligible studies. Then, 48 studies were excluded because they did not fulfil the inclusion criteria. Finally, five studies remained for the narrative synthesis process (figure 1).3435 41,43

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram of selected studies.

Furthermore, a comprehensive systematic review was conducted, including a thorough evaluation of the five studies that met the specific inclusion criteria. Four of these selected studies were classified as randomised controlled studies, while the remaining one was categorised as a cross-sectional study. Across these five studies, text messaging was identified as the primary intervention used to assess the well-being of healthcare professionals.

Selected studies descriptions

In this systematic review, the main characteristics of the carefully chosen studies are shown in online supplemental appendix B. For instance, the included studies provided a comprehensive overview of the effects of motivational messaging on healthcare professionals, encompassing various research methods and contexts. Many countries, including Iran, Turkey and Canada, have conducted studies that primarily use RCTs to investigate how such interventions affect nurses’ overall well-being, occupational burnout, CF, communication skills, job stress and job satisfaction. To guarantee the results’ validity and reliability, these studies included intervention groups and control groups, with sample sizes varying from 60 to 87 people. Motivational or health-related text messages were typically sent over a period of 3 weeks to 3 months. Moreover, the included studies used validated scales (such as the OSIPOW Job Stress Questionnaire, Job Satisfaction Scale, Compassion Fatigue Scale and Satisfaction with Life Scale) to measure the interventions’ effectiveness. Furthermore, the included studies provided strong comparisons of the effects by the consistent use of pre-intervention and post-intervention assessments. These assessments added to the growing body of evidence supporting the effectiveness of MMs in reducing work-related stress and enhancing psychological well-being in healthcare settings.34 35 41 42

In these included studies, the impact of text-based interventions was highlighted in relation to a number of factors that enhance occupational health and well-being among healthcare professionals.43 In one of these included studies, the authors used a longitudinal cross-sectional methodology in addition to RCTs to evaluate user satisfaction with text messaging software among Canadian public safety professionals.43 In addition, they emphasised the intervention’s high satisfaction rate and its reported advantages, particularly reduced anxiety, reduced depression and improved coping skills for loneliness.

Risk of bias

In this systematic review, the risk of bias assessment showed a low to moderate risk of bias for the included RCTs, indicating an overall high quality of evidence. For instance, the internal validity of the results was guaranteed by constantly reporting true randomisation and allocation concealment for each study. Additionally, all studies used reliable and structured outcome assessments, and appropriate statistical analyses were conducted. Moreover, the baseline similarity was maintained in all of the experiments to ensure that the intervention groups and control groups were similar at the beginning of the trials. Furthermore, most RCTs did intervention blinding, which is a critical methodological technique used in research, especially in trials, to minimise bias and ensure the credibility of study results.

However, while intervention blinding was accomplished in most RCTs, participant and outcome assessment blinding was frequently ambiguous, potentially adding bias. Moreover, there was a low likelihood of attrition bias since follow-up completeness was reported as high. It should also be reported that the robustness of the results may be impacted by the unclear use of intention-to-treat analysis in some studies. Finally, due to ambiguous assessment validity and reliability and due to a lack of confounding factor management measures, the one cross-sectional study that was part of the review showed a low quality of evidence.

Despite these minor limitations, the reliability of the results (on the effectiveness of MMs in reducing work-related stress and enhancing psychological outcomes among healthcare professionals) was supported by the overall high quality of the data.34 35 41 42

Synthesis of results

The synthesis of results from the included studies revealed several key findings (reported below) regarding the impact of motivational and health messages on healthcare professionals.

Standardisation of MMs intervention protocol based on the included RCTs

Motivational or health-related text messages were sent to healthcare professionals as part of the RCT interventions. For instance, in the study by Esbakiyan Bandpey et al,41 the intervention involved the consistent delivery of health messages by SMS at a frequency of two messages per week for 3 months. A similar strategy was used by Heravi-Karimooi et al,42 who sent nurses two text messages per week for 3 months. By comparison, Köse et al35 and Goktas et al34 carried out shorter and more intense interventions. Over 21 days, Goktas et al34 sent out MMs three times a day at predetermined periods (07:00, 12:00 and 16:00 hours). Köse et al35 made use of a similar timetable; he sent out messages for 21 days at 09:00, 12:00, 17:00 and 19:00 hours.

These variations in duration and frequency (tailored to different settings and needs) reflected a standardised yet adaptable protocol. This protocol demonstrates the flexibility and potential efficacy of text-based interventions in managing healthcare professionals’ stress and well-being (table 3). According to the data presented, two protocols for the MMs intervention were identified: an intensive protocol, which delivers messages three to four times per day over a short periodof up to 21 days; and an extensive protocol, which delivers two messages per week over a longer duration of up to 3 months.

Impact of MMs on the healthcare professionals’ work

Impact on job stress

The included studies found that the reception of motivational or health-related text messages significantly reduced job stress among intensive care unit (ICU) nurses. For instance, Esbakiyan Bandpey et al41 found that ICU nurses reported a notable decrease in job stress levels by using the extensive protocol of motivational and health messages. Similarly, ICU nurses who received well-chosen SMS messages (at a comparable frequency and length) showed a substantial reduction in job stress and burnout,42 and also showed enhanced overall mental health and well-being.43

Impact on Job satisfaction and communication skills

According to Goktas et al34, unlike the control group, the MMs significantly enhanced the communication skills and job satisfaction of emergency nurses in the intervention group, which included delivering MMs three times a day for 21 days. The results of the intensive protocol of MMs showed an increased level of optimism and life satisfaction, and showed a decreased level of hopelessness among the intervention group.35

Impact on compassion fatigue

Goktas et al34 showed that the CF significantly decreased among emergency nurses who received MMs. Unlike the control group, the intervention group’s nurses showed a substantially reduced CF after receiving MMs three times a day for 21 days. In a similar study, during the COVID-19 epidemic, nurses were susceptible to CF; nevertheless, MMs improved the nurses’ job satisfaction and also reduced their sense of hopelessness.35

Impact on occupational burnout and quality of work life

As an instance, in a study by Heravi-Karimooi et al,42 the researcher found substantial increases in quality of work life (QWL) and decreases in occupational burnout among ICU nurses who regularly received motivational text messages. This shows that well-designed MMs may significantly improve the QWL of nurses and may mitigate the negative effects of these nurses’ burnout. Therefore, the employment of such extensive interventions may have promising results in the future.

The content of the motivational text messages on the professional quality of life

Given that MM content has a big influence on thoughts, feelings and behaviour, it promotes beneficial outcomes. By focusing on promoting resilience, self-care practices and motivation towards achieving personal or professional goals, these messages are designed to uplift, inspire and provide practical guidance. Moreover, MMs may successfully support adaptive coping skills and may maintain a feeling of purpose by addressing specific obstacles or stresses (such as emotional strain or weariness) that individuals may encounter. To reiterate, MMs are deemed significant since they connect with the recipients by providing timely reminders, emotional support and empowerment. This, in turn, helps overcome obstacles, helps retain motivation and helps enhance long-term well-being and productivity. Discussion

This systematic review evaluated the effectiveness of MMs on work-related stress among healthcare professionals. Although many articles have been published in the same field, the review targeted only five articles published between 2014 and 2024, all of which met the review’s inclusion criteria. These five studies included four RCTs and one cross-sectional study, all of which have investigated and tested the effectiveness of MMs on work-related stress among healthcare professionals. The analysis of the review identified a number of characteristics that may be used to characterise the ways in which MMs can help healthcare professionals who are experiencing stress at work.

Generally speaking, it can be claimed that mobile phone text messaging is an affordable, accessible and effective way to provide guidance and counselling in different domains.44 This finding aligns with the results of the current review. For example, a study conducted in Spain demonstrated the positive effects of teachers’ engaging messages on students’ motivation to learn, as well as on their academic performance.45 This finding aligns with the results of the present review, where using MMs as an intervention to manage job stress levels and enhance job performance was effective among ICU nurses, who reported a notable decrease in job stress levels and enhanced their job performance.41 42 Furthermore, the impact of MMs was consistent with the findings of three cross-sectional studies, in which MMs were sent to subscribers over varying durations (ranging from 6 weeks to 3 months). These studies demonstrated positive effects on participants’ levels of stress, anxiety, depression, psychological safety and overall well-being.46,48

To reiterate, the findings of this review highlight the potential of such interventions to enhance the well-being of nursing staff. The review demonstrated that frequent, structured MMs can successfully reduce job-related stress in high-pressure healthcare environments. These findings align with a cross-sectional study that reported a negative correlation between motivation and depression, fatigue and turnover intention. Conversely, motivation was positively correlated with overall health and job satisfaction.49 Moreover, according to Spoorthy et al,50 healthcare personnel’s motivation was boosted by factors such as a supportive workplace, positive role models and recognition from peers and patients.50 These elements contributed to improved mental health and well-being among nurses by reducing despondency and increasing optimism and job satisfaction.

According to the results of the current study, nurses can effectively boost job satisfaction and improve their communication skills if they are motivated with regular and well-timed motivational messaging, potentially leading to better overall job performance and patient care quality. In a study on Empowerment Predicting Nurses’ Work Motivation and Occupational Mental Health, it was found that structural and psychological empowerment increases nurses’ work motivation while also improving their mental health by reducing stress and improving job outcomes.51 These findings are consistent with those of the current study, which highlight the positive impact of motivation on improving nurses’ job-related outcomes. Additionally, a scoping review of nurses’ work motivation revealed that nurses can be motivated to enhance their communication skills and increase their job satisfaction.52 These findings are consistent with the results of the current study.

It can be inferred that mitigating the emotional exhaustion and stress associated with CF could be reduced by frequent MMs, which can ultimately support the mental well-being of nurses who work in high-stress environments such as the emergency departments. These results highlighted the potential of mindfulness-based intervention to support compassionate care and to improve resilience in stressful healthcare settings. Congruently, a systematic review53 examined mindfulness-based practices and their potential to improve healthcare workers’ emotional exhaustion, stress, well-being and professional satisfaction. It revealed notable improvements in resilience, quality of life, work engagement and well-being; it also revealed decreases in burnout, perceived stress, anxiety and depression.

To sum up, the results of this review emphasise that fostering a more supportive and sustainable work environment in stressful work settings could be achieved by such interventions, ultimately benefiting both the healthcare providers and their patients. Further, numerous studies have shown that nurses’ and other healthcare professionals’ motivation and support eventually affect the overall quality level of patient care and patient outcomes.1728 34 35 41,43 49

Limitations

The results in the present review might have been affected by some limitations. The ability to draw conclusions is diminished by the possibility that a limited sample of participants may not accurately reflect the larger body of the population. Additionally, the included studies used diverse protocols (eg, variable frequency, format and duration) to convey MMs. This diversity can make it challenging to compare results across research, which may lower the reliability and consistency of conclusions. Moreover, the varied quality and designs of the included studies limit the review’s ability to draw definitive conclusions about causality and generalisability. Deeper analysis was further restricted by inconsistent reporting of important confounders (such as demographics, specialisation and access to technology) and variations in message delivery. Finally, excluding non-English studies may result in overestimating or underestimating the truthful effect due to the lack of varied sources.

Conclusion

This systematic review’s findings highlight the potential of MMs as a beneficial intervention for reducing work-related stress among healthcare employees. Underscoring the significance of timely and customised motivational support in healthcare settings, the examined studies continuously showed positive impacts on stress reduction and other related issues. It can be claimed that healthcare institutions may create a positive workplace environment, which encourages resilience and improves employee well-being. This can be accomplished by using technology platforms and then incorporating MMs into routine operations.

Recommendations and implications

To effectively reduce job-related stress, it is essential to suggest incorporating MMs into the work environment of healthcare employees. The unique stresses frequently present in healthcare settings, such as emotional difficulties and task constraints, should be addressed in these messages. Messages should be customised using culturally appropriate symbols and language to be aligned with the recipients’ cultural values. Moreover, healthcare institutions should seek advice from local leaders and adapt the content in a way that equally highlights the team accomplishments and individual accomplishments. This, in turn, maintains inclusivity and acknowledges the diverse experiences of the nursing staff.

Moreover, it should be noted that MMs can have a more significant impact and can be more accessible when they are frequently sent through accessible channels such as mobile applications. Furthermore, it is necessary to provide training courses to educate medical personnel on the benefits of MMs in stress reduction, and on the proper deployment of these MMs. Finally, future research should concentrate on longitudinal studies to evaluate the long-term benefits of MMs in lowering work-related stress and in enhancing well-being among healthcare employees.
