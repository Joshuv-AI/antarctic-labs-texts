Obituary: Jose Manuel García‐Verdugo (1954–2025), an Exceptional Eye Into the Fine Structure of the Brain's Germinal Centers
Authors: Arantxa Cebrian‐Silla, Mercedes Paredes, Arturo Alvarez‐Buylla
Published: 2025

Under the electron microscope, one gazes upon the hidden constellations of the cell's inner world: tiny ribosomes scattered like distant stars, organelles orbiting the nucleus like planets around the sun, all held together in perfect harmony by the cytoskeleton. Much like the images of space, electron microscopy (EM) captures a frozen moment of our cells’ universal dance. Such complexity is not easily deciphered. Only the truly trained and devoted few can glimpse the dynamic nature behind these frozen moments and extract new meaning. As Santiago Ramón y Cajal beautifully put it: “It is not enough to examine, we also need to contemplate. Let us infuse the things we observe with emotion and affection; let us make them our own with both our hearts and our intellects.” Jose Manuel García‐Verdugo, or “Verdugo” as everyone knew him, was one of those rare scientists who could make this leap, to contemplate and become immersed in the ultrastructure of the central nervous system. His electron microscopic images and inferences unlocked a new understanding of the germinal centers in the adult vertebrate brain. The beloved professor, colleague, friend, and teacher (Figure 1) died at the age of 70 in Valencia, Spain.

Verdugo was born in Ceuta, located on the North African coast. He earned his degree in Biological Sciences from the University of La Laguna in Tenerife and completed his PhD at the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona. He began his scientific career focusing on the neuroanatomy of the reptilian brain. Early on, he developed an expertise in EM and showed an extraordinary ability to identify the brain's cytoarchitecture and cellular components with remarkable precision. Working with his thesis advisor, Carlos Lopez‐Garcia, Verdugo discovered that new neurons are generated in the adult reptilian brain (Garcia‐Verdugo et al. 1986, 1989) and can contribute to brain regeneration (López‐García et al. 1992; Font et al. 1997). These findings emerged at a time when the field of adult brain neurogenesis was in full resurgence. Driven by the curiosity to explore whether adult neurogenesis occurred in species beyond reptiles, Verdugo began a long‐term collaboration with one of us (A.A.‐B.) to investigate the mechanism of neuronal birth and neuronal migration in the adult mammalian and avian brains. His ultrastructural observations were key to the discovery of neuronal chain migration (Figure 2a) (Lois et al. 1996) and to the identification of the cell types and their organization within the main germinal centers of the adult rodent brain: the ventricular‐subventricular zone (V‐SVZ) (Doetsch et al. 1997) on the walls of the lateral ventricles and the subgranular zone (SGZ) of the hippocampus. His observations at the electron microscope were also critical to the identification of neural stem cells (NSCs), the primary progenitors for the generation of new neurons in adult brains (Figure 2b,c) (Doetsch et al. 1999; Seri et al. 2001). His earlier observations in adult lizards, characterizing the cellular composition of the ventricular epithelium (Garcia‐Verdugo et al. 1981), paved the way to the characterization of adult NSC niches, both in birds (Alvarez‐Buylla et al. 1998, 1990) and rodents (Doetsch et al. 1997). Proliferating radial glia in adult birds and lizards were suggested to correspond to the NSCs giving rise to the new neurons (Alvarez‐Buylla et al. 1998, 1990; Garcia‐Verdugo et al. 2002). In rodents, the basal process of radial glia retracts, and they transform into local astrocytes; Verdugo's careful EM analysis identified the two types of astroglial cells (B1 and B2 cells) that later work showed corresponded to the NSCs (Doetsch et al. 1999; Mirzadeh et al. 2008; Cebrian‐Silla et al. 2025). He also contributed to the demonstration that the multiciliated ependymal cells in the lateral ventricles are mostly born in the embryo and do not function as NSCs in the adult (Spassky et al. 2005).

In the SGZ of the hippocampus, Verdugo's EM work contributed to identifying NSCs as a type of astroglial cell with a prominent radial process that traverses the dentate gyrus (Figure 2c). We referred to these cells as radial astrocytes, later termed Type 1 progenitors or radial glial‐like cells in other studies (Seri et al. 2004, 2001; Filippov et al. 2003; Kempermann et al. 2015; Moss et al. 2016). Through a systematic serial section analysis of the rodent SGZ, Verdugo identified that, in addition to the radial process that profusely ramifies in the molecular layer, radial astrocytes also had horizontal processes that nestle intermediate progenitors. Verdugo's EM analysis provided strong evidence that cells previously identified as astrocytes correspond to the primary progenitors in the SGZ and V‐SVZ, evidence later fully confirmed by more recent studies (Ming and Song 2005; Kriegstein et al. 2020). His observations revealed that, beyond the function as NSCs, B1 and B2 cells in the V‐SVZ and radial astrocytes in the SGZ, likely have important support “astroglial” functions in the homeostasis of the germinal niches and immediate surroundings.

Verdugo's observations are also key in the current debate on whether neurogenesis continues in the adult human brain. In the middle of the 90s, a study suggested that the V‐SVZ in humans was connected with the olfactory bulb by a major migratory stream of young neurons surrounding an open ventricle (Curtis et al. 2007). Verdugo's analysis of this region not only showed that there was no open ventricle, but that migrating cells were absent or extremely rare along the path that joins the forebrain with the olfactory bulb in humans (Sanai et al. 2007). More recently, he worked on a serial section reconstruction of the adult human hippocampus. In his careful observations, he noted the absence of the classical SGZ organization seen in rodents; a distinct cellular layer between the hilus and the granular layer that contains the soma of radial astrocytes and newly generated neurons (Sorrells et al. 2018). One of his final projects provided ultrastructural evidence that this geminal zone is present during embryonic development, but dissolves rapidly in infancy (unpublished data with M.P.). This evidence, independently, corroborated other studies at the light microscope, suggesting that if neurogenesis continues in the adult human brain, this is a rare phenomenon (Sorrells et al. 2018).

Verdugo's extraordinary ability to interpret ultramicrographs and to detect very subtle features in cellular ultrastructure led to numerous discoveries across vertebrate systems. Some of these observations include: the fine structure of NSCs such as the thin apical processes of B1 cells with a primary cilium, and their junctional complexes and interdigitations with surrounding ependymal cells (Figure 2d) (Mirzadeh et al. 2008), the formation and organization of the basal bodies in ependymal cells as they differentiate and become polarized (Figure 2e) (Mirzadeh et al. 2010; Ohata et al. 2015), the close contact between ependymal cells or B1 cells with intraventricular axons (Figure 2f) (Tong, Cebrián‐Silla, et al. 2014; Tong, Chen, et al. 2014), and the identification of distinct cell types within the lateral ventricles of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord across different species (Quiñones‐Hinojosa et al. 2006; Gil‐Perotin et al. 2009; Guerrero‐Cázares et al. 2011; Sawamoto et al. 2011; Alfaro‐Cervello et al. 2014, 2012; Fiorelli et al. 2013; Torres‐Pérez et al. 2017; Edwards‐Faret et al. 2018; González‐Granero et al. 2023; Torrijos‐Saiz et al. 2025).

A generous and curious collaborator, he also worked with researchers from other disciplines. Verdugo applied this broad understanding of NSCs to reveal conserved properties of progenitor cells in other organs, including the carotid body, bone marrow, and heart (Bonilla et al. 2005; Platero‐Luengo et al. 2014; Iglesias‐García et al. 2015). His expertise in subcellular structures also contributed to defining the organization of centrioles in oral‐facial‐digital syndromes (Singla et al. 2010; Thauvin‐Robinet et al. 2014). In more recent work, he helped to identify how long‐term organoid cultures recapitulate NSCs’ properties (unpublished work with J. Knoblich and N. Corsini). While only a few of his many contributions are mentioned here, his impact extended well beyond these examples. His expertise was respected around the world; many scientists came to him for his ultrastructural knowledge and perspective. He inspired others not only by his dedication to the scientific process and meticulous observations but also through his passion and love for science.

Beyond his extraordinary ability to extract meaning from EM analysis, he was equally passionate about teaching, both in the classroom and at the bench. He became Full Professor and, later, Emeritus Professor at the University of Valencia, Spain. His lab, located in the basement of the Cavanilles Institute for Biology and Evolutionary Biology, was one of the places where he was happiest: surrounded by students, ideas, and new discoveries.

Trainees were a constant presence in his laboratory and his life. Those fortunate enough to have him as a mentor, including one of the authors (A.C.S.), know how rare it is to find someone who would sit beside you at the electron microscope, patiently explaining every detail, and examining images as if discovering them for the first time. For him, every project was an adventure, and every analysis an opportunity to learn and to teach. But more than just his curiosity and passion, it was his ability to see the potential in his students and guide their growth as scientists and as human beings that set him apart. This is why his legacy is not only defined by meaningful scientific discoveries but also by his scientific progeny: the numerous neuroscientists and stem cell biologists around the world whom he taught to believe that rigorous science can be pursued with heart. Jose Manuel García‐Verdugo will be greatly missed for the person he was and for all those wonderful moments of shared excitement playing with novel findings.
