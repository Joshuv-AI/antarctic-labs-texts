Association of longitudinal changes in depressive symptoms and handgrip strength with cardiovascular disease in middle-aged and elderly populations
Authors: Yu Du, Kai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause of death among the elderly population. While handgrip strength and depressive symptoms have each been independently associated with CVD risk, their combined longitudinal changes assessed over two time points and their association with incident CVD have not been fully investigated. This study aimed to examine the association of longitudinal changes in these two factors with incident CVD in middle-aged and elderly individuals, with the goal of providing preliminary observational evidence for future prevention research in this population.MethodsBased on data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) between 2011 and 2018, we used Cox proportional hazards regression models to analyze the independent and joint associations of longitudinal changes in handgrip strength and depressive symptoms with incident CVD. Longitudinal changes in both exposures were defined using two repeated measurements from 2011 to 2013.ResultsPersistent and new-onset depressive symptoms were associated with a significant increase in incident CVD risk, with hazard ratios (HR) of 1.36 (95% CI: 1.11–1.66, P = 0.003) and 1.32 (95% CI: 1.04–1.66, P = 0.02), respectively. In quartile-based analyses of handgrip strength change, participants with the greatest increase in handgrip strength (Q4) had a 23% lower risk of incident CVD (HR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.60–0.98, P = 0.03). An exploratory joint association was observed: in the subgroup of participants with new-onset depressive symptoms, the greatest increase in handgrip strength was associated with a 73% reduction in incident CVD risk (HR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.13–0.56, P < 0.001).ConclusionsThis observational study suggests that longitudinal changes in depressive symptoms showed more consistent associations with incident CVD, whereas evidence for handgrip strength change was more limited and was mainly observed in quartile-based analysis. These findings highlight the potential relevance of the “muscle-psychological” interplay in cardiovascular health among middle-aged and elderly populations. The subgroup finding involving new-onset depressive symptoms and marked handgrip strength improvement should be considered exploratory.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27797-0.Keywords: Handgrip Strength, Depressive Symptoms, Middle-Aged and Elderly Populations, Cardiovascular Disease, Longitudinal Changes

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause of global mortality, with approximately 17.3 million deaths annually [1]. As the global population ages, the burden of CVD is projected to escalate substantially in the coming decades—by 2030, more than 23.3 million people are expected to die from heart disease and stroke each year [2, 3]. This places a substantial burden on public health systems worldwide. While reliable prevention strategies exist for traditional CVD risk factors such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes [4–6], the association between longitudinal changes in muscle function and psychological state and CVD remains inadequately investigated. Exploring this link may be of clinical and public health relevance, as it may help inform risk assessment and future prevention research in older adults.

Middle-aged and elderly populations frequently face two interrelated health challenges: impaired muscle function and poor mental health status [7]. Handgrip strength (HGS) is a core marker of muscle health, reflecting not only muscle mass and strength but also showing a close correlation with cardiovascular health [8, 9]. Epidemiologically, low handgrip strength is associated with a significantly higher risk of severe cardiovascular events [10]. Longitudinal studies based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) have provided additional evidence that progressive decline in handgrip strength is associated with an increased risk of CVD [11]. The underlying biological mechanisms linking handgrip strength to CVD include reduced muscle mass leading to elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and systemic low-grade inflammation, impaired endothelial function, and reduced capacity to regulate cardiac output and metabolic homeostasis [12, 13]. These findings support the value of monitoring changes in handgrip strength over time.

Depressive symptoms and CVD also exhibit a bidirectional association, a key focus of mind-body medicine research [14]. Current evidence indicates that depressive symptoms may increase CVD risk through several pathways, including activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and sympathetic nervous system, chronic inflammation, and changes in metabolism and health behaviors [15–17]. Importantly, depressive symptoms are not static; they may persist, remit, or newly emerge over time. A multinational cohort study reported that worsening depressive symptoms was associated with higher cardiovascular risk, whereas remission was associated with lower risk [18, 19]. However, these studies did not incorporate muscle function markers, leaving an important gap regarding whether improvement in handgrip strength may modify the cardiovascular relevance of depressive symptom changes.

Handgrip strength and depressive symptoms also interact closely and may jointly influence CVD risk. In this study, the term “muscle-psychological” interaction refers to the synergistic and interactive effects of handgrip strength and depressive symptoms on CVD risk, including their bidirectional biological and behavioral interplay. Biologically, depressive symptoms may reduce willingness to engage in physical activity, thereby contributing to muscle atrophy and declining handgrip strength [7]; conversely, impaired muscle function may limit functional independence and social participation, thereby worsening depressive symptoms [20]. Epidemiologically, both factors may converge on common pathways relevant to cardiovascular health. Despite this, relatively few studies have investigated the combined effect of longitudinal changes in handgrip strength and depressive symptoms on incident CVD in middle-aged and elderly populations. Existing research has two main limitations: first, few studies have characterized two-point longitudinal changes in depressive symptoms and handgrip strength within the same analytical framework; second, evidence remains limited on whether the association between handgrip strength change and incident CVD differs across depressive symptom change groups.

To address these gaps, we used data from the CHARLS cohort to examine three questions: (1) Is longitudinal change in handgrip strength associated with incident CVD? (2) Is longitudinal change in depressive symptoms associated with incident CVD? (3) How do the combined changes in these two factors relate to incident CVD?

A key feature of this study is its focus on two-point longitudinal changes in both handgrip strength and depressive symptoms, rather than single cross-sectional measurements, and on their joint associations across subgroups defined by depressive symptom change. This design may help clarify the potential pathophysiological basis of the muscle-psychological interplay in CVD development. We aimed to provide preliminary observational evidence to inform future research on combined muscle function and psychological health in relation to cardiovascular risk in older adults.

Methods

Study design and population

Building on existing evidence showing that handgrip strength and depressive symptoms are both associated with cardiovascular risk in middle-aged and older adults [21–23], this study further examined the associations of longitudinal changes in these two factors with incident CVD. We also explored whether the association between handgrip strength change and incident CVD differed across depressive symptom change groups.

This prospective cohort study was conducted using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The period from Wave 1 (2011, baseline) to Wave 2 (2013) was set as the exposure assessment window for measuring two-point changes in depressive symptoms and handgrip strength. Participants were followed up for incident CVD from Wave 2 (2013) to Wave 4 (2018), with outcomes ascertained at Wave 3 (2015) and Wave 4 (2018). As exposures were measured at only two discrete time points, we defined their changes as two-point longitudinal shifts rather than continuous longitudinal trajectories. All data analyses were approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University, and each participant provided written informed consent prior to study enrolment.

To ensure the scientific rigor and generalizability of the study results, we applied the following exclusion criteria (Fig. 1): (1) participants with missing age data or aged < 50 years at baseline (Wave 1, 2011) (n = 4454); (2) participants diagnosed with cardiovascular disease (CVD) or with missing CVD-related data at Wave 1 (2011) and Wave 2 (2013) (n = 4612); (3) participants with missing CVD outcome data during follow-up (Wave 3, 2015; Wave 4, 2018) (n = 1820); (4) participants with missing handgrip strength data at Wave 1 (2011) and Wave 2 (2013) (n = 2830); and (5) participants with missing depressive symptom data at Wave 1 (2011) and Wave 2 (2013) (n = 634). After these exclusions, the final analytic cohort included 3895 middle-aged and elderly adults.

Depressive symptoms assessment

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10). This scale consists of 10 items, each scored from 0 to 3, with a total score ranging from 0 to 30. A score ≥ 10 was defined as the presence of depressive symptoms [24]. Items Q5 and Q8 were reverse-scored and included in the final total score calculation [25].

Longitudinal changes in depressive symptoms from Wave 1 to Wave 2 were classified into four two-point change groups: no depression→no depression, depression→no depression, depression→depression, and no depression→depression.

Handgrip strength assessment

Handgrip strength (HGS) was selected as the primary indicator of muscle function. Trained interviewers measured HGS using a standard dynamometer. Participants with new hand injuries, inflammation, severe pain affecting grip, or those who declined the test were excluded from HGS measurement. During the test, participants stood with their arms hanging naturally and held the dynamometer at a standardized distance from their bodies. Each hand was tested twice, and the maximum value from the two measurements was recorded as the HGS value [26].

To examine whether the association between handgrip strength change and incident CVD differed across the distribution of change, we analyzed handgrip strength change both as a continuous variable and as a quartile categorical variable. This allowed us to assess the overall linear association and localized contrasts across levels of change. Absolute change in HGS from 2011 to 2013 was calculated and then divided into four quartiles based on the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of the final analytic cohort (n = 3895), without manual adjustment of cutoff values. The quartile categories were defined as follows: Q1 (steepest decrease, <−5 kg), Q2 (mild decrease, − 5 to − 0.5 kg), Q3 (slight fluctuation, − 0.5 to 4 kg), and Q4 (largest increase, > 4 kg). Q1 was used as the reference group in quartile-based analyses.

Cardiovascular disease assessment

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) was defined based on self-reported physician diagnosis, including self-reported physician-diagnosed angina, myocardial infarction, heart failure, or stroke, as well as corresponding medication use [27, 28]. Participants with prevalent CVD at baseline (2011) or Wave 2 (2013), or with missing CVD-related data, were excluded.

Follow-up was conducted from Wave 2 (2013) to Wave 4 (2018). Participants were followed until incident CVD, loss to follow-up, death, or the end of the study period, whichever came first. Incident CVD was ascertained at each follow-up wave, and event time in the Cox analysis was approximated by the first follow-up wave at which incident CVD was ascertained, rather than by the exact date of disease onset. The maximum possible follow-up time was 5 years (2013–2018), and the median observed follow-up duration for the final analytic cohort (n = 3895) was also 5 years because most participants contributed the full follow-up period. Specifically, 82.24% of participants did not develop CVD during follow-up and were censored at 5 years, 11.91% were first ascertained as having incident CVD at the 2018 wave, and 5.85% were first ascertained as having incident CVD at the 2015 wave.

Statistical analysis

Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) was used to handle missing covariate data. The imputation model included all variables used in the final analysis: incident CVD outcomes (event status and follow-up time), the two core exposure variables (handgrip strength change and depressive symptom change group), all covariates in the multivariable-adjusted model (Model 2), and age and sex as auxiliary variables. Five imputed datasets were generated with 10 iterations per chain, and a fixed random seed (42) was used to ensure reproducibility. Rigorous statistical diagnostic tests were conducted to validate the reliability of the imputation: (1) The Gelman-Rubin statistic was used to verify the convergence of imputation chains, with the R-hat statistic of all variables < 1.05, confirming full convergence; (2) The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square test (Fisher’s exact test when expected frequency < 5) for binary variables were applied to compare the distribution of imputed values (originally missing parts) with original observed values, with all tests yielding P > 0.05, indicating no significant systematic bias. The results of multiple imputation are presented in Table S1.

Baseline characteristics of participants were stratified by handgrip strength change quartiles for descriptive analysis. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. All variables’ specific definitions and coding are provided in Table S2. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare continuous variables across groups, and Pearson’s chi-square tests (χ²) were used for categorical variables. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.5); no other software tools were utilized.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to examine the independent associations between handgrip strength changes, depressive symptom change groups, and new-onset CVD. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated for each model. Two regression models were constructed with different sets of adjusted covariates: Model 1 (unadjusted model): This model only included the exposure variables (handgrip strength changes and depressive symptom change groups) and the outcome variable (new-onset CVD), with no additional covariates adjusted. Model 2 (multivariable-adjusted model): In addition to the variables in Model 1, this model was adjusted for the following covariates: age, sex, marital status, education level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, weight, height, social activity, baseline handgrip strength, total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), C-reactive protein (CRP), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and systolic blood pressure (SBP).

The proportional hazards (PH) assumption of the Cox regression models was formally tested using the Schoenfeld residual test. The test results indicated that the core study models (including key exposure factors and main covariates) satisfied the PH assumption overall. For individual covariates that failed to meet the PH assumption, a time-varying coefficient Cox model was fitted to correct potential bias by introducing interaction terms between the non-compliant covariates and centered follow-up time (calculated as follow-up time minus the mean follow-up time of the study population). Detailed results of the Schoenfeld residual test are presented in Table S3-S5 of the supplementary materials.

Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). A VIF > 10 was considered indicative of significant multicollinearity. The results of this test are presented in Table S6.

Stratified analyses were performed by depressive symptom change groups. Q1 (steepest decrease in handgrip strength) was set as the reference group in all stratified analyses. HRs and 95% CIs were estimated for each stratum. We further examined whether the association between handgrip strength changes and new-onset CVD varied across different depressive symptom change groups. Formal statistical interaction tests were conducted to examine the interaction between the two factors: a product interaction term (handgrip strength change × depressive symptom change group) was added to the multivariable Cox model to test for multiplicative interaction. For additive interaction analysis, a 2 × 2 exposure framework was constructed by dichotomizing handgrip strength changes at the median and combining depressive symptom change groups into two groups (no persistent depression/remitted depression vs. persistent depression/new-onset depression), followed by calculation of the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and synergy index (SI) with their 95% CIs.

Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For the combined effect analysis involving 12 pairwise comparisons, both Bonferroni correction and false discovery rate (FDR) correction were applied. The uncorrected P values are shown in the main figure for visualization clarity, and the corrected HRs, 95% CIs, and P values are presented in Supplementary Table S7. To explore whether the association between handgrip strength change and incident CVD departed from linearity, we additionally performed a restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis within the multivariable-adjusted Cox model.

Five sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the study findings: (1) Baseline (Wave 1) covariates—including height, weight, smoking status, alcohol consumption, marital status, education level, and social activity—were replaced with Wave 2 data in the multivariable Cox model; (2) Additional adjustments for potential confounders (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia) were incorporated into Model 2; (3) Outliers of continuous variables (HbA1c, CRP, and SBP) were excluded. Outliers were defined as values below the first quartile (Q1) minus 3 interquartile ranges (IQR) or above the third quartile (Q3) plus 3 IQR. After exclusion, the analysis was repeated to eliminate any potential influence of these outliers; (4) A sensitivity analysis was conducted by including the physical activity variable as an additional covariate in the multivariable-adjusted model; (5) A complete case analysis was performed, including only participants with complete data on all key variables of the primary analysis, and the same multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression model was repeated.

Independent association between handgrip strength changes and new-onset CVD

Figure 2 shows the independent association between changes in handgrip strength and the risk of new-onset CVD. Analysis of handgrip strength changes as a continuous variable revealed no significant linear association with new-onset CVD. However, statistically significant differences in CVD risk were observed between the four quartile groups, with Q1 (steepest decline in handgrip strength) as the reference group: participants in Q4 (largest increase in handgrip strength) had a 23% lower risk of new-onset CVD (HR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.60–0.98, P = 0.03); participants in Q2 (mild decline in handgrip strength) and Q3 (minimal changes in handgrip strength) exhibited no statistically significant differences in CVD risk compared with Q1 (both P > 0.05). In additional RCS analysis (Figures S2), neither the overall association nor the test for non-linearity reached statistical significance (P for overall association = 0.184; P for non-linearity = 0.102). Although the spline curve suggested that the association might not be strictly linear across the full range of handgrip strength change, the overall shape of the association remained uncertain.

Persistent depressive symptoms (depression→depression) + Q4 (largest increase in handgrip strength): A marked increase in handgrip strength was associated with a trend toward reduced CVD risk, although this did not reach statistical significance (HR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.38–1.09, P = 0.102).

Remitted depressive symptoms (depression→no depression) + Q2 (mild decline in handgrip strength): A mild decline in handgrip strength was associated with a 38% reduction in CVD risk, with a marginal statistical trend (HR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.34–1.14, P = 0.121).

New-onset depressive symptoms (no depression→depression) + Q4 (largest increase in handgrip strength): A marked increase in handgrip strength was associated with a significant 73% reduction in new-onset CVD risk (HR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.13–0.56, P < 0.001). Notably, in the subgroup of participants with new-onset depressive symptoms, handgrip strength changes as a continuous variable were significantly associated with new-onset CVD (HR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.93–0.99, P = 0.004).

Formal statistical interaction tests confirmed the interactive effect between handgrip strength changes and depressive symptom change groups, with a significant multiplicative interaction (P for interaction = 0.034). The 95% CIs of key additive interaction indicators were as follows: relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) (-0.152 to 0.927), attributable proportion (AP) (-0.096 to 0.409), and synergy index (SI) (0.799 to 5.465). Because the additive interaction indices included null values, uncertainty remained regarding the magnitude of additive interaction. The key finding for new-onset depressive symptoms + Q4 handgrip strength increase remained statistically significant after both Bonferroni and FDR correction (Table S7).

Sensitivity analysis

All five sensitivity analyses yielded results broadly consistent with the primary findings, supporting the robustness of the main results. The sensitivity analyses included: replacement of baseline covariates with Wave 2 data in the multivariable Cox model; additional adjustment for potential confounders (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia) in Model 2; exclusion of outliers of continuous variables (HbA1c, CRP, and SBP); inclusion of the physical activity variable (missing rate > 50%) as an additional covariate in the multivariable-adjusted model; and completion of a complete case analysis (restricted to participants without missing values in key variables). Across all scenarios, the direction and statistical significance of associations between handgrip strength changes, depressive symptom change groups, and incident CVD remained consistent (Figures S3–S17).

Discussion

This study explored the associations between longitudinal changes in handgrip strength and depressive symptoms and the risk of incident CVD among Chinese middle-aged and elderly adults from the perspective of the “muscle-psychological” interplay, and examined the potential interaction between these two factors. As an observational cohort study, the findings should be interpreted as prospective associations rather than causal effects and may provide preliminary observational evidence for future research on cardiovascular risk in this population.

Persistent and new-onset depressive symptoms were both significantly associated with an elevated risk of new-onset CVD in this population, which is consistent with the conclusions of multinational cohort studies reporting links between changes in depressive symptoms and cardiovascular risk [18]. Specifically, compared with the reference group with no depressive symptoms at both baseline and follow-up, participants with persistent depressive symptoms had a 36% higher risk of new-onset CVD (HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.11–1.66, P = 0.003), and those with new-onset depressive symptoms had a 32% higher risk (HR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.04–1.66, P = 0.02), while no significant association was found between remitted depressive symptoms and new-onset CVD risk. This finding refines the link between distinct depressive symptom change groups and CVD risk in Chinese middle-aged and elderly adults, and such associations may align with previously proposed mechanisms involving vascular endothelial damage induced by long-term stress responses and chronic low-grade inflammation [29, 30].

In quartile-based analysis, only participants in the Q4 group (largest increase in handgrip strength) exhibited a statistically significant lower risk of new-onset CVD compared with the Q1 group (steepest decrease), with a 23% lower risk of new-onset CVD (HR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.60–0.98, P = 0.03); no statistically significant differences in CVD risk were observed for the Q2 group (mild decrease) or Q3 group (slight fluctuation) relative to the Q1 group. However, the continuous model did not show a significant linear association, and the additional RCS analysis showed neither a statistically significant overall association nor significant evidence of non-linearity. Therefore, this finding is more appropriately interpreted as a quartile-based localized contrast rather than evidence of a stable dose-response pattern or a confirmed non-linear association.

This study systematically evaluated the joint and interactive associations of longitudinal changes in handgrip strength and depressive symptoms with the risk of incident CVD, and to some extent supplements previous studies that have mostly examined these two factors separately. After multivariable adjustment and stratified analysis by depressive symptom change groups, we observed a significant multiplicative interaction, with a P value for interaction of 0.034, and heterogeneous association patterns were present across subgroups. Notably, only in the subgroup with new-onset depressive symptoms was the greatest increase in handgrip strength (Q4) associated with a 73% lower risk of incident CVD, with an HR of 0.27, a 95% CI of 0.13–0.56, and P < 0.001; handgrip strength change modeled as a continuous variable was also associated with a lower risk of CVD in this subgroup. This heterogeneous protective association suggests that individuals with different patterns of depressive symptom change may respond differently to improvements in muscle function, which may reflect potential pathophysiological heterogeneity in the link between depression and cardiovascular health [31, 32]. However, this subgroup had a relatively small sample size, and the additive interaction indices included null values, indicating uncertainty regarding the magnitude and biological relevance of the interaction. Therefore, this finding is more appropriately interpreted as exploratory evidence rather than definitive evidence.

This study also contributes to the literature related to CVD risk assessment. Traditional cardiovascular risk stratification primarily focuses on conventional modifiable risk factors such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes [4–6], with limited attention paid to changes in muscle function and psychological state—two common health issues in middle-aged and elderly populations [10, 33]. In this study, depressive symptom changes showed consistent associations with incident CVD, whereas the handgrip strength finding was more limited: only the highest quartile of improvement showed lower risk, and continuous as well as spline analyses did not support a clear overall association. We also observed significant baseline differences in anthropometric measures, baseline handgrip strength, and systolic blood pressure across handgrip strength change groups, which may reflect underlying heterogeneity between groups and could partially influence the observed associations, despite our multivariable adjustment for these factors.

The findings may provide preliminary reference for future research on CVD prevention in middle-aged and elderly adults, but all interpretations remain constrained by the observational study design. Longitudinal assessment of depressive symptom change and handgrip strength change may help identify subgroups for further cardiovascular risk research, but the present data do not justify direct causal or interventional inference. In particular, the significant protective association of handgrip strength improvement observed in the subgroup with new-onset depressive symptoms suggests that this population may merit further investigation in future intervention research, but whether improving muscle function can reduce CVD risk in this subgroup requires confirmation in future interventional studies [34, 35].

From a public health perspective, these results suggest the potential value of considering both muscle function and depressive symptoms in future community-based cardiovascular risk assessment and monitoring research for middle-aged and elderly populations. However, any clinical or programmatic application should be regarded as preliminary until validated in larger cohorts and interventional studies [36, 37].

Several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting the findings, and these limitations also suggest directions for future research. First, we defined incident CVD based on self-reported physician diagnosis and concomitant medication use, consistent with outcome definitions used in prior CHARLS-based studies [38, 39]. However, self-reported diagnoses are subject to recall error and reporting inaccuracy, which may have introduced outcome misclassification. Second, because CVD status was assessed only at discrete survey waves, event timing was interval-based; in the Cox analysis, event time was approximated by the first follow-up wave at which incident CVD was ascertained rather than by the exact onset date, which may have reduced the precision of hazard ratio estimates. Third, the maximum possible follow-up was 5 years and the median observed follow-up was also 5 years; although this 5-year follow-up duration has been employed in prior CHARLS-based cardiovascular studies [11, 40], subclinical CVD before formal event ascertainment cannot be fully excluded, and reverse causality remains a possibility. Fourth, muscle function was represented only by absolute changes in handgrip strength, and other relevant indicators such as muscle mass, gait speed, or sarcopenia diagnosis were not available. In addition, although we adjusted for a wide range of potential confounding factors and further adjusted for physical activity in sensitivity analyses, residual confounding by physical activity, functional status, general health status, dietary factors, or genetic factors cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the observed associations may have been influenced both by the limited characterization of muscle function and by incompletely measured or unmeasured confounding. Fifth, the cross-classification of depressive symptom change groups and handgrip strength change quartiles resulted in relatively small sample sizes for some joint exposure subgroups in the combined effect and interaction analyses, and the significant finding in the new-onset depression + Q4 subgroup should therefore be interpreted as exploratory, especially because additive interaction indices included null values. Finally, significant baseline differences in baseline handgrip strength, systolic blood pressure, and anthropometric measures across handgrip strength change groups may reflect underlying heterogeneity. Future studies with longer follow-up duration, more precisely adjudicated clinical outcomes, and more comprehensive variable measurements are warranted to validate our findings and further explore the causal relationship between the muscle-psychological interplay and cardiovascular health.

Conclusion

The study found that compared with the absence of depression, persistent and new-onset depressive symptoms were significantly associated with an elevated risk of new-onset CVD, while no significant association was observed for remitted depressive symptoms. In quartile-based analysis, participants with the greatest increase in handgrip strength (Q4) had a 23% lower risk than those with the steepest decline. Additionally, a significant multiplicative interaction existed between these two factors (P for interaction = 0.034), but the subgroup finding in the new-onset depression group should be interpreted as exploratory.

These results highlight the potential relevance of the “muscle-psychological” interplay in cardiovascular health among middle-aged and elderly populations. Longitudinal assessment of depressive symptoms and handgrip strength may provide supplementary information for future cardiovascular risk stratification research, but the present findings should be interpreted as observational associations rather than causal evidence.
