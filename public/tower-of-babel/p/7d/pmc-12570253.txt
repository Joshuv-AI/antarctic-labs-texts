Lactate combined with lymphocyte counts as useful predictors for the severity and prognosis of necrotizing enterocolitis: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Dabin Huang, Yuying Lin, Yueju Cai, Zhihui Chen, Zhenyu Liang, Xiaowen Chen, Heng Su, Mou Wei, Qiong Meng, Wei Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNecrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) lacks useful biomarkers for early risk stratification. Despite evidence of lactate metabolism and immune dysregulation in NEC pathogenesis, their synergistic diagnostic potential remains underexplored.MethodThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 118 neonates with NEC (2017–2022), stratified into two subgroups: mild NEC (Bell’s stage ≤ IIa, n = 59) and severe NEC (Bell’s stage ≥ IIb, n = 59). Threshold effects, multivariable logistic regression, and mediation analysis were employed to assess nonlinear relationships and biomarker interaction.ResultsSevere NEC exhibited significantly lower lymphocytes (1.58 vs. 3.71 × 109/L, p < 0.001), increased mechanical ventilation requirements (89.8% vs. 39.0%, p < 0.001), and elevated lactate levels (6.4 mmol/L vs. 2.8 mmol/L, p < 0.001). Multivariable regression identified lactate (OR 3.84, p < 0.001) and lymphocytes (OR 0.33, p = 0.021) as independent severity predictors. Threshold analysis showed nonlinear correlations of lactate with NEC severity, surgical intervention, and mortality (turning point: 6.1, 6.4, and 5.4 mmol/L), each 1 mmol/L lactate increase above threshold raised mortality risk 1.7-fold (OR 1.70, p = 0.022). Lymphocytes exhibited significant indirect mediation in combined lactate-NEC evaluation (7.79%, p = 0.008). The lactate-lymphocyte combination achieved useful prognostic accuracy (AUC = 0.96, sensitivity = 89.8%, specificity = 89.8%), outperforming lactate alone (AUC = 0.93, sensitivity = 91.5%, specificity = 81.4%; p < 0.001).ConclusionCombined lactate and lymphocytes enhance risk stratification and prognostic accuracy in NEC, bridge metabolic and immune pathways, and offer clinical utility for guiding early interventions in high-risk neonates with NEC.Keywords: Necrotizing enterocolitis, lactate, lymphocytes, mortality, biomarkers

Introduction

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), a life-threatening gastrointestinal disorder predominantly, affects 5–12% of very low birth weight (VLBW) neonates, with 20–40% of cases progressing to severe disease requiring surgical intervention, while survivors often endure lifelong consequences, including short-bowel syndrome and neurodevelopmental impairments [1–4]. Despite advances in neonatal care, early identification of high-risk patients remains challenging due to the nonspecificity of clinical signs (e.g. abdominal distension, feeding intolerance) and radiographic findings (e.g. pneumatosis intestinalis), which frequently manifest only after irreversible bowel damage has occurred [5]. Furthermore, current diagnostic tools or conventional biomarkers lack both sensitivity and specificity for early risk stratification of NEC, underscoring an urgent need for biomarkers that integrate pathophysiological mechanisms to guide preemptive therapeutic strategies.

Emerging evidence underscores the multifactorial pathophysiology of NEC, where intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury, exaggerated immune activation, and microbial dysbiosis converge to drive disease progression [6]. Hypoxic-ischemic injury constitutes a key pathological mechanism underlying intestinal mucosal damage in NEC, driving dysregulation of cellular processes including apoptosis, autophagy, proliferation, and migration within intestinal tissues [7]. In infants with NEC, persistent hypoxemia and compromised tissue perfusion induce metabolic reprogramming, marked by a shift toward glycolytic dominance—a compensatory adaptation to oxygen deprivation. Within this framework, serum lactate, as a well-established marker of tissue hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism, has garnered attention for its prognostic relevance. Elevated lactate levels correlate with transmural intestinal necrosis and predict fatal outcomes in NEC, reflecting the severity of ischemic insult [8,9]. However, lactate alone lacks specificity, as hyperlactatemia may arise from concurrent sepsis or perinatal asphyxia, limiting its standalone utility [10,11]. Recent studies propose combining lactate with immune-inflammatory biomarkers (such as intestinal fatty acid binding protein or albumin) to address this limitation [12,13]. Peripheral blood cell analysis serves as a widely accessible and rapid auxiliary diagnostic tool in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs). Dynamic changes in peripheral white blood cell (WBC) counts and subset distribution have been validated as biomarkers closely associated with NEC [14]. Accumulating evidence underscores the involvement of lactate metabolism in immunomodulation and its influence on disease pathogenesis [15]. However, the combined utility of lactate levels and peripheral WBC profiles for NEC diagnosis and prognostic stratification remains unexplored.

A recent database-derived retrospective cohort study has confirmed a linear association between lactate levels and in-hospital mortality, with each 1 mmol/L increase in lactate concentration correlating to a 40–45% rise in mortality among neonates with NEC [16]. However, the aforementioned study was constrained by a limited sample size and interpretational limitations, and it failed to explore the potential interplay between lactate metabolism and immune parameters (e.g. peripheral leukocyte subsets) in NEC pathogenesis—a critical gap, given that both metabolic dysregulation and immune dysfunction drive NEC progression. This study hypothesizes that the combination of lactate and lymphocyte levels can serve as useful biomarkers for NEC severity and prognosis, potentially bridging metabolic and immune pathways in disease pathogenesis. The specific aims of this study are: (1) to evaluate the independent and synergistic predictive value of lactate and lymphocytes for severe NEC (Bell’s stage ≥ IIb), surgical intervention, and mortality; (2) to determine potential threshold effects of lactate levels on NEC outcomes (severity, surgery, mortality); and (3) to compare the predictive performance of lactate alone, lymphocytes alone, and their combination in distinguishing severe from mild NEC and predicting adverse outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center between January 2017 and December 2022. Neonates diagnosed with NEC during hospitalization were systematically screened for eligibility. Of 220 neonates initially identified, 118 met predefined inclusion criteria and were stratified into two groups: a mild NEC group (n = 59, Bell’s stage I and IIa) and a severe NEC group (n = 59, Bell’s stage IIb and III) (Figure 1). NEC diagnosis and staging were rigorously confirmed using modified Bell’s staging criteria [17]. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (Approval number: No. 2024-209A01), and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

Flow chart illustrating the selection of patients.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Diagnosis based on clinical symptoms (e.g. abdominal distension, bloody stools), imaging evidence (intestinal pneumatosis, portal venous gas, or pneumoperitoneum), and pathological confirmation of NEC according to established guidelines [18]; (2) Availability of complete medical records for data extraction.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Congenital gastrointestinal anomalies (e.g. intestinal atresia, Hirschsprung’s disease); (2) Inherited metabolic disorders; (3) Cases involving treatment abandonment or voluntary discharge; (4) Incomplete clinical data.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted through structured extraction from electronic medical records. The following variables were systematically retrieved: (1) Neonatal history, including sex, gestational age, birth weight, and history of birth asphyxia (e.g. Apgar scores); (2) Maternal medical history, specifically encompassing delivery mode, gestational diabetes mellitus, and premature rupture of membranes; (3) Infant feeding patterns (exclusive breastfeeding, formula feeding, or mixed feeding); (4) NEC-related clinical parameters: Bell’s staging, clinical manifestations (abdominal distension, bloody stools, lactate levels), complications (patent ductus arteriosus [PDA], sepsis, shock), therapeutic interventions (mechanical ventilation, surgical treatment), and mortality; (5) Complete blood count parameters at NEC onset, with a focus on leukocyte profiles indices (WBC, absolute neutrophil count [NEU], absolute lymphocyte count [LYM], absolute monocyte count [MON], neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio [NLR], neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio [NMR], and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio [LMR]). To minimize sample selection bias, this study strictly adhered to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, systematically screening all NEC cases from 2017 to 2022 via the electronic medical record system to ensure representativeness of included subjects. Data extraction was independently conducted by two researchers using standardized case report forms, with missing or ambiguous records subjected to third-party review and adjudication to avoid subjective selection bias. During statistical analysis, sensitivity analyses (e.g. excluding cases with incomplete data) were performed to verify result stability and reduce potential confounding effects. After comparing the baseline characteristics between groups, imbalanced variables were incorporated into the multivariate model for adjustment to reduce confounding-related bias.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined based on the primary outcome of distinguishing mild and severe NEC. Using published data [16] and preliminary NEC cases from our center, the expected between-group Cohen’s d was calculated as 2.03 (derived from a 3.6 mmol/L lactate difference). With a two-tailed α = 0.05 and statistical power (1-β) = 0.80, G*Power 3.1 software was used to determine a minimum of 42 patients per group—adjusted to 47 per group accounting for 10% potential data loss. The final cohort included 59 patients per group; post-hoc validation via R packages pwr and powerMediation confirmed an actual statistical power of 92.3% for detecting the observed between-group lactate difference, with > 85% power for secondary outcomes (surgical intervention and mortality).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation, median (interquartile range), or frequency (%) for normally distributed, skewed, and categorical variables, respectively. All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 for statistical computing and graphical visualization. Group comparisons utilized χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, while Student’s t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were applied to continuous variables based on data distribution. Predictors of severe NEC were identified through univariate logistic regression, followed by multivariate logistic regression incorporating variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis, with results expressed as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Nonlinear relationships between lactate levels and NEC outcomes (Bell’s staging, surgical intervention, mortality) were explored using smoothing curve fitting via EmpowerStats version 4.1 and R version 4.3.1, with threshold effects quantified by piecewise regression and likelihood ratio tests. Mediation analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro 4.1 (Hayes, 2022) with 1,000 bootstrap iterations to decompose total effects into direct and indirect pathways [19,20]. To evaluate the predictive performance of individual and combined biomarkers (lactate, lymphocytes, and lactate-lymphocyte combination) for severe NEC, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) with 95% CIs, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated to assess predictive accuracy. Youden’s Index (Youden’s Index = sensitivity + specificity − 1) was calculated, and the maximum Youden’s Index was used to determine the optimal cutoff value for each biomarker/biomarker combination. Statistical comparisons of AUCs between different biomarker models were conducted using the Z-test to verify whether the combined biomarkers exhibited superior predictive accuracy compared to individual biomarkers. All analyses adhered to two-tailed testing, and statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the neonates with NEC

A cohort of 220 neonates diagnosed with NEC was enrolled from January 2017 to December 2022. Of these, 118 neonates met the inclusion criteria, with 59 assigned to the mild NEC group and 59 to the severe NEC group (Figure 1). The study population comprised 71 males (60.2%) and 47 females (39.8%). Clinical management included surgical intervention in 46 cases (39.0%) and conservative treatment in 72 cases (61.0%). The overall outcomes included 100 survivors (84.7%) and 18 fatal cases (15.3%).

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the two groups are summarized in Table 1. Notably, infants with severe NEC exhibited significantly lower gestational age and birth weight compared to those with mild NEC (31.70 weeks vs. 34.23 weeks; 1390 g [IQR 910–1900] vs. 1970 g [IQR 1480–2400]; both p < 0.05). No significant intergroup differences were observed in sex distribution, delivery mode, incidence of premature rupture of membranes, maternal gestational diabetes mellitus, feeding patterns, or enteral nutrition practices.

Additionally, comparative analysis revealed significantly elevated rates of comorbidities in severe NEC cases compared to mild cases: PDA (37.3% vs. 13.6%; p = 0.003), early-onset sepsis (16.9% vs. 5.1%; p = 0.04), late-onset sepsis (69.5% vs. 11.9%; p < 0.001), and shock (57.6% vs. 8.5%; p < 0.001) (Table 1). All patients in the mild NEC group received conservative management with zero mortality, whereas 46 cases (78.0%) in the severe group required surgical intervention, with 18 fatalities (30.5%) recorded.

Notably, the severe group demonstrated significantly higher mechanical ventilation requirements (89.8% vs. 39.0%; p < 0.001) and elevated lactate levels (median [IQR]: 6.4 mmol/L [4.80–7.50] vs. 2.8 mmol/L [1.85–3.80]; p < 0.001) compared to the mild group. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified mechanical ventilation (OR = 7.16, 95% CI 1.45–35.47; p = 0.016) and lactate levels (OR = 3.22, 95% CI 1.84–5.62; p < 0.001) at baseline as independent risk factors for severe NEC progression (Figure 2).

The segmented regression analysis demonstrated distinct risk patterns: (1) Severe NEC progression: Below the lactate threshold of 6.1 mmol/L, each 1 mmol/L increment was associated with increased disease severity (OR 3.11, 95% CI 1.80–5.36; p < 0.001). Multivariable logistic regression modeling identified two independent predictors of severe NEC: Lymphocyte count demonstrated a protective association (OR = 0.33, 95% CI 0.13–0.85; p = 0.021), whereas elevated lactate levels showed strong positive correlation with disease severity (OR = 3.84, 95% CI 2.23–6.61; p < 0.001).

Synergistic predictive value of lactate and lymphocyte levels in NEC severity assessment

To elucidate the mechanistic interplay between lactate metabolism and lymphocytic response in NEC progression, we conducted causal mediation analysis. The results indicated that lactate had a substantial mediating effect in neonates receiving mechanical ventilation support, with an indirect mediating ratio of 63.96% (p < 0.001) (Figure 4a and Table S1). Moreover, lymphocytes played a significant mediating role when combined with lactate for NEC evaluation (proportion indirect mediated = 7.79%, p = 0.008), revealing their synergistic contribution to NEC severity determination (Figure 4b; Table S2). Specifically, elevated lactate levels were positively associated with severe NEC (β = 1.34, 95% CI 0.80–1.87, p < 0.001), whereas lymphocytes demonstrated a protective association (β = −0.61, 95% CI −0.98 – −0.24, p = 0.001) (Figure 4b).

Importantly, inverse correlation analysis confirmed significant lactate-lymphocyte crosstalk (r = −0.256, p = 0.005) (Figure 4c). The predictive performance was ultimately validated through ROC curve analysis, with key diagnostic indices (optimal cutoff value, sensitivity, specificity) and AUC calculated to quantify discriminative capacity. Serum lactate alone demonstrated excellent discriminative capacity, with an optimal cutoff value of 4.08 mmol/L (maximum Youden’s Index = 0.729) at which it achieved a sensitivity of 91.5% and specificity of 81.4%, accompanied by an AUC of 0.93 (95% CI 0.89–0.98). The combination of lactate with mechanical ventilation status further enhanced predictive accuracy compared to lactate alone, with an optimal cutoff value of 0.32 for the combined predictive probability (maximum Youden’s Index = 0.763) that yielded a sensitivity of 94.9% and specificity of 81.4%, along with an AUC of 0.95 (95% CI 0.92–0.98). Most notably, the integrative dual-marker approach combining lactate and lymphocyte count achieved useful diagnostic performance, with an optimal cutoff value of 0.52 for the combined predictive probability (maximum Youden’s Index = 0.796) that exhibited balanced high sensitivity of 89.8% and specificity of 89.8%, as well as an AUC of 0.96 (95% CI 0.92–0.99); statistical comparison confirmed that the AUC of this lactate-lymphocyte combination was significantly higher than that of lactate alone (p < 0.001), underscoring the clinical value of this dual-marker strategy for precise risk stratification of severe NEC (Figure 4d,e and Table S3).

Discussion

NEC persists as a life-threatening gastrointestinal emergency in neonates, particularly among VLBW infants, with its pathogenesis involving complex interactions between intestinal ischemia, dysbiosis, and inflammatory cascades [22]. However, current indicators, including clinical manifestations (e.g. abdominal distension, hematochezia) and nonspecific inflammatory biomarkers (e.g. C-reactive protein), lack adequate sensitivity and specificity to reliably predict disease progression or clinical outcomes [22]. Through multivariate logistic regression, mediation effect analysis, and ROC curves, our study systematically validated the combination of lactate and lymphocytes as useful biomarkers for predicting both NEC progression and prognosis, which represents a novel approach to addressing the clinical challenges of this devastating neonatal condition. Our findings demonstrate that elevated serum lactate and reduced lymphocytes counts correlate significantly with NEC outcomes (Bell’s staging, surgical intervention, mortality), suggesting their synergistic potential in risk stratification and outcome prediction.

Elevated serum lactate, a well-established marker of tissue hypoxia and impaired perfusion, has been implicated in NEC progression. Intestinal ischemia and systemic hypoperfusion in severe NEC drive anaerobic glycolysis, resulting in lactate accumulation. Previous studies have linked hyperlactatemia to intestinal necrosis and poor outcomes in NEC [9,16], consistent with our observations of escalating lactate levels in severe NEC and non-survivors. One of the novel dimensions of our study is that, contrary to the linear dose-response reported by Wang et al. [16], we identified nonlinear threshold effects of lactate on NEC outcomes (Table 2): lactate levels > 6.1 mmol/L for severe NEC progression, > 6.4 mmol/L for surgical requirement, and > 5.4 mmol/L for mortality risk. These thresholds provide clinically actionable cutoffs for early intervention and guide precise risk stratification (e.g. prioritizing monitoring for lactate > 5.4 mmol/L), rather than reacting to arbitrary elevations.

However, lactate alone may lack specificity, as systemic hypoperfusion from sepsis or cardiopulmonary instability could independently elevate lactate levels [23,24]. A retrospective study of 100 NEC patients from the Pediatric Intensive Care (PIC) database demonstrated that patients with lactate/albumin ratio ≥ 0.56 had significantly elevated risks of in-hospital mortality (OR = 11.35, 95% CI: 1.40–91.93, p = 0.02) [13]. Indeed, lactate alone may not fully capture the multifactorial nature of NEC, which involves dysregulated immune responses and systemic inflammation. Peripheral leukocyte profiles serve as convenient and accessible biomarkers reflecting systemic inflammatory status, with substantial evidence establishing their utility in discerning the immunomodulatory status in NEC [25–28]. Lymphocytes, particularly T lymphocytes, play a pivotal role in mucosal immunity and bacterial clearance during NEC [29]. Notably, lymphocyte subpopulations, including regulatory T cells (Tregs), have been implicated in modulating inflammatory cascades, suggesting that lymphocyte indices may serve as dynamic indicators of host immune resilience [30,31]. Clinical evidence confirms that lymphopenia serves as an independent risk factor for advanced-stage (Bell’s stage III) or fulminant NEC and a useful predictor of mortality or surgical intervention [32,33]. A single-center retrospective cohort study conducted at Shanghai Children’s Hospital revealed that infants with fulminant NEC exhibited significantly lower absolute lymphocyte counts 72 h prior to NEC diagnosis compared to those with non-fulminant disease [32]. In our study, multivariate logistic regression analysis also confirmed that lymphocytopenia is a significant risk factor for severe NEC (OR = 0.33, 95% CI 0.13–0.85, p = 0.021). Subsequently, we confirmed a negative association between lactate and lymphocytes, while mediation analysis revealed that lymphocytes played a mediating role in the relationship between lactate and severe NEC. Emerging evidence suggests that lactate accumulation may directly impair immune function, as manifested by lactate excess-driven acidic microenvironment inhibiting lymphocyte proliferation [34,35]. The second key novelty of our study is that, unlike previous studies which only confirmed the independent prognostic value of these biomarkers [10,16,33], we quantified their synergistic mechanistic interplay through causal mediation analysis, demonstrating that lymphocytes mediate 7.79% of lactate’s effect on severe NEC (Figure 4b; Table S2). The synergistic interplay between metabolic stress (lactate) and immune exhaustion (lymphopenia) may reflect the intricate pathological progression from metabolic dysregulation to immune imbalance in NEC. For instance, although Wang et al. [16] reported a linear association between lactate and mortality, they did not investigate how immune parameters modify this relationship; our study fills this gap, showing that lactate-induced lymphopenia is not merely an incidental finding but a functional mediator of NEC severity. This mechanistic insight helps distinguish NEC-specific metabolic-immune dysregulation from other conditions (e.g. isolated sepsis, where lactate may increase without concurrent NEC-related lymphopenia [10,11]), addressing the limitation of poor specificity with standalone biomarkers.

The third key novelty of our study lies in that the timeliness and effective predictability of the lactate-lymphocyte combination address critical barriers in early NEC stratification and disease progression monitoring. Compared with existing combined biomarkers (e.g. the lactate/albumin ratio [13], which requires additional albumin measurement), our dual-marker approach utilizes lymphocyte counts—a routine component of complete blood (CBC) count panels in NICUs—enabling real-time, bedside application of both lymphocytes and lactate in resource-limited or busy NICUs. Notably, the lactate-lymphocyte combination exhibits strong adaptability across diverse clinical settings—an advantage critical for translating research findings to real-world practice. Their combination achieved useful prognostic accuracy—quantified via ROC curve analysis—with AUC of 0.96 (95% CI 0.92–0.99), an optimal cutoff value of 0.52 for the combined predictive probability (determined by maximum Youden’s Index = 0.796), and balanced high sensitivity (89.8%) and specificity (89.8%). This performance was superior to that of individual markers: serum lactate alone exhibited an AUC of 0.93 (95% CI 0.89–0.98), with an optimal cutoff of 4.08 mmol/L (maximum Youden’s Index = 0.729), sensitivity of 91.5%, and specificity of 81.4%; while lymphocyte count alone had a lower AUC of 0.79 (consistent with its standalone prognostic utility). This dual-marker approach likely captures both the ischemic (via lactate) and inflammatory (via lymphocytes) axes of NEC pathogenesis, providing actionable thresholds for clinical risk assessment.

In terms of the time window for capturing disease deterioration, our data (Figure 4e and Table S3) confirm that the optimal cutoff values support the early warning utility of serial monitoring of blood gas (with a focus on lactate levels) and CBC, (emphasizing the dynamics of lymphocyte counts) during the early symptomatic phase of NEC—corresponding to Bell’s stage I/IIa, where neonates present with nonspecific symptoms such as increased oxygen requirement, feeding intolerance, or mild abdominal distension. For instance, a neonate with mild oxygen dependency and a 25% reduction in feeding volume would trigger heightened clinical vigilance if lactate levels rise from 2.1 mmol/L to 5.4 mmol/L and lymphocyte counts decrease from 4.2 × 109/L to 3.0 × 109/L within 8 h—even before the appearance of radiological signs (e.g. pneumatosis intestinalis) or severe symptoms (e.g. shock), this early warning enables clinicians to adjust treatment earlier (e.g. suspend enteral feeding or initiate antibiotic prophylaxis). In resource-limited settings or non-tertiary centers, this combination can act as a rapid ‘triage tool’: neonates with suspected NEC who have lactate > 4.08 mmol/L (optimal cutoff for lactate alone) and lymphocyte count < 3.08 × 109/L (optimal cutoff for lymphocytes alone) can be prioritized for referral to tertiary centers, avoiding delays in managing severe cases. Notably, while our retrospective data support the value of monitoring during the subtle symptomatic phase and at diagnosis, the exact, standardized time window remains to be precisely defined. Therefore, future large-scale prospective cohort studies are crucial.

This study is a single-center retrospective design, which may introduce selection bias. For instance, included cases were primarily from a tertiary maternal and child health care institution, with a higher proportion of severe NEC cases, potentially related to referral patterns from primary hospitals. Furthermore, data collection relying on electronic medical records introduces the risk of selection bias due to variability in data documentation; meanwhile, retrospective assignment of Bell’s staging may be influenced by the detail of clinical records, thereby introducing selection bias in group allocation. It should be noted that information bias and potential recall bias inherent in retrospective studies may subtly impact the validity and generalizability of our findings. Specifically, information bias can stem from three aspects: non-random missing data (e.g. sparse serial biomarker measurements in mild NEC cases), heterogeneous testing timing (lactate measurements taken 1–6 h after symptom onset), and unrecorded confounding variables. Additionally, EMRs may be subject to documentation-related recall bias, which arises from omissions or insufficient details in clinical documentation by clinicians. Future studies should adopt multicenter prospective cohort designs, enroll NEC patients from diverse healthcare institutions, standardize inclusion criteria and data collection procedures, and use random sampling or propensity score matching to balance baseline characteristics between groups, thereby minimizing these biases.

Current evidence is constrained by retrospective designs, small sample sizes, and heterogeneity in biomarker assays. Critical gaps include the lack of longitudinal data on lymphocyte dynamics and the need for standardized lymphocyte subset analysis (e.g. regulatory T cells). Future studies should: (1) validate lymphocyte indices and key lactate thresholds in large, multi-center cohorts encompassing tertiary, secondary, and community hospitals—ensuring the generalizability of the lactate-lymphocyte combination’s performance across diverse NEC severity spectra; (2) investigate lactate’s direct effects on lymphocyte function, with mechanistic validation via established NEC animal models to clarify causal links between lactate accumulation, lymphopenia, and NEC progression; (3) explore the dual-marker’s applicability in differentiating NEC from overlapping neonatal conditions (e.g. neonatal sepsis, preterm feeding intolerance); (4) integrate multi-omics approaches to uncover novel metabolic-immune interactions; and (5) develop machine learning models to optimize composite biomarker performance.

Conclusions

Lactate remains a useful prognostic marker for NEC, but its integration with lymphocyte parameters offers a transformative approach to risk assessment. By bridging metabolic stress and immune dysfunction, lactate combined lymphocytes as useful and readily accessible predictors have the potential to revolutionize clinical decision-making, ultimately improving clinical outcomes of NEC in vulnerable preterm infants.
