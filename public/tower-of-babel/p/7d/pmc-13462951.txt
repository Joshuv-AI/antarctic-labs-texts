Multi-elemental fingerprinting of the human brain: distribution of toxic, essential, and rare earth elements across neocortical, limbic, and subcortical regions
Authors: Wojciech Flieger, Przemysław Niedzielski, Michał Flieger, Zofia Wojciechowska, Aleksandra Proch, Jędrzej Proch, Alicja Forma, Andrzej Torbicz, Dariusz Majerek, Grzegorz Teresiński, Jacek Baj, Eliasz Dzierżyński, Jolanta Flieger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the regional accumulation of toxic, essential, and rare earth elements (REEs) in 42 human brain samples across neocortical, limbic, and subcortical regions. Using ICP-MS and multivariate frameworks, we demonstrate that elemental ratios (specifically Fe/Mg and Fe/Zn) provided stronger regional discrimination than absolute concentrations, with PCA explaining 51% versus 34% of variance, effectively capturing the brain’s stoichiometric architecture. Mn and Fe exhibited the strongest regional gradients, with median concentrations in subcortical nuclei approximately two-fold higher than in the neocortex, consistent with DMT1-mediated transport and established tropism for dopaminergic structures. Elevated Mn in the limbic continuum supports a hybrid accumulation scenario combining systemic and olfactory pathways, while Fe enrichment in subcortical regions reflects its physiological role in dopamine metabolism rather than olfactory transport. Elevated Fe/Zn and Fe/Mg ratios in subcortical structures, driven by localised Fe surges rather than depletion of neuroprotective cofactors, suggest a relative weakening of antioxidant defense’s and potential predisposition to ferroptotic neurodegeneration. Cd showed preferential accumulation in subcortical structures, consistent with Ca²⁺ mimicry-mediated neuronal entry and age-related blood-brain barrier degradation, with low limbic concentrations arguing against a dominant olfactory route for this element. Lanthanides accumulated as a unified geochemical block. 96% of their total variance was captured by a single principal component, with most REEs exhibiting identical significance levels (pBH = 0.0107). A spatial segregation was identified between light REEs enriched in subcortical nuclei via calcium channel mimicry and heavy REEs preferentially distributed in limbic structures, suggesting dual entry routes. The disappearance of regional differences in relative-profile space (CLR) points toward a non-selective lanthanide accumulation mechanism, potentially involving axonal pathways or vascular niches. The neocortex emerged as the most chemically distinct compartment, maintaining a unique elemental baseline compared to limbic and subcortical structures. These findings underscore the importance of anatomical stratification in neurotoxicological risk assessments, with particular relevance to the etiopathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases associated with metal dyshomeostasis in the basal ganglia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55776-3.

Introduction

Traditional neurotoxicological paradigms have long been based on the assumption that the Central Nervous System (CNS) is effectively sequestered from circulating xenobiotics by the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB)1–4. However, contemporary neuroanatomy identifies a critical vulnerability in this defensive system: the direct nose-to-brain pathway5–12. This route, facilitated by the olfactory epithelium and the trigeminal nerve, enables the transport of substances directly into the CNS, completely bypassing the systemic circulation and the BBB. Consequently, limbic structures such as the hippocampus, nucleus accumbens, and cingulate gyrus, which possess direct or near-synaptic connections to the olfactory bulb, become primary hubs for the accumulation of exogenous pollutants13–16.

However, the olfactory route is not the sole contributor to the brain’s elemental landscape. Most environmental metals enter the organism via the pulmonary-circulatory interface. Ultrafine particles (UFPs) and metallic nanoparticles (Cd, Ni, Mn, Cr) inhaled into the deep alveolar regions can translocate across the alveolar-capillary membrane into the bloodstream17–21. Once in the systemic circulation, these metals, including toxic metals such as Pb and Hg, as well as essential trace elements like Fe, Zn, Cu, and Se, must cross the BBB. This systemic entry is often mediated by molecular mimicry, in which toxic ions exploit endogenous transport systems designed for essential nutrients. For instance, the “calcium mimicry” mechanism allows certain trivalent and divalent ions to compete for binding sites on voltage-gated calcium channels, thereby sequestering them in metabolically active subcortical nuclei such as the thalamus and caudate nucleus22,23.

The hazard posed by these dual entry points is amplified by the physicochemical properties of modern air pollutants. Metallic nanoparticles derived from industrial emissions and vehicle traffic, such as magnetite and cadmium-bearing aerosols, exhibit the capacity for persistent accumulation. Upon entering the brain, they act as catalysts for oxidative stress and disrupt the homeostasis of essential elements, potentially initiating neurodegenerative or oncogenic processes24–30.

While the olfactory pathway serves as a conduit for environmental neurotoxins, recent pharmacological breakthroughs have pivoted toward utilizing this route for “direct-to-brain” delivery of therapeutics27,31–48. These advancements underscore a critical scientific gap: if life-saving drugs can be targeted to specific structures via the nose, it confirms that environmental toxins likely utilize the same high-affinity pathways. Despite these insights, most existing studies have focused either on total brain concentrations or a single structure, rarely performing a systematic comparison across multiple anatomical regions within a single study model.

In this study, a total of 36 elements were analysed including 22 metals and metalloids, and 14 rare earth elements (lanthanides). The selection was guided by three complementary criteria: documented presence in airborne particulate matter and known capacity to enter the brain via the pulmonary-circulatory interface or the olfactory pathway, established or emerging relevance to brain physiology and neuropathology, and the need to capture both environmental exposure markers and endogenous homeostatic indicators within a single analytical framework.

Toxic and potentially neurotoxic elements (Al, As, Ba, Cd, Cr, Cs, Ni, Pb, Tl, V) were included based on their well-documented presence in ambient air pollutants, vehicular emissions, and industrial particulate matter, as well as their established neurotoxic potential49. Aluminium (Al) accumulates in the brain and has been implicated in neurological disorders, through mechanisms including oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and interference with calcium-dependent processes50. Lead (Pb) and cadmium (Cd) disrupt the BBB integrity, while arsenic (As) triggers glutamate excitotoxicity, neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction51. Thallium (Tl), by mimicking potassium ions, inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and disrupts axonal transport, causing peripheral neuropathy and central nervous system damage52. Hexavalent chromium (Cr) is an increasingly recognised neurotoxicant that impairs olfactory function, induces oxidative neuronal damage, and may contribute to motor neuron diseases53. Nickel (Ni) induces neurodegenerative changes through mitochondrial dysfunction, histone hypoacetylation, and disruption of presynaptic neurotransmission54. Vanadium (V) crosses the BBB and induces neurotoxicity via reactive oxygen species generation, MAPK-mediated neuroinflammation, astrogliosis, and demyelination55. Barium (Ba) and caesium (Cs), though less characterised as isolated neurotoxicants, are consistently detected in atmospheric particulate matter and represent environmental contaminants of emerging concern whose regional brain distribution has not yet been systematically characterised.

Five essential macroelements such as calcium (Ca), potassium (K), magnesium (Mg), sodium (Na), and phosphorus (P) were included because of their fundamental roles in neuronal excitability and signal transduction. Ca ion homeostasis is critical for synaptic transmission, neuroplasticity, and neuroprotection56. Mg regulates neuronal excitability through voltage-dependent blockade of NMDA receptors and exerts neuroprotective effects by limiting excitotoxicity and neuroinflammation57. Ramos et al. reported that Mg, distributed more homogeneously across brain regions, was present at approximately 2.3-fold higher concentrations than the more heterogeneously distributed Ca58. K and Na, maintained by Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, establish the electrochemical gradient essential for action potential generation and neurotransmitter uptake. Their post-mortem regional distribution in the human brain has been systematically characterized, with K identified as the most abundant alkali metal59.

Five essential microelements such as copper (Cu), iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), selenium (Se), and zinc (Zn) were analysed because of their dual roles in brain physiology and neurotoxicology. Fe, Cu and Mn are required for oxidative phosphorylation, neurotransmitter synthesis, and reactive oxygen species detoxification, yet their excess promotes ferroptosis, cuproptosis, and neuroinflammation, contributing to Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease pathology. Post-mortem reference levels and regional distribution of these elements in the human brain have been systematically established60–62. Zn is a critical modulator of glutamatergic neurotransmission and synaptic plasticity, with its deficiency or excess impairing hippocampal-dependent memory and inducing neurodegeneration62,63. Se, primarily through selenoproteins, protects neuronal cells from oxidative damage and participates in the BBB maintenance; regional post-mortem studies have demonstrated the highest selenium concentrations in the putamen and parietal inferior lobule64,65.

Two elements were included as biological analogues of essential ions. Rubidium (Rb) is treated by cellular transport systems as a potassium surrogate and co-distributes with K⁺ in intracellular compartments; post-mortem studies have demonstrated that Rb levels in brain tissue and blood are altered in Alzheimer’s disease, and its regional distribution closely mirrors that of K59,66. Strontium (Sr) substitutes for calcium in membrane transport systems and can participate in calcium-dependent processes including neurotransmitter release, justifying its inclusion alongside essential divalent cations.

Finally, all 14 naturally occurring lanthanides including cerium (Ce), dysprosium (Dy), erbium (Er), europium (Eu), gadolinium (Gd), holmium (Ho), lanthanum (La), lutetium (Lu), neodymium (Nd), praseodymium (Pr), samarium (Sm), terbium (Tb), thulium (Tm), and ytterbium (Yb) were measured as a separate group. Promethium (Pm) was excluded as it possesses no stable isotopes and is absent from environmental and biological matrices. Although the biological effects of lanthanides have been studied since the mid-twentieth century, comprehensive data on their toxicity in humans remain scarce, with most evidence derived from occupational exposure studies and animal experiments67–69. Early toxicological investigations demonstrated that lanthanides can accumulate in multiple organs including the liver, bone, kidney, and brain, following inhalation or parenteral exposure70–72, yet systematic data on their distribution in human brain tissue remain sparse in the published literature73,74. Rare earth elements (REE) are present in ambient fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅) from industrial, vehicular, and agricultural sources, constituting a recognised inhalation and olfactory exposure pathway67,68. The best-documented example of lanthanide accumulation in the human brain concerns Gd, which deposits preferentially in the dentate nucleus and globus pallidus following repeated MRI contrast-enhanced examinations, though its clinical significance remains unclear75. Pagano and colleagues have made significant contributions to the understanding of REE toxicity in humans and animals through a series of comprehensive reviews. Their work established that REEs exert toxic effects primarily through oxidative stress induction, disruption of calcium-dependent signalling, attributable to the similar ionic radius of trivalent lanthanide cations and Ca²⁺, and genotoxicity, with experimental evidence demonstrating impaired learning and memory in animal models following prolonged exposure76,77. La, Ce, and Nd are capable of crossing the BBB and accumulating in neural tissue, where they may trigger neuroinflammatory responses even at low chronic doses70. Across all three reviews, Pagano et al. consistently identified the near-total absence of systematic data on REE distribution in the human brain and the lack of long-term epidemiological studies on human exposure, underscoring the need for targeted post-mortem investigations76–78.

While prior post-mortem investigations have characterised the distribution of typically a few metals at a time across various brain regions49,50,57,59,61,62,65,73–75, the present study uniquely analysed a total of 36 elements within a single cohort. This study, for the first time, systematically compares high-exposure regions near the nasal inlet (limbic system) with neocortical areas potentially protected by the BBB and deep subcortical structures. The division into specific zones: ABC (Neocortex), DE/EJ (Limbic continuum), and FI (Subcortical nuclei), is supported by phylogeny and fluid dynamics. The hippocampus (E) was included in both groups due to its dual anatomical and functional role. It is a key component of the limbic system (DE group), but also has direct connections with regions included in the EJ group. This division was intentional and resulted from the adopted anatomical-functional classification. By treating the DE and EJ areas as a functional continuum, we aim to track the progression of elemental deposition from the primary olfactory interface to distal limbic structures.

The objective of the study is to comprehensively map the elemental landscape of the human brain, identifying regional chemical signatures and elemental ratios that define differential tissue susceptibility to neurotoxicity induced by environmental pollutants entering the organism via multiple routes.

Results

Descriptive statistics and elemental concentration hierarchy of toxic and essential metals

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the forensic post-mortem cohort (n = 42) are summarised in Table S1. Descriptive statistics of elemental concentrations measured across the analysed brain regions (Table S2) indicate a clear hierarchy: EJ > ABC > FI > DE. Analyzing the medians, the highest concentrations are observed for macroelements, i.e., K, P, and Na, followed by Mg, Ca, and Fe. The overall chemical profile is characterized by these elements, while trace and toxic elements have low baseline values and only occasionally reach high values. The presence of high positive skewness and wide max-min ranges supports the interpretation of episodic enrichment.

In EJ, we observe high values for K (median 2900.6 µg/g) and P (median 2707.4 µg/g), with significant spread (SD and IQR) for several metals. For Pb, Cd, and Cr, the marked divergence between mean and median highlights the influence of outliers.

In the ABC area, the elemental profile is the most asymmetric of the four groups (median skewness 4.74) and has the largest number of elements with a median of zero (8). At the same time, Na has the highest median among the areas (2097.8 µg/g), while the medians for K and P are lower than in the EJ/FI area.

In the FI area, high medians are found for several biologically important elements: P (2753.6 µg/g), Mg (121.5 µg/g), Fe (83.1 µg/g), Cu (3.7 µg/g), Mn (0.980 µg/g), and Se (0.242 µg/g). Compared to the other areas, the FI area has the lowest median skewness (3.11).

In DE, the descriptive profile indicates a higher central tendency for calcium (median Ca 62.2 µg/g) with the lowest median for iron (Fe 30.4 µg/g). Similar to the EJ area, selected trace/toxic elements retain strong asymmetry and positive skewness. Variability metrics (SD, IQR) and skewness show that the examined brain regions are characterized by base shifts dependent on abundant elements (visible in the medians of K, P, Na, and Fe) and heterogeneity dependent on trace elements as outliers. In the next step of the analysis, right-skewness reduction was performed before multivariate inference (Table S3). The Yeo-Johnson transformation reduced right-skewness in all 22 elements and reduced the median absolute skewness from 5.615 to 0.515. This means that skewness was reduced in 22/22 elements, transforming an initially highly asymmetric concentration matrix into a substantially more stable basis for multivariate comparison, even though full parametric assumptions still remained unmet.

Global and pairwise multivariate differentiation of toxic and essential metals across brain regions

Diagnostic checks for MANOVA revealed substantial assumption violations (Table S4): only 4 of 84 within-group Shapiro-Wilk tests were non-significant, only 14 of 22 Levene tests supported variance homogeneity, and Box’s M was significant, indicating covariance-matrix heterogeneity. Taken together, these results show that strict parametric multivariate assumptions were not met in the elemental concentration dataset. While omnibus MANOVA statistics were significant (Pillai’s trace = 0.95, p < 10− 44; Table S5), the failure to meet strict assumptions necessitated the use of PERMANOVA, which is robust to non-normality and “heavy-tailed” distributions. Accordingly, the parametric result was interpreted only as a supportive reference, whereas the main inference was based on the permutation framework. This distinction is important because, in the present dataset, statistical significance alone was insufficient to justify reliance on covariance-based multivariate models without explicit assumption control. PERMANOVA confirmed a statistically significant multivariate separation between regions (F = 7.303, R2 = 0.060, p < 0.0001). A permutation test for multivariate dispersion yielded a non-significant result (p = 0.0763), confirming that the observed differences stem from centroid shifts (compositional profiles) rather than artifacts of unequal dispersion. The effect size (R2; approx 6%) indicates that anatomical location accounts for a notable portion of the total elemental variability, consistent with complex biological datasets. At the same time, this effect size should not be interpreted as evidence of complete regional segregation. Rather, it indicates a structured but partial anatomical signal, superimposed on substantial within-region variability expected for postmortem human material exposed to multiple biological and environmental sources of heterogeneity.

Post-hoc pairwise PERMANOVA with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction confirmed that all regional pairs differ significantly (Table 1). This indicates a distributed regional structure rather than an effect driven by a single outlier group. The magnitude of separation was heterogeneous across pairs. The strongest contrast was observed between ABC and FI (R2 = 0.058, F = 12.11, pBH = 0.0002), representing the largest shift in elemental centroids. Conversely, the weakest separation occurred between EJ and FI (R2 = 0.017, F = 2.61, pBH = 0.006). These moderate but systematic effect sizes align with shared biological processes across brain regions, while highlighting distinct area-specific elemental signatures.

Element-level analysis and regional spatial fingerprinting of toxic and essential metals

Univariate drivers of regional differentiation

Element-wise analysis confirms that regional multivariate separation is driven by a significant subset of the elemental panel. After Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction, 11 out of 22 elements remained significant, with effects concentrated in approximately half of the analytes (Table 2). The strongest regional dependencies, ranked by ηH2 effect sizes, were observed for iron (Fe; 0.303), manganese (Mn; 0.285), and rubidium (Rb; 0.191). Other significant contributors included essential macro-elements (Na, Mg, K, P) and trace elements such as Se, Zn, Cs, and Cd (ηH2 = 0.021). Conversely, elements including As, Al, V, Cu, and Pb showed no significant area dependence (pBH > 0.05), indicating that their distribution is either genuinely stochastic or that area-specific signals are masked by high within-group variability.

Patterns of pairwise contrast density

Dunn’s post-hoc tests further elucidate the spatial structure of these differences, with 38 out of 66 pairwise contrasts retaining significance after BH correction (Table 3, Table S7). The “contrast density” varied significantly between elements. Fe, Mg, and Mn exhibited the broadest separation, with five out of six possible regional pairs showing significant differences. In contrast, elements like Cd displayed a highly localized effect; despite global significance in the Kruskal-Wallis test, Cd only differentiated a single pair (DE vs. FI). The frequency of involvement in significant contrasts also varied by region: ABC (25 contrasts), FI (19), DE (17), and EJ (15). This hierarchy suggests that the neocortex (ABC) is the most chemically distinct region, driving the global multivariate effect through a combination of macro-element shifts and selective trace-element accumulation. The pairwise contrast matrix revealed a highly robust differentiation pattern, particularly for Fe and Mn, which reached the highest significance levels (pBH < 0.0001) across almost all anatomical comparisons.

Ratio-based neurochemical profiling

Ratios were defined as numerator/denominator using measured concentrations. When the denominator was equal to zero, the ratio was treated as missing (NA) to avoid undefined values. Multivariate ratio analyses were conducted on complete cases across all 12 ratios (108 observations), while univariate ratio analyses used all available non-missing observations per ratio (Table S8). Descriptive statistics for 12 elemental ratios (Table S9) reveal a heterogeneous scale and asymmetric distribution across all brain regions. Fe-centered ratios (Fe/Ni, Fe/Cu, Fe/Zn) dominate the pooled medians, with FI showing the highest values, consistent with an enriched iron profile. Conversely, DE occupies lower Fe-normalized baselines (e.g., Fe/Ca median 0.4497). Extreme right-skewness and mean-to-median separation, most notably for Pb/Ca and Cd/Ca, indicate episodic enrichment rather than a uniform distribution. Yeo-Johnson transformation effectively reduced absolute skewness in all ratios (Table S10), yet major assumption violations persisted (Table S11): only 9 of 48 within-group Shapiro-Wilk tests were non-significant, only 9 of 12 ratios satisfied Levene’s homogeneity criterion, and Box’s M remained highly significant. Therefore, permutation-based multivariate inference was retained as the primary analytical framework for ratio data. Consequently, PERMANOVA was prioritized for robust multivariate inference.

Multivariate discrimination via ratio profiles

Global PERMANOVA on the complete-case ratio matrix (n = 108) confirmed a significant regional effect (F = 4.21, R2 = 0.1083, p = 10− 4), with anatomical location explaining 10.8% of the total multivariate ratio variability. This proportion of explained variance is materially higher than that observed for absolute concentrations, indicating that ratio-based normalization enhances regional contrast. However, the effect remains moderate in multivariate terms, which is consistent with partial overlap between anatomical compartments rather than fully discrete neurochemical classes. The non-significant results of the multivariate dispersion test (p = 0.218) confirm that this signal reflects genuine centroid/location shifts in ratio composition rather than dispersion artifacts. While omnibus MANOVA statistics were also significant, the permutation-based approach remains methodologically superior given the distribution’s heavy tails.

Pairwise contrast analysis

Post-hoc pairwise PERMANOVA (Table 4) demonstrates that ratio-level differences are broadly distributed, with four out of six comparisons remaining significant after BH correction. The strength of separation was highly non-uniform: the most robust contrast was observed between FI and DE (R2 = 0.1965, F = 11.5, pBH = 0.0012), representing nearly 20% of explained variability for this pair. In contrast, the comparison between EJ and ABC was the weakest and statistically insignificant (R2 = 0.023, pBH = 0.212), suggesting a high degree of neurochemical similarity between these two compartments in terms of ratio architecture.

Ratio-Level Follow-Up Analysis

Univariate analysis confirms that the multivariate regional signal is primarily driven by a subset of 7 out of 12 ratios (Table 5). The strongest contributors are iron-based ratios, specifically Fe/Mg (ηH2 = 0.366), Fe/Zn (ηH2 = 0.349), and Fe/Ca (ηH2 = 0.148). These large effect sizes indicate that Fe-normalization significantly enhances regional discrimination compared to absolute concentrations. Significant but smaller effects were also observed for Fe/Cu, Cd/Ca, Fe/Ni, and Ca/Zn, while ratios such as Cd/Se, Pb/Ca, and Cr/Zn showed no significant area dependence (pBH > 0.05).

Spatial patterns of ratio differentiation

Dunn’s post-hoc tests localized these effects to specific regional pairs, with 22 out of 42 contrasts reaching significance (Table 6). A highly robust “contrast density” was observed for Fe/Ca, Fe/Mg, and Fe/Zn, each differentiating 5 out of 6 possible area pairs. This broad discriminative power suggests a fundamental shift in iron homeostasis relative to macro-element baselines across the studied regions. In contrast, Cd/Ca and Ca/Zn exhibited highly selective profiles, each differentiating only a single specific pair (FI vs. DE and EJ vs. ABC, respectively).

The frequency of involvement in significant contrasts was highest for FI (14) and DE (13), followed by ABC (9) and EJ (8). This gradient reinforces the conclusion that the functional and chemical architecture of the flow-related areas (FI, DE) is most distinct in terms of elemental stoichiometry, providing a more graded and complex regional structure than a simple binary classification.

The integrated multivariate analysis of elemental concentrations and their stoichiometric ratios reveals a highly non-random chemical architecture across the studied brain regions. While absolute concentrations of 11 elements (notably Fe, Mn, and Rb) provide a robust baseline for regional separation, the use of elemental ratios, specifically Fe/Mg and Fe/Zn, significantly amplifies the discriminative power, explaining over 36% of the univariate variance (ηH 2 > 0.34). The neocortex (ABC) and the flow-sensitive regions (FI, DE) emerge as the primary poles of this neurochemical gradient. The consistent dominance of iron-centered ratios across almost all pairwise contrasts (pBH < 0.0001) suggests that regional differentiation is not merely a product of individual element accumulation, but rather a systemic shift in metal-homeostasis and macro-element buffering. These results provide a quantitative foundation for investigating localized metabolic demands or specific entry routes, such as the ‘nose-to-brain’ pathway, in further detail.

Regional accumulation and geochemical profiling of lanthanides

Descriptive profiling of 14 lanthanides belonging to rare earth elements (REEs) reveals a consistent concentration hierarchy across brain areas, with Ho, Lu, and La exhibiting the highest medians (Table S12). All distributions are characterized by extreme right-skewness (2.27–8.00) (Table S13). While log-transformation reduced absolute skewness by 83%, residual asymmetry in Sm, La, and Dy justifies the use of permutation-based multivariate inference (PERMANOVA) and rank-based univariate follow-up. In practical terms, the transformation improved distributional symmetry across the entire REE block, but did not eliminate the residual asymmetry typical of low-level environmental concentration data. The REE analysis was therefore intentionally framed around robust multivariate and rank-based procedures rather than strict parametric normal-theory models.

Multivariate differentiation: scale over shape

Global multivariate testing yielded a critical distinction: regional differences are statistically significant in absolute concentration space (F = 3.17, R2 = 0.026, p = 0.0224), but vanish entirely in relative-profile space (CLR-transformed, p = 0.8986; Table S14). The small effect size in absolute-concentration space indicates that the REE signal is real but weak, and should be interpreted as a low-amplitude shift in total accumulation burden rather than a strong restructuring of inter-element proportions. The disappearance of the effect after CLR normalization strengthens this interpretation by showing that regional variation is primarily expressed in scale, not in profile shape. This indicates that while the total “load” of lanthanides varies across the brain, the underlying geochemical signature (the ratio of one REE to another) remains uniform.

Pairwise PERMANOVA (Table 7) localized this effect primarily to the ABC vs. FI contrast (R2 = 0.0389, pBH = 0.024). Notably, the significant dispersion difference in ABC vs. EJ (pBH = 0.014) suggests that for some regions, differentiation is driven by accumulation heterogeneity (spread) rather than a simple shift in median values.

Toxic and essential metals

The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of metal concentrations indicates that the first two axes summarize a significant portion of the multielement structure (PC1 = 22.3%, PC2 = 12.1%, jointly 34.4%; Fig. 1a). Regional separation is most prominent along PC1, as confirmed by a highly significant Kruskal-Wallis test (H = 56.49, p = 3.3 × 10–12), while PC2 provides a significant secondary gradient (H = 16.75, p = 7.97 × 10− 4). In the PC1-PC2 plane, the largest centroid distance was observed between ABC and FI (d = 1.701), whereas the EJ vs. FI pair showed the highest overlap (d = 0.08). Loading analysis reveals that PC1 is primarily driven by Mg, P, K, Rb, and Fe, while PC2 reflects gradients in Sr, Ca, Cu, Pb, and Ni. This geometry is consistent with PERMANOVA results, suggesting a robust but localized anatomical signature driven by a subset of key elements.

Elemental Ratios

The PCA for ratios (Fig. 1b) explains a substantially higher proportion of total variance (50.9%), confirming that stoichiometric ratios provide superior regional discrimination. While centroids show clear shifts, the partial overlap in the PC1 —PC2 plane reflects the moderate effect sizes observed in PERMANOVA. This structured, biologically plausible heterogeneity indicates that ratio-level profiles are sensitive markers of regional neurochemical architecture, particularly in differentiating the neocortex (ABC) from flow-sensitive compartments.

Lanthanide profiles

The PCA pattern for lanthanides (Fig. 1c) reveals a dominant concentration gradient, with PC1 explaining 95.96% of total variance. The PC1 scores show significant between-area differences (p = 0.00929), whereas PC2 (2.06%) remains non-significant (p = 0.428), indicating that secondary variation is shared broadly across regions. Biplot vectors show that the positive side of PC1 is aligned with Tb, Ce, and La, while the negative side reflects Dy, Eu, and Yb (Figure S2). The largest centroid distance in reduced space was observed for EJ vs. FI (1.414), while ABC and EJ were nearly identical (0.039), reinforcing the geochemical unity of REEs across specific brain compartments.

Unsupervised clustering and internal structure

Unsupervised k-means clustering was employed to verify the intrinsic partitioning of the multielement space independent of regional labels. The data exhibit a compact internal structure, with the highest average silhouette width of 0.627 at k = 2 (Table S15). However, cluster composition showed mixed regional membership (e.g., ABC was nearly equally distributed at 37% and 35.3% between clusters). This suggests that while the transformed data possess a strong internal structure, it is only partially aligned with predefined anatomical groupings, reflecting complex, overlapping chemical gradients within the brain tissue.

Discussion

The predefined anatomical groups used in this study should be understood as a priori functional-anatomical compartments, not as chemically homogeneous microregions. Each group combines structures that share anatomical proximity, exposure-related rationale, or functional relevance, but this does not imply that metal levels are identical across all constituent structures. In particular, differences in cytoarchitecture, vascularization, local metabolism, neuronal density, and transporter expression may generate subregional variability within the same compartment. Such heterogeneity likely contributes to the overlap observed in the PCA and to the moderate effect sizes obtained in multivariate analyses.

We did not further subdivide these compartments into individual anatomical structures because the primary aim of the study was to compare broader exposure-related brain compartments rather than to perform a microanatomical atlas of elemental distribution. A structure-level analysis would represent a different inferential framework and would substantially reduce the number of observations per comparison, thereby increasing the risk of unstable estimates and overinterpretation. Accordingly, the present results are interpreted at the compartment level, with subregional heterogeneity acknowledged as a source of residual variability.

Multivariate analysis (the PCA, PERMANOVA) reveals that the elemental architecture of the human brain is governed by a highly structured geometric and anatomical logic rather than a stochastic distribution. The primary axis of variation (PC1), driven mainly by essential macro-elements such as Mg, P, K, Rb, and Fe, reflects a statistically supported shift in multielement profiles between neocortical regions (ABC) and deep subcortical structures (FI). However, this pattern should not be interpreted as a sharp segregation of individual observations. The PCA plots show substantial overlap between groups, indicating that regional elemental architecture forms partially overlapping anatomical gradients rather than discrete chemical clusters. The PCA provides a low-dimensional projection of the multielement space and, in the case of elemental concentrations, the first two components explain only part of the total variance. Therefore, visual overlap in the PC1 – PC2 plane does not exclude statistically significant centroid shifts in the full multivariate space. Conversely, the overlap indicates that the predefined anatomical groups should not be treated as fully separable chemical classes. This chemical stratification is further refined by a secondary gradient of trace and toxic elements (Pb, Ni, Cu), which highlights region-specific accumulation dynamics. Notably, the finding that limbic structures share a chemical profile more closely aligned with thalamic regions (EJ, DE) than with the neocortex suggests that deep-seated brain areas constitute a distinct functional-chemical unit, likely reflecting shared metabolic demands and common pathways of environmental exposure.

A hierarchy of element discrimination emerges when examining specific markers: major elements, particularly Fe and Mn, act as global discriminators, indicating a fundamental regional restructuring of the brain’s chemical matrix. The demonstrated variability (area-dependent pattern) for Mn and Fe confirms the regional heterogeneity of the brain.

High Fe accumulation in the caudate nucleus and thalamus (FI), combined with low concentrations in the limbic system (DE, EJ), indicates specific physiological conditions in these regions. As the area with the highest natural Fe demand in the brain, Fe is essential for dopamine synthesis and myelin sheath formation, and its high content in this location reflects its crucial role in motor control. Mn also accumulates selectively in the thalamus, where it is taken up by astrocytes and serves as an essential component of enzymes such as glutamine synthetase, responsible for protecting neurons from toxic ammonia. The high concentration of Mn in the caudate nucleus (part of the striatum) confirms the hypothesis that the basal ganglia (BG) represent one of the primary targets of metal accumulation79–82.

As explained by Nyarko-Danquah et al.80, metal levels in the brain are not random but are strictly regulated by a system of specialized transporters. Both Fe and Mn compete for the same transport system, namely the DMT1 protein, whose density is highest in the caudate nucleus and thalamus. These structures therefore act as strong attractors, capturing metals from the bloodstream far more efficiently than the cerebral cortex or limbic system. This supports the hypothesis that the high density of transporters in the blood vessels of subcortical structures82 and their specific metabolic requirements determine the final map of metal deposition. Dysregulation of these transport proteins may lead to selective metal accumulation in subcortical structures, triggering a cascade of neurotoxic processes. Thus, the convergence of the highest Fe and Mn concentrations in the caudate nucleus and thalamus provides substantial evidence for the particular vulnerability of the extrapyramidal system to disturbances in metal homeostasis. The use of the BG as an anatomical biomarker of toxicity81 is fully justified in light of the demonstrated interregional differences, and is of key clinical significance given the direct link between this process and the etiopathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease83,84.

This pattern is consistent with reference data obtained post-mortem from brains of healthy adult humans, in which the highest Fe and Mn concentrations were recorded in the BG, including the caudate nucleus85,86. As noted by Péran et al.87, the highest Fe content was measured in the thalamus, a characteristic feature even in young, healthy individuals. These authors, supporting the work of Bartzokis et al.88, emphasize that the BG exhibit a natural, age-related tendency toward gradual Fe deposition. Furthermore, chronic Mn exposure leads to measurable changes in the anatomy of the BG. Lao et al.82 demonstrated using 3D morphometry that Mn accumulation correlates with volumetric changes in the BG (particularly in the putamen and caudate nucleus), which directly translates into impairment of fine motor skills.

The regional distribution of Mn revealed a distinct accumulation pattern, with the highest median concentrations observed in subcortical nuclei (FI: 0.980 µg/g), followed by the limbic continuum (EJ: 0.802 µg/g), both approximately two-fold higher than in the neocortex (ABC: 0.472 µg/g). This gradient is consistent with the established tropism of manganese for dopaminergic structures of the BG79,80,83,84, where Mn competes with Fe for shared DMT1-mediated transport mechanisms89. The predominance of Mn concentrations in the thalamus and caudate nucleus suggests that systemic (bloodstream) transport played a major role in this model; however, the elevated Mn in the limbic continuum (EJ) may additionally reflect preferential delivery via the olfactory pathway, which provides a direct anatomical route from the nasal epithelium to limbic structures, bypassing the BBB90,91. Inhaled Mn particles have been demonstrated to undergo anterograde axonal transport along the olfactory nerve to the olfactory bulb, which maintains extensive projections to limbic areas17,30, suggesting a hybrid accumulation scenario that may be particularly relevant in the context of environmental or occupational Mn exposure29,80. Elevated concentrations of Mn in the olfactory bulb, frontal cortex, and striatum after inhalation exposure have been described by Elder et al.91, who identified the olfactory neuronal pathway as the primary route of CNS translocation for inhaled Mn oxide particles. In contrast, the elevated Fe concentrations observed in subcortical nuclei (FI: 83.1 µg/g) are more likely attributable to the physiological role of Fe in dopamine metabolism and mitochondrial function within the BG85,86,92, rather than olfactory transport. It was confirmed by inhalation studies demonstrating that Fe, unlike Mn, is not efficiently transported to the brain via the olfactory route93.

In contrast, elements like Cd provide a more localized signal, suggesting that certain metals exert their influence within specific anatomical niches rather than across the entire brain. The highest Cd content observed in the thalamus and caudate nucleus (IF) suggests that transport of this metal occurred primarily via the systemic (blood) pathway. Selective accumulation in these areas may be explained by their intense metabolism and the high density of ion channels, which Cd exploits through molecular mimicry of Ca²⁺ to enter neurons; consequently, only specific anatomical niches, perhaps those with the highest synaptic density or the weakest blood-brain barrier integrity, such as the thalamus, appear truly susceptible to cadmium-induced disruption94,95. The relatively low Cd content in limbic structures (DE, EJ) further weakens the hypothesis of a dominant role of the olfactory pathway in the transport of this element, supporting systemic delivery as the principal route of CNS penetration, facilitated by chronic Cd exposure which progressively increases the BBB permeability94.

These findings are strongly supported by the literature. Panayi et al.96 demonstrated that the thalamus exhibits the highest predisposition to Cd accumulation regardless of the health status of the subjects, while the elevated Cd content in the caudate nucleus may be explained by age-related degradation of the BBB, which is most pronounced in this particular region90. Due to the low expression of protective metallothioneins in neurons, the accumulation noted in FI region may pose a significant risk to motor and sensory processes.

This gradient is further supported by the high density of differences observed in Fe-based ratios (Fe/Mg, Fe/Cu, Fe/Ca and Fe/Zn), which underscores that the balance between pro-oxidant factors and neuroprotective cofactors is not uniform across brain regions. It has long been established that metals can cross the BBB through transporter proteins like DMT1 or transferrin-mediated endocytosis97,98. Our stoichiometric analysis reveals that the elevated Fe ratios in the thalamus and caudate nucleus are primarily driven by a localized surge in Fe concentrations rather than a depletion of other bioelements, as the levels of Zn, Cu, Ca, and Mg remained relatively stable across the studied regions. This selective failure of Fe homeostatic mechanisms within the BG and thalamic circuits suggests a state of relative weakening of antioxidant defenses, which may predispose these high-Fe regions to oxidative damage via the Fenton reaction, lipid peroxidation, and activation of ferroptotic pathways97–101.

Consistent with Walker et al.102 and Bishop et al.97, our results indicate significant regional dysregulation of Fe homeostasis within the BG. The disproportionate Fe accumulation relative to neuroprotective elements such as Mg and Zn may indicate regional the BBB dysfunction and overload of glial Fe-sequestration mechanisms. As emphasized by Zheng et al.1, astrocytes play a key role in buffering excess metals; however, the selective change in ion stoichiometry observed in subcortical structures suggests that iron accumulation exceeds the protective capacity of the glial environment, which may lead to accelerated neurodegeneration. These trends were not fully confirmed by Ashraf et al.103, who observed a simultaneous increase in Fe, Cu and Zn levels in the BG of aging mice, suggesting that age-related metal co-accumulation may partially offset stoichiometric imbalances.

The results for the lanthanides (REE) provide strong statistical evidence for significant differences in absolute concentrations across regions (p = 0.0224). However, the lack of a significant difference in the relative shape of the profile (p = 0.8986) indicates that the proportions between individual elements are conserved, suggesting that lanthanides bypass primary barriers in a uniform manner. The highest median concentrations were found in the neocortex (ABC), which serves as the main reservoir for long-term accumulation. Furthermore, the subcortical nuclei (FI) showed higher absolute concentrations than the limbic system (EJ/DE), yielding an overall gradient of ABC > FI > EJ/DE.

This pattern is strongly supported by the calcium mimicry theory: lanthanide ions, particularly La³⁺ and Ce³⁺, possess ionic radii closest to that of Ca²⁺ and exhibit high affinity for Ca-binding sites and voltage-gated Ca channels104. The regional vulnerability of FI is further exacerbated by the exceptionally high density of Ca channels and intense metabolic activity of the thalamus, compounded by age-related increases in the BBB permeability in the BG105, which together facilitate the sequestration of LREEs in this region via systemic circulation.

In contrast, the chemical signature of the limbic system is dominated by HREEs (Dy, Yb, Lu), which exerted the greatest influence on variability within this region. Europium (Eu), despite its formal geochemical classification as a light rare earth element (LREE; Z = 63), co-accumulated with HREEs in the limbic system. This anomalous behaviour may reflect Eu’s unique position at the LREE/HREE ionic radius boundary (Eu³⁺: 0.947 Å, threshold ≈ 0.95 Å) and its capacity to enter cells via voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels104,106. The presence of these elements in area EJ suggests that the olfactory pathway acts as a selective filter and that direct axonal translocation from the olfactory bulb to the limbic cortex bypasses systemic the BBB filters. Many studies confirm that evolutionarily older rhinencephalon and limbic structures (DE, EJ) are anatomically predisposed to act as traps for contaminants entering via the nasal route28,29. This association between heavy metal exposure and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and ALS, where specific metal imbalances and oxidative damage disrupt cell survival, has been documented across multiple lines of evidence107–112.

The difference in chemical profiles between areas EJ and FI (maximum centroid distance: 1.414 in our the PCA model) is consistent with the hypothesis of distinct entry routes, suggesting that the olfactory epithelium and the subcortical the BBB act as biologically distinct “sieves,” favoring specific subsets of lanthanides based on their physicochemical properties.

Direct metals transfer to the brain along olfactory nerve bundles in rodents has been documented for mercury (Hg), cobalt (Co), and uranium (U)113–115. Sharapova et al.116 demonstrated that the olfactory epithelium acts as a gateway for bloodborne nanoparticles to the CNS, exhibiting size-selective transport properties. However, uptake by sensory nerve endings and subsequent translocation likely depend not only on particle size but also on shape, chemical composition, surface properties, agglomeration state, solubility, and dose. Given that this neural translocation pathway has been demonstrated in non-human primates, it is likely to function in humans as well.

The toxicological relevance of REE accumulation in brain tissue is further highlighted by Zhuang et al.117, who demonstrated significantly higher concentrations of Th, La, Ce, Gd, and Lu in brain tumor tissues compared to normal brain tissue (p < 0.01 or p < 0.001), suggesting that metabolically active and rapidly proliferating cells may preferentially accumulate lanthanides. This is particularly concerning given their widespread environmental distribution: the abundance of Ce in the Earth’s crust is comparable to that of Cu and Zn, and even the rarest lanthanides occur in larger quantities than Cd or Se118.

Limitations of the study

Despite the robust statistical evidence presented, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the observed concentration gradient characterized by the significant separation between the neocortex (ABC), limbic area and deep subcortical nuclei (FI), is suggestive of a nose-to-brain transport route17,119, however, the contribution of systemic circulation cannot be definitively excluded. In the absence of a control group with ligated olfactory tracts or longitudinal data on the BBB integrity, the reported accumulation patterns should be interpreted as a potential synergy between olfactory and hematogenous pathways. Furthermore, this analysis provides a cross-sectional snapshot, which does not account for the temporal dynamics of accumulation, such as the biological half-life of elements in varying brain regions or the possibility of retrograde transport over time.

Another limitation concerns the use of predefined compound anatomical groups. Although these groups were biologically motivated, they may include internally heterogeneous structures. The observed regional effects should therefore be interpreted as compartment-level shifts rather than evidence of uniform metal concentrations within each constituent region. The substantial overlap visible in the PCA supports this cautious interpretation, indicating that the elemental organisation of the brain is better represented as a continuum of partially overlapping profiles than as a set of discrete chemical classes.

The scope of the study is further constrained by its modest sample size within specific area pairs, which may limit statistical power to detect subtle pairwise differences, despite the use of robust permutation-based MANOVA (PERMANOVA) and Kruskal-Wallis tests. Additionally, the findings lack correlation with clinical neuroanatomical outcomes or histological evidence of neurotoxicity120. The observations remain geochemical in nature and require immunohistochemical validation to determine cellular localisation within neurons, glia, or interstitial spaces. Finally, the partial alignment (35–37%) between unsupervised k-means clusters and predefined anatomical labels suggests that latent variables, such as localised differences in microvascular density or calcium-binding protein expression, may influence lanthanide distribution independently of gross anatomical boundaries.

The potential influence of age and sex on elemental concentrations was not formally assessed in the present study, representing a notable limitation. Prior post-mortem studies have demonstrated that both variables can significantly modulate brain element levels. Ramos et al. reported age-related increases in Zn and Mn alongside a negative correlation between Cu and age across 14 brain regions in neurologically intact adults62, while Graham et al. observed analogous age-associated Cu decline and Fe accumulation in the neocortex of cognitively unimpaired controls, yet found no age-related changes in Zn121. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in study design: Ramos et al. sampled 14 anatomically diverse regions including the hippocampus, where Zn is particularly abundant, whereas Graham et al. restricted analysis to a single neocortical area and used an elderly control group age-matched to patients with neurodegenerative disease, potentially introducing selection bias. These methodological discrepancies underscore the need for future studies with larger, population-matched cohorts to formally disentangle age, sex, and region as sources of variability in brain elemental composition.

Although post-mortem delay (PMD) is an inherent feature of forensic cohorts, evidence suggests that levels of most essential and toxic metals remain stable across a broad range of post-mortem intervals. Scholefield et al.122 demonstrated that concentrations of nine essential metals in post-mortem human brain tissue are unaffected by post-mortem delay, age, disease stage, or brain bank location, and all post-mortem delays in the present cohort fell within the ≤ 72 h range validated by those authors. It should be noted, however, that this validation was performed in a disease-specific human cohort and an animal model, and direct extrapolation to neurologically intact forensic material should be made with caution. Critically, the post-mortem stability of lanthanide concentrations in brain tissue has not been systematically investigated, and we cannot fully exclude the possibility that autolytic redistribution contributed to inter-individual variability in REE levels. This represents a specific gap in the literature warranting dedicated methodological investigation.

Materials and methods

Study Population and

The study was conducted on brain tissue samples collected postmortem from 42 adults residing in southeastern Poland. Tissue samples were collected no later than 3 days (≤ 72 h)after death at the Department of Forensic Medicine, Medical University of Lublin. The study was approved by the Local Ethical Committee (Medical University of Lublin, Poland, approval number KE-0254/152/2021) and the sample collection was approved by the Prosecutor’s Office responsible for the performed autopsies. The study was conducted in accordance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association and the Helsinki Declaration on human experimentation. Exclusion criteria included a history of neuroinfection, brain neurosurgery, or chronic chelation therapy. Individuals who died in an advanced stage of decomposition or in accidents with injuries to the studied organs were also excluded from the study. The study cohort comprised 42 post-mortem cases (30 males, 71.4%; 12 females, 28.6%). All subjects were residents of the Lublin region of eastern Poland, ensuring a geographically and environmentally homogeneous cohort with comparable exposure to local air quality, water sources, and food supply.

Age data were available for 31 cases (73.8%), ranging from 20 to 86 years (mean ± SD: 55.6 ± 16.6 years). Cases were distributed across the following age groups: 18–30 years (n = 2), 31–50 years (n = 9), 51–85 years (n = 19), and ≥ 86 years (n = 1). Age data were unavailable for the remaining 11 cases (26.2%) due to incomplete forensic documentation.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated for 41 subjects with available anthropometric data (mean ± SD: 25.1 ± 5.7 kg/m²). Approximately half of the cohort presented with normal BMI (51.2%), while overweight and obesity were recorded in 22.0% and 17.1% of cases, respectively.

The predominant cause of death was sudden death (n = 28, 66.7%), followed by hanging (n = 6, 14.3%), fall from height (n = 3, 7.1%), traffic accident (n = 2, 4.8%), and other causes including bronchopneumonia, drowning, and acute poisoning (n = 3, 7.1%).

The most frequently documented comorbidities were cardiovascular disease, defined as the presence of atherosclerosis (coronary, cerebral, or aortic), cardiomegaly, arterial hypertension, or prior myocardial infarction (n = 30, 71.4%), and hepatic steatosis or cirrhosis (n = 20, 47.6%). Smoking was reported in 21.4% of cases (n = 9), history of alcohol use disorder or abuse in 16.7% (n = 7), and diabetes mellitus in 7.1% (n = 3).

Tissue sampling

During the autopsy, a board-certified forensic expert conducted precise anatomical dissections to identify specific areas representing three different levels of exposure: Group ABC — Neocortical Extension: Polus frontalis (A), gyrus precentralis (B), gyrus postcentralis (C); Isolated neocortical regions (A, B, C): Serving as internal reference points for purely systemic exposure; these regions are protected by the blood-brain barrier and are anatomically distant from the nasal cavity. Group DE — Primary Limbic Targets: cortex cingularis (D) and Hippocampus (E); Group EJ — Limbic-Paralimbic Interface: Hippocampus (E) and cingulate gyrus (J); Identified as primary sites of accumulation due to their direct neuroanatomical proximity to the olfactory tract and high synaptic activity. Group FI — Deep Subcortical Nuclei: Thalamus (I) oraz Caudate nucleus (F); Indirect exposure (internal transport) via cerebrospinal fluid and via blood or metabolic pathways. To examine the regional distribution of elements, a paired sampling method was used. Tissue samples were collected simultaneously from each subject from both the high-exposure “inhalation-targeted” and “environmentally isolated” areas. This internal control model ensures that observed changes in element concentrations are attributable to regional anatomical deposition rather than inter-individual physiological differences. Tissue samples (at least 500 mg each) were collected using ceramic instruments to prevent contamination with trace elements and immediately frozen at −80 °C.

Sample preparation

Tissue samples were subjected to a microwave-assisted digestion procedure. Briefly: 0.5–2.5 g of tissue was placed in perfluoroalkoxy (PFA) teflon vessels. Then 7 mL of 69% Suprapur Nitric Acid (Baker, Radnor, PA, USA) and 1 mL of deionized water were added. Digestion was performed in a closed-circuit, a Mars 6 microwave system (CEM, Matthews, NC, USA), according to the following program: 20 min at maximum temperature of 185 °C, with a rise time of 10 min and a holding time of 10 min. After cooling, the digests were quantitatively transferred to sterile Falcon polypropylene conical tubes with Plug-Seal caps, then 1 mL of 35% HCl ultrapure (Baker, Radnor, PA, USA) was added and diluted to a final volume of 25.0 mL by ultrapure water (> 18.2 MΩ cm at 25 °C) obtained via the Milli-Q purification system (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). A sample without tissue was used as a control.

ICP MS analysis

The determination of toxic (Cd, Pb, Ni, Cr, Hg, As), essential trace elements (Fe, Zn, Cu, Se, Mg), and 14 lanthanides (La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd, Tb, Dy, Ho, Er, Tm, Yb, Lu) was performed using ICP-MS (PQ MS Q, Analytik Jena, Germany) equipped with an Integrated Collision-Reaction Cell (iCRC). The operating parameters, detection limits (DL), and the full validation procedure using multiple certified reference materials (CRMs) including DB001 (human hair), ERM BB 184 (bovine muscle), and BCR 185R (bovine liver) were conducted according to the methodology detailed in previous work123. Recovery rates for all analyzed elements were within the acceptable range of 80–120%. Multi-element external standards were used for calibration. To control the sample introduction system, the mass analyzer, detection system, matrix effect and instrumental drift, the multielement internal standard of 5 µg L⁻¹ (Li, Sc, Y, Rh, Ir, Bi) was added online through the Y-shape connector by a separate channel of the spectrometer peristaltic pump.

Statistical analysis

The analysis starts with descriptive statistics computed separately for each area. This provides a direct view of concentration scale, dispersion, zero inflation, and outliers for every element before any inferential model is imposed. Because elemental concentrations are typically heavy-tailed and strongly right-skewed, the next step applies a Yeo-Johnson transformation to each element (estimated on the pooled sample). The purpose is to reduce asymmetry and limit the influence of rare extreme values that can destabilize covariance-based methods. Yeo-Johnson is preferred here because it naturally handles zeros and preserves the ordering structure of observations. After transformation, the procedure explicitly evaluates assumptions required by parametric multivariate inference. The assumption checks served as a formal decision layer rather than a descriptive add-on. Specifically, MANOVA was treated as the primary inferential framework only when within-group normality, variance homogeneity, and covariance-matrix homogeneity were reasonably satisfied. When these assumptions remained violated, inference was shifted to PERMANOVA, and the corresponding multivariate dispersion test was used to distinguish centroid (location) differences from spread-driven effects. This stepwise strategy was applied consistently to absolute concentrations, elemental ratios, and lanthanide datasets in order to preserve interpretability under strongly skewed biological data. Within-group normality is assessed with Shapiro-Wilk tests, variance homogeneity with Levene tests, and covariance-matrix homogeneity with Box’s M. These checks are used as a formal decision layer: if assumptions are acceptable, MANOVA can be the primary model; if assumptions remain violated, inference is shifted to PERMANOVA, which is distance-based and permutation-driven, and therefore more robust to non-normality and heavy tails. When PERMANOVA is used, multivariate dispersion homogeneity is additionally tested to separate true centroid (location) differences from spread-only effects. Once a global multivariate difference is established, the analysis proceeds to localization: pairwise PERMANOVA comparisons between all area pairs and element-wise Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn post-hoc contrasts. Benjamini-Hochberg correction is applied in follow-up analyses to control false discovery under multiple testing. Independence is treated as satisfied by design, with each sample considered an independent observation.

Conclusion

The findings indicate structured, but partially overlapping, regional differences in brain elemental profiles. This study demonstrates that the human brain does not accumulate metals uniformly. Its regional chemical identity is shaped by a combination of physiological demand, transport-route selectivity, and element-specific physicochemical properties. The superiority of stoichiometric ratios over absolute concentrations as regional discriminators highlights that neurotoxicological risk is embedded not in individual metal burdens but in the disruption of inter-elemental balance. The convergence of iron overload, antioxidant cofactor stability, and ferroptotic vulnerability in subcortical structures, together with the selective olfactory delivery of heavy lanthanides to the limbic system, points to fundamentally distinct toxic niches within a single organ.

These findings carry practical implications. First of all, standard single-element risk assessments may systematically underestimate regional neurotoxic risk by ignoring the stoichiometric context in which metals operate. Future studies should integrate these elemental profiles with neuropathological staging, environmental exposure histories, and neuroimaging data to establish whether the identified stoichiometric imbalances represent early biomarkers of neurodegeneration or reflect end-stage pathological remodelling of the brain’s metal landscape.
