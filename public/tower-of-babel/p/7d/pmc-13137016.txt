Remplissage for “on-track” shoulders yields similar overall but different short-term game participation outcomes vs. Bankart repair alone
Authors: Ryan T. Lin, Shaquille Charles, Sahil Dadoo, Tyler Williams, Andrew Liu, Ryan Gilbert, Michelle Zhang, Jonathan D. Hughes, Albert Lin, Adam Popchak, Adam Popchak, Mark Rodosky, Bryson P. Lesniak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe impact of arthroscopic Bankart repair (ABR) alone vs. ABR with remplissage (ABR + R) on athlete return-to-play rates and number of games played after surgery is poorly understood. The objective of this study was to utilize online sports databases to compare number of games played and return-to-play rates between athletes who underwent ABR vs. ABR + R for “on-track” Hill-Sachs lesions (HSLs). We hypothesized that there would be no difference in relative games played nor return-to-play rates post-operatively between patients undergoing ABR vs. ABR + R.MethodsPatients aged 14-40 years with “on-track” HSLs who underwent either ABR or ABR + R between 2007 and 2022 for anterior shoulder instability were retrospectively reviewed. Exclusion criteria included revision surgery, <1-year follow-up, “off-track” HSLs, >20% glenoid bone loss, nonathletes, and missing data in online sports databases. Athletes were queried in online sports databases and games played were recorded the season before and 3 seasons after surgery, if available. The primary outcome was relative change in games played in the seasons after surgery compared to the season before surgery (RelativeGamesPlayed=#GamesinSeasonAfterSurgery#GamesinSeasonBeforeSurgery). Secondary outcomes included recurrent anterior shoulder instability, defined as recurrent dislocation and/or subluxation, and return-to-play.ResultsEighty-one patients (ABR: 60 | ABR + R: 21) were included in the analysis, with average age of 18 ± 2 years and average follow-up of 6.5 ± 3.7 years (range, 1.0-14.4 years). “Near-track” HSLs (distance-to-dislocation <10 mm) were present in 20% of the ABR group vs. 76% of the ABR + R group (P < .01). Return-to-play rates were similar between groups (ABR: 75% vs. ABR + R: 81%, P = .58). In the first season after surgery, the ABR group had significantly higher relative games played compared to the ABR + R group (ABR: 1.5 ± 1.5; n = 38 | ABR + R: 0.8 ± 0.5; n = 13, P = .02). This difference did not persist in the second and third seasons after surgery (P > .05). In an adjusted Generalized Estimating Equation model, surgical technique was not significantly associated with relative games played after surgery. Rates of recurrent anterior shoulder instability were not statistically different between groups (ABR: 28% (17/60) vs. ABR + R: 10% (2/21), P = .13).ConclusionAmong athletes with “on-track” HSLs, ABR + R resulted in similar long-term game participation and return-to-play rates and compared to isolated ABR alone. Although patients undergoing ABR + R demonstrated fewer relative games played in the first post-operative season, this difference did not persist in subsequent seasons. Remplissage augmentation may therefore have a temporary impact on early post-operative performance without compromising overall return-to-play outcomes.

Anterior shoulder instability is common among young athletes and active individuals, with arthroscopic Bankart repair (ABR) serving as one of the primary surgical stabilization treatment options, especially in patients with subcritical glenoid bone loss (GBL) less than 13.5%.19,34,37 However, recurrence rates after ABR can reach 49% in high-risk patients, prompting emphasis on identifying risk factors such as younger age, contact sports participation, “near-track” Hill-Sachs lesions (HSLs), humeral bone loss, and shoulder hyperlaxity.2,4, 5, 6,12,17,18,26,42 In these high-risk patients, ABR with remplissage (ABR + R) has proven effective even for “on-track” HSLs.31 Despite these differences in recurrence rates, patient-reported functional outcomes have been similar between ABR and ABR + R.14,36 Furthermore, concerns persist regarding potential range-of-motion limitations following ABR + R, particularly in overhead athletes, where 65.5% of patients report difficulty with throwing mechanics post-operatively.20

However, there is limited evidence on how these factors translate to functional outcomes. Current literature focuses on binary return-to-sport outcomes1,14 rather than quantifiable measures of function, such as games played after return-to-sport. As a result, it remains unclear whether the improved stability of ABR + R translates to greater functional performance in athletes. Quantifying outcomes through metrics like relative games played may provide a more nuanced assessment of post-operative recovery and athletic performance than these traditional binary return-to-play measures.

It is essential to further evaluate and compare the post-operative performance relative to baseline and return-to-play rates among athletes undergoing ABR vs. ABR + R. Thus, the objective of this study was to utilize online sports statistics databases to compare games played post-operatively (relative to pre-operative games played) and return-to-play rates between patients with “on-track” HSLs who underwent ABR vs. ABR + R. We hypothesized that there would be no difference in relative games played nor return-to-play rates post-operatively between patients undergoing ABR vs. ABR + R.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the institutional review board at the University of Pittsburgh (IRB # STUDY20030061). This was a retrospective review of patients who underwent either ABR or ABR + R for shoulder instability at a single institution between 2007 and 2022 by 5 fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeons. First, nonanterior stabilization procedures were excluded. Prior to database creation, initial exclusion criteria included inadequate pre-operative imaging (absence of magnetic resonance imaging [MRI], MRI obtained more than 6 months before surgery, poor image quality, or insufficient sagittal sequences preventing accurate assessment of humeral and/or GBL), ABR performed with fewer than 2 anchors, patients aged <14 or >40, prior ipsilateral shoulder surgery, open stabilization, multidirectional or posterior instability, connective tissue disorders, uncontrolled seizures within 2 years of surgery, and concomitant greater tuberosity or proximal humerus fractures. Following database creation, only patients who participated in organized competitive sports (football, basketball, baseball/softball, hockey, soccer, tennis, rugby, wrestling) at the high school, collegiate, or professional level were included. Athletes were further excluded if they underwent revision surgery, had <1-year follow-up, “off-track” HSLs, >20% GBL, or unavailable data in online sports statistics databases.

Surgical indications to perform remplissage augmentation to ABR in patients with subcritical bone loss less than <20% were dictated by the patient's pre-operative risk profile. Patients with multiple high-risk characteristics for failure of ABR, including younger age,39 contact athletes,39 >1 pre-operative instability events,10 hyperlaxity,4 increased GBL,38 near-track HSLs,4 and inferior Hill-Sachs extension,12 were more likely to undergo ABR + R. In the absence of these high-risk factors, isolated ABR was utilized as the primary stabilizing intervention.

Patient pre-operative demographic data, including age, gender, body mass index, past surgical history, and sport-specific participation, were retrospectively collected. Sports-specific participation was categorized into 3 nonexclusive classifications: contact athletes (eg, football, soccer, hockey, basketball), collision athletes (eg, football, hockey, wrestling, rugby), and overhead athletes (eg, tennis, baseball, basketball).24,35 Injury characteristics were also retrospectively collected, including in-season vs. preseason injury and injury laterality. Pre-operative shoulder stability was graded upon exam under anesthesia of both shoulders in the operating room prior to arthroscopic surgery. Shoulder hyperlaxity was defined as external rotation greater than 85° or grade 2+ load-and-shift scores in the anterior, posterior, and inferior plane on exam under anesthesia, reflecting increased capsular laxity in shoulders with unidirectional instability.3,4

Imaging analysis and bone loss measurements

Measurements of glenoid and humeral bone loss were determined using pre-operative T2-weighted MRI sequences. GBL (d) was measured on sagittal-plane MRI and the Hill-Sachs interval (HSI) was calculated on axial-plane MRI using previously described methodology.4 The glenoid track (GT) was calculated as described by Yamamoto et al using the equation GT = 0.83 x glenoid diameter (D) – d, using the best-fit circle method.23,43 Percent GBL was then calculated by dividing the GBL by glenoid diameter (GBL = d/D x 100). Finally, as described by Li et al,30 the distance to dislocation (DTD) was calculated as the difference between the GT and HSI (DTD = GT – HSI). Off-track lesions were defined as a DTD less than 0 mm, near-track was defined as a DTD between 0 and 10 mm, and on-track was defined as a DTD of greater than 10 mm.4 All MRI measurements were obtained by 2 fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeons. Other imaging findings, including posterior labral extension, superior labrum anterior–posterior tears, bony Bankart lesions, loose bodies, glenolabral articular disruption lesions, anterior labral periosteal sleeve avulsion lesions, and humeral avulsion of the glenohumeral ligament, were also recorded using radiographs or MRI scans.

Rehabilitation protocol

All patients followed a standardized, phased rehabilitation program consisting of protection, staged recovery of range of motion (ROM), strengthening, and functional optimization. Post-operatively, the shoulder was immobilized with restricted ROM for approximately 2-6 weeks to allow for healing. Early passive ROM within a protected range was introduced around 3-4 weeks post-operatively. Progressive strengthening typically began during ROM recovery, approximately 8-10 weeks post-operatively. Functional training was initiated once patients achieved painless active ROM and strength comparable to the contralateral shoulder. Overhead strengthening, including sport-specific activities, was introduced between 10 and 16 weeks and continued through 4-6 months post-operatively, after which patients were cleared for unrestricted activity and return to sport.

Post-operative outcomes

Online sports statistics databases were used to obtain publicly available performance and participation data, including confirmation of return to sport, number of games played, and season-level participation following surgery. High school statistics were obtained using MaxPreps and 247Sports, with sports that have high levels of collegiate recruitment, such as football, hockey, and basketball, being more likely to have available high school data. Collegiate and professional statistics were obtained from official collegiate athletics websites and ESPN. These databases also allowed verification of sport and competitive level. Total games played were recorded for the season before and for 3 seasons after surgery using the above online sports statistics databases.

The primary outcome of interest was relative change in games played in the seasons after surgery compared to the season before surgery. Relative games played was calculated for each post-operative season using the formula: elativeGamesPlayed=#GamesinSeasonAfterSurgery#GamesinSeasonBeforeSurgery. Thus, a “relative games played” value of 1 meant the same number of games were played post-operatively and pre-operatively, <1 meant less games played post-operatively than pre-operatively, and >1 means more games post-operatively than pre-operatively. The first post-operative season was defined as the first complete season following surgery. Any partial participation in a season occurring in the same year as surgery was excluded from this definition. Secondary outcomes included return-to-play, recurrent anterior shoulder instability, and post-operative return to the operating room. Return-to-play was defined as being cleared by the operating surgeon and having games played in the subsequent seasons after surgery as reported in the online sports statistics databases. Recurrent anterior shoulder instability and subsequent return to the operating room for revision surgery were obtained via chart review of the electronic medical record as well as phone interviews. Recurrent anterior shoulder instability was defined as any incidence of post-operative glenohumeral dislocation and/or subjective subluxation. Level of play (high school, collegiate, or professional) was recorded, and athletes were additionally identified if they successfully transitioned from high school to collegiate athletics. The time from date of surgery to date cleared return-to-play by the attending orthopedic surgeon was also analyzed between the ABR and ABR + R cohorts.

Statistical analysis

For descriptive analysis, continuous variables were displayed as mean with standard deviation, and categorical variables were displayed as count with percentages. Descriptive analysis employed either Student t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables depending on normality via the Shapiro–Wilk test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher' exact test as indicated. A Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) model was implemented for multivariate analysis of repeated measurements of relative games played across multiple seasons. This approach accounts for the correlation of multiple observations from the same athlete over time, allowing for more accurate estimation of the effects of different factors on performance. In the GEE analysis, the unadjusted model examines the outcome with surgery type only, while the fully adjusted model includes factors in addition to surgery type such as, DTD, seasons played, age, sex, contact athlete status, history of ≥1 pre-operative instability episode, and shoulder hyperlaxity.

Subgroup analyses were performed to compare post-operative outcomes across athletes stratified by primary sport, 1 vs. multiple pre-operative instability episodes, and “near-track” vs. “on track” HSLs. Another subgroup analysis was also performed between ABR and ABR + R in a 1:1 propensity-matched cohort matched for age, sex, contact and overhead athlete status, number of pre-operative instability episodes, GBL, and DTD. Statistical significance was set to a P value <.050, and all statistical analysis was performed in Stata v.18.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). A post hoc power analysis to determine the minimum effect size that this study could reliably detect was performed. Given a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and 80% power, the study was sufficiently powered to detect a minimum effect size of Cohen d = 0.72, indicating that the study was powered to detect moderate-to-large differences between groups but would likely lack the sensitivity to detect small-to-medium effects.16,32 All DTD measurements were performed by the same trained rater using previously validated MRI measurement techniques detailed above. Intra- and inter-rater reliability for DTD have been previously reported as good (ICC = 0.71) and excellent (ICC = 0.82), respectively, based on the same dataset and measurement protocol.4

Results

Out of 413 eligible patients, 179 (43%) were excluded due to no formal participation in organized sports, and 141 (34%) athletes had no data available in online sports statistics databases. Of these 93 patients remaining, 1 (1%) patient were excluded due to less than 1 year of follow-up, 7 (7%) patients were excluded due to prior stabilization procedures of the ipsilateral shoulder, and 4 (4%) patients were excluded due to having an off-track HSL or more than 20% GBL (Fig. 1). The final cohort consisted of 81 patients (ABR: 60 | ABR + R: 21) with average age of 18 ± 2 years and average follow-up of 6.5 ± 3.7 years (range, 1.0-14.4 years). Fifty-three (65%) athletes were football players, 15 (19%) athletes were hockey players, 8 (10%) athletes were basketball players, and 5 (6%) athletes were baseball players. Considering level of play, 58 (72%) were high school athletes, 22 (27%) were collegiate athletes, and 1 (1%) was a professional athlete. All athletes who did not achieve successful return-to-play were high school–level athletes only (100%, 19/19), while 40% (23/58) of those that had successful return-to-play made the transition from high school to college-level athletes (P < .01). Discussion

The main finding of this study was that patients undergoing ABR + R had a significantly lower number of games played relative to the preinjury season in the first season after surgery; however, in the second and third seasons after surgery, there were similar relative games played compared to patients who underwent isolated ABR. Furthermore, the results of the present study demonstrated no significant difference in return-to-play rates between patients undergoing ABR vs. ABR + R. Thus, the results of this study support our hypothesis that there would be comparable relative games played and return-to-play rates between patients undergoing either ABR or ABR + R.

Current studies on athlete performance in the professional setting after ABR have been observed to be similar to presurgery levels.7,9,28 However, these studies did not specifically examine athletes undergoing arthroscopic stabilization surgery with remplissage augmentation. Higgins et al25 examined 208 professional baseball, football, hockey, and basketball athletes after shoulder arthroscopy for either instability, labral tear, rotator cuff tear, or distal clavicle excision and found no significant decline in performance after surgery. Digambar et al15 examined 120 National Hockey League players after shoulder surgery and found similar games played before and after surgery across all positions. In contrast, the current study compared athletes undergoing ABR and those undergoing ABR + R found that there were lower relative games played in the first season after surgery, but similar relative games played in the second and third seasons after surgery between groups. This finding suggests that the addition of remplissage to arthroscopic stabilization surgery may have a temporary impact on early return-to-sport performance but does not result in long-term deficits when compared to isolated ABR.

These short-term negative effects of ABR + R may be attributable to the added biomechanical or functional limitations introduced by remplissage augmentation. While ABR + R has been shown to reduce injury recurrence by enhancing shoulder stability, several studies have also shown that ABR + R may have a detrimental effect on short-term post-operative external rotation, indicating that selective use of ABR + R may be necessary to achieve optimal outcomes.8,27,33 However, not all studies have shown that patients undergoing ABR + R have this post-operative functional deficit. Gonzalez-Morgado21 performed a systematic review on 378 patients undergoing ABR + R across 27 studies and found no statistically significant difference in post-operative external rotation at minimum 2-years follow-up. These results may also reflect a more cautious return-to-sport rehabilitation approach with patients undergoing ABR + R compared to ABR that results from underlying differences in injury severity, consistent with the greater humeral bone loss observed in those undergoing ABR + R.

Current literature has shown that patients undergoing ABR + R have similar or increased rates of return-to-sport with lower rates of injury recurrence compared to ABR alone. In a systematic review of 609 athletes across 16 studies, Abdul-Rassoul et al1 found a comparable return-to-sport rate of 97.5% for ABR alone and 95.5% after ABR + R. Similarly, Davis et al13 performed a systematic review on 538 athletes from 17 included studies, and found that patients undergoing ABR + R had a return-to-sport rate of 86%, with higher odds of return-to-sport and lower odds of injury recurrence and revision surgery compared to both ABR alone and Latarjet. Other systematic reviews have also shown that ABR + R has superior rates of return-to-sport compared to ABR alone.22,41 Although no difference in return-to-play rates was observed between ABR and ABR + R, likely due to limited power, these findings support ABR + R as an effective treatment in young athletes. The lower return-to-sport rates in this study compared to the broader scientific literature may reflect the high proportion of high school athletes in this present study, many of whom underwent surgery after their senior season and did not pursue return-to-sport despite adequate recovery. Overall, ABR + R remains a promising strategy for young, contact athletes at high risk for recurrent instability.

Furthermore, athletes with multiple pre-operative instability episodes compared to only 1 pre-operative instability episode demonstrated worse post-operative outcomes, including fewer relative games played by the third season, delayed return-to-play, and higher rates of recurrent anterior shoulder instability and reoperation, despite similar rates of in-season vs. off-season surgical timing. This threshold has been well established in the scientific literature. Studies by Verweij et al40 and Cheng et al11 demonstrated that more than 1 pre-operative instability episode significantly increases the risk of recurrence after ABR. These findings suggest that repeated anterior shoulder instability events prior to surgery may negatively impact shoulder function and long-term athletic performance, potentially due to cumulative soft tissue or bone loss. Early surgical stabilization after initial anterior shoulder instability, whether by ABR or ABR + R depending on patient risk factors, may therefore be beneficial in preserving shoulder integrity and optimizing post-operative outcomes.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. The present study is retrospective in nature, which may limit the ability to establish causal relationships. A major limitation is that certain variables, including reasons for failure to return-to-play, could not be collected due to incomplete documentation in the electronic medical record. In addition, a substantial proportion of otherwise eligible athletes were excluded due to unavailable participation data in online sports statistics databases, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. This cohort also consisted predominantly of high school and collegiate athletes, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to professional athletes with different performance demands and return-to-play considerations. Although all athletes who did not return-to-play were high school students, the comparison between high school and college athletes did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample size, which may have reduced the ability to detect true differences in return-to-play outcomes. It is also common practice in our institution to utilize the remplissage procedure in patients with higher risk for recurrent anterior shoulder instability after surgery. Thus, the ABR + R group has higher rates of concomitant injury, length of HSI, length of DTD, and rates of “near-track” HSLs, which may introduce selection bias. The sample size of this study is also relatively small, especially in the ABR + R group, which reduces power and may limit generalizability. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis demonstrated that our power to detect smaller effects was limited, particularly given the unequal sample sizes. Therefore, while our findings for the observed large effect are robust, our results do not preclude the existence of smaller, potentially clinically relevant differences that the study was underpowered to detect, especially with outcomes such as recurrent anterior shoulder instability and return-to-play. In our propensity score matching subgroup analysis, concomitant injuries were not included and may have influenced surgical decision-making and post-operative outcomes. Athlete data from online sports databases are also a potential limitation, as the accuracy or comprehensiveness of these databases are not subject to standardized validation. Inclan et al29 found that publicly obtained data in sports medicine research often lacks standardized reporting of data sources, has potential selection and information bias, limited generalizability due to focus on elite athletes, and raises concerns about data quality verification. In addition, patients may miss games due to reasons unrelated to shoulder injury, such as academic ineligibility or injuries unrelated to recurrent anterior shoulder instability. In addition, games played are reported as total seasonal appearances in online sports databases, which include both regular-season and postseason games. As a result, seasonal variability in postseason participation may have influenced relative games played independent of shoulder-related performance.

Conclusion

In patients with “on-track” HSLs, ABR + R was associated with fewer relative games played during the first post-operative season compared to isolated ABR, although this difference was no longer observed by the second season. Return-to-play rates were comparable between the 2 procedures. Athletes with multiple pre-operative instability episodes played fewer relative games by the third post-operative season, required longer to return-to-play, and had higher rates of recurrent anterior shoulder instability and reoperation compared with those with a single episode.
