Phenotyping Heart Failure: Looking Beyond Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction
Authors: Awais A. Malik
Published: 2026

The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) has remained the cornerstone of heart failure classification for decades. Clinical practice has traditionally equated higher LVEF with better ventricular performance, a concept deeply embedded in diagnostic algorithms, therapeutic decision-making, and clinical trial design. However, accumulating evidence challenges this simplistic paradigm. Patients with heart failure and supranormal ejection fraction (HFsnEF), typically defined as an LVEF ≥65%, represent a distinct phenotype characterized not by exceptional systolic function but by profound structural and hemodynamic abnormalities.1, 2, 3 The emerging recognition of this group marks an important shift in our understanding of heart failure physiology.

The notion of a U-shaped relationship between LVEF and mortality has gained increasing support over recent years. Although reduced ejection fraction clearly portends poor prognosis, multiple population-based studies have reported that mortality begins to rise again once LVEF exceeds ∼65%-70%.4,5 The biological explanation for this observation lies in the distinction between myocardial contractility and degree of forward flow. A supranormal LVEF often reflects a small, concentrically remodeled ventricle ejecting a high proportion of a markedly reduced end-diastolic volume rather than enhanced myocardial performance. Consequently, stroke volume may remain normal or even reduced despite an apparently excellent ejection fraction.

This distinction has important clinical implications. Ejection fraction is fundamentally a ratio rather than an absolute measure of pump performance. Two patients with identical LVEF may have vastly different stroke volumes, ventricular compliance, and cardiac output. In HFsnEF, the ventricle frequently operates under conditions of reduced chamber capacitance, increased myocardial stiffness, impaired relaxation, and elevated filling pressures. The supranormal ejection fraction therefore masks substantial physiological impairment.

In this issue of Mayo Clinic Proceedings: Innovations, Quality, & Outcomes, the study by Chen et al6 makes an important contribution to this evolving entity of HFsnEF by identifying two distinct clinical phenotypes. Among the 200 patients included, 88 (44%) were classified as having a hemodynamic loading phenotype, with atrial fibrillation (36%), hypertension (27%), and valvular heart disease (21%) representing the predominant underlying etiologies. The remaining 112 patients (56%) comprised the intrinsic cardiomyopathy phenotype, including individuals with hypertrophic (77%), amyloid (14%), and Fabry (9%) cardiomyopathies. Compared with the hemodynamic loading group, patients with intrinsic cardiomyopathy were over 12 years younger. However, rather than representing a healthier subset, they exhibited more advanced cardiac dysfunction, characterized by higher left ventricular filling pressures, lower stroke volume index, and a greater prevalence of conduction abnormalities. Importantly, the intrinsic cardiomyopathy phenotype was also associated with worse clinical outcomes, underscoring that supranormal ejection fraction may reflect fundamentally different pathophysiological processes with distinct prognostic implications rather than simply enhanced systolic performance.

The findings of this study offer several important clinical insights. First, they reinforce the growing recognition that HFsnEF is distinct from conventional heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and represents a unique biological phenotype rather than simply the extreme end of the HFpEF spectrum. Second, the study highlights the substantial heterogeneity within HFsnEF itself. Clinical features such as increased left ventricular wall thickness, concentric remodeling, elevated filling pressures, reduced stroke volume, and conduction abnormalities should raise suspicion for an underlying intrinsic cardiomyopathy and prompt further diagnostic evaluation. This distinction has important clinical implications, as patients with intrinsic cardiomyopathy demonstrated markedly worse outcomes than those with HFsnEF attributable to hemodynamic loading conditions, emphasizing the need for early recognition and phenotype-specific evaluation and management.

The findings of this study also prompt reconsideration of the continued reliance on LVEF as the principal metric of heart failure classification. Although LVEF remains simple, widely available, and clinically valuable, it provides only a limited assessment of ventricular systolic function. It is influenced by geometric assumptions, loading conditions, image quality, and inter-observer variability, while reflecting only a single aspect of the heart's complex three-dimensional mechanics. Given the intricate myocardial fiber architecture, a preserved or even supranormal LVEF may coexist with a considerable impairment in myocardial function. These limitations highlight the need to complement LVEF with more sensitive measures of myocardial mechanics to achieve a more comprehensive assessment of ventricular performance.

Longitudinal myocardial shortening deteriorates early during disease progression even when circumferential function compensates sufficiently to maintain or increase LVEF.7 Global longitudinal strain has consistently emerged as a more sensitive marker of myocardial dysfunction and prognosis across multiple cardiovascular conditions.7 As acknowledged by Chen et al, strain imaging was not universally performed in their cohort, thus limiting a more in-depth understanding of myocardial dysfunction. Routine incorporation of strain imaging into heart failure evaluation could therefore improve identification of patients whose apparently "normal" ejection fraction conceals significant myocardial impairment.

This study also offers important insights into the relationship between supranormal ejection fraction and infiltrative cardiomyopathies, particularly cardiac amyloidosis. Although cardiac amyloidosis has traditionally been associated with HFpEF.8,9 contemporary evidence suggests that transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy spans the full spectrum of LVEF at the time of diagnosis.10 Although most patients present with preserved ejection fraction, a substantial proportion (39%) have mildly reduced or reduced systolic function, and underscoring the heterogeneity of this disease.10 Against this backdrop, the finding that 8% of patients with HFsnEF had cardiac amyloidosis is particularly noteworthy, suggesting that a supranormal (or any) ejection fraction should not be regarded as evidence against amyloid cardiomyopathy. Rather, these observations reinforce the limitations of relying on LVEF alone and highlight the importance of integrating clinical suspicion with multimodality imaging and disease-specific diagnostic testing when evaluating patients with unexplained heart failure.

Despite valuable contributions of this study, several limitations deserve consideration. As with many observational investigations, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Comorbidities such as atrial fibrillation, obesity, chronic kidney disease, and pulmonary hypertension substantially influence outcomes in HFsnEF and may complicate interpretation of prognostic associations. Furthermore, LVEF measurements remain susceptible to interobserver variability and loading conditions, particularly at extreme values. Standardization of imaging protocols and incorporation of three-dimensional echocardiography and myocardial deformation parameters would strengthen future investigations.

In conclusion, this study identifies two distinct phenotypes of HFsnEF and highlights the limitations of relying on a single numerical measure to characterize ventricular function. Rather than representing superior cardiac performance, a supranormal ejection fraction may reflect distinct pathophysiological mechanisms that produce a heart failure syndrome despite apparently excellent systolic function. This recognition shifts the focus from arbitrary LVEF thresholds toward understanding the biological diversity underlying heart failure and supports a more individualized approach to diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment.
