Overlapping sociodemographic factors of single and multiple forms of malnutrition in Guatemalan households: a secondary data analysis of the Epidemiological Health and Nutrition Surveillance System (2013–2019)
Authors: Diana Sagastume, Mónica Mazariegos, Katja Polman, Mireya Palmieri, Karla Mesarina, José L Peñalvo, Lenka Beňová, Manuel Ramírez-Zea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo inform initiatives aiming to address all forms of malnutrition in Guatemala, the degree of overlap of sociodemographic factors between the double burden of malnutrition (DBM) and single forms of malnutrition must be further understood. This study aimed to identify sociodemographic factors uniquely associated with households with DBM and those shared with households with single forms of malnutrition in Guatemala.MethodsThis secondary data analysis uses nationally representative household data from the Guatemalan Epidemiological Health and Nutrition Surveillance System 2013–2019. DBM was defined as the coexistence of a woman (15–49 years) with overweight/obesity and an infant or young child (6–60 months) with stunting within the same household. Households with single forms of malnutrition were those with only a woman with overweight/obesity or only a child with stunting. Descriptive statistics and multinomial logistic regression were applied.ResultsFrom the 3622 households included, one-fifth of households were identified with DBM. Sociodemographic factors uniquely associated with DBM were having a middle-level of household assets (adjusted odds ratio (aOR): 1.55, 95% CI 1.02 to 2·36) and lack of women’s health insurance (aOR: 1.62, 95% CI 1.00 to 2·62). Shared factors for households with DBM and households with a woman with overweight/obesity were residence in the Central region, women aged >25 years and children >18 months. For households with DBM and households with a child with stunting, shared factors were women’s primary education and Indigenous ethnicity. Women reporting no academic education, parity >3 and having children aged 18–36 months were shared factors across households with DBM and households with both single forms of malnutrition.ConclusionThese findings emphasise the need for integrated, equity-focused nutrition strategies that address both undernutrition and overnutrition across key life stages. The identified overlapping sociodemographic factors provide actionable opportunities for policymakers to design targeted household-level interventions to reduce both child undernutrition and adult overweight/obesity in Guatemala.Keywords: Body Mass Index, Nutrition Surveys, Obesity, Public Health

Introduction

Malnutrition, including undernutrition and overnutrition, continues to represent a silent global pandemic.1 The WHO defines the double burden of malnutrition (DBM) as the coexistence of undernutrition (eg, stunting and micronutrient deficiencies) along with overweight, obesity or diet-related non-communicable diseases within individuals, households, populations and across the life course.2 The occurrence of DBM is largely attributed to increasing globalisation, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs),3 which is accelerating the nutritional transition away from traditional diets and towards greater availability of calorie-dense foods, while simultaneously reducing opportunities for physical activity.4 This shift leads to higher rates of overweight and obesity alongside persistent undernutrition conditions.5

In some Latin American countries, DBM is a case in point.6 A recent systematic literature review and meta-analysis estimated that the prevalence of DBM can reach up to 24% in some countries of this region.7 With a long-standing challenge of stunting among children under 5 years of age,8 9 alongside a more recent and steady increase in obesity in the general population,10 11 Guatemala has emerged as one of the countries with the highest burden of DBM in the Latin American region and worldwide, particularly for the combination of ‘woman with overweight/obesity and child with stunting’.3 Our previous analysis of the DBM situation in Guatemala showed a persistently high prevalence of 18%–19% for this DBM combination between 1998 and 2015.12

Determinants of DBM include biological (eg, intergenerational malnutrition), environmental (eg, food system), behavioural (eg, unhealthy lifestyle) and societal (eg, socioeconomic disadvantage) factors.2 5 In Guatemala, demographic and social determinants appear to play a key role in the development of both single malnutrition and DBM. For example, previous research has shown a higher prevalence of stunting among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, while obesity is more prevalent among higher-income groups.11 Moreover, higher odds of DBM have been reported among women with more than two children, women older than 25 years and those of Indigenous ethnicity.12 13 In contrast, lower odds of DBM have been observed among rural residents, female-headed households and households with higher socioeconomic status and education levels.12 Despite this existing evidence, the relationship between sociodemographic factors and single and multiple forms of malnutrition remains fragmented, and the extent to which these factors overlap across DBM and single malnutrition outcomes is still unclear.

In line with the Sustainable Development Goals Target 2.2, which aims to end all forms of malnutrition by 2030,14 the WHO has proposed double-duty actions (DDAs).15 DDAs are strategies designed to simultaneously reduce the risk or burden of both undernutrition and overnutrition.15 16 While DDAs outline broad opportunities for action, their effectiveness is likely to depend on context-specific shared drivers of DBM and single forms of malnutrition.15

To support and strengthen existing programmes and inform new national strategies aimed at ending all forms of malnutrition in Guatemala by considering overlapping factors of single and multiple forms of malnutrition, this study aimed to (1) quantify trends over time in the prevalence of households with DBM and households with single forms of malnutrition and (2) identify sociodemographic factors that are unique to households with DBM as well as those shared with households experiencing single forms of malnutrition.

Materials and methods

This study followed the Reporting of studies Conducted using the Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) statement,17 which is presented in online supplemental material 1, p3–6.

Definition of DBM

This study focuses on intergenerational DBM, particularly defined as the coexistence of a woman with overweight/obesity and an infant or young child (IYC) with stunting within the same household. As this specific DBM combination has been identified as a relevant public health concern in Guatemala based on our previous work,12 other scenarios of DBM within households, such as a woman with overweight/obesity and an IYC with wasting, or a woman with underweight and an IYC with overweight, were not considered in this analysis. Following WHO standards,18 a woman with overweight/obesity was defined as having a body mass index (BMI) greater than 25 kg/m2. Stunting in IYC was defined as a length/height-for-age z-score below −2 SD using the WHO’s growth reference standard.19 Implausible anthropometric values were excluded, defined as BMI<12 or >60 kg/m² for women and z-scores <−6 or >6 SD for IYC.

Design, data source and data collection

This cross-sectional study was a secondary data analysis of the Epidemiological Health and Nutrition Surveillance System (SIVESNU; Sistema de Vigilancia Epidemiológica de Salud y Nutrición), conducted in 2013,18 2015,19 2016,20 2017/201821,24 and 2018/2019.25,27 Detailed methodologies of each survey round have been reported elsewhere.18,27 Briefly, SIVESNU is a periodic, nationally representative, household-based survey designed to monitor health and nutrition indicators in Guatemala.28 Multistage cluster sampling was used, in which 100 clusters were first selected nationwide, followed by the random selection of 30 households within each cluster. Data were collected at the household level and, where available, from one woman of reproductive age (15–49 years) and one IYC aged 0–60 months per household. When more than one eligible woman and IYC were present, only one woman and IYC were randomly selected for inclusion.

Briefly, data were collected using three validated questionnaires (for households, women and IYC), and administered by trained staff in Spanish or, when necessary, in the local Indigenous language. Responses to the household questionnaire could be given by any member aged 18 years or older. In each household, one woman aged 15–49 years responded to the staff’s questions on the women’s and IYC’s questionnaire, where applicable. The household questionnaire primarily captured sociodemographic information, while the women’s and IYC’s questionnaires focused mainly on health-related indicators. Anthropometric measurements for women and IYC were taken by two trained health professionals following standardised protocols.18,27 Detailed descriptions of data collection procedures are provided in the respective survey reports.18,27

Study sample

The unit of analysis was the household. Households were eligible for inclusion if they included both a non-pregnant woman of reproductive age (15–49 years) and an IYC aged 6–60 months. The woman and IYC were not required to be biologically related; however, the majority (95%) were biological mother–child pairs. To avoid misclassification of women’s nutritional status during the postpartum period, women within 6 months post partum were excluded; therefore, infants aged 0–5 months were not included in the analysis.

Outcome variable

To identify factors uniquely associated with households experiencing DBM as well as those shared with households with single forms of malnutrition, households were classified into four mutually exclusive categories: (1) households with DBM defined as the coexistence of a woman with overweight/obesity and an IYC with stunting (referred to as households with DBM), (2) households with only a woman with overweight/obesity (households with WO), (3) households with only an IYC with stunting (households with CS) and (4) households with neither condition, defined as households in which the woman did not have overweight/obesity and the IYC did not have stunting (households with NC).29 30

Exposure variables

Sociodemographic variables at both the household and individual levels (including women and IYC) were considered exposure variables. These variables were selected based on previous literature, including studies conducted by experts from the Institute of Nutrition of Central America and Panama (INCAP),11,13 as well as the availability of variables across all survey rounds and assessed in the full study sample. The sociodemographic variables related to the household level included region (Metropolitan, North, Northeast, Southeast, Central, Southwest, Northwest, Petén), urbanisation (rural, urban), household members (number), home ownership (owned, rented, borrowed, other), level of household assets (low, middle, high) and participation in governmental or non-governmental nutritional programmes (yes, no). The variable ‘level of household assets’ was used as a proxy for household income. This variable was created as an index derived from principal component analysis based on the presence of five household assets (the availability of a toilet, soap for hand washing, television, radio and exclusive space for cooking) and further recoded into three categories based on tertiles; the lowest corresponding to the low level of household assets, the intermediate to the middle level and the highest to the high level. This approach is stated in the official reports of SIVESNU,20,29 therefore applied in this study. Government nutritional programmes included the Vitacereales and Raciones/Mi Bolsa Segura, and non-governmental programmes comprised Mercy Corps, World Food Programme, SHARE, Fundazucar and Save the Children.

At the individual level, women’s sociodemographic variables included: age and age groups (years and 15–24, 25–34, 35–49 years), academic education level (none, primary, secondary, university), occupation (unemployed/others, unemployed referring to as currently not working but actively seeking; blue-collar/merchants such as labour, trading or farming; white-collar/students for example health professional or managerial positions; housewives referred to as being fully dedicated to unpaid household tasks), ethnicity (Indigenous, non-Indigenous), parity category (based on median; <3 children, ≥3 children), health insurance (yes, no) and received nutritional education after last birth (yes, no, don’t know). The variable health insurance was created based on whether the woman had social security or private health insurance. IYC’s sociodemographic variables included: age and age groups (months and 6–17, 18–35, 36–60 months), sex (boy, girl) and availability of health card, ‘carné de salud’ in Spanish (yes, no).

A limited set of additional variables was included for descriptive purposes only, as these variables were either not available across all surveys or were assessed only in specific subsamples. These variables included household food security status (food secure, mildly insecure, moderately insecure, severely insecure), women’s marital status (cohabiting, married, single), whether the IYC was ever breastfed (yes, no, don’t know) and the age (in months) at which breastfeeding was discontinued. Household food security was assessed using the Latin American and Caribbean Food Security Scale (ELCSA), an instrument that captures household-level food insecurity through reported experiences of uncertainty, compromised food quality and reduced food quantity due to limited access to food.31 The scale consists of 15 dichotomous (yes/no) items reflecting increasing severity of food insecurity, ranging from concerns about food availability to skipping meals or going an entire day without eating. Each affirmative response is assigned one point, with total scores of 0 indicating food security, 1–5 mild food insecurity, 6–10 moderate food insecurity and 11–15 severe food insecurity. Information on household food security was available only in the 2016 and 2017/2018 surveys, women’s marital status in the 2016 and 2017/2018 surveys, and breastfeeding-related variables only for IYC under 36 months of age.

The questions remained consistent across surveys for all variables. However, minor variations in response options were present across survey rounds, particularly for variables related to occupation and participation in government programmes. Further information on data collection procedures of each variable is fully described in the survey reports.18,27

Patient and public involvement

None.

Statistical analysis

All data variables were assessed for completeness, accuracy and internal consistency. Values identified as inaccurate or inconsistent were treated as missing. Variables were standardised using appropriate coding procedures. All analyses were conducted using available data only; therefore, no imputation methods were applied.

Prevalence estimates and 95% CI of each outcome category were calculated per survey year and tested for temporal trends using logistic regression models. In these models, the survey year was used as continuous time to calculate the percentage of annual rate change in the prevalence estimates and reported as OR and 95% CI. To complement the aforementioned analysis, the sociodemographic factors were summarised by survey year using descriptive statistics, counts and percentages for categorical variables, and medians and IQRs for continuous variables. Given the anticipated modest variation in sociodemographic factors and outcome categories across the survey rounds, and to increase statistical power of the sample size for more robust estimates, data from all surveys were pooled for subsequent analyses. The sociodemographic characteristics are presented overall and by outcome category using descriptive statistics. A multivariable multinomial logistic regression was used to identify sociodemographic factors associated with households experiencing DBM and households with single forms of malnutrition (households with OW and households with CS), compared with households with NC. All sociodemographic variables were considered exposure variables, and the model was adjusted for survey year. Only variables available and consistently measured across all survey rounds and in the full sample were included to maximise sample size. Prior to analysis, sociodemographic variables were assessed for multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor method. Model results are presented as a coefficient plot, and averaged probabilities were derived by predicting the average marginal effects for all the variables in the multinomial model. As per SIVESNU’s survey design, only the analyses at the individual (woman and IYC) level were adjusted for clustering and sampling weights. A p value of 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered statistically significant. Stata (Release 16/SE College Station, Texas, USA: StataCorp) was used to conduct analyses.

Temporal trends in the prevalence of households with DBM and households with single forms of malnutrition

Table 1 presents temporal trends in the prevalence of households with DBM, WO, CS and NC across survey rounds in Guatemala. The prevalence of DBM in households has remained stable between 2013 (19.4%) and 2019 (21.5%), with no significant time trend observed across surveys (OR: 1.02; 95% CI 0.98 to 1.07). In contrast, the prevalence of households with WO increased from 29.1% in 2013 to 35.3% in 2019, corresponding to an average annual increase of 4% (1.04; 1.00 to 1.08). Conversely, the prevalence of households with CS decreased by 6% annually (0.94; 0.91 to 0.99), from 26.4% in 2013 to 19.7% in 2019. No significant change in prevalence was observed for households with NC between 2013 (25.1%) and 2019 (23.5%) (OR: 0.98; 95% CI 0.95 to 1.02). Sociodemographic characteristics of the included households

Using pooled data from all survey rounds, table 2 presents household, women’s and IYC characteristics for households with DBM and for the overall sample. Overall, 69.8% of households were located in rural areas, had an average of six household members and 74.9% owned their homes. Regarding household assets, 26.6% of households reported a high asset level, 39.2% a middle level and 34.2% a low level. The mean age of women was 28 years, 40.1% were married and 51.8% self-identified as Indigenous. Most women (58.7%) reported primary education as their highest level attained, 76.7% were housewives and had an average of three children. Only 9.9% reported health insurance coverage. The mean age of IYC was 33 months, with a balanced sex distribution (50.8% boys and 49.2% girls), and 96.6% of households reported having the child’s health card. Compared with households with NC, the sociodemographic factors uniquely associated with households with DBM were having a middle level of household assets (aOR: 1.55, 95% CI 1.02 to 2.36) compared to the highest level of assets, and women lacking health insurance coverage (1.62, 1.00 to 2.62) compared to those who were insured. Consistent with these findings, the average predicted probability of a household experiencing DBM was 5% higher among households with a middle asset level than among those with a high asset level, and 7% higher among households in which women did not have health insurance compared with those in which women were insured (online supplemental table 7).

Overlapping sociodemographic factors between households with DBM and households with single forms of malnutrition (WO and CS)

Compared with households with NC, several sociodemographic factors were shared between households with DBM and households with WO. These included residence in the Central region compared with the Metropolitan region (DBM 1.81, 1.05 to 3.12; WO 1.75, 1.11 to 2.75) women aged 25–34 years (DBM 1.53, 1.09 to 2.15; WO 1.38, 1.04 to 1.84) and 35–49 years (DBM 3.06, 1.84 to 5.09; WO 2.78, 1.76 to 4.38) compared with women aged 15–24 years, and having a child aged 36–60 months compared with 6–17 months (DBM 2.25, 1.51 to 3.34; WO 1.72, 1.25 to 2.36).

Relative to households with NC, shared sociodemographic factors between households with DBM and households with CS included women with primary education compared with secondary education (DBM 1.76, 1.25 to 2.48; CS 2.18, 1.51 to 3.15) and Indigenous ethnicity compared with non-Indigenous (DBM 1.98, 1.46 to 2.67; CS 1.47, 1.08 to 1.99).

In addition, several sociodemographic factors were shared across households with DBM, households with WO, and households with CS. These included women with no formal education compared with secondary education (DBM 2.68, 1.21 to 5.91; WO 2.19, 1.05 to 4.56; CS 3.25, 1.45 to 7.29), women with ≥3 children compared with those with <3 children (DBM 2.01, 1.43 to 2.81; WO 1.47, 1.09 to 1.97; CS 1.83, 1.29 to 2.60), and IYC aged 18–35 months compared with those aged 6–17 months (DBM 2.60, 1.73 to 3.91; WO 1.43, 1.03 to 2.00; CS 1.51, 1.03 to 2.21).

No statistically significant differences were observed across survey rounds.

Discussion

Using five rounds of nationally representative data collected between 2013 and 2019, this study provides comprehensive evidence on the prevalence and sociodemographic determinants of DBM and single forms of malnutrition in Guatemala. Our findings demonstrate modest improvements in child stunting (26%–23%) alongside a continued rise in overweight and obesity (29%–31%) among women; however, most concerningly, one in five households consistently experienced DBM throughout the study period. This persistent trend suggests that improvements in undernutrition have been counterbalanced by the rapid rise of overnutrition, highlighting the limitations of nutrition approaches that address these conditions in isolation.

The identified sociodemographic determinants indicate that DBM is particularly driven by overlapping vulnerabilities across the life course and the social spectrum. The unique associations between DBM, the middle level of household assets and lack of women’s health insurance suggest that DBM disproportionately affects households in a transitional socioeconomic position, no longer experiencing extreme poverty, yet still lacking sufficient access to health services. Furthermore, shared factors between households with DBM and households with OW, such as residence in the Central region, older maternal age and older child age, likely reflect broader processes of urbanisation leading to changes in dietary and physical activity patterns. In contrast, factors shared between households with DBM and households with CS, including lower maternal education and Indigenous ethnicity, underscore the persistence of structural and intergenerational inequalities that continue to constrain child growth. Importantly, factors common to households with DBM and households with both single forms of malnutrition, such as very low maternal education, higher parity and children aged 18–36 months, were generally more strongly associated with DBM than with single malnutrition outcomes, suggesting compounded disadvantage in households facing multiple nutritional challenges.

Overall, our findings align with previous research from Guatemala, which has shown that socially disadvantaged populations are particularly vulnerable to both single forms of malnutrition and DBM.11,13 At the same time, our results show that households undergoing socioeconomic advancement also experience DBM, underscoring that this burden is not restricted to traditionally vulnerable groups. Moreover, this study contributes to the existing literature by highlighting the overlapping sociodemographic factors of single and multiple forms of malnutrition, which offer context-specific opportunities to address DBM. As the overarching purpose of this study was to inform and strengthen initiatives aiming to address all forms of malnutrition, the subsequent discussion focuses on how these findings are reflected in current initiatives and how they can inform and improve future interventions.

In recent years, Guatemala has developed and implemented several government-led policies and programmes with the potential to address key sociodemographic factors of DBM, while also aligning with the DDA definition.11 For example, the ongoing ‘National Food and Nutrition Security Policy 2022–2037’ which aims to ensure food and nutritional security of the Guatemalan population and includes measures such as strengthening exclusive breastfeeding programmes and limiting marketing for breast milk substitutes.32 This policy also prioritises women of reproductive age, children below 5 years of age, and those of lower sociodemographic status.32 In the education sector, the ‘Regulation of the School Meal Law’, enacted in 2018,33 establishes standards for the quantity and quality of school meals and promotes school gardens, agricultural practices and ongoing training for parents in public schools on administration, health, nutrition and the preparation of healthy and nutritious meals.33 Although this law does not directly target the population currently most affected by DBM, as it primarily focuses on school-age children, it holds long-term promise for addressing DBM by potentially breaking the intergenerational cycle of malnutrition,34 if adequately implemented, monitored and evaluated. Its impact could be further strengthened by promoting sustained school attendance, which remains suboptimal,35 with only 82% of the Guatemalan population being literate in 2022.36 Other ongoing national programmes aimed at tackling malnutrition, predominantly focused on undernutrition among women of reproductive age and IYC include ‘IYC growth monitoring and promotion programme’, ‘Iron and folic acid supplementation in pregnant women’ and ‘Vitamin A supplementation in IYC’, among others.37 While these programmes prioritise population groups most affected by DBM and single forms of malnutrition, clearer strategies for outreach, implementation and integration are needed to ensure equitable impact; otherwise, there is a risk of widening nutrition-related health inequities. Given that undernutrition-focused programmes are likely to continue (eg, micronutrient supplementation programmes), there is also an important opportunity to integrate overnutrition prevention strategies, such as obesity awareness and the inclusion of overnutrition indicators, within existing nutrition platforms.38 The outlook of this study’s findings suggest that, to optimise resource allocation and strengthen ongoing and future initiatives to address DBM in Guatemala, policymakers and researchers should (1) prioritise equitable initiatives that include targeted strategies for the most vulnerable groups, (2) further integrate undernutrition and overnutrition programmes within existing programmes and (3) strengthen the policy and programme cycle through evidence-based implementation, monitoring and impact evaluation, with results actively informing programme redesign.

Evidence from similar settings offers important lessons for addressing DBM. A literature review in LMICs found that the majority of maternal and IYC health interventions and school-based programmes focused on behaviour change (eg, education on IYC feeding practices) were generally effective in reducing DBM, whereas interventions centred on the provision of specific foods or nutritional supplements had suboptimal effects.39 Another study examining shared drivers of multiple forms of malnutrition and the potential effectiveness of recommended DDAs suggested that DDAs could be strengthened by incorporating social support systems to improve education, family planning and access to health services, and by ensuring that antenatal care programmes address women’s health across the entire reproductive life course.40 In addition, evidence from Peru highlights key barriers to government-level actions on multiple forms of malnutrition in IYC, including weak monitoring and evaluation of policies, limited implementation oversight and insufficient enforcement mechanisms.41 Summarising, this evidence underscores the importance of ongoing and future initiatives in Guatemala through integrated behaviour change strategies, broader and more inclusive programme designs, and robust monitoring and evaluation frameworks to ensure effective implementation and sustained impact.

This is the first study to investigate the overlap in sociodemographic factors between DBM and its single forms, identifying context-specific opportunities to address DBM among households, IYC stunting alone and overweight in women. The study also draws on the most recent and available nationally representative nutrition data from Guatemala. To our knowledge, this is the first analysis to examine DBM using SIVESNU data, providing new estimates and enabling comparisons with other data sources. This study has some limitations. It focuses exclusively on the household outcomes derived from the combinations of women with or without overweight and IYC with or without stunting. This decision was made because previous analyses have shown that this typology of DBM is the most frequent in Guatemala12; however, this focus does not intend to minimise the relevance of other DBM typologies in Guatemala but to prioritise the typology with the largest burden in this context. Also, this study was limited to only sociodemographic factors, as these factors appear to be key factors of malnutrition and DBM in Guatemala. Nevertheless, this focus narrows the scope by excluding other important associated factors, such as environmental, biological and behavioural factors that also play a critical role. As the main objective was to inform national programmes targeting malnutrition in Guatemala, these findings and their interpretation might not be generalisable to other settings. Furthermore, information bias from self-reported sociodemographic data is common, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors, or from factors measured inconsistently across surveys, cannot be entirely ruled out and should be considered when interpreting these findings.

Conclusion

Using data derived from the Epidemiological Health and Nutrition Surveillance System in Guatemala collected between 2013 and 2019, this study identified sociodemographic factors that were uniquely associated with households with DBM, as well as factors shared with households experiencing single forms of malnutrition. Middle household asset level and lack of women’s health insurance emerged as factors uniquely associated with households experiencing DBM. In contrast, characteristics such as low maternal education, Indigenous ethnicity and higher parity were shared factors across households with DBM and households with single malnutrition; however, the strength of these associations was generally greater among households with DBM. These findings highlight the need for integrated, equity-oriented public health interventions that simultaneously address undernutrition and overnutrition, target critical life stages, and are tailored to Guatemala’s diverse sociodemographic contexts. We encourage decision-makers to consider these findings when adapting or formulating actions to address malnutrition in Guatemala.
