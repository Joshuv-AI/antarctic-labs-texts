Thicker polyethylene inserts for varus deformity versus thinner inserts for flexion contracture in total knee arthroplasty: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Seung-Hun Lee, Hyuck Min Kwon, Woo-Suk Lee, Kwan Kyu Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe success of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) depends on achieving precise mechanical alignment and stable soft tissue balance, with the thickness of the final polyethylene (PE) insert serving as a critical component. However, the impact of preoperative factors on PE thickness remains unclear. In this study, we investigated the effects of preoperative knee alignment and clinical deformities on PE thickness.Materials and methodsThe medical records of patients who underwent primary TKA between August 2024 and July 2025 were retrospectively evaluated. Preoperative radiographic parameters, including the hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle, medial proximal tibial angle, lateral distal femoral angle (LDFA), joint-line convergence angle, weight-bearing line ratio (WBLR), and posterior tibial slope, were measured, along with the flexion contracture (FC) as a clinical factor. Intra-observer reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). Linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the effects of these preoperative factors on PE thickness.ResultsThis study included 233 knees (158 patients). The intra-observer reliability of the radiographic parameters was excellent (ICC > 0.9). Multivariable linear regression revealed that preoperative HKA angle (β = 0.033, p < 0.001), WBLR (β = −0.008, p < 0.001), and LDFA (β = 0.065, p = 0.003) were significantly associated with final PE thickness. Preoperative FC demonstrated a significant negative correlation (β = −0.027, p = 0.011). In a subgroup analysis of knees with minimal FC, the mean PE thickness increased from 10.37 mm in mild varus to 12.75 mm in extreme varus.ConclusionsPreoperative HKA angle and FC were identified as significant predictors of PE insert thickness in TKA. Specifically, greater varus deformity resulted in a thicker PE insert, whereas more severe FC led to a thinner insert. Clinically, surgeons must anticipate gap expansion after medial release in varus knees, while accommodating the tight extension space in the FC.Level of evidence Level III, retrospective comparative study.

Background

The optimal management of end-stage knee osteoarthritis requires precise surgical intervention to restore function and alleviate pain. Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is firmly established as the standard of care for these patients, demonstrating high success rates and substantial improvements in quality of life [1–4]. Given the increasing prevalence of TKA in an aging society, ensuring the longevity of implants and patient satisfaction is imperative.

However, achieving ideal surgical outcomes is not always feasible owing to the complexity of individual anatomical variations. The long-term survival after TKA is heavily dependent on restoring accurate mechanical alignment and securing a stable soft tissue balance throughout the range of motion [5–7]. Improper alignment or imbalance can lead to premature polyethylene (PE) wear, loosening, and instability [8, 9]. These factors contribute to the challenge of surgical planning, particularly in knees with severe preoperative deformities, such as significant varus/valgus angulation, ligamentous laxity, or bony defects.

The PE insert acts not only as an articular interface but also as a critical component for fine-tuning the flexion and extension gaps to compensate for soft tissue imbalances [10, 11]. The thickness of the final PE insert reflects the degree of intraoperative correction required to achieve stability [12]. In particular, inaccurate gap balancing during this step can lead to an excessively loose extension gap, resulting in iatrogenic genu recurvatum. Intraoperative recurvatum is a highly distressing condition for surgeons, as postoperative recurvatum is notoriously associated with persistent instability and poor clinical outcomes that tend to worsen over time [13]. Therefore, understanding the preoperative factors that necessitate a thicker PE insert is expected to enhance preoperative planning and predictive accuracy. Nevertheless, there is currently a gap in the existing research; while many studies have focused on postoperative alignment or wear, few have systematically analyzed the impact of both preoperative radiographic deformities and clinical factors on the intraoperative decision of PE thickness.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to investigate the effects of preoperative knee alignment and deformity on the final PE insert thickness used in TKA, including key clinical measures such as flexion contracture. It was hypothesized that preoperative factors indicating severe deformity would significantly predict PE insert thickness and, specifically, that a larger hip–knee–ankle (HKA) angle would necessitate a thicker PE insert.

Materials and methods

Patient recruitment

This study was approved by our institutional review board (no. 4-2026-0078), who waived the requirement for informed consent owing to the retrospective nature of the study. The electronic medical records of patients who underwent primary TKA at our hospital between 1 August 2024 and 31 July 2025 were retrospectively reviewed. The patient cohort was carefully selected, and individuals were excluded on the basis of the following criteria: (1) history of primary or revision arthroplasty of the ipsilateral hip or knee joint; (2) presence of post-traumatic arthritis with severe bone loss or active infection around the knee; and (3) history of fracture or prior surgical intervention on the affected lower extremity. Thus, the final cohort comprised solely patients with primary knee osteoarthritis who underwent standard primary TKA (using either the Persona [Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA] or PNK [Skyve Co. Ltd, Seoul, Republic of Korea] implant systems). Patients were allocated to receive either the PNK or Persona TKA design in a random manner. The two TKA designs share the same minimum PE insert thickness of 10 mm but have a slight difference in their available size increments. Specifically, the PNK system offers PE thicknesses of 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, and 18 mm, whereas the Persona system provides thicknesses of 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, and 18 mm. All surgical procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon with the aim of achieving neutral mechanical alignment using a measured resection technique.

Demographic data and radiograph acquisition

Baseline demographic data, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and operative side, were comprehensively recorded. Standardized preoperative imaging, consisting of full-length weight-bearing anteroposterior (FLWAP) radiographs and lateral knee radiographs, was performed for every patient scheduled for TKA.

For the FLWAP acquisition protocol, patients were positioned with the patella facing strictly anteriorly toward the X-ray beam. A constant tube-to-detector distance of 300 cm was used to ensure dimensional accuracy and to mitigate magnification artifacts. These segmental images were digitally stitched to generate a continuous single-view representation of the lower limb axis. For lateral knee radiographs, the precise superimposition of both femoral condyles is required to confirm a strictly true lateral view.

Clinical measurement

The preoperative flexion contracture (FC) was measured at an outpatient clinic using a standard goniometer. Measurements were performed with the patient in the supine position and recorded as lacking full knee extension.

Radiographic measurements

Preoperative evaluation of alignment and bony deformity was performed using a comprehensive set of radiographic parameters (Fig. 1). The HKA angle was used to quantify the overall mechanical axis deviation by measuring the angle between the mechanical axes of the femur and tibia. To isolate the compartment-specific bony pathology, the MPTA and LDFA were calculated to describe the morphological condition of the proximal tibia and distal femur, respectively. Furthermore, the JLCA provided an objective measure of intraarticular angular deformity and implied ligamentous deficiency. The WBLR determines the location at which the mechanical axis intersects the tibial plateau. Finally, the PTS was evaluated using lateral knee radiographs, defined as the angle between the tangent to the medial tibial plateau and the posterior cortex of the proximal tibia [15].

All radiographic calculations were performed using a dedicated Picture Archiving and Communication System. To ensure maximum accuracy and reliability, a single experienced orthopedic surgeon meticulously performed all radiographic measurements and repeated the measurements at 4-week intervals to minimize assessment bias. Crucially, the observer remained strictly blinded to both the patient identification data and the specific PE insert thickness used during the operation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The intra-observer reliability was assessed to establish the measurement reliability of the radiographic parameters. For the main analysis, the mean values of the two measurements were employed for the radiographic variables (HKA, WBLR, MPTA, LDFA, JLCA, and PTS) to mitigate measurement error. To assess reliability, the intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals, utilizing a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement, were calculated for these continuous radiographic variables based on paired measurements taken 4 weeks apart. Clinical variables, such as FC, were analyzed using a single measurement recorded during the outpatient clinic visit. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of the continuous variables was assumed on the basis of the central limit theorem, given the sufficiently large sample size (n = 233), which justified the use of linear regression analyses.

Linear regression analyses were conducted to investigate the association between preoperative factors and final PE insert thickness. Initially, univariable linear regression was performed to assess the unadjusted impact of each radiographic and clinical parameter. Subsequently, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors, adjusting for potentially confounding demographic factors, including age, sex, and BMI. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Furthermore, a subgroup analysis was performed on knees with minimal FC (≤ 5°) by categorizing the HKA angle into 5° intervals to intuitively evaluate the isolated effect of varus deformity severity on the mean PE insert thickness.

Discussion

The principal finding of the present study was that preoperative radiographic alignment and clinical deformity were decisive predictors of the final PE insert thickness in primary TKA. Specifically, the overall coronal mechanical alignment (HKA angle) and FC severity were identified as prominent independent determinants. While numerous preceding studies have extensively investigated the impact of PE thickness on postoperative clinical outcomes such as range of motion, implant wear, laxity, and revision rates, there remains a paucity of research systematically analyzing the preoperative factors that necessitate a specific PE thickness [16–19]. To the best of our knowledge, this study differs from others in that it shifted the focus of its analysis from “postoperative outcomes” to “preoperative decision factors.” By elucidating the causal relationship between preoperative deformities and the required insert thickness, this study provides significant clinical value and offers surgeons predictive data to optimize preoperative planning.

Our results demonstrated a strong positive correlation between the severity of coronal deformity (HKA angle, LDFA, and JLCA) and the final PE thickness, statistically validating the conventional surgical wisdom that “a larger varus deformity inevitably leads to a thicker PE insert.” The biomechanical rationale for this is straightforward. In a knee with severe varus deformity, the medial soft tissue structures, including the deep and superficial medial collateral ligaments, are chronically contracted, while the lateral structures may be lax [20–22]. To achieve a rectangular flexion and extension gap perpendicular to the mechanical axis, the surgeon must perform an extensive medial soft tissue release. As the contracted medial tissues are released to restore neutral alignment, the tibiofemoral joint space expands naturally. Unlike the concave (medial) side, the convex (lateral) side often presents with laxity or requires minimal release. Consequently, once medial contracture is resolved and the limb is straightened, the resulting joint gap volume becomes significantly larger than that of a nondeformed knee. A thicker PE insert is required to bridge this expanded gap and achieve appropriate ligamentous tension without instability. Failure to adequately fill this expanded space can result in an excessively loose extension gap and subsequent genu recurvatum, a complication notoriously associated with progressive worsening and poor clinical outcomes. This mechanism explains why the HKA angle is a potent positive predictor. Although this relationship might be considered natural among experienced surgeons, this study provides empirical evidence that the degree of preoperative varus deformity is directly proportional to the required increase in PE thickness. As specifically demonstrated in our subgroup analysis, the mean PE thickness increased by over 2 mm, ranging from 10.37 mm in mild varus (0–5°) to 12.75 mm in extreme varus (> 20°), when the confounding effect of FC was minimized. This apparent increase provides surgeons with a more concrete numerical expectation of gap expansion during the preoperative planning phase.

Perhaps the most clinically intriguing finding of this study was the significant negative correlation between preoperative FC and final PE thickness. Our data indicated that patients with severe FC tended to receive thinner PE inserts. This counterintuitive result warrants a deeper discussion regarding surgical strategies for addressing FC and its biomechanical consequences. Severe FC is characterized by pathological shortening of the posterior capsule and associated posterior structures [23, 24]. In the standard TKA workflow, after routine distal femoral and proximal tibial resections, these patients often present with a flexion–extension gap mismatch, where the extension gap is significantly tighter than the flexion gap owing to posterior tethering. When confronted with a tight extension gap, the surgeon faces a critical intraoperative dilemma: either perform additional distal femoral resection to widen the extension gap, or perform extensive posterior soft tissue release and accept a thinner PE insert to accommodate the tight space.

Although increasing the distal femoral cut is a potent method of correcting FC, it requires extreme caution. As noted in surgical principles, if the distal femur is resected excessively to match the standard PE thickness, the extension gap may become disproportionately wider than the flexion gap. This is particularly perilous if the patient has inherent soft tissue laxity or if the posterior release eventually renders the tissue lax. An excessively wide extension gap can lead to mid-flexion instability or hyperextension (recurvatum) postoperatively. Furthermore, elevating the joint line via excessive distal resection can induce patellar baja, altering patellofemoral mechanics, and potentially causing anterior knee pain or impingement. Our finding that FC leads to a thinner PE suggests that the surgical procedures in this study tended to adopt a safer, more conservative strategy. Rather than aggressively resecting the distal femur and risking gap instability or joint line elevation, surgeons appear to prioritize posterior capsular release and osteophyte removal, ultimately accepting a thinner PE insert to “fit” the naturally tight extension gap. This approach minimizes the risk of creating an iatrogenic loose extension gap, which is difficult to manage and often requires constrained liners. Thus, the negative correlation observed in the present study reflects a prudent surgical compromise to maintain stability and preserve the native joint line.

Another noteworthy observation is the differential impacts of the regional and global alignment parameters. Both the femoral-side parameter (LDFA) and WBLR showed stronger associations with final PE thickness than the tibial-side parameter (MPTA). In standard TKA techniques, proximal tibial resection is relatively standardized and is typically perpendicular to the long axis, thereby providing a consistent platform regardless of the deformity. By contrast, distal femoral resection was the primary variable adjusted to correct the deformity. Therefore, the morphological variations in the distal femur (LDFA) and displacement of the mechanical axis (WBLR) exert a more direct and dominant influence on the final gap dynamics and subsequent choice of PE thickness than tibial variations.

Collectively, the preoperative HKA angle and FC served as the two principal axes that determine the requisite PE thickness. While the HKA angle reflects the magnitude of the necessary soft tissue release and the subsequent expansion of the joint space, favoring a thicker insert, the FC imposes a restrictive ceiling on the extension gap dimension, necessitating a conservative PE selection. Therefore, by concurrently assessing these two distinct biomechanical drivers, surgeons can precisely predict the final implant construct to be inserted during TKA and execute the surgical procedure.

Ultimately, the clinical relevance of this study extends beyond the mere statistical correlation between radiographic parameters and implant specifications and lies in its potential to bridge the gap between preoperative analysis and intraoperative surgical reality. Just as precise radiographic assessment is a prerequisite for successful treatment, the ability to anticipate the necessary PE thickness serves as a vital adjunct to surgical execution. In this context, severe varus deformity and FC should not be viewed solely as pathologies to be corrected but as predictive indicators that assist surgeons in considering gap dynamics. Specifically, these factors highlight the potential need for a thicker insert owing to release-induced gap expansion versus a thinner insert to accommodate a tight extension space. By integrating these predictive factors into the surgical planning phase, surgeons can select the optimal PE insert thickness, thereby ensuring accurate mechanical alignment and securing stable soft tissue balance throughout the range of motion. Consequently, this approach helps mitigate the risks of instability and premature wear caused by improper balance, thereby contributing to the ultimate goal of securing long-term survival after TKA.

The present study has several limitations. First, it was conducted by a single surgeon at a single institution. While this ensures high consistency in surgical techniques and decision-making regarding PE thickness selection, it may limit the external validity and generalization of the findings to other surgeons with different gap-balancing philosophies. Second, the retrospective nature of this study introduces potential selection and confounding biases. Third, as all procedures in this study aimed for neutral mechanical alignment using a measured resection technique, our findings may not be directly generalizable to other alignment strategies, such as kinematic or functional (robotic) alignment, which involve different intraoperative gap dynamics. Fourth, although patients were randomly allocated to the two TKA designs, there was a slight discrepancy in the available PE insert sizes between the systems. While both systems have a minimum thickness of 10 mm, the Persona system includes a 13 mm option that is absent in the PNK system. This minor difference in size increments could be considered a limitation, as it might have subtly influenced the exact thickness distribution in our comparative analysis. Lastly, we focused on the correlation between preoperative deformity and final PE thickness but did not quantitatively measure the intraoperative soft tissue status, such as the exact extent of ligament release or inherent laxity. The lack of quantified intraoperative data on soft-tissue tension and ligamentous laxity remains a limitation that should be addressed in future studies. Future prospective studies are warranted to overcome these limitations and validate our findings.

Conclusions

Preoperative HKA angle and FC were identified as significant independent predictors of final PE insert thickness in TKA. Specifically, a greater varus deformity (larger HKA angle) resulted in a thicker PE insert, whereas more severe FC led to a thinner PE insert. Clinically, surgeons must anticipate gap expansion after medial release in varus knees, which requires a thicker insert while accommodating the tight extension space in the FC.
