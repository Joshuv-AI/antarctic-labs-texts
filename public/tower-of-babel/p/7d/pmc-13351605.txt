Diagnostic Challenges and Clinical Management of Area Postrema Syndrome: A Case Report on Persistent Nausea and Vomiting
Authors: Andreea‐Gabriela Manole, D. Ion, Alexandra Bolocan, O. Andronic, D. N. Păduraru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArea postrema syndrome (APS) is a rare neurological syndrome that presents a significant diagnostic challenge due to its nonspecific clinical manifestations, most commonly persistent nausea and vomiting, which frequently lead to initial misdiagnosis as a gastrointestinal disorder. This case report illustrates the diagnostic complexity of APS and underscores the importance of considering central nervous system etiologies in patients with unexplained, refractory gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly in younger individuals. Early recognition through brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and serologic testing for aquaporin‐4 immunoglobulin G (AQP4‐IgG) antibodies is essential, as it enables prompt initiation of high‐dose corticosteroid therapy and appropriate long‐term immunomodulatory treatment. Delayed diagnosis may result in cumulative neurological injury, including severe complications such as optic neuritis. This case highlights the critical role of a multidisciplinary approach in the evaluation and management of APS, to prevent irreversible neurological disability.

Introduction

Area postrema is a highly vascularized structure situated at the floor of the fourth ventricle in the dorsal medulla [1]. It is one of the brain's circumventricular organs, lacking a typical blood–brain barrier (BBB) and containing fenestrated capillaries that render it permeable to circulating substances [2]. This unique anatomy allows the area postrema to function as a key chemoreceptive zone interfacing between the circulatory and nervous systems, detecting blood‐borne chemicals and toxins [3]. Stimulation of the area postrema's receptors by emetic agents triggers downstream nuclei (sometimes referred to collectively as the “vomiting center”) that induce physiological responses such as nausea and vomiting [4]. Owing to its location outside the BBB and high vascular permeability, the area postrema is especially vulnerable to blood‐borne pathological factors, effectively serving as a critical sensor linking systemic circulation to central autonomic responses [5, 6].

Area postrema syndrome (APS) is a clinical syndrome defined by the triad of intractable nausea, vomiting, and hiccups attributable to lesion or dysfunction of the area postrema [7]. From a clinical perspective, APS represents a cluster of symptoms secondary to an underlying neurological disorder rather than a distinct disease itself [8]. Among the various reported causes of APS, the most frequently reported underlying cause is neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD)—an autoimmune, relapsing inflammatory disorder marked by severe demyelination and astrocyte injury, predominantly affecting the optic nerves and spinal cord [4]. In fact, the 2015 International Panel for NMO Diagnosis (IPND) recognized APS as one of the six core clinical characteristics of NMOSD [9]. Notably, APS may represent the initial manifestation of NMOSD, preceding more classically recognized features such as optic neuritis or transverse myelitis [10].

Clinically, APS typically presents with persistent episodes of intractable nausea and vomiting (often accompanied by hiccups) that can last for days or even weeks [11]. These protracted emetic symptoms often mimic primary gastrointestinal (GI) disorders, leading patients to undergo extensive gastroenterological evaluations. Misdiagnosis is common: many patients are initially thought to have GI conditions (e.g., gastroparesis, reflux, or infection) and receive ineffective treatments before the neurological origin is considered [12]. Consequently, there can be significant delays in recognizing the neurological nature of APS, especially when standard GI investigations fail to reveal an explanation [13]. This diagnostic challenge underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for APS in patients with unexplained, intractable vomiting syndromes.

The diagnosis of APS is supported by characteristic findings on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brainstem, typically demonstrating lesions in the dorsal medulla at the level of the area postrema [14]. Such imaging evidence of a medullary lesion can be crucial, particularly in seronegative cases, to confirm that the nausea and vomiting are neurologically mediated [10]. Serologic testing for aquaporin‐4 immunoglobulin G (AQP4‐IgG) antibodies does not diagnose APS per se; rather, the presence of AQP4‐IgG confirms NMOSD as the underlying etiology when APS is one of its clinical manifestations [15]. Early recognition of APS is therefore clinically significant: identifying APS as a harbinger of NMOSD allows prompt initiation of appropriate immunotherapy, which can alleviate the syndrome and reduce the risk of further neurological relapses [16]. Delayed diagnosis, on the other hand, may postpone effective treatment and increase the likelihood of additional central nervous system injury and disability associated with NMOSD [17].

Case History/Examination

A 27‐year‐old female patient with a history of multiple episodes of nausea and vomiting, which began approximately two months prior to presentation, occurring as recurrent daily episodes without complete symptom‐free intervals, was previously diagnosed with microlithiasis via ultrasound and underwent an uneventful laparoscopic cholecystectomy at another medical facility. No significant unintentional weight loss was reported during this period. She presented to the General Surgery Clinic due to the persistence of symptoms postoperatively. The patient was admitted for further investigations to establish the etiology of her symptoms and to receive specialized treatment.

The patient's medical history includes significant surgical interventions such as bilateral salpingectomy for ectopic pregnancies and a traditional appendectomy. Upon clinical examination, the patient was found to be in satisfactory general condition, afebrile, and stable from both a hemodynamic and respiratory standpoint. Physical examination revealed a recently scarred abdomen (post‐laparoscopic cholecystectomy) with intact sutures, which was mobile with respiration, non‐tender on palpation, and without signs of peritoneal irritation. Bowel transit was present for both gas and fecal matter, and she had normal urination with normochromic urine. Neurological examination revealed no focal motor, sensory, cerebellar, or cranial nerve deficits.

Initial laboratory investigations were within normal limits, except for mild electrolyte imbalances characterized by hypernatremia and hypokalemia, likely due to repeated vomiting episodes.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The patient was diagnosed with a persistent emetic syndrome, and a differential diagnosis was considered, including both digestive and extra‐digestive pathologies such as delayed postoperative ileus, postoperative adhesion syndrome, gastroenteritis, hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), otorhinolaryngological conditions, neuropsychiatric disorders, and oncological diseases.

Delayed postoperative ileus was ruled out due to the presence of bowel transit for gas and fecal matter. Given the patient's surgical history, postoperative adhesion syndrome was considered, but subsequently ruled out following a thoraco‐abdomino‐pelvic CT scan, which showed no gastric, small intestine, colonic, or tumor dilations. However, parietal submucosal edema with an inflammatory appearance was observed throughout the entire colonic frame, raising suspicion of a Clostridium difficile infection. Nevertheless, fecal testing for this bacterium was negative.

To investigate the possibility of GERD or hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, an esophagogastroduodenal examination with oral administration of iodinated contrast was performed. The radiological examination revealed a 3 cm reducible hiatal hernia, which was noted during the vomiting reflex, absent gastroesophageal reflux, and a normal appearance of the stomach, pylorus, duodenal bulb, and duodenal frame (Figure 1).

Thus, after ruling out the primary digestive conditions, attention shifted toward suspecting extra‐digestive pathologies that could manifest through nausea and vomiting. Following a psychiatric evaluation, the patient was diagnosed with mood disorder, and after an ENT consultation, she was diagnosed with vertiginous syndrome. Based on the neurologist's recommendation, a re‐evaluation was scheduled after brain imaging, and a cerebral MRI was performed. The native MRI revealed a small focal area with T2/FLAIR hyperintensity and minimal DWI hyperintensity located in the posterior bulbo‐spinal region adjacent to the floor of the fourth ventricle, without mass effect on the adjacent structures, findings consistent with APS (Figure 2).

Native cerebral MRI: Sagittal section (A), axial section (B). A small hyperintense area is highlighted in the posterior bulbo‐spinal region adjacent to the floor of the fourth ventricle, consistent with APS.

Based on the MRI findings, serological testing for AQP4‐IgG was initiated, with results pending at the time of transfer to the Neurology department. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was not performed during the surgical admission.

Following the diagnosis, the patient was treated with 1 g of intravenous Methylprednisolone daily for 5 days.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Following corticosteroid therapy, the patient showed a favorable clinical evolution, with significant improvement of nausea and vomiting and normalization of electrolyte imbalances. She was subsequently transferred to the Neurology Department for further investigations, diagnostic confirmation of suspected NMOSD, and initiation of long‐term immunotherapy. At the time of transfer, she was hemodynamically stable, tolerating oral intake, and without newly developed neurological deficits. Serological testing for AQP4‐IgG antibodies was pending, and close neurological follow‐up was planned.

Discussion

This case is notable due to the initial attribution of the patient's symptomatology to a digestive pathology, specifically microlithiasis, as diagnosed by ultrasound. However, the persistence of symptoms following a laparoscopic cholecystectomy, which was performed without complications, raised significant suspicions. This highlights the diagnostic challenge associated with cases where symptoms such as nausea and vomiting are commonly linked to gastrointestinal disorders. In such instances, it is crucial to consider alternative diagnoses, particularly when the symptomatology persists postoperatively.

APS should be regarded as a clinical syndrome rather than a distinct disease entity and it represents a rare but important cause that should be considered in the differential diagnosis of persistent nausea and vomiting [18]. The diagnosis is typically based on a clinical presentation characterized by intractable nausea and vomiting lasting more than 48 h without an apparent cause [19]. The gold‐standard imaging modality for confirming the diagnosis is cerebral MRI, which reveals characteristic lesions in the area postrema [20]. APS has been reported in association with multiple underlying etiologies, including NMOSD, ischemic stroke, tumors, infections, and metabolic disturbances. Among inflammatory etiologies, particularly NMOSD, approximately 80% of patients presenting with APS have detectable AQP4‐IgG in serum [21]. While MRI findings support the diagnosis of APS, AQP4‐IgG seropositivity supports NMOSD as the underlying etiology [22].

The therapeutic approach to APS must therefore be etiology‐specific and should not be generalized. In cases where APS is secondary to inflammatory demyelinating disorders such as NMOSD, the administration of high‐dose intravenous Methylprednisolone (1 g per day for 3–5 days) is the first‐line treatment for acute exacerbations [23]. In patients who do not tolerate corticosteroids, plasmapheresis may be considered as an alternative therapeutic option [24]. Early and effective management is critical to prevent the progression to more severe neurological impairments, including sensory and motor dysfunctions [25]. Given the potential for significant neurological disability in NMOSD‐associated APS, it is recommended to initiate long‐term immunomodulatory therapy as soon as possible after diagnostic confirmation [26]. Current evidence supports long‐term, often prolonged or indefinite treatment in patients with AQP4‐IgG–positive NMOSD, as there is no clearly defined indication for treatment discontinuation after a fixed duration [27].

The long‐term management of APS in the context of NMOSD is typically guided by the presence of AQP4‐IgG [28]. For example, if these antibodies are detected in the serum, the patient may benefit from treatment with eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that targets complement protein C5 and has been shown to be effective in reducing relapses in patients with NMOSD [29]. More recently approved therapies, including ravulizumab, inebilizumab, and satralizumab, have expanded the therapeutic options for long‐term disease modification in NMOSD [30]. By contrast, for patients who do not have AQP4‐IgG (seronegative cases), management often relies on traditional immunosuppressive drugs, such as rituximab, azathioprine, or mycophenolate mofetil, which have been used to reduce relapse frequency in AQP4‐IgG‐negative or myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein immunoglobulin G (MOG‐IgG)–associated cases [31]. These treatments aim to modulate the immune response and prevent further autoimmune attacks that could exacerbate neurological symptoms [12].

The early identification and treatment of APS as a potential first clinical manifestation of NMOSD are critical in mitigating long‐term complications [8]. Undiagnosed and untreated patients are at risk of progressive neurological injury with each attack—for instance, recurrent NMOSD relapses can cumulatively cause severe visual loss, paraplegia, or other permanent deficits [25]. One of the most common neurological manifestations associated with NMOSD presenting with APS is optic neuritis, which can lead to significant visual impairment if left untreated [32]. For this reason, it is strongly recommended that patients with APS undergo ophthalmologic evaluation, including Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) of the optic nerve, to assess for early signs of optic nerve damage [6].

The case discussed here underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in managing patients with persistent nausea and vomiting, particularly when initial treatment directed at gastrointestinal causes is ineffective. Collaboration between surgeons, neurologists, psychiatrists, and other specialists is essential to ensure a comprehensive diagnostic workup and to tailor appropriate treatment strategies for each patient. Additionally, this case highlights the need for greater awareness of APS in clinical practice, as early recognition and intervention can significantly improve patient outcomes.

Several cases reported in the medical literature illustrate the diagnostic challenges and diverse presentations of APS within the spectrum of NMOSD, particularly when APS represents the initial manifestation of the disease. APS often manifests with persistent nausea and vomiting and can be misdiagnosed as a gastrointestinal disorder. For instance, a case involving a young woman from Togo presented with APS as the first indication of NMOSD, which was later complicated by central nervous system tuberculosis. This highlights the need for thorough investigation, especially in regions with high tuberculosis prevalence, as infections like tuberculosis have been reported in association with NMOSD and may coexist or complicate its clinical course [33].

Another case involved a 72‐year‐old woman, where APS was accompanied by painful tonic spasms, showcasing how diverse and debilitating the symptoms of NMOSD can be. Her diagnosis was confirmed via MRI, revealing characteristic lesions in the area postrema. Early treatment with steroids and long‐term immunotherapy was essential in managing her condition [34].

These cases emphasize the importance of early MRI imaging to identify characteristic brainstem lesions and prompt initiation of appropriate immunotherapy in NMOSD, particularly when AQP4‐IgG is present. Without timely diagnosis and intervention, patients are at significant risk of developing severe neurological complications, such as optic neuritis [19, 35].

Finally, it is important to consider the broader implications of this case for clinical practice. APS, although rare, serves as a reminder that the differential diagnosis for nausea and vomiting should extend beyond common gastrointestinal disorders. Neurological, psychiatric, and other extra‐digestive causes must be carefully considered, particularly in patients with atypical presentations or those who do not respond to standard treatments. By expanding the diagnostic framework, healthcare providers can improve the likelihood of identifying less common but equally important conditions such as APS, leading to better patient care and outcomes.

Conclusion

APS presents a significant diagnostic challenge due to its clinical manifestations, which often mimic gastrointestinal disorders such as persistent nausea and vomiting. In patients with unexplained gastrointestinal symptoms and no clear digestive findings, APS should be considered as part of the differential diagnosis. Failure to recognize this rare condition in a timely manner may delay appropriate treatment and increase the risk of severe neurological complications. Early diagnosis through imaging and prompt initiation of treatment can significantly improve outcomes and help prevent long‐term disability.
