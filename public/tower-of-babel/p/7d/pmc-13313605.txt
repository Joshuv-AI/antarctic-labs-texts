Orchestrating neuroinflammation: A symphony of Interleukin-1 and cell type-specific Interleukin-1 receptor type 1 signaling in the brain
Authors: Daniel Nemeth, Vesal Farsian, Matt Schrier, Ning Quan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interleukin-1 (IL-1) was one of the first inflammatory cytokines to be discovered in the peripheral immune system. It is a master regulator of the inflammatory responses to infection and injury in the entire body. Infection and injury to the central nervous system (CNS) inevitably activate the IL-1 system similar to the periphery. While the existence of inflammation in the CNS, neuroinflammation, is not surprising, the CNS represents a unique structure-functional entity that requires special consideration for the understanding of the role of the IL-1 system in the brain. First, the presence of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) restricts the entry of many immune signal molecules and cells from easy access to the brain, preventing undesirable consequences of peripheral style of inflammation (swelling, for example) that could overwhelm the finite space of the brain; second, the presence of brain specific cell types—neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, ventricular ependymal cells, and choroid plexus cells—suggests IL-1 system may conduct neuroinflammation differently in this special environment through these cell types; third, recent studies have revealed that IL-1 can directly modulate neural circuitry and function and the subsequent neuroimmune crosstalk impacts neuroinflammation, neural function, and behavior, expanding the role of the IL-1 system into the realm of neurobiology. This mini-review summarizes the current state of knowledge about how the CNS IL-1 system might regulate neuroinflammation.

Introduction

The discovery of IL-1 immediately implicated its dual role in the immune system and the nervous systems. IL-1 was first identified as the endogenous pyrogen that is produced by immune cells (Garlanda et al., 2013). Thus, the febrile response typically induced by bacterial endotoxin was found to be mediated by IL-1. As fever was known as a CNS-controlled activity rather than a malfunction of peripheral thermoregulatory elements, IL-1 was suspected to have central functions beyond its peripheral inflammatory activities (Blatteis and Sehic, 1998; Li et al., 2001). Administration of IL-1 confirms that brain IL-1 affects neurophysiological functions including sleep (Opp et al., 1991), body temperature regulation (Blatteis and Sehic, 1998), hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis activity (Goshen and Yirmiya,2009), food and water intake (Liu et al., 2019), neuroendocrine control of energy metabolism (Del Rey et al., 2016), and brain-regulated immunological negative feedback control of peripheral immunity (Lebonville et al., 2016; Paniccia et al., 2018) (See reviews by Goshen and Yirmiya, 2009; Nemeth and Quan, 2021). More recently, CNS IL-1 was found to modulate neurodevelopment through neurocircuit remodeling (Ma et al., 2014), and the regulation of cognitive (Avital et al., 2003; Ben Menachem-Zidon et al., 2011; Besedovsky, 2019; DiSabato et al., 2020; Goshen et al., 2007; Yirmiya and Goshen, 2011) and social behaviors (Borreca et al., 2024; DiSabato et al., 2020). Not surprisingly, such broad influence of IL-1 on neurophysiological functions naturally led to investigations of its role in various CNS disorders and neuropathological conditions. Links between IL-1 and almost all CNS disorders have been found, including traumatic brain or spinal cord injury (Freria et al., 2020; Vincent et al., 2023), epilepsy (Iori et al., 2017), Alzheimer’s disease (Shaftel et al., 2007), Parkinson’s disease (Muhammad et al., 2023; Niu et al., 2020), cerebral ischemia (Allan et al., 2005), depression (Dantzer et al., 1999; Zhu et al., 2010), anxiety disorders (Koo and Duman, 2009; McKim et al., 2018; Murray et al., 2013), and neurological sequela of CNS bacterial or viral infections (Fekete et al., 2025; Garber et al., 2018). Thus, understanding CNS IL-1 biology is of paramount importance.

A technical difficulty for studying the CNS IL-1 system is that both the ligands (IL-1α and IL-1β) and the functional receptors for IL-1 (type 1 IL-1 receptors, IL-1R1 and the IL-1R accessory protein, IL-1RAcP) are expressed at very low levels in the CNS. Previous in situ hybridization results published in the widely referenced Allen Brain maps did not show convincingly detectible IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-1R1, and IL-1RAcP in normal adult brains although functional analyses suggest otherwise. After brain tissue injury or inflammatory stimulations, IL-1 is readily detectible in the brain with conventional methods, but the IL-1 receptors are still difficult to detect. Previous studies found IL-1 system can function at extremely low levels, rendering solving the detection problem a critical bottle neck for elucidating CNS IL-1 function (Smith et al., 2009). Indeed, using IHC or ISH labeling methods to detect brain IL-1 or its receptors has generated controversial results (Nemeth et al., 2024).

An additional challenge is to dissect the cell-type specific roles of CNS IL-1 signaling. In the peripheral immune system, IL-1 is known to stimulate endothelial cells to express adhesion molecules, tissue macrophages to produce chemokines, dendritic cells to facilitate antigen presentation, T cells to promote clonal expansion, B cells to enhance proliferation and antibody class switching, thus modulating all aspects of immunity (Pyrillou et al., 2020). In the brain, IL-1 may function similarly to the periphery in specialized regions with reduced CNS-specific immunological privilege, such as the choroid plexus and the meningeal tissue, where most of the peripheral immune components (i.e. T cell, B cells, mast cells, neutrophils, and bone marrow derived monocytes) are present (Courtney et al., 2025). In the brain parenchyma, however, scarce cells of adaptive immunity are present, and innate immune responses are thought to be orchestrated by glial cells such as microglia and astrocytes (Rustenhoven and Kipnis, 2022). Therefore, inflammatory signaling via IL-1 in the brain parenchyma must be investigated in these non-conventional “immune” cell types. These glial cells are now known to play important roles in supporting neurotransmission and neurocircuit remodeling (Borreca et al., 2024), such that IL-1 signaling through these cells could translate to neurological and behavioral outcomes in addition to inflammation. Different signaling pathways have been found to mediate distinct IL-1-stimulated effects in different cell types and the overall effects of IL-1 in the CNS are likely the results of the combined activities of IL-1-stimulated effects from multiple cell types. Therefore, understanding the brain-specific, yet pleiotropic, actions of IL-1R1 signaling requires isolating these responses in a cell-type specific manner.

Structure and signaling of IL-1R1

IL-1 receptor type-1 (IL-1R1) is an important member of the IL-1 receptor/Toll-like receptor superfamily that are involved in the inflammatory processes related to detection and clearance of pathogens (Zhou et al., 2022). IL-1R1 is comprised of three extracellular immunoglobulin domains (D1-D3), a transmembrane domain, and an intracellular Toll-like/Interleukin-1 Receptor (TIR) domain. The ligands that initiate signaling bind to D1-D3 regions of IL-1R1 and are IL-1α and IL-1β. This leads to the formation of high-affinity 1:1 complexes essential for signaling initiation (Thomas et al., 2012). Endogenous antagonists, IL-1R antagonist (IL-1RA) are known to competitively bind to the same sites as IL-1α and IL-1β, at near identical affinities, blocking IL-1R complexes from forming. Crystallographic studies have revealed that to initiate signaling IL-1β contacts IL-1R1 to create a composite surface for IL-1RAcP docking and this aligns intracellular TIR domains of IL-1R1. IL-1RAcP is required for intracellular adaptor protein recruitment for proper signal transduction (Thomas et al., 2012). Indeed, deletion of IL-1RAcP with genetic approaches suppresses high-affinity IL-1β binding, prevents downstream transcriptional responses, and impairs inflammatory reactions following immune challenges suggesting IL-1RAcP is the major co-receptor required for IL-1R1 signaling (Cullinan et al., 1998). The intracellular TIR domains of IL-1R1 and IL-1RAcP interact to initiate various signaling cascades during inflammation, infection, and autoimmune disease (Cullinan et al., 1998; Thomas et al., 2012; Zhou et al., 2022). Canonical IL-1R1 signaling cascade requires Myeloid Differentiation Factor 88 (MyD88), a distinct adaptor protein of the IL-1/TLR signaling pathway(Medzhitov et al., 1998). MyD88 is thought to be the primary bridge between TIR domains and downstream kinases during inflammation through its dual domain structure containing both TIR and death domains for receptor docking and interleukin receptor associated kinases (IRAK) family kinase recruitment (Burns et al., 2003; Medzhitov et al., 1998). MyD88 and IRAK kinase recruitment leads to the activation of TRAF6 and TAK1, which subsequently phosphorylate the IKK complex to release NF-κB for nuclear translocation and inflammatory gene transcription (Holtmann et al., 2001; Wesche, Henzel, et al., 1997). TAK1 has been detected as a critical integrator of IL-1 signals in multiple cell lineages, such as fibroblasts, immune cells and epithelial barriers, at early timepoints and, at later timepoints, more extensive pathway branching is found activating p38 MAPK and JNK/ERK cascades that control diverse stress-responsive transcriptional and post-translational programs (Holtmann et al., 2001; Ninomiya-Tsuji et al., 1999). Other studies have found, following pathway dissection in multiple systems, IL-1 signaling converges on Src activation (Viviani et al., 2003), PI3K/AKT/mTOR in CD4+ T cells through the adaptor BCAP (Deason et al., 2018), and in developing brain, IL-1R1 activation in microglia restrains mTOR activity (Borreca et al., 2024). Altogether, it is interesting that a single IL-1/IL-1R1/IL-1RAcP complex can initiate a multitude of downstream pathways. This underscores the importance of studying cell type-specific IL-1R1 expression and signaling to understand how individual cell populations contribute uniquely to IL-1-mediated responses.

IL-1R1 distribution throughout the CNS

Mapping IL-1R1 in the CNS is challenging, arising from technical limitations in detection methods that results in conflicting reports of expression patterns (Nemeth et al., 2024). IL-1R1 was difficult to detect reliably through traditional methods due to low expression levels, antibody cross-reactivity issues, and limited resolution for distinguishing between neurons, glia, and endothelial cells within complex CNS tissue architecture (Licinio et al., 1991). Initial autoradiographic and immunohistochemical approaches in both fixed and isolated CNS tissue sections led to the variable detection of IL-1R1 in multiple cell types without definitive cell-type-specific resolution (Licinio et al., 1991). Because of this, we have developed genetically modified mice in which the Il1r1 gene was interrupted by an intronic loxP-flanked disruption cassette between exon 9 and 10 and a HA-tag/tdTomato reporter insertion at the 3’ end of exon 11. An IL-1R1 reporter mouse was created through germline expression of Cre recombinase and excision of the introduced disruption sequence permits IL-1R1 expression under endogenous promoters while allowing for direct visualization of both IL-1R1 mRNA (tdTomato) and protein (HA-tag) expression within all tissues (Liu et al., 2015). In addition, cell type specific Cre expression allows tracking of IL-1R1 in specific cell types. Using state-of-the-art cell type specific IL-1R1 restoration mouse lines, we have now definitely established that IL-1R1 is expressed in a subset of endothelial cells, astrocytes, ependymal cells, choroid plexus epithelial cells, and region-specific neurons (Liu et al., 2019; Nemeth et al., 2024).

Previous investigation of cell-type specific IL-1R1 function relied on in vitro assays orforced cell-type specific IL-1R1 overexpression (Ingiosi et al., 2015), and conditional knockouts in vivo (Pinteaux et al., 2020;Robson et al., 2016). However, none of these methods allow a direct test of the individual role of a singular cell-type specific IL-1R1. Liu et. al. has developed the selective “add-back” approach that allows us to test the individual cell type IL-1R1 mediated effects without the confounding effect of other cell-type IL1R1 signaling (Liu et al., 2015). Analysis of cell-type specific IL-1R1-dependent signaling in the CNS can be comprehensively examined by using all of these models. Here, we have examined the responsiveness and function of each individual cell-type specific IL-1R1 in the context of neuroinflammation.

Endothelial/pericyte IL-1R1

Inflammatory activity throughout the periphery and CNS requires signaling at the vasculature for elevated adhesion molecule production to aid slowing and rolling of leukocytes, activation of local macrophage chemokine expression, and extravasation of blood-borne leukocytes into a region of infection or injury. These basic properties of innate immune cell homing have been shown to be coordinated by cytokine signaling onto vasculature suggesting that endothelial cells and other cells of the vasculature may express cytokine receptors like IL-1R1. Indeed, endothelial cells are the predominant cell type that produce IL-1R1 (Liu et al., 2019, 2015; Song et al., 2018). In the brain, IL-1R1 is expressed primarily in medium-sized blood vessels throughout brain vasculature (Liu et al., 2015) with recent single cell RNAseq studies further pinpointing the expression pattern to veins and venules (Marchetti and Engelhardt, 2020). Veins and venules are well known as sites of immune cell trafficking as the physical characteristics of these locations permit cell diapedesis (i.e. low blood pressure, lack of smooth muscle cell barrier, small gaps in astrocyte endfeet, and abundant cytokine receptors) (Amersfoort et al., 2022; Galea, 2021; Marchetti and Engelhardt, 2020). Thus, endothelial IL-1R1 is perfectly situated to regulate leukocyte recruitment to the brain.

For immune cells to traffic into an inflamed region, slowing and- rolling needs to occur, and these features of immune cell homing require adhesion molecules. In response to central IL-1 stimulation, endothelial IL-1R1 is known to directly regulate vasculature adhesion molecule expression. IL-1 is a potent inducer of adhesion molecules such as PECAM, VCAM, and ICAM, all of which allow for the slowing and rolling of leukocytes to sense the site of inflammation (Liu et al., 2019; Pyrillou et al., 2020). Next, endothelial IL-1R1 signaling is known to induce strong microglial inflammatory responses. Central IL-1 stimulation leads to the production of cytokines (IL-1β, TNFα, IL-6) and chemokines (CXCL2) by microglia by way of endothelial IL-1R1 through unknown mediators (Gabriel Knoll et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019; Proescholdt et al., 2002). One potential inflammatory mediator that we have identified is hyaluronic acid (HA). Low molecular weight chains of HA are known stimulators of macrophage and microglia and bulk RNAseq of IL-1-stimulated endothelial cell cultures reveal hyaluronidase, an enzyme known to cleave HA into fragments, is upregulated (Zhu et al., 2019). Further, both microglia activation and hyaluronidase mRNA upregulation are both NFkB-dependent, giving evidence to HA as the primary suspect for IL-1 induced neuroinflammation (Zhu et al., 2019). Interestingly, brain endothelial IL-1R1 acts as a conduit for some but not all immune signals originating from the periphery. In peripheral LPS-indued neuroinflammation, endothelial cells upregulate IKK and IL-1β mRNA in the brain vasculature which then propagates the expression of IL-1 further into the brain (Quan et al., 1998). However, peripheral injections of IL-1β, even at large doses (1 ug) stimulates endothelial IKK activity but cannot stimulate microglia immune activity (Proescholdt et al., 2002) suggesting endothelial IL-1R1 may restrict some peripheral immune signals to the luminal side of the vasculature, effectively preventing the brain from sensing this immune signal.

Endothelial IL-1R1 is a strong inducer of leukocyte extravasation across the BBB. Indeed, central, but not peripheral, IL-1 stimulation elicited strong immune cell recruitment into the brain parenchyma (Ching et al., 2005; Liu et al., 2019; Proescholdt et al., 2002).Interestingly, elimination of microglia did not prevent endothelial IL-1R1-dependent leukocyte recruitment suggesting that microglia-induced cytokine and chemokine expression is not a prerequisite for leukocyte recruitment and is a feature of endothelial IL-1R1 signaling. In addition, conditional restoration and deletion studies show that endothelial-specific IL-1R1 is necessary and sufficient for leukocyte infiltration while microglia- or myeloid-restricted IL-1R1 restoration failed to rescue these responses following inflammatory challenges thereby pinpointing endothelial IL-1R1 is the major mediator for leukocyte recruitment to the brain (Liu et al., 2019; X. Liu et al., 2015).

Endothelial IL-1R1 also acts as a conduit transmitting inflammatory signals to neural signals. Early studies showed that central IL-1 is a potent inducer of fever and related sickness behaviors (i.e. decreased locomotor activity, anhedonia, and anorexia); (Dantzer, 2001; Quan, 2008). More recent studies using conditional deletion and restoration models of IL-1R1 confirmed IL-1 enhances fever and promotes sickness behavior exclusively thorough endothelial IL-1R1 signaling in the brain (Blomqvist and Engblom, 2018; Gabriel Knoll et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019). The fever response was dependent on endothelial COX-2 and subsequent prostaglandin synthesis which is known to alter medial preoptic neuronal activity leading to fever (Blomqvist and Engblom, 2018). In models of psychosocial stress, endothelial IL-1R1 also relays microglial inflammatory responses to promote and prolong anxiety-like behaviors (McKim et al., 2018; Wohleb et al., 2014). Altogether, endothelial IL-1R1 respondsto peripheral and central immune signals and can modify behavior via prostaglandin synthesis or recruitment of immune cells to the brain.

Astrocytic IL-1R1

Astrocytes are cells that are indispensable for the proper functioning of neurons, formation of the glial limitans of the BBB, and regulating the extracellular milieu throughout a range of conditions. Upon brain injury, astrocytes are important to form a glial scar. The literature as a whole firmly supports that astrocytes express full-length IL-1R1. This expression is normally low in vivo, as few astrocytes have appreciable IL- 1R1 mRNA or protein expression under homeostatic conditions within the mouse, rat, or human (Zhang et al., 2016); however, astrocyte IL-1R1 is known to be induced by IL-1β (Liu et al., 2019), TNFα (Friedman, 2001), and excitotoxic injury from kainic acid (Ravizza and Vezzani, 2006). Some studies suggest that astrocyte production of IL-1β provides cell-autonomous feedback for astrocyte IL-1R1 signaling (Iori et al., 2017; Ravizza and Vezzani, 2006). Evidence gathered by both Liu et. al. and Liddelow et. al, evinces that astrocyte IL-1/IL-1R1 signaling is not typically cell-autonomous. Rather, stimulation of astrocyte IL-1R1 largely depends on microglial or leukocytic IL-1 release (Liddelow et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019).

Astrocytes IL-1R1 signaling can be neurotoxic. One of the main responses of IL-1 stimulated astrocytes is production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Long term activation of astrocytes in vitro can lead to neurotoxic effects through ROS (Thornton et al., 2006). In a model of spinal cord damage, IL-1 is shown to stimulate astrocyte IL-1R1 to induce the expression of ROS to cause oligodendrocyte death (Bastien et al., 2015; Bretheau et al., 2022). However, central administration of IL-1 alone is not sufficient for apparent cell death (Liu et al., 2019), yet IL-1 enhances excitotoxic injury induced cell death (Lawrence et al., 1998) suggesting other injurious factors may be necessary for apoptosis.

The harmful impact of ROS production by IL-1-stimulated astrocytes is partially counterbalanced by the concurrent antioxidant production and anti-inflammatory cytokine production. Glutathione is the primary thiol antioxidant in cells (millimolar concentrations) and supports the activity of cytoprotective peroxidases, among other roles (Ulrich and Jakob, 2019). IL-1β upregulates the mRNA and protein expression of the glutamate/cystine antiporter, xCT, in primary astrocyte cultures, in an NF-κB- and partial p-38MAPK-dependent manner. IL-1β also increases the half-life of xCT mRNA by increasing its interaction with an RNA-binding protein (Shi et al., 2016). IL-1R1-dependent xCT upregulation in astrocytes directly increases extracellular glutamate, intracellular cystine, and glutathione synthesis, which protects neurons from oxidative insults (Chowdhury et al., 2018; He et al., 2015). On the contrary, in a NFκB reporter mouse, central injection of IL-1 failed to elicit NFκB responses in astrocytes (Nemeth et al., 2024); suggesting other pathways, like p-38MAPK, are more likely to transmit astrocyte IL-1R1 signaling in vivo. Notably, astrocyte IL-1R1 activation also upregulates known anti-inflammatory factors such as nerve growth factor (NGF) (Juric and Carman-Krzan, 2001; Spranger et al., 1990) or transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ) (Barnum and Jones, 1994; Benveniste et al., 1994; da Cunha et al., 1993). In accordance, central administration of IL-1 increases cFos mRNA expression without astrogliosis (Proescholdt et al., 2002) and selective astrocyte IL-1R1 deletion led to an increase of IL-6 mRNA expression after central inflammation suggesting astrocytes can inhibit inflammatory cytokine expression (Liu et al., 2019). Further, in a model of NMDA-induced retinal neural damage, astrocyte IL-1R1 is sufficient for prevention of neuronal death (Todd et al., 2019). Taken together, astrocyte IL-1R1 signaling can exert powerful neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory responses.

Interestingly, astrocytes have been reported to respond differently to IL-1β compared to IL-1α, despite binding to the same receptor, IL-1R1 (Liddelow et al., 2017). A recent study of immunopanned mouse astrocytes stimulated with IL-1β or IL-1α shows distinct gene expression patterns between the treatment groups. IL-1β stimulated astrocytes expressed pan-reactive genes which were categorized as A1, and IL-1α-stimulated astrocytes were categorized as A2 which were shown to be neurotoxic (Liddelow et al., 2017). It is unclear how IL-1α and IL-1β impart different transcriptional patterns in astrocytes. Due to the relative lack of sequence homology between IL-1α and IL-1β (March et al., 1985), it is possible the selective transcriptional effects of both cytokines may stem from differential engagement with IL-1R1. Therefore, astrocytic IL-1R1 can mediate diverse functions under both physiological and pathological conditions.

Microglial IL-1R1

Microglia are an important ontologically distinct population of CNS- resident macrophages, arising from yolk-sac progenitors in embryonic stages of development, that are implicated in the homeostatic regulation of normal brain function, detection/clearance of cellular waste, debris, and foreign pathogens and mounting innate immune responses within the CNS (Paolicelli et al., 2022). Whether IL-1 can directly signal on microglia has been shown to be dynamically regulated. Under normal conditions, in contrast to bone marrow-derived peripheral macrophages that express ample IL-1R1, microglia do not express IL-1R1 and do not respond to direct IL-1 challenge in vitro and in vivo (Liu et al., 2019; Nemeth et al., 2022; Pinteaux et al., 2002; Zhu et al., 2019). Alternatively, microglia cytokine production following IL-1 typically uses bi-directional signaling between the endothelial IL-1R1 signaling-derived products and microglia (See Section 4). However, microglial IL-1R1 expression can be induced in various contexts. Early in vitro studies show that direct LPS stimulation of microglia induced IL-1R1 mRNA (Pinteaux et al., 2002) and recent in vivo studies show repeated peripheral LPS stimulation, as a model of LPS preconditioning/innate immune training, induces IL-1R1 in brain microglia. These microglia exhibit a newfound responsiveness to exogenous IL-1β by producing anti-inflammatory cytokine, IL-1Ra (Nemeth et al., 2022), and elicit a neuroprotective phenotype in the spinal cord following aortic ischemia induced paralysis (Freria et al., 2020). During microglia repopulation, following diphtheria toxin-induced microglial depletion, IL-1R1 was found on repopulating microglia and blockade of IL-1R1 signaling dampened the repopulation rate suggesting that the induced-microglial IL-1R1 is important for the proliferation of new microglia (Bruttger et al., 2015). One major caveat to these studies is whether microglia can truly be distinguished from infiltrating monocytes. Peripheral monocytes are known to infiltrate the brain during inflammatory contexts and exhibit similar characteristics to microglia such as ramified morphology and increase in microglial markers Iba-1/P2YR12 and decrease in leukocyte antigen, CD45.

Although it is well known that microglia can be stimulated through TLR4 ligands to induce NFκB, Nemeth et. al. did not identify NFκB activity in microglia following central stimulation of IL-1 (Nemeth et al., 2024). Recently microglial IL-1R1 has been found to signal through mTOR pathways. Borreca et. al. showed microglia from IL-1R1KO mice or WT microglia treated with IL-1Ra increased mTOR in microglia primary cultures (Borreca et al., 2024), suggesting IL-1R1 signaling in microglia leads to the inhibition of mTOR.

Neuronal IL-1R1

Direct cytokine-to-neuron signaling has been implicated in inflammation-induced behavior changes. However, there has been considerable controversy of the distribution pattern of neuronal IL-1R1. As briefly described, most prior studies used radiolabeled ligand-binding assays, immunohistochemistry, or in situ hybridization to detail the distribution of IL-1R1 in the brain which led to conflicting results. Recently, we used genetic methods to track IL-1R1 mRNA and protein expression which were restricted to various neurotransmitter systems. The majority of neuronal IL-1R1 is expressed in glutamatergic neurons of the dentate gyrus, somatosensory cortex, and serotonergic neurons of the dorsal raphe nucleus. Interestingly, most of the IL-1R1 expression was within sensory processing and relay nuclei of the brain with emphasis on the olfactory nuclei. It has yet to be studied whether IL-1/ IL-1R1 signaling in these olfactory regions is important for the detection and discrimination of olfactory cues highlighting a gap in our understanding of neuronal IL-1R1 systems (Nemeth et al., 2024).

Impacts of IL-1R1 signaling in neurons is a topic that is fraught with conflicting data previously due to the various methodologies used and diverse cell types tested (Nemeth and Quan, 2021). Recent studies on chronic stress induced anxiety behavior using the cell-type specific restoration and deletion models, showed dentate neuronal IL-1R1 as a necessary pathway to elicit anxiety-like behaviors (DiSabato et al., 2020), stress-sensitization (DiSabato et al., 2022; Goodman et al., 2024), synapse related gene expression, and alterations of spontaneous inhibitory and excitatory currents (Goodman et al., 2025). Further, chronic overexpression of IL-1Ra or viral-mediated deletion of IL-1R1 in hippocampal neurons blocked social stress induced cellular and behavioral changes (DiSabato et al., 2020, 2022) suggesting IL-1 has a direct action on dentate neurons. However, the exact pathways mediating neuronal IL-1R1 function remains to be elucidated.

An important insight regarding neuronal IL-1R1 function is that, different from non-neuronal cells, neurons express an alternatively spliced isoform of the IL-1RAcP, named IL-1RAcPb. Following comparative studies, non-neuronal cells were found to express canonical IL-1RAcP. As previously mentioned, IL-1RAcP enables high-affinity complex formation and scaffolding for intracellular signaling; however, IL-1RAcPb rewires the same recognition event toward neuron-specific outputs critical for brain function (Cullinan et al., 1998; Huang et al., 2011; Smith et al., 2009; Thomas et al., 2012; Wesche, Korherr, et al., 1997). IL-1RAcPb has been detected in neurons following tissue-specific expression analysis in brain regions, such as hippocampus, cortex, and cerebellum, as a neuron-restricted splice variant (Smith et al., 2009). At functional levels, divergent signaling through its altered C-terminal intracellular region is found to support rapid kinase activation rather than transcriptional programs in neurons (Huang et al., 2011). Indeed, stimulation of hippocampal neurons with IL-1β demonstrates IL-1RAcPb mediates Src phosphorylation and enhances NMDA-receptor calcium influx while showing minimal NF-κB activation or transcriptional activity following cytokine exposure suggesting this co-receptor channels neuronal IL-1 signals toward synaptic modulation rather than inflammatory gene expression (Huang et al., 2011; Nemeth et al., 2024; Viviani et al., 2003). Recent analysis of dentate gyrus neurons of the hippocampus corroborates previous findings that dentate neurons express the IL-1RAcPb variant and stimulation of only dentate neuronal IL-1R1 did not lead to short term transcriptional alterations in dentate granule cells; however, long term restoration of IL-1R1 in dentate gyrus neurons led to the suppression of synapse-related gene pathways compared to IL-1R1-null neurons (Nemeth et al., 2024). This suggests that chronic basal stimulation of neuronal IL-1R1 may act to suppress synapse-related genes and could affect synapse structure.

Indeed, the IL-1/IL-1R1/IL-1RAcPb complex may have physical actions at the synapse. Alone, IL-1RAcPb has been shown, in vitro, to be an adhesion molecule that is important for synaptogenesis via trans-synaptic interactions with protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor delta (PTPRD) (Yoshida et al., 2011, Yoshida et. al. 2012). Application of IL-1β to hippocampal neuron cultures leads to increase binding of IL-1R1 to GLUR2, a NMDAR subunit, possibly through the unique intracellular tail of IL-1RAcPb (Gardoni et al., 2011). Other accessory proteins, such as IL-1RAPL1, may also interact with the IL-1R1/IL-1/IL-1RAcPb complex to alter neuron morphology (Montani et al., 2017); however, how IL-1β or the IL-1R1 complex may interact with the IL-1RAPL1 protein in synaptic formation has yet to be understood. Together, the mechanism for IL-1 signaling to non-transcriptionally modify neural circuits through alterations of synaptic transmission and structure warrants further study.

Ependymal, tanycyte, and choroid plexus IL-1R1

Ventricular associated IL-1R1 signaling is relatively understudied. There are multiple cell types within the ventricles that express IL-1R1, such as the ependymal cell lining, choroid plexus epithelial cells, mesenchymal cells (fibroblasts, pericytes, and smooth muscle cells), endothelial cells, and third ventricle specialized ependymal cells called tanycytes (Böttcher et al., 2020; Dani et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2019).

During central inflammatory challenge with IL-1β, reporter of NFκB quickly activates in the ependymal cells and choroid plexus epithelial cells suggesting rapid signal transduction and usage of NFκB (Knoll et al., 2017; Nemeth et al., 2024). Further, IL-1 challenge in mice that have conditional deletion of IL-1R1 from only endothelia resulted in ventricular specific responses such as increased adhesion molecules (ICAM, VCAM) of ependymal and choroid plexus cells, activation of microglial morphological changes in the absence of prototypical proinflammatory cytokine gene expression (IL1β, TNFα, NOS2), and recruitment of monocytes to the brain through the ventricles (Liu et al., 2019). Regarding the propagation of neuroinflammation to the brain, the consequences of ventricular IL-1R1 stimulation is poorly understood, however, due to the apparent different response patterns compared to endothelial IL-1R1 signaling, it is likely that this ventricular IL-1R1 signaling system produces its own unique functions.

Tanycytes are specialized ependymal cells that are located in the third ventricle that send their end feet from the ventricular lining into the mediobasal hypothalamus, a brain region responsible for controlling food intake. Tanycytes highly express IL-1R1 which suggests that the brain-to-ventricle and ventricular-to-brain IL-1 signaling may occur. Indeed, systemic injections of IL-1 increases NFκB activity in tanycytes, similar to other ependymal cells, that leads to a NEMO-dependent prostaglandin synthesis, and, interestingly, mediates IL-1-induced anorexia (Böttcher et al., 2020). This study suggests that IL-1R1 signaling on the uniquely positioned tanycytes can alter regional specific brain function through detection of CSF IL-1.

Recent studies have revealed that, under homeostasis, the only detectable brain IL-1/IL-1R1 interaction is in the choroid plexus, not in the brain parenchyma, suggesting that modulation of normal brain function by IL-1 may originate from the choroid plexus. Indeed, RNAseq analysis of choroid plexus cells shows basal IL-1β expression is in immune cells and IL-1R1 expression is in epithelial cells and mesenchymal cells (Dani et al., 2021). A new transgenic mouse model that can temporally visualize all IL-1β producing cells, called IL-1β-TRAP, also corroborates these data (Nemeth et al., 2026). Under basal conditions, IL-1β expression was found to be restricted to MHCII+ , stromal macrophages of the choroid plexus with no detection of IL-1β expression in the brain parenchyma. How choroid epithelial IL-1R1 and basal choroid macrophage IL-1β production modifies brain functions has yet to be identified.

Concluding remarks and future directions

In a symphony, the music is produced by a combination of all the individual instruments in the orchestra. Similarly, IL-1-mediated neuroinflammation is the result of contributions from multiple individual cell-type specific IL-1 signaling that harmonize to ensure detection and clearance of foreign and endogenous pathogens and ultimately promote the resolution of inflammatory processes to prevent pathogenesis. In this mini-review, we have detailed the multiple, recently identified, IL-1R1 expressing cell types and their contributions to neuroinflammation (Illustrated in Fig. 1). Future studies are needed to clarify how cell-type specific IL-1/IL-1R1 signaling is involved in specific neurological disorders and diseases.
