Differential diagnoses and therapeutic features of giant cell tumor of the bone in an elderly patient: A case report
Authors: Makram Zrig, Youssef Othman, Firas Chaouech, Mezri Maatouk, Ahmed Zrig, Abderrazek Abid
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGiant cells tumor (GCT) of the bone is usually seen in young adults between the ages of 20 and 40. When occurring in older patients, diagnosis and therapeutic specific feature are to be considered. We underline those aspects through this case report.Case presentationWe report the case of a 73-year-old man presenting with a painful swelling knee. The diagnosis of GCT of proximal tibia was retained and the patient underwent extensive curettage, subchondral bone grafting and cementation of the residual cavity. The long-term results show a good functional outcome and no recurrence.DiscussionIn this age group, other lesions have similar clinical and radiological presentations as GCT of bone. The diagnosis of a metastatic carcinoma is to be carefully ruled-out in this situation. While the option of large resection with joint arthroplasty is better accepted in ageing patients as a treatment of GCT of bone, this case suggests that jointpreserving methods should also be considered especially when there are no signs of osteoarthritis. While polymethylmethacrylate cementation is an efficient adjuvant in order to reduce recurrences, it may cause secondary osteoarthritis. Subchondral grafting seems to be a good alternative to prevent this complication.ConclusionMalignancies should be ruled-out before retaining the diagnosis of GCT of the bone after the age of 50. Conservative surgical treatment is always an option in this age group.Keywords: Giant cell tumor, Elderly patient, Bone cement, Bone grafting, Case report

Introduction

Giant cells tumor (GCT) of the bone is a locally aggressive benign tumor that is usually seen in the knee. It usually occurs in young adults between the ages of 20 and 40 [1]. It is exceptional to see this lesion after the age of 55 [2]. We report the case of a 73-year-old man treated for a GCT of the proximal tibia in our academic health center. Through this report, we put the accent on the differential diagnoses that should be ruled-out in this age group. Then we discuss the evolutive particularities of GCT in elderly patients and we review therapeutic features.

Methods

This report was written in line with the SCARE guidelines [3].

Case presentation

A 73-year-old man with history of diabetes and atrial fibrillation treated with acenocoumarol, presented with a mild right knee inflammatory pain evolving for 18 months. There is neither trauma, recent loss of weight, nor fever. Physical examination found a good general condition and a mild pain with fingertip palpation all around the proximal tibia. Range of motion of the right knee was normal. There was neither local swelling nor inflammatory signs. Plain radiographs showed a round-shaped osteolytic image interesting the epiphysis and the metaphysic of the proximal tibia. The lesion had well-defined borders with a surrounding osteo-condensation. The image was assessed as a Lodwick type IA lesion (Fig. 1). At this stage, GCT of proximal tibia was called to mind mainly for the metaphyso-epiphyseal localization, but further explorations were necessary because of the age of the patient. Scanner exploration showed that the lesion had a tissue content and had no extension to the soft tissue. It measured 50 mm of height and 30 mm of width (Fig. 2). In a first time, a surgical biopsy was performed. Histopathologic examination confirmed the diagnosis of GCT. The tumor was a Campanacci grade 1 lesion. The patient was then operated. Through an anteromedial approach of the proximal tibia, the tumor was located and was accessed through a cortical window on its medial wall. An extensive curettage of the tumor was then made. A cancellous bone autograft was then used to fill the epiphyseal subchondral side of the bone defect. The remaining cavity was filled using polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) cementation. Histopathological examination of the curettage product further confirmed the diagnosis. Two months later, the graft was incorporated, and pain was completely relieved (Fig. 3). After five years of follow-up, no recurrence was found.

Discussion

There are only few reports of GCT in elderly patients that mainly stressed on the cytogenetic and therapeutic features of the tumor [4]. Large series of GCT of the bone included very few patients of this age group without focusing on their particularities. The largest series that reported 10 case of GCT of the bone in elderly patients found no differences in the behavior of this tumor compared to GCT happening in younger patients [2]. However, the author suggested that this lesion might be less aggressive because he found no recurrence after a follow-up of two years at least [2]. We think that the small number of patients in this series makes such conclusion weak. In the other hand, even if it is commonly accepted that most recurrences happen in the first two years after primary treatment, it can be delayed as long as fourteen years [5]. In our case, the tumor presented typical epiphyso-metaphyseal localization. In fact, GCT of the bone is usually a well-defined osteolytic lesion that evolves in the extremities of long bones. Together with proximal tibia, distal femur and distal radius are the most affected locations. The same distribution was found in elderly patient [2].

While the diagnosis of GCT of the bone is often easily made in young patients, it causes some difficulties in elderly patients. It is crucial to rule-out the possibility of a metastatic neoplasm, mainly carcinoma [2]. Although the preferred location of these metastatic tumors is the spine and the pelvis, it is not exceptional to see such lesions in long bones [6]. Investigation should inquire about recent loss of weight, extra-osseous symptoms and an unexplained fever. Physical examination should be exhaustive in order to find a primitive neoplasm. Eventually, the diagnosis of metastatic carcinoma should be definitively ruled-out by the histopathologic examination using cytokeratin staining [2].

GCT of the bone in elderly could also be confounding with osteoarthritic cysts [7]. When plain radiographs shown signs of osteoarthritis, it is reasonable to seek the help of MRI findings that would show a T2 hypersignal in case of osteoarthritic cyst.

Infectious etiology is always considered in all age groups in front of bone destructing lesions. In this chronic context, non-specific infections such as tuberculosis are more likely to occur. In both tuberculosis and GCT, pulmonary lesions could be seen. At histopathologic examination, giant cells are also seen in tuberculosis granuloma. The presence of central caseum leads to the diagnosis of tuberculosis rather than GCT.

Finally, once the diagnosis of GCT of bone is retained in an elderly patient, the fact that this tumor could be a complication of Paget's disease should be kept in mind [8].

One may say that it is rational to opt for resection and arthroplasty as a primary treatment for GCT of the knee in elderly patients since this method ensures the lowest rate of recurrence [6]. While this is a heavy procedure that could be disabling for a young active patient, this potential disability would be better accepted for older patients. However, to treat our patient, we opted for a joint-preserving method for two reasons: first, there were no signs of osteoarthritis and second, arthroplasty is a major procedure that requires large resection and would cause an important bleeding for our old patient treated with acenocoumarol.

We used the technique of curettage associated with PMMA cementation and subchondral bone grafting. We used PMMA in order to obtain immediate full and solid filling of the residual cavity without the need of complementary hardware fixation. Other methods such as full bone grafting would significantly delay weight-bearing which is to consider seriously in our elderly patient with heavy medical history [9]. In the other hand, since there were no radiological signs of osteoarthritis, we interposed a bone graft between PMMA and articular cartilage to protect the cartilage from thermal damage caused by cementation [10].

A large number of surgical procedures were described and studied in GCT of bone starting from intra-tumoral curettage only and ending to large resection [9]. It is widely admitted that performing only intra-tumoral curettage is associated to a high risk of recurrence and that the adjunction of adjuvant procedures based on physical and/or chemical methods is imperative in order to eradicate residual tumoral cells [11]. Among these methods, the use of PMMA has the benefit of acting in both ways: physical by thermal necrosis of tumoral cells and chemical by free radicals cytotoxicity [12,13]. However, proper efficacy of PMMA cementation has not been well studied since most papers reported series where this method is used together with other procedures such as hydrogen peroxide and phenol. Recently, few studies including patients treated by cementation without any other adjuvant showed that the use of PMMA reduces the likelihood of recurrence compared to intratumoral curettage by factor of 2 [14] to 8 [9]. Using bone grafting has inferior efficacy in terms of recurrence since it reduces this risk only to the half [15]. This could be explained by the fact that PMMA cementation provides immediate stability which permits the surgeon to perform more aggressive curettage without compromising toughness. Furthermore, this method allows easier identification of local recurrences due to distinctive radiological features [16]. To enhance efficacy, Balke and al. recommend additional high-speed burring [9].

Nevertheless, the technique of PMMA cementation has been often criticized for causing degenerative changes in knee joint [13,14,17,18]. Less than 10 mm of residual thickness of the remaining subchondral bone after curettage is correlated to a higher risk of this complication [10]. Although this has been radiologically demonstrated, its functional impact seems to be modest [19]. The adjunction of subchondral bone grafting reduces the risk of secondary osteoarthritis by factor of 1.5 [10]. A cadaveric study of Radev and al. revealed that rather than causing direct damage to the articular cartilage, hyper-thermic reaction of PMMA is harmful to the subchondral bone resulting in necrosis and subsequent cartilage collapsing [20]. Subchondral bone grafting would then prevent this cartilage collapsing by thickening the underlying supporting bone. An other option that could be suitable for elderly patients is the use of calcium phostphate cement. This material offers a biological interface and a good stability with no need for internal fixation [21].

Conclusion

In summary, it is imperative to carefully rule-out a certain number of differential diagnoses before retaining the diagnosis of GCT in elderly patients. The possibility of a metastatic carcinoma must be always kept in mind. In this age group, it is easier to opt for resectionarthroplasty as a primary treatment of GCT of the knee, but this method should not be systematically used and joint-preserving procedures have their place, especially when there are no signs of osteoarthritis initially.
