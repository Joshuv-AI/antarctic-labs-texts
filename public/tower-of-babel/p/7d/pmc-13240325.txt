APOE ε4, modifiable risk factors, and dementia in community‐based older Japanese adults
Authors: Masaya Kumamoto, Toshiharu Ninomiya, Yoshihiko Furuta, Mao Shibata, Tomoyuki Ohara, Jun Hata, Tetsuro Ago, Yasuyuki Taki, Tatsuya Mikami, Tetsuya Maeda, Kenjiro Ono, Masaru Mimura, Ritsuko Hanajima, Jun‐ichi Iga, Minoru Takebayashi, Yukihide Momozawa, the Japan Prospective Studies for Aging and Dementia (JPSC‐AD) Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONApolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 is a major genetic risk factor for dementia. We examined whether APOE ε4 genotype modified the associations between a modifiable risk factor (mRF) score and dementia in older Japanese adults.METHODSThis cross‐sectional study included 9605 community‐dwelling Japanese individuals aged ≥65 years. APOE ε4 genotype and mRF scores were assessed. Dementia was clinically diagnosed. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measures were available in a subset.RESULTSThe odds ratio (OR) for dementia increased with the number of APOE ε4 alleles. A low mRF score was associated with lower ORs for prevalent dementia in non‐carriers and heterozygotes but not in homozygotes. On MRI, low mRF scores were associated with preserved brain structure in non‐carriers and heterozygotes, but not in homozygotes.DISCUSSIONFavorable risk factor profiles were associated with lower dementia risk and preserved brain structure in APOE ε4 non‐carriers and heterozygotes, but not in homozygotes, supporting genotype‐tailored prevention strategies.Keywords: amyloid beta, Aβ, aging population, APOE ε4, biomarkers, dementia risk, epidemiology, modifiable risk factors, magnetic resonance imaging, MRI, neurofilament

BACKGROUND

Dementia affects a growing number of people worldwide, despite declines in age‐standardized incidence reported in several high‐income countries. 1 , 2 The global prevalence is projected to nearly triple by 2050, underscoring rising public‐health and socioeconomic burdens. 3 Therefore, prevention efforts increasingly focus on modifiable risk factors (mRFs) such as education, hypertension, and physical activity, supported by epidemiological evidence that a meaningful fraction of dementia cases could be prevented or delayed through modification of these factors. 4 , 5

Genetic factors also play a role in dementia onset. Among them, the apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene is the strongest and most consistently replicated common risk factor for late‐onset Alzheimer's disease (AD). In particular, the ε4 allele shows a dose‐dependent association with increased risk and earlier symptom onset. 6 , 7 Moreover, a recent large‐scale study demonstrated that APOE ε4 homozygotes exhibit earlier emergence of AD‐related biomarker abnormalities and earlier clinical onset than other genotypes, highlighting a biologically distinctive high‐risk subgroup. 8 Therefore, clarifying how mRFs may influence dementia risk associated with the APOE ε4 genotype is of critical importance for dementia prevention.

Nonetheless, the interaction between mRFs and the APOE ε4 genotype remains uncertain. Some population‐based studies using composite mRF profiles reported limited benefit among those at high genetic risk, 9 whereas large‐scale cohort studies and multidomain intervention trials have found no significant interaction. 10 , 11 To address this discrepancy, we examined the combined influence of ε4 genotype and mRF score groups (low [favorable] vs high [unfavorable]) on dementia risk in a large‐scale population‐based cohort of community‐dwelling older Japanese adults, incorporating analyses of brain structure and plasma biomarkers to provide biological context.

METHODS

Study participants

The Japan Prospective Studies Collaboration for Aging and Dementia (JPSC‐AD) is a multisite, ongoing community‐based cohort across eight sites in Japan. The design of the JPSC‐AD Study has been described elsewhere. 12 Study examinations were conducted from 2016 to 2018. Among 11,408 participants aged 65 years or older, 9605 were included in the dementia analyses and 8793 in the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) analyses after applying exclusion criteria (Figure S1). The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Kyushu University Institutional Review Board (approval no. 24116) and the ethics committees of all participating research sites. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

APOE haplotyping

The APOE haplotype consisting of rs429358 and rs7412 variants was directly determined using a multiplex PCR‐based targeted sequencing method. 13 Quality control of the sequencing data was implemented based on sequencing read depth, call rates, and deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.

mRF score

The mRF score was calculated using a previously developed dementia risk assessment tool for the Japanese population, 14 which demonstrated fair discrimination for 10‐year incident dementia (Harrell's C = 0.718), indicating that lower composite scores corresponded to lower predicted longitudinal incidence risk. The score comprises seven factors related to lifestyle and medical history (other than age and sex), weighted by assigned points: ≤9 years of education (1), hypertension (1), diabetes (2), body mass index (BMI) < 18.5 kg/m2 (1), history of stroke (2), current smoking (1), and sedentariness (2). The primary categorization dichotomized the mRF score as high (≥3 points) versus low (0 to 2 points). Details are described in the Supporting Information Methods.

Ascertainment of dementia

Dementia and AD were diagnosed according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Third Edition, Revised 15 and National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association criteria, 16 with a standardized two‐stage procedure across sites. In the first stage, trained staff conducted cognitive screening with the Mini‐Mental State Examination 17 , 18 supplemented by brief ancillary tests 19 ; those with suspected dementia proceeded to second‐stage evaluation. In the second stage, dementia was diagnosed by expert psychiatrists or neurologists through physical and neurological examinations, including the logical memory IIA subscale of the Wechsler Memory Scale–Revised 20 , 21 and the Pareidolia test. 22 All dementia cases were independently adjudicated by expert psychiatrists or neurologists from participating institutions that were not those where the initial diagnoses were made. Operational thresholds and instruments are detailed in the Supporting Information Methods.

For baseline dementia cases (prevalent at baseline), age at dementia onset was obtained from participants and/or informants and, when available, corroborated using information from primary physicians and medical records (including neuroimaging) per protocol (Supporting Information Methods). For descriptive purposes, onset age was summarized as the median (interquartile range [IQR]) within each APOE ε4 genotype group.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: The authors reviewed the literature (PubMed, Google Scholar) on APOE ε4, mRFs, and dementia. Evidence for interaction between APOE and mRFs is inconsistent, with some cohort studies suggesting attenuated benefits of favorable risk profiles among ε4 carriers and others showing no significant interaction.

Interpretation: In this community‐based cross‐sectional study of older Japanese adults, a favorable mRF profile was associated with lower dementia prevalence and greater brain volumes among APOE ε4 non‐carriers and heterozygotes. In contrast, ε4 homozygotes showed markedly higher dementia prevalence and lower brain volumes regardless of mRF profile. These findings support the presence of distinct pathophysiological processes in ε4 homozygotes and suggest limited modifiability of their risk by conventional clinical factors.

Future directions: Longitudinal studies and genotype‐stratified prevention trials should evaluate intervention timing in ε4 homozygotes and incorporate imaging and plasma biomarkers to elucidate mechanisms underlying their limited responsiveness to mRF modification.

Brain MRI

Brain MRI was performed at the baseline examination during 2016 to 2018. Three‐dimensional T1‐weighted images were acquired on 1.5T or 3T scanners across sites and processed with FreeSurfer (version 5.3). 23 Cortical regions were parcellated according to the Desikan–Killiany atlas, 24 and subcortical segmentations followed the standard aseg outputs. Volumes for hippocampus, amygdala, gray matter, and white matter lesions (WMLs) were extracted and standardized by estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV). WML values were log‐transformed owing to right skew.

Measurement of plasma biomarkers for dementia

Plasma biomarkers were measured in a subset of 1502 participants at the Hisayama site (Fukuoka, Japan), one of the eight participating research sites. Blood samples were collected at the baseline examination from 2017 to 2018. Plasma was aliquoted and frozen at −80°C within 3.5 to 6.0 h. In 2023, frozen aliquots were thawed once and assayed on a Simoa HD‐X analyzer (Quanterix). Amyloid beta (Aβ) 42 (Aβ42), Aβ40, and neurofilament light chain (NfL) were quantified using the Neurology 4‐Plex E kit, and plasma phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 (p‐tau181) was quantified using the Simoa pTau‐181 V2 Advantage kit. The Aβ42/40 ratio was calculated as the concentration of Aβ42 divided by that of Aβ40. All assays were run in duplicate with a single reagent lot.

Statistical analysis

Characteristics of the study participants were summarized as mean (standard deviation [SD]) for continuous variables, median (IQR) for skewed variables, and number (percentage) for categorical variables. Differences in clinical characteristics across ε4 genotype groups (non‐carriers [−/−], heterozygotes [±], and homozygotes [+/+]) were assessed using linear regression models for continuous variables and logistic regression models for categorical variables, with the number of ε4 alleles (0 for non‐carriers, 1 for heterozygotes, 2 for homozygotes) entered as an ordinal variable to test for trend. First, we estimated the association of APOE ε4 genotypes with the presence of all‐cause dementia and AD using a logistic regression, from which odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. In these analyses, the multivariable‐adjusted models included age, sex, research sites, lower education (≤9 years), hypertension, diabetes, low BMI, history of stroke, current smoking, and sedentariness. In addition, we estimated the ORs of the presence of dementia comparing low‐mRF‐score groups with high‐mRF‐score groups in each ε4 genotype, as well as the ORs for dementia across combined categories of APOE ε4 genotypes and mRF score groups, using a logistic regression model after adjustment for age, sex, and research site. The interaction between mRF‐score group and the dementia across APOE ε4 genotypes was tested by adding a multiplicative interaction term: mRF‐score group × APOE ε4 genotype coded as the ordinal number of ε4 alleles (0, 1, 2) to the relevant logistic model. Associations between age and brain volumes (hippocampus, amygdala, gray matter, and log‐transformed WML, all z‐standardized) were modeled using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) including age, sex, research site, APOE ε4 genotype, and an age × genotype interaction term. Associations were visualized by genotype using locally weighted regression (LOESS). 25 , 26 Within ε4 genotype strata, differences by mRF‐score group were evaluated for standardized MRI volumes (ANCOVA adjusted for age, sex, and research site) and for Aβ42/40, p‐tau181, and NfL levels (ANCOVA adjusted for age and sex). All statistical analyses were performed with R software version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two‐sided p values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Association between APOE ε4 genotype and dementia risk

Among participants with prevalent dementia at baseline (dementia cases only; non‐carriers: n = 337; heterozygotes: n = 107; homozygotes: n = 15), the median (IQR) age at dementia onset decreased with an increasing number of ε4 alleles: 82.0 (77.0 to 86.3), 79.7 (75.0 to 84.2), and 71.1 (65.6 to 80.4) years, respectively (p for trend < 0.001; Figure 1). In multivariable models, ORs for dementia were 2.30 (95% CI: 1.76 to 2.97) for heterozygotes and 11.04 (95% CI: 5.33 to 21.68) for homozygotes compared with non‐carriers (Table S1). Similar findings were observed for the association between ε4 genotype and AD.

The age‐related decline in hippocampal, amygdala, and gray matter volumes, as well as the increase in WML volume, was greater in homozygotes compared with heterozygotes and non‐carriers. Significant interactions between age and ε4 genotype were observed for age‐related changes in hippocampal volume (p = 0.01), amygdala volume (p = 0 .02), and gray matter volume (p = 0 .007), but not for WML volume (p = 0.42).

Interaction between APOE ε4 genotype and mRF score group for dementia risk

Next, we investigated the interaction between ε4 genotype and mRF‐score group for dementia risk. In the combined analysis of ε4 genotype and mRF‐score group, the unadjusted prevalence of dementia increased with the number of ε4 alleles in both the low (17.4%) and high (16.7%) mRF‐score groups, whereas participants with low mRF scores had a lower crude prevalence of dementia than those with high mRF scores among non‐carriers (2.7% and 9.6%) and heterozygotes (5.1% and 12.3%) (Figure 3A). In multivariable‐adjusted analyses, the OR for dementia in heterozygotes with low mRF scores was comparable to that among non‐carriers with high mRF scores (Table S2). When we evaluated the OR for the presence of dementia comparing the low‐mRF‐score group with the high‐mRF‐score group within each ε4 genotype, the lower‐mRF‐score group was associated with lower ORs of dementia among non‐carriers (OR 0.35; 95% CI: 0.28 to 0.45) and heterozygotes (OR 0.60; 95% CI: 0.38 to 0.97), but no clear association was observed among homozygotes (OR 1.34; 95% CI: 0.30 to 7.69) (Figure 3B). A significant interaction was observed in the magnitude of the association between mRF‐score group and dementia risk across ε4 genotypes (ε4 coded 0, 1, 2) (p for interaction = 0.03).

Plasma biomarkers for dementia according to mRF‐score group and APOE ε4 genotype

The age‐ and sex‐adjusted geometric mean values of the Aβ42/40 ratio declined with an increasing number of ε4 alleles within both low‐ and high‐mRF‐score groups (both p for trend ≤ 0.01; Table S3), whereas there was no difference in the adjusted Aβ42/40 ratio between mRF‐score groups within any of the individual ε4 genotypes (all p ≥ 0.45). Plasma p‑tau181 levels also increased significantly with higher numbers of APOE ε4 alleles in both low‐ and high‐mRF‐score groups (both p for trend ≤ 0.002), whereas differences between low‐ and high‐mRF‐score groups within each genotype were not statistically significant (all p ≥ 0.07). On the other hand, the trend in the age‐ and sex‐adjusted geometric mean values of NfL across the number of ε4 alleles was statistically significant (p = 0.03) only in the high‐mRF‐score group. In addition, in the comparisons within individual genotypes, higher NfL levels were observed in the high‐ compared to the low‐mRF‐score group for both non‐carriers and heterozygotes (both p < 0.001), with no difference in NfL levels among homozygotes (p = 0.93).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that dementia risk increased progressively with the number of ε4 alleles and homozygotes had a markedly higher risk of dementia, in a large population‐based cohort of community‐dwelling older Japanese adults. Moreover, in the combined analysis of ε4 genotype and mRF status, non‐carriers and heterozygotes in the lower‐mRF‐score group had a lower risk of dementia compared with those in the higher‐mRF‐score group. In contrast, homozygotes showed a high risk of dementia regardless of their mRF status, indicating a significant interaction between ε4 genotype and clinical risk. Structural brain MRI patterns were consistent with these results. Among non‐carriers and heterozygotes, the lower‐mRF‐score group was associated with less brain atrophy and lower WML volume, whereas homozygotes exhibited greater atrophy and more extensive WML regardless of mRF status. These findings support the view that homozygotes represent a biologically distinct subgroup, underscoring the need for genotype‐specific prevention strategies that reconsider intervention targets, timing, and intensity.

Previous studies consistently showed that ε4 homozygotes had the greatest risk of developing dementia and earlier symptom onset than heterozygotes and non‐carriers, 6 , 27 , 28 with estimates exceeding 50% cumulative risk of AD by age 85 in homozygotes and a 7‐ to 10‐year shift in clinical and biomarker milestones. 8 , 28 In our cohort, the median age at dementia onset decreased with an increasing number of ε4 alleles. This descriptive pattern is consistent with previous studies reporting an association between APOE ε4 dosage and earlier symptom onset. 6 , 7 , 8 Given this markedly elevated and earlier risk, an important clinical question is whether dementia prevention through optimization of mRFs can meaningfully mitigate risk in genetically high‐risk individuals.

Numerous epidemiological and clinical studies have also suggested that optimizing mRFs such as hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and sedentary behavior can reduce the risk of cognitive impairment and dementia. 4 , 5 , 10 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 However, whether the effects of mRF extend equally to genetically high‐risk groups remains uncertain. Some cohorts have reported limited or no benefit among ε4 carriers, 9 whereas others have found no interaction, 10 , 11 and still others have suggested effect modification in the opposite direction for certain behaviors, 33 , 34 , 35 leading to inconsistent results across studies. In this study, non‐carriers and heterozygotes in the lower‐mRF‐score group had a lower risk of dementia compared with those in the higher‐mRF‐score group, whereas homozygotes showed no clear difference in dementia risk across mRF strata. These findings suggest that heterozygotes and homozygotes may differ in their responsiveness to modifiable risk profiles, newly informing the ongoing debate about the interaction between ε4 and mRFs.

Consistent with the clinical findings, this study found that the interaction between ε4 genotype and mRFs was directionally replicated on structural brain MRI. Homozygotes exhibited the steepest age‐related declines in hippocampal, amygdala, and total gray matter volumes, as well as a higher WML burden, with minimal separation between low‐ and high‐mRF groups. In contrast, non‐carriers and heterozygotes showed clear structural preservation in the low‐mRF‐score group. These findings are consistent with previous reports of brain atrophy and greater WML burden in ε4 carriers, especially in homozygotes. 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 Moreover, the age dependence of the effects of ε4 on hippocampal volume – becoming evident from the early 60s in homozygotes – supports our interpretation of earlier and steeper structural change in this group. 40 Although a meta‐analysis indicated heterogeneity in the association between ε4 genotype and WMLs, 41 our MRI results align with this literature: WML burden was not increased among heterozygotes but was elevated in homozygotes. In addition, previous studies of lifestyle and vascular risk have shown that healthier profiles are associated with larger brain volumes and lower WML burden, corresponding to an estimated 2‐ to 6‐year delay in brain aging. 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 Notably, the large population‐based UK Biobank study reported broadly similar associations between lifestyle and MRI findings across APOE ε4 genotypes, 46 although heterozygotes and homozygotes were combined into a single “carrier” category in that study. In our study and in a previous report, 47 the reduced dementia risk observed in the low mRF score group diminished with an increasing number of ε4 alleles and became minimal in homozygotes, suggesting that differences across ε4 genotypes may be obscured when heterozygotes and homozygotes are analyzed together.

In the analysis of plasma biomarkers for dementia, a high number of APOE ε4 allele was associated with lower plasma Aβ42/40 ratio levels, with minimal differences by mRF score group within each genotype. Furthermore, plasma p‐tau181 levels were positively associated with the number of APOE ε4 alleles in both the low‐ and high‐mRF‐score groups (both p for trend ≤ 0.002), whereas no significant differences were detected between the mRF score groups within each genotype. Since plasma levels of Aβ42/40 ratio and p‐tau181 are considered an amyloid‐related biomarker, these findings suggest that the APOE ε4 genotype contributes to Aβ accumulation, 8 , 48 which appears to be relatively less influenced by mRFs. In contrast, plasma NfL levels were significantly lower in the low‐mRF‐score group than in the high‐mRF‐score group in both non‐carriers and heterozygotes, whereas no consistent dose‐dependent association was observed between APOE ε4 genotypes and plasma NfL levels. The observed link between mRF and NfL is consistent with previous studies showing that NfL is related to small vessel disease and WML burden. 49 , 50 Based on the exploratory biomarker analyses, these patterns may provide biological context for the observed interaction between APOE genotype and the mRF score in our dementia analyses. However, mechanistic inferences cannot be drawn from these findings. These interpretations are based on observational data and a limited sample size, particularly of ε4 homozygotes, and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Further studies with larger samples are warranted to confirm these findings.

This study has some limitations. First, although this is one of the largest community‐based cohorts with multimodal imaging and biomarkers in APOE ε4 homozygotes, the number of ε4 homozygotes was limited, which may have resulted in insufficient statistical power to evaluate the effects of mRF within this group. Second, because of the observational cross‐sectional design, causal relationships cannot be inferred. Third, although this study included a wide range of covariate data, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded. Fourth, clinical risk was assessed at a single time point, but trajectories and cumulative exposure were unavailable. Fifth, plasma biomarker data for dementia were available from only one research site. Finally, the generalizability of our results may be limited by potential survivorship and volunteer biases.

CONCLUSIONS

In this community‐based cross‐sectional study of older Japanese adults, dementia risk increased with the number of ε4 alleles, and homozygotes had the highest dementia risk. Among non‐carriers and heterozygotes, a low mRF score was linked to a lower risk of dementia. However, this reduced dementia risk associated with a more favorable mRF profile was not observed in homozygotes. These clinical findings were mirrored on MRI and complemented by exploratory biomarker data, a pattern compatible with a distinct pathophysiological trajectory in homozygotes. These findings suggest that homozygotes may have a different disease process and that the targets, timing, and intensity of prevention strategies should be tailored according to genetic risk.
