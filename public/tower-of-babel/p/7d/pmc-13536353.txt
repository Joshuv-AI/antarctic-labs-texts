Effect of horizontal dimensional malocclusions on smile changes in frontal, lateral, and oblique views before and after orthodontic treatment
Authors: Johnson Hsin-Chung Cheng, Sarah Jia-Min Lin, Yuan-Che Hsu, Tsendsuren Khurelochir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/purposeSmile esthetics are an important aspect of orthodontic treatment; however, most studies have focused on frontal views. This study examines how horizontal skeletal and dental changes impact smiles from frontal, lateral, and oblique views before and after treatment.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study analyzed lateral cephalometric X-rays from orthodontic patients grouped by ANB (I: ≤0°, II: 0–4°, III: ≥4°) and overjet (A: ≤0 mm, B: 0–4 mm, C: 4 ≥ mm). A total of 20, 22, and 22 smile parameters were measured in frontal, lateral, and oblique images. ANOVA and Scheffé tests assessed associations between ANB, OJ, and smile parameters. AI machine learning ranked influencing factors.ResultsAfter treatment, group A showed the greatest maxillary incisor display increase in the frontal view. In the oblique view, only smile length and visible maxillary dental length significantly differed among ANB groups. Smile changes were most prominent in the lateral view. In group I, upper lip landmarks moved upward; in group III, they moved downward. AI machine learning ranked upper maxillary incisor display as the key factor in the frontal view and visible maxillary dental length in lateral and oblique views.ConclusionLateral views show the most smile changes post-orthodontic treatment. ANB influences smile changes more than OJ in lateral and oblique views, while OJ dominates in the frontal view. Class III cases show the most significant changes. Upper incisor display is key in the frontal view, while maxillary dental length dominates laterally and obliquely. Including lateral and oblique data can enhance orthodontic diagnosis accuracy.

Introduction

A smile is among the most crucial human facial expressions. According to Goldstein,1 a smile ranks second only to the eyes in terms of influence on perceived facial attractiveness. In addition, research indicates that individuals with aesthetically pleasing smiles may receive social benefits,2 rendering smile aesthetics a primary concern for both patients and orthodontists. Traditionally, orthodontic diagnoses were primarily based on cephalometric analysis norms, with these norms used to achieve an aesthetic facial profile, proper tooth alignment, occlusion, and functional balance. However, Ackerman and colleagues indicated that patients who achieved optimal occlusal relationships through conventional orthodontic treatment did not always attain satisfactory smile aesthetics.3 Because an attractive smile is a key motivator for many patients seeking orthodontic care, orthodontic treatment approaches have increasingly prioritized smile enhancement.

Multiple factors affect smile aesthetics. In 1958, Frush and Fisher introduced the concept in prosthodontics,4 and Hulsey later identified 5 key factors affecting smile aesthetics,5 concluding that a smile line ratio near 1.00 and an upper lip positioned at the gingival margin of the upper incisors contribute to a more attractive smile. Furthermore, Sarver emphasized the importance of maintaining or enhancing the smile arc to achieve greater harmony and aesthetic appeal.6 The horizontal and vertical dimensions of dentofacial morphology also affect smile esthetics.7 Notably, studies evaluating smile changes before and after orthodontic treatment have mainly focused on the frontal view,8–10 few have incorporated lateral and oblique smile changes into their analyses. Previous studies from the Cheng group have shown that various malocclusion types present differently across frontal, lateral, and three-quarter smile views, and that clinicians and patients evaluate these views differently. Their work also identified distinct factors influencing smile esthetics in each view and demonstrated that horizontal anterior discrepancies exert greater effects on multi-view smile characteristics than vertical discrepancies.11–13

However, no study has compared pre- and post-treatment smile changes across these three perspectives. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate how horizontal dimensional discrepancies affect smile characteristics in frontal, oblique, and lateral views before and after orthodontic treatment.

To guide the analysis, two null hypotheses were tested:

smile changes are equivalent across frontal, oblique, and lateral views; and

smile changes are equivalent across frontal, oblique, and lateral views; and

philtrum inferius, labiale superius, and stomion superius show no significant positional change after horizontal correction.

philtrum inferius, labiale superius, and stomion superius show no significant positional change after horizontal correction.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This study was approved by the Joint Institutional Review Board of Taipei Medical University (TMU-JIRB N201803029). This retrospective cohort study was conducted using data randomly sampled from an archive of eligible cases from 2017 to 2020. Patients were considered to be eligible if they were over 18 years of age and had complete pretreatment and posttreatment records, including smile photographs (frontal, lateral, and oblique views), study models, panoramic X-rays, and cephalometric X-rays. Patients with craniofacial deformities, including cleft lip, cleft palate, and facial asymmetry, and those with a history of orthognathic surgery were excluded.

Samples were categorized on the basis of skeletal and dental anterior-posterior (AP) dimensions, which were represented by the A-Nasion-B (ANB) angle and overjet (OJ), respectively. The ANB groups were defined as follows: Group I: ANB ≤0°, Group II: ANB = 0°–4°, and Group III: ANB ≥4°. The OJ groups were classified as Group A (negative OJ, ≤0 mm), Group B (average OJ, 0–4 mm), and Group C (large OJ, ≥4 mm).

Extraoral photography and image adjustment

Pretreatment and posttreatment photographs were taken by the same examiner. Participants were asked to sit 1.5 m from a camera with the lens positioned at eye level and to look straight ahead. Images were captured by s digital camera (Canon 400D Melville, NY, USA) with a shutter speed of 1/160, F7.1 and ISO 200. For each participant, 6 photographs were taken before and after treatment, with these including the frontal, lateral (90° left), and oblique (45° left) smile views. The camera was consistently placed 1.5 m away from each patient. During the photography session, the patients were instructed to maintain a straight gaze, and their head position was adjusted using Bass’s method to achieve a natural head position with the aid of the clinician. In lateral and oblique photographs, the Frankfort plane was aligned parallel to the floor, with the eyes open and directed forward. For the oblique view, the patients were instructed to rotate their entire body 45° to the left, which ensured that the nose tip was tangent to the border of the contralateral cheek. For the lateral view, the patients turned 90° to the left, and the photograph was taken from the right side of the face, which ensured that the left eyebrow was not visible.

All 6 photographs were processed in Photoshop (Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) at a resolution of 300 pixels per inch. Pretreatment and posttreatment smile images were resized and aligned by overlaying them. To facilitate alignment, the opacity of the posttreatment image was reduced to 50 % before the overlap was finalized. For the frontal smile view, images were aligned using the interpupillary line, and the ear-to-eye line was used for the lateral and oblique views. The patient’s head position was adjusted to a plane close to the Frankfort horizontal plane.

Smile variables

This study included 22 smile variables, that is, 8 landmark coordinates, 13 linear measurements, and 1 ratio. Each variable is explained in Fig. 1, and detailed definitions is provided in Appendix 1.

Linear variables were calculated by subtracting the pretreatment value (T1) from the posttreatment value (T2) and dividing the result by the pretreatment value (T1) to eliminate bias caused by individual differences.

Landmark variables were determined using the Y-axis intercepts of all landmark coordinates. The difference between posttreatment (T2) and pretreatment (T1) values was divided by the sum of the nasal ridge length and nasal height. The X axis was defined as the interpupillary line, and the Y axis was defined as the vertical line passing through the midpoint of the interpupillary line, perpendicular to the X-axis.

A total of 20, 22, and 22 smile variables were assessed in the frontal, lateral, and oblique views, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). The study was powered at 80 %, with the significance level set at P < 0.05. G-Power (version 3.1; Heinrich-Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) was used to determine the required sample size. Means and standard deviations were calculated for each cephalometric and smile variable. ANOVA was conducted to assess changes in smile variables in the ANB and OJ groups. Intragroup post-hoc comparisons were performed using the Scheffé test. An AI-assisted machine learning workflow (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) was used to automate facial landmark detection, extract smile measurements, and quantify changes across frontal, oblique, and lateral views. The pipeline consisted of standardized image preprocessing, automated landmark identification, and measurement extraction, with manual verification as needed for accuracy. This workflow ensured consistent and reproducible evaluation of smile characteristics, particularly regarding horizontal skeletal (ANB) and dental (overjet) effects. To evaluate systemic errors, 5 % of the patient photographs and lateral cephalometric X-rays were randomly reexamined 2 weeks after the initial measurements. Reliability was tested using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis, with a Kappa value greater than 0.75. To avoid random errors, this study included a large sample size.

Discussion

In frontal views, the interlabial gap change was influenced by ANB; however, no significant differences were observed among the 3 ANB groups (Table 2). Variations in the anteroposterior relationship of the maxilla and mandible may lead to different degrees of retraction of the orbicularis oris muscle and lips during smiling, resulting in varying interlabial gaps in the frontal view. However, in the current study, the changes in the 3 groups were minimal, which may explain the lack of significant differences. This could also be attributed to the uneven distribution of the sample sizes. Future studies should increase their sample sizes to determine whether statistical significance can be more clearly demonstrated.

In this study, maxillary incisor display significantly differed among the OJ groups but not among the ANB groups. This indicates that maxillary incisor display during smiling was affected more by dental horizontal discrepancies than by skeletal horizontal discrepancies, likely because individuals with skeletal differences do not always exhibit corresponding dental malocclusions.

The changes in maxillary incisor display were significantly greater in Group A than in Groups B and C. This indicates that patients with a negative OJ experienced greater maxillary incisor proclination and display following treatment, which would be consistent with the results of a previous study.7

In oblique views, few studies have focused on smile analysis from the oblique view, and no study has investigated treatment-related changes in oblique smile variables, increasing the difficulty of comparing our findings with others in the literature. In the current study, in the oblique view, only smile length and maxillary dental length significantly differed among the ANB groups. This finding may be attributable to horizontal vector changes during smiling, which can significantly affect the appearance of the oblique view, particularly in cases of mandibular prognathism and maxillary protrusion. However, among the OJ groups, these horizontal vector changes did not produce effects as noticeable as those observed with skeletal differences, rendering them less prominent. This finding indicates that skeletal structure has a greater influence than dental alignment does on smiling changes in the oblique view. Because the oblique view lies between the lateral and frontal perspectives, some horizontal vector changes may be diluted, increasing the difficulty of detecting meaningful differences.

For lateral views, in the present study, we found that the lateral view had more affected variables than the oblique and frontal views did. This is primarily because anteroposterior changes in the horizontal dimension are most prominently displayed in the 90° lateral view. Thus, the number of variables affected in the lateral view was significantly greater than that in the other 2 views.

Changes in landmarks representing upper lip position (philtrum inferius, labrale superius, and stomion superius) differed significantly among the ANB groups. Patients in the group I exhibited upward movement of the upper lip when smiling after treatment, whereas those in the group III exhibited downward movement. In Group A, reverse OJ was associated with decreased upper lip tension and insufficient incisor exposure. Because the upper anterior teeth provide structural support for the adjacent upper lip, correction of crossbite caused the upper teeth to flare outward, increasing upper lip tension and leading to upper lip upward movement during smiling, which resulted in greater tooth exposure.7

By contrast, in Group C, when large OJ was corrected, the upper anterior teeth became more upright, reducing lip tension and causing the upper lip to move downward, leading to decreased tooth display. This finding is consistent with that of a previous study.7

Three landmarks were affected by ANB but not by OJ, likely because these landmarks are more affected by skeletal morphology than by dental relationships.

Because the lateral view most effectively reveals changes in the anteroposterior relationship of the maxilla, mandible, and teeth, significant differences were observed in smile length, maxillary dental length, and canine length in relation to both ANB and OJ.

According to the machine learning ranking shown in Fig. 2, the inclination of the maxillary incisors varied before treatment and among the 3 types of malocclusion, exhibited the greatest change after treatment. Thus, maxillary incisor display in the frontal smile view exhibited the most significant change before and after treatment. In the frontal view, the effect of ANB was not as readily observable, whereas changes in dental relationships were more pronounced, rendering OJ a more influential factor than ANB. By contrast, in the oblique and lateral views, posterior traction of the muscles during smiling led to the most noticeable changes in maxillary visible dental length before and after treatment. In these views, the effect of the anteroposterior relationship of the maxilla and mandible (ANB) was more apparent than that of the dentoalveolar relationship (OJ).

Although several steps were taken to minimize bias, certain limitations should still be noted. This study enrolled 182 subjects (Table 1), but subgrouping resulted in unequal sample distributions. A G-Power analysis confirmed that most subgroups achieved sufficient statistical power, although two groups fell short due to smaller sample sizes. Increasing the number of subjects in future investigations would help improve the robustness of subgroup analyses.

To account for individual anatomical variation, all measurements were normalized using either (posttreatment – pretreatment)/nasal length or relative pretreatment values rather than absolute distances. While this standardization helped reduce interindividual variability, it also narrowed the overall data range, which may have made it more difficult to detect subtle changes.

Furthermore, several known modifiers of smile characteristics—including sex,14–16 age,16,17 and extraction versus nonextraction treatment modalities18—were not included in the present analysis. These variables were intentionally excluded to avoid excessive model complexity, but their omission may have influenced the observed outcomes. Future studies incorporating these factors may achieve a more comprehensive assessment and further reduce residual confounding.

The findings of this study indicate that the frontal smile view alone does not fully capture horizontal vector changes after orthodontic treatment for all types of malocclusions. In some cases, these changes are more clearly observed in the lateral and oblique views. Therefore, incorporating lateral and oblique view data into orthodontic examinations and diagnoses is recommended to improve treatment planning and mechanism design.

Changes in the horizontal dimension affect smile characteristics in the frontal, lateral, and oblique views after orthodontic treatment. However, these changes are more pronounced in the lateral view than in the frontal and oblique views. In the lateral and oblique views, ANB variations have a greater impact on smile changes than OJ variations do, whereas in the frontal view, OJ has a stronger effect than ANB does. Regarding types of malocclusion, Class III cases exhibited the most significant smile changes. Furthermore, this study revealed that the primary factor affecting the frontal view is upper incisor display, whereas maxillary dental length plays a key role in the lateral and oblique views. Given these findings, integrating lateral and oblique view data into orthodontic assessments is crucial to developing more precise treatment strategies.
