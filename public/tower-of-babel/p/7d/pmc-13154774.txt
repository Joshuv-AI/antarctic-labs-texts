Population Pharmacokinetics of the Novel Myeloperoxidase Inhibitor Mitiperstat
Authors: Mikael Sunnåker, Jacob Leander, Hans Ericsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMitiperstat is a novel myeloperoxidase inhibitor being investigated for the treatment of heart failure, metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Pharmacokinetic data from five clinical trials were used to develop a mitiperstat population pharmacokinetics (popPK) model. In total, 2856 plasma samples from 128 mitiperstat‐treated participants were collected during the first‐in‐human single‐ascending dose (SAD) study; multiple‐ascending dose studies (MAD) in healthy volunteers, including healthy Japanese and Chinese volunteers (JCMAD); the phase 2a SATELLITE study in patients with heart failure with preserved/mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFpEF/HFmrEF); and from a study in patients with severe renal impairment. The mitiperstat concentration‐time data were pooled and analyzed with the software NONMEM. The covariates considered in this analysis were baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), baseline body weight, baseline body mass index, sex, race (Asian or non‐Asian), age, disease status (healthy volunteers or patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF), and formulation (oral suspension or tablet). The final popPK model is a two‐compartment model with first‐order absorption and linear elimination. Baseline body weight, disease status, Asian race, and eGFR were identified as significant covariates for apparent clearance, and age was identified as a significant covariate for apparent central volume of distribution. The final model predicts that severe renal impairment and lower body weight have the largest impact on exposure. Exposure to mitiperstat increased as eGFR declined. This popPK model constitutes an important step toward optimizing doses for efficacy and safety in the different mitiperstat development programs. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02712372

Introduction

Myeloperoxidase contributes to the development and progression of cardiovascular disease, playing key roles in processes such as endothelial dysfunction [1], atherogenesis [2, 3, 4], and plaque instability [5]. Mitiperstat is a potent myeloperoxidase inhibitor investigated for the treatment of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) [6, 7], metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH) [8], and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [9]. Mitiperstat is generally well tolerated in healthy volunteers and in patients with HFpEF or heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF), with maculopapular rash being the most commonly reported adverse event [7, 10, 11, 12].

To date, the pharmacokinetics (PK) of mitiperstat have been evaluated in several clinical trials. In a single‐ascending dose (SAD) study (NCT02712372; 5–405 mg) [10] and a multiple‐ascending dose (MAD) study (NCT03136991; 5–45 mg) [11] conducted in healthy volunteers, mitiperstat was rapidly absorbed with a median time to maximum concentration of approximately 1 h. The elimination of mitiperstat was biphasic, with a mean terminal half‐life of approximately 60 h in the MAD study, with PK reaching steady state within 10 days. Maximum plasma concentration (C max) and area under the plasma concentration‐time curve (AUC) were approximately dose‐proportional in the mitiperstat dose ranges of 5–45 mg in the MAD study and 45–405 mg in the SAD study, and reported to be supra‐proportional at doses less than 45 mg in the SAD study. A similar PK profile, although dose linear PK in the dose range evaluated, was reported in a MAD study conducted in healthy Japanese and Chinese volunteers (JCMAD, NCT04232345; 2.5–10 mg) [12]. In the phase 2a SATELLITE study (NCT03756285) in patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF, PK samples were collected for evaluation of exposure levels [7].

More than 30% of total apparent oral plasma clearance of mitiperstat occurs via renal excretion [13, 14]. Additionally, patients with cardiovascular diseases often have comorbidities which include impaired renal function [15, 16]. Therefore, the PK of mitiperstat was also evaluated in patients with severe renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] ≥ 15 and < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2; NCT04949438) following a single oral dose of mitiperstat 2.5 mg [14]. These patients had an approximately twofold higher exposure and twofold lower apparent clearance compared with patients with normal renal function (eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m2).

This study aims to develop a population PK (popPK) model describing the plasma concentration‐time profile of mitiperstat using the pooled data from healthy volunteers, patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF, and patients with severe renal impairment. This model will be used to simulate clinically relevant scenarios and analyze the impact of covariates on mitiperstat exposure [6].

Methods

Clinical Studies

All studies were performed in accordance with ethical principles that had their origin in the Declaration of Helsinki and were consistent with the International Council for Harmonisation Guideline for Good Clinical Practice and the AstraZeneca policy on Bioethics and Human Biological Samples.

This popPK analysis was based on data from 128 study participants in three phase 1 SAD/MAD studies in healthy volunteers, in the phase 2a SATELLITE study in patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF, and in a phase 1 study in patients with severe renal impairment (Table 1).

In the phase 1 SAD study (NCT02712372), 40 healthy volunteers in five sequential cohorts were randomized 3:1 to receive a single oral dose of mitiperstat 5, 15, 45, 135, or 405 mg, or placebo after overnight fasting. After a washout period of at least 7 days, participants in the mitiperstat 45 mg cohort received an additional mitiperstat 45 mg dose after a high‐calorie meal [10].

In the phase 1 MAD study (NCT03136991), 37 healthy volunteers received a once‐daily oral dose of mitiperstat 5, 10, 15, or 45 mg, or placebo for 10 days, with the exception of the 10 mg cohort, which received mitiperstat for 14 days [11].

In the phase 1 JCMAD study (NCT04232345), 24 healthy Japanese volunteers were randomized 3:1 to receive a once‐daily oral dose of mitiperstat 2.5, 5, or 10 mg, or placebo for 10 days. Eight healthy Chinese volunteers were randomized 3:1 to receive a once‐daily oral dose of mitiperstat 5 mg or placebo for 10 days. Japanese and Chinese race was defined as having both parents and four grandparents who were ethnically Japanese or Chinese [12].

In the phase 2a SATELLITE study (NCT03756285), 41 patients with symptomatic heart failure, left ventricular ejection fraction of ≥ 40%, and elevated B‐type natriuretic peptides were randomized 2:1 to receive once‐daily oral mitiperstat 2.5 mg or matching placebo for 10 days. The mitiperstat dose was then uptitrated to 5 mg for an additional 80 days [7].

In the phase 1 renal impairment study (NCT04949438), 10 patients with severe renal impairment, defined as eGFR ≥ 15 and < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 and not on dialysis, and 10 group‐matched controls (eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m2) received a single oral dose of mitiperstat 2.5 mg [14].

In the SAD, MAD, and JCMAD studies, all volunteers were male, aged 18–50 years, with body mass index (BMI) of 18–30 kg/m2, and a body weight of 50–100 kg. Mitiperstat was administered in the form of an oral suspension. In the SATELLITE and renal impairment studies, mitiperstat was administered as a film‐coated tablet. In all studies except for one food cohort each in SAD and SATELLITE studies, mitiperstat was administered under fasted conditions. Detailed PK sampling schedules for all studies are described in Table 1.

Bioanalytical Methods

Plasma samples collected for mitiperstat PK analysis were assayed using liquid chromatographic‐tandem mass spectrometric (LC–MS/MS) methods that were validated in accordance with current regulatory guidance. The lower limit of concentration was 2 nmol/L in all studies with the exception of the renal impairment study, where it was 0.2 nmol/L.

Data Set Assembly

Data files were extracted from the clinical study database at AstraZeneca and merged into a pharmacometrics (NONMEM) analysis data set by a pharmacometrician. Participants with at least one measurable PK sample were included. All PK samples before the first dose were removed from the NONMEM data set.

Pharmacometric Model Development

Structural Base Model

A two‐compartment, first‐order absorption and linear elimination base model was developed based on descriptive analysis of the PK data. The two compartments were motivated by the biphasic nature of the mitiperstat elimination phase. Baseline eGFR (CKD‐EPI [17]) was included as a covariate on clearance using a power model due to the known impact of renal function on mitiperstat clearance [14].

Statistical Model

Interindividual variability was removed or added from the model based on statistical significance and feasibility of inclusion, and was estimated using the following equation: θi=θ×eηi where θi is the parameter for participant i, θ is the parameter for a typical individual in the population, and ηi is the random effects for participant i. The random effects ηi are independent, identically distributed random variables with a mean of 0 and a variance of ω2ηi~N0ω2. Interindividual variability was included for all structural parameters except for apparent inter‐compartmental clearance Q/F.

Residual error was accounted for using an additive error model where observation and model prediction values are log‐transformed: logyij=logy^ij+e,e~N0σ2 where yij is observation j in the individual i, y^ij is the corresponding model prediction, and e is the additive residual error with variance σ2.

Covariate Model

Baseline eGFR was included in the base model. The additional covariates considered in this analysis include baseline body weight (kg), baseline BMI (kg/m2), sex, race (Asian or non‐Asian), age, disease status (healthy volunteers or patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF), and formulation (oral suspension or tablet).

Covariates included in the model were identified using a stepwise covariate modeling (SCM) approach, as implemented in Perl‐speaks NONMEM (PsN) [18], with a forward elimination step (p < 0.01), followed by a backward elimination step (p < 0.001).

Continuous covariates were estimated using the power model: θx,i=θx*CiCmedianβx where θx,i is the estimate of population parameter θx for individual i, Ci is the value of the covariate for individual i, Cmedian is the median value of the covariate in the population, and βx is a population parameter.

Categorical covariates were estimated using the equation: θx,i=θx*1+θxcov*COVi where θx,i is the estimate of population parameter θx for the individual i, COVi is either 0 or 1 for a specific level of the covariate for the individual i, and θxcov is the effect of the specific covariate level on the parameter estimate.

Model Evaluation

Models were evaluated based on the plausibility and precision of parameter estimates, goodness‐of‐fit plots, visual predictive check (VPC) plots, distribution of random effect parameters, and the correlation of random effect parameters with covariates.

Discrimination between models was mainly based on the inspection of graphical diagnostics, statistical significance of parameters, and on changes in the objective function value (OFV). The difference in OFVs (ΔOFVs) were nominally χ 2 distributed. Inclusion of covariates in models was assessed based on plots of the distribution of covariates versus random effect parameters and the precision of the related parameter estimates.

Simulations

The final model was used to simulate a range of different eGFR values to investigate the impacts of renal impairment on the PK of mitiperstat. Other covariates included in the final model were set to the values most relevant for the SATELLITE study—a phase 2a study in patients with HFpEF or HFmrEF, or to values considered typical for patients with heart failure and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) > 40%. A forest plot was used to illustrate the link between covariates and the mitiperstat exposure.

The final popPK model was also used to simulate steady state exposures for the mitiperstat doses 2.5 and 5 mg used in the ENDEAVOR study [19]. The results of the model simulations are presented with interindividual variability, but without residual error.

Software

The software package NONMEM, version 7.3.0 (Icon Development Solutions, Ellicott City, MD, USA, 2009) was used in this analysis. PsN version 4.4.8 (psn.sourceforge.net) was used for executing NONMEM runs and post‐processing of NONMEM output, that is, assessing goodness‐of‐fit. R version 3.5.1 (R‐project, www.r‐project.org) was used for preprocessing of data, exploratory analysis, submitting NONMEM runs to the computational cluster, model evaluations, and visualizations of simulations. The R package mrgsolve, version 0.8.12, was used for simulating the final model. For the analyses done during the revision of the manuscript R version 4.1.3 and NONMEM version 7.5.1 were used.

Results

Baseline Patient Characteristics and Covariates

In total, 2856 plasma PK samples from 128 participants receiving mitiperstat were included in this analysis (Table 1). Participants receiving placebo were not included. Samples with mitiperstat plasma concentrations below the lower limit of quantification (BLQ) were excluded (n = 139, 4.9% of total samples). BLQ samples were mostly observed in the terminal phase. Given their distribution and limited representation during the absorption phase, exclusion was deemed appropriate, and other BLQ handling methods were not implemented. No samples were excluded owing to missing information, except for the record mentioned in Table 1.

A summary of baseline demographic characteristics is shown in Table 2. Only male participants were included in the SAD, MAD, and JCMAD studies, whereas male and female participants were equally represented in the SATELLITE and renal impairment studies. Apart from the 24 participants in the JCMAD study, only two other Asian participants (in the MAD study) were included in the data set. Overall, the Asian participants in the JCMAD study had a lower average body weight than participants in other studies (69.9 vs. 77.5–84.8 kg), which was expected. Participants in the SATELLITE study were older compared with healthy volunteer studies (mean age 75.2 vs. 33.9–35.5 years) and had a diagnosis of heart failure, with LVEF ≥ 40%. These factors could contribute to their lower mean eGFR values (69 mL/min/1.73 m2 vs. 104–112 mL/min/1.73 m2 in healthy volunteer studies), which were still considered within the normal range.

Exploratory Data Analysis

Analysis of Covariates

A strong positive correlation (Pearson correlation coefficient: 0.8) was observed between baseline body weight and baseline BMI. A negative correlation between eGFR and age (Pearson correlation coefficient: −0.7), probably due to declining renal function with age, was also observed.

Pharmacokinetics Analysis

Descriptive analyses of PK for the SAD, MAD, JCMAD, and renal impairment studies have previously been published [10, 11, 12, 14].

In the phase 2a SATELLITE study [7], patients received mitiperstat 2.5 mg for 10 days, which was then uptitrated to 5 mg for 80 days. The trough concentration (Ctrough) of mitiperstat was dose‐proportional, with the Ctrough for 2.5 mg approximately half of the Ctrough for 5 mg (Figure S1). At a dose of mitiperstat 5 mg, patients in the SATELLITE study had a higher Ctrough compared with the healthy volunteers in the MAD study (Figure S1).

Population Pharmacokinetics Model

Base Model

A two‐compartment, first‐order absorption and linear elimination base model constituted the starting point for model development. A model with three compartments was also evaluated, but did not improve the description of the data. Bioavailability (F) could not be estimated because intravenous data were not available, and parameters were estimated as apparent clearance(s) and volume(s) of distribution. Goodness of fit and OFV were similar for the model with and without interindividual variability for F, but the precision of the parameter estimates was better without (Figure S2). Therefore, interindividual variability was not included for F in the base model.

The only covariate included in the base model was eGFR on apparent clearance (CL/F). When eGFR was not included on CL/F, an increase of 63 units in the OFV (−4445 vs. −4508) and a clear trend in the individual random effects for CL/F versus eGFR were observed.

Covariate Model

Baseline BMI was not included in the SCM analysis because of its strong correlations with body weight. Sex was also excluded because of the small number of female participants (17% of total patients) and potential confounding with body weight and formulation (female participants were included in the two studies that also used a tablet formulation). The covariate eGFR, which was included in the base model, was retained in all SCM analyses.

Baseline body weight, age, disease status (healthy volunteers vs. patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF), and race (Asian vs. non‐Asian) were assessed on the apparent central volume of distribution (Vc/F) and CL/F. CL/F increased with eGFR and body weight; Asians had 27% higher CL/F compared to non‐Asians, participants with HFpEF/HFmrEF had 23% lower clearance than HVs, and Vc/F increased with age (Table S1). Formulation (tablet or oral suspension) was not a significant covariate for the absorption rate.

Final Model

The covariates identified with SCM analysis were included in the final model. Goodness‐of‐fit plots (Figure S3), VPC plots (Figures S4–S6), and plots of distributions of random effect parameters (Figure S7) showed no significant correlations between random effects. The highest correlation, which can be considered weak to moderate (0.46), was observed for CL/F‐Vc/F. Although no correlations were included in the model, accounting for the CL/F‐Vc/F correlation coefficient could potentially reduce simulated prediction intervals. There were also no clear trends between the covariates and the random parameter estimates. Parameter estimates from the final model are shown in Table 3.

An attempt to add allometric scaling with fixed exponents to the final model resulted in a slightly reduced fit (Table S2).

The final model includes eGFR normalized to a body surface area of 1.73 m2 on CL/F. The final model was therefore also re‐estimated with eGFR normalized to body surface area replaced by nonnormalized eGFR (correlation 0.95), which resulted in similar parameter estimates.

AUCss, Cmax, and Ctrough demonstrated comparable change patterns across the tested covariates. Severe renal impairment and lower body weight had the largest impact on exposure. In patients with eGFR of 30 versus 72 mL/min/1.73 m2, AUCss, Cmax, and Ctrough increased by 49%, 44%, and 55%, respectively. In patients with baseline body weight of 55 versus 86 kg, the respective increases were 44%, 35%, and 50%. Patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF had lower CL/F and therefore higher exposure. No covariates resulted in exposure decreases of > 50%, and no covariates resulted in exposure increases significantly larger than 50%.

Inferred Relationship Between eGFR and CL/F

An approximate linear relationship was observed between eGFR and the estimates of individual CL/F (Figure 2A). The y‐intercept was > 0, suggesting that other metabolic pathways besides urinary excretion are involved in the elimination of mitiperstat.

Exposure to mitiperstat (AUCss ) increased as eGFR declined (Figure 2B). This was expected because exposure at steady state is inversely proportional to CL/F (AUCss = Dose/(CL/F)).

Simulations of Mitiperstat Doses in the ENDEAVOR Study

In the phase 2b ENDEAVOR study, patients with heart failure and LVEF > 40% were randomized 1:1:1 to receive once‐daily mitiperstat 2.5 mg or 5 mg, or placebo for 48 weeks [6]. The final model was used to estimate mitiperstat exposure for these doses at three levels of renal function: eGFR of 30, 72, and 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Figure 3). The other covariates for this model were set to the typical values for a participant in the SATELLITE study [7].

A linear increase in mitiperstat exposure versus dose was observed in the simulations at all levels of renal function (Figure 3). The accumulation of mitiperstat results from its long terminal half‐life (~60 h). Decreased renal function resulted in increased mitiperstat exposure and increased time to steady state (Figure 3). A patient with an eGFR value of 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 had approximately 50% higher exposure to mitiperstat than a patient with an eGFR value of 72 mL/min/1.73 m2.

Discussion

We report a popPK model based on 128 patients from five clinical trials with mitiperstat. The final popPK model is a two‐compartment model with first‐order absorption and linear elimination. In the final model, CL/F increased with eGFR and body weight, Asians had 27% higher CL/F compared to non‐Asians, participants with HFpEF/HFmrEF had 23% lower clearance than HVs, and Vc/F increased with age.

Descriptive PK analyses of the SAD, MAD, JCMAD, and renal impairment studies were previously published [10, 11, 12, 14]. The elimination of mitiperstat is biphasic with a half‐life of ~60 h [11], which indicates that a two‐compartment PK model is appropriate. The long half‐life resulted in approximately threefold accumulation of mitiperstat, and steady state was reached at around day 10. The SATELLITE study was conducted in patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF [7]. Patients in the SATELLITE study had higher age (mean 75.2 vs. 33.9 years) and decreased renal function (mean eGFR 69 vs. 104 mL/min/1.73 m2) compared with healthy volunteers in the MAD study, although their eGFR values were still within the normal range. These differences could explain the higher Ctrough observed in the SATELLITE study compared with the MAD study at a dose of mitiperstat 5 mg. A more than dose‐proportional increase in AUC was seen in the SAD study in healthy participants at doses < 45 mg and the reason for that finding is not known. However, a similar study in Japanese healthy participants at lower doses did not indicate deviation from dose linearity and results agreed with the results from the SAD study. Thus, this was not further evaluated in the model development. When the mitiperstat dose was increased from 2.5 mg to 5 mg, Ctrough increased by approximately twofold as well, suggesting a dose‐proportional relationship similar to that observed in the MAD and JCMAD studies [11, 12].

In the final model, age was included as a significant covariate for Vc/F. Age was higher in SATELLITE than in other studies, and it was also the only study that included patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF; therefore, the potential association between Vc/F and age should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the limited impact of age on exposure (Figure 1) suggests that age alone is not a key factor for dosing recommendations. Baseline body weight, disease status (healthy volunteer vs. patient with HFpEF/HFmrEF), and race (Asian vs. non‐Asian) were included as significant covariates for CL/F. Overall, the impact of these covariates on PK was predicted to be < 50%, compared with a typical patient in the SATELLITE study (Figure 1).

The effect of eGFR on CL/F predicted using the final model was in agreement with findings in the MAD and renal impairment studies. In the MAD study, renal clearance ranged from 10.5 to 13.6 L/h, accounting for approximately 45% of total plasma clearance of mitiperstat (20.3–22.8 L/h) [11]. In the renal impairment study, total apparent mitiperstat clearance in the severe renal impairment cohort (10.8 L/h) was half that of the control cohort (25.6 L/h), suggesting that lower clearance was driven by reduced renal clearance [14].

Severe renal impairment also had the largest impact on exposure: a reduction of eGFR from 72 to 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 resulted in increases of > 40% in AUC. This is again in agreement with findings in the renal impairment study where AUC in the renal impairment group (558 h*nmol/L) was ~twofold of that in the control cohort (264 h*nmol/L) [14].

Urinary excretion of mitiperstat involves not only glomerular filtration but also tubular secretion [14]. Additionally, several metabolic pathways and possibly enterohepatic recycling are also involved in mitiperstat elimination [13, 14, 20]. This is supported by the model‐predicted CL/F as a function of baseline eGFR, where the y‐intercept is > 0 and is consistent with findings in human absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion studies where urinary secretion accounts for 32%–44% of mitiperstat clearance [13]. A mechanistic model separating renal and nonrenal clearance was explored but failed to converge, likely due to sparse data in the low eGFR range.

Although the final model accounts for eGFR differences, patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF may have other metabolic differences compared with healthy volunteers. These differences should be considered when using the model to simulate scenarios for patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF, and refinement for specific patient groups may be needed.

There are several limitations to the study: (1) PK data in the absorption phase for the tablet formulation were only available from the renal impairment study. The SAD, MAD, and JCMAD studies used oral suspensions, which are expected to be absorbed more rapidly than tablets. A covariate to distinguish between oral suspension and tablet formulation was evaluated for the absorption rate, but was found to be not significant; (2) apart from the Japanese and Chinese volunteers in the JCMAD study, there were only two other Asian participants (in the MAD study). It is therefore difficult to distinguish the effect of Asian race on PK exposure against other potential differences that may have existed between the JCMAD and the other studies; (3) limited representation of individuals with reduced eGFR precluded robust development of a mechanistic model separating renal and nonrenal clearance; (4) data from patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF were only available from the SATELLITE study, which had limited information about the absorption profile. Further development of the model could be revisited when more data are available.

This is the first popPK model developed for mitiperstat. The model‐based analysis revealed that mitiperstat exposure was dependent on age, renal function, body weight, and Asian race. The popPK model constitutes an important step toward optimizing doses for efficacy and safety in the different mitiperstat development programs.
