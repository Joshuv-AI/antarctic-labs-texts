Long-term HBV infection of engineered cultures of induced pluripotent stem cell-derived hepatocytes
Authors: Yang Yuan, Vedant V. Bodke, Christine Lin, Shang Gao, Jalees Rehman, Jisu Li, Salman R. Khetani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:HBV infects ~257 million people and can cause hepatocellular carcinoma. Since current drugs are not curative, novel therapies are needed. HBV infects chimpanzee and human livers. However, chimpanzee studies are severely restricted and cost-prohibitive, while transgenic/chimeric mouse models that circumvent the species barrier lack natural HBV infection and disease progression. Thus, in vitro human models of HBV infection are useful in addressing the above limitations. Induced pluripotent stem cell-derived hepatocyte-like cells mitigate the supply limitations of primary human hepatocytes and the abnormal proliferation/functions of hepatoma cell lines. However, variable infection across donors, deficient drug metabolism capacity, and/or low throughput limit iHep utility for drug development.Methods:We developed an optimal pipeline using combinations of small molecules, Janus kinase inhibitor, and 3’,5’-cAMP to infect iHep-containing micropatterned co-cultures (iMPCC) with stromal fibroblasts within 96-well plates with serum-derived HBV and cell culture-derived HBV (cHBV). Polyethylene glycol was necessary for cell-derived HBV but not for serum-derived HBV infection.Results:Unlike iHep monocultures, iMPCCs created from 3 iHep donors could sustain HBV infection for 2+ weeks. Infected iMPCCs maintained high levels of differentiated functions, including drug metabolism capacity. HBV antigen secretion and gene expression patterns in infected iMPCCs in pathways such as fatty acid metabolism and cholesterol biosynthesis were comparable to primary human hepatocyte-MPCCs. Furthermore, iMPCCs could help elucidate the effects of interferons and direct-acting antiviral drugs on the HBV lifecycle and any hepatotoxicity; iMPCC response to compounds was similar to primary human hepatocyte-MPCCs.Conclusions:The iMPCC platform can enable the development of safe and efficacious drugs against HBV and ultimately help elucidate genotype-phenotype relationships in HBV pathogenesis.

INTRODUCTION

HBV has infected ~257 million people, with ~20 million deaths estimated between 2015 and 2030 due to HBV-induced liver disease.1 Current drug therapies cannot eradicate the virus following infection.2 Nucleot(s)ide analog drugs inhibit HBV DNA replication but can have a low rate of HBV antigen seroconversion (ie, insufficient generation of antibodies to clear HBV). In contrast, pegylated interferon prevents de novo infections by regulating the immune response but is only effective in a third of the patients and can have severe side effects.3 Thus, there is a need for the development of curative and safe anti-HBV drugs.

HBV primarily infects chimpanzees and humans. The woodchuck hepatitis virus is similar to human HBV, and woodchucks have been used as animal models to study HBV and test anti-HBV drugs and vaccines; however, woodchucks are not used routinely in pharmaceutical practice. Several transgenic and chimeric mouse models have been developed to overcome the species barrier for HBV infection. Still, they are expensive to maintain and can have a compromised immune response to the virus.4 Regardless, in vivo studies are not suitable for high-throughput drug screening, which necessitates in vitro models of human liver HBV infection. Such models can be generated using primary human hepatocytes (PHHs), but these cells are in short supply and display considerable variations across donors/lots in quality for long-term culture.5 While the sodium taurocholate co-transporter polypeptide (NTCP)-overexpressing HepG2 and HepaRG hepatoma lines provide for relatively abundant and reproducible cell sources for HBV infection,6,7 they are restricted to specific donor backgrounds, display abnormal proliferation, and have lower drug metabolism capacities compared to PHHs.8 In contrast, human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived hepatocyte-like cells (iHeps) allow modeling of the genotype-phenotype relationships in disease progression and response to antiviral drugs.5,9,10,11 The persistence of HBV infection and HBV-related disease progression is significantly associated with host genetic factors.12,13 Previous studies showed that iHeps in 2-dimensional (2D) monolayers and 3-dimensional (3D) organoids can be infected with HBV; however, low functional maturity, especially related to drug metabolism capacity, the inability to infect iHeps from multiple donors, and low throughput are significant hurdles for the routine use of iHep-containing cultures for drug development.14,15

We previously engineered an iHep-containing micropatterned co-culture (iMPCC) platform with stromal fibroblasts within 96-well plates that displays higher differentiated functions than iHep conventional confluent (iCC) monocultures and randomly distributed iHep/fibroblast co-cultures.16 Here, we develop protocols to infect iMPCCs from multiple donors with patient sera- and cell culture-derived HBV sources. We compare phenotypic markers and gene expression patterns in infected iMPCCs with those in iCCs and PHH-MPCCs. We also demonstrate the utility of infected iMPCCs for antiviral drug discovery. We anticipate that the HBV-infected iMPCC platform can enable a significant paradigm shift in the routine use of iPSC technology for high-throughput HBV drug discovery efforts and investigating genotype-phenotype relationships in HBV pathogenesis.

METHODS

Cell culture

iHeps were commercially sourced from Fujifilm Cellular Dynamics International (Madison, WI): iCell 2.0 (donor 1) human iHeps were from a Caucasian female and programmed from dermal fibroblasts; myCell (donor 2) human iHeps (#01279) were from a Caucasian male and programmed from a blood mononuclear cell; and, human iHeps (donor 3, #01177)—the gift of Dr. Paul Watkins of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill—were from a female (unknown race) and programmed from a blood mononuclear cell, also by the same vendor as above. To create iMPCCs, 96-well plates (Corning Life Sciences, Tewksbury, MA) were subjected to a polydimethylsiloxane mask, which was generated by soft lithography, to micropattern circular collagen islands (500 µm diameter, 1200 µm center-to-center spacing) as described16 (Figure 1A, B). The iHep colonies were surrounded by 3T3-J2 murine embryonic fibroblasts at a 3-fibroblasts to 1 iHep ratio. PHHs were obtained from 3 donors (lot HUM4055A: 54-year-old Caucasian female, HUM4145: 20-month-old Caucasian male, HUM4192: 16-year-old Asian female; Lonza, Walkersville, MD). PHH-MPCCs were generated as described.17 Further details on culture methods, including seeding densities and media formulations, are provided in Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5. Hepatocyte functions, staining, and gene expression, including RNA-sequencing analysis to identify differentially expressed genes and gene ontology (GO) pathway analyses, were assessed as described in Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5.

HBV infection

Patient serum-derived HBV (sHBV) with genotype C was used to infect iMPCCs. All sHBV isolates came from pre-collected HBeAg-positive and HBsAg-positive serum samples from Shanghai, China,18 with viral/subviral particles concentrated by ultracentrifugation through a 10% to 20% sucrose cushion.19 The purification of sHBV and genotype determination was executed following protocols outlined in previous studies.18,19,20 The sHBV inoculum (5.0 × 1010 viral particles/mL) was diluted in a medium, and the final multiplicity of infection was tested between 100 and 1200. The iMPCC and iCC culture platforms were incubated with HBV for 24 hours on day 12 following the initiation of iHep culture, while PHH-MPCCs were incubated with HBV for 24 hours on day 8. For some conditions, Janus kinase inhibitor (JAKi, EMD Millipore, Burlington, MA) was added at 2 µM to the medium 24 hours before infection and for only 24 hours during infection. The cultures were rinsed three times with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s medicum (DMEM, Corning Life Sciences) after 24 hours to remove the virus (and JAKi), and then the maintenance medium was added back to each well. Medium was collected every 2 days and stored at 4°C for downstream assays.

Cell-derived HBV (cHBV) was generated from the HepDE19 cell line; previously, 1.1 copies of HBV genome of genotype D were incorporated into HepDE19 chromosomes, and the transcription of pregenomic RNA was activated by the removal of doxycycline.19,21 The concentration of virus measured in our hands was consistent with previous papers.22 iMPCCs were infected with cHBV (1.0 × 1010 viral particles/mL) at different multiplicity of infections (175–2100) in the presence of 4% polyethylene glycol (PEG) 8000 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) for 24 hours. The remaining procedure for infection with cHBV was the same as detailed above for infection with sHBV. Assessment of HBV DNA and Ags were executed as described in Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5. The sequence of primers for polymerase chain reaction for gene expression analysis are listed in Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5.

Compound treatment

HBV-infected cultures were treated with entecavir (ETV, Sigma-Aldrich), tenofovir (TEV, Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI), interferon (IFN) α (Sigma-Aldrich), IFN-β (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN), or vehicle control, 0.2% DMSO (Corning Life Sciences). The final concentrations tested were 2 µM for ETV (66.7 × Cmax, human steady-state peak plasma concentration), 4 µM for TEV (4 × Cmax), 1000 U/mL (0.5 × Cmax) for IFN-α, and 40 U/mL for IFN-β (8 × Cmax).23,24,25 Drug treatment was started on the postinfection day (PID) 3, and fresh drug was added to the replaced medium every 2 days until PID 17. Collected supernatants were assayed for HBV Ags/DNA and hepatocyte functions as described in Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5.

Statistics

All findings were confirmed in at least 2 independent experiments (2+ wells per condition). Data processing was performed using Microsoft Excel, and images were processed/visualized using NIH ImageJ. GraphPad Prism (La Jolla, CA) was used for data visualization and statistical analysis. Mean and SD are displayed for all data sets. Statistical significance of (a) 2 groups was determined by the two-tailed t-test, (b) multiple groups was determined through one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons tests, and (c) multiple groups with more than 1 independent parameter was determined using two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons tests. Significance in figures is indicated with *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

RESULTS

HBV infection in iCC and iMPCC

The iHeps selectively attached to the patterned collagen domains and were surrounded by 3T3-J2 murine embryonic fibroblasts to generate iMPCCs (Figure 1D, E). We also generated iCCs using the same donors and infected both models with HBV (Figure 1A). The iHeps stained positive for albumin in both models (Figure 1B, F). Donor 2 and donor 3 iCCs secreted very low and declining levels of HBeAg than donor 1 iCCs, which displayed increasing HBeAg secretion over time, as much as ~6-fold of PID 5 levels by PID 13 (Figure 2A). Similarly, HBsAg secretion displayed a decline over time in donor 2 and donor 3 iCCs compared to donor 1 iCCs (Figure 2B). Albumin secretion was stable for donor 1 iCCs and increased and then stabilized for donor 2 iCCs, suggesting that the overall differentiated state of these 2 iHep donors in iCCs was not responsible for the differences observed in HBV kinetics; however, albumin secretion for donor 3 iCCs declined over time (Figure 2C).

In contrast to iCC, the same 3 donors of iHeps could sustain HBV infection over time in iMPCCs, as evident with time-dependent increases in both HBeAg (Figure 2D) and HBsAg (Figure 2E) secretions over the first 2 weeks after infection. Albumin secretions were stable for donor 1 and 3 iMPCCs but showed a decline after the first week of culture for donor 2 iMPCCs and stabilized again until day 15 (Figure 2F). Since nearly 9-fold more iHeps were initially attached in iCCs than in iMPCCs, albumin secretions from donor 1 and donor 2 iCCs were 4.5-fold and 6.2-fold higher after 2 weeks than in iMPCCs from the same 2 donors, respectively; however, albumin secretion in donor 3 iMPCCs was 3.1-fold higher than in donor 3 iCCs (Figure 2C vs. F). In addition to albumin, secretion of urea over 2 weeks demonstrated that iHeps were well differentiated in both iCCs and iMPCCs (Supplemental Fig. S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5), again suggesting that differences in HBV kinetics were not due to the differentiated state of the iHeps across the culture platforms.

Lastly, we tested gene expression patterns of key HBV host factors in iCCs and iMPCCs across donor 1 and 2 iHeps. Several hepatic markers, including key HBV host factors, were significantly increased (1.7-fold–168-fold) in donor 2 iMPCCs compared to donor 2 iCCs (Supplemental Fig. S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5). Donor 1 iMPCCs showed downregulation of some HBV host factors compared to donor 1 iCCs, but this was insufficient to alter the infection kinetics across iCCs and iMPCCs (Figure 1).

Effects of JAKi and cAMP on infection kinetics in iMPCC

A broad-spectrum JAKi was previously demonstrated to depress the IFN-stimulated innate immune response of PHHs, thereby allowing PHHs to become more permissive to HBV infection.5 Here, with JAKi treatment (1 and 2 µM), HBV-infected iMPCCs showed up to ~2-fold increased HBeAg secretion (Figure 3A); up to ~2.9-fold increased HBsAg secretion (Supplemental Fig. S3B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5); and, increased intracellular HBcAg staining (Figure 3G) compared to untreated infected control. JAKi also promoted up to ~2.4-fold higher albumin secretion (Figure 3B) and up to ~3-fold higher urea (Supplemental Fig. S3A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5) secretion than the untreated infected control. Overall, 2 µM of JAKi led to higher hepatic function and HBV infection than 1 µM; however, higher 5–10 µM JAKi led to the loss of iHeps from islands over time without improving HBV infection (Supplemental Fig. S4, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5).

Accumulation of intracellular cAMP reconstructs actin filaments and microtubules, thus assisting NTCP translocation to the cell membrane.26 Upon treatment with 1- and 2-mM cAMP, iMPCCs exhibited up to ~2.4-fold higher albumin (Figure 3D) and up to ~9.8-fold higher urea (Supplemental Fig. S3C, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5), indicating that cAMP promoted iHep maturation in iMPCCs. Overall, 2 mM of cAMP led to higher hepatic function than 1 mM. The ratio of ALB and AFP expression was evaluated to confirm iHep maturation further. With cAMP treatment, ALB/AFP was increased ~1.5-fold compared to the nontreated group (Figure 3E). Treatment with cAMP also modestly (~1.1-fold–1.8-fold) increased the expression of HBV-related host factor genes (Figure 3F). However, treatment with cAMP led to only transient increases in HBeAg (Figure 3C) and HBsAg (Supplemental Fig. S3D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5) secretions.

Optimization of infection in iMPCC

Given the results above, iMPCCs were treated with 1 or 2 mM of cAMP plus 2 µM JAKi. The combination treatment led to ~ 8.8-fold increase in albumin secretion (Figure 4D), up to ~30-fold increase in urea secretion (Figure 4E), and up to ~4-fold increase in Cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) activity (Figure 4F) compared to treatment with JAKi alone. Combining 2 mM cAMP and 2 µM JAKi led to higher hepatic functions in iMPCCs than 1 mM cAMP plus 2 µM JAKi. The combination treatment also led to ~6-fold enhanced secretion of HBeAg (Figure 4A), up to ~11-fold enhanced secretion of HBsAg (Figure 4B), and up to ~6.2-fold enhanced secretion of extracellular HBV DNA (Figure 4C) compared to treatment with JAKi alone. The combination treatment allowed iMPCCs to maintain HBV infection for ~4 weeks, albeit infection parameters decreased after ~2 weeks but were still significantly higher than treatment with JAKi alone. Overall, 2 mM cAMP plus 2 µM JAKi led to higher HBeAg and HBsAg secretions but lower extracellular HBV DNA from infected iMPCCs compared to 1 mM cAMP plus 2 µM JAKi. Given that the treatment of iMPCCs with cAMP alone led to only transient increases in HBeAg (Figure 3C) and HBsAg secretions (Supplemental Fig. S3D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5), the increases observed in infection parameters due to the combination of cAMP and JAKi compared to JAKi treatment alone are more than the additive effects of the individual factors.

DMSO can further differentiate hepatoma cell lines and promote their infection with HBV.27 Here, while iMPCCs treated with 1% DMSO displayed up to ~5.3-fold higher (up to ~2.8-fold with 0.5% DMSO) and up to ~3.3-fold higher (up to ~2.1-fold higher with 0.5% DMSO) secretions of HBeAg and HBsAg, respectively (Supplemental Fig. S5A, B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5), urea decreased significantly with DMSO treatment, up to ~57% with 0.5% DMSO and entirely inhibited with 1% DMSO compared to untreated controls (Supplemental Fig. S5C, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5). To investigate whether DMSO could enhance HBV infection with cAMP plus JAKi and whether the 3-factor combination could ameliorate DMSO-induced hepatic dysfunction, 0.5% DMSO was combined with 2 mM cAMP and 2 µM JAKi. This 3-factor combination decreased HBeAg secretion by up to ~27% (Supplemental Fig. S5D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5), HBsAg secretion by up to ~46% (Supplemental Fig. S5E, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5), and urea secretion by up to ~83% (Supplemental Fig. S5F, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5) compared to the 2-factor combination of cAMP and JAKi. Therefore, adding DMSO was suboptimal for improving iHep differentiated functions and improving HBV infection in the presence of cAMP and JAKi.

Infection of iMPCC with different HBV genotypes

The data above were collected from iMPCCs exposed to patient sHBV (genotype C) directly without PEG. We also infected iMPCC with cHBV (genotype D) generated from HepDE19 cells.21 The iMPCCs treated with 1 mM or 2 mM cAMP plus 2 µM JAKi displayed up to ~2.9-fold higher secretion of HBeAg secretion (Supplemental Fig. S6A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5) and up to ~2.9-fold higher HBsAg secretion (Supplemental Fig. S6B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5) compared to JAKi alone for at least 17 days following cHBV infection; 1 mM or 2 mM cAMP plus 2 µM JAKi led to similar Ag secretion levels. However, in contrast to sHBV, infection with cHBV required 4% PEG, suggesting that soluble factors in human serum may facilitate sHBV infection.19

As demonstrated above with sHBV (Figure 2), donor 2 and 3 iCCs could not support robust HBV infection, exhibiting low and declining secretions of HBeAg and HBsAg. In contrast, iMPCCs from donors 2 and 3 showed robust long-term HBV infection and replication. To confirm that this result was not dependent on the viral source, we infected donor 3 iHeps in both iMPCCs and iCCs with cHBV and observed similar infection kinetics as with sHBV infection, in that donor 3 iCCs showed a decline in HBeAg and HBsAg secretions over time (Supplemental Fig. S7A, B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5). In contrast, cHBV-infected iMPCC exhibited increasing HBeAg and HBsAg secretions for 2 weeks (Supplemental Fig. S7D, E, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5). Both models secreted albumin, albeit levels gradually declined over time in iCCs whereas they increased and stabilized in iMPCCs over 2 weeks in culture (Supplemental Fig. S7C, F, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5). Lastly, to demonstrate the lowest viral genome copies to obtain an infection, we tested different genome equivalents of the virus and found as low as 100 genome equivalents of sHBV (Supplemental Fig. S8A, B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5) and 175 genome equivalents of cHBV (Supplemental Fig. S8C, D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5) were able to infect iMPCCs.

Comparison of infection kinetics and gene expression in iMPCC and PHH-MPCC

Given their physiological relevance, PHHs are the “gold-standard” cell type for studying HBV. PHH-MPCCs have previously been shown to support robust infection with HBV over weeks in contrast to declining HBV infection in PHH monocultures.5 Thus, we compared functions and infection kinetics in PHH-MPCCs and iMPCCs. Both models maintained comparable secretion levels and kinetics for HBeAg (Figure 5A) and HBsAg (Figure 5B). However, the 2 PHH donors showed more significant variation in Ag secretions than the 2 different donors of iHeps, thereby making differences not statistically significant across iHeps and PHHs. Furthermore, iMPCCs displayed up to 4-fold higher albumin expression (Figure 5C), 26%–44% of urea secretion (Figure 5D), and 26%–30% of CYP3A4 activity (Figure 5E) compared to PHH-MPCCs. These results suggest that while iHeps in iMPCCs are not fully differentiated to the same level as functionally stabilized PHH-MPCCs, especially for urea secretion and CYP3A4 activity, they are sufficiently differentiated to enable similar HBV infection kinetics over several weeks in culture.

We conducted RNA-sequencing analysis for sHBV-infected and mock-infected iMPCCs and PHH-MPCCs to further elucidate HBV-induced host cell metabolism changes. The mouse fibroblast sequence data was removed computationally as described in the methods. We found that HBV upregulated 67 genes and downregulated 105 genes in iHeps within iMPCC (Figure 6A). Gene ontology (GO) analysis demonstrated that HBV enhanced iHep oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial ATP synthesis through higher MT-CO1, MT-CO3, COX6C, COX7C, and ATP6 expression, which is responsible for the final step of oxygen phosphorylation in the electron transport chain (Figure 6B, C). Oxidative phosphorylation can produce reactive oxygen species, which can cause liver inflammation.28 We also found that HBV-infected iMPCC had lower cholesterol biosynthesis and abnormal alcohol and fatty acid metabolism, as observed via the downregulation of ACADSB, ACOX1, SCD, ACSS2, IDI1, HMGCR, HMGCS1, and related genes (Figure 6B, D).

In PHH-MPCC, HBV upregulated the expression of 48 genes and downregulated the expression of 51 genes (Figure 7A). HBV activated innate immune response in PHH-MPCCs by upregulating interferon-stimulated gene expression and IL-2 relevant gene expression, such as ISG15, IFI6, ACTG1, and GBP1 (Figure 7B, C), which may be due to a more robust innate immune response and viral clearance mechanisms in PHHs relative to iHeps. Consistent with iMPCCs, PHH-MPCCs also displayed lower cholesterol biosynthesis and impaired fatty acids metabolism with lower expression of ACSL1, ACLY, HMGCS1, ACSS2, and related genes. (Figure 7B, D). HBV also decreased the expression of ADH1B and ADH4 in PHH-MPCCs, suggesting impaired alcohol metabolism in HBV-infected PHHs (Figure 7D). Lastly, PCK1 expression was negatively affected by HBV in PHH-MPCCs (Figure 7D), suggesting impaired gluconeogenesis.

Platform utility for testing antiviral compounds

The iMPCCs were infected with sHBV, and then at PID 3, they were treated for 2 weeks with ETV, TEV, IFN-α, and IFN-β (Figure 8A). All compounds suppressed extracellular HBV DNA by 60%–90% after 6 days and 14 days of compound treatment (Figure 8B). Additionally, all compounds, except for TEV, caused suppression of intracellular HBV by 60%–70% after 14 days of compound treatment compared to DMSO-treated controls (Figure 8C). HBsAg secretion was reduced by 20%–50% by TEV, IFN-α, and IFN-β, but not ETV to any significant degree, after 8 days and 12 days of compound treatment compared to DMSO-treated controls (Figure 8D). However, neither HBeAg secretion (Figure 8E) nor intracellular covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) (Figure 8F) was reduced to any significant degree by any of the compounds. For overall hepatic functions, IFN-α and IFN-β enhanced albumin and urea secretions by ~1.3-fold, whereas ETV and TEV suppressed functions by ~25% compared to DMSO-treated controls. As with donor 1 iHeps above, donor 2 iHeps displayed similar responses to antiviral drug treatment in iMPCCs (Supplemental Fig. S9, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5). Unlike donor 1 iHeps, all compounds caused slight increases in albumin and urea secretions in donor 2 iHeps (Supplemental Fig. S9F, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5).

Lastly, we conducted similar compound studies with PHH-MPCCs to compare against the iMPCC response. The tested compounds suppressed both extracellular and intracellular HBV DNA up to 87% and 80%, respectively, in PHH-MPCCs generated from 2 donors (Supplemental Fig. S10A, B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5, and Supplemental Fig. S11A, B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B5). DISCUSSION

Current approaches for infecting iHeps with HBV display low functional maturity, cannot always be infected across multiple iHep donors, or are not sufficiently high throughput for routine screening. We previously developed iMPCCs with higher levels of differentiated functions than iCCs and randomly distributed iHep/fibroblast co-cultures.16 Here, we infected both iMPCCs and iCCs with sHBV (genotype C) and cHBV (genotype D) and found that 2 of the 3 donors of iHeps did not display high infection levels in iCCs. However, in iMPCCs, all 3 iHep donors and both virus sources led to high and sustained infection for 2+ weeks. Such discrepancy was not due to differentiated hepatic functions; albumin secretion was maintained across iHeps donors in both platforms even after HBV infection. Instead, we found that several known liver host factors (eg, RXR, PPARα) involved in HBV infection were upregulated at the gene expression level in iMPCCs compared to iCCs fabricated from iHeps; receptors for HBV uptake in hepatocytes, NTCP and ASGR1, were upregulated by ~168-fold and ~11-fold, respectively.7,29,30,31

Infection of iMPCCs with sHBV did not require complexing the virus with 4% PEG, whereas cHBV required PEG to obtain detectable levels of HBV Ags in the culture supernatants. This result shows that iMPCCs have the appropriate receptors to take up HBV. Still, other co-factors in infected human serum (as opposed to the bovine-serum-containing culture medium used to generate cHBV) may be needed for the optimal binding of the virus to the receptors on the hepatocyte surface. For instance, HBV quasispecies present in infected human serum may enhance viral uptake and replication by modulating cellular immune response;32 HBV is known to bind reversibly to human serum albumin,33 and such binding may facilitate HBV uptake into hepatocytes; extracellular vesicles, including exosomes, present in human serum may enable the hepatocytes to take up viral proteins/DNA or modulate cellular immune responses.34 However, investigation of such mechanisms will require further studies in the future.

DMSO is often used with hepatoma cell lines to differentiate them further and increase their susceptibility to HBV through altering intracellular levels of cAMP and inducing the expression of NTCP on the cell surface by inhibiting the activity of phosphodiesterase (PDE) 4D and phosphodiesterase 12.27 Here, we found that the use of DMSO, up to 1%, also induced HBeAg and HBsAg secretions in iMPCCs by up to ~5-fold and 3-fold, respectively. However, DMSO severely downregulated urea secretion, with 0.5% reducing urea by ~57% and 1% entirely abolishing urea secretion by day 7 of culture. Such downregulation of essential hepatic functions is consistent with previous studies in which CYP3A4 enzyme activity was downregulated by 40%–50% in both PHHs35 and iHeps following DMSO treatment,36 whereas DMSO upregulated CYP3A4 in HepaRG cells.37 Therefore, while DMSO can increase susceptibility to HBV infection, loss of vital hepatic functions, especially those relevant to drug metabolism and hepatotoxicity assessment, precludes the use of DMSO for culture platforms developed for robust and routine HBV drug discovery.

Our results with modulated cholesterol pathways due to HBV align with published RNA sequencing of primary rat hepatocytes infected with the HBV genome through adenoviral delivery.38 However, exact comparisons are difficult to make due to the inability of the rat hepatocytes to be infected with HBV through receptors found on human hepatocytes. Nonetheless, chronic HBV infection has been shown to correlate inversely with total cholesterol and triglyceride levels in human patients.39,40,41 Furthermore, consistent with studies in HBV-infected HepG2 cells42 and clinical data showing lower levels of apolipoproteins in the sera of patients infected with HBV,39 ApoB (transports cholesterol from the liver to peripheral tissues) expression was downregulated by HBV in iHeps in iMPCCs. The downregulation of genes in alcohol metabolism may provide insights into how alcohol consumption can potentially exacerbate HBV-induced liver disease progression, including fibrosis and hepatocellular carcinoma.43,44 Lastly, while the downregulated pathways in HBV-infected iHeps were consistent with those in HBV-infected PHHs, innate immune response pathways (eg, type 1 interferon, interferon-gamma, IL-2 regulation) were upregulated in HBV-infected PHHs, which may explain the need for JAKi to enable robust infection in PHHs. In contrast, JAKi was not required for HBV infection in iHeps. However, the inclusion of JAKi improved HBV infection in iHeps, which suggests that iHeps are also capable of mounting an innate immune response to HBV like their PHH counterparts.

To determine the utility of iMPCCs for HBV drug screening, we tested the effects of 2 nucleot(s)ide analog drugs and IFN-α/β; concentrations were selected from other in vitro studies. We treated iMPCCs with these compounds for up to 14 days after HBV infection since HBeAg, HBsAg, and HBV DNA were detected at maximal levels in culture supernatants for this duration, after which a decline was observed in infected iMPCCs. None of these compounds caused downregulation of either albumin or urea secretion by at least 50%, which was previously shown to correlate strongly with DILI.37,45 Given the widespread use of these compounds to suppress HBV in patients without frequent reports of DILI, our results are consistent with clinical observations. While the compounds did not cause DILI, they were effective at significantly suppressing HBV DNA. However, HBsAg, HBeAg, and cccDNA were not significantly suppressed, except for HBsAg reduction by 14%–50% through IFN-β. Infected PHH-MPCCs responded to compounds in a similar manner as iMPCCs, except that TEV was more effective at reducing intracellular HBV DNA, and interferons were more effective at modestly reducing HBeAg in PHH-MPCCs across 2 PHH donors. Since interferon modulates the innate immune response, it has been shown to cause more HBsAg loss in patients than ETV,46 which is consistent with our above findings. Furthermore, treatment with compounds for a longer duration than 14 days is likely needed to observe changes in serology markers such as HBsAg and HBeAg; indeed, in the clinic, while treatment with nucleot(s)ide analogs effectively reduces HBV DNA in patients, viral seroconversion takes years to achieve.47 Lastly, the inability to significantly suppress cccDNA with tested compounds with a 14-day treatment regimen is likely due to the half-life of cccDNA being between 61 and 122 days.48

In conclusion, the iMPCC platform enables the robust (ie, PHH-like) infection of highly differentiated iHeps from multiple donors with both sHBV and cHBV for 2+ weeks within a 96-well plate format amenable to higher throughput screening, as demonstrated here through proof-of-concept treatments with prototypical compounds. We anticipate that iMPCCs can also be ultimately used to elucidate genotype-phenotype relationships in HBV pathogenesis.
