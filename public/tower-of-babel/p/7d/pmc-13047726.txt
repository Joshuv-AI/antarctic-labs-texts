Privacy-Aware Meta-Optics for Person Detection
Authors: Zaid Tasneem, Yongyi Zhao, Johannes E. Fröch, Arka Majumdar, Ashok Veeraraghavan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ubiquitous use of computer vision technologies in our personal lives has led to privacy concerns. This paper presents a computational camera that optically filters out private attributes such as identity and still enables downstream vision task of person detection. Our approach involves replacing a traditional lens in an imaging setup with broadband meta-optics (MOs), the parameters of which are optimized in an end-to-end fashion using a differentiable look-up table for the MO and a person detection neural network. Privacy is introduced to the optimization pipeline using a novel and computationally inexpensive private Strehl integral regularization to preserve low-frequency details while filtering out high-frequency details that contain facial identity information. We experimentally validate our approach using captures from our privacy-aware meta-optics and demonstrate that this method achieves a better privacy utility trade-off compared to existing techniques. As such, we present the first privacy-aware broadband meta-optics for person detection.

Introduction

The rapid proliferation of camera and computer vision technologies in private spaces such as our homes for security, augmented reality, and personal devices has intensified privacy concerns. − Traditional methods to mitigate these concerns, like postcapture sanitization or encryption, remain susceptible to data breaches and do not protect against server-side access. As a result, a growing body of research has explored the potential of computational cameras that inherently preserve privacy by modifying the optics and providing precapture privacy for both high-level , and low-level inference. ,

These advances in privacy-aware computational imaging have demonstrated that optical systems can be designed to filter out sensitive information, such as facial identity, before photons are converted to digital signals, ensuring privacy is enforced at the hardware level. Notably, end-to-end optimization of refractive and diffractive optical elements has enabled the design of task-specific imaging systems that balance utility and privacy. These systems have shown effectiveness in supporting downstream tasks such as monocular depth estimation, scene captioning, and human pose estimation, , while obfuscating identifiable features. However, the optical bandwidth and fabrication limits of traditional refractive and diffractive optics restrict their performance and scalability in complex real-world applications. ,

Meta-optics have recently emerged as a transformative platform for designing compact, lightweight, and multifunctional imaging systems. − In contrast to traditional refractive and diffractive optics, meta-optics employ subwavelength-scale, nanostructured scatterers, often referred to as meta-atoms, to manipulate the phase, amplitude, and polarization of light with high precision. These planar optical elements enable unprecedented control over light propagation, including the ability to achieve wavelength-dependent responses across different color channels. − Moreover, meta-optics are compatible with standard semiconductor fabrication processes, allowing scalable and cost-effective manufacturing. − Their unique ability to address various challenges such as miniaturization, , combining multiple functionalities, , integrating several subapertures, , and spectral control − position them as an ideal foundation for next-generation computational imaging systems.

Despite their flexibility, meta-optics inherently suffer from chromatic aberrations that introduce significant blur into captured images. Previous works have addressed this challenge by jointly optimizing meta-optics and neural networks end-to-end to restore image quality or improve performance on downstream tasks such as depth estimation and classification. ,,− These methods typically aim to remove blur introduced by the meta-optics (MOs). In contrast, our work proposes to leverage and control this blurspecifically to suppress high-frequency details associated with privacy-sensitive features such as facial identity, while retaining low-frequency content necessary for utility tasks such as person detection. This approach is outlined in Figure , where a scene is encoded with a meta-optic, which precludes face recognition, while maintaining enough features to allow accurate person detection. While privacy is preserved and an adversary cannot decode facial features, a downstream neural network is still capable of detecting a person.

Pre-capture privacy with learned meta-optics. Our approach ensures privacy at the point of image formation by modulating the optical encoding of the imaging device. The incoming signal is filtered by a learned meta-optics layer before reaching the imaging sensor, producing a privacy-preserving image in the analog domain. This private capture obscures facial identity, thwarting potential adversaries performing face recognition, while still allowing accurate person detection through a downstream neural network in the digital domain.

To achieve privacy-aware control over the spatial frequency content in captured images, our first key contribution is a novel regularization based on a “private” Strehl integral. This formulation enforces a band-pass constraint on the modulation transfer function (MTF) of the meta-optics, effectively filtering out high-frequency details, such as those that reveal facial identity, while preserving low-frequency content essential for utility tasks like person detection. Our second contribution addresses the practical challenge of differentiable meta-optics optimization under broadband (RGB) illumination. We introduce a physics-inspired look-up table that maps nanoscatterer duty cycles and wavelengths to phase delays, enabling end-to-end gradient-based optimization. This differentiable look-up table is adapted from the phase-to-geometry calibration methods used in previous design frameworks and ensures accurate phase modeling across wavelengths.

We report a design that is evaluated both in simulation and hardware with respect to the privacy-utility trade-offs. This optimized meta-optics outperforms existing state-of-the-art optical privacy methods, including diffractive and refractive approaches. , Our results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves a 2× improvement in the privacy-utility trade-off compared to prior optical privacy approaches. Specifically, we reduce privacy leakagemeasured via face identification accuracyby 50% while incurring only 30% drop in person detection performance. In contrast, the closest previous method using DOE experiences a comparable privacy gain at the cost of a 50% drop in utility. In addition, we provide a qualitative assessment of the effectiveness of meta-optics in maintaining accurate person detection and a rigorous quantitative study that evaluates their ability to obscure facial identity through facial verification metrics. Lastly, we showcase the feasibility and practical utility of our proposed meta-optical design by presenting results captured directly from our hardware implementation. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of privacy-aware meta-optics.

Results and Discussion

Design of a Privacy-Aware Meta-Optic

Our objective is to optimize an optical encoding meta-optic for privacy-aware imaging. To achieve this goal, we aim to suppress facial identity information while preserving critical visual details for person detection tasks. To accomplish this, we propose a novel end-to-end learning framework, which optimizes the meta-optic blur with respect to the MTF. The framework is outlined in Figure a, consisting of two key components: (1) a differentiable lookup table (Figure b), which accurately models the phase response of subwavelength nanostructures, and (2) an analytical regularization objective that imposes frequency-domain constraints aligned with privacy goals.

A differentiable optics simulator generates the PSF from meta-optic parameters, which is used to convolve the input scene, forming a captured image. This image is then passed through a downstream object detection network. Our total loss combines detection loss and a frequency-domain regularization loss based on the MTF. (b) The phase response of subwavelength posts is modeled using a differentiable lookup table learned via a SIREN network trained on RCWA simulations. This enables differentiable mapping from geometry and wavelength to phase. (c) Comparison of simulated PSFs under narrowband and broadband illumination, illustrating the impact of spectral integration on PSF structure.

The sensor layer function is a convolution of a 2D image and the point spread function (PSF) of the system. Once in the digital domain, the image is passed on to a downstream neural network for inference, such as person detection or depth estimation. On the other hand, once in the digital domain, these images are susceptible to data-sniffing attacks. We optimize the sensor layer in such a way that it filters out high-frequency details that contain sensitive facial features while preserving low-frequency details like the silhouette of a person to enable person detection. A primary challenge in optimizing meta-optics arises because traditional electromagnetic simulations, such as rigorous coupled wave analysis (RCWA), are computationally intensive and inherently nondifferentiable. RCWA links physical meta-optic parameters to resulting optical phase delays. To address this, we use a differentiable lookup table implemented using implicit neural representations, enabling efficient gradient-based meta-optic design. The second challenge is to train the sensor layer for both person detection and identity obfuscation. We propose an end-to-end training approach using a person detection network guided by an MTF-based regularization methodtermed the private Strehl integralthat directly controls the optical filtering characteristics of the meta-optics, suppressing high-frequency facial details. Our complete optimization framework, illustrated in Figure , minimizes the following composite loss function 1 Ltotal=Lpersondetection+λMTFLMTF where the parameter λMTF modulates the influence of privacy-related regularization. We initially employ RCWA to generate a discrete data set that maps the dimensions of meta-optic posts (d) and incident wavelengths (λ) to corresponding optical phase delays (ϕ). Drawing inspiration from differentiable meta-optics techniques discussed in ref , we train a SIREN neural network to approximate this mapping 2

Once trained, this network serves as a continuous, differentiable lookup table that is integrated into our optimization pipeline. After training, the network parameters remain fixed and act as a lightweight, differentiable substitute for electromagnetic simulation during subsequent optimization steps. The efficacy of this approach is validated by comparing its predicted PSFs against those obtained from original RCWA simulations in Figure c. While prior works have explored optimizing meta-optics for utility tasks like imaging and depth estimation, , privacy-specific meta-optics designs remain largely unexplored. In our framework, privacy is defined as the inability of an adversary (e.g., a facial recognition network) to identify individuals from captured images. We introduce an MTF-based regularization directly within the optimization. Our proposed private Strehl integral leverages the PSF’s modulation transfer function M(f) to penalize high-frequency components beyond a predetermined cutoff frequency f cutoff while maximizing the lower-frequency signal. This integral is expressed as 3 LMTF=∫f0fcutoff(M(f)−η)⁡df−λpriv∫fcutofffmax(M(f)−η)⁡df where η denotes the noise floor of the baseline, f cutoff is the frequency threshold chosen to effectively remove details related to identity, and λpriv regulates the balance between preserving low-frequency signals to optimize person detection and suppressing high-frequency signals that compromise privacy. Through iterative designs, optimal hyper-parameters were determined as λpriv = 0.5 and λMTF = 0.5. f cutoff is chosen based on the sensitivity analysis, where the sensor layer is trained with different values and the privacy leakage is measured against a facial recognition network.

In essence, by selecting a fixed frequency threshold f cutoff and jointly optimizing the sensor layer along with the person detection network, our framework ensures optimal person detection accuracy under a specified privacy constraint defined by this frequency threshold. This co-optimization guarantees that the resulting sensor layer is ideally tailored to maximize utility while consistently enforcing the desired level of privacy.

Design Evaluation

To evaluate this design, we utilize separate data sets for person detection and privacy assessment. For utility assessment, we employ the COCO data set, while for privacy evaluation, we use VGGFace2 for facial identification and LFW for facial verification, following the protocol in ref . Face images are randomly placed at distances of 0.3 m and 1 m. Since actual face sizes in VGGFace2 and LFW are unknown, we assume a standard face size of 18.2 cm, consistent with prior studies. For person detection evaluation, we use a Faster R-CNN model with a ResNet-50-FPN backbone (V2); for privacy evaluation, we use EfficientNet-B0. All reported networks are retrained on images simulated using the final optimized meta-optic design.

All sensor types are compared at a focal length f′ = 4 mm. All, except low-resolution sensors, have a resolution of 320 × 320 pixels, a pixel pitch of 15.8 μm, and a working range of z = [0.3, 10.0] m. The defocused sensor is simulated by having an open aperture of the size 0.84 mm. Our meta-optics has the same aperture size for a fair comparison.

As illustrated in Figure a, our optimized meta-optic achieves the most favorable privacy-utility trade-off, significantly outperforming low-resolution and naively blurred sensors while surpassing diffractive optics. We attribute this advantage over diffractive optics to the independent optical kernel across spectral channels for MO, a property known as high chromaticity. Unlike diffractive optics, where all spectral channels exhibit similar phase responses, our meta-optic design introduces channel-specific variations, enhancing privacy while preserving person-identification accuracy. This phenomenon has been previously leveraged to improve depth estimation, polarization multiplexing, and for optical encoder for neural networks.

Conclusion

Privacy concerns have increased as camera deployments have become increasingly pervasive. Our approach addresses this challenge directly in the optical domain. This work presents the first privacy-aware broadband meta-optics device optimized in an end-to-end fashion to suppress identity-revealing attributes while preserving essential features for person detection. Unlike postcapture obfuscation methods, this meta-optics physically encodes privacy into the imaging pipeline, which ensures that sensitive facial details are never digitally recorded.

The key innovation in our work are a differentiable look-up table and MTF regularized privacy optimization. During optimization, we ensure that lower spatial frequency signals are maintained, while the contrast for larger spatial frequency values fall below the noise level. Thus, finer features are essentially blurred out and hard to reconstruct. At the same time, higher contrast of the MTF at lower frequency values ensures better person detection performance as compared to other privacy-preserving methods. We effectively balance person detection with facial privacy preservation.

While we successfully demonstrate this concept, several aspects warrant further exploration to extend the applicability of our approach to broader vision tasks. One limitation of our current design is the relatively narrow field of view (FOV). Unlike traditional all-in-focus or diffractive optics-based designs, meta-optics are inherently constrained by the design of their phase profiles, which are optimized for specific imaging conditions. Recent advances have demonstrated the feasibility of ultrathin, wide-field optical systems that leverage learned phase modulations. Integrating such techniques into our framework would allow for privacy-aware cameras with significantly larger FOV while maintaining accurate person detection.

Ultimately, these results highlight the potential that meta-optics hold for machine vision tasks, given their large freedom in design, and the capability to embed certain tasks such as privacy directly in the hardware component of a camera. As cameras become increasingly present in public and personal spaces, this marks an important step toward ensuring that privacy is guaranteed and protected against adversaries, even before the data is captured.

Methods

Fabrication

The meta-optic was fabricated in silicon nitride on a quartz substrate. First, a SiN layer was grown on a quartz wafer using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, yielding a SiN thin film thickness of about 800 nm. The wafer was diced and the individual chips were coated with a polymer resist (ZEP-520A). Then the meta-optic design was converted to a machine-readable lithography compatible GDS file, which was patterned into the ZEP resist using a 100 keV electron beam lithography system. After patterning, the exposed resist was developed using amyl acetate and after a short descumming step in O2 plasma, a alumina hard mask with a thickness of about 100 nm was deposited using an electron beam evaporation system. The mask was then lift-off in an NMP bath overnight. After further cleaning, the pattern was then transferred into the SiN layer using an ICP-RIE etch step with a fluorine based etch chemistry.

Imaging and Characterization

We experimentally characterize the PSF of our fabricated metalens using a broadband white light source. We then fine-tune both person detection and face recognition networks using images captured through the fabricated meta-optics. For validation, we employ a display-capture setup, displaying test images on an LED display at an imaging distance of 30 cm (>hyper-focal distance) from the prototype.
