Multicenter Real-World Outpatient Use of Intravenous Omadacycline
Authors: Lucinda J. Van Anglen, Cathy L. Koo, Kimberly A. Couch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOmadacycline is indicated for the treatment of acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections and community-acquired bacterial pneumonia. With limited real-world data, we report the clinical experience and outcomes of intravenous (IV) omadacycline in outpatient settings for treatment of any infection.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter, retrospective review of adults who received IV omadacycline for any infection between April 2019 and November 2022. Clinical data included infection type, microbiology, therapy characteristics, and adverse events. Clinical success was defined as complete or partial symptom resolution at the completion of IV omadacycline. Recurrence data at 12 months were assessed for patients with bone and joint infections (BJI).ResultsThe study included 67 patients (median age, 59 years; 56.7% male; median Charlson index, 4) from 17 infectious disease office infusion centers. Most had BJI (53.7%), followed by complicated skin and skin structure infections (29.8%), complicated intra-abdominal infections (7.5%), respiratory tract infections (7.5%), and urinary tract infections (1.5%). The most common Gram-positive pathogen was methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (14.2%), and the most common Gram-negative pathogen was Enterobacter spp. (7.5%). Nontuberculous mycobacteria were identified in nine patients. Clinical success occurred in 86.9% of evaluable patients. Non-success was due to persistent infection (6.7%), adverse events (3.3%), and resistant pathogens (1.7%). Patients with BJI had sustained clinical success at 12 months in 72.4%.ConclusionsOmadacycline was shown to be safe and effective when used as IV therapy in the outpatient setting to treat a variety of serious infections, including bone and joint infections, and mycobacterial infections.

Key Summary Points

Introduction

Omadacycline is an aminomethylcycline, derived from the tetracycline class with a broad spectrum of antimicrobial activity [1, 2]. It was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2018 for the treatment of acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections and community-acquired bacterial pneumonia [3–7]. Because it overcomes major mechanisms of tetracycline resistance via efflux pumps and ribosomal protection proteins, omadacycline demonstrates in vitro activity against Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and anaerobic bacteria, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus spp. (VRE), some extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) producing Gram-negative bacilli, and Acinetobacter baumannii [1, 7–14]. In addition, omadacycline has been shown to have activity against nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), such as Mycobacterium abscessus and M. fortuitum [15–17]. Omadacycline has a long half-life of 16 h, which allows once-daily dosing, and it is available in both intravenous (IV) and oral formulations with limited oral bioavailability at 35% [6]. As a result, IV omadacycline may be an option as outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy (OPAT) to treat polymicrobial infections, resistant organisms and NTM diseases.

To date, studies of omadacycline outside of controlled clinical trials for FDA-approved indications are limited [3–5]. A few case reports and case series reported clinical success with osteomyelitis, intra-abdominal infection, and pneumonia [18, 19]. Moreover, several observational studies have indicated favorable data for the use of omadacycline in the treatment of infections with M. abscessus [20–22]. M. abscessus is a species within the NTM family, which are difficult-to-treat due to intrinsic resistance to antimicrobial therapies and intolerance to therapy. As such, more information is needed on the use of omadacycline, where there are limited treatment options for severe M. abscessus infections. To address this gap, we present a real-world assessment of IV omadacycline as OPAT for any infection: bone and joint infection (BJI), complicated skin and skin structure infection (CSSSI), complicated intra-abdominal infection (CIAI), respiratory tract infection (RTI) and urinary tract infection (UTI) caused by all susceptible pathogens, including NTM with evaluation of clinical outcomes and safety.

Methods

We conducted a multicenter, retrospective review of all patients who received IV omadacycline between April 2019 and November 2022 in 17 infectious disease (ID) clinics providing OPAT in the US. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) who received at least one dose of IV omadacycline for any infection were identified from a centralized electronic medical record. Retrospective data collection included patient demographics (age, sex), body mass index (BMI), comorbid conditions, infection type, microbiological results, patient setting prior to omadacycline therapy and prior IV antibiotic therapies. Comorbidity burden was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index [23]. Therapy characteristics included omadacycline dosing regimen, duration of treatment, and concomitant IV antibiotics. Clinical outcomes were assessed at the end of IV omadacycline therapy by the treating physicians. Clinical success was defined as complete or partial resolution of signs and symptoms of infection with allowance for switch to oral omadacycline or other oral antibiotics. Persistent or recurrent infection, or the need for early omadacycline discontinuation, was deemed non-success. Patients who discontinued therapy prematurely due to non-clinical reasons (e.g., insurance denial, transfer of care) were classified as non-evaluable for clinical outcome. In patients with BJI, recurrence of infection within 12 months of completion of omadacycline therapy was assessed, and included all patients re-treated for the same diagnosis. Data on transitioning to oral omadacycline were captured, along with reported adverse events (AE). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables and were reported as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables.

The participating infusion centers are managed by a single organizational entity and share a centralized electronic medical record and data management system. As all data were maintained and accessed through this centralized system and no site-specific research activities were conducted, a single institutional review board approval covered all participating locations. The investigator and authors are authorized employees of this organizational entity and had organizational permission to access and analyze de-identified data for research purposes. The exempt study was conducted under our IRB approval from BRANY IRB #22–12-677–1297, and all data were handled in compliance with applicable privacy regulations.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Sixty-seven patients who received IV omadacycline were identified during the study period. Most patients were male (56.7%), and the median age of the patient cohort was 59 years (IQR 50–71) with 41.8% of patients aged 65 years or greater. The majority of patients (59.7%) were obese with a BMI of 30 kg/m2 or more. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort are displayed in Table 1. Thirty-six patients (53.7%) were treated solely in ID office infusion centers without prior hospitalization; conversely, 31 patients (46.3%) were hospitalized for a median duration of 7 days (IQR 6–11) before OPAT. The median Charlson Comorbidity Index was 4 (IQR 3–6). The most common comorbid conditions were diabetes mellitus (50.7%), cardiovascular disease (37.3%), chronic kidney disease (17.9%), malignancy (16.4%), and immunocompromised status (14.9%). The most prevalent infection was BJI in 53.7% of patients (n = 36), followed by CSSSI in 29.8% (n = 20), CIAI (n = 5) and RTI (n = 5) in 7.5%, respectively, and UTI in 1.5% (n = 1). The predominant infection among patients with BJI was osteomyelitis in 88.9% of patients (n = 32), followed by prosthetic joint infection in 11.1% (n = 4). The RTI cohort consisted of patients with pneumonia (n = 4) and chronic sinusitis (n = 1). The entire cohort was heavily pretreated with IV antibiotics, with 73.1% receiving additional IV antibiotics prior to omadacycline.

Microbiology

A total of 106 pathogens were identified in 55 patients, including Gram-positive pathogens in 52.8% of isolates, Gram-negative pathogens in 30.2%, and anaerobic pathogens in 8.5% (Table 2). Overall, the most common Gram-positive pathogen was MRSA (14.2%), followed by Enterococcus spp. (13.2%). The most common Gram-negative pathogens were Enterobacter spp. (7.5%), Escherichia coli (4.7%), Klebsiella spp. (4.7%), Acinetobacter spp. (4.7%), and Stenotrophomonas spp. (4.7%). Anaerobes were isolated in 8.5% of isolates, primarily in BJI. Three multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacteria were identified: carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella aerogenes (n = 1) in the BJI group, and MDR A. baumannii (n = 2) in the CSSSI group. Mycobacterium spp. were identified in nine patients. Cultures isolated from skin abscesses in five patients yielded M. abscessus (n = 2), M. fortuitum (n = 1), Mycobacterium spp. (n = 2). Respiratory tract cultures from pulmonary infections returned with M. abscessus (n = 3) and from BJI generated M. fortuitum (n = 1). Thirty-six patients (53.7%) had two or more pathogens identified and 21 patients (31.3%) had mixed Gram-positive and Gram-negative infections, including 11 BJI, seven CSSSI, two CIAI, and one RTI patient. Testing for omadacycline susceptibility was not performed except on one isolate of M. fortuitum, which reported a MIC of 0.12 mcg/ml by broth microdilution, performed at an academic reference laboratory. Therapy Characteristics

The median duration of omadacycline treatment was 35 days (IQR 16–48). Overall, 73.1% (n = 49) received IV antimicrobials prior to omadacycline with a median duration of 18 days (IQR 6–30). Concomitant therapy was given in 19.4% (n = 13). Six patients were transitioned to oral omadacycline. The therapy and utilization details by infection are noted in Table 3. The longest duration of therapy was in patients with RTI at 47 days (IQR 15–60), primarily driven by NTM infections. The median duration of treatment for NTM infections was 60 days (IQR 47–209). Loading doses of 200 mg were given in 57 patients (85.1%) with maintenance doses of 100 mg IV every 24 h in 65 patients (97.0%). One patient was transitioned to oral omadacycline after the loading dose and the other experienced rash and pruritus following the loading dose, resulting in treatment discontinuation. The maintenance dose was reduced to 100 mg every 48 h in one patient with mycobacterial pulmonary infection to manage persistent nausea and headache. Omadacycline was dispensed in elastomeric devices in 35 patients (52.2%) for self-administration at home. The remaining 32 patients (47.8%) received therapy in office infusion centers. Thirteen patients (19.4%) received at least one concomitant IV antibiotic (six in BJI, six in CSSSI, and one in RTI) with the most common being amikacin (n = 5), imipenem/cilastatin (n = 4), cefoxitin (n = 3) and cefepime (n = 3). Of those with concomitant IV therapy, seven had mycobacterial infections, three had polymicrobial infections with intrinsically resistant pathogens (two Pseudomonas aeruginosa and one Morganella morganii), one had vertebral osteomyelitis that failed three prior courses of broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, one had A. baumannii, and one had Stenotrophomonas maltophilia. There were 21 patients in the cohort with mixed Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, of which 19 (90.5%) received monotherapy with IV omadacycline. One patient developed resistance to tetracycline during therapy with a change in therapy. Clinical Outcomes

Among 67 patients, 53 (79.1%) were classified as clinical success, eight (11.9%) as non-success, and six (9.0%) as non-evaluable. In the 61 evaluable patients, the clinical success rate was 86.9% and the non-success rate was 13.1%. Outcomes of both cohorts are shown by infection type in Fig. 1. Clinical success rate of the evaluable cohort was 90.9% in BJI (n = 30), 77.8% in CSSSI (n = 14), 100.0% in CIAI (n = 5), 75.0% in RTI (n = 3), and 100.0% in UTI (n = 1), which was a patient with pyelonephritis. Non-success in the evaluable cohort was observed in eight patients (13.1%) due to persistent infection (BJI, n = 3 and CSSSI, n = 2), treatment discontinuation for AEs (CSSSI, n = 2), and treatment discontinuation for antimicrobial resistance (RTI, n = 1). Patients with BJI with persistent infection all had a history of hypertension and MRSA chronic osteomyelitis. Two patients had diabetes mellitus, and one patient had a history of intravenous drug use. Patients with CSSSI with persistent infection had a history of diabetes mellitus and chronic wound infections due to S. maltophilia infection (n = 1) and a polymicrobial infection caused by coagulase-negative staphylococci, E. faecalis, Klebsiella oxytoca, and Enterobacter cloacae (n = 1). Two patients had an AE leading to drug discontinuation, one with rash and pruritus, and one with an infusion reaction. The patient with RTI with refractory chronic sinusitis discontinued omadacycline after a follow-up culture identified K. aerogenes and Staphylococcus pseudintermedius.

Six patients were non-evaluable for outcomes due to non-clinical reasons. Two were hospitalized for reasons unrelated to omadacycline treatment, two were transferred to other sites of care (one to home infusion for convenience and one to a skilled nursing facility for additional care). One patient had therapy denied by insurance and one had difficulty managing the infusion device.

Of the 30 patients with BJI who had initial clinical success, recurrence of infection was assessed in 29 patients who had follow-up data. At 12 months, 72.4% of patients (n = 21) had no recurrence of infection. The eight patients with recurrent osteomyelitis had a median time to recurrence of 67 days (IQR 58–96). Complicating factors in five of the eight patients with recurrent osteomyelitis infection included concurrent diabetic foot infection (n = 3), chronic osteomyelitis due to underlying bone and tendon defect with multiple grafts and surgeries (n = 1), and chronic mastoiditis with A. baumannii (n = 1). The provider assessment of recurrence was made by clinical judgment in five patients and by culture data in three of the eight patients.

Of the patients with unsuccessful outcomes, three patients with osteomyelitis and two patients with cellulitis developed worsening infections with discontinuation of omadacycline. Two patients with cellulitis discontinued omadacycline due to adverse events, and one patient with sinusitis discontinued due to antibiotic resistance.

Overall, 54 patients (80.6%) received omadacycline as monotherapy, of which 42 (62.7%) had clinical success, seven (10.4%) had non-success and five (7.5%) were non-evaluable. Of the entire cohort group with non-successful outcomes, 87.5% (seven of eight patients) received monotherapy.

Upon evaluation of outcomes by pathogen, clinical success was observed in 89.5% (17 of 19) of patients with evaluable outcomes who had mixed Gram-positive and Gram-negative infections. Furthermore, clinical success rate was 88.2% (15 of 17) in patients with omadacycline monotherapy for the mixed Gram-positive and Gram-negative infections. Two patients with mixed Gram-positive and Gram-negative infections were treated with concomitant IV antibiotics and both patients had clinical success. The three patients with MDR Gram-negative infections all had successful outcomes. Among seven patients with NTM infections caused by M. abscessus and M. fortuitum, 87.5% with pulmonary (n = 2) and extrapulmonary (CSSSI, n = 4; osteomyelitis, n = 1) infections had clinical success, including the patient with M. fortuitum with the omadacycline MIC of 0.12 mcg/ml. Non-success occurred in one CSSSI patient with NTM who experienced an infusion reaction resulting in treatment discontinuation.

Safety

A total of 33 AEs were reported in 20 patients (29.8%) (Table 4). The most prevalent AE reported was gastrointestinal AEs occurring in 9 patients (13.4%), of which 6 patients had nausea. All patients continued the therapy with one requiring dosing interval adjustment from every 24 h to every 48 h. Hepatic enzyme elevations were the next most frequent AE, reported by eight patients (11.9%). The elevations were mostly mild with alkaline phosphatase (ALP) elevation less than 3 times the upper limit of normal (ULN) occurring most frequently (n = 5). Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and ALP were elevated greater than three times ULN in two patients. Four patients with underlying chronic kidney disease had adverse events, including one with hepatic enzyme elevation, one with fatigue, one with lower extremity edema and one with elevated blood pressure who also had hypertension. None required discontinuation. One patient with a NTM CSSSI had an increase in AST of 8.7 times ULN, ALT of 5.7 times ULN and ALP of 4.3 times ULN but was able to remain on therapy with no sequelae. Three patients with osteomyelitis had elevation of serum creatinine (all < 50% above baseline) but none required discontinuation of omadacycline. These patients were not receiving concomitant aminoglycoside therapy but had multiple comorbidities (median Charlson Comorbidity Index of 4), including cardiovascular disease, diabetes and cancer with chemotherapy. Adverse events led to treatment discontinuation in two patients. One experienced rash and pruritus within 10 min of infusion with immediate discontinuation of omadacycline. The second patient with the infusion reaction developed chest pain and shortness of breath during the first infusion. Omadacycline was discontinued and the patient was treated at the emergency department with no sequelae. No patients reported Clostridioides difficile infections. Intravenous catheter complications occurred in three patients including dislodgement (n = 2) and catheter-related infection (n = 1). Discussion

These retrospective study results present real-world data on outpatient use of IV omadacycline therapy for the treatment of complex, difficult-to-treat MDR organisms causing osteomyelitis, prosthetic joint infection, complicated skin and skin structure infections, intra-abdominal infection, pneumonia and pyelonephritis. A substantial proportion of patients were 65 years or older with significant comorbidities. Most patients had chronic, recurrent infections and over 30% had mixed polymicrobial Gram-positive and Gram-negative infections, with 90.5% of these receiving omadacycline as monotherapy. Despite these factors, a high clinical success rate of 86.9% was achieved in the evaluable cohort with consistent results in patients with NTM infections due to M. abscessus and M. fortuitum, as well as in patients treated with omadacycline monotherapy for mixed Gram-positive and Gram-negative infections. The rates of clinical success for the different types of infections varied, with limited or no historical data on omadacycline use in those infections.

This is the largest cohort of patients with BJI treated with IV omadacycline with an initial success rate of 90.9% in evaluable patients, sustained at 72.4% at 12 months, which is comparable to other outpatient therapies in the treatment of BJI [24–26]. These findings support studies showing excellent bone tissue penetration of omadacycline in animal models [27, 28] and are consistent with a study demonstrating clinical success in patients treated with omadacycline for polymicrobial and resistant Gram-negative osteomyelitis [19]. In our patients with CSSSI, the clinical success rate was 77.8%, similar to the response rates reported in the clinical trials, even though our cohort included patients with complicated chronic infections [3, 4]. The study cohort with CIAI was small but all patients completed omadacycline therapy successfully. Of four RTI patients with evaluable outcomes, including three with pneumonia and one with chronic sinusitis, 75.0% achieved successful outcomes. Finally, one patient with UTI due to pyelonephritis was treated with omadacycline, resulting in a successful outcome.

Notably, nine patients received omadacycline for pulmonary and extrapulmonary mycobacterial infections caused by M. abscessus and M. fortuitum. Nontuberculous mycobacterial infections, which are notoriously recalcitrant, often require a multidrug regimen for an extended duration [29]. All nine patients previously received IV antibiotics, with the addition of omadacycline to the combination therapy or substitution of omadacycline for a current portion of the regimen due to intolerance or treatment failure. Clinical success was achieved in 87.5% of these patients. These results are consistent with the accumulating evidence on the effectiveness of omadacycline in the treatment of M. abscessus and other NTM [20–22]. An ongoing clinical trial of omadacycline in adults with NTM infections caused by M. abscessus will provide comparative data to further define the role of omadacycline [30].

The overall safety profile of omadacycline in this study was similar to that reported in the clinical trials with the most common adverse events being gastrointestinal symptoms [3–5, 31]. Although nausea was a prominent AE, only one patient required dosing interval modification, and none required treatment discontinuation. Hepatic enzyme elevations were mostly mild, and no patients required treatment discontinuation. Interestingly, omadacycline has demonstrated in vitro activity against C. difficile [32, 33]. In this study, no patients developed C. difficile infection during treatment, similar to the results from clinical trials [3–5].

All patients in this study initiated omadacycline as IV therapy. Of these, 73.1% switched from another IV antibiotic, allowing for rapid transition to IV omadacycline in patients with established vascular access. Additionally, 19.4% were also receiving concomitant IV antibiotics. Most patients completed therapy with IV omadacycline but almost one-third transitioned to oral therapy, with six patients transitioned to oral omadacycline. Initial use of IV omadacycline was chosen for treatment of serious infections and provides advantages in tolerance over the oral formulation, with rapid distribution and elimination of the need for fasting [21]. The IV omadacycline offered financial access for patients in federally funded programs through OPAT. A study of claims data showed barriers in obtaining oral omadacycline with 36% of prescriptions not approved by insurance, while 64% were approved that were administered intravenously [34]. Lastly, administration of IV omadacycline through an office infusion center with strict patient compliance and monitoring may lead to enhanced treatment success and safety.

Limitations

Limitations of this study include its retrospective, observational design and the absence of a comparator antimicrobial agent. Consequently, the impact of severity of infection, surgical interventions, and other potential confounders on treatment response could not be assessed. In addition, as is inherent to observational studies, data was dependent upon accurate and complete documentation in the electronic medical record across multiple infectious diseases offices. Mitigating this dependence, all patients were managed with OPAT under standard protocols and documentation by centrally trained nurses and pharmacists and under consistent oversight by the infectious disease physicians.

Outcome assessment was limited to the completion of omadacycline therapy, with the exception of BJI, and was based upon provider assessment. Susceptibility testing was not routinely performed for most isolates, particularly in the absence of established Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute breakpoints. Additionally, sustained clinical success rates could potentially be lower if follow-up data were available for non-BJI patients.

Conclusions

This multicenter real-world study supports intravenous omadacycline as a safe, effective, and feasible OPAT option for complex infections, including bone and joint and nontuberculous mycobacterial infections. High clinical success rates were observed despite substantial comorbidity, prior antibiotic exposure, and polymicrobial disease, with favorable outcomes frequently achieved using monotherapy. These findings extend the evidence base beyond approved indications and suggest a role for intravenous omadacycline in selected patients with limited treatment options.
