Spatial autoregressive quantile regression modeling of gross regional domestic product data in Java Island
Authors: Sulvirah Rahmi, Nurtiti Sunusi, Nirwan Ilyas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study analyzes the Gross Regional Domestic Product (GRDP) data of Java Island to understand the region’s economic conditions and the key factors influencing GRDP. Java Island contributes the largest share to the national GRDP; however, the data show the presence of spatial dependence, spatial heterogeneity, and outliers. To address these issues, this study employs the Spatial Autoregressive Quantile Regression (SARQR) model. The findings show that the number of workers significantly influences GRDP across all quantiles, whereas Local Own-Source Revenue (LOSR), Regency/City Minimum Wage (RCMW), and the Human Development Index (HDI) only impact specific quantiles. The SARQR model demonstrates the best performance at the 0.6 quantile, with a Quantile Verification Skill Score (QVSS) of 0.9052, indicating strong explanatory power.• The model was estimated using the Instrumental Variable Quantile Regression (IVQR) method.• Variable significance at each quantile was tested using the Wald test.• The best model was selected based on the QVSS value.

Background

Gross Regional Domestic Product (GRDP) is a key metric used to analyze the economic condition of a region over a specific time period [1]. However, the diversity in GRDP data can lead to the presence of outliers, which often cause issues such as increased error variance and heteroscedasticity. In 1978, [2] introduced the quantile regression method to address these heteroscedasticity problems. Quantile regression is effective when applied to data with non-homogeneous and asymmetric distributions. Additionally, it remains robust in the presence of outliers, helping maintain data stability [3]. This study uses spatial units of observation, specifically regencies and municipalities across Java Island. Based on Statistics Indonesia data in 2024, Java Island has the largest GRDP contribution in Indonesia. Exploratory analysis of the GRDP data shows evidence of spatial dependence and heterogeneity. When such spatial effects exist—meaning a region is influenced by its neighbors—spatial regression is the appropriate method. Spatial regression is a statistical technique used to explore the relationship between dependent and independent variables while accounting for spatial dependencies [4]. In this context, spatial effects refer to spatial dependence and spatial heterogeneity [5]. Spatial dependence arises when one region’s condition affects another, while spatial heterogeneity refers to varying relationships between regions [6].

To handle both effects, [7] and [2] suggest the Spatial Autoregressive Quantile Regression (SARQR) model, which combines the Spatial Autoregressive (SAR) model with quantile regression (QR). According to [7], SARQR parameters can be estimated using Two-Stage Quantile Regression (2SQR) or Instrumental Variable Quantile Regression (IVQR). The 2SQR method estimates spatial quantiles in two stages and performs well on spatial data with asymmetric distributions [8]. Prior studies using SARQR with 2SQR include analyses of flooding and housing prices [9], urban blue spaces [10], and accident rates [11]. The IVQR method, meanwhile, addresses endogeneity—when an independent variable correlates with the error term [12,13]—by using instrumental variables to yield unbiased estimates [14]. Studies using IVQR within SARQR include research on HDI in East Java [15] and unemployment in West Java [16]. This research can serve as a basis for formulating more focused and data-driven economic policies, particularly in development planning that takes into account differences in GRDP distribution across regions in Java. Exploratory analysis of Java Island’s GRDP data reveals signs of endogeneity and spatial effects. Thus, this study applies the SARQR model with IVQR to provide more accurate and representative parameter estimates.

Spatial autoregressive quantile regression (SARQR)

The Spatial Autoregressive Quantile Regression (SARQR) model not only addresses the issue of heteroscedasticity but is also effective in handling spatial dependence [7]. This method examines the influence of a particular region (or data unit) as affected by its neighboring regions, while taking into account variations in influence across different quantiles of the data distribution. Imagine we want to determine whether house prices in a certain area are high because they are influenced by the high prices of houses in neighboring areas. Furthermore, we want to understand whether this effect differs for houses with low, medium, or very high prices. The SARQR model can be expressed in the following equation:(8)Y=ρ(τ)WY+Xβ(τ)+ɛ(τ)

In this context, Y represents the predicted value, for example, house prices. The term WY indicates the weighted average value of neighboring regions or data units (spatial lag), while ρ(τ) describes the strength of the influence from neighboring values at a specific quantile (τ). The component Xβ(τ)) explains the effect of local factors, such as house size or location, at a given quantile. Meanwhile, ɛ(τ) represents random factors or unobserved variables.

In the SARQR model equation that involves endogeneity, the parameters of the model are determined through the Instrumental Variable Quantile Regression (IVQR) approach. The SARQR modeling with the IVQR method involves instrumental variables in parameter estimation, namely the variable Z [12]. Spatial outliers

According to [22], a point in Moran's scatterplot is classified as a spatial outlier if it is detected to be significantly different from the values of its neighbors, whether low or high. This indicates that a region with low observation values is surrounded by regions with high observation values, or vice versa.

Based on the Moran’s scatterplot in Fig. 1, the distribution of GRDP (Gross Regional Domestic Product) values shows a significant spatial pattern. Regions in Quadrant I (such as Central Jakarta, North Jakarta, East Jakarta, South Jakarta, Bekasi, and Surabaya) form a positive cluster with high GRDP values, meaning these areas are surrounded by regions with similarly high GRDP values. Meanwhile, regions in Quadrant III form a negative cluster with low GRDP values, indicating areas with low GRDP that are also surrounded by regions with similar low values. Additionally, points in Quadrants II and IV represent spatial outliers, areas that have significantly different GRDP values compared to their surrounding regions. For example, Tangerang, Bekasi, and Bandung fall into this category, indicating that these regions have high local GRDP values but are situated in areas with low GRDP.Fig. 1Moran’s Scatterplot of GRDP, LOSR, NW, HDI, and RCMW Values.Fig. 1

Parameter estimation of the SARQR model

In estimating the parameters of the SARQR model, the Instrumental Variable Quantile Regression (IVQR) method is used. According to [14], the IVQR method can effectively address the issue of endogeneity when estimating parameters in the SARQR model. To detect the presence of endogeneity within the model, a Residual Test can be applied. The resulting p-value from this test is 2,2×10−16. Since the p-value is less than the significance level (α=0.05), this indicates the presence of endogeneity in the regression model. In other words, the regression error term is correlated with the independent variables. Therefore, the use of IVQR is appropriate, as it helps produce unbiased and consistent parameter estimates even in the presence of endogenous explanatory variables. Conclusions

In modeling the Gross Regional Domestic Product (GRDP) data of Java Island for 2023, the IVQR method was applied to estimate the parameters of the SARQR model across various quantiles of the GRDP distribution. The results indicate that the Number of Workers (NW) has a significant effect on GRDP across all quantiles, whereas Local Own-Source Revenue (LOSR), Regency/City Minimum Wage (RCMW), and the Human Development Index (HDI) only have an effect at certain quantiles. The SARQR model exhibited optimal performance at the τ = 0.6 quantile, with a Quantile Verification Skill Score (QVSS) of 0.9052, indicating that the model is capable of explaining 90.52 % of the variation in GRDP. This finding is consistent with the research conducted by [17], which states that, overall, the magnitude of GRDP in a regency/city in Indonesia is influenced by HDI, RCMW, NW, and LOSR.

These findings emphasize that the influence of independent variables on GRDP is dynamic and depends on the quantile level of the distribution. This underscores a strong relevance to regional economic policymaking, particularly in determining intervention priorities. The results also show that NW has a consistently significant influence across all quantiles, suggesting that enhancing workforce capacity and productivity is a fundamental requirement across the entire spectrum of economic regions, without exception. Therefore, workforce development policies should be a primary agenda in regional economic development throughout Java. Meanwhile, interventions related to HDI, LOSR, and RCMW should be specifically targeted at groups of regions within certain quantiles where these variables are shown to have significant effects.

Furthermore, in terms of targeted spatial strategies, these findings open up opportunities to identify spatial clusters based on the economic characteristics of each region. For instance, metropolitan areas such as Jakarta, which have high GRDP levels, are recommended to prioritize development in variables such as the NW, RCMW, and HDI. This aligns with the estimation results showing that these three variables have a significant influence in the upper quantiles of the GRDP distribution, which generally represent regions with greater economic capacity. Meanwhile, for regions with low GRDP in Java, such as several regencies in the central and eastern parts of the island, policy interventions are recommended to focus on the development of NW. This is because NW has been shown to have a consistently significant effect across all GRDP distribution quantiles, including the lower quantiles, indicating that improving workforce capacity can be a key factor in driving economic growth in areas with fiscal and infrastructural constraints.

Therefore, economic policies cannot be uniform but must be adapted to the characteristics of each quantile and region. The findings of this study can serve as a foundation for formulating more focused and data-driven economic policies, particularly in development planning that takes into account differences in GRDP distribution across regions in Java. Future research can be extended to areas outside Java, such as Sumatra, Kalimantan, or the whole of Indonesia, to provide a more comprehensive spatial perspective on GRDP distribution and interregional relationships.
