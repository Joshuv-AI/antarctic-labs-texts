Objective Neurologic and Sedation Monitoring in The ICU: A Comparative Evaluation of Automated Blink-rate Analytics And Quantitative Pupillometry
Authors: Cyriac G Sebastian, Sandra Monis, Mathangi Krishnakumar, Natasha O Vaz, Moses C Dsouza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionObjective assessment of neurologic responsiveness, pain, and sedation depth in critically ill patients remains limited by reliance on subjective bedside tools such as the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS), and Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Technological monitoring approaches may enhance accuracy and reduce observer variability. Two modalities of interest include automated blink-rate analytics, a contact-free, video-based system enabling continuous physiologic monitoring, and quantitative pupillometry, a validated handheld method generating neuro-physiologic parameters including the Quantitative Pupillometry Index (QPI). A structured comparison of these modalities in the ICU has not previously been reported.ObjectivesTo compare automated blink-rate analytics and quantitative pupillometry as objective indicators of neurologic status and sedation depth, and to evaluate their correlation with established ICU clinical scales.Materials and methodsThis prospective observational study is being conducted in the Surgical and Neuro Intensive Care Unit of a tertiary academic center and is structured into two phases. Phase 1 – Feasibility and Systems Validation (Completed; n = 6). Paired blink-rate and pupillometry measurements were collected to evaluate: Feasibility of continuous automated blink-rate acquisition using a video-analytics platform; signal reliability, calibration protocols, and artifact management; integration of the technology into routine ICU workflows; temporal alignment of physiologic data with GCS, RASS, and VAS scoring. Phase 2 – Comparative Correlation Study (Target n = 50). Fifty adult ICU patients are being enrolled. For each assessment window, the following are obtained: Blink-rate analytics captured over standardized 1–2 minute epochs using frontal-camera video recording in controlled lighting conditions; Quantitative pupillometry including baseline pupil diameter, constriction metrics, variability, reactivity, and QPI; Concurrent clinical assessment using GCS, RASS, and VAS (when applicable). Primary analyses include correlations between blink rate and pupillometry metrics, and between each modality and clinical scales. Secondary analyses explore physiologic sensitivity, measurement reliability, and concordance patterns across sedation depth and neurologic injury.ResultsFeasibility-phase findings (n = 6) demonstrated reliable data acquisition and physiologic consistency. Higher blink rates (21–42/min) corresponded with higher GCS scores (E4M6VT) and lighter sedation (RASS –1 to 0), while absent or reduced blinking was associated with deep sedation (RASS –4). Pupillometry showed higher QPI and preserved reactivity in patients with intact neurologic function, whereas reduced reactivity and variability indicated neurologic impairment or deeper sedation. Blink rate more strongly reflected behavioral and nociceptive responsiveness, while pupillometry aligned with intrinsic neuro-physiologic reactivity. These findings support progression into the 50-patient comparative phase.ConclusionAutomated blink-rate analytics and quantitative pupillometry are feasible, reliable, and complementary modalities for objective neuromonitoring in the ICU. Preliminary data show that blink rate captures dynamic behavioral responsiveness, whereas pupillometry reflects underlying neuro-physiologic function. The 50-patient dataset will provide a robust comparative evaluation to inform integration of multimodal ocular monitoring into critical care practice.Keywords: Blink-rate analytics, Quantitative pupillometry, Neuromonitoring, ICU sedation, Objective physiologic monitoring
