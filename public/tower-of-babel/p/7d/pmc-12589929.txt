Maternal Immune Stimulation With Interferon‐Gamma Limits Embryonic Valproic Acid Induced Apoptotic Gene Expression
Authors: Daniel Frascella, Murali K Mallela, Robert Cullen, Terry C Hrubec
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNeural tube defects (NTDs) are developmental malformations affecting 1300 newborns in the United States each year. Valproic acid (VA), a drug to treat seizures and mood disorders, can cause NTDs. Apoptosis is increased in the developing neural tubes (NTs) of embryos exposed to VA thereby identifying a possible mechanism for NTD formation. Numerous studies show that maternal immune stimulation (MIS) in the periconceptual period reduces birth defects including VA‐induced NTDs. It is hypothesized that immunoregulatory cytokines may normalize the dysregulated apoptosis. This study examined maternal cytokine production and embryonic apoptotic gene expression following prebreeding stimulation with interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ) and gestational VA exposure.MethodsConcentrations of granulocyte monocyte colony stimulating factor (GM‐CSF), IFN‐γ, interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), interleukin‐10 (IL‐10), and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNFα) were determined in VA + IFN‐γ‐treated pregnant mice. Expression of pro‐apoptotic and antiapoptotic pathway genes was determined in VA and VA + IFN‐γ exposed embryos.ResultsNTDs decreased from 45.3% to 21.2% per litter with MIS. MIS altered the profiles of GM‐CSF and IFN‐γ throughout pregnancy and altered the profiles of GM‐CSF, IL‐6, and IL‐10 over the time of NT closure. Pro‐apoptotic genes were upregulated, and anti‐apoptotic genes were downregulated in VA‐exposed embryonic heads with open or closed NTs. Gene expression was normalized with VA + IFN‐γ in embryonic heads with closed NTs.ConclusionThis study identified GM‐CSF and IFN‐γ as possible effectors of MIS and specifically GM‐CSF, IL‐6, and IL‐10 in preventing NTDs. MIS normalized apoptotic gene expression in embryos with closed NTs identifying a possible molecular mechanism for the prevention of VA‐induced NTDs.Keywords: apoptosis, cytokines, interferon‐gamma, maternal immune activation, maternal immune stimulation, neural tube defects, neural tube development, valproic acid

Introduction

Neural tube defects (NTDs) are frequent and disabling fetal malformations resulting in anencephaly cranially and spina bifida caudally. Approximately 1300 newborns in the United States are affected each year with a major NTD (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2025). The drug valproic acid (VA) is used to treat epilepsy and mood disorders but is teratogenic. Administration of VA during early organogenesis can induce fetal malformations including craniofacial, urogenital, and skeletal anomalies in addition to NTDs in animals and humans (Dieterich et al. 1980; Nau et al. 1991; Clayton‐Smith and Donnai 1995). The mouse model of VA‐induced NTDs is well characterized and has been used for many years as a research tool to study the causes of human NTDs both in general and specific to VA.

Apoptosis, or programmed cell death, is an essential component of normal fetal development. Apoptosis plays a major role in organogenesis, tissue remodeling and maintenance of tissue architecture during development (Shi et al. 1998; Meier et al. 2000). Tissue remodeling by apoptosis is a tightly controlled process. Studies have suggested that teratogenic deregulation of apoptosis during development may be responsible for birth defect formation including NTDs (Mirkes and Little 1998; Jiang et al. 2008; Oyama et al. 2009; Singh et al. 2010). Additionally, altered apoptosis in several knockout mouse strains resulted in NT closure defects (Hsu et al. 2008; Shirane et al. 2008; Wong et al. 2008; Barrier et al. 2009). Previously we showed by both flow cytometry and TUNEL assay that apoptosis is increased in embryos from VA‐exposed dams. The increase in apoptosis was localized to the midline along the site of NT closure suggesting apoptotic involvement in VA teratogenicity (Mallela and Hrubec 2012).

Peri‐conceptual stimulation of the maternal immune system in mice can prevent a wide variety of teratogen‐induced birth defects from a variety of sources including chemical agents, hyperthermia, x‐rays, and diabetes mellitus (Nomura et al. 1990; Holladay et al. 2000; Holladay et al. 2002; Hrubec, Prater, Punareewattana, et al. 2005; Hrubec, Prater, et al. 2006; Hrubec, Yan, et al. 2006; Hrubec et al. 2009; Hrubec et al. 2012). Maternal immune stimulation (MIS) reduced limb defects (Prater et al. 2004), tail malformations, cleft palate (Sharova et al. 2002; Hrubec et al. 2009), heart malformations (Gutierrez et al. 2009), craniofacial defects (Hrubec, Prater, et al. 2006), and NTDs (Torchinsky et al. 1997; Punareewattana et al. 2003; Punareewattana and Holladay 2004; Hrubec, Yan, et al. 2006; Mallela and Hrubec 2012). The protection conferred by MIS appears to be independent of the manner of stimulation. Diverse methods including intraperitoneal (ip) injection of inert particles, intrauterine injection of xenogenic lymphocytes and, intrauterine or ip injection of immunostimulatory cytokines have all been effective in reducing birth defects. The operating mechanisms by which MIS reduces fetal dysmorphogenesis are unknown; however, the collective literature suggests that immunoregulatory cytokines of maternal or placental origin may be effector molecules that normalize dysregulated apoptosis or timing of cell proliferation in the fetus (Sharova et al. 2000; Punareewattana and Holladay 2004; Hrubec et al. 2012; Mallela and Hrubec 2012).

Previously, we showed that apoptotic levels in embryos with closed NTs from immune stimulated VA exposed dams were similar to controls inferring that normalization of apoptosis may be involved in protection. The objective of this investigation was to examine the effects of MIS on immunoregulatory cytokines in the mother and the role of apoptosis in the developing embryo. Maternal immunoregulatory cytokines were determined throughout pregnancy in the dam to identify potential effector cytokines that were altered by MIS. Emphasis was placed over the time of NT closure to identify ones involved in preventing NTDs. Apoptotic pathway genes were identified in gestational day (GD) 8.5 and 9.5 embryos of mice treated with VA or VA + IFN‐γ to identify possible apoptotic mechanisms responsible for the prevention of NTDs. This study identified maternal cytokines and embryonic apoptotic pathways that may play a role in preventing VA‐induced NTDs.

Materials and Methods

Animal Husbandry

All procedures related to animal use were approved by and conducted in compliance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the College of Veterinary Medicine at Virginia Tech, an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC) accredited facility. CD‐1 mice were raised in conventional cages with a 12‐h day length, 70°F–73°F, and 50% ± 20% humidity. Mice were fed Rodent Diet 18‐6 (Zeigler, Gardners, PA) and distilled water ad libitum. For breeding, two or three females were paired with a male provided with the same experimental conditions for one heat cycle. The females were checked for copulation plugs every morning. When a copulation plug was found, the female was separated, and the midpoint of the preceding dark cycle was designated as GD zero.

Animal Treatment Groups

For both maternal cytokine determination and apoptotic gene expression, female mice were randomly divided into 4 treatment groups: saline control, IFN‐γ only, VA only, and VA + IFN‐γ. For the IFN‐γ only and VA + IFN‐γ groups, 1000 IU of IFN‐γ (Peprotech, Rocky Hill, NJ) was administered prebreeding by ip injection on Day 10 and again on Day 5 before pairing with a male. Saline controls and VA only mice received an equal volume of phosphate buffer saline (PBS) by ip injection on Days 10 and 5 before pairing with a male. For the VA and VA + IFN‐γ groups, 500 mg/kg body wt. VA (Sigma Aldrich, St Louis MO) was administered on GD 8.0 by ip injection. The saline and IFN‐γ only groups received an equal volume of PBS by ip injection on GD 8.0. For maternal cytokine analysis, six pregnant dams were analyzed for each treatment at each time point throughout gestation. For apoptotic gene expression, embryos from three litters were analyzed for each treatment on GD 8.5 and 9.5.

Maternal Cytokine Determination

To determine the maternal cytokine response to MIS with IFN‐γ the TH1 (TNFα, IFN‐γ) and TH2 (IL‐6, IL‐10, GM‐CSF) cytokine concentrations were determined in pregnant females on GD 0.5, 3.5, 6.5, 8.5, 9.5, 10.5, 12.5, 15.5, and 18.5 for the saline and IFN‐γ only groups. To determine cytokines that may confer protection against NTDs, the cytokines listed above were determined in the VA only and VA + IFN‐γ groups on GD 8.5, 9.5, and 10.5 as this spans the time of NT closure and is when VA is teratogenic. Pregnant mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and blood was collected from the heart into pediatric serum tubes. Blood clotted for 1 h before centrifugation and collection of serum. After blood collection, mice were euthanized by an overdose of anesthetic. Serum was collected and stored at −30°C until cytokine determination with murine specific ELISA assays (R&D Systems Inc. Minneapolis, MN) following manufacturer's instructions.

RNA Isolation

Pregnant mice were euthanized on GD 8.5 and 9.5 by an overdose of CO2 and embryos were isolated under sterile conditions in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM). In CD‐1 mice, the NT is still open at GD 8.5 whereas at GD 9.5 the NT has closed allowing for evaluation of NTDs (Mallela and Hrubec 2012). NT closure was evaluated under a stereozoom microscope (Olympus SZX7, Melville, NY) equipped with a CFW 1310 Scion camera (Scion Corporation, Frederick, MD).

Embryonic heads were removed sterilely on GD 8.5 or 9.5 as described previously (Mallela and Hrubec 2012) and were stored in RNAlater (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) until RNA extraction. GD 9.5 embryos from each dam were divided into two pools based on whether they had open or closed NT as described previously (Mallela and Hrubec 2012). Pooling embryos from the same litter (keeping embryos with open and closed NT separate) was necessary, since the amount of RNA from one embryo was not sufficient for the RT2 Profiler PCR Arrays experiments. RNA was isolated separately from each pool using RNeasy Mini, RNA isolation kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was prepared from 1 μg total RNA of each pooled sample by using a RT2 PCR array first strand kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Real Time PCR for Gene Expression

Real time PCR was performed using the Mouse Apoptosis RT2 Profiler PCR array (Qiagen, Valencia, CA). The expressions of 84 genes associated with apoptosis were analyzed according to the manufacturer's instructions. PCR amplification was conducted using the Bio‐Rad iCYCLER iQ5 Real Time PCR instrument with an initial 10‐min step at 95°C (required to activate the HotStart DNA polymerase) followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min (denaturation and annealing steps). The fluorescent signal from SYBR Green (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA) was detected immediately after the extension step of each cycle, and the cycle at which the product was first detectable was recorded as the cycle threshold.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of maternal cytokine concentrations was conducted in Statistix (Tallahassee, FL). A two‐sample t test was used to determine differences in cytokine concentrations between saline and IFN‐γ only groups on the different days of gestation. A two‐way ANOVA was used to determine differences in cytokine concentrations between VA and VA + IFN‐γ over GDs 8.5, 9.5, and 10.5. If the 2‐way ANOVA indicated no interaction between treatment and day, a 1‐way ANOVA was used to identify significant differences by treatment and by day independently. If the two‐way ANOVA identified a significant treatment × day interaction, then simple main effect analysis was used to identify treatment/day differences. In all cases, significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. For analysis of apoptotic pathway genes, data were imported into an Excel database and analyzed using the comparative cycle threshold (ΔΔC t) method with normalization of the raw data to housekeeping genes using software provided with the RT2 Profiler PCR array. The fold change for each gene between control and treatment groups was calculated as 2−ΔCt. The p‐value for each gene was calculated with a Student's t test of the replicate 2−ΔCt values for each gene in control and treatment groups. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Genes with greater than two‐fold changes in expression and with p ≤ 0.05 were considered biologically and statistically significant.

Results

Reduction in NTDs From Prebreeding Treatment With IFN‐γ

No NTDs were observed in either the saline control or IFN‐γ only groups. Embryos from dams treated with VA + IFN‐γ had significantly fewer NTDs (21.2% ± 4% per litter) than embryos from dams treated with VA alone (45.3% ± 10.4% per litter).

Cytokine Changes With Prebreeding IFN‐γ Treatment

To determine the effect in the mother from the pre‐breeding immune stimulation with IFN‐γ, we measured concentrations of the immunoregulatory cytokines GM‐CSF, IFN‐γ, IL‐6, IL‐10, and TNFα in maternal blood throughout gestation. Descriptive statistics for each cytokine are given in Tables S1–S5. Concentrations of IL‐6, IL‐10 and TNFα were not appreciably different between saline‐treated and IFN‐γ‐treated dams (Figure 1). GM‐CSF was significantly elevated on GD 9.5, 10.5, and 12.5 in dams treated pre‐breeding with IFN‐γ (Figure 1). IFN‐γ was significantly lower on GD 6.5 but then became significantly elevated throughout the latter half of pregnancy on GD 10.5, 12.5, and 15.5 (Figure 1). The significant increase in GM‐CSF and IFN‐γ in response to pre‐breeding MIS may be involved in the maternal response mechanism which confers protection against teratogens.

Maternal blood concentrations of immunoregulatory cytokines throughout gestation following prebreeding treatment with saline or IFN‐γ. Concentrations of GM‐CSF and IFN‐γ were significantly different between saline and IFN‐γ treated dams over mid and mid to late pregnancy respectively. Significance was determined by T‐test with significance set at p ≤ 0.05 and indicated by an *. N = 2–6 for GM‐CSF and 3–6 for the other cytokines.

Cytokine Changes With VA and VA + IFN‐γ Treatments

To examine maternal immunoregulatory cytokines that may play a role in specifically preventing VA induced NTDs, cytokine concentrations were measured on GD 8.5, 9.5, and 10.5, spanning the time of NT closure in VA and VA + IFN‐γ treated dams. Descriptive statistics for each cytokine are given in Tables S1–S5.

GM‐CSF

Two‐way ANOVA, to evaluate the effect of both treatment and time, for GM‐CSF identified a significant effect of treatment but not of day or treatment × day interaction (Figure 2). GM‐CSF concentrations were significantly elevated above controls in VA‐treated mothers on GD 9.5 and 10.5 (Figure 2). GM‐CSF concentrations in VA + IFN‐γ‐treated mothers were not different from controls indicating MIS prevented the increase in GM‐CSF following VA exposure (Figure 2).

Two‐way ANOVA results for IFN‐γ identified a significant effect of treatment but not day or treatment × day interaction (Figure 3). IFN‐γ concentrations were significantly elevated in VA + IFN‐γ treated mothers compared to saline and VA‐only‐treated dams on GD 10.5 (Figure 3).

IL‐6

Two‐way ANOVA results for IL‐6 identified significant effects of treatment and of day but no treatment × day interaction (Figure 4). IL‐6 concentrations were significantly elevated in VA + IFN‐γ treated dams compared to saline and VA only treated mothers on GD 8.5 (Figure 4). This indicates that elevated maternal IL‐6 may play a role in preventing VA‐induced NTDs. IL‐6 concentrations decreased significantly across GD 8.5, 9.5, and 10.5 for all treatment groups (Figure 4).

IL‐10

Two‐way ANOVA results for IL‐10 identified significant effects of treatment and day and a significant treatment × day interaction (Figure 5). Simple main effect analysis (Table S6) identified that IL‐10 concentrations were significantly elevated in VA + IFN‐γ treated dams compared to saline and VA only treated mothers on GD 8.5 and 9.5 (Figure 5). This indicates that elevated maternal IL‐10 may play a role in preventing VA induced NTDs. IL‐10 concentrations were unchanged for saline and VA only treated dams over GD 8.5, 9.5, and 10.5. In VA + IFN‐γ treated dams, however, IL‐10 concentrations decreased significantly across this period (Figure 5).

TNFα

Two‐way ANOVA results for TNFα identified a significant effect of treatment but not of day nor treatment × day interaction (Figure S1). TNFα concentrations were significantly lower in VA treated dams compared to controls on GD 8.5, although, the difference was small. VA + IFN‐γ treatment increased TNFα concentrations to a level statistically similar to both controls and VA only treated mice. Since the differences were small, these changes may not be clinically relevant.

No embryos from saline or IFN‐γ only treated dams had NTDs so only a single sample of pooled embryonic heads was used for each litter. Changes in gene expression of GD 8.5 and GD 9.5 embryos from VA only treated mice were compared to embryos from control mice treated with saline. Changes in gene expression of GD 8.5 and GD 9.5 embryos from VA + IFN‐γ treated mice were compared to embryos from mice treated with IFN‐γ only. In our study, genes with greater than 2‐fold changes in expression and with p ≤ 0.05 were considered biologically and statistically significant. Gene Expression Changes in GD 9.5 Embryos

Changes in gene expression of GD 9.5 embryonic heads with open and closed NTs from VA‐treated dams were compared to embryos from control dams treated with saline. Gene expression changes were almost identical in embryonic heads with either open or closed NT (Figure 7). Promoting the apoptotic process was upregulation of the pro‐apoptotic genes Bad, Bak1, Bok, Casp9 and Cideb, and downregulation of the antiapoptotic genes Akt1, Bcl2l1, Cflar, Dad1, and Cd40. Dampening the apoptotic process was upregulation of the anti‐apoptotic genes Mcl1 and Prdx2, and downregulation of the pro‐apoptotic genes Apaf1, Casp8, Cradd, Dffa, Fadd, Ripk1, and Traf2. While the number of altered pro and antiapoptotic genes was roughly equal, the fold changes were greater for genes promoting apoptosis. These results indicate that VA exposure altered overall gene expression to favor pro‐apoptotic processes in GD 9.5 embryonic heads regardless of the NT status (Figure 7).

Discussion

To identify a possible mechanism for the protection conferred by periconceptual MIS with IFN‐γ, maternal cytokine concentrations were determined throughout gestation. Of the cytokines measured, only GM‐CSF and IFN‐γ concentrations in the IFN‐γ treated dams differed substantially from saline controls. The differences were mainly observed in the latter half of gestation with both increased GM‐CSF and IFN‐γ concentrations observed after GD 9.5 and 10.5 respectively. Mice rapidly clear administered IFN‐γ within minutes (Kiuchi et al. 1984), thus the maternal concentrations measured during gestation are not residual from the initial pre‐breeding IFN‐γ injection. This is further supported by the finding of lower IFN‐γ concentrations on GD 6.6 than saline controls. Higher concentrations of GM‐CSF on GD 9.5 indicate that this cytokine could play a role in the prevention of VA induced NTDs as serum concentrations were elevated over the period of NT closure. IFN‐γ concentrations also were changing over the time of NT closure but were not significantly different until GD 10.5, after the NT had closed. Monitoring IFN‐γ concentrations at shorter time intervals throughout NT closure would indicate a more precise time when concentrations begin to rise and may identify a possible protective role for IFN‐γ as well.

The immune system is integral in promoting a successful pregnancy. As the mediator between the mother and fetus, the immune status of the placenta plays a critical role in directing fetal development. Some cytokines such as IL‐6 and TNFα can cross the placenta; others are produced in the placenta or come from maternal circulation (Aaltonen et al. 2005). Loss of normal physiological relationships between placental cytokines and growth factors can lead to adverse developmental outcomes. Placental damage or dysfunction can itself be teratogenic resulting in birth defects in embryos or fetuses even when the inciting agent does not cross the placenta (Ornoy and Miller 2023). In mice, the picture is slightly more complicated as they utilize the yolk sac as a placenta for the first half of gestation. The definitive placenta begins to develop on GD 10 and will function throughout the remainder of gestation (Ornoy and Miller 2023). The different placentas can exhibit differential permeability to compounds, cytokine profiles, and susceptibilities to teratogens.

Identifying a clear mechanism delineating how periconceptual MIS leads to protection against fetal malformations will be a complicated task. Compounding the difficulty posed by the two different placenta types is the fact that many cytokines are pleiotropic and can have different and even opposite functions throughout pregnancy. The action of maternal systemic and placental cytokines can promote a healthy pregnancy or cause adverse effects including fetal demise and birth defects, depending on the timing and concentration of the cytokine. In some instances, even a systemic maternal immune response is sufficient to induce NTDs (Yockey and Iwasaki 2018).

IL‐6 and TNFα both cross the placenta and are necessary for normal pregnancy and fetal development but can also cause adverse effects if concentrations are not tightly regulated. TNFα is important for normal hormone synthesis, placental architecture, and trophoblast differentiation, as well as normal embryonic development (Calleja Agius et al. 2012; Hunt, Miller, Hunt et al. 1996). Increased TNFα can cause fetal loss, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), and NTDs (Taubeneck et al. 1995; Torchinsky et al. 2003; Xu et al. 2006). IL‐6 is produced by decidual cells and trophoblasts and is necessary for implantation (Vilotić et al. 2022; Jauniaux et al. 2006). However, either decreased or increased IL‐6 concentrations cause fetal loss, IUGR, preterm birth, and NTDs (Prins et al. 2012; Krajewski et al. 2014; Mukhopadhyay et al. 2016). IFN‐γ is important for placenta formation and maintenance of the decidua; but, before implantation IFN‐γ and TNFα induce apoptosis and impair embryonic growth (Yockey and Iwasaki 2018). IL‐10 can be both inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory. Anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 production often occurs after pro‐inflammatory cytokine release in the early phase of the inflammatory process (Carlini et al. 2023). IL‐10 maintains the balance between pathogen control and detrimental immune response against pathogens during pregnancy.

In the present study, maternal GM‐CSF was elevated over the time of NT closure in VA treated dams. GM‐CSF concentrations were normalized to control levels in dams receiving pre‐breeding treatment with IFN‐γ. This identifies elevated GM‐CSF as a possible cause of VA induced NTDs. Normalization of GM‐CSF concentration accompanied the reduction in NTDs from 45.3% in VA only embryos to 21.2% in VA + IFN‐γ embryos indicating that GM‐CSF concentration may be part of the protective mechanism. IL‐6 concentrations were increased in VA + IFN‐γ treated dams compared to other groups at the initiation of NT closure on GD 8. This may indicate elevated IL‐6 plays a protective role at this early stage of NT closure. Similarly, IL‐10 was greatly increased in VA + IFN‐γ treated dams compared to other groups. As IL‐10 concentrations can increase following inflammatory cytokine production, this observed increase is likely a compensatory mechanism (Carlini et al. 2023). Prebreeding treatment with IFN‐γ appears to facilitate this compensatory response while VA treatment alone appears to limit or inhibit the IL‐10 response. Thus, it seems likely that IL‐10 is involved in conferring protection against VA induced NTDs probably by modulating a maternal inflammatory response.

Elucidating the specific roles of cytokines in NTD formation would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing NTD formation and prevention. However, cytokine availability alone is not sufficient for conferring protection against NTDs as downstream targets such as gene expression are also implicated. We previously demonstrated a role for apoptosis in VA‐induced NTDs and wanted to investigate the interconnectedness of MIS, cytokine response, and apoptosis in the embryo.

Regulation of apoptosis is an essential component of normal development. Teratogens alter apoptosis by changing the expression of apoptotic regulatory genes. Analysis of single genes provides limited information regarding the regulation and control of apoptosis. Conversely, analysis of a number of pathway‐focused genes can provide insight into overall apoptotic mechanisms. In this study, the expression of 84 key genes involved in apoptosis was determined in response to VA with and without MIS. The genes comprising the pathway‐focused array included the major regulators of apoptosis: TNF ligands and their receptors; members of the Bcl‐2 family, caspases, IAP, TRAF, CARD, death domain, death effector domain, and CIDE families; as well as genes involved in the p53 and ATM pathways.

Apoptotic gene expression change from VA and VA + IFN‐γ treatments was minimal in GD 8.5 embryonic heads. This may be due to limited apoptotic activity in embryonic heads at this stage of development, or because VA has a limited effect on embryos at this age. In our study, the p53 pathway gene, Trp73, which induces apoptosis, was downregulated significantly in embryonic heads from VA + IFN‐γ‐treated dams. This suggests that corrections to the p53 pathway may be involved to some extent in the protective effect of MIS on VA teratogenicity. Dysregulation of p53 genes and increased apoptosis are involved in ethyl carbamate teratogenicity (Sharova et al. 2000), and congenital eye malformations (Wubah et al. 1996; Charlap et al. 2003). Loeken (2006) suggested a role for p53 induced apoptosis in diabetes‐induced NTDs by demonstrating inhibition of Pax3 by excess glucose that is needed to check p53 dependent apoptosis.

In GD 9.5 embryos, both apoptotic and anti‐apoptotic gene expression was affected by VA. Bcl‐2, p53, and death effector domain family genes were predominantly affected in embryonic heads from VA‐treated dams compared to saline control dams. Pro‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 family genes appear to be involved in VA teratogenesis as Bad, Bak1, and Bok, were upregulated in embryonic heads from VA‐treated dams. Chemical and physical agents induce death stimuli by causing oxidative stress and DNA damage. Death stimuli induced by chemicals lead to activation of pro‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 family proteins such as Bax, Bid, and Bak (reviewed by Torchinsky et al. 2005). Bok is a pro‐apoptotic family gene that promotes chemotherapeutic‐induced apoptosis in growing cells (Rodriguez et al. 2006) and is essential for p53‐induced apoptosis (Yakovlev et al. 2004). Increased apoptosis in embryos from VA‐treated dams in our study could be related to upregulation of Bcl‐2 family genes such as Bad, Bak1, and Bok.

In addition to upregulating pro‐apoptotic genes in the Bcl‐2 family, VA exposure also enhanced apoptosis by downregulating anti‐apoptotic genes. This is similar to results obtained by Dawson et al. (2006), who found decreased expression of the anti‐apoptotic gene, Bcl2, in VA‐exposed embryos with both open and closed NTs. Increases in the ratio of pro‐apoptotic to antiapoptotic genes of the Bcl‐2 family are associated with increased apoptosis (Dawson et al. 2006). We observed this as well. Downregulation of antiapoptotic Bcl2l1, and upregulation of pro‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 family genes shifted the balance toward increased apoptosis in VA‐exposed embryos. These findings support an earlier study by Mallela and Hrubec (2012) which demonstrated through flow cytometric analysis and TUNEL staining higher percentages of apoptosis in VA‐exposed embryos with open NTDs.

MIS prevented the change in pro‐apoptotic regulatory molecules belonging to the Bcl‐2 family. Expression of the Bcl‐2 family apoptotic genes, Bad, Bak1, Bok, were normalized in embryonic heads with closed NTs from VA + IFN‐γ dams. Similar results were obtained by Savion et al. (2009), with cyclophosphamide teratogenicity. Cyclophosphamide induced expression of Bcl2, whereas MIS normalized the expression. Sharova et al. (2000) also demonstrated that MIS normalized the expression of Bcl‐2 family genes in embryos exposed to urethane. In the present study, not all NTDs were prevented by MIS. In these embryos with open NT, the expression of apoptotic genes including those of the Bcl‐2 family resembled that of VA‐exposed embryos. Thus, it is likely that the Bcl‐2 family genes play a major role in VA teratogenesis and normalization of expression by MIS is protective.

Casp9 in the p53 family is another pro‐apoptotic gene commonly involved in teratogenesis. Induction of Casp9 is a major cellular event in stress conditions (Torchinsky et al. 2005). Teratogens such as hyperthermia, cyclophosphamide, staurosporin (Little and Mirkes 2002), and arsenic (Singh et al. 2010) induce cell death in GD 9 mouse embryos by induction of Casp9. Our results are in line with the above studies. Up‐regulation of Casp9 was observed in VA‐exposed embryos with open NTs indicating a role for Casp9 in VA teratogenesis. Casp9 was also upregulated in GD 9.5 embryonic heads with closed NTs from VA + IFN‐γ dams; however, fold change was lower (2.548) compared to VA‐only exposed embryonic heads (3.837). In embryonic heads with open NTs from VA + IFN‐γ dams, fold change was similar to VA‐only heads (3.63). This indicates MIS partially normalized the expression of Casp9 toward control embryos but failed to reduce the expression of Casp9 in all embryos and likely contributed to NTD formation.

Akt negatively regulates apoptosis by suppressing a variety of pro‐apoptotic molecules such as Bad and Casp9 (Kim et al. 2001). VA induces apoptosis in cell lines by reducing the expression of Akt1 with subsequent activation of Casp9 (Chen et al. 2006). Downregulation of Akt1 in our study likely contributed to VA teratogenesis through the pro‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 and p53 pathways. MIS prevented downregulation of Akt1 in embryonic heads with closed NTs from VA + IFN‐γ dams. Thus Akt1 and its negative effect on pro‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 and p53 family genes may be involved in the protection conferred by MIS.

GM‐CSF is generally regarded as anti‐apoptotic and protective by inhibiting the expression of pro‐apoptotic p53 and inducing anti‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 family genes in neurons both in vitro, in vivo, and in neuroprogenitor cells (Huang et al. 2007; Choi et al. 2011; Schäbitz et al. 2008). However, recently it has been shown that GM‐CSF can both inhibit and induce apoptosis (Lotfi et al. 2019). We observed increased maternal blood concentrations of GM‐CSF with VA‐only treatment and normalization of GM‐CSF with VA + IFN‐γ. However, it is not clear whether GM‐CSF can cross the placenta so making a direct connection between maternal blood GM‐CSF concentration and events in the embryo is difficult (Matsumoto et al. 2011). Similarly, IL‐10 can induce or prevent apoptosis (Carlini et al. 2023). IL‐10 has a fundamental role in reducing neuronal injury during infection, inflammation, ischemia, and trauma, and increasing neuron survival and adult neurogenesis. IL‐10 reduces apoptosis in neurons by downregulating the expression of pro‐apoptotic Bax and upregulating antiapoptotic Bcl‐2. While not known in the mouse, IL‐10 does cross the placenta in the rat. It is possible then that the increase in maternal IL‐10 concentration seen with VA + IFN‐γ treatment could be shifting gene expression in the embryo to an antiapoptotic and protective profile.

In summary, prebreeding MIS with IFN‐γ altered the maternal blood profiles of GM‐CSF and IFN‐γ throughout pregnancy. Maternal GM‐CSF, IL‐6, and IL‐10 were altered over the time of NT closure and are possibly involved in protection from VA teratogenesis. Prebreeding MIS reduced the percentage of NTDs per litter and prevented VA‐induced changes in apoptotic gene expression in embryos with closed NT. To expand and reinforce the conclusions of this study, functional knockdown and eventual knockout lines of pro‐ and antiapoptotic genes should be generated to further identify key players in the regulation of gene expression in the presence of immune priming with IFN‐γ.
