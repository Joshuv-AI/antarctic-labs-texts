Characterization of selenium-enriched Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and its effects on egg selenium deposition and quality in laying hens
Authors: Chuanpi Xiao, Huixin Wang, Hepeng Li, Qimeng Yao, Haoran Zhao, Luke Comer, Shu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Selenium-enriched Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (SEL) is a selenium supplement with potential probiotic properties, yet its characterization and dose-response efficacy in laying hens remain poorly defined. This study aimed to characterize the selenium speciation and nanoparticle properties of a laboratory-prepared SEL product and to establish a dose-response framework for egg selenium biofortification. Multi-method physicochemical characterization confirmed that elemental selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) constituted 76.54% of total selenium, with an organic biomolecular surface coating, confirming efficient biotransformation of inorganic selenite by L. plantarum GS-3. In the animal trial, 240 Hy-Line Brown laying hens at 22 weeks old were randomly allocated to five treatments, including an unsupplemented control and four graded SEL levels (0.3, 0.6, 1.5, and 3.0 mg Se/kg diet). The animal experiment lasted for 8 weeks. The results showed that SEL supplementation had no significant effect on any production performance (P > 0.05). Eggshell thickness was significantly reduced in SEL-supplemented groups at both weeks 4 and 8 (P < 0.001), possibly reflecting selenium-associated modulation of uterine calcium transport or eggshell mineralization processes; albumen height and Haugh unit were transiently reduced at week 4 but recovered at week 8. Egg selenium content increased linearly with dietary SEL level in both albumen and yolk at weeks 4 and 8 (P < 0.001), with evidence of a deposition plateau in yolk at 1.5 mg Se/kg. These findings provide comprehensive physicochemical characterization of a biogenic SEL product and establish a dose-response framework for selenium biofortification of eggs using selenium-enriched L. plantarum.

Introduction

Selenium (Se) was first identified as a biological essential element in 1957 (Schwarz and Foltz, 1999). Selenium functions primarily through its co-translational incorporation into selenoproteins as the amino acid selenocysteine, with the human genome encoding at least 25 such proteins, including glutathione peroxidases (GPx) and thioredoxin reductases (TrxR) are the most studied, serving as the enzymatic backbone of cellular antioxidant defense and redox homeostasis (Steinbrenner et al., 2016). Beyond antioxidation, selenoproteins regulate thyroid hormone metabolism, immune activation, and reproductive function (Brown and Arthur, 2001; Rocca et al., 2019). Despite this well-established importance, selenium deficiency remains widespread, as the uneven global distribution of selenium in soils causes large populations in sub-Saharan Africa, China, and Europe to consistently fall below recommended dietary intakes. Eggs are among the most bioavailable dietary sources of selenium for humans, and supplementation of laying hens represents a practical strategy for producing selenium-biofortified eggs (Lu et al., 2020). Multiple studies have demonstrated that selenium supplementation reliably increases egg selenium content without compromising production performance (Zhou et al., 2025).

Selenium supplementation in poultry has evolved through three categories of sources. Inorganic selenium, primarily sodium selenite (SS), was adopted first for its low cost, but carries well-documented limitations: relatively high toxicity, narrow safety margins, and lower bioavailability as intestinal absorption depends on chelation by organic ligands which are subject to competition from other dietary components (Bakhshalinejad et al., 2018). Organic selenium, particularly selenium-enriched yeast (SeY) containing predominantly selenomethionine, substantially improved bioavailability and egg selenium deposition efficiency and it remains the dominant supplement in commercial production (Chen et al., 2023b). More recently, selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) have attracted considerable interest due to their low cytotoxicity, high surface reactivity, and superior biocompatibility relative to both inorganic and conventional organic forms (Ferrari et al., 2023). Selenium-enriched probiotics represent a further advancement, combining the nutritional value of selenium with the health-promoting properties of probiotic microorganisms, including reported modulation of antioxidant status, immune function, and intestinal microbiota in a single additive (Chen et al., 2023a).

Among selenium-enriched probiotics, selenium-enriched lactic acid bacteria (LAB) have attracted growing attention, with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum emerging as a particularly suitable host species. L. plantarum itself contributes toward well-established probiotic functions including acid and bile tolerance, intestinal colonization, and immunomodulatory activity, making selenium-enriched L. plantarum (SEL) a potential dual-function additive whose benefits extend beyond selenium delivery alone (Martínez et al., 2020), including potential probiotic effects on gut health and immune function that warrant systematic investigation. In laying hens, selenium-enriched LAB have been shown to improve egg quality, antioxidant capacity, and ileal microbiota composition relative to selenium-enriched yeast, and selenium-enriched Lactobacillus coryniformis has been shown to alleviate LPS-induced intestinal inflammation in chicks (Xu et al., 2025). However, two important knowledge gaps remain. First, the physicochemical characterization of commercially produced SEL, including selenium speciation, nanoparticle morphology, crystal structure, surface charge, and functional group composition, has not been systematically reported. Second, the dose-response relationship between dietary SEL inclusion levels and egg selenium deposition in laying hens has not been established, leaving producers without evidence-based guidance on optimal supplementation rates.

The present study was designed to address both gaps. We performed comprehensive physicochemical characterization of a laboratory-prepared SEL product derived from L. plantarum GS-3 (CGMCC 24264) using multiple complementary analytical techniques, including electron microscopy, spectroscopic methods, and selenium speciation analysis by HPLC-ICP-MS. We also conducted an 8-week dose-response trial in which 240 Hy-Line Brown laying hens were fed diets supplemented with four graded levels of SEL (0.3, 0.6, 1.5, and 3.0 mg Se/kg) alongside an unsupplemented control, evaluating production performance, egg quality, and egg selenium deposition evaluated at weeks 4 and 8, with the hypothesis that increasing dietary SEL levels would produce a linear dose-dependent increase in egg selenium content without impairing production performance. Together, these two components provide a structural basis for understanding SEL bioactivity and practical dosing recommendations for selenium-biofortified egg production. The present study focuses on the selenium enrichment function of SEL. The probiotic properties of SEL, including effects on gut microbiota, intestinal morphology, and antioxidant capacity, are identified as priorities for future investigation.

Materials and methods

Preparation of SEL

L. plantarum GS-3 (deposited at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center under accession number CGMCC 24264) was activated and propagated in MRS broth before transfer to a fermentation tank. Fermentation was conducted under anaerobic conditions at 30-35°C with the pH maintained between 6.2 and 6.5. Sodium selenite was added to the fermentation medium to a final concentration of 10 mmol/L, a level selected based on published optimization studies demonstrating that this concentration range supports maximal selenium incorporation into LAB biomass while maintaining bacterial viability (Wang et al., 2024). Following fermentation, the broth was centrifuged and the resulting pellet was spray-dried to yield SEL powder.

Physicochemical characterization of SEL

Selenium content and speciation analysis. Total selenium content was determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; Agilent 7900, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Briefly, 0.05 g of SEL powder was weighed into a conical flask, digested with 15 mL of concentrated nitric acid (GR grade) on a graphite heating plate until the solution became clear, then transferred to a centrifuge tube, diluted with ultrapure water, and filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter prior to analysis. Selenium standard solutions at 0, 5, 10, 50, 100, 200, and 500 ng/mL were prepared from a certified reference material (National Institute of Metrology, China) for calibration. The ICP-MS operating parameters were as follows: radiofrequency power, 1500 W; plasma gas flow, 15 L/min; carrier gas flow, 0.80 L/min; auxiliary gas flow, 0.40 L/min; helium collision gas flow, 4–5 mL/min; nebulizer temperature, 2°C; sampling depth, 8–10 mm; monitored isotopes, 78Se, 80Se, and 82Se.

Nano-selenium content was determined by selective extraction. A 0.05 g aliquot of SEL powder was suspended in 40 mL of 5 mM tetrasodium pyrophosphate containing 1 mM potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4), incubated on a shaker at 200 rpm for 1 h, sonicated for 30 min, and allowed to stand at room temperature for 6 h. A 3 mL aliquot was withdrawn from 1 cm below the liquid surface, passed through a 0.45 μm membrane filter, and the retained membrane was transferred to a centrifuge tube containing 2 mL nitric acid and 2 mL ultrapure water. The membrane was digested at 70°C for 6 h, and the resulting solution was analyzed by ICP-MS as described above. This selective extraction method was adapted from Yang et al. (2024).

Inorganic selenium content was determined according to local food safety standard (DBS42/010-2018). Briefly, 0.05 g of SEL powder was extracted in a leaching solution (0.1 mmol/L KH2PO4 + 1.56 mmol/L CuSO4) made up to 10 mL with ultrapure water, extracted at room temperature for 30 min, then incubated at 70°C with shaking at 1500 rpm. The extract was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter prior to ICP-MS analysis.

Selenium speciation, including selenomethionine (SeMet), selenocystine (SeCys2), methylselenocysteine (MeSeCys), selenite (SeO32-), and selenate (SeO42-), was determined by HPLC-ICP-MS. For sample preparation, 0.1 g of SEL powder was suspended in 10 mL of Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), sonicated in an ice bath for 30 min, and then incubated sequentially with 0.02 g protease type XIV at 37 ± 2°C for 18 h and a further 0.02 g protease type XIV at 37 ± 2°C for an additional 18 h (both at 250 rpm). The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter. Certified reference standard solutions of SeMet, SeCys2, MeSeCys, SeO32-, and SeO42- (National Institute of Metrology, China) were prepared in ultrapure water and stored at −20°C; working mixed standards at 1, 5, 10, 30, and 60 mg/L were prepared fresh in Tris-HCl buffer before each run. Chromatographic separation was performed on a ZORBAX SB-Aq column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm; Agilent Technologies) using isocratic elution with a mobile phase of 10 mM citric acid, 5 mM sodium 1-hexanesulfonate, and 1% methanol at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. The enzymatic extraction procedure was adapted from Xiao et al. (2021) and Bakirdere et al. (2018). The ICP-MS was operated with a helium gas flow of 10 mL/min, a sampling depth of 8 mm, and an integration time of 0.5 s per mass; all other parameters were identical to those used for total selenium analysis.

Electron microscopy and elemental analysis. For TEM observation, 20 μL of SEL fermentation broth or a freshly prepared aqueous suspension of SEL powder was deposited onto a 200-mesh carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to adsorb for 3-5 min. Excess liquid was blotted, and the grid was negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 1-2 min, air-dried at room temperature, and examined under a transmission electron microscope (HT7800/HT7700, HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan).

For surface morphology and elemental mapping of the dried powder, SEL powder was mounted on metallic stubs using conductive carbon tape and sputter-coated with gold. Elemental analysis was performed by line-scan EDS (ULTIMMAX 100, Oxford Instruments, Oxford, UK) under a scanning electron microscope (SU8100, HITACHI).

For cross-sectional elemental mapping of bacterial cells, SEL fermentation broth was centrifuged and the pellet was washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cells were fixed in electron microscopy fixative, rinsed in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series followed by isoamyl acetate, and critical-point dried (K850, Quorum Technologies, Laughton, UK). Area-scan EDS mapping was then performed under the SEM as described above.

Spectroscopic and physicochemical characterization. For UV-Vis, FT-IR, and zeta potential measurements, SEL fermentation broth was centrifuged and the pellet was resuspended in 0.2 mol/L PBS (pH 6.8) with brief sonication. The suspension was scanned over 200-800 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-3600, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) to confirm SeNP formation. Surface functional groups were identified by FT-IR spectroscopy (Nicolet NEXUS 670, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using the same suspension. Zeta potential was measured on the same suspension using a Zetasizer ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). For XRD analysis, approximately 50 mg of SEL powder was spread uniformly over a 1 cm × 1 cm area on the sample stage and scanned using an X-ray diffractometer (D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) to determine the crystalline structure.

Animals, diets, and experimental design

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Institute of Animal Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (approval no. IAS2024-179). The experiment was conducted at the Tianjin Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Research Institute.

A total of two hundred and forty 22-week-old Hy-Line Brown laying hens with similar egg production rates were randomly allocated to five treatment groups, with 16 replicates per group and 3 hens per replicate (one cage). The five treatments were: 1) CON, basal diet with no supplemental selenium source; 2) 0.3 SEL, basal diet + 0.3 mg Se/kg SEL; 3) 0.6 SEL, basal diet + 0.6 mg Se/kg SEL; 4) 1.5 SEL, basal diet + 1.5 mg Se/kg SEL; and 5) 3 SEL, basal diet + 3.0 mg Se/kg SEL. The basal diet was a corn soybean meal diet formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of laying hens according to NRC (1994) and the Chinese Poultry Feeding Standard (NY/T 33-2004), the feed composition and analyzed nutrient levels are presented in Table 1. The measured selenium content of the basal diet was 0.3698 mg/kg (weeks 1-4) and 0.3944 mg/kg (weeks 5-8). Husbandry and management

Hens were housed in a semi-enclosed, naturally ventilated facility in three-tier battery cages. Feed was provided twice daily (9:00 and 16:00) and water was available ad libitum. House temperature was maintained at 22-26°C, relative humidity at 40-55%, and the lighting regimen consisted of 16 h light and 8 h darkness per day at an intensity of 10 lux. Egg production was recorded daily at 15:00. The facility was cleaned and disinfected weekly throughout the trial.

Data collection and sample analysis

Production performance. Egg number and total egg weight per replicate were recorded daily. Laying rate (LR), average daily egg weight (ADE), and average egg weight (EW) were calculated for each four weeks period. Feed intake was monitored daily and summarized every four weeks to calculate average daily feed intake (ADFI). Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as ADFI/ADE.

Egg quality. At the end of weeks 4 and 8, 16 eggs per treatment (1 egg per replicate) were collected for egg quality assessment. Egg shape index was measured using an egg shape index tester (NFN-385, Bulader, Japan). Albumen height, yolk color, and Haugh unit were determined using an egg multi-function tester (EA-01, Orka, Israel). Eggshell strength was measured with an eggshell strength tester (EFR-01, Orka, Israel). Eggshell thickness (after membrane removal) was measured at the blunt end, sharp end, and equator using a vernier caliper, and the mean of the three measurements was recorded.

Egg selenium content. At the end of weeks 4 and 8, one egg was collected from every two replicates per treatment (8 eggs per treatment per time point). Albumen and yolk were separated, snap-frozen at −80°C, freeze-dried (LGJ-1A, Yataikelong, China), and homogenized. Lyophilized samples (0.2 g) were weighed into microwave digestion vessels, pre-digested overnight with 8 mL of concentrated nitric acid (GR grade), and digested using a microwave digestion system (MARS 6, CEM, Matthews, NC, USA). Digests were diluted with ultrapure water, filtered through 0.45 μm syringe filters, and analyzed for total selenium by ICP-MS under the conditions described by Zhang et al. (2025), with minor modification.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using the PROC MIXED procedure in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Treatment effects were evaluated by one-way ANOVA with means separated using Tukey's HSD test. Linear and quadratic dose-response relationships were assessed by regression analysis using the actual dietary selenium concentrations (0, 0.3, 0.6, 1.5, and 3.0 mg Se/kg) as the independent variable. Results are expressed as mean and pooled standard error of the means (SEM), and data were considered statistically significantly different when P < 0.05.

Results

Physicochemical characterization of SEL

Selenium content and speciation. The total selenium content of SEL was 50,289.94 mg/kg (dry weight). HPLC-ICP-MS speciation analysis revealed that SeNPs constituted the predominant selenium form, accounting for 76.54% of total selenium (38,489.67 mg/kg). Residual inorganic SeO32- was detected at 90.79 mg/kg (0.18%), and SeCys2 was present at 40.77 mg/kg (0.08%). No SeO42-, SeMet, or MeSeCys was detected. The remaining 23.20% (11,668.71 mg/kg) was classified as other selenium species (Fig. 1). Zeta potential and XRD. The zeta potential of the SEL suspension was −16.5 mV (Fig. 4c), indicating a net negative surface charge that provides electrostatic repulsion sufficient for colloidal stability in aqueous suspension. XRD analysis of the SEL powder revealed multiple sharp diffraction peaks in the 2θ range of 8°–35°, corresponding to hexagonal selenium (trigonal, α-Se), and additional peaks at higher angles up to 90°, consistent with cubic selenium (β-Se) (Fig. 4d). The high intensity and sharpness of the diffraction peaks confirm a high degree of crystallinity, and the co-occurrence of both crystal phases indicates a mixed-crystallinity structure in the SEL product.

Effects of SEL on production performance

Dietary supplementation with SEL at all levels had no significant effect on any production performance parameter throughout the 8-week period (Table 2). ADFI, LR, ADE, EW, and FCR did not differ significantly among the CON, 0.3 SEL, 0.6 SEL, 1.5 SEL, and 3 SEL groups during either week 1- 4 or week 5- 8 (P > 0.05). Discussion

Selenium is an essential trace element whose biological functions are mediated primarily through its co-translational incorporation into selenoproteins as selenocysteine, with the human genome encoding 25 such proteins (Khan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023). The physicochemical characterization of SEL revealed that L. plantarum GS-3 efficiently converts inorganic selenite into elemental SeNPs as the predominant selenium species, with SeNPs accounting for 76.54% of total selenium. This conversion efficiency is consistent with the well-established intracellular reduction pathway in Lactiplantibacillus species, in which selenite is first chelated by glutathione to form selenodiglutathione, which is subsequently reduced by glutathione reductase to yield elemental Se⁰. The high SeNPs proportion observed in the present study compares favorably with values reported for other biogenic LAB-SeNPs preparations, where SeNPs typically constitute 60–80% of total selenium depending on fermentation conditions and selenite concentration (Martínez et al., 2020). The trace residual selenite and absence of selenate, selenomethionine, and methylselenocysteine indicate near-complete biotransformation and confirm that the product is not a simple mixture of inorganic and organic selenium forms. TEM imaging confirmed the intracellular localization of SeNPs, and the 20-300 nm size range is within the range reported for biogenic SeNPs from L. plantarum and related species (Chen et al., 2025a; Malik et al., 2025). The zeta potential of −16.5 mV, while below the conventional ±30 mV threshold for purely electrostatic colloidal stability, is consistent with values reported for biogenic SeNPs whose stability is maintained primarily through steric repulsion provided by the adsorbed protein and polysaccharide corona rather than electrostatic charge alone (Zambonino et al., 2023). FT-IR analysis confirmed the presence of protein- and polysaccharide-associated functional groups on the SeNP surface, supporting this interpretation. The XRD pattern revealed a mixed hexagonal (α-Se) and cubic (β-Se) crystalline structure with high peak intensity, which is atypical of biogenic SeNPs synthesized under mild aqueous conditions, where amorphous or poorly crystalline structures are more commonly reported (Lashani et al., 2024). We attribute the elevated crystallinity to the spray-drying step used in product preparation, as the thermal energy imparted during spray-drying (inlet temperatures typically 150-200°C) is sufficient to promote crystallization of amorphous Se⁰ into thermodynamically stable crystalline phases. This thermal crystallization effect has been noted in other spray-dried selenium-containing materials and should be considered when interpreting the biological activity of spray-dried SEL products, as crystallinity may influence dissolution rate and bioavailability in the gastrointestinal tract (Bulgarini et al., 2021; Lashani et al., 2024).

Dietary supplementation with SEL at all levels (0.3-3.0 mg Se/kg) had no significant effect on any production performance parameter, including laying rate, average egg weight, average daily feed intake, and feed conversion ratio, throughout the 8-week trial. This finding is consistent with the broad consensus in the literature that selenium supplementation, regardless of source, does not significantly alter production performance in laying hens when administered within the physiologically relevant range (Liu et al., 2020). The absence of performance effects at the highest dose tested (3.0 mg Se/kg) is particularly noteworthy, as it suggests that SEL did not exert overt adverse effects on production at this inclusion level, although dedicated toxicological biomarker analyses were not conducted and definitive safety conclusions at this dose require further investigation. This is consistent with the well-documented low cytotoxicity of biogenic SeNPs relative to inorganic selenite, which is attributed to the organic biomolecular corona coating the nanoparticle surface, the controlled dissolution kinetics of elemental Se0, and the reduced reactivity of nanoparticulate selenium compared with ionic selenite (Nabi et al., 2020). In vivo studies in poultry have further confirmed that dietary SeNP supplementation does not compromise tissue ultrastructure or hepatic and renal function at nutritional doses (Bień et al., 2023). The safety margin of SeNPs is substantially wider than that of sodium selenite, for which toxic effects in poultry have been reported at dietary concentrations approaching 2-4 mg Se/kg (Hadrup and Ravn-Haren, 2023). These findings suggest that SEL is well tolerated across the dose range evaluated.

SEL supplementation produced a significant, linearly decreasing effect on eggshell thickness at both weeks 4 and 8, with all SEL-supplemented groups showing reduced eggshell thickness relative to the unsupplemented control. This finding is unexpected given that selenium has generally been reported to have neutral or mildly positive effects on eggshell quality in studies using inorganic or organic selenium sources (Muhammad et al., 2021). Eggshell formation is a calcium-intensive process: a single eggshell requires approximately 2 g of calcium deposited over 15-20 h in the shell gland (uterus), drawing on both intestinal absorption and medullary bone resorption as calcium sources (Fu et al., 2024). The integrity of this process depends on the coordinated expression of calcium transport proteins in the shell gland epithelium, including calbindin-D28k, plasma membrane Ca2+-ATPase (PMCA), and the sodium-calcium exchanger (NCX), whose expression is tightly regulated by the local redox environment and selenoprotein activity (Yang et al., 2025). One plausible mechanism for the observed reduction in eggshell thickness involves competition between selenium and calcium at the level of intestinal absorption or oviducal mineral transport. High selenium intake may partially suppress calcium uptake or redirect mineral resources toward selenium metabolism, thereby reducing the calcium available for eggshell calcification (Muhammad et al., 2021). An alternative explanation involves the redox-active properties of selenium: at higher intracellular selenium concentrations, reactive oxygen species generated transiently during selenium metabolism may disrupt the oxidative environment of the shell gland epithelium, impairing the expression of calcium transport genes and matrix proteins such as ovocalyxin-32 and osteopontin that are essential for normal eggshell mineralization (Feng et al., 2023). This interpretation is supported by evidence that uterine inflammation and oxidative stress consistently reduce calbindin-D28k expression and impair calcium deposition in the shell gland, leading to thinner eggshells. Although we did not measure serum calcium concentrations or shell gland histology directly in the present study, the dose-independent nature of the eggshell thickness reduction at week 4, in which a significant linear decrease was detected across increasing SEL doses but the 0.3 SEL group was not significantly different from CON while all higher doses produced equivalent reductions, is more consistent with a threshold-type disruption of shell gland redox homeostasis than with a strictly graded calcium-competition effect, and future studies should include measurements of serum calcium and phosphorus, shell gland calbindin-D28k expression, and uterine histomorphology to distinguish between these mechanisms. The partial recovery of eggshell thickness in the 1.5 SEL group by week 8, where values were no longer significantly different from CON, suggests that hens may adapt to selenium supplementation over time through upregulation of compensatory antioxidant and calcium transport mechanisms. The transient reduction in albumen height and Haugh unit observed at week 4, which resolved completely by week 8, is consistent with an adaptation response rather than a sustained adverse effect. Albumen quality is primarily determined by the integrity of ovomucin gel structure in the thick albumen, and selenium-dependent selenoproteins including thioredoxin reductase and glutathione peroxidase play roles in maintaining the redox environment of the oviduct magnum where albumen is secreted (Muhammad et al., 2022). We hypothesize that the initial perturbation in albumen quality reflects a transient period of metabolic adjustment as the oviduct adapts to elevated selenium flux, after which selenoprotein expression stabilizes and albumen quality is restored. This interpretation is supported by the observation that the effect was dose-independent at week 4 and absent at week 8, which is more consistent with an adaptation phenomenon than a dose-dependent toxic effect. Notably, Zhou et al. (2025) reported that selenium-enriched lactobacilli improved albumen height and Haugh unit relative to selenium-enriched yeast at equivalent selenium doses, suggesting that the probiotic component of SEL may confer additional benefits to albumen quality under longer supplementation periods or at optimized doses.

The most consistent and biologically significant finding of the animal trial was the significant, linear dose-dependent increase in selenium content in both albumen and yolk across all SEL supplementation levels at weeks 4 and 8. This dose-response relationship confirms that SEL is an effective vehicle for selenium biofortification of eggs and that egg selenium content can be predictably modulated by adjusting dietary SEL inclusion. The linear relationship between dietary selenium dose and egg selenium content is consistent with findings for other selenium sources in laying hens, including selenium-enriched yeast (Lu et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2025) and nano-selenium (Chen et al., 2025b; Shu et al., 2026), and reflects the saturable but dose-responsive nature of selenium transport from the bloodstream into the developing follicle and oviduct. Selenium is transported in blood primarily bound to selenoprotein P (SelP), which serves as the principal selenium carrier protein and delivers selenium to peripheral tissues including the ovary and oviduct (Li et al., 2025). As dietary selenium intake increases, plasma SelP concentration rises proportionally within the physiological range, driving greater selenium delivery to the follicle and ultimately higher selenium deposition in the yolk (Qin et al., 2026). The observation of a deposition plateau in yolk selenium at approximately 1.5 mg Se/kg - where the 1.5 SEL and 3 SEL groups were not significantly different at week 8 - suggests that yolk selenium uptake becomes saturated at this dietary level, likely reflecting the finite capacity of the vitellogenin-mediated yolk precursor transport system to incorporate selenium into yolk lipoprotein complexes (Song et al., 2023). In contrast, albumen selenium continued to increase linearly up to 3.0 mg Se/kg, consistent with the different deposition mechanisms operating in albumen (passive diffusion and active transport in the magnum) versus yolk (receptor-mediated endocytosis of vitellogenin in the follicle) (Gu and Gao, 2022). The progressive increase in egg selenium content from week 4 to week 8 across all SEL groups reflects the time-dependent accumulation of selenium in the body pool and the gradual equilibration of selenium flux between dietary intake, tissue storage, and egg deposition, a pattern that has been consistently observed with organic selenium sources (Meng et al., 2021). From a practical standpoint, the dose-response data suggest that a dietary SEL inclusion at 1.5 mg Se/kg represents an effective target for maximizing yolk selenium deposition, while higher doses (3.0 mg Se/kg) provide additional benefit for albumen selenium enrichment without compromising production performance. This pattern is further supported by a direct comparison of selenium-enriched lactobacilli and selenium-enriched yeast in laying hens, in which the lactobacilli-derived source produced a dose-dependent increase in egg selenium deposition comparable to that of selenium yeast (Zhou et al., 2025).

The present study demonstrates that SEL produced from L. plantarum GS-3 is a physicochemically well-defined product in which the predominant selenium species is biogenic SeNPs, and that dietary SEL supplementation effectively biofortifies eggs with selenium in a dose-dependent manner without impairing production performance. Compared with conventional selenium sources, SEL offers several potential advantages. Relative to sodium selenite, SEL provides selenium predominantly in the nanoparticulate elemental form, which has lower acute toxicity, a wider safety margin, and comparable or superior bioavailability (Hadrup and Ravn-Haren, 2021). Relative to selenium-enriched yeast, SEL differs in that it additionally delivers viable probiotic bacteria, which have been associated with benefits for intestinal health, immune function, and microbiota composition in poultry (Saeed et al., 2025). Organic selenium sources, including selenium-enriched yeast, have been shown to improve albumen quality and Haugh unit scores in laying hens, with effects potentially mediated through modulation of intestinal microbiota (Li et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2020). Whether the viable L. plantarum cells present in SEL contribute to albumen quality through probiotic mechanisms, such as modulation of oviduct microbiota or immune function remains to be determined, as the present study did not include measurements of intestinal microbiota composition or gut health parameters. The present study extends this evidence base by providing the first systematic physicochemical characterization of a biogenic SEL product and establishing a quantitative dose-response framework for egg selenium biofortification. Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the trial did not include a positive control group supplemented with an equivalent dose of sodium selenite or selenium-enriched yeast, which would have allowed direct comparison of selenium bioavailability and egg deposition efficiency across sources. Second, the mechanisms underlying the transient reduction in eggshell thickness and albumen quality were not investigated at the molecular level; future studies should examine oviduct selenoprotein expression, serum calcium and phosphorus concentrations, shell gland calbindin-D28k expression, and uterine histomorphology to elucidate these effects. Third, the characterization data were obtained from a single laboratory-prepared batch of SEL, and batch-to-batch variability in selenium speciation and nanoparticle properties under commercial-scale fermentation conditions remains to be evaluated. In this regard, future research should systematically optimize the fermentation conditions for SEL production, including selenite concentration, fermentation temperature, pH, inoculation density, and incubation duration, as these parameters are known to substantially influence the efficiency of selenite-to-SeNP biotransformation, the size distribution and surface properties of the resulting nanoparticles, and ultimately the biological activity of the product (Wang et al., 2024). Establishing optimized and standardized fermentation protocols will be essential for ensuring consistent product quality and maximizing the selenium enrichment and potential probiotic benefits of SEL at commercial scale. Future research should also investigate the effects of SEL on antioxidant capacity, immune function, and gut microbiota composition in laying hens, as these are the primary mechanisms through which the probiotic component of SEL is expected to exert additional benefits beyond selenium delivery, consistent with the antioxidant-immunity-microbiota axis documented for other functional feed additives in poultry (Liu et al., 2023).

Conclusion

In conclusion, L. plantarum GS-3 efficiently converts inorganic selenite into biogenic selenium nanoparticles as the predominant selenium species, yielding a physicochemically well-defined SEL product with a stable organic surface coating. Dietary SEL supplementation effectively biofortifies eggs with selenium in a significant, linear dose-dependent manner without impairing production performance, with a dietary inclusion level of 1.5 mg Se/kg identified as effective for maximizing yolk selenium deposition. These findings demonstrate that SEL represents a safe and efficacious selenium supplement for laying hen production and provide a quantitative dose-response framework for producing selenium-enriched eggs to address human selenium deficiency. Future studies should investigate the effects of SEL on gut microbiota composition, intestinal health, and immune function to fully evaluate its potential as a multifunctional feed additive.
