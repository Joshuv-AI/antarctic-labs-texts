Association between diabetes and elevated circulating advanced oxidation protein products: a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Muhammad Shahid Iqbal, Mohd Faiyaz Khan, Sadaf Farooqui, Muhammad Zahid Iqbal, Salah-Ud-Din Khan, Nazia Khan, Vidya Devanathadesikan Seshadri, Amr Ali Mohamed Abdelgawwad El-Sehrawy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOxidative stress is a central feature of the metabolic disturbances observed in diabetes, often leading to cellular and molecular damage. Advanced oxidation protein products (AOPPs), formed during oxidative modification of plasma proteins, have been proposed as biomarkers of systemic oxidative stress. Despite increasing interest, the relationship between these markers and diabetes remains inconclusive. This study aimed to systematically evaluate and quantify differences in circulating AOPPs levels in diabetes.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was performed in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Searches were conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Embase and Web of Science databases up to March 2025 to identify relevant observational studies involving adults aged 18 years or older. Studies were included if they reported circulating levels of AOPPs in individuals with type 1 diabetes (T1DM), type 2 diabetes (T2DM), or impaired fasting glucose (IFG) or impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), compared to non-diabetic controls. Data extraction and quality assessment were conducted independently by two reviewers, and a random-effects model was used for meta-analysis.ResultsSixteen eligible articles with twenty individual studies were identified, encompassing diverse populations and diabetes subtypes. The meta-analysis demonstrated significantly elevated circulating AOPPs in both T1DM (WMD ≈ 17.5 µmol/L) and T2DM (WMD ≈ 24.68 µmol/L) compared with controls, though heterogeneity was high. Subgroup analyses confirmed the consistent direction of effect, with stronger associations in younger populations, studies with higher baseline AOPPs, and certain designs or regions. Meta-regression showed that examined moderators (continent, study design, baseline AOPPs, age, sample size, gender, study quality, presence of overweight/ obesity, hypertension, smoking and abnormal serum lipids) explained little to none of the variability. Sensitivity analyses indicated that results were robust. Publication bias assessment revealed funnel plot asymmetry for type 2 diabetes, but trim-and-fill analysis did not materially alter the pooled estimates, supporting the stability of the findings.ConclusionElevated levels of AOPPs appear to be associated with diabetes, supporting the role of oxidative stress in its pathophysiology. Although pooled analyses demonstrated significantly higher circulating AOPP concentrations in patients with diabetes mellitus, substantial between-study heterogeneity and wide prediction intervals suggest that the magnitude and consistency of this association may vary across different populations and study settings. Also, the observational design of included studies limits the strength and generalizability of the pooled estimates. Therefore, causal inferences cannot be drawn, and further well-designed prospective research is warranted to clarify whether these biomarkers can reliably predict the onset or progression of diabetes.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251275798Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02228-7.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a major global health concern, affecting millions of individuals across all age groups. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), the prevalence of diabetes has risen dramatically over the past few decades, with an estimated 537 million adults (20–79 years old) living with diabetes in 2021, a number expected to reach 783 million by 2045. This increasing burden is largely driven by lifestyle changes, urbanization, and rising obesity rates [1]. It is important to distinguish between the two main types of diabetes. Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), also known as insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (IDDM), results from the destruction of pancreatic β-cells, leading to insufficient insulin production. In contrast, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), previously referred to as non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (NIDDM), develops primarily due to insulin resistance in target tissues, often accompanied by a gradual decline in β-cell function. In both forms of diabetes, the progressive loss of β-cell mass and/or function impairs glucose regulation, ultimately leading to persistent hyperglycemia, the hallmark clinical feature of the disease [2, 3]. The role of hyperglycemia in the progression of diabetes and its complications is well established. However, oxidative stress further exacerbates the harmful effects of hyperglycemia, contributing to both diabetes development and its associated complications. Advanced oxidation protein products (AOPPs) are formed in response to chronic hyperglycemia, primarily as a result of oxidative stress-induced albumin degradation, leading to the production of di-tyrosine-containing protein derivatives. Due to their strong association with oxidative stress, AOPPs serve as important biomarkers in diabetes and its progression [4]. AOPPs are recognized as cardio-metabolic markers of protein oxidative modification, particularly affecting albumin. They are generated through reactions involving chlorinated oxidants, such as hypochlorous acid (HOCl) and chloramines, which interact with plasma proteins. The accumulation of AOPPs disrupts the balance between pro-oxidants and antioxidants, leading to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and subsequent tissue damage [5–8].

Numerous studies have identified elevated AOPPs levels as key indicators of various chronic non-communicable diseases, including cardiovascular disease [9–13], diabetes [5, 14–18], metabolic syndrome [19–21], and chronic kidney disease [22, 23]. These findings highlight the critical role of oxidative stress in the pathophysiology of diabetes and its complications, emphasizing the need for further research into targeted antioxidant therapies.

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate hydrogen (NADPH) oxidase plays a crucial role in β-cell dysfunction and destruction through the Bcl-2/Bax apoptotic pathway, contributing to endothelial dysfunction in diabetes [24, 25]. Additionally, AOPPs facilitate the destruction of islet microvascular endothelial cells, partially via the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGEs) pathway, which is regulated by glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1). These mechanisms highlight AOPPs’ involvement in the pathogenesis of diabetes [26]. AOPPs are also implicated in vascular complications associated with diabetes by promoting inflammatory responses. They stimulate the production of tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)−1, and IL-6, activate macrophages, and increase levels of monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)−1, thereby intensifying inflammation [27]. Furthermore, AOPPs elevate MCP-1 expression in vascular cells by activating the nuclear factor (NF)-κB pathway through ROS-mediated signaling, contributing to vascular damage in diabetes [28]. Several studies have examined the role of circulating AOPPs in type 1 and type 2 diabetes, as well as in individuals with impaired fasting glucose (IFG). In a study by Kalousová et al., [16], patients with T2DM exhibited significantly higher circulating AOPP levels compared to healthy controls. Similarly, Leoncini et al. reported that AOPPs concentrations were significantly elevated in individuals with both T1DM and T2DM compared to non-diabetic controls. These findings further support the role of AOPPs as key markers of oxidative stress and inflammation in diabetes progression [29].

The variability and inconsistencies among studies pose challenges in obtaining reliable conclusions about the relationship between diabetes and AOPPs. Findings across individual studies remain inconsistent, often limited by small sample sizes and heterogeneous methodologies. A comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence is therefore necessary to clarify the association between diabetes and AOPPs and to highlight their possible role in monitoring disease burden. To address this, the present meta-analysis consolidates findings from multiple studies to assess the association between circulating AOPPs and different types of diabetes in adults.

Methods

This study was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (e.g. Supplementary Table 1) [30]. Additionally, the research protocol has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO registration umber: CRD420251275798).

Search strategy and selection of studies

By March 2025, a systematic search of e-journals, including Embase, Scopus, and PubMed, and web of sciences yielded 1339 papers, without language restrictions. A comprehensive hand search of the reference lists did not identify any additional relevant articles. The search strategy is outlined in Supplementary Table 2. The retrieved articles were imported into EndNote for further processing. After removing 780 duplicate articles and excluding 520 articles based on title and abstract screening, a total of 16 articles which were extracted to twenty individual studies were included in the final analysis, as confirmed by three independent reviewers (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study flowchart

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We selected cross-sectional and case-control studies with an observational design that investigated the relationship between circulating AOPPs and diabetes in adults. The extracted data included the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of circulating AOPPs levels in patients with diabetes compared to the control group. In the included studies, circulating AOPPs concentrations were expressed in µmol/L. Articles excluded from the analysis included case-series, case reports, experimental and in vitro studies, as well as interventional studies, review articles, letters to the editor, short communications, and abstracts from conferences or seminars. Data extraction and quality assessment of included studies

Using a standardized Excel spreadsheet, four researchers independently extracted data from the articles. The primary information gathered from each study included publication year, first author’s name, age range, country, total number of participants, study design, gender distribution, adjusted confounders, setting, methods for measuring circulating AOPPs, and the main findings of the investigation. The quality of the selected cross-sectional studies was evaluated using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) checklist [31]. The studies were assessed for risk of bias, which was categorized into three groups: a score of 0 to 4 indicated high risk, 5 to 7 signified moderate risk, and 8 to 11 represented low risk of bias [32]. According to the results of AHRQ checklist (Table 2) all of the studies had moderate risk of bias. The methodological quality of the case-control studies was evaluated using Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [33]. Each criterion received a score of 1 for “yes” responses and 0 for “no” or “unclear” responses. Based on the total score, studies were categorized as low quality (0–3), moderate quality [4–6], or high quality [7–9]. The scale assesses three major domains, including selection, comparability, and exposure/outcome. Table 3, the NOS checklist results, shows that six studies had moderate risk of bias and three studies had high quality score.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using STATA version 13. Statistical significance was set at a P-value of less than 0.05. The weighted mean difference (WMD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated to determine the unstandardized effect size using the mean and standard deviation (SD) of the variables. WMD was chosen because all studies reported AOPPs levels in the same units, enabling direct interpretation of differences between diabetic and control groups. To estimate the pooled WMD, we applied the DerSimonian and Laird random effects models. Cochran’s Q and I-squared (I²) tests were used to assess heterogeneity. If I² was less than 25%, no heterogeneity was found; if I² was between 25 and 50%, moderate heterogeneity was present, and if I² exceeded 50%, high heterogeneity was observed [34]. The random effects model was employed in cases of significant heterogeneity, indicated by a Q statistic P-value of less than 0.1 or an I² greater than 50% [35]. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity by stratifying studies according to predefined characteristics, including continent, study design, baseline AOPPs, age, sample size, gender, study quality, presence of overweight/obesity, hypertension, smoking and abnormal serum lipids. Alcohol intake was reported in only one study [36] and subgrouping was not possible. These analyses allowed us to examine whether pooled estimates differed across subgroups. We also, performed random-effects meta-regression analyses, in which study effect sizes were regressed on potential moderators (e.g., gender, geographic region, study design, baseline AOPPs, age and sample size). Although subgroup analyses were performed according to the reported presence or absence of obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia and smoking status, formal meta-regression analyses for these variables were not feasible due to incomplete reporting across the included studies and the limited number of studies available within several subgroups. Meta-regression allowed us to examine whether these study-level characteristics explained part of the between-study heterogeneity. Prediction intervals were reported alongside confidence intervals to illustrate the expected range of effects in future studies. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the pooled effect estimates. We performed leave-one-out analyses, in which each study was sequentially omitted and the meta-analysis re-run to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall results. Begg’s Funnel plots, followed by Egger’s regression asymmetry tests, were utilized to evaluate potential publication bias. Trim and fill analysis was performed when publication bias was identified.

Results

Study characteristics

In this study, a total of twenty individual research studies extracted from 16 articles were included, and their characteristics are summarized in Table 4. Since only two studies examined the association between AOPPs and impaired fasting glucose (IFG), and only one study assessed the association with impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), the available evidence was insufficient. Therefore, the meta-analysis included only patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus in two separate meta-analysis. When a single article reported separate results for independent populations (e.g., type 1 and type 2 diabetes), each population was entered as a distinct study in the meta-analysis. This approach preserved independence of effect sizes. In cases where multiple outcomes or overlapping subgroups were reported, estimates were either combined or the most relevant outcome was selected to avoid duplication.

The study by Gradinaru et al. [15], reported results separately for IFG and T2DM; therefore, only the results for T2DM were included. Similarly, the study by Korkmaz et al. [37], presented results for patients with IGT, IFG, and T2DM, but only the results for T2DM patients were considered in the meta-analysis. The study by Kalousová et al. [16] involved both T1DM and T2DM patients, so it was represented by two independent datasets in the meta-analysis. Additionally, the study by Jakus et al. [38] included two distinct results for diabetic patients with or without complications. All studies included both genders. In all studies, the measurement of AOPPs was performed using the modified spectrophotometry method first described by Witko-Sarsat et al. [39]. Among the studies, nine studies had case-control design [14, 18, 40–46] and 6 studies had cross-sectional design [5, 15, 16, 36–38, 47]. Geographically, four studies were conducted in Italy [40, 41, 43, 47], two in Turkey [18, 37], one in Prague [16], one in Cuba [42], three in India [14, 44, 45], one in Romani [15], one in Slovak Republic [38], one in Poland [46], one in Iran [5] and one in China [36]. All studies showed that circulating AOPPs were significantly higher in individuals with diabetes (both T1DM and T2DM), except for three studies, which found no significant increase in circulating AOPPs in diabetic patients [36, 40, 47]. Following re-evaluation of the included studies, the study by Koçak et al. [48] was excluded from the quantitative synthesis because its AOPP measurement units and reporting methodology were not comparable with those of the remaining studies, potentially compromising the validity of the pooled estimates.

Results of meta-analysis

The Forest plots of the association between circulating AOPPs, T1DM and T2DM are represented in Fig. 2 (a, b). The analysis revealed that circulating AOPPs were 17.68 µmol/L higher in subjects with T1DM and 24.58 µmol/L higher in subjects with T2DM compared to control subjects (WMD: 17.534; CI 4.537, 30.531; P < 0.001; I²: 99.1 and WMD: 24.678, CI: 15.361, 33.994, P < 0.001; I²: 96.8 respectively). Details of results is represented in Table 5. Considerable heterogeneity was observed across the included studies. The 95% prediction intervals crossed the null value in both analyses, indicating variability in the direction and magnitude of the association across future populations. Given the high degree of heterogeneity in the studies, we conducted subgroup analyses and meta-regression tests separately for T1DM and T2DM to explore potential sources of this variation (Tables 7 and 9). Subgroup analyses for T1DM (Table 7) showed that circulating AOPPs levels were consistently higher in patients compared with controls (overall WMD = 17.53 µmol/L, 95% CI: 4.54–30.53). Stronger associations were observed in cross-sectional studies, younger populations (< 40 years), and studies with higher baseline AOPPs levels (≥ 100 µmol/L). In contrast, case-control studies and older populations (≥ 40 years) yielded weaker or non-significant estimates. Although the magnitude of effect varied across subgroups, the overall direction remained stable, while substantial heterogeneity persisted in most categories. Subgroup analyses were additionally conducted according to the reported presence or absence of overweight/obesity, hypertension, and abnormal serum lipid profiles. Elevated circulating AOPP concentrations were generally observed across most subgroups; however, substantial heterogeneity remained in several analyses. Studies reporting overweight/obesity and hypertension showed significantly higher AOPP levels, whereas studies without these conditions demonstrated less consistent findings. Similar variability was observed across subgroups based on abnormal serum lipid status. Notably, a considerable number of studies did not report these clinical characteristics, and therefore the corresponding subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of studies and incomplete reporting. Subgroup analyses for T2DM (Table 9) demonstrated that circulating AOPPs levels were consistently higher in patients with T2DM compared to controls across all strata. Stronger associations were observed in European studies, case-control designs, younger populations (< 60 years), smaller sample sizes (< 100), and studies with higher baseline AOPPs levels (≥ 100 µmol/L). Although the magnitude of WMD varied between subgroups, the overall direction of effect remained stable, while substantial heterogeneity persisted within most categories.

Additional subgroup analyses based on reported presence or absence of overweight/obesity, hypertension, smoking, and abnormal serum lipid profiles in studies involving patients with T2DM showed significantly elevated circulating AOPP levels in most subgroups, particularly among studies reporting overweight/obesity, hypertension, smoking, and abnormal serum lipid status. However, substantial heterogeneity persisted across several subgroup analyses. Moreover, a number of studies did not report these clinical characteristics, and some subgroup categories included only a limited number of studies. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for the “not reported” subgroups and analyses with small sample sizes. Discussion

Our meta-analysis demonstrated that circulating AOPPs are significantly elevated in both T1DM and T2DM compared with non-diabetic controls, supporting the involvement of oxidative protein damage in the pathophysiology of diabetes. Despite consistent directionality across all subgroup and sensitivity analyses, the magnitude of the pooled effects varied substantially, and heterogeneity remained high. These findings indicate a robust overall association between diabetes and increased AOPPs levels, while also highlighting variability across studies that warrants cautious interpretation.

Hyperglycemia is the primary trigger for the formation of AOPPs, and their concentration increases as serum glucose levels rise, further aggravating diabetes-related complications such as diabetic nephropathy and diabetic retinopathy [17, 18, 38, 43]. Hyperglycemia also impairs the production and availability of nitric oxide (NO), which contributes to endothelial dysfunction and elevated blood pressure in diabetic patients [15]. D’Souza JM et al. [14]. proposed that circulating AOPPs may be more reliable marker for tracking the progression and duration of diabetes. Elevated AOPPs levels are believed to play a crucial role in the depletion of the -SH group (e.g., thiol), a key component in chronic diseases. The increased ratio of AOPPs to thiol, termed “thiol stress,” may significantly contribute to the development of diseases associated with AOPPs, such as diabetes. This imbalance between ROS production and the antioxidant defense system, reflected by reduced -SH group levels and increased AOPPs and lipid peroxidation markers, is a hallmark of the pathophysiology of diabetes [41, 49, 50]. Conversely, the autoimmune nature of T1DM amplifies the negative effects of AOPPs. In T1DM, oxidative stress is more strongly linked to autoimmune processes [16]. It has also been suggested that fructosamine, a precursor to AOPPs, is elevated by oxidative stress and, in turn, contributes to the generation of oxidative stress. Fructosamine, which is formed through glycosylation of proteins, disrupts the function of antioxidant enzymes, impairing their ability to bind, transport, and maintain structure. Both fructosamine and AOPPs are found to be elevated in patients with diabetes [51]. Beyond serving as markers of oxidative stress, AOPPs are recognized as pro-inflammatory molecules that contribute to the inflammation seen in diabetes. They stimulate the activation of monocytes, promote the infiltration of macrophages, increase smooth muscle cell proliferation, and ultimately lead to insulin resistance [52].

Subgroup analyses showed that the association between circulating AOPPs and diabetes was consistently positive across all strata, but its magnitude varied according to population and study characteristics. Stronger effects in younger participants and in studies with higher baseline AOPPs likely reflect more active oxidative and inflammatory pathways early in the disease course, consistent with evidence that AOPPs stimulate ROS generation through NADPH oxidase activation and promote endothelial dysfunction [53, 54]. Studies with smaller sample sizes or specific designs (e.g., cross-sectional in T1DM and case-control in T2DM) also reported larger effects, a pattern aligned with known small-study effects and selective inclusion of patients with more severe metabolic disturbances [55]. Geographic differences, particularly stronger associations in European cohorts, may relate to variations in laboratory methods, genetic background, or dietary oxidative load, as suggested by regional studies on oxidative stress biomarkers [56].

Recent evidence supports our subgroup finding that younger individuals show stronger associations between diabetes and oxidative protein damage. Mechanistic reviews indicate that early-stage diabetes is characterized by more active oxidative and inflammatory pathways, including enhanced NADPH-oxidase–driven ROS production and endothelial dysfunction, processes that directly promote the formation of AOPPs [57]. These mechanisms are particularly pronounced in younger or early-onset patients, who typically exhibit higher metabolic stress before long-term treatment or chronic complications attenuate oxidative responses. Additional reviews on oxidative stress in diabetes further emphasize that hyperglycemia-induced ROS generation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory signaling are more active in earlier disease stages, contributing to greater oxidative protein modification Together, these mechanistic insights support our observation that age modifies the magnitude of the AOPPs–diabetes association, with younger populations demonstrating more pronounced oxidative protein damage [58]. It should be noted a considerable proportion of studies lacked detailed information regarding potential oxidative stress–related confounding factors, including obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking status, and alcohol intake, which limited the ability to perform more comprehensive adjusted analyses and may have contributed to the substantial between-study heterogeneity. Therefore, the subgroup findings related to “not reported” categories should be interpreted cautiously. Also, meta-regression analyses did not fully explain the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies, as considerable residual between-study variance persisted after inclusion of the examined covariates. This finding suggests that the heterogeneity was likely influenced by multiple unmeasured methodological and clinical factors that could not be adequately evaluated using the available study-level data. Therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously.

A significant strength of this study is that, for the first time, we have provided a comprehensive overview of articles exploring the relationship between diabetes and circulating AOPPs in this systematic review and meta-analysis. One of the key contributions of this study is the potential for AOPPs to be used as a predictive biomarker for diabetic patients in clinical settings. More important than the exact magnitude is the consistent direction across studies, showing higher AOPPs levels in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, which supports biological plausibility.

However, a limitation of this study is the scarcity of available data, particularly for T1DM, which underscores the need for further research to reach definitive conclusions. Additionally, since the studies included were observational, establishing a cause-and-effect relationship is not feasible. Given the high heterogeneity, the pooled WMD should be interpreted with caution. Although elevated circulating AOPP concentrations were generally identified in patients with diabetes mellitus, considerable variability remained between studies even after subgroup and meta-regression analyses. This heterogeneity may be related to differences in patient characteristics, glycemic control, disease duration, assay methodologies, and the incomplete reporting of important oxidative stress–related factors such as obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking status, and alcohol intake. In addition, because all included studies were observational in nature, a causal relationship between circulating AOPP levels and diabetes mellitus cannot be established. Therefore, the present findings should be considered as evidence of an association rather than causation, and further well-designed prospective studies are warranted to clarify the clinical significance of AOPPs in diabetes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, circulating AOPP levels are significantly higher in individuals with diabetes compared to those without, with this elevation observed in both T1DM and T2DM. The observational in design of included studies and the observed heterogeneity limits the strength and generalizability of the pooled estimates. Therefore, causal inferences cannot be drawn. Further research and clinical trials are necessary to better understand the specific role of AOPPs in the pathogenesis of diabetes and its potential implications for treatment strategies.
