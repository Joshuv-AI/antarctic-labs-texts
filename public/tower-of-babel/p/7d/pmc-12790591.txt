AI-driven breath biopsy from a case-control study assists in the early detection of paediatric brain tumours
Authors: Shangzhewen Li, Zhengnan Cen, Yufan Chen, Yuerun Huang, Shanshan Dong, Yang Zhao, Xiang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPaediatric brain tumours (PBT) are among the deadliest childhood cancers, with delayed diagnosis often limiting early intervention. This study explores exhaled volatile metabolites as potential biomarkers for PBT, employing AI to identify diagnostic markers and develop a predictive risk assessment model.MethodsWe conducted a case–control study using untargeted volatile metabolomics on exhaled breath samples from 161 patients with primary paediatric brain tumours (PBT) and 140 non-tumour controls, analysed via a custom-built analysis platform. Machine learning, combined with univariate analysis, identified volatile organic compound (VOC) biomarkers linked to PBT. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using four supervised learning classifiers: Support Vector Machine (SVM), Naive Bayes (NB), Logistic Regression (LR), and Random Forest (RF), and model robustness was validated in an independent internal cohort of 32 participants. Incorporating PBMCs transcriptome for multi-omics analysis to construct a gene-VOC interaction network for investigating the biological origins of VOC biomarkers. An ensemble learning model was developed to enhance risk assessment by integrating clinical indicators.FindingsSignificant differences in exhaled volatile metabolomics were observed between PBT patients and controls. A panel of 12 VOC biomarkers was identified, achieving a best performed AUC of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.72–0.91) on SVM classifier, with all AUC >0.70 for all classifiers in the discovery cohort, highlighting strong diagnostic potential. Multi-omics analysis revealed VOC alterations linked to immune dysregulation, with indole and NOX components NCF1/2 emerging as key regulatory factors. An AI-assisted risk assessment model, incorporating immune-inflammatory clinical indicators, achieved high sensitivity (0.90, 95% CI: 0.77–0.90), specificity (0.86, 95% CI: 0.76–0.90), and accuracy (0.85, 95% CI: 0.80–0.89), outperforming traditional diagnostic models by nearly 20%.InterpretationThis study underscores the potential of AI-driven analysis of exhaled volatile metabolites for identifying biomarkers of paediatric brain tumours. The VOC biomarker panel, integrated with clinical indicators, offers a valuable biomarker resource for non-invasive early diagnosis and risk stratification in PBT, highlighting the potential of breath biopsy as a promising clinical tool.FundingThis work was supported by the 10.13039/501100001809National Natural Science Foundation of China (No. 22476023 and No. 22276038), the 10.13039/501100012226Fundamental Research Funds for the Central Universities (No. KLSB2023KF-06), AI for Science Foundation of Fudan University (No. FudanX24Al026), Agilent Research Gift (No. 4956) and the Foundation of Xinhua Hospital (No. GD202501).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Paediatric brain tumours (PBT) are among the deadliest childhood cancers, with early diagnosis being crucial for effective intervention. However, current diagnostic methods, including MRI and CT scans, face challenges such as high cost, limited accessibility, and exposure to radiation. Tumour biopsy-based diagnostics—such as tissue genomic profiling or cerebrospinal fluid ctDNA analysis—are invasive and unsuitable for repeated use in children. Although liquid biopsy methods, such as blood-based biomarkers, have shown promise, their non-invasiveness and capacity remain inadequate for clinical application. A new non-invasive strategy that combines high accuracy, biological relevance, and clinical practicality is urgently needed for early PBT detection. Exhaled volatilomics, an emerging non-invasive strategy, has proven effective for the early detection and monitoring of various diseases. Despite its potential, comprehensive studies exploring exhaled volatilomics, biomarker screening, and their sources in PBT patients remain limited.

Added value of this study

In the current study, we applied untargeted exhaled metabolomics and AI-driven analysis to identify a biomarker panel for paediatric brain tumours. Using a self-established cohort of 301 breath samples, we generated a panel of 12 exhaled VOCs with strong diagnostic potential and validated its performance across multiple machine learning classifiers. Our multi-omics analysis, integrating transcriptomic profiles of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and exhaled VOCs, suggested a potential biological mechanism linking immune dysregulation to VOC biomarker production, advancing the understanding of tumour–host interactions. Based on this insight, we established a non-invasive PBT risk assessment model with high accuracy by integrating immune-inflammatory markers and using an ensemble learning strategy.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings demonstrate that the combination of exhaled VOCs and AI modelling represents an efficient diagnostic strategy for paediatric brain tumours. The biomarker panel and risk model developed in this study show great promise for future clinical use-especially in children, where minimally invasive tools are essential, with advantages including non-invasive sampling, biological interpretability, and high diagnostic accuracy. This approach offers a valuable new direction for personalised, systems-level diagnostics in paediatric oncology.

Introduction

Primary paediatric brain tumours (PBT) are the most common solid tumours in children and the leading cause of cancer-related death in those under 19 years.1 Projections from the Global Cancer Observatory (GCO) (https://gco.iarc.fr/) estimate 37,608 new PBT cases and 15,687 deaths in children aged 0–19 in 2025, yielding a mortality rate of 42%. This significant public health challenge severely impacts children's quality of life and has spurred extensive research. Early cancer detection is associated with improved prognosis and potential cure.2 However, diagnosing PBT is difficult, as symptoms like headaches and visual disturbances are often nonspecific and difficult for children to articulate.3 Consequently, the average diagnostic delay is 26 weeks,4 underscoring the urgent need for timely and accurate detection methods.

Several screening modalities are available for PBT, each with distinct performance characteristics. Imaging techniques, such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are commonly used for initial detection and monitoring tumour recurrence.5 However, their high cost, radiation risks, and limited specificity6 restrict their widespread use in large-scale screening. Tumour biopsy-based strategies, such as genetic profiling through fine needle aspiration7 or sequencing of circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) from cerebrospinal fluid (CSF),8 are invasive and unsuitable for repeated sampling. These challenges have prompted the search for cost-effective, non-invasive diagnostic approaches that are accurate and accessible. Recent advancements in second-generation sequencing and mass spectrometry have led to the emergence of liquid biopsy-based methods as promising tools for PBT diagnosis. Several blood-based biomarkers, including ctDNA, immuno-inflammatory markers, RNA, antigens, and proteins,2,9, 10, 11 have been identified. However, the complex pathophysiology of PBT, involving immune dysregulation,12 oxidative stress,13 gut microbiota disruption,14 and energy metabolism,15 limits the diagnostic capacity of single biomarkers. Additionally, current sampling methods still pose minor injury to paediatric patients. Therefore, a novel, non-invasive approach integrating highly accurate, multi-parametric biomarkers is urgently needed for the early diagnosis of PBT in clinical practice.

Exhaled breath metabolomics, an emerging non-invasive strategy, has proven effective for the early detection and monitoring of various diseases.16 It contains a complex mixture of over 3000 volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which are derived from metabolites that transfer from the bloodstream into the lung lining fluid and subsequently enter the breath via alveolar gas exchange.17 Exhaled VOCs—gaseous carbon-based byproducts of both physiological and pathological metabolic processes18—offer insights into genetic, transcriptional, and translational alterations, as well as disruptions in gut microbiome metabolism and systemic responses to environmental factors. This approach fulfils the need for comprehensive screening of the complex pathological mechanisms underlying multifactorial diseases. Increasing evidence suggests that respiratory volatilomics can detect metabolic abnormalities associated with neurological disorders. For instance, in a study on biomarkers for major depressive disorder (MDD), Henning et al. found that exhaled VOCs reflected MDD-induced metabiotic imbalances in altered microbiota–gut–brain axis (MGBA).19 Laila et al. also proposed that disruptions in exhaled VOC concentrations indicated dysfunction of amino acids acting as inhibitory neurotransmitters.17 Furthermore, breath sampling is simple, clinically actionable, and repeatable, making it well-suited for large-scale disease screening and cancer progression monitoring. The non-invasive nature of the exhaled breath test makes it particularly suitable for children and critically ill patients.20

Despite its potential, research on exhaled VOCs as biomarkers has been impeded by several challenges. The lack of standardised protocols for the collection, detection, analysis, and processing of VOCs has led to variability in results, undermining stability and reproducibility.21 Additionally, existing biomarkers often lack reliability and generalisability due to small sample sizes or suboptimal study designs.22 The complex physiological processes and metabolic pathways underlying exhaled VOCs have also been a subject of ongoing debate.23 Consequently, comprehensive studies exploring exhaled VOCs, biomarker screening, and their sources in PBT patients remain limited.

To address these challenges, we developed a robust VOC analysis platform, integrating GC × GC-TOF MS/FID and a modified breath sampler to standardise exhaled breath sample detection. Through rigorous variance analysis and advanced artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms, we identified a VOC biomarker panel for PBT diagnosis and grading. Furthermore, we conducted multi-omics analysis, combining respiratory volatilomics with peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) transcriptomics to elucidate the mechanistic basis of core VOC regulation by key functional genes. Finally, we integrated this biomarker panel with immuno-inflammatory markers to create an AI-assisted, sensitive model for PBT risk assessment, highlighting the potential of respiratory volatilomics for non-invasive PBT detection.

Methods

Participants enrolment

In this study, 436 hospitalised children were recruited consecutively from Xinhua Hospital affiliated with Shanghai Jiaotong University during October 2022 to July 2024, and 301 subjects were included in retrospective cohort, with 269 for model development and 32 for initial independent verification (internal validation). Most participants were from Shanghai or the Yangtze River Delta region, with dietary preferences centred on lighter foods with minimal variation. Eligibility for inclusion in the VOC analysis cohort was retrospectively determined based on clinical evaluations, pathological confirmation, and strict eligibility criteria, ensuring that only qualified exhaled breath samples were retained for downstream analysis.

Inclusion criteria for PBT group were: (i) a newly confirmed diagnosis of paediatric brain tumours (PBT), (ii) clinical grading based on WHO classification, and (iii) signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria included a history of other malignancies, prior neoadjuvant therapy, respiratory or metabolic disorders. Ultimately, 161 PBT patients were enrolled, comprising 66 low-grade (I–II) and 95 high-grade (III–IV) cases for LBT and HBT subgroups respectively. Additionally, 140 non-brain tumours (NBT) children were recruited as control group. NBT controls underwent routine physical exams, including blood tests, MRI, and CT scans, and were considered neurologically normal. They were selected based on criteria including no history of tumours, other metabolic diseases, or respiratory diseases. Two experienced pathologists independently diagnosed subjects based on pathological examination and were blinded to VOCs analysis. Similarly, researchers performing VOCs analysis were blinded to pathological diagnosis process. Demographic and clinical data comparisons between PBT patients and NBT controls are shown in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S2a–e. The detailed recruitment process and sample allocation for respiratory volatilomics study are depicted in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Breath sampling

To minimise potential confounding factors such as medication exposure, surgical intervention, or hospital environmental influence on VOC profiles, all breath samples were collected from participants on the morning of the second day after admission, prior to any medical examination and therapeutic intervention. Exhaled breath samples were collected using a ReCIVA sampler (Owlstone Medical, Cambridge, UK) and a clean air supply (CARSPER, Owlstone Medical, Cambridge, UK). To minimise dietary and chemical interference, samples were collected between 6:00 and 8:00 am after an overnight fast, with participants instructed to avoid cleaning products prior to sampling. For each participant, 1.5 to 2 L of alveolar breath gas were collected, along with corresponding ambient samples. Target exhaled VOCs were captured in two duplicate multibed thermal desorption (TD) tubes filled with Tenax/TA and Carbograph 5TD (Markes Biomonitoring Tubes, Markes International Ltd, UK), aiming to enhance data reliability and prevent sample loss due to potential technical issues.

Exhaled volatilomics profiling

All participants’ exhaled breath and paired ambient air samples underwent pre-treatment and were further analysed using the TD-GC × GC-TOF MS/FID system. The sample preparation and VOCs signal acquisition were performed in accordance with our previously published method.24 Briefly, after sampling, all thermal desorption (TD) tubes were dry purged with nitrogen at a flow rate of 100 mL .min−1 (purity ≥99.999%) for 5 min, loaded with 1 μL of a mixed internal standard (Dr. Ehrenstorfer, DRE-YA09000011ME, Germany) containing bromochloromethane (20 μg .mL−1), chlorobenzene-d5 (20 μg .mL−1), and 1,4-Dichlorobenzene-d4 (20 μg .mL−1), sealed with brass caps, and stored at 4 °C until for instrumental analysis. The TD-GC × GC-TOF MS/FID system consisted of a UNITY 2 automated TD unit (Markes International, UK) coupled to comprehensive two-dimensional gas chromatography (GC × GC, 7890A, Agilent, USA) with time-of-flight mass spectrometry (TOF MS, GGT 0620, HEXIN, China)/flame ionisation detector (FID, Agilent, USA). Each TD tube was desorbed at 300 °C for 5 min, and the released VOCs were cold trapped at 30 °C in a hydrophobic trap (Markes International, UK) before being introduced into the gas chromatograph system in the splitless mode via a transfer line maintained at 180 °C. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a moderately polar DB-624 column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d. × 1.4 μm, Agilent, USA) as the primary (1D) column and a polar DB-WAX column (5 m × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm, Agilent, USA) as the secondary (2D) column. Helium was used as the carrier gas with constant flow rates of 0.6 mL .min−1 and 23.67 mL .min−1 for the 1D and 2D columns, respectively, and a modulation period was set to 3 s (2.799 s for load and 0.201 s for injection). The oven temperature program was initialised at 50 °C for 5 min, ramped to 140 °C at 6 °C.min−1, and then to 220 °C (held for 30 min) at 10 °C.min−1. The two columns shared the same temperature program. Synchronised recording of FID and MS spectra was achieved using a three-way purged splitter mounted after the 2D column, directing approximately one-tenth of the flow to the TOF MS and the remaining nine-tenths to the FID. The FID was operated at 280 °C with hydrogen, air, and makeup nitrogen flows of 40.0, 350, and 2.0 mL .min−1, respectively, at an acquisition rate of 200 Hz. The TOF MS was operated in electron impact ionisation mode (70 eV) with the ion source and transfer line at 230 °C and 250 °C, respectively, scanning m/z 34–350 at 100 Hz with a 1660 V multi-channel plate voltage. In addition to the previously validated stability of our analytical platform,24 multiple quality control measures were implemented to ensure analytical consistency, including internal standard normalisation to minimise signal fluctuation, periodic analysis of TD tubes containing only internal standards to monitor retention time drift and signal response stability, alternating analysis of case and control samples to minimise group-specific temporal bias, and routine instrument maintenance.

Obtained MS and FID files were further analysed using GC Image GC × GC Edition software (version 2022, SepSolve Analytical, UK) for VOCs identification and quantification, respectively. After baseline correction and peak picking, MS files were used for VOCs identification by multiple strategies: (i) for 33 VOCs with available commercial standards, both retention times and mass spectra were matched; (ii) for VOCs without reference standards, tentative identification was based on NIST library searching (mainlib and replib). Peaks with a similarity index >600 were selected for molecule name assignment, and further confirmed by matching their experimental retention indices (RI), calculated from n-alkane (C3–C20) standards, with reported RI values in the literature and the NIST database; compounds not meeting these criteria were considered absent. This procedure yielded 99 reproducibly detected VOCs across all samples. After that, an alignment template was established based on the retention times of the identified VOCs to correct for potential analytical drift, with maximum tolerances set at 0.3 min for the first dimension and 0.03 s for the second dimension.

For quantification, sample quality was firstly evaluated based on two criteria: (1) absence of evident methanol tailing (a marker of improper sample pre-treatment); (2) sufficient alignment with the established VOC template (matching rate ≥70%). Only samples passing quality criteria were retained for analysis. Qualified FID files underwent baseline correction, peak alignment and peak picking. Semi-quantitative analysis was performed using internal standard-normalised response ratios (chlorobenzene-d5 as reference), further normalised by the volume of collected end-tidal breath to address variability in sampling volume. Notably, ambient VOC values were not subtracted from the corresponding exhaled VOC data considering that sampling system selectively capture alveolar breath enriched in endogenous VOCs and supply clean inhaled air, combined with the fact that all participants were sampled under uniform hospital conditions. Missing values in the VOCs dataset were imputed using the k-nearest neighbour (KNN) algorithm. No additional normalisation or scaling was applied aiming to preserve the integrity of original metabolite distributions. For VOCs data of each participant's duplicate breath samples, if the coefficient of variation was acceptable (CV < 20%), the mean value was used to construct final exhaled volatilomics dataset, otherwise only the sample with the higher signal quality was retained. The median values and interquartile ranges (IQRs) of the final 99 semi-quantified VOCs are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Evaluation and correction of potential confounders

To assess the impact of ambient air and cohort-related variance (CSV) on VOC composition, multiple strategies were implemented. Given that most VOCs did not follow a normal distribution, we selected non-parametric statistical methods (Spearman correlation analysis, Wilcoxon rank sum test) for confounder evaluation, with significance thresholds adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05 considered statistically significant).

For environmental interference, we first conducted a partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) using the ropls R package to distinguish breath samples from ambient air. Subsequently, for VOCs with a variable importance in projection (VIP) score greater than 1, we assessed the breath-to-air concentration ratio and the Spearman correlation coefficients between breath and ambient concentrations to estimate the independence of breath VOCs from environmental background. To address CSV, we first applied Wilcoxon rank sum test (for sex) and Spearman correlation analyses (for BMI and BSA) to identify relevant covariates. Subsequently, a generalised linear model (GLM) was then employed to adjust for these confounders and to examine potential interaction effects between disease status and cohort variables. Additionally, the reliability of the multivariable model was assessed using the Events Per Variable (EPV) criterion, which recommends a minimum of 10–20 samples per variable. With 301 participants and four variables (one main variable and three covariates), the EPV was 75.25, well above the recommended threshold for robust model estimation. VOCs significantly affected by CSV were excluded from subsequent biomarker selection.

AI-assisted integration of candidate exhaled VOCs biomarker panel

To generate a biomarker panel of exhaled VOCs reflecting metabolic abnormalities in PBT patients, we first used a kernel-weighted based outlier-robust volcano plot to calculate the fold change and p-value for identifying differential VOCs in PBT patients’ exhaled breath, utilising open-source software (https://github.com/nishithkumarpaul/Rvolcano) in R (Version 4.2.1).25 Feature selection was then performed using the Boruta R package (v8.0.0) based on VOCs with statistically significant differences (FDR-padj <0.05, Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini-Hochberg procedure) identified by RVP, and no clinical variables were included as candidate features. Boruta identifies robust predictors by comparing the importance of real features to shadow features generated via permutation.26

For downstream machine learning modelling, the dataset was randomly partitioned using a 7:3 train-test split strategy, stratified by clinical outcome to preserve class balance. To minimise the impact of data partitioning bias, the train-test splitting and model training were repeated across five different random seeds. Four supervised machine learning algorithms were trained on the selected features: Logistic Regression (LR, glm), Random Forest (RF, randomForest), Naive Bayes (NB), and Support Vector Machine (SVM, all via e1071 R package). Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and area under the curve (AUC) metrics computed with the pROC package. To validate model generalisability, an independent cohort (n = 32) was further used for internal validation.

Isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells

Blood samples were collected from participants after an overnight fast using 5 mL Vacutainer EDTA tubes. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using Ficoll–Paque Premium density gradient media (Cytiva, 17544203, Washington, USA). Blood was centrifuged at 1500×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was stored at −80 °C. The remaining pellet was resuspended in an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Absin, abs962, Shanghai, China). The blood-PBS mixture was carefully layered over Ficoll (5 mL Ficoll for 4 mL blood + PBS) in a 15-mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 800×g for 20 min at 20 °C with acceleration and deceleration set to 3 r/min. PBMCs and platelets were collected from the Ficoll-plasma interface and washed by centrifugation at 250×g for 10 min in PBS. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer (Absin, abs9101, Shanghai, China) to purify the PBMCs. After a second wash, total RNA was extracted from the PBMCs using TRIzol (Absin, abs60154, Shanghai, China) and stored at −80 °C.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from PBMCs of 14 PBT and 9 NBT participants, and its integrity was evaluated using the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). For RNA sequencing, 50 ng of total RNA per sample was sent to Novogene (Beijing, China), where standard procedures including library construction, sequencing, raw data quality control (QC), genome alignment, and gene expression quantification (via featureCounts) were performed.

Bioinformatics analysis

After obtaining the transcriptomic data, differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 R package (v1.20.0), with p values adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure; genes with adjusted p ≤ 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes were then conducted using the clusterProfiler R package, with significant terms defined as corrected p < 0.05. We further curated gene sets for enriched MAPK signalling pathway, GABAergic synapse, and Glycerophospholipid metabolism (Supplementary Data 3) to perform single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (GSVA, Bioconductor package v1.30.033), which calculated the enrichment scores of the specific biological pathways for each sample. Additionally, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted with the WGCNA R package to identify gene modules correlated with disease status and GSVA-derived traits, with modules were defined with a minimum size of 50 genes. Modules significantly associated with traits (p < 0.05, correlation ≥0.5) were retained, and functional genes were filtered based on gene significance (GS ≥ 0.8) and module membership (MM ≥ 0.5). To further explore immune characteristics, CIBERSORT was applied to normalised transcriptomic data to infer the absolute proportions of 22 infiltrating immune cell types.

For multi-omics integration, sparse generalised canonical correlation discriminant analysis (DIABLO) implemented in the mixOmics R package was first applied to integrate multiple data types across the same biological samples and jointly identify key omics features across multiple datasets. VOC-gene interaction networks were further constructed using MetaboAnalyst and visualised in Cytoscape (v3.7.1) to identify core VOCs and their related functional genes.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry and evaluation quantification

IHC was performed to assess ki-67, p53 and CD163 expression in brain tissues. Four-micron sections of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) surgical specimens were stained. The following primary antibodies of immunohistochemistry were used: CD163 (Servicebio, GB115709, Wuhan, China), ki67 (Abcam Cat# ab15580, RRID: AB_443209), and P53 (Abcam Cat# ab131442, RRID: AB_11155283). All antibodies were validated by the manufacturers. Digital images were acquired and the integrated optical density (IOD) of semi-quantitative analysis was performed. All the representative images were observed at 100× or 200× magnification. Staining results and tumour content were reviewed by a board-certified neuropathologist (AL).

Establishment of a PBT risk evaluating system using ensemble model

We developed an ensemble machine learning approach to enhance the accuracy and robustness of a PBT risk assessment system.27 Exhaled VOCs and immuno-inflammatory markers data from the discovery cohort (n = 269) were used to construct the system. All included features were pre-selected based on their prior relevance: VOC biomarkers were derived from our previously validated diagnostic panel, while immune-inflammatory markers were selected based on significant differences via by Wilcoxon rank sum test. No additional clinical variables (e.g., age, sex) were incorporated into the model. Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Naive Bayes (NB) algorithms served as baseline models due to their effectiveness in validating the VOC biomarker panel. Both models were individually trained to generate PBT risk scores for each participant. An ensemble stacking model was then implemented, using the predictions of the baseline models as input features for a meta-classifier logistic regression (LR) model. The resulting risk assessment formula (Eq 1) was:Eq1RiskScorePBT=7.901×ScoreSVM−1.527×ScoreNB−3.422

The task was considered as a binary classification problem, with the model outputting the score of a sample being identified as PBT. The optimal threshold for high- and low-risk classification was determined using the maximum Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Confusion matrices were computed to evaluate the model's stratification performance, demonstrating its potential for precise PBT risk prediction. In addition, to ensure the methodological rigour and validity of our PBT risk evaluating system establishment using machine learning algorithms, we evaluated whether the available sample size was adequate to support reliable machine learning model development using the R package pmsampsize (version 1.2.0), developed by Riley et al. for minimum sample size estimation in clinical prediction modelling.28 We estimate the minimum required sample size under the following parameters: 15 candidate predictors, an outcome prevalence of 53.9%, an anticipated Cox-Snell R2 of 0.30–0.35, and a target shrinkage factor of ≥0.9.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v.26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), GraphPad Prism v.9.5 (GraphPad Software), and RStudio v.4.2.3 (RStudio Inc., Boston, MA, USA). Continuous variables were summarised as median with interquartile ranges. For multi-group comparisons with normally distributed data, a two-way ANOVA was applied. For non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon rank sum test and Kruskal–Wallis test was used for two-group and multi-group comparisons, respectively. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages, with comparisons made using the chi-squared test. To control the risk of false positive results due to multiple comparisons, several strategies were utilised: for analyses involving multiple groups, the two-way ANOVA was followed by Sidak-adjusted post-hoc pairwise comparisons, while the overall significance of Kruskal–Wallis test was followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure to control false discovery rate (FDR); for analyses involving multiple simultaneous tests across features, such as differential analysis of gene and VOCs, the BH procedure for FDR correction was also applied. In contrast, for cases with fewer comparisons (e.g., two-group comparison for demographic and clinical variables), p-values were reported without adjustment. Statistical significance was defined as p/FDR-padj <0.05. Dimensionality reduction and clustering of VOC and transcriptome data were conducted using Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) and principal component analysis (PCA) in RStudio, respectively. For PCA, low-expression genes were filtered, and variance-stabilising transformation (VST) was applied using DESeq2 to normalise the transcriptome counts prior to PCA.

For machine learning–based analyses, differentially expressed VOCs were initially identified using a robust volcano plot (FDR-padj <0.05, Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini-Hochberg procedure), followed by feature selection via the Boruta algorithm to identify the most informative VOCs from the discovery cohort. Selected features were used to build multivariable models using four supervised classifiers (SVM, NB, LR, RF). Immune-inflammatory markers were additionally selected based on significant group differences identified through Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. These markers, alone or in combination with VOCs, were used to build multivariable models through an ensemble stacking strategy. No additional clinical variables (e.g., age, sex) were included in model construction. All machine learning models were developed using a stratified 7:3 train-test split repeated across five random seeds to minimise data partitioning bias. Generalisability was assessed using an internal validation cohort (n = 32). Model performance was evaluated using consistent metrics including receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy, computed using the pROC package in R. The optimal classification threshold was determined by the Youden index.

Results

Clinical characteristics and study workflow

In this study, we applied a multi-omics approach integrating respiratory volatilomics and blood transcriptomics to analyse alterations in exhaled VOCs in PBT patients. Of the 436 recruited participants, 301 provided qualified breath samples collected following the standardised procedures described in Methods. These participants were further included in exhaled volatilomics cohort, which comprised PBT patients with tumours in nine brain regions and matched controls and was further divided into discovery and independent verification cohorts. All exhaled breath samples underwent non-targeted metabolomics analysis. Additionally, a transcriptomics sub-cohort (n = 23 cases) was collected from patients who also provided qualified breath samples, ensuring individual-level matching for subsequent multi-omics integration, with balanced distribution across demographic and clinical characteristics (Supplementary Table S2). Detailed sample collection strategies for both volatilomics and transcriptomics cohorts are illustrated in Fig. 1a and reasons for exclusion (n = 135) are summarised in Supplementary Fig. S1. The cohort included 66 low-grade (WHO I–II) PBT patients, 95 high-grade (WHO III–IV) PBT patients, and 140 controls, with median ages of 8.0 (5.3–10.0), 7.5 (6.0–10.3), and 7.0 (6.0–10.0) years, respectively. To standardise bioinformatic analyses, we identified 95 VOCs reliably extracted across the entire cohort using a custom pipeline for exhaled breath samples. These VOCs included alkanes, ketones, aldehydes, carboxylic acids, esters, aromatic hydrocarbons, alcohols, alkenes, and seven other classes (Supplementary Fig. S2f), forming the basis for respiratory volatilomic analysis. Meanwhile, raw RNA sequencing data were processed with fastp for quality control and featureCounts for quantification. Based on these two datasets, our objectives were to (1) identify a robust exhaled VOC biomarker panel for PBT diagnosis using comprehensive statistical analyses and machine learning; and (2) explore biological mechanisms associated with the VOC biomarkers production via transcriptomic variation analysis in PBT patients. After excluding unqualified samples, we performed differential analyses and feature selection, identifying a PBT diagnostic panel consisting of 12 VOCs, which was potentially regulated by 745 functional genes. From participants with both volatilomic and transcriptomic data (n = 23), we conducted VOC-gene network analysis to investigate mechanisms underlying exhaled VOC biomarkers, providing initial biological relevance for panel accuracy and reliability (Fig. 1b). Finally, we developed a promising PBT risk assessment system, integrating the VOC biomarker panel with routine immuno-inflammatory indicators, highlighting its advantages over existing tumour risk metrics and potential clinical applications (Fig. 1c).

Exhaled Volatilomic Profiles Reflect PBT Severity

The objective of exhaled VOC analysis was to identify biomarkers exhibiting consistent alterations across PBT stages. Prior to analysis, we evaluated the impact of cohort-related variations (CRV) and environmental factors on the composition of exhaled VOCs, considering demographic differences following the exclusion of unqualified samples and variations in VOC composition between the environment and exhaled breath. Factors such as sex, body mass index (BMI), and environmental composition were assessed for their impact on VOCs. Our analysis showed no significant influence of these variables on VOC composition, except for bromochloromethane, 1-butanol, 3-methylundecane, and allylcyclohexane (Supplementary Fig. S3, Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Data 2). Therefore, subsequent analysis based on a standardised pipeline focused only on the rest impervious VOCs across the entire cohort, minimising confounding effects and enabling stable downstream volatilomic investigations.

To uncover robust VOC biomarkers for PBT diagnosis, the study encompassed patients with diverse tumour types and WHO I-IV grades, avoiding bias toward specific cancer types or severities. BMI, muscle mass markers, and immunohistochemistry analyses for cancer markers revealed progressive body weight loss, muscle wasting and abnormal cell proliferation in patients transitioning from low-grade (LBT) to high-grade brain tumours (HBT), indicating an increasing of tumour severity (Fig. 2a and b). Consistent with clinical findings, albumin, a liver-derived negative acute phase protein (APP), was significantly reduced in LBT and HBT patients, correlating with advanced tumour stages and poor prognosis29 (Supplementary Fig. S4a–f). These changes illustrated a gradient of symptom severity from non-brain tumour controls (NBT) to LBT and HBT groups. AI-driven identification of exhaled VOCs biomarker panel

We conducted an AI-driven study to develop and validate a VOCs biomarker panel for PBT diagnosis and grading, leveraging differential VOCs identified previously (Fig. 3a). The study comprised three key components: (1) AI-based biomarker panel generation, (2) panel efficacy validation for tumour diagnosis, and (3) derivation of panel grading applicability. Breath samples from a discovery cohort (n = 269) were randomly split into training and test sets in a 7:3 ratio, while an independent verification cohort was recruited to assess potential overfitting. To rank the importance of VOC features, we employed the Boruta algorithm, generating a feature importance list after 100 iterations using random forest scores. Four machine learning classifiers—Support Vector Machine (SVM), Logistic Regression (LR), Naive Bayes (NB), and Random Forest (RF)— were trained using the 12 VOC biomarkers from the training set. Their diagnostic and grading performances were evaluated on the withheld test set, and generalisability was further validated using the verification set (Fig. 3c). All models exhibited area under the curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.67 to 0.93 (Supplementary Fig. S6a–d), with Random Forest excluded due to the risk of overfitting (Supplementary Fig. S7). Among these classifiers, SVM demonstrated the highest AUC for both diagnosis (AUC = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.72–0.91) and grading (AUC = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.73–0.96) in the test set (Fig. 3d and e). The SVM model's performance in the test set significantly outperformed other classifiers and aligned with results from 5-fold cross-validation in the training set (Supplementary Fig. S6a–d), indicating strong classifier integrity. At the optimal threshold defined by the highest Youden's Index, the SVM classifier achieved a sensitivity of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.62–0.89) at a specificities of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.65–0.92) for diagnosis, and a sensitivity of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.58–0.91) at a specificities of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.64–0.98) for grading in the test set, underscoring the panel's accuracy, with overall accuracies of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.69–0.87) for diagnosis and 0.82 (95% CI: 0.67–0.92) for grading. Additionally, when applied to an independent verification cohort of 32 participants from diverse regions, the SVM classifier yielded an AUC of 0.7 for both diagnosis (AUC = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.60–0.93) and grading (AUC = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.61–0.92) (Fig. 3d and e). This performance further supported the robustness and generalisability of our VOC biomarker panel to a certain extent.

Tracing exhaled biomarkers through transcriptomic changes

Given that peripheral blood cells share over 80% of their transcriptome with brain tissue,30 and serve as a source of circulating metabolites, some of which are volatile can be exhaled through alveolar-capillary gas exchange, we analysed the transcriptome of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from PBT patients to explored how brain tumours affect exhaled VOCs through gene expression modulation. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a trend of separation between PBT patients and controls (PC1 = 96.6%, PC2 = 1.0%) (Fig. 4a), indicating a tumour-driven remodelling of the transcriptome. Differential gene expression analysis identified 1639 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between PBT and control groups, highlighting the tumour's effect on gene regulation (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Data 4). Both respiratory volatilomics and blood transcriptomics were significantly altered in PBT patients, suggesting that upstream genes may regulate changes in exhaled VOC concentrations. Gene Ontology (GO) overrepresentation analysis of DEGs revealed enrichment in biological processes related to inflammatory and immune responses (FDR-padj <0.1, hypergeometric test with Benjamini-Hochberg procedure) (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Data 5). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further indicated that pathways involved in immunosuppressive cell activation and cytokine production were upregulated in PBT, while T-cell immunity and oxidative stress resistance were downregulated (Supplementary Fig. S8). These findings suggested that tumorigenesis in PBT remodels the PBMC immune response, supporting the ‘cold’ nature of brain tumours.31 Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis on DEGs identified key pathways influenced by the tumour were related to nervous system (e.g., GABAergic synapses), signal transduction (e.g., MAPK signalling pathway), and metabolism (e.g., glycerophospholipid metabolism) (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Data 6). As previous studies have linked dysfunctions of PI3K, MAPK, and p53 signalling in tumour tissue to PBT,32,33 our observations suggested some common tumour-related alterations can be detected in the blood, implying variation in genes of PBMC could indirectly reflect tumour-driven alterations in the patient's physiological state. Endogenous volatile metabolites produced by gene-regulated processes in PBMCs likely enter the bloodstream, reach the lung lining fluid, and are exhaled through alveolar gas exchange. Immunosuppression-driven gene changes affect exhaled volatilomics

We conducted a multi-omics analysis integrating data from 12 VOC biomarkers and 745 functional genes derived from the same biological samples to construct a comprehensive profile of PBT. This approach enabled us to investigate the regulatory influence of functional genes on exhaled VOCs in the context of PBT. The DIABLO model revealed significant differences between PBT patients and controls in both volatilomics and transcriptomics, with the two datasets showing a high correlation (Supplementary Fig. S10a and b). This indicates that changes in gene expression and VOCs are closely linked, suggesting a potential interaction between functional genes and VOC biomarkers. Further integration using MetaboAnalyst identified core genes and VOCs at regulatory centres, revealing a negative correlation between genes such as NCF1, NCF2, ALOX5AP, and indole, a key VOC biomarker (Fig. 5a and b). This was consistent with reduced indole concentrations and increased expression of NCF1, NCF2, and ALOX5AP observed in differential analysis of both volatilomics and transcriptomics. These genes, enriched in neutrophils, activate myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), contributing to an immunosuppressive tumour environment.36,37 The reduced indole concentration in PBT patients may be due to the regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by NCF1/2, components of NADPH oxidase.37,38 These components disrupt the indole pathway by promoting the kynurenine pathway in tryptophan metabolism, ultimately reducing indole production. The upregulation of the kynurenine pathway, a hallmark of immunosuppression,39 further supports this finding. To further explore the immune disruption caused by PBT, we analysed immune cell composition and performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of tumour tissues. Routine blood tests revealed significant changes in neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, with neutrophil numbers increased and lymphocyte percentages decreased in PBT patients compared to NBT patients (Fig. 5d). These changes suggest that the tumour disrupts the immune environment in PBT patients. Additionally, the neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was dramatically elevated in PBT patients (Fig. 5d), indicating a weakened systemic immune response, as NLR upregulation is associated with impaired anti-tumour immunity.40 Immune cell profiling using Cibersort further revealed a reduction in natural killer (NK) cells in PBT patients, suggesting NK cell depletion contributes to the reduced lymphocyte count in peripheral blood (Fig. 5c and d, Supplementary Fig. S10c). These abnormalities signal immune evasion in PBT, confirming that the systemic immune system in these patients is suppressed. CD163 IHC staining of tumour tissues was then performed to investigate the tumour microenvironment (TME) immune status (Fig. 5e). CD163 expression in monocytic cells promotes inflammation and impairs T cell function.41 We observed increasing CD163 positivity with tumour progression (Fig. 5f), suggesting a potential immunosuppressive TME in PBT. These findings indicate that the systemic immune response and TME immune status in PBT patients are both significantly disrupted by the tumour. This disruption likely results in the upregulation of genes that activate immunosuppressive pathways in PBMCs, leading to the production of molecules that alter exhaled VOC profiles. Together, these results underscore the role of immune dysregulation in driving tumour-related changes in gene expression and VOC production in PBT.

Combining VOCs and immune markers to improve PBT risk assessment

Building on our previous findings that PBT induces the alternation in the systemic immune environment and that exhaled VOCs can detect PBT, we proposed combining VOC and immune-inflammation markers to enhance PBT risk assessment. This approach was tested using data from 269 participants with blood tests and exhaled volatilomics profiles (Fig. 6a). Immune-inflammation markers, including the neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and lymphocyte-monocyte ratio (LMR), were compared across groups correctly and incorrectly classified by VOC-based diagnoses. In VOC-misclassified groups, significant differences in immune-inflammation metrics were observed between actual PBT patients and NBT controls, aligning with immunosuppressive phenotypes (Fig. 6b). In contrast, correctly classified groups showed less pronounced differences in these metrics (Fig. 6c). To evaluate the effectiveness of combined markers, we applied a stacking-based ensemble algorithm for PBT risk stratification (Fig. 6a). Using immuno-inflammatory markers, VOC markers, and combined markers, we developed PBT risk scoring models and plotted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. In the training set, area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.70 (95% CI: 0.62–0.76, immune-inflammatory markers), 0.93 (95% CI: 0.89–0.97, VOC markers), and 0.92 (95% CI: 0.87–0.96, combined markers). Although VOC and combined markers showed comparable AUCs, the combined markers demonstrated superior specificity (89.5% vs. 87.2%) (Fig. 6d and e). Moreover, applying the same approach to the test set yielded consistent results, with the combined markers showing outperformance and a stable risk threshold (AUC = 0.81 (95% CI: 0.72–0.91), threshold = 0.184; Fig. 6f and g). Confusion matrices generated for the entire discovery cohort, based on training set risk thresholds optimised for Youden's index, confirmed that combined markers reduced false positives and false negatives compared to individual marker classes (Fig. 6h, Supplementary Data 9). These results highlight that while both exhaled VOCs and immune-inflammation markers are effective for PBT risk assessment (Fig. 6i), their combination offers superior alignment with clinical diagnoses. Furthermore, the reliability of the overall machine learning results at the current cohort size was supported by retrospective sample size estimation, which confirmed that the discovery cohort (n = 269; 145 events) met the recommended thresholds for initial model development (230–270 participants with at least 110–130 events), supporting the adequacy of the sample size for preliminary development of a clinical risk stratification model. This integrated approach provides a more stable and clinically relevant framework for assessing PBT risk.

Discussion

Our machine learning model aims to assist in the early detection of PBT by analysing VOCs in exhaled breath. The VOCs biomarker panel was developed using exhaled volatilomics data from 301 paediatric participants: 269 in a discovery cohort and 32 in an independent internal validation cohort. Using a two-step feature selection approach, we identified 12 VOCs most strongly associated with PBT. The robustness of the panel was further validated using four machine learning algorithms with cross-validation (Fig. 3c), achieving AUCs of 0.70–0.81 for diagnosis and 0.81–0.84 for grading, with approximately 0.70 AUC in the independent validation set. To explore initial biological relevance, we integrated transcriptome data from PBMCs, which highlighted indole as the critical predictive feature potentially linked to immune dysregulation. Thus, we combined the 12 VOCs with immune-inflammatory markers to establish a risk assessment model, which achieved an accuracy of 85% and an AUC of 0.92 in the discovery cohort. A workflow diagram (Fig. 6a) illustrates how the model processes data and generates predictions. This non-invasive approach has the potential in being integrated into clinical practice as a frontline screening tool in paediatric neurosurgery outpatient clinics, supporting clinicians in triaging patients and enhancing decision-making. Future steps include evaluation of model consistency across clinical subgroups, external validation across multiple centres, and engineering refinements for clinical point-of-care testing. We envision this AI-driven breath biopsy as a complement to existing diagnostic methods, enhancing early detection of PBT.

This study provides a preliminary evaluation of respiratory volatilomics for PBT risk assessment. Significant alterations in exhaled VOCs profiles were identified between PBT patients and non-tumour controls, and integrative analyses with transcriptomics highlighted potential immune-related mechanisms. These findings underscore the promise of exhaled VOCs as candidate biomarkers while also offering insights into disease-associated metabolic and immunological dysregulation.

The clinical application of exhaled VOCs for non-invasive PBT diagnosis is limited by challenges such as inefficient sample collection, low VOC concentrations, and external contamination. To overcome these challenges, we firstly compared several commonly used breath collection techniques for deciding the more advantageous one. Polymer sampling bags, such as Tedlar,42 were widely used in early studies due to their low cost and ease of use, but have been gradually replaced due to risks of introducing exogenous compounds. More recently, thermal desorption (TD) tube-based sampling using devices like ReCIVA and Mistral has been widely adopted in clinical studies. Based on Di Gilio's study on respiratory sampling techniques,43 ReCIVA has been shown to preferentially capture end-tidal breath, which is enriched in endogenous VOCs. Previous studies have demonstrated its applicability in gastrointestinal, pulmonary, and cardiovascular conditions. For instance, Altomare et al. and Woodfield et al. both achieved over 80% sensitivity in colorectal cancer detection using ReCIVA and GC–MS,44,45 and Ibrahim et al.46 developed a breath-based multi-marker score for cardiopulmonary disease screening. While no prior studies have applied this approach to paediatric brain tumours (PBT), its proven utility in other clinical settings supports its suitability for our study. Building on this, we optimised the sampling protocol, extending the collection time to 15–20 min and collecting up to 2 L of breath per subject, thereby increasing VOC enrichment and enabling comprehensive VOC profiling. This method also ensures that the air inhaled during collection remains clean, minimising external contamination. OPLS-DA and correlation analyses demonstrated minimal ambient VOC interference, confirming the efficacy of our approach, which aligns with Di Gilio's study on respiratory sampling techniques.43 This advantage further supports our rationale for not directly subtracting ambient VOC concentrations during data processing. To date, no gold-standard method exists for accurately eliminating exogenous VOCs from exhaled breath data. Existing methods often rely on complex physiological models (e.g., compartment models) that require parameters such as blood: air partition coefficients, metabolic clearance rates, and other respiratory dynamics,47 most of which remain poorly characterised for the majority of VOCs. Therefore, given the strong performance of our sampling system in minimising environmental interference and the standardised hospital conditions under which all participants were sampled, our non-subtraction strategy is methodologically justified. During cohort exclusion, we inadvertently introduced cohort-related variance (CSV) based on sex, BMI, and BSA, which can influence VOC distribution in exhaled breath.48 Despite the calibration, our analysis showed significant correlations between CSV factors and specific VOCs such as bromochloromethane, 1-butanol, 3-methylundecane, and allylcyclohexane. Previous studies have linked bromochloromethane with high BMI,49 and 1-butanol with obesity,50,51 which is further related to BMI. The relationship between 3-methylundecane and BMI has also been well documented.52 While no studies have connected allylcyclohexane with physiological factors, its concentration did not differ significantly between PBT patients and controls (Padjusted = 0.452, Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini–Hochberg procedure), suggesting it is not a potential PBT biomarker. To ensure the accuracy of our respiratory volatilomics analysis, VOCs affected by confounding factors were excluded from further investigation. These measures address potential limitations and enhance the reliability of exhaled VOCs for clinical applications, ensuring reliable results for future screening programs based on exhaled biomarkers.

Subsequently, we employed a two-stage strategy, integrating enhanced variance analysis with AI techniques, to develop an exhaled VOC biomarker panel for PBT diagnosis. In the first stage, we introduced a kernel-weighted outlier-robust volcano plot (RVP)25 to identify differential VOCs from noisy exhaled VOC datasets. Traditional parametric and non-parametric methods, which rely on p-values for statistical significance, may misclassify metabolites due to irregular concentrations or discard important ones during preprocessing. In contrast, the RVP integrates a kernel weight function with the classical volcano plot, assigning smaller weights to outlying observations and ensuring robust variance analysis.25 A comparison between RVP and classical volcano plots demonstrated that the kernel weighting effectively highlighted differentially expressed VOCs obscured by outlier fluctuations, validating its utility in VOC identification (Supplementary Fig. S11). In the second stage, we applied AI-based feature selection using the Boruta algorithm to refine the VOC panel. Unlike typical feature selection algorithms, Boruta focuses on identifying features with the highest relevance to the target variable,26 ensuring the selection of the most predictive VOCs for PBT. A biomarker panel consisted of 12 key features were identified and further evaluated using four machine learning algorithms on discovery cohort (n = 269) and validated on an internal validation cohort (n = 32), achieving AUCs of 0.70–0.81 for diagnosis and 0.81–0.84 for grading in the test set, and approximately 0.70 in the validation set. These findings suggest that our approach enables the biomarker panel to retain superior diagnostic and grading potential compared to a similar biomarker panel for gastric cancer53 across classifiers, although the reduced performance in the internal cohort underscores the need for larger-scale validation. This improvement is likely due to our consideration of the distributional characteristics of exhaled VOCs data and the compatibility of the biomarker panel with multiple classifiers. In summary, our strategy supports the identification of a relatively stable VOC biomarker panel, partially addressing limitations of marker applicability across multiple models, and provides a promising resource for non-invasive PBT diagnosis using exhaled VOCs.

Following, we explored the origins and production mechanisms of exhaled biomarkers relevant to PBT detection. Previous studies indicate that peripheral blood cells share over 80% of their transcriptome with brain tissue, particularly monocytes, which correlate with brain disruptions.30 Qi et al. observed similar pathway alterations in peripheral blood of PBT patients as in brain tumours.54 In addition, PBMCs are metabolically active and contribute to the generation of circulating metabolites, including volatile compounds. These volatile substances can diffuse across the alveolar-capillary barrier and subsequently be exhaled, as supported by established respiratory kinetics. Building on these insights, we integrated transcriptomic data from PBMCs with exhaled VOC biomarkers to explore their biological interpretability. The biomarker panel included three alkanes, three esters, two aldehydes, one ketone, one terpene, one organosulfide, and indole. Pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs identified oxidative stress, immune modulation, and lipid and fatty acid metabolism as key tumour-related pathways generating VOCs in PBMCs of PBT patients. Consistent with these findings, alkanes are altered in the exhaled breath of cancer patients and are known by-products of oxidative stress. The elevated ketones in cancer patients correlate with enhanced fatty acid oxidation, while lipid metabolism may contribute to the dysregulation of aldehyde and ester VOCs.55, 56, 57 Furthermore, we suggest that ingestion of drugs that act as inflammatory immunomodulators may drive the presence of d-limonene58 and methanesulfonyl chloride59 in exhaled biomarkers. These integrative analyses suggest that the majority of VOCs in our panel reflect broad cancer-associated metabolic alterations. This is partly attributable to the nature of exhaled VOCs as terminal or secondary metabolic byproducts, which complicates precise tracing of their physiological origins.21 Furthermore, unlike gastrointestinal or pulmonary diseases, the central nervous system (CNS) where PBT arises has a relatively weak physiological connection to exhaled breath. Pathological changes in the brain are often relayed to the peripheral circulation via cytokines, neuroactive molecules, and dysregulated signalling pathways, thereby indirectly affecting monocyte metabolism. Consequently, VOC alterations in PBT likely reflect downstream effects of tumour-driven signalling reprogramming (e.g., MAPK, GABA), subsequently affect metabolic pathways shared across various cancer types.

Although Agnes W. Boots et al. emphasised that mechanistic elucidation is secondary to predictive accuracy in breath-based diagnostics,60 we sought to address current limitations in breath biopsy for enhancing the physiological credibility and specificity of our VOC biomarker panel for PBT. To this end, we employed multi-omics integration to construct a VOC-gene interaction network, exploring how functional genes regulate the VOC biomarker panel, and further proposed the potential mechanism by which core VOCs driven the diagnostic efficacy of the PBT biomarker panel: An immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment (TME) exists in PBT patients,31 where tumour-derived cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-8, IL-17, TNF-β, IFN-γ) inhibit T cell and NK cell function by activating myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs).61,62 Immunohistochemical staining confirmed elevated M2 macrophage marker expression (CD163) in PBT tumour tissues, with increased expression correlating with higher tumour grade, a finding consistent with Liu et al.'s study on brain tumour characterisation.41 These immunosuppressive cytokines, along with neuromolecules such as GABA, could also alter the blood–brain barrier (BBB), converting it into a permeable blood-tumour barrier (BTB) that facilitates cytokine entry into circulation,63 further perpetuating systemic immune suppression. Routine blood tests and PBMC transcriptomic analyses confirmed an elevated NLR in PBT patients, reflecting this immunosuppressive state,40 with upregulation of neutrophils and downregulation of NK cells. Notably, the NCF1/NCF2 genes, central to the interaction network, were upregulated. These NOX components activate neutrophils as MDSCs through reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, which in turn promotes the kynurenine pathway in tryptophan metabolism, as ROS is a substrate and a co-factor for IDO enzyme.38,40 Our transcriptomic analysis revealed significant upregulation of IDO1, leading to increased kynurenine production. However, tryptophan depletion may impair the indole pathway, reducing indole production, which correlates with its lower levels in exhaled breath (Fig. 7). These systemic shifts underscore the central role of tryptophan metabolism in PBT-related VOC alterations. Notably, the link between tryptophan and brain tumours has been well-documented: tryptophan transporters at the blood–brain barrier enable brain-specific uptake, and its metabolites (e.g., quinolinic acid, kynurenic acid) are elevated in brain tumour patients contributing to cognitive impairment and seizures.64 Elevated IDO1 expression and loss of ACMSD further increase neurotoxic QUIN, promoting tumour growth.65 Therefore, the presence of indole, a tryptophan derivative, in our VOC biomarker panel reflects its potential specificity for PBT diagnosis. Moreover, an elevated [Kyn]/[Trp] ratio in PBT patients, a hallmark of immunosuppression,38 further reinforces the relevance of tryptophan metabolism. Collectively, these findings imply that exhaled VOC alterations may reflect systemic immune dysregulation in PBT, providing a preliminary biological relevance for their clinical utility. Given the observed association between exhaled VOC biomarkers and the immunosuppressive environment, as well as the diagnostic potential of immune-inflammatory markers for brain tumours,9 we combined these markers to develop a risk assessment system for PBT using an ensemble approach. This system aims to address challenges such as the high costs of clinical PBT screening (e.g., imaging), low test willingness, and limited cooperation from paediatric patients, thereby enhancing large-scale risk screening for early detection and intervention. Our discovery cohort demonstrated that the combined marker system stratified PBT risk with an accuracy of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.87–0.96) and an AUC of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.87–0.96), outperforming individual markers in detecting PBT with in our set. This result was further supported by retrospective sample size estimation, which confirmed that the discovery cohort met the minimum statistical requirements for initial model construction. Currently, AI-assisted PBT diagnosis relies heavily on radiomics,66 and liquid biopsy markers, such as plasma-based assays, show potential but face clinical limitations. Imaging techniques like MRI are expensive and expose children to radiation, while liquid biopsies require improvements in non-invasiveness and accuracy compared to exhaled VOC markers (accuracy: 84.8% vs. 71.6%67). These findings highlight that exhaled VOC markers not only have the potential to independently detect PBT but also synergise with immune-inflammatory factors to provide comprehensive risk stratification. Moreover, the favourable agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities in the calibration curve, together with the consistent net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities in decision curve analysis further indicate our PBT risk stratification model's predictive reliability and clinical utility to a certain extent (Supplementary Fig. S12). Building upon this foundation, our AI-assisted risk assessment model demonstrates translational potential for integration into clinical workflows. Specifically, we envision its deployment as a non-invasive, frontline screening tool in paediatric neurology or neurosurgery outpatient settings. For children presenting with nonspecific neurological symptoms such as chronic headache, dizziness, or visual disturbances, the breath biopsy could precede more costly and invasive diagnostics like MRI, helping to minimise unnecessary medical procedures and reduce the financial burden on families. Furthermore, breath biopsy can complement MRI-based diagnosis by providing metabolic insights. When VOC signatures suggest high risk and align with equivocal MRI findings, this dual evidence can reinforce diagnostic certainty and guide clinical decision-making. Overall, we assume after further validation across clinical subgroups (e.g., sex, age) and multiple centres, this advancement could complement existing diagnostic pathway, supporting personalised, timely decision-making while optimising healthcare resource allocation for paediatric brain tumour patients.

In summary, our study introduces methodological innovation and performance advance in sample collection, data processing, classification modelling, and marker identification compared to similar research. These strengths contributed to favourable clinical diagnostic outcomes in our cohort. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the limited size of the independent verification (internal validation) cohort may reduce the precision of verification results, necessitating larger cohorts for more reliable illustration of model effectiveness. Second, all breath samples were collected exclusively from one medical centre, introducing potential bias and restricting the generalisability of our findings across geographic regions, ethnic backgrounds, and healthcare settings, future external validation across multiple independent centres will be valuable to further establish the generalisability of our VOCs biomarker panel. Third, although we carefully excluded CRV confounders during VOCs analysis, further evaluation is needed to confirm model stability and consistency across clinical subgroups (e.g., by age and sex). Fourth, due to the invasiveness and additional clinical constraints of peripheral blood collection in young paediatric patients, the size of our breath-blood paired sub-cohort was limited, indicating a larger-scale transcriptomic profiling is needed to validate and enhance the robustness of identified multi-omics correlations. Additionally, although our observation indicates that PBMC genes regulate VOCs via the immunosuppressive environment in PBT, these mechanistic insights remain preliminary and require further sophisticated biological validation (e.g., cytokine quantification, cell-based assays, and animal models). Another crucial improvement to be concerned is the engineering of VOCs biomarker detection for technical feasibility of point-of-care testing. Research on exhaled VOC biomarkers remains in an early exploratory stage, and the current methodologies, though effective, are too complex for routine clinical implementation. Just like our current breath biopsy protocol requires approximately 30 min for sample collection and preprocessing, followed by around 75 min of analysis using GC × GC-TOF MS/FID, making it impractical for real-time diagnostics. Nevertheless, sensor-based VOC detection technologies present a promising solution for achieving clinically practical, point-of-care breath diagnostics,68 owing to their high sensitivity and specificity, low cost, portability, rapid response time of specific compounds. For instance, sensor arrays utilising functionalized gold nanoparticles or silicon nanowire field-effect transistors have demonstrated rapid and accurate breath-based diagnosis for conditions such as lung cancer and asthma, with total detection times of less than 30 min.69,70 Building on these technological advances, our ultimate mission is to translate the validated biomarker panel into a customised sensor array that is capable of delivering rapid diagnostic results comparable to the urea breath test for Helicobacter pylori. Future studies will address these limitations, refining our approach and enhance generalisability, ultimately advancing breath biopsy for non-invasive paediatric brain tumour screening toward clinical adaptation in a scalable, user-friendly, and repeatable manner.

Despite these limitations, the proposed AI-driven risk assessment model built on initial biologically grounded VOCs biomarker panel offers a promising tool to support personalised decision making in PBT care. The observed associations between VOCs biomarkers and systemic immune context provide a direction for mechanistic studies. These results can be further explored in basic and translational studies to better understand the regulation and manipulation of cancer–metabolite interactions that alter the course of cancer development, progression, and therapy.
