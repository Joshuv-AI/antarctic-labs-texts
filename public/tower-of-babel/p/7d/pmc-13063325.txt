Prevotella intermedia oral infection induces cognitive impairment in C57BL/6 mice via neuroinflammation and barrier damage
Authors: Yifan Cai, Shuqin Han, Meijiao Zhao, Xinning Xiang, Yifan Zhang, Luyingmei Zhang, Qianyi Lai, Jiaolong Wang, Lan liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral pathogens play a significant role in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) via the oral-brain axis and oral-gut-brain axis. However, how Prevotella intermedia (P. intermedia) affect the development of AD remains unclear.ObjectiveTo investigate the effects of P. intermedia infection on neuroinflammation and cognitive function.DesignSixty C57BL/6 mice were divided into two cohorts and infected with P. intermedia via gingival injection or oral gavage, mimicking pathogen invasion. The microbiota changes of saliva collected from 5 AD patients and 5 healthy individuals were analyzed by 16S rRNA sequencing.ResultsGingival injection resulted in cognitive impairment, neuronal loss in the hippocampal CA1/CA3 regions (p < 0.05), overactivation of microglia and increased expression of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and TNF-α, p < 0.05) and NF-κB in hippocampus (p < 0.05). Similar cognitive impairments and cerebral changes were noted after oral gavaging. Additionally, oral gavage induced gut microbiota dysbiosis, intestinal inflammation and decreased expression of tight junction in intestinal barrier and blood-brain barrier (ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin-1 and Claudin-5, p < 0.05). Furthermore, AD patients exhibited oral microbiota dysbiosis, with a higher relative abundance of P. intermedia (p < 0.05).ConclusionP. intermedia is associated with cognitive impairment and AD-related neuroinflammation via the oral-brain and oral-gut-brain axes.

Introduction

With the world's population aging, Alzheimer's disease (AD) has emerged as an important medical and sociological challenge [1–3]. The global prevalence of dementia is estimated to reach 152.8 million by 2050, of which AD accounts for 50%–70% [4,5]. Major hypotheses in AD suggest that the amyloid cascade, tau pathology, neuroinflammation and low level of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine may be key factors leading to persistent cognitive impairment and behavioral disturbances [6–8]. Oral pathogens, representing a significant and modifiable risk factors, can lead to neuroinflammation and blood‒brain barrier damage in the pathogenesis of AD [9–11]. Since oral health management is a promising strategy for oral pathogens controlling [12], it is meaningful to understand how oral pathogens affect the development of AD, which may be a promising target to prevent or control the development of AD.

Prevotella intermedia (P. intermedia), a Gram-negative, black-pigmented anaerobic bacterium, is considered a common oral pathogen in periodontitis, apical periodontitis and oral squamous cell carcinoma [13–15]. Recently, numerous epidemiological studies have demonstrated that P. intermedia is associated with various systemic diseases [16], including AD [17,18], knee osteoarthritis [19], circulatory system diseases [20], respiratory diseases [21] and preterm birth and low birth weight [22]. A cross-sectional study showed that the IgG antibodies against P. intermedia were negatively associated with cognition [17]. L'Heureux JE et al. identified P. intermedia as a predictor of apolipoprotein E4 (APOE4)-carrier status using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing [23]. There is also a case-control study reported that the loads of P. intermedia was significantly more abundant in the AD group compared with the healthy group [24]. Despite a series of epidemiological findings suggesting an association between P. intermedia and AD, regrettably, direct evidence of causality and in-depth mechanistic research remain lacking at present [25,26].

Research indicates that oral pathogens may induce cognitive impairment and neuroinflammation through multiple pathways, particularly via the oral–brain axis and the oral–gut–brain axis [27]. Oral pathogens can cause local inflammation and tissue damage through dental plaque, infected root canals and dental surgeries [28,29]. Subsequently, pathogens and toxins can enter the bloodstream, increasing the level of antibodies against oral pathogens [30]. These processes can further activate the host immune response, resulting in systemic low-grade chronic inflammation characterized by significantly elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [31]. Toxic substances may have the opportunity to enter the brain due to increased permeability of the blood‒brain barrier (BBB) under the disruption of systemic inflammation [32]. This invasive action is thought to trigger a cascade of neuroinflammatory responses and further promote the development of AD [33,34]. P. intermedia possesses an array of virulence factors capable of eliciting the host immune response. The proteases of P. intermedia can degrade collagen in periodontal tissues [35], while its lipopolysaccharide (LPS) can induce the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines [36]. These findings suggest that P. intermedia may act as a key pathogenetic link via the oral–brain axis, connecting local inflammation with AD.

The pathways through which oral pathogens affect the brain are complex and diverse. Increasing studies support that oral pathogens may exacerbate the pathogenesis of AD through the oral–gut–brain axis [37–39]. Oral pathogens may lead to alterations in the gut microbiota through daily swallowing or eating [40,41]. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota disrupts host‒microbe symbiosis, which can lead to gastrointestinal disorders and has been demonstrated to be associated with AD [42,43]. After oral gavage with saliva from periodontitis patients, APPswe/PS1ΔE9 mice exhibited impaired cognitive function, increased Aβ accumulation and enhanced neuroinflammation [37]. These pathological alterations are associated with gastrointestinal dysfunction resulting from the dissemination of microorganisms [44,45], which further interferes with the brain through direct (vagus nerve) or indirect (such as short-chain fatty acid secretion) pathways [46]. All these findings provide evidence that the oral–gut–brain axis represents a pathway through which P. intermedia contributes to AD.

In this in vivo study, we investigated the effect of P. intermedia on cognitive function in C57BL/6 mice by gingival injection and oral gavage. The Morris water maze (MWM) test was used to assess the cognitive impairment in mice. Histopathological and molecular assessments were performed on the hippocampus and ileum for the exploration of the mechanism underlying. 16S rRNA sequencing was performed to assess the gut microbiota changes. Furthermore, we observed the oral microbiota changes between AD patients and healthy individuals by 16S rRNA sequencing.

Materials and methods

Bacterial culture

P. intermedia (ATCC25611) was purchased from the Shanghai Microbiological Culture Collection Center (SHMCC) and cultured on tryptose soy agar (TSA) plates supplemented with 5% defibrillated sheep blood in an anaerobic container at 37 °C. A total of 109 colony-forming units (CFU) of live P. intermedia was suspended in 1 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 2% carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC).

Animals and experimental protocol

A total of 60 specific pathogen–free (SPF) male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old, weighing 20–25 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All the mice were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle with controlled temperature and humidity and free access to food and water. After one week of adaption, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (15 mice/group).

To investigate the effects of P. intermedia gingival infection on cognitive function, 50 μL of PBS with 2% CMC containing 1 × 109 CFU/mL P. intermedia was injected into the gingiva of the left maxillary first molar tooth (denoted as PG group). The control group was treated with 50 μL of sterile PBS with 2% CMC (denoted as Con-PG group).

To investigate the effect of P. intermedia oral gavage on cognitive function, 200 μL of PBS with 2% CMC containing 1 × 109 CFU/mL P. intermedia was oral gavaged (denoted as PO group). The control group was oral gavaged with 200 μL of sterile PBS with 2% CMC (denoted as Con-PO group).

The four groups were treated differently every other day for four consecutive weeks (Figures 1A, 3A). The body weights of the mice were recorded every other day for the same time period. Zoletil-50 (Virbac, France) was used for anesthesia. All the animal studies were conducted under the guidance of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanchang University (NCULAE-20250519001).

Behavioral test

The spatial learning memory of the mice was analyzed by the MWM test. The MWM test was conducted in a circular pool (L × B × H: 100 × 100 × 60 cm) and filled with water mixed with nontoxic white dye. The pool was divided into four quadrants, and a 9-cm-diameter platform was submerged 1 cm under the water surface in the second quadrant. The platform trial was performed three times a day for four days, followed by a probe trial on the fifth day. In the platform trial, the mice navigated to the platform if they failed to reach the platform within 90 s and were allowed to stay on it for 5 s. In the probe trial, the platform was removed, and the mice were allowed to swim freely for 60 s. The escape latency, target crossing times, the time spent in target-quadrant and the time spent in the central area were recorded.

Tissue collection

After the behavioral test, fresh feces of mice were collected and stored at −80 °C. Then the mice were transcardially perfused with saline. The brain, gingiva and ileum samples were harvested. The left hemisphere of the brain, gingiva and ileum were stored at −80 °C for biochemical analysis. The right hemisphere of the brain and ileum were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) fixation solution for histopathological analysis.

Microbiota analysis

16S rRNA gene analysis was selected to analyze the changes in the microbiota of saliva collected from participants and feces from mice. DNA extraction and 16S rRNA sequencing of the salivary and fecal microbiota were performed by BGI Co., Ltd., China (Shenzhen, China). First, extract bacterial DNA using the DNA extraction kit MagPure Stool DNA KF Kit B (MAGEN, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer's instruction manual, and transfer the extracted DNA solution to a 1.5 ml centrifuge tube for storage. The V3V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR using primers 338F (ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG) and 806 R (GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT). The PCR products were purified and sorted using DNA sorting magnetic beads (BGI, LB00V69). The libraries that pass the quality check are denatured to obtain a single-stranded DNA library molecule. Then, through the cyclization reaction system, a single-stranded circular product is obtained, and the linear DNA molecules that are not circularized are digested. The single-stranded circular DNA molecule is replicated by phi29 and rolling circle replication to form a DNA nanoBall (DNB) containing multiple copies. The obtained DNBs were added to the mesh holes on the chip using high-density DNA GeneChip technology, and PE300/250 sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-G400 sequencing platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China) by Combinatorial Probe-Anchor Synthesis (cPAS).

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and Nissl staining

HE staining was chosen to observe the histopathological structural changes of ileum and Nissl staining was selected to identify the neurons in the hippocampus. The ileum and brain fixed with 4% PFA were then embeded in paraffin and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. HE staining images of the ileum and Nissl staining images of the hippocampal CA1, CA3 and dentate gyrus (DG) regions were captured with a Pannoramic Scan II Digital Scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary).

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded brain tissue sections were subjected to dewaxing and rehydration. Subsequently, antigen retrieval was performed on the tissue sections using citrate antigen retrieval solution (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). The sections were washed three times with PBS, each for 5 min, followed by blocking endogenous enzymes with an endogenous peroxidase blocking buffer (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) for 20 min. Subsequently, 3% bovine serum albumin (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) was added, and the sections were incubated in the dark for 30 min. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the Iba-1 primary antibody (Table 1). The sections were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each, incubated with the secondary antibody (Table 1) at room temperature for 1 h, and mounted with an antifade mounting medium with DAPI. Section images were captured using a inverted fluorescence microscopy (DMi8, Leica, Germany).

Western blot

The ileum and hippocampus were homogenized, and total protein was isolated. The concentration of total protein was determined by a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China), and the samples were boiled in 5× loading buffer for 5 min at 100 °C. 10 μg samples from each group were seperated in 8% tris-glycine SDS‒PAGE gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (Table 1) of target protein overnight at 4°C and then incubated with secondary antibodies (Table 1) at room temperature for 1 h. By using Ultra hypersensitive ECL chemiluminescence kit (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China), the membrane was imaged by the iBright CL750 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher, USA). The relative expression of target proteins was calculated by dividing the grayscale of the target protein by that of the marker protein β-actin. The ImageJ software was used to quantify the grayscale of each band.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‒qPCR)

The mRNA expression of Il-1β and Tnf-α in the hippocampus, gingiva and ileum was tested by RT-qPCR. The total mRNA was extracted by using FastPure Complex Tissue/Cell Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). The RNA samples were then reverse transcribed into cDNA by using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) under RNase-free conditions. Then, qPCR amplification was performed on ABI StepOne Plus system by Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). The relative expression of mRNA was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences are listed in Table 2.

Participants' basic information and sample collection

A total of 10 participants were recruited, including 5 patients with AD from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University and 5 healthy participants (Control group) from the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Nanchang University. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethical Committee of The Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Nanchang University (No: 2024085). Informed written consent was signed by all participants or their legal guardians before participation. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in Table S1. Before sampling, basic information was collected, including gender, age, body mass index (BMI), educational level and systemic diseases (diabetes, hypertension and heart disease). Unstimulated saliva (2–5 mL) was collected with no sputum in a sterile, enzyme-free cryopreservation tube. These samples were quickly stored at −80 °C until use.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.5.0. software was used for statistical analysis. The data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For the analysis of body weight change and escape latency in MWM test were analyzed with the two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. For all other data except microbial differences between groups, were analyzed with Student's t-test. For microbiota analysis, ACE, Chao1, Shannon and Simpson indices were calculated to estimate α-diversity, while principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and the Bray‒Curtis distance were employed to evaluate β-diversity. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was applied to assess associations between key species and clinical characteristics. For α-diversity, relative abundances of key species and Spearman correlation, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini‒Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. The value of p < 0.05 was defined as statistical significance.

Results

Effects of P. intermedia gingival infection on body weight and cognitive function

After gingival injection with different solutions, the body weight of C57BL/6 mice in the Con-PG group gradually increased over time. In contrast, the body weight of the mice in the PG group decreased in the first week and then gradually increased thereafter, but remained significantly lower than that of the Con-PG group since day 5 (pday 5 < 0.01, Figure 1B). After 4 weeks of continuous infection, the MWM test was used to assess the spatial learning and memory abilities of the mice. There was no significant difference in the average swimming speed between the two groups (Figure 1C), ruling out the possibility of motor or visual impairments in the mice. After the platform was placed below the water surface on the second day of the platform trail, the escape latency of both groups increased and then gradually decreased with training time. The escape latency of the PG group was slightly longer than that of the Con-PG group (Figure 1C). During the probe trail, the target crossing times of the Con-PG group were slightly higher than that of the PG group (Figure 1D). The PG group spent slightly less time in the target quadrant than the Con-PG group (Figure 1D). Further analysis indicated that the Con-PG group spent significantly more time in the central region than the PG group (p < 0.05, Figure 1D). Representative diagrams of the MWM test for Con-PG and PG groups in probe trail are shown in Figure 1E. The mice in the Con-PG group spent more time in the target region, with behavioral trajectories tending toward the center of the pool, while PG group exhibited behavioral trajectories primarily along the pool walls, with fewer platform crossing trajectories. These results indicate that local infection with P. intermedia in the gingiva led to reduced body weight in C57BL/6 mice and impaired cognitive function.

P. intermedia gingival infection induced neuronal injury and neuroinflammation in the hippocampus

The gingival inflammation was observed by RT-qPCR, which showed that the mRNA expression of Il-1β and TNF-α in the gingiva of the PG group was significantly increased (p < 0.0001, Figure S4). Then, Nissl staining was used to observe the morphological changes in the hippocampal neurons in the CA1, CA3 and DG regions of the mice after gingival injection treatment. Nissl staining images revealed that the hippocampal neurons in the PG group showed partial cellular structural shrinkage and collapse, resulting in deeply stained (Figure 2A). The quantitative analysis of Nissl staining demonstrated significant neuron loss in the CA1 and CA3 regions of the hippocampus in the PG group (p < 0.0001 and p < 0.01), while there was no significant difference in the DG region between the two groups (Figure 2B).

The results exhibited that there were more Iba-1-positive microglia with green fluorescence in the hippocampus of the PG group compared with the Con-PG group (Figure 3C). Overactivated microglia release proinflammatory factors, leading to tissue damage. Effects of P. intermedia infection by oral gavage on body weight and cognitive function

After different oral gavage treatments, the body weight of the two groups of C57BL/6 mice showed an overall upward trend during 4weeks. The body weight of the PO group were slightly lower compared with Con-PO group. At day 26, the body weight of the PO group was significantly lower than that of the Con-PO group (pday 26 < 0.05, Figure 3B). The MWM test was used to assess the cognitive function of the mice after four weeks of different oral gavage treatment. The results demonstrated that there was no significant difference in the average swimming speed between the two groups (Figure 3C). After the platform was placed below the water surface on the second day of the platform trail, the escape latency of both groups increased and then gradually decreased with training time. The escape latency of the PO group was slightly longer than that of the Con-PO group (Figure 3C). During the probe trail, the target crossing times of the Con-PO group was slightly higher than that of the PO group (Figure 3D). The mice of the PO group spent less time in the target quadrant than the Con-PO group (P < 0.05, Figure 3D). The mice in the Con-PO group spent slightly more time in the central region than the PO group (Figure 3D). Representative diagrams of the MWM test results for both groups in the probe trail are shown in Figure 3E. The mice in the Con-PO group spent more time in the target region, with behavioral trajectories tending toward the center of the pool, while PO group exhibited behavioral trajectories primarily along the pool walls, with fewer platform crossing trajectories. These results above indicated that P. intermedia infection by oral gavage led to reduced body weight in C57BL/6 mice and impaired cognitive function.

P. intermedia infection by oral gavage induced neuronal injury, neuroinflammation and BBB impairment in the hippocampus

Nissl staining was used to observe pathological changes in neurons within the CA1, CA3 and DG regions of the hippocampus in C57BL/6 mice after different gavage treatments. Nissl staining images revealed that neurons within the CA1, CA3 and DG regions of the PO group exhibited wrinkled and collapsed cellular structures, resulting in intense staining (Figure 4A). The number of neurons in the CA1 and CA3 regions of the hippocampus in the PO group was significantly lower than that in the Con-PO group (p < 0.01 and p < 0.01, Figure 4B), while there was no significant difference in the number of neurons in the DG region between the two groups (Figure 4B). Subsequently, immunofluorescence staining results showed that there were more Iba-1-positive microglia with green fluorescence in the hippocampus of the PO group compared with the Con-PO group (Figure 4C). Overactivated microglia can release proinflammatory factors, leading to tissue damage. RT-qPCR results indicated that the mRNA expression of Il-1β and Tnf-α in the hippocampus of the PO group was significantly greater than those in the Con-PO group (p < 0.01 and p < 0.01, Figure 4F). The western blot results demonstrated that the protein expression of IL-1β in the PO group was significantly increased compared with the Con-PO group (Figure 4E, H). Further results revealed that the protein expression of NF-κB p65 was significantly increased in the PO group (p < 0.05, Figure 4E, H). These results indicate that P. intermedia infection via oral gavage led to neuronal loss in the hippocampal region of the mice. Neuroinflammatory responses in the hippocampus may be associated with overactivation of microglia, elevated levels of the proinflammatory factors IL-1β and TNF-α, and activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway.

P. intermedia infection by oral gavage induced intestinal inflammation and intestinal barrier injury

After 4 weeks of gavage treatment, histopathological changes in the ileum tissue of the mice were observed by HE staining. The Con-PO group exhibited more intact villus structures arranged in an orderly manner. The cell nuclei were evenly distributed, with consistent size and shape, and the crypt structures were clearly defined and arranged in a regular pattern (Figure 5A). In the PO group, the villus structures appeared irregular, with partial villus atrophy and damage. The cell nuclei exhibited partial heterogeneity, and the crypt structures were damaged and irregular, collectively indicating an inflammatory state (Figure 5A). The villus length in the Con-PO group was significantly longer than that in the PO group, and the crypt depth was significantly deeper than that in the PO group (p < 0.0001 and p < 0.0001, Figure 5B). Subsequently, the RT-qPCR results showed that the mRNA expression of Il-1β in the PO group significantly increased compared with that in the Con-PO group, while the mRNA expression of Tnf-α significantly decreased compared with that in the Con-PO group (p < 0.0001 and p < 0.01, Figure 5C). The western blot results demonstrated that the protein expression of IL-1β in the PO group was significantly greater than that in the Con-PO group (p < 0.05, Figure 5D, E). Additionally, the western blot results indicated that the expression of the intestinal barrier tight junction proteins ZO-1, Occludin and Claudin-1 in the ileum of the PO group was significantly decreased than that of Con-PO group (p < 0.05, p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, Figure 5D, E). These findings indicate that infection with P. intermedia via oral gavage induced inflammatory responses in intestinal tissues, increased the expression of proinflammatory factors and subsequently decreased the levels of intestinal tight junction proteins, thereby compromising the integrity of the intestinal barrier.

Oral gavage P. intermedia altered the gut microbiota composition

The gut microbiota composition was analyzed by 16S rRNA sequencing to further investigate the mechanisms of intestinal inflammation after oral gavage with P. intermedia. A total of 442 OTUs were detected in the Con-PO group, and 475 were detected in the PO group (6 fecal samples/group), with 423 OTUs shared between the two groups (Figure 6A). The α-diversity, which represents the diversity of the bacterial community, was analyzed using the ACE, Chao1, Shannon and Simpson indices. The ACE, Chao1, and shannon index of the PO group were slightly lower than those of the Con-PO group, while the Simpson index was slightly higher than that of the Con-PO group. However, all four indices between the two groups showed no significant differences (Figure 6B). β-diversity represents the degree of difference in microbiota composition. PCoA revealed significant differences in β-diversity between the two groups (p < 0.05, Figure 6C), while the Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) analysis showed no significant difference (Figure 6C). Furthermore, LEfSe analysis was employed to detect species exhibiting significant differences in abundance between the two groups. Alloprevotella, Odoribacter and Parabacteroides_distasonis were enriched in the PO group (Figure 6D). At the genus level, there was a significant difference in Alloprevotellabetween the two groups. The means of Alloprevotella in the PO group was significantly higher than that in the Con-PO group (p < 0.05, Figure 6E). At the phylum and genus levels, the relative abundance of species between the two groups are shown in Figure 6F. The ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes (F/B) in the PO group was slightly lower than that in the Con-PO group, decreasing from 0.472 to 0.360. The results above demonstrated that oral gavage of P. intermedia altered the gut microbiota composition in the mice.

Composition of the saliva microbiota was altered in patients with AD

The demographic characteristics of the participants in both groups are shown in Table S2. A total of 933 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were obtained from 5 samples in the AD group and 975 in the control group, with 816 OTUs shared between the two groups (Figure 7A). The α-diversity was analyzed by using the Chao1 index and Shannon index. The results showed similar bacterial abundance and uniformity in the two groups, with no significant differences (Figure 7B). The β-diversity was analyzed by the PCoA, and there were significant differences in the distribution of the microbiota between the AD group and the control group, and the species diversity in the AD group was significantly lower than that in the control group (p < 0.01 and p < 0.01, Figure 7C). Significant differences in the composition of the oral microbiota between the two groups were observed at the phylum, class, order, family, genus and species levels (Figures S1 and S2). The relative abundances of Bacteroidota (AD: 32.02%, control: 15.06%, p = 0.037), Cyanobacteriota (AD: 0.07%, control: 0.32%, p = 0.012) and Mycoplasmatota (AD: 0.29%, control: 0.07%, p = 0.012) differed significantly between the two groups at the phylum level (Figure 7D). The relative abundance of Prevotella (AD: 18.81%, control: 4.85%, p = 0.012) differed significantly between the two groups at the genus level (Figure 7E). The relative abundances of Prevotella veroralis (AD: 8.20%, control: 1.56%, p = 0.021), Prevotella melaninogenica (AD: 3.61%, control: 1.60%, p = 0.037) and P. intermedia (AD: 4.26%, control: 0.28%, p = 0.022) differed significantly between the two groups at the species level (Figure 7F). In addition, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was performed on saliva samples from the two groups. The results showed the significant differences in the oral microbiota between the two groups through cluster plots and LDA plots, and further screened for biomarkers that could distinguish between the two groups (Figure 7G). The AD group had higher abundances of Bacteroidales, Bacteroidia, Bacteroidota, Prevotellaceae and Prevotella compared with the Control group. The results of Spearman's rank correlation analysis between key species and clinical characteristics are shown in Figure S3. All these results demonstrated that the composition of the saliva microbiota was altered in patients with AD.

Discussion

To determine if P. intermedia influences AD through the oral–brain and oral–gut–brain axes, C57BL/6 mice were infected with the pathogen via gingival injection and oral gavage. The results demonstrated that gingival injection led to cognitive impairment, which was attributed to neuron loss in the hippocampus. The expression of proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, was significantly increased in the hippocampus, alongside the overactivation of microglia and the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. The results above indicated that P. intermedia induces a series of changes within the brain and behavioral alterations via the oral–brain axis pathway. Brain changes analogous to those in the PG group were detected in the PO group. Furthermore, the PO group showed significant BBB damage. It is worth noting that the mice in the PO group demonstrated intestinal inflammation and disruption of the intestinal barrier, which may have been caused by gut microbiota dysbiosis. The above findings indicate that the gut‒brain axis represents an additional pathway through which P. intermedia influences the progression of AD.

P. intermedia is a member of the orange complexes, frequently isolated from subgingival plaque in patients with periodontitis [47]. P. intermedia is also more commonly detected in oral squamous cell carcinoma tissues and in infected root canals [48]. The pathogenicity of P. intermedia is highly correlated with its virulence factors, including the capsule, fimbriae, extracellular vesicles, LPS, elastase, trypsin-like serine protease, dipeptidase, immunoglobulin G protease and immunoglobulin A protease [25]. Specifically, P. intermedia promotes attachment to and invasion of host cells through adhesins distributed on the outer membrane [49]. The nonendotoxic glycoproteins and LPS of P. intermedia stimulate host cells to secrete proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8 and TNF-α [49,50]. Furthermore, the cysteinyl protease Inp A from P. intermedia significantly reduces serum bactericidal activity by degrading the C3α chain. Activation of the C1 complex in serum inhibits complement system activation, thereby creating a pathogen-friendly environment [51]. Through the synergistic action of multiple virulence factors, P. intermedia induces inflammatory responses and tissue damage, thereby promoting the development of systemic diseases.

Neurons and synaptic function in the hippocampus region play a crucial role in maintaining normal cognitive and memory functions. Cognitive impairment in mice infected with P. intermedia may attributed to neuron loss and neuroinflammation. The results demonstrated that both infection ways caused cognitive impairment in mice, with Nissl staining revealing significant hippocampal neuron loss in CA1 and CA3 regions. The CA1 and CA3 regions are responsible for information transmission and storage, respectively, playing crucial roles in spatial and episodic memory [52,53]. Neuron loss in these areas leads to impairments in spatial memory and learning [54]. This also helps explain the poor performance observed in mice following infection with P. intermedia in the MWM test. Furthermore, the results demonstrated overactivation of microglia within the hippocampus, alongside increased expression of proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α. Progressive neuron loss and neuroinflammation modulated by microglia play a critical role in the pathological changes of AD [55]. Significant neuronal death occurs in multiple brain regions of AD patients, such as the hippocampus, temporal lobe and parietal cortex, leading to symptoms including cognitive impairment and memory decline [56]. The neuronal death is associated with persistent chronic neuroinflammation, in which microglia and astrocytes play a key role [57]. Upon exposure to sustained external stimuli, such as pathogen infection, injury, stroke, or toxic agents, microglia produce substantial inflammatory mediators including proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6 [58,59]. This process damages neurons within the nervous system.

NF-κB is a multifunctional transcription factor present in numerous cells that participates in various biological processes, such as inflammation, immunity, differentiation, cell growth, tumorigenesis, and apoptosis [60]. Multiple stimuli can activate the NF-κB signaling pathway, including bacterial LPS, free radical oxidation and ultraviolet radiation [61]. As part of the NF-κB signaling pathway, p65 frequently appears in inflammatory responses [62]. The western blot results revealed that both P. intermedia infection ways lead to increased expression of NF-κB p65 in mice, and further results demonstrated increased expression of IL-1β in these mice. Recent studies have demonstrated that LPS is capable of binding to toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) and toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), subsequently triggering the downstream signaling pathway involving NF-κB to regulate the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 and TNF-α, thus initiating an immune response against pathogens [63–65]. Further studies indicate that cross-coupling between NF-κB p65 and AP-1 c-Fos transcription factors enhances the binding of NF-κB p65 to IκB sites, resulting in synergistic potentiation of IL-1β promoter activity [66]. The current results suggest that P. intermedia infection may activate the NF-κB signaling pathway, triggering proinflammatory factor release and ultimately resulting in neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment.

The BBB, as a natural physiological barrier, plays a crucial role in maintaining the microenvironment within the brain [67]. It prevents neuronal injury by inhibiting the entry of toxic substances from the bloodstream into the brain [68]. Tight junction proteins and adhesion-related proteins constitute essential components of the BBB [69]. Extended exposure to peripheral toxic substances results in the impairment of tight junction integrity among vascular endothelial cells, affecting critical proteins such as ZO-1, Occludin and Claudin [70]. This results in increased permeability of the BBB, facilitating the infiltration of pathogens or toxic agents into the brain, which subsequently induces overactivation of microglia and neuroinflammation [71,72]. Western blot analysis in this study revealed that the expression of the tight junction proteins ZO-1, Occludin and Claudin-5 in the PO group was significantly decreased compared to the Con group, which indicated that the neuroinflammation induced by oral gavage with P. intermedia were strongly correlated with BBB disruption. It is noteworthy that the neuroinflammation caused by gingival infection with P. intermedia may not be mediated primarily through significant BBB disruption. Western blot analysis revealed no significant difference in BBB protein expression between the two groups. Recent studies indicate that Porphyromonas gingivalis-LPS induces neuroinflammation via TLR4 and TLR2 expressed on microglia during relatively early stages of chronic inflammation, even preceding BBB disruption [73]. Thus, P. intermedia infection might cause neuroinflammation earlier than significant BBB disruption.

Numerous studies have indicated that oral pathogens can induce AD not only through low-grade inflammatory responses triggered by local infections but also by inducing AD-like pathological changes via the oral–gut–brain axis [46]. The oral microbiota maintains continuous communication with the gut microbiota, and this interplay between microbial communities plays a pivotal role in the oral–gut–brain axis theory [37,40]. Previous studies have demonstrated that after oral gavage of 109 CFU/ml Porphyromonas gingivalis, bacteria were detected in the ileum within 3 h. Gut dysbiosis was observed within 16 h [74]. Our results demonstrated an altered microbial community structure in the PO group, characterized by significant differences in β-diversity and a slight reduction in the F/B ratio compared to the Con group. A recent study by Schmidt et al. reported that the oral Prevotella genus is capable of colonizing the lower gastrointestinal tract [75]. This ectopic colonization of oral bacteria may represent a mechanism by which P. intermedia disrupts the gut microbiota. Moreover, certain bacteria, such as Escherichia coli and Prevotella, can induce the release of zonulin from intestinal cells, leading to the disintegration of the tight junction protein Claudin-Occludin-ZO1 complex between intestinal epithelial cells [76]. Disruption of tight junction proteins leads to persistent intestinal barrier leakage, allowing harmful substances to infiltrate connective tissue and trigger intestinal inflammation [77]. Subsequently, bacteria and their metabolites pose a risk to the BBB through peripheral circulation [78], thereby playing a contributory role in the pathogenesis of AD [79]. In our research, the mice in the PO group exhibited intestinal inflammation, alongside decreased expression of tight junction proteins in the ileum. All these findings suggested that infection with P. intermedia may induce gut microbiota dysbiosis, leading to disruption of the intestinal barrier and inflammatory reactions. This may represent an intrinsic mechanism whereby P. intermedia infection via oral gavage causes neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment.

Existing clinical evidence indicates a strong correlation between P. intermedia and AD [17,80]. Oral P. intermedia is even considered as a predictor of APOE4-carrier status [23]. Our in vivo studies demonstrated that P. intermedia induces cognitive impairment in mice via the oral–brain and oral–gut–brain axes, potentially mediated by inflammatory responses and barrier disruption. These results strengthen the evidence for the pivotal role of P. intermedia in the development of AD. Subsequently, our 16S rRNA sequencing analysis of salivary samples revealed a significant elevation in the relative abundance of P. intermedia in AD patients compared to healthy controls. Nevertheless, the fact that a statistically significant difference was still detected despite the limited sample size underscores the strength of the association between P. intermedia and AD. Therefore, rather than standing alone, our salivary data complement and extend the existing epidemiological literature and, for the first time, are biologically supported by in vivo evidence of pathogenicity. Furthermore, this study indicated that the oral microbiota composition in AD patients differs from that of the control group. Research suggests that dysbiosis of the oral microbiota may trigger dynamic alterations in the systemic microbiota, particularly the gut microbiota, which could potentially contribute to AD through complex gut–brain crosstalk. Thus, at a more fundamental level, addressing the dysbiosis of the oral microbiota may represent a novel and promising strategy for the prevention and management of AD. As these pilot sequencing findings are preliminary, future studies with rigorous confunder management are warranted.

Although this study has several limitations, it is the first study to investigate the role of P. intermedia in the development of AD. The molecular biology results from in vivo experiments should be interpreted with caution due to the limited biological replication. Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these findings. While we explored the effects of P. intermedia on AD through the oral–brain and oral–gut–brain axes in this research, we believe that there may be other ways in which P. intermedia could induce AD. In addition, the specific role of virulence factors in P. intermedia is worthy of further investigation. Moreover, research into the underlying mechanisms remains preliminary. Our findings suggest that the NF-κB signaling pathway may play a role in neuroinflammation. However, the activation of target molecules both upstream and downstream remains unclear and needs to be determined in future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this research provides novel evidence that P. intermedia may induce cognitive impairment in C57BL/6 mice through the oral–brain axis and the oral–gut–brain axis pathways. Neuronal damage and neuroinflammation after P. intermedia infection are associated with microglial overactivation and increased expression of proinflammatory factors. The underlying mechanism is at least partially linked to the NF-κB signaling pathway. P. intermedia infection causes gut microbiota dysbiosis, leading to intestinal inflammatory responses and compromised both the intestinal barrier and the BBB. P. intermedia may serve as a potential biomarker for AD, offering a foundation for future studies exploring its utility in diagnostic and therapeutic contexts.
