Characterization of the genotypic and phenotypic spectrum of TCF7L2-related neurodevelopmental disorder (TRND)

ABSTRACT
PurposeTCF7L2 (OMIM:602228; HGNC:11641) is a transcription factor and critical effector of the Wnt/β-Catenin pathway. In 2021, 11 pediatric patients with mono-allelic predicted loss-of-function (pLOF) TCF7L2 variants and syndromic features were observed. Characterization of patients with pLOF TCF7L2 variants and neurodevelopmental features – herein referred to as TCF7L2-related neurodevelopmental disorder (TRND) – is urgently needed.MethodsWe leveraged multiple methods (GeneMatcher, DECIPHER, literature review, public/private repositories) to identify an international cohort of 76 patients with pLOF TCF7L2 variants and neurodevelopmental features and phenotypically characterized them. We also retrospectively searched for an independent cohort of adults with pLOF TCF7L2 variants (n = 11) from 60,000+ PennMedicine BioBank (PMBB) patients.ResultsAmong 76 patients with pLOF TCF7L2 variants, speech delay (95.3%), craniofacial dysmorphisms (73.3%), ophthalmologic conditions (65.5%), autism (62.1%), and orthopedic abnormalities (52.6%) were most commonly observed. Phenotypic differences did not cluster by variant type or genomic locus. Among PMBB patients, an association of nominal significance with type 2 diabetes with renal manifestations (OR = 5.8; p-value = 0.03) was detected, warranting further investigation.ConclusionsThis represents the most comprehensive characterization to date of TRND, a novel neurodevelopmental disorder, defining its genotypic and phenotypic spectrum. We opened a Simons Searchlight natural history study (https://www.simonssearchlight.org/research/what-we-study/tcf7l2/) to enhance understanding of this condition.

Introduction

Transcription factor 7-like 2 (TCF7L2) (OMIM:602228; HGNC:11641) – formerly TCF4-transcription factor 4 – is a key mediator of canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling across human biology and disease1,2. In fact, genome-wide association studies have linked TCF7L2 single nucleotide variants (SNVs) with increased risk for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D), colon cancer, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and autism spectrum disorder (ASD)2–4. TCF7L2 is located on chromosome 10q25.2, and the encoded protein is a member of the TCF/LEF (T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer binding factor) family of proteins, which complex with beta-catenin (CTNNB1) to bind DNA via a highly conserved high-mobility group (HMG Box) domain1,5. Transcriptional regulation of Wnt-responsive genes is vital for fetal development and adult tissue homeostasis1–2. TCF7L2, specifically, has a complex splicing pattern that generates distinct protein isoforms – each with agonistic and antagonistic activities on transcriptional regulation of canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways2,6.

TCF7L2 is especially critical for central nervous system (CNS) development. In fact, TCF7L2 homozygous knockout in mice results in neural tube duplications and severe truncations of caudal endoderm incompatible with life7. Single-cell RNA sequencing of human neuronal tissue has demonstrated TCF7L2 expression in several CNS cell populations throughout development8, and TCF7L2 has been implicated in astrocyte maturation8–9, thalamic and midbrain development10–11, and premyelination and maturation of oligodendrocytes12–13. In humans, TCF7L2 has high intolerance to loss-of-function variation (probability of loss-of-function intolerance (pLI) = 1). Several large-scale studies over the past decade have identified occasional patients with intellectual disability (ID) and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) with variants in TCF7L214–19.

Recently, Dias et al.20 reported a small case series of 11 pediatric patients with de novo heterozygous nonsense, splice-site, and missense variants in TCF7L2 with neurodevelopmental syndromic features. This small cohort all had developmental delays in addition to a range of ophthalmologic, orthopedic, and neuropsychiatric comorbidities, such as ASD and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). All variants were in highly conserved regions expected to disrupt TCF7L2 function. Functional mouse models with two of these variants demonstrated reduced TCF7L2 HMG Box binding, anxiety-like phenotypes, and impaired vocalization consistent with previous murine ASD models21. These findings collectively suggest TCF7L2 haploinsufficiency contributes to risk for a novel neurodevelopmental disorder, herein referred to as TCF7L2-related neurodevelopmental disorder (TRND).

Given the small sample size and limited research to date, many questions remain, including those regarding TRND’s natural history and phenotypic and genotypic spectrum. In this study, we sought to comprehensively establish this for future investigation.

Methods

During this retrospective cohort study, we identified patients with predicted loss-of-function (pLOF) variants in TCF7L2 from 06/2022–10/2024 (Figure 1). We also opened a Simons Searchlight natural history study (https://www.simonssearchlight.org/research/what-we-study/tcf7l2/) currently available for prospective patient enrollment.

Identifying and classifying patients with predicted loss-of-function TCF7L2 variants

Orthogonal identification strategies were implemented, including: (i) patient identification with GeneMatcher22 through the Matchmaker Exchange Network, (ii) initiating contact with clinicians of cases published in DECIPHER23, (iii) collating patient data from public/private repositories (Simons Simplex Collection (SSC)24, Simons Foundation Autism Research Initiative (SFARI)24, ClinVar25, Ambry Genetics), and (iv) outreach to authors/clinicians of cases yielded from systematic literature reviews. Literature review was conducted using the keyword search term “TCF7L2” on PubMed, and corresponding authors were contacted regarding potential cases. Following initial identification (n = 125), 37 cases were excluded as they were either (i) identified as duplicates under different source-specific identifiers and removed to ensure patients were uniquely represented or (ii) outside of the coding sequence (e.g., intron variants).

Patients with TCF7L2 variants were classified as pLOF and “Likely TRND” (Supplemental Table 1) if: (a) any neurodevelopmental phenotype was reported, (b) the patient had no other variants known to contribute to a neurodevelopmental phenotype (per report using patient-specific identification methods listed in Supplemental Table 1), and (c) the following inclusion criteria for each variant class were met: (i) nonsense variants were located ≥50 nucleotides upstream of the final exon-exon junction, and, thus, expected to cause nonsense-mediated decay of the transcript20,28; (ii) splice-site variants scoring highly on splicing prediction tools (MaxEntScan26 |delta score|≥8 or SpliceAI27 score≥0.8); (iii) out-of-frame indel variants expected to introduce a stop codon ≥50 nucleotides upstream of the final exon-exon junction20,28; (iv) missense variants with <0.01% total allele frequency in gnomADv4.1.029 and with scores supportive of pathogenicity in at least 2/4 predictive tools (AlphaMissense30 0.56–1.0, ClinPred31≥0.5, PolyPhen32 “possibly” or “probably damaging”≥0.446, and/or PHRED-like CADD33≥25); (v) CNV deletion variants expected to disrupt TCF7L2 not otherwise spanning genes known to explain the presenting phenotype; or (vi) in-frame indel variants not present in gnomAD29 in a highly conserved region. Pathogenicity scores generated using prediction algorithms for missense and splice-site variants are summarized in Supplemental Table 2. Some identified patients had suspicious TCF7L2 variants and no other clear genetic contributor for their neurodevelopmental phenotype but did not meet aforementioned criteria. These cases were separately classified as “Suspected TRND,” with case-specific reasons for exclusion in Supplemental Table 4.

Genetic variants were primarily identified using exome sequencing and/or Sanger sequencing (n = 49). Remaining cases were identified using non-Sanger genome sequencing (n = 7), other non-Sanger assays (n = 6; molecular inversion probe assay, expanded gene panel, microarray, and/or FISH), or were otherwise unable to be confirmed due to case-specific limitations (n = 14). All reported variants were converted to the MANE select transcript (NM_001367943.1) using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor tool34. An overview of patient identification and classification is summarized in Figure 1. Composite genotypic and phenotypic information for “Likely TRND” and “Suspected TRND” cases is available in Supplemental Tables 1 and 4, respectively.

Phenotypic characterization of TRND patients

Following patient identification, clinicians were provided a tabular survey (Supplemental Table 3) to complete with de-identifiable data regarding their case, including free-text response questions regarding genotypic, phenotypic, and historical details. Of the 76 “Likely TRND” and 12 “Suspected TRND” patients, 20 and 7, respectively, could not be further characterized through the tabular survey by the referring clinician/site/author, and only publicly available data were used in these cases. Written responses were clarified during follow-up and phenotypic information was standardized using the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO)35 during analysis. Translated phenotypic details were corroborated with clinicians and updated at the study’s conclusion. To characterize the spectrum of features shared across “Likely TRND” patients, we extracted HPO-standardized terms that co-occurred in at least 2 patient cases, comprising 81 features (Figure 1, Supplemental Table 1). 10 broad categories of non-neurodevelopmental phenotypic features were additionally included in analysis (Supplemental Table 1).

Interrogation of adult biobank and EHR data for individuals with pLOF TCF7L2 variants

We also conducted a retrospective screen of the PennMedicine Biobank (PMBB) at the University of Pennsylvania – a repository linked to the PennMedicine electronic health record (EHR) system – to identify adults with predicted TCF7L2 loss-of-function variants. The biobank includes approximately 60,000 adult patient volunteers, who have had a genome-wide SNP array performed, a subset of whom also received exome sequencing. Predicted loss of function (pLOF) variants in TCF7L2 were defined as frameshift, splicing, or stop-gain variants, as annotated by ANNOVAR36, and filtered for pathogenicity using REVEL37 scores ≥0.5. Following enforcement of this criterion, 12 different heterozygous pLOF variants in TCF7L2 were detected among an initial pool of 22 PMBB patients. 11 patients had nonsense or out-of-frame indel variants not meeting the ≥50 nucleotides before the last exon-exon junction threshold criterion and were excluded, resulting in a final n = 11 sample size. Age/sex-matched controls were identified by selecting patients with common variants in TCF7L2 (allele frequency ≥10%). PCA-based matching on Euclidean distance was used to randomly select approximately 10 controls/case from the n = 22 unfiltered dataset (n = 198 controls). All diagnoses in the EHR for each patient were recorded, as well as any ICD-9 codes. Median age and BMI were reported relative to date of last encounter. PMBB “phecodes”, groups of ICD-9 codes depicting clinically meaningful phenotypes, were compared between control and pLOF cohorts. Statistical significance was evaluated using Fisher’s exact tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U tests for quantitative variables.

Results

pLOF TCF7L2 variants in highly conserved protein domains are present in TRND

76 patients were identified as “Likely TRND,” with nonsense (n = 10), out-of-frame indel (n = 18), missense (n = 33), splice-site (n = 10), in-frame indel (n = 1), and CNV (n = 4) variants in TCF7L2. The median patient age at the most recent recorded clinical assessment was 8.17 years (interquartile range (IQR) = 3.94–13.25) and 55.71% were male. Most patients for which reported ethnicity was available were identified by clinicians as White Non-Hispanic (39.47%). An additional 12 patients were identified as “Suspected TRND,” with insufficient evidence to meet criteria. Features of both cohorts are included in Table 1.

Most variants (n=72) were predicted to impact the coding sequence of (1) the full-length isoforms of TCF7L2 (FL-TCF7L2) and (2) the short isoforms of TCF7L2 lacking the N-terminal β-catenin binding domain that act as a dominant-negative antagonist of canonical Wnt signaling (DN-TCF7L2). However, 4 cases (P11, P17, P18, P21) included variants prior to exon 4, predicted to impact only FL-TCF7L2 and lncRNA, excluding DN-TCF7L2 isoforms (NM_001349870.2, NM_001349871.1).

Of the 76 identified variants, 7 were shared by 2 patients each (14 patients total). The 7 shared variants among “Likely TRND” cases were: (1) NM_001367943.1:c.1268A>G, NP_001354872.1:p.(Tyr423Cys), NG_012631.1:g.207190A>G, NC_000010.11:g.113152439A>G; (2) NM_001367943.1:c.1001+1G>A, NP_001354872.1:p.?, NG_012631.1:g.205875G>A, NC_000010.11:g.113151124G>A; (3) NM_001367943.1:c.634_635dup, NP_001354872.1:p.(Pro213ArgfsTer13), NG_012631.1:g.196016_196017dup, NC_000010.11:g.113141265_113141266dup; (4) NM_001367943.1:c.162del, NP_001354872.1:p.(Asn55IlefsTer53), NG_012631.1:g.5669del, NC_000010.11:g.112950918del; (5) NM_001367943.1:c.686–1G>A, NP_001354872.1:p.?, NG_012631.1:g.198673G>A, NC_000010.11:g.113143922G>A; (6) NM_001367943.1:c.1172T>C, NP_001354872.1:p.(Leu391Pro), NG_012631.1:g.207094T>C, NC_000010.11:g.113152343T>C; and (7) NM_001367943.1:c.1604C>T, NP_001354872.1:p.(Ser535Leu), NG_012631.1:g.220467C>T, NC_000010.11:g.113165716C>T. All other variants were found in unique patients. The spectrum of TCF7L2 variants were mapped to the MANE select transcript, NM_001367943.1 (Figure 2). Patient cases with “Likely TRND” clustered at the C-terminal ends of the CTNNB1 binding domains and HMG Box domain. Variants were also clustered at a highly conserved region between both domains.

TRND patients most frequently presented with developmental, ophthalmologic, and orthopedic abnormalities

Of the 76 “Likely TRND” patients, they were marginally heavier and relatively average height compared to reference populations – with median weight and height Z-scores of 0.85 (IQR = −0.25–1.60) and 0.14 (−0.95–0.66), respectively. 81 HPO-standardized ontology terms were described by clinicians in at least 2 TRND patients as well as 10 broader categories of non-HPO, non-neurodevelopmental features. HPO codes associated with each term are listed in Supplemental Table 1. Among “Likely TRND” cases (Figure 3A–B, Supplemental Figure 1), nearly all presented with speech delay (61/64, 95.31%). Other frequently reported neurodevelopmental phenotypes included ASD (36/58, 62.07%, OMIM:209850), gross motor delay (29/58, 50.0%), ID (27/56, 48.21%), ADHD/ADD (20/47, 42.55%, OMIM:143465), learning disability (9/32, 28.13%), and fine motor delay (12/54, 22.22%). When degree of ID was clinician-reported or IQ scores were provided, most patients with ID were in the mild-to-moderate range (11/16, 68.75%); a minority (5/16, 31.25%) were classified as “severe” or overlapping with the “severe” range. Among patients for whom information was available, the median age of patients’ first words (n = 30; 7 non-verbal; 39 unknown) was markedly delayed to 30 months (IQR = 18.75–36) and their first steps/ability to walk (n = 28; 48 unknown) was mildly delayed to 15 months (IQR = 13.13–18.13). Among those with reported behavioral abnormalities, aggression (7/21, 33.33%) was among those most frequently reported, aside from ASD-like and ADHD-like features.

A few patients experienced regression (8/55, 14.55%), primarily of expressive language. Psychiatric diagnoses were less commonly reported (7/53, 13.21%), with anxiety most frequent (5/54, 9.26%, OMIM:607834). Other neurological complications included sleep abnormalities (16/56, 28.57%), coordination abnormalities or gait disturbances (12/53, 22.64%), hypotonia (12/54, 22.22%), seizures (8/56, 14.29%), and toe walking (6/53, 11.32%). Most seizures manifested as generalized (7/8, 87.5%), 4 were uncomplicated febrile seizures, and all onset at ≤7 years old or age was otherwise not specified.

The most frequently observed non-developmental features included ophthalmological conditions (38/58, 65.52%) – most frequently, myopia (29/54, 53.70%), strabismus (16/56, 28.57%, OMIM:185100), and astigmatism (6/52, 11.54%, OMIM:603047) – and musculoskeletal/orthopedic abnormalities (30/57, 52.63%) – most frequently abnormal thorax morphology (8/53, 15.09%), clinodactyly (5/54, 9.26%), Achilles tendon contractures (4/53, 7.55%), bilateral club feet (4/54, 7.41%), and pes planus (3/53, 5.66%). Three cases were described with insulin resistance/pre-diabetes (n = 1) or diabetes (type 1, n = 1, OMIM:222100; type 2, n = 1, OMIM:125853). Other notable conditions included respiratory/ENT abnormalities (18/55, 32.73%) – including chronic otitis media infections (12/54, 22.22%, OMIM:166760). Hearing deficits were described (8/52, 15.38%), many of which were conductive or due to a history of chronic infections. Abnormal brain imaging was reported in 10/27 (37.04%) patients with available magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). A subset of 4 patients demonstrated periventricular abnormalities on MRI, including periventricular hyperintensities, white matter volume loss, and/or leukomalacia. Two of these cases were described as having signs of upper motor neuron dysfunction and spastic paraplegia. While two had unremarkable pregnancies/deliveries, one was born prematurely and one was complicated by perinatal asphyxia.

Dysmorphic craniofacial features were frequent (44/60, 73.33%); however, specific features varied and were not uniformly observed. The most frequently reported features included abnormalities of ear morphology (13/59, 22.03%), hypertelorism and/or telecanthus (12/58, 20.69%, OMIM:145400), down-slanted palpebral fissures (11/58, 18.97%), as well as frontal bossing and/or prominent forehead (9/58, 15.52%).

Patients with shared genetic variants (n = 7 variants among 14 patients) had similar neurodevelopmental presentations; however, some features, such as craniofacial dysmorphic features and orthopedic abnormalities, were variably present, suggesting potential differences in penetrance of certain phenotypes and/or variable expression. For example, unrelated patients P11 and P18 (NM_001367943.1:c.162del, NP_001354872.1:p.(Asn55IlefsTer53), NG_012631.1:g.5669del, NC_000010.11:g.112950918del) both presented with speech delay, gross motor delay, concentration deficiency (no formal diagnosis of ADD/ADHD), behavioral issues, and myopia. However, P11 additionally presented with craniofacial dysmorphic features, orthopedic abnormalities (including abnormal thorax morphology), hypotonia, and absence of facial/axillary hair discordant with age. P18, by contrast, presented with a growth disorder with delayed bone age, primary enuresis, strabismus, no craniofacial dysmorphic features, and no orthopedic abnormalities.

Other patients with shared variants, P8 and P48 (NM_001367943.1:c.1172T>C, NP_001354872.1:p.(Leu391Pro), NG_012631.1:g.207094T>C, NC_000010.11:g.113152343T>C), had speech delay, concentration deficiency or diagnosed ADHD, normal initial motor development, myopia, orthopedics abnormalities (lumbar hyperlordosis vs. brachydactyly and syndactyly), and recurring ear infections and chronic otitis media. However, P48 had no neurologic features or craniofacial dysmorphic features, while P8 additionally presented with ASD, limb hypertonia and CN IV paresis, as well as craniofacial dysmorphic features (hypertelorism and downslanted palpebral fissures among others).

Compared to patients first identified in Dias et al.30 (n = 12), remaining cases in this cohort (n = 64) had no statistically significant differences across all HPO-standardized terms and categories of phenotypic features. There was a nominally increased frequency of ASD in our cohort (69.57% vs. 33.33% Dias; nominal p-value = 0.042) and decreased frequency of hypertrichosis (0% vs. 22.2% Dias; nominal p-value = 0.029). However, these differences did not survive correction for multiple hypothesis testing (Figure 3C, Supplemental Figure 2).

TRND patient phenotypes do not grossly cluster by genomic locus or transcript domain

Next, we sought to investigate if variant type and genomic locus were associated with phenotypic differences among patients. A comparison between variant types is displayed in Figure 3D and Supplemental Figure 3. When compared to non-CNV variant groups, patients with CNV variants exhibited higher frequencies of cardiac abnormalities compared to patients with missense (100.0% vs. 9.09% missense; BH-adjusted p-value = 0.029) and out-of-frame indel variants (100.0% vs. 7.14% out-of-frame indel; BH-adjusted p-value = 0.029). This difference was of nominal significance when compared to other variant types (splice-site, nonsense) but did not survive multiple hypothesis testing. Other inter-group differences were not significant following Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment, and differences of nominal significance are summarized in Supplemental Figure 3.

Despite the limited sample size, we investigated whether phenotypic differences existed among variants predicted to disrupt all 17 RefSeq isoforms of both FL- and DN-TCF7L2 (n = 72) vs. FL-TCF7L2 only (15 isoforms; n = 4) (Figure 3E). Variants predicted to affect the protein sequence of only FL-TCF7L2 isoforms were more frequently associated with short palpebral fissures (50.0% vs. 3.70%; nominal p-value = 0.021) and skin/hair/nails abnormalities (75.0% vs. 17.65%; nominal p-value = 0.029), including sparse lateral eyebrows (50.0% vs. 0%; nominal p-value = 0.004) and slow-growing hair (50.0% vs. 0%; nominal p-value = 0.004). However, these differences did not survive multiple hypothesis correction. While not statistically significant, 2/4 patients with variants predicted to impact only FL-TCF7L2 transcripts had notable endocrine abnormalities (50.0% vs. 10.9%; nominal p-value = 0.09), including a growth disorder with delayed bone age and absent axillary/facial hair in adolescence. Differences between the two isoform groups – across all HPO-standardized phenotypes – are summarized in Supplemental Figure 4.

Next, we sought to evaluate if clinical presentation varied based on variant location within the TCF7L2 gene (Supplemental Figure 5). Generally, specific phenotypes across patients were well-distributed in type and location across the TCF7L2 transcript but a few trends were noted. Much of the sub-phenotypes of orthopedics/musculoskeletal abnormalities were concentrated beyond exon 6. Notably, severe phenotypes, including seizures, did not grossly cluster with location on the TCF7L2 transcript but were often sequestered between exons 5 and 11 (Supplemental Figure 5). A subset of patients with variants clustering at the terminal end of the HMG Box domain presented with co-occurring craniofacial features, including frontal bossing, downslanted palpebral fissures, and ear morphology abnormalities.

Small retrospective adult cohort with pLOF TCF7L2 variants nominally associated with increased risk of T2D

To explore phenotypes in adults with pLOF TCF7L2 variants, we conducted a retrospective analysis of PMBB adults. Following enforcement of inclusion and exclusion criteria (Methods), 11 patients were identified – of whom 2 had nonsense, 7 splice-site, and 2 out-of-frame indel variants introducing a premature stop codon (Supplemental Table 5). All had 2 or more EHR clinician encounters documented. However, notably, none of the variants impacted all TCF7L2 isoforms. In fact, 9/11 impacted only one FL-TCF7L2 isoform. Notably, a neurodevelopmental phenotype could not be confirmed in any PMBB patients in this cohort (n = 11) potentially due to a lack of a neurodevelopmental phenotypes or limitations of this methodology and represents a distinct group compared to “Likely TRND” patients. The median age of this cohort was 55.06 years (IQR = 54.0–74.5), 7/11 (63.6%) were male, and median BMI was 31.75 (IQR = 26.78–35.44). Comparatively, the median age of the control cohort (n = 198) was 60.11 years old (p-value = 0.3944), 119/198 (60.1%) were male (p-value = 1), and median BMI was 28.60 (IQR = 24.85–32.61; p-value = 0.1539). The lack of neurodevelopmental phecodes, and inability to confirm a neurodevelopmental phenotype, limit the ability to make clear inferences from this cohort to TRND patients. PMBB patients did trend toward a marginally increased risk of some features identified from the “Likely TRND” cohort, including congenital deformities of the limbs/feet (OR = 9.8; nominal p-value = 0.15) and myopia (OR = 1.1375; nominal p-value = ~1) in the absence of increased risk of other congenital anomalies; however, these associations were not statistically significant.

Across all 1748 distributed phecodes, T2D with renal manifestations (OR = 5.81; 3/11 vs. 12/198; nominal p-value = 0.03) and chronic kidney disease (OR = 8.91; 3/11 vs. 8/198; nominal p-value = 0.01), were both represented at increased frequency among PMBB patients meeting pLOF TCF7L2 variant criteria. TCF7L2 has been historically associated with metabolic disorders; metabolic syndromes, including dysmetabolic syndrome X, were trending towards an association with patients in the PMBB cohort (OR = 6.5; nominal p-value = 0.196), but the difference was not statistically significant. Some non-metabolic pathologies were nominally associated with the PMBB patient cohort compared to controls, including unspecified diseases of hair and hair follicles (OR = 8.58; nominal p-value = 0.046). However, no differences survived multiple hypothesis testing. Neurodevelopmental and other phenotypic comparisons between the “Likely TRND” and PMBB pLOF TCF7L2 variant cohorts were not performed due to lack of overlapping phecodes, including age-relevant diagnoses. A composite summary of odds ratios and p-values for the full list of phecodes is provided in Supplemental Table 6.

Discussion

This study characterizes the largest cohort of patients to date with TCF7L2 pLOF variants constituting TCF7L2-related neurodevelopmental disorder, or TRND. There are several key findings arising from this study.

First, the spectrum of TCF7L2 genetic variants implicated in TRND includes missense, nonsense, indel, and splice region variants. CNV variants did not differ phenotypically with the exception of cardiovascular abnormalities. Consistent with Dias et al.20, a number of missense variants localized to the C-terminal ends of the HMG Box and CTNNB1 domains, though, de novo variants were also identified between the CTNNB1 and HMG Box domains and upstream of Exon 4. This study identifies TCF7L2 variants before Exon 4 and could yield insight into functional differences for FL- vs. DN-TCF7L2 transcripts. Variants without predicted effects on the coding sequence of DN-TCF7L2 transcripts were associated with differences in vision/ophthalmological, endocrine, and skin/hair/nails manifestations – though differences were not statistically significant and caution should be used in interpretation of this result. Additional cases are warranted, and if these differences are re-observed with greater sample size, one hypothesis is that impacting DN-TCF7L2 transcripts contributes to these differences. DN-TCF7L2 has unique patterns of selective tissue expression (e.g., DN-TCF7L2 expression has been detected in murine brain but not murine liver or pancreas), which can be postulated to contribute to these phenotypic differences38–39.

Second, across this expanded cohort, neurodevelopmental, visual, and orthopedic/musculoskeletal abnormalities were most prevalent and likely form the defining syndromic features of TRND (Figure 3A, Supplemental Figure 1). Craniofacial dysmorphism was observed in a majority of patients (44/60, 73.33%), with heterogeneous features – most commonly abnormal ear morphology, hypertelorism, down-slanted palpebral fissures, as well as frontal bossing and/or a prominent forehead. These findings align with Dias et al.20, though a nominally higher frequency of ASD and under-representation of hypertrichosis was observed; however, the Dias cohort was small (n = 12) and this did not survive multiple hypothesis correction. Increased ASD representation may be attributed to selection bias as ASD repositories and articles were searched to find patients in this study. Importantly, this study expands on and establishes a spectrum of sub-phenotypes across phenotypic categories. Abnormal thorax morphology, Achilles tendon contractures, and clinodactyly were the most prevalent orthopedic/musculoskeletal abnormalities while myopia, strabismus, and astigmatism were the most frequently represented ophthalmologic abnormalities. Phenotypic differences by isoform or variant type of nominal significance did not survive multiple hypothesis testing and should be interpreted with caution. Phenotypic differences also did not grossly cluster by genomic locus. Interestingly, some patients, such as SP1, had a de novo nonsense variant but no reported neurodevelopmental phenotype. Additionally, some patients (e.g., P11/18) shared variants but differed in key phenotypic traits. There are several potential explanations for this, including variable expressivity and/or differences in penetrance, additive contributions of other unknown variants, epigenetic differences, differences due to mode of inheritance (mosaicism and differences in mutational burden for germ-line vs. post-zygotic variants), and/or limitations in data collection (e.g., inter-observer differences) or timing of symptom manifestation. Functional studies on biospecimens from these patients may clarify mechanisms contributing to variable feature manifestation.

Third, this study includes a biobank-based search of thousands of adults for pLOF variants in TCF7L2. In a retrospective case-control analysis of PMBB patients, carriers of pLOF TCF7L2 variants demonstrated a nominally increased risk of T2D with renal manifestations (p-value = 0.03), but this did not survive multiple hypothesis testing and there was a trend towards increased BMI between the cohorts. Expanded searches across additional biobanks could clarify the validity of this association. Due to phecode restraints and the retrospective design, pediatric neurodevelopmental phenotypes (and other indirect phenotypic markers of the “Likely TRND” group) of these adult PMBB patients could not be assessed. In addition to ambiguity added by absence of a reported neurodevelopmental phenotype, interpretation of these findings is further limited by nominal significance and absence of functional evidence supporting loss-of-function. Thus, direct comparisons with the pediatric group and inference of relative risk in TRND are not possible. Neurodevelopmental follow-up of adult PMBB patients and validation of functionally reduced TCF7L2 expression of patients’ banked samples is required to: (1) correlate with TRND’s natural history; and (2) estimate TRND population incidence.

Since TCF7L2’s discovery as a locus strongly associated with T2D4, there has been controversial evidence regarding how intronic risk alleles put patients at increased risk for T2D – as a result of agonistic or antagonistic effects on TCF7L2 expression or non-TCF7L2-mediated mechanisms. In the primary “Likely TRND” cohort, only 2 cases of diabetes were identified – one of whom was an adult with T2D – potentially reflecting the patient cohort’s young median age. Future functional investigation of this relationship is needed through patient-derived sources (e.g., induced pluripotent stem cells)40.

This study has several limitations. First, uniform characterization of patients was challenging due to heterogeneous identification methods, leading to potential data gaps and increased susceptibility to ascertainment bias. Further, as previously noted, searching ASD repositories may introduce selection bias, increasing representation of ASD. We sought to mitigate both of these risks by implementing HPO-standardized ontology terms across cases and differentiating absence (“0”) of phenotypic features vs. lack of clinical information (“N/A”) to accommodate differences in confidence in age-specific clinical assessment. Making this distinction mitigated the dilution of features that would otherwise be under-represented by inclusion of patients from these repositories. Importantly, no other features were grossly enriched among these patients that would be likely also over-represented with ASD. Secondly, retrospective EHR data analysis has inherent limitations, including incomplete or inaccurate documentation of clinical features, lack of longitudinal data predating tertiary care at PennMedicine, and potential ascertainment/referral bias. The adult cohort further lacks neurodevelopmental phenotyping, limiting direct comparisons with the pediatric TRND group. Thirdly, by nature of this investigation, a lack of functional studies limits validation of some identified variants, and, toward that end, in vitro and in vivo validation experiments replicating these variants would further corroborate these findings.

In conclusion, this study establishes an expanded genotypic and phenotypic spectrum for TRND that requires further investigation and functional validation. We have launched a natural history study with Simons Searchlight (https://www.simonssearchlight.org/research/what-we-study/tcf7l2/) that is open for enrollment to further characterize TRND patients.
