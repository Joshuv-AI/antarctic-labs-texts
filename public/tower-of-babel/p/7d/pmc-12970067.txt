High-dose taurine supplementation exacerbates alcohol-associated liver disease by inducing gut microbiota dysbiosis and bile acid dysregulation in mice
Authors: Jingdan Pei, Lixian Chen, Rahul Pushparaj, Panpan Huang, Guihua Pan, Chunbao Sun, Xing Gao, Lihua Zhang, Md Manirujjaman, Chiung-Kuei Huang, Peng-Sheng Ting, Zhongbin Deng, Shaoyu Chen, Xiang Zhang, Vatsalya Vatsalya, Craig J McClain, Wenke Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundβ-aminoethanesulfonic acid (taurine) is a conditionally essential amino acid that plays critical roles in bile acid (BA) conjugation, antioxidative defence and metabolic regulation. Previous studies showed that faecal taurine level was reduced in patients with alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD), suggesting that taurine supplementation may have beneficial effects. This study aimed to determine whether oral taurine supplementation prevents the development of ALD in mice and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.MethodsA total of 8-week-old male mice were subjected to a chronic-plus-binge ALD model. Taurine was administered orally via the diet for ten days before and during ethanol exposure. Faecal 16S ribosomal RNA metagenomic analysis, liver RNA sequencing and BA profiling were performed.ResultsHigh-dose taurine supplementation (3 g/kg body weight/day) was associated with worsened ethanol-induced liver injury, as indicated by increased serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels, hepatic steatosis, apoptosis and inflammation. At the molecular level, high-dose taurine treatment was associated with reduced Cpt1a expression, altered expression of genes involved in fatty acid β-oxidation and lipogenic gene Fasn, and decreased expression of Baat, accompanied by changes in taurine-conjugated BA profiles. These alterations were accompanied by changes in BA composition and intestinal FXR-associated gene expression. Taurine supplementation was also associated with shifts in gut microbial composition, including enrichment of hydrogen sulfide-producing bacteria, increased microbial H2S production, impaired intestinal barrier-related parameters and increased bacterial translocation to the liver, paralleling enhanced hepatic inflammatory responses. In contrast, low-dose taurine supplementation (0.2 g/kg body weight/day) was associated with improved liver phenotypes, including reduced steatosis, lower serum ALT and AST levels, decreased Fasn expression and enhanced BA conjugation. Collectively, these results indicate a dose-dependent association between taurine supplementation and ALD-related outcomes.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that high-dose taurine supplementation is associated with unfavourable alterations in gut microbiota composition, intestinal barrier integrity, BA metabolism and hepatic taurine-related pathways in ALD, coinciding with exacerbated liver injury. In contrast, low-dose taurine supplementation was associated with improved hepatic outcomes. These results highlight the importance of dose considerations in taurine supplementation and support the concept that taurine may exert divergent effects on ALD depending on the administered dose.Keywords: Liver Diseases, Alcoholic; Taurine; Bile Acids and Salts; Gastrointestinal Microbiome; Intestinal Barrier Function

Introduction

β-aminoethanesulfonic acid (taurine) is a conditionally essential amino acid that plays a critical role in various physiological functions. It is the most abundant free amino acid in many tissues, including the brain, heart and liver. While dietary intake, primarily from meat and seafood,1 is the main source of taurine, a small amount of taurine can also be synthesised in the liver from cysteine or methionine.2 Taurine is involved in bile acid (BA) conjugation,3 osmoregulation,4 calcium homeostasis5 and antioxidative defence,6 making it essential for maintaining cellular function and metabolic balance.

An increasing number of studies suggest that taurine supplementation exerts protective effects against alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) through multiple mechanisms. Research has demonstrated that taurine stimulates alcohol metabolism by enhancing the activity of alcohol dehydrogenase and aldehyde dehydrogenase, leading to improved fat metabolism.7 Additionally, taurine has been shown to inhibit hepatocyte apoptosis8 and prevent alcohol-induced reductions in serum adiponectin levels, which play roles in metabolic regulation.9 Its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties help counteract alcohol-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in the liver.10 Furthermore, taurine influences lipid metabolism by regulating the expression of genes involved in lipogenesis and fatty acid oxidation.11

Despite its potential benefits, the safe dosage range for taurine supplementation remains unclear. Although taurine is generally considered safe, excessive intake may have unintended consequences, particularly in individuals with pre-existing liver disease. Low-dose taurine supplementation (50–500 mg/kg body weight (bw)) has been reported to exert beneficial effects in multiple disease contexts, including neuroprotection12 and attenuation of liver fibrosis.13 In contrast, long-term high-dose taurine administration (4 g/kg or 16 g/kg bw) has been associated with adverse outcomes in mouse models, such as hepatic lipid accumulation14 and growth retardation,15 respectively. In a rat model of chronic alcohol exposure, taurine supplementation at 30 g/L in drinking water increased circulating adiponectin levels, a response considered protective against alcohol-associated fatty liver disease.9 A recent study reported that taurine enhances alcohol intake and promotes anxiolytic-like behaviours, suggesting a possible role in modulating alcohol consumption.16 Additionally, a case report described the development of acute hepatitis in a patient who consumed large amounts of taurine-containing energy drinks.17 Surveys indicate that taurine intake is widespread, particularly among young adults. One study found that 51% of college students regularly consume energy drinks, and 99% of these individuals mix energy drinks with alcohol.18 Despite these findings, there is a lack of research investigating the effects of taurine on liver pathophysiology in individuals who consume alcohol.

Our recent studies have provided new insights into the relationship between taurine and ALD. We found that faecal taurine concentrations were significantly lower in patients with moderate alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH) compared with healthy controls, as well as in alcohol-fed (AF) mice compared with pair-fed (PF) controls.19 This reduction was linked to disruptions in the intestinal taurine upregulated gene 1-microRNA 194-farnesoid X receptor (FXR)-fibroblast growth factor 15/19 (FGF15/19) signalling pathway.19 Based on these findings, we hypothesised that taurine supplementation would compensate for taurine loss and mitigate alcohol-induced liver injury.

Material and methods

Animal study

Male C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks of age) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA). All animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tulane University. Mice were maintained at 22°C with a 12-hour:12-hour light and dark cycle and access to food and water ad libitum.

The chronic-plus-binge ethanol feeding model was used in this study following the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) alcohol exposure protocol.20 Mice were fed the Lieber DeCarli liquid diet containing 5% alcohol (v/v) (AF, Bio-Serv, Flemington, NJ, USA, F1258SP) or isocaloric maltose dextrin (PF, Bio-Serv, F1259SP). Briefly, AF or PF mice were initially fed with a control Lieber-DeCarli diet ad libitum for 2 days to acclimatise them to a liquid diet and tube feeding. For the AF treated group, the concentration of alcohol was then gradually increased from 1.6% to 5% (v/v) over the following 4 days and maintained at 5% for 10 days. PF mice were fed with the isocaloric control diet (maltose dextrin). On day 11, AF and PF mice were orally gavaged with a single dose of ethanol (5 g/kg bw) or isocaloric maltose dextrin solution, respectively. The mice were euthanised 9 hours later, and tissues were collected for further analysis. For taurine groups, taurine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, T0625) was supplemented at two doses (3 g/kg and 0.2 g/kg bw daily) in drinking water for 10 days before the liquid diet feeding and incorporated into the liquid diet for 16 days.

Serum ALT and AST assay

Whole blood samples were collected and allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 4000×g for 30 min at room temperature and carefully transferred to fresh tubes. Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels were determined using commercial assay kits (ALT: Cat. No. A7526-150; AST: Cat. No. A7561-150, Pointe Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Liver triglycerides assay

Liver tissue was weighed and homogenised in 50 mM NaCl, followed by lipid extraction with chloroform:methanol (2:1, 4 mL). The mixture was left to stand overnight at 4°C and then centrifuged (1800×g, for 20 min at room temperature), the lower chloroform phase was collected and its volume was recorded. The chloroform layer was transferred to a clean glass tube and evaporated to dryness under a fume hood. Dried lipids were dissolved in 1% Triton X-100, and triglyceride (TG) concentrations were determined using a commercial assay kit (Cat. No. T7532-120, Pointe Scientific, Canton, MI, USA).

Histological, immunofluorescence, immunohistochemistry analysis and TUNEL assay

Liver and intestine sections from paraffin-embedded tissues were prepared at a thickness of 5 µm. Liver and intestine pathology was examined by haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Immunofluorescence (IF) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining were conducted according to the protocol established by Jiang et al.19 Tissue sections were immunolabelled with the following primary antibodies: anti-F4/80 (1:100 dilution; #70076, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-E. coli (1:100 dilution; ab137967, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and anti-claudin-1 (1:100 dilution; ab307692, Abcam). Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase–mediated dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL)-positive cells were identified by the Assay kit (S7100, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Bile acid analysis

Liver and faecal BA profiling was carried out following the methods described in our previous study.21 Briefly, liver BAs were extracted with acetonitrile:H2O (4/1, v/v) and centrifuged at 14 000×g for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was lyophilised, reconstituted in H2O and the BAs were extracted by solid phase extraction in an OASIS HLB cartridge (Waters, Milford, Massachusetts, USA). The eluents were analysed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Faecal samples were homogenised in 80% acetonitrile in water in the ratio of 1:10 (mg:µL). The remaining steps were the same to those used for liver samples. The concentration of each BA in a biological sample was calculated using a calibration curve constructed from the LC-MS/MS data of a corresponding BA standard.

Faecal 16S rRNA metagenomic analysis

Faecal DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Faecal microbial community composition was assessed by Illumina MiSeq sequencing targeting the V3–V4 regions of the 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene. Sequencing and data analysis were performed following our previously described methods.19 21

Taurine concentration analysis

Taurine concentrations in liver, faecal and serum samples were determined using a Taurine Assay Kit (MET-5071, Cell Biolabs, San Diego, CA, USA). Briefly, weighed tissue or faecal aliquots were homogenised, while serum was used directly. The taurine concentration in the supernatants (for tissues and faecal samples) or in serum was measured according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Results for liver and faeces were normalised to the initial sample weight and expressed as nmol per mg; whereas serum taurine concentration was expressed as µM.

Western blotting analysis

Liver proteins were isolated by RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. Then, western blot analysis was performed using antibodies against cysteine sulfonic acid decarboxylase (Csad) (ab91016, Abcam), carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (Cpt1a) (ab234111, Abcam), fatty acid synthase (Fasn) (ab22759, Abcam) and Toll-like receptor 4 (Tlr4) (sc-293072, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA). The blots were developed by using ECL Western blotting reagents (34095, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and visualised by Bio-Rad ChemiDoc System (Hercules, California, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalised to β-actin.

Bulk RNA-sequencing analysis and real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA from liver and ileal tissues was isolated with TRIzol (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Bulk RNA-seq libraries were prepared using TruSeq RNA CD indexes kit and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq2000 platform (San Diego, California, USA), yielding ~20 million paired-end reads per sample; data were processed using Illumina DRAGEN and analysed with standard differential expression thresholds (adjusted p<0.05, |log2FC|≥2).22 For real-time PCR analysis, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesised from the isolated RNA using the qScript cDNA SuperMix (95 048–100, Quantabio, Beverly, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative Real-Time PCR was performed using Power Up SYBR Green Master Mix (A25742, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primers used in this study are listed in the online supplemental table 1. Data were expressed as relative messenger RNA (mRNA) levels using the △△CT method.

Gut hydrogen sulfide production assay

Mouse cecum contents were collected and suspended in phosphate-buffered saline. The suspensions were transferred to a 96-well plate and supplemented with 10 mM cysteine and 1 mM pyridoxal-5-phosphate. A piece of Whatman (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) Filter Paper soaked in 20 mM lead acetate was dried and placed over the 96-well plate. The plate was covered with a lid and incubated at 37°C for 3–5 hours. The formation of lead sulfide was indicated by dark circles on the filter paper, which were recorded by an imager. The resulting images were analysed using ImageJ to quantify H2S production.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as the mean±SEM. Differences between groups were analysed by unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance followed by a post hoc test where appropriate. The level of significance was set at p<0.05. Statistical analyses and generation of graphs were performed with GraphPad Prism (Version 9.2.0; GraphPad Software, LLC; San Diego, California, USA).

Results

High-dose taurine supplementation exacerbated alcohol-induced liver injury

Previous studies have shown that patients with AH have lower faecal taurine concentrations compared with healthy individuals.19 We hypothesised that taurine supplementation would be beneficial for subjects with ALD. To test this, we administered taurine at 3 g/kg bw/day to mice fed with an alcohol-containing Lieber-DeCarli diet using the NIAAA chronic-plus-binge model (figure 1A). Surprisingly, taurine supplementation at this dose exacerbated alcohol-induced liver injury. The liver-to-bw ratio was significantly increased in AF mice compared with PF controls, which was further increased by taurine supplementation (figure 1B). Similarly, liver TG levels were elevated in AF mice and further increased with taurine supplementation (AF group supplemented with 3 g/kg taurine) (figure 1C), indicating enhanced hepatic fat accumulation, particularly in taurine-treated AF mice. Liver steatosis was further confirmed by H&E staining of liver sections (figure 1D). Serum ALT and AST levels were elevated in AF mice and further increased by taurine supplementation (figure 1E). TUNEL staining revealed increased hepatocyte apoptosis in AF mice, which was further exacerbated in mice treated with taurine (figure 1F).

Taurine supplementation decreased liver Cpt1α and increased Fasn expression

To investigate the underlying mechanism by which the supplement of taurine increased fatty liver in AF mice, we performed RNA-seq analysis on liver tissues. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear distinction in gene expression between AF group supplemented with 3 g/kg taurine and AF mice (figure 2A). The volcano plot indicated that a substantial number of genes were upregulated, while fewer genes were downregulated in response to taurine supplementation (online supplemental figure S1A). Clustering analysis (online supplemental figure S1B) demonstrated distinct gene expression patterns between groups. Notably, several upregulated genes were associated with liver injury markers, including Sult1e1,23 Defb124 and Moxd125 (online supplemental figure S1C). Pathway analysis identified the top five upregulated pathways in taurine-supplemented AF mice, including glucuronidation, oxidation, Phase II-conjugation of compounds, metabolism of lipids and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (figure 2B).

However, despite the upregulation of the TCA cycle, genes associated with the initiation of β-oxidation, such as Tbk1, Cpt1a, Ppard, Cpt2, Acox1 and Acadl, were downregulated following taurine supplementation (figure 2C). Cpt1a, a key enzyme that catalyses the transfer of the long-chain acyl group from acyl-CoA to carnitine, allowing fatty acids to enter the mitochondrial matrix for oxidation, was significantly reduced at both the mRNA and protein levels in AF mice with taurine supplementation (figure 2D,E). In addition, the taurine supplementation increased the expression of a lipogenic protein, Fasn, in AF mice (figure 2F). Further analysis of the liver RNA-seq data showed that the expression of additional genes involved in lipogenesis exhibited a trend toward increased expression by taurine supplementation (online supplemental figure S1D). Collectively, these data suggest that taurine supplementation impairs fatty acid oxidation while increasing lipogenesis, contributing to increased hepatic steatosis in AF mice.

Taurine levels in faeces, serum and hepatic tissues

Next, we measured taurine levels in mouse samples. Faecal taurine concentrations were decreased by alcohol feeding but remained unchanged by taurine supplementation (figure 3A). Ileum mRNA levels of the major taurine transporter, Taut1,26 were not affected by alcohol feeding, but were increased by taurine supplementation in both PF and AF mice (figure 3B). Serum taurine levels showed an increase with alcohol feeding compared with pair-feeding, and taurine supplementation significantly elevated serum taurine concentrations in PF mice but had no effect in AF mice (figure 3C). Interestingly, liver taurine levels were significantly reduced by alcohol feeding, and supplementation failed to restore them (figure 3D). Unlike in the ileum, liver Taut1 mRNA levels decreased with alcohol feeding and remained unaffected by taurine supplementation (figure 3E).

We next examined taurine de novo synthesis (figure 3F). Immunoblot and quantitative PCR analysis of liver tissues revealed a significant reduction in Csad, the key enzyme in taurine biosynthesis,27 following taurine supplementation in both PF and AF mice (figure 3G,H). In addition, mRNA expression of another taurine de novo synthesis protein, Fmo1, was slightly increased by taurine supplementation in PF mice and reduced in AF mice. However, taurine treatment failed to increase its expression in AF mice (figure 3I). These findings indicate that high taurine concentration suppresses hepatic taurine de novo synthesis without affecting taurine transport in the liver.

High-dose taurine supplementation exacerbated liver inflammation and bacterial translocation

A 3 g/kg taurine supplementation significantly increased hepatic macrophage activation (figure 4A) and mRNA expression of Tnfα and Cxcl2 in AF mice (figure 4B), indicating an exacerbated inflammation. RNA-seq analysis on liver tissues showed that additional proinflammatory molecules were moderately increased by taurine supplementation (online supplemental figure S1E). Intestinal bacterial translocation is a hallmark of ALD, and it is known to trigger liver inflammation. We quantified liver bacterial levels using 16S rRNA PCR. As shown in figure 4C, liver 16S rRNA levels were elevated by taurine supplementation in PF mice. Alcohol feeding increased hepatic 16S rRNA levels significantly, which were further increased by taurine supplementation in AF mice. To further investigate bacterial translocation, we performed E. coli protein staining in liver sections. Alcohol feeding increased E. coli presence, and it was further exacerbated by taurine supplementation in AF mice (figure 4D), indicating worsening bacterial translocation. Tlr4 is a key mediator of bacterial recognition and inflammatory signalling. Liver Tlr4 protein levels were significantly increased by alcohol feeding. While taurine supplementation increased Tlr4 protein in PF mice, no further increase was observed in AF mice (figure 4E).

Next, we assessed intestinal histology and barrier-related parameters. Ileal H&E staining showed that alcohol feeding reduced villus and crypt length and taurine supplementation during alcohol feeding was associated with intestinal enterocyte damage (figure 4F). Alcohol feeding significantly reduced the mRNA expression of Cldn1, a key tight junction protein in the intestinal epithelium, and taurine supplementation was associated with a further reduction in Cldn1 expression. Consistent with these findings, IF analysis of the ileum showed altered claudin-1 protein localisation, with focal loss along the villus epithelium in AF mice; this alteration appeared more pronounced in mice receiving taurine supplementation (figure 4G). Together, these data indicate that alcohol feeding is associated with impaired intestinal barrier integrity and suggest that taurine supplementation may further exacerbate this defect under the experimental conditions.

Taurine supplementation caused dysbiosis

Gut dysbiosis is a hallmark of alcohol-induced liver injury. To assess the impact of taurine supplementation on gut microbial composition, we performed 16S rRNA sequencing of faecal microbiota. A 3 g/kg taurine supplementation led to a marginal decrease in observed operational taxonomic units (OTUs) and Chao1 indices, indicating a reduction in α-diversity (figure 5A). PCA and principal coordinates analysis revealed a clear separation of gut bacterial communities between the AF group supplemented with 3 g/kg taurine and AF mice, suggesting a substantial shift in microbial composition (figure 5B). At the phylum level, taurine supplementation resulted in an increase of Verrucomicrobiota and a decrease of Firmicutes (figure 5C). At the genus level, we observed an increase in bacteria associated with detrimental roles28,30 and a reduction in beneficial bacteria31 (figure 5D,E). Since hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) is linked to oxidative stress and gut barrier dysfunction, and taurine supplementation has been shown to increase H₂S levels,32 we analysed the abundance of H₂S-producing bacteria in AF mice. As shown in figure 5F, taurine supplementation increased the presence of H₂S-producing bacteria. Prevotella33, a known H₂S-producing bacterium with detrimental effects on gut health, was markedly enriched in the taurine-supplemented AF mice (figure 5G). To confirm the functional impact of these microbial changes, we measured H₂S levels in caecal contents. As shown in figure 5H, alcohol feeding led to a slight increase in H₂S levels, which was significantly elevated by taurine supplementation in AF mice. Overall, these findings suggest that taurine supplementation exacerbates gut dysbiosis in AF mice, promoting an overgrowth of H₂S-producing bacteria. This microbial shift is likely responsible for the increased oxidative stress and intestinal barrier dysfunction observed in taurine-supplemented AF mice.

Taurine supplementation altered bile acid profiles

BAs are conjugated with taurine during synthesis to reduce their toxicity.2 34 Liver BAs were profiled using LC/MS (online supplemental figure S2A). Total hepatic BAs revealed a trend toward an increase in the AF group, which appeared to be partially reduced by taurine supplementation, although these differences did not reach statistical significance (online supplemental figure S2B). Notably, the ratio of taurine-conjugated-BAs (tauro-BAs) to total BAs remained unchanged in PF mice and showed a tendency to decrease in AF mice following taurine supplementation (figure 6A).

Previous studies have shown that certain tauro-conjugated BAs, such as tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) and taurohyodeoxycholic acid (THDCA), have hepatoprotective effects through FXR activation, despite their low concentrations.35 36 In our study, taurine treatment tended to increase hepatic THDCA levels in PF mice but decreased in AF mice, while TUDCA levels remained unchanged in PF mice and showed a tendency to decrease in AF mice (figure 6B). The reduction in tauro-conjugated BAs led us to examine two key enzymes involved in BA conjugation (figure 6C). Alcohol feeding decreased BA Coenzyme A synthetase mRNA levels, which remained unchanged with taurine supplementation in both PF and AF mice. However, taurine supplementation reduced BA-CoA: amino acid N-acetyltransferase (Baat) mRNA expression in AF mice (figure 6D), explaining the observed decrease in tauro-conjugated BAs (tauro-BAs) following taurine supplementation. The ratio of hepatic taurine-conjugated βMCA to unconjugated βMCA, a parameter used to infer BA conjugation-related effects on FXR signalling because taurine conjugation enhances the FXR antagonistic activity of βMCA, did not differ among the experimental groups (figure 6E). In addition, the expression of the FXR target gene Shp, Ntcp and Bsep remained unchanged with taurine supplementation in both PF and AF mice (figure 6F). These results suggest that the counteracting effects of different tauro-BAs may explain the unchanged liver FXR activity.

Next, we analysed the faecal BA profile (online supplemental figure S2C). Alcohol feeding reduced total faecal BA levels, suggesting an impaired BA elimination, and this effect was not altered by taurine supplementation in either PF or AF groups (online supplemental figure S2D). Faecal tauro-BA and total BA levels remained unaltered between PF and AF groups but were significantly increased by taurine supplementation in AF mice (figure 6G). Alcohol feeding significantly increased faecal TβMCA:βMCA ratio, while taurine supplementation had no effect on the ratio in PF mice but increased them modestly in AF mice (figure 6H). Moreover, the ileal Shp mRNA expression, an indicator of intestinal FXR activity, was reduced by alcohol feeding and further suppressed by taurine supplementation (figure 6I). Additional FXR target genes, including ileal Ostb and Fgf15, were not significantly changed among the experimental groups (figure 6I). Together, these data are consistent with a selective alteration of intestinal FXR-associated gene expression in response to alcohol feeding and taurine supplementation, while hepatic FXR target gene expression remained largely unchanged under the conditions examined.

A low dose of taurine supplementation alleviated alcohol-induced liver injury

As demonstrated above, supplementation with 3 g/kg bw/day taurine exacerbated alcohol-induced liver injury. We hypothesised that a lower dose of taurine might have beneficial effects. To test this, we supplemented mice with 0.2 g/kg bw/day (AF group supplemented with 0.2 g/kg) taurine in the NIAAA model of binge-on-chronic alcohol feeding. Interestingly, this lower dose did not exhibit liver toxicity; instead, it improved liver health, as evidenced by the lower hepatic fat accumulation (figure 7A) and showed a downward trend in serum ALT and a marked decrease in AST (figure 7B). Liver taurine concentrations showed only a marginal decrease (figure 7C), and Csad protein levels remained unchanged (figure 7D), suggesting that, unlike the 3 g/kg taurine supplementation, low-dose taurine does not suppress hepatic taurine de novo synthesis. Notably, liver tauro-BA levels were significantly increased, as indicated by the higher tauro-BA:total BA ratios (figure 7E). Although RNA-seq analysis revealed an upregulation of Cpt1a expression (figure 7F), it was not significantly different at the protein level (figure 7G). Additionally, a 0.2 g/kg taurine supplementation reduced Fasn protein levels in AF mice, indicating a suppression of hepatic lipogenesis (figure 7H). These findings suggest that a low dose of taurine may provide protective effects by enhancing fatty acid oxidation and reducing lipogenesis.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that taurine supplementation at 3 g/kg bw/day exacerbates alcohol-induced liver injury in a mouse model of ALD, contrary to our initial hypothesis that taurine might be beneficial in ALD. Instead of providing hepatoprotection, high-dose taurine worsened hepatic steatosis and inflammation in AF mice (figure 8). The increased liver-to-bw ratio, elevated TG accumulation, upregulated Fasn and downregulated Cpt1a indicate that taurine promotes hepatic lipogenesis and reduces fatty acid oxidation in the presence of alcohol. Furthermore, the exacerbation of hepatocyte apoptosis and inflammatory cytokine expression suggests that high-dose taurine intensifies alcohol-induced liver damage rather than mitigating it.

The detrimental effects of high-dose taurine were associated with alterations in taurine metabolism. While serum taurine levels increased with supplementation, hepatic taurine levels remained unchanged in AF mice. The apparent discrepancy between systemic and hepatic taurine levels reflects differences in compartmental regulation and hepatic taurine handling rather than a disproportionate response to dosing. Serum taurine represents the circulating pool, which is strongly influenced by intestinal absorption and renal handling. In contrast, hepatic taurine reflects the intracellular free taurine pool, which is tightly regulated and not stored in large quantities in hepatocytes. Moreover, in the liver, taurine is rapidly used for BA conjugation and biliary secretion rather than accumulation. The suppression of a key enzyme in taurine biosynthesis, Csad, and a major taurine uptake transporter, Taut1, further supports this notion. These findings suggest that high-dose taurine intake is associated with alterations in hepatic mechanisms involved in taurine homeostasis, which may coincide with broader metabolic changes. In addition to its role in taurine biosynthesis, Csad has been implicated in metabolic regulation; for example, overexpression of Csad has been reported to attenuate high-fat diet-induced hepatic lipid accumulation.37 In the present study, the marked reduction in Csad expression observed with high-dose taurine supplementation occurred in parallel with increased hepatic lipid accumulation following alcohol exposure. While these observations are consistent with a potential association between reduced Csad expression and altered lipid homeostasis, the precise mechanisms by which Csad may influence hepatic lipid metabolism remain unclear.

Additionally, taurine supplementation altered BA metabolism. Although taurine is a known conjugating agent for BA, the expected increase in tauro-conjugated BA was not observed. Instead, Baat mRNA levels were significantly reduced in AF mice, correlating with decreased tauro-BA levels. This suggests that high-dose taurine supplementation may impair BA conjugation, potentially exacerbating BA-related toxicity in the liver. Despite prior studies suggesting that tauro-BAs activate FXR signalling,38 we did not observe significant changes in hepatic FXR activity. Instead, taurine supplementation suppressed intestinal FXR activation, as indicated by reduced ileum Shp mRNA expression. FXR signalling is highly compartmentalised along the gut–liver axis, and intestinal FXR is particularly sensitive to changes in BA composition, conjugation and microbial metabolism.19 39 The selective reduction in intestinal SHP, in the absence of changes in hepatic SHP or hepatic BSEP expression, suggests that alcohol and taurine supplementation preferentially disrupt intestinal FXR activation without globally suppressing hepatic FXR signalling. This pattern is consistent with a model in which alcohol-induced alterations in gut BA composition and microbiota-derived BA transformation primarily affect FXR-dependent transcriptional programmes in the intestine.

Alcohol-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction increases gut permeability, enabling translocation of microbes and microbial products into the portal circulation, which activates hepatic inflammation and exacerbates liver injury.40 41 A critical finding of our study is that high-dose taurine supplementation aggravates gut dysbiosis and bacterium translocation. The 16S rRNA sequencing revealed a significant shift in gut microbial composition, with an increase in H₂S-producing bacteria, Prevotella. Elevated gut H₂S levels suggest that taurine supplementation creates an intestinal environment favouring microbial populations associated with oxidative stress and intestinal permeability dysfunction.

Transcriptomic analysis further supports the role of taurine supplementation in disrupting hepatic metabolism regulation. RNA-seq analysis revealed that taurine supplementation upregulated the TCA cycle, yet paradoxically, it downregulated Cpt1a, the key transporter involved in fatty acid β-oxidation. This suggests that while taurine may enhance energy metabolism through the TCA cycle, it concurrently suppresses fatty acid oxidation, leading to lipid accumulation in the liver. The reduction in Cpt1a expression at both mRNA and protein levels further highlights the inhibitory effect of taurine on mitochondrial fatty acid utilisation, which is likely to contribute to the exacerbation of hepatic steatosis.

Interestingly, our findings suggest that low-dose taurine supplementation (0.2 g/kg bw/day) exerts hepatoprotective effects in ALD, in contrast to the detrimental impact observed at higher doses. Unlike high-dose taurine, low-dose supplementation reduced serum ALT and AST levels, hepatic lipid accumulation and Fasn expression, suggesting a reduction in lipogenesis. Notably, tauro-BA levels increased in low-dose taurine-supplemented mice, suggesting that this dosage may support BA conjugation and metabolism without disrupting hepatic or intestinal FXR signalling.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. The high dose of taurine that exacerbated ALD in mice may exceed physiologically relevant intake levels in humans; therefore, the dose-dependent effects warrant further systematic investigation. Although high-dose taurine was associated with alterations in BA composition, gut microbiota and intestinal barrier function, causal relationships were not directly established, and the contribution of endogenous taurine—including potential feedback regulation of de novo synthesis—remains unclear. Importantly, these limitations do not detract from the central finding that taurine exerts distinct, dose-dependent effects on ALD, with high-dose supplementation worsening disease severity in this mouse model.

In summary, our study highlights a dose-dependent effect of taurine on alcohol-induced liver injury. While low-dose taurine supplementation appears beneficial by promoting fatty acid oxidation and maintaining BA homeostasis, high-dose taurine supplementation disrupts metabolic balance with reduced liver taurine synthesis and BA conjugation, exacerbates gut dysbiosis and weakens intestinal barrier function, ultimately worsening liver injury. These findings underscore the importance of carefully considering taurine dosage in the context of liver disease and suggest that while taurine may hold therapeutic potential, excessive supplementation may be detrimental. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying these dose-dependent effects and to determine the optimal taurine concentration for therapeutic use in ALD.
