Investigation of a hyperspectral Scanning Laser Optical Tomography setup for label-free cell identification
Authors: Hannes Benecke, Sonja Johannsmeier, Tobias May, Tammo Ripken
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of non-destructive, tomographic imaging systems is a current topic of research in biomedical technologies. One of these technologies is Scanning Laser Optical Tomography (SLOT), which features a highly modular setup with various contrast mechanisms. Extending this technology with new acquisition mechanisms allows us to investigate untreated and non-stained biological samples, leaving their natural biological physiology intact. To enhance the development of SLOT, we aimed to extend the density of information with a significant increase of acquisition channels. This should allow us to investigate samples with unknown emission spectra and even allow for label-fee cell identification. We developed and integrated a hyperspectral module into an existing SLOT system. The adaptations allow for the acquisition of three-dimensional datasets containing a highly increased information density. For validation, artificial test objects were made from fluorescent acrylic and acquired with the new hyperspectral setup. In addition, measurements were made on two different human cell spheroids with an unknown spectra, to test the possibilities of label-free cell identification. The validation measurements of the artificial test target show the expected results. Furthermore, the measurements of the biological cell spheroids show small variations in their tomographic spectrum that allow for label-free cell type differentiation. The results of the biological sample demonstrate the potential of label-free cell identification of the newly developed setup.Keywords: Hyperspectral imaging, Tomography, Scanning Laser Optical TomographySubject terms: Lasers, LEDs and light sources; Optics and photonics; Optical techniques; Imaging and sensing; Optical spectroscopy

Introduction

Non-destructive tomographic imaging has become a widely used tool in the field of biomedical imaging. Since the discovery of X-rays in 18951, many different tomographic devices with different kinds of contrast mechanisms have been developed. From the classical computer aided tomography2 and ultrasonography utilizing soundwaves3 to the use of light for modern optical coherence tomographs4, various technologies can be used for different purposes. Similarly, there are several different implementations of optical tomographs. Each variation of optical setup has its own benefits. Different solutions have been developed to meet the trade-off between resolution and sample size. A two-photon excitation microscope5 features high resolution and high sensitivity, but also possesses a limited imaging depth. Light sheet microscopes provide good resolution at high imaging depth, but require the samples to be optically clear and cannot detect high-order harmonic generation signals5. Our department developed the Scanning Laser Optical Tomography (SLOT) in 2011 for the investigation of in vitro samples6. SLOT was intended to be a derivative of Optical Projection Tomography7 with an improvement for scattering biological samples. The technology now features a highly modular setup with a high signal-to-noise-ratio, able to detect transmission, scattering, fluorescence and second harmonic generation signals6,8. A major advantage of SLOT is the angle independency of the light collection, attributed to the decoupled pathways of excitation and detection. This leads to a large quantity of exploitable signal, which is especially beneficial for samples with fluorescent markers. Such markers can be used to visualize specific parts of a biological sample9. This labelling helps to identify different types of tissue in a sample. However, the treatment needed for tissue labeling might modify the sample itself and staining of three-dimensional (3D) cell or tissue constructs often lacks efficiency. Previous work has shown that label-free tissue discrimination can be achieved with an optical setup10,11. This can be realized by analyzing the autofluorescence signal of different cell types. Taking the next step in SLOT development, we reworked the classical SLOT setup and modified the optics to be used with a broadband light source and spectrometer12. Our main objective was to create a device for label-free cell identification and visualization in a tomographic setup. In this work, we present the first milestone towards highly informative label-free 3D imaging by providing a technical description of the newly constructed hyperspectral SLOT. We show the results of a first hyperspectral tomographic measurement of a fluorescent polymer sample. Lastly, we present the results of a hyperspectral acquisition of human osteoblast and fibroblast cell spheroids to demonstrate the possibility of label-free cell identification.

Material and methods

Scanning Laser Optical Tomography

Scanning Laser Optical Tomography (SLOT) is a 3D imaging technique, used for a broad variety of technical13,14 and biological9,15,16 samples on a micro- to mesoscale. SLOT can be considered as an optical counterpart of computed tomography, using laser light instead of X-rays. To acquire tomographic images, a set of tilted two-dimensional (2D) images (projections) is recorded and subsequently processed computationally by specific reconstruction algorithms. The tomographic images can then be stacked to a 3D model of the sample.

The basic SLOT setup is shown in Fig. 1A. A weakly focused laser beam is adapted with respect to the beam size and collimation. The basic setup utilizes a continuous wave laser, such as a 532 nm diode pumped solid state laser, to optimize the optical properties of the device. The beam is then scanned across a sample, which is mounted on a rotation stage. The transmitted light of each scanned point is acquired and merged as a 2D image by a photo diode (PD) behind the sample. Projections of different angles of the sample are acquired while the sample is rotated by 360°. Different contrast mechanisms can be acquired simultaneously. Especially for weak signals, such as fluorescence, SLOT offers a high collection efficiency6, since the signal can be collected with high numerical apertures (NA) from any mechanically possible angle. The signal is filtered and measured with a sensitive photo multiplier tube (PMT).

The basic SLOT setup and its focusing principle. (A) A simple setup of the Scanning Laser Optical Tomograph (SLOT). The beam of a laser source is expanded to adjust the focal length. Then, the beam is scanned and focused across the sample by a set of xy-scanners and a telecentric f-theta lens. The transmitted light is collected by a PD which thereby acquires 2D gray-value images of the sample. The sample is rotated stepwise to create a set of projections. Simultaneously to the transmission light, other contrast mechanisms can be acquired by a PMT, such as scattered light or fluorescence. (B) The beam diameter of the laser is adjusted to change the NA of the resulting focus beam. The Rayleigh length zr of the beam must cover half the size of the probe diameter d to provide the highest possible resolution.

SLOT is able to image many different sample types. However, transparent samples provide sharper images, which accordingly results in better reconstructions. Therefore, a biological sample is usually prepared by clearing and fixation. The Curing Resin-Infiltrated Sample for Transparent Analysis (CRISTAL) protocol provides samples that are both optically clear and mechanically stable17. For this protocol, the refractive index of the sample is adjusted by dehydration with ethanol and clearing solutions, such as methyl salicylate, benzyl alcohol and/or benzyl benzoate. The solution used depends on the sample and must be tested and trialed individually. Afterwards, the processed sample is embedded and cured in a refractive index matched optical glue in a cylindrical shape. The subsequently fixed sample is mounted on a rotation stage, which rotates the sample within a glass cuvette. The cuvette is filled with refractive index matched clear silicone oil to reduce scattering and aberrations.

After the acquisition, the projected 2D images are computationally processed into sets of tomographic images. Pre-processing algorithms are used to correct or reduce possible artifacts caused by motion of the sample during rotation. This step increases the quality of the reconstructed slices. For the reconstruction itself, the open source tools IMOD and FIJI are used, which utilize the commonly used filtered back projection (FBP) algorithm18,19. The tomographic images can finally be stacked up, resulting in a volumetric dataset.

To ensure the highest possible optical resolution of the acquisitions, SLOT requires the Rayleigh length of the scanning beam to be half the size of the examined sample diameter, to cover the sample with the full focus length (illustrated in Fig. 1B). Hyperspectral SLOT setup

For the realization of a hyperspectral SLOT setup, the acquisition components needed to be modified to detect a larger spectrum of information. We therefore replaced the PMT of the original setup with a QE Pro (OceanOptics, USA) spectrometer (see Fig. 2) with a HC1-QE (OceanOptics, USA) grating which is sensitive in the visible spectrum and has a spectral resolution of ~ 1.2 nm. The new setup was optimized for hyperspectral analysis of biological samples, which often emit fluorescence signals in the visible range11. A 100 µm long collecting light entrance slit INTSMA-100 (OceanOptics, USA) was used for the spectrometer, resulting in a balanced proportion of intensity counts and noise level. For the light collection, an achromatic high-NA collimator F950SMA-A (Thorlabs, USA) with a clear aperture of 11 mm in diameter was placed close to the cuvette containing the sample. The collimator and the spectrometer were coupled by a round-to-linear multimode fiber PL100-2UV-VIS (OceanOptics, USA). The second major change was the exchange of the excitation source. The laser light source was replaced by the broadband white light source LED3000 (ILO electronic, Germany). To avoid chromatic aberrations, a parabolic mirror collimator RC12SMA-P01 (Thorlabs, USA) was used instead of the collimation lenses. It was coupled to the excitation source by a multimode fiber M96L01 (Thorlabs, USA). An adjustable iris was placed behind the collimator to adapt the beam diameter. Furthermore, we replaced the f-theta lens by achromatic lenses TRS254-040-A (Thorlabs, USA). This resulted in a reduced scanning field of view but ensured a reduced focal shift.

The modified hyperspectral SLOT setup. A broadband white light was used as light source. The emitted light was coupled into a parabolic mirror collimator, followed by an iris for beam diameter adjustment. The beam was then scanned across the sample. A Steinheil triplet achromatic lens was used as scan lens. The fluorescence light from the sample was then collected by a high-NA collimator. The collimator coupled the light into a multimode fiber, which was connected to a spectrometer.

The setup presented here can be varied for different uses of a hyperspectral SLOT setup. With this setup, we were able to acquire primary fluorescence light, emitted by the sample, as well as scattered light.

The spectrum of the excitation source is shown in Fig. 3. To record the spectrum, a silver mirror PF-10-03-P01 (Thorlabs, USA) was placed in the position of the sample in order to reflect the incoming light directly onto the collimator of the spectrometer. An integration time of 0.1 s was used.

Experimental validation acquisitions of synthetic samples

To validate the new hyperspectral setup, we designed a test target with known dimensions and acquired first measurements. For the sample we used a thin sheet of fluorescent acrylic glass 6C02 (PLEXIGLAS, Germany) with an excitation between 450 to 650nm, according to the manufacturer (see Fig. 5A). The sheet was cut into a smaller triangle shape with dimensions of 3 mm (width) × 3 mm (length) × 5 mm (height). The hypotenuse was milled into a staircase-like shape to create finely shaped details within the sample.

The hyperspectral test target. (A) A piece of fluorescent acrylic glass, which was cut and milled into a specific shape. The maximum dimensions are 3 mm (width) × 3 mm (length) × 5 mm (height). (B) The acrylic glass was embedded into transparent optical glue, similar to the CRISTAL clearing protocol, in order to mount the sample in the SLOT setup 12.

The sample was treated similar to the CRISTAL protocol. The acrylic piece was embedded into transparent optical glue in a cylindrical form (see Fig. 5B). For the glue we used Norland Optical Adhesive (NOA) 68, which provides good transmission properties in the visible spectrum and is also used for the embedding of biological samples for our purposes. A refractive index mismatch was accepted between the acrylic glass with n = 1.49 and the embedding material with n = 1.54. However, the selected shape of the acrylic glass sample avoids light scattering. After the curing of the glue, which leaves a transparent cylinder, the sample could be mounted in the SLOT setup. The mounted sample was then submerged in a cuvette, filled with a refractive index matching fluid. Here we used a silicone oil (mixture of AP150Wacker (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) and Tetra (Indomet, Germany)) with a refractive index of n = 1.54 to match the optical glue.

For the acquisition of the hyperspectral dataset, we had to reduce the recorded dimensions drastically to reduce the acquisition time. A normal SLOT acquisition is composed of a set of 800 equidistant images within a 360° rotation of the sample. One image consists of 500 pixels in x-direction and an equivalent number of pixels in y-direction, depending on the xy-ratio. This results in a typical number of 250,000 scanned points, which are acquired in a total scan time of 2 s per image. The number of pixels and the acquisition time per image is variable and dependent on the sample and application. The acquisition time of a single pixel increases when a spectrometer instead of a PD or PMT is used. The minimum acquisition time of the spectrometer used in this work is 0.01 s per pixel, which is 1000 times longer than the acquisition time of a pixel with the original SLOT setup. Due to these restrictions, we reduced the number of xy-pixels to 100 pixels in x-direction and 100 pixels in y-direction. Furthermore, we increased the acquisition time per pixel (spectrogram) to 0.03 s for an improved acquisition signal. 800 images were recorded per full rotation to guarantee a low-artifact reconstruction of the tomographic data. These settings resulted in a total acquisition time of 2.78 days.

During this measurement we acquired a higher resolution of 500 × 500 pixels to get a better visualization of the results to be expected.

Experimental acquisition of label-free biological samples

To test the capabilities of our new setup, we conducted a second experiment utilizing biological samples. Here we examined two cell spheroids. The first cell spheroid consisted of 100,000 non-labeled human osteoblast cells. The second cell spheroid consisted of an equal number of human fibroblast cells. For the production of the spheroids, cell lines (huFIB and huOB, InSCREENeX GmbH, Germany) were used that were developed with a novel expansion technology which was recently described 22. For spheroid formation a single cell suspension of 100.000 cells were cultivated for three to four days in 96 wells. The spheroids were optically cleared and embedded according to the CRISTAL protocol: The samples were dehydrated with a 50%, 75% and 100% solution of ethyl alcohol (Carl Roth, Germany), for 1 h respectively. Afterwards, the samples were cleared with benzyl alcohol (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) for 15 min. In preliminary tests, benzyl alcohol has proven to be a more suitable clearing agent for our spheroids than the classic benzyl alcohol and benzyl benzoate mixture. They were then separately embedded in NOA 68 and cured in a cylindrical shape. The prepared samples were finally mounted in the SLOT setup and immersed in a glass cuvette filled with silicone oil (n = 1.54), according to “Experimental validation acquisitions of synthetic samples” section.

First, we acquired images with the basic SLOT setup, as described in “Scanning Laser Optical Tomography” section. The transmission signal was acquired with a 532 nm laser. Since the spheroids were unlabeled, no fluorescence signal was acquired. We used a typical resolution of 500 × 284 pixels (y pixels are dependent to the chosen x-resolution, due to the spatial resolution and the chosen imaging region) and an acquisition time of 2 s per image.

For the hyperspectral SLOT setup acquisition of the cell spheroids, we decided to acquire a 2D image for each spheroid with a size of 300 × 235 pixels and an acquisition time of 1 s per pixel. The comparatively high integration time was chosen to guarantee a noise reduced signal.

Results

Measurement (1) was performed with the basic SLOT setup with a 532 nm laser and the PD. It served as a reference measurement to obtain a standard of a high-resolution SLOT acquisition and to evaluate the quality of the sample. With this setup a NA of 0.02 (1.95 mm beam diameter with the f-theta scan lens), which in turn results in an optical resolution of 16.513 µm, was used. The results are shown in Fig. 7A,B. The transmission signal (A) points out that the test target itself has some minor damages in one of the milled steps, as well as traces of the cutter used on the lower side of the sample. The embedding glue shows minor pollution of dust and small air bubbles which may lead to image artifacts. The reconstruction (B) of the transmission signal visualizes the sharp profile of the sample. Because of different refractive indexes of the embedding glue and the fluorescent acryl, the edges of the acryl were highly reflective, which resulted in high intensities at the edges. The test target consisted of a homogeneous plastic with constant absorption throughout its body. The sample showed inhomogeneities at the corners, which was due to reconstruction artifacts and the high reflectivity. To measure the fluorescence, a 445 nm laser and a PMT with a 530/50 nm bandpass fluorescence filter was used, see Fig. 7C,D. The fluorescence emission signal (C) shows evenly distributed fluorescence properties. Dark surfaces appear when the angle of the excitation and the surface cause a critical angle that leads to total reflection. The homogeneity of the material is also shown in the reconstruction of the fluorescence signal (D). Here, the material showed evenly distributed intensities with some minor inhomogeneities due to the corners of the sample, which resulted in critical angles.

After verifying the quality and shape of the cell spheroids, we acquired datasets of the spheroids with the hyperspectral SLOT setup. Here, the focal length was chosen to fit the sample size plus the focal shift of the used optics. This leads to a lower NA of 0.044 (1.98 mm beam diameter with our hyperspectral SLOT lens), which in turn results in an optical resolution of 10.429 µm. Figure 12B shows the same cell spheroid, imaged at the same angle as in (A). One full acquisition consisted of 804 wavelengths between 300 and 900 nm. Figure 12B shows the spectrometer signal acquired at 513.68 nm. In comparison to the human osteoblast cell spheroid, the acquired human fibroblast (huFib) cell spheroid is shown in Fig. 12C and D.

The hyperspectral dataset extracted from the cell spheroids was finally evaluated with a python script and Excel. The red dashed square in Fig. 12B,D indicates the area that was used for data analysis of the huOb and huFib cell spheroid . The spectral data of each pixel was averaged for each wavelength to extract a smoothed signal. This was also done for the green dashed square in Fig. 12B,D. This area was used as an averaged background signal and was subtracted from the signal of the spheroids. An excerpt of the resulting spectral data between 400 and 750 nm is shown in Fig. 13. No significant signal was obtained below or above these wavelengths.

The spectral data of the two different cell types of cell spheroids with the hyperspectral SLOT setup. The blue line shows the spectral data of the human osteoblast cell spheroid. The orange line shows the spectral data of the human fibroblast cell spheroid. The osteoblast signal appears weaker and differs especially in the wavelength range of 430 to 500 nm.

An offset caused by the dark current of the spectrometer was subtracted from both datasets. Overall, the acquired signal of the both cell types was very weak, with huOb (Fig. 13, blue line) showing a higher reflectance than the signal of the huFib cell spheroid (Fig. 13, orange line). It has to be noted that fluorescence intensity was not corrected for by taking cell number into account, since the focus was on the qualitative spectral differences. Additionally, there is a slight shift of the emitted wavelengths by 5 nm, which is likely to be a measurement artifact. Qualitative differences can be observed in the range between 430 and 500 nm. Here, the signal varies in type-dependent manner that allows for distinction of the two cell types.

Discussion

In this work, we developed and tested a hyperspectral SLOT. We were able to acquire tomographic hyperspectral datasets which were reconstructed and analyzed with respect to the underlying imaging properties. The design itself showed advantages and challenges which have to be met in the future. In the present setup, we implemented a solution which reduces the negative effects of working with broadband light sources, especially the chromatic aberration and the focal shift of the different wavelengths. Particularly for SLOT the focal shift cannot be neglected. The consequence is an extension of the focal length, which leads to a reduced optical resolution of the tomographic setup. To mitigate this, two options should be considered. First, the resolution depends on the sample size. Small samples yield a better resolution. However, due to the square root in Eq. (3), the resolution does not decline at the same rate that the sample increases in size. Secondly, the optical components of the setup can be adapted to a larger sample size to optimize it for larger samples.

SLOT is a highly modular setup with large potential for modifications. There are various possibilities to realize a hyperspectral SLOT. The group of Chen et al.23 created a hyperspectral tomograph with a different configuration of optics and working principles, and demonstrated its functionality on excised mouse thymus gland samples. However, only a single excitation wavelength was used. This requires previous knowledge about the sample to be investigated considering its labelling or autofluorescence. For the excitation of a specific fluorophore, it is more efficient to use a single laser line to avoid chromatic aberrations and improve focus sizes. Using a broadband light source as done here renders such previous knowledge unnecessary. The hyperspectral SLOT is designed to be used with samples of unknown emission spectra, as the broadband source ensures a large excitation spectrum.

Measuring absorption instead of fluorescence is another possibility. To achieve this, the PD instead of the PMT would be replaced by the spectrometer. Such a setup requires a reference measurement of the initial light source to calculate the absorption of the different wavelength in the material. Future experiments will show which setup is more efficient for different kinds of applications.

Furthermore, the optical properties of the components other than the sample have to be taken into account. For our setup this applies to the glass cuvette, the immersion oil and the embedding material of the sample. Since we used materials with a high transmission grade in the visible spectrum, the influence of these materials was neglectable here, but should be considered depending on the configuration. This also applies to the tissue clearing process, which might change the spectral information of the sample. However, since the samples were treated equally, we found this to be neglectable for our purposes.

The total acquisition time is a challenge that needs to be overcome as well. In this work we had to reduce the acquired pixel resolution drastically depending on the integration time to complete the measurements within an acceptable time frame. The spectrometer we used features a good signal-to-noise ratio, but faster and more sensitive models could reduce the acquisition time. A high signal-to-noise ratio is preferable when low fluorescence signals are expected.

To improve the new hyperspectral SLOT system, the evaluation of the first acquisitions is helpful. A comparison of the transmission signal of the three measurements highlights the hurdles which still need to be overcome. In Fig. 7A we obtained a clear image of the test target with a uniform illumination. In the next measurement, shown in Fig. 8A, we obtained some differences from the previous one. The optical resolution was decreased due to the reduced amount of acquired pixels and because the narrowband laser was replaced with a broadband light source. A more significant factor is, however, the inhomogeneous illumination of the test sample. This is thought to originate in the smaller size of the used optics which lead to a vignette in the image. This should be overcome by measuring the background signal before the actual measurement, which was only digitally removed in this work and not measured. The inhomogeneous illumination can also be observed when comparing the fluorescence images in Figs. 7C and 8C. The effects of scattered light are larger in the second acquisition. This effect can be explained by the manufacturing damages we obtained in the images before. These damages induced considerable scattering artifacts. The images of the dataset in Fig. 10A feature a different distribution of light illumination. In this case, this is not mainly due to the inhomogeneous illumination obtained before, but due to the optics used for the light collection. The collecting efficiency of the high-NA light collimator was highly dependent on the position placement of the collimator, which was due to the incident angle of the collected light. These difficulties can be overcome with different optics which are more robust with respect to these specific influences.

Analyzing the plotted graphs in Fig. 9, we obtained a low background noise signal of a few counts, shown in Fig. 9A, which might stem from scattered light of the contaminants or the embedding glue which might not yield exactly 100% transmissivity due to possible pollution. The signal of the sample in Fig. 9B, however, features a smooth graph with clear peaks in the expected wavelength range. This demonstrates that the newly integrated hardware and software yield good results for the measurement of suitable samples and parameters such as illumination area, acquisition area and integration time.

The artificial test target consisting of two different acrylic compounds showed the expected spectra in their measurements. Slight differences can be observed such as a smother curvature or a slight shift of the expected spectrum. The shift is likely to be a measurement error which might originate from the control program or a measurement error of the spectrometer. Still, the fourth measurement showed the capabilities of the new setup of displaying distinct areas within a single sample, and confirmed the reproducibility of the measurements.

The results of the hyperspectral SLOT acquisitions of the two different cell spheroid types demonstrate the capabilities for label-free cell identification of the new setup. The two graphs in Fig. 13 differ in a cell type-specific manner, as expected. For this proof-of-concept experiment, we designed the experiments similar to10,11,24. Based on those works, we expected that the two datasets would be similar but differ in terms of intensity and would exhibit slight, type specific qualitative differences for a few acquired wavelengths. Furthermore, similarities between the graphs of the excitation source in Fig. 3 and the graphs of the two measured spheroids in Fig. 13 are noticeable, indicating that the sample still shows scattering despite clearing. Only in the range between 400 and 500 nm noticeable differences can be found. However, these differences can make a significant statement about the spectral fingerprint of the sample, which was shown in11. Again, a slight shift of the emission peaks was visible. This artifact is not crucial when only relative comparisons with focus on the form of the spectrum are performed, but will hinder any statements concerning the absolute placements of emission spectra. Additional effort will therefore be necessary to identify and eliminate its source. The results presented here confirm the capability of the new setup to differentiate between unlabeled biological samples. However, further development is necessary before a full non-labelled 3D dataset can serve to distinguish between cell types in a mixed 3D construct. Here, we acquired data of two separated single-cell type spheroids. Analysis of a mixed spheroid requires improved resolution. For the investigation of the cell spheroids, the calculated optical resolution of the hyperspectral SLOT was approximately half that of the basic setup. Still, a low-resolution setup as the one shown here is sufficient to identify wavelengths of interest that can be investigated more closely in subsequent measurements.

Conclusion

The new hyperspectral SLOT setup considerably increases the information density, but also presents some technical challenges which need to be considered. The experimental data acquisition performed in this work shows that the new setup offers new possibilities for imaging samples with a broad emission spectrum. Furthermore, samples with unknown excitation spectra can be examined, as no presumptions about the sample need to be made. The challenges presented by the new setup are of a technical nature, and we have shown methods to overcome these hurdles. However, some issues remain that cannot be easily fixed, such as the comparatively long integration time. The pixel resolution of the system needed to be reduced to achieve a measurement in an acceptable time, which in turn resulted in a reduction of information density. To handle this decreased resolution, the sample size should be adapted according to the resulting optical resolution of the setup, considering the Nyquist-criterion for optical setups. The work presented here has shown that the newly implemented setup yields valuable information on 3D samples, which can be optimized by matching the parameters such as illumination area, acquisition area and integration time to the specific sample. Additionally, the results of the validation and proof-of-concept experiments demonstrate the capabilities of the hyperspectral SLOT setup for label-free imaging of biological samples. Overcoming the current technical limits of the setup will extend the capabilities of the system even further, which will lead to new possibilities for biomedical SLOT imaging.
