Identification of New CD36 Antagonists by Structure‐Based Virtual Screening
Authors: Sandra L Guerrero‐Rodríguez, Luis Córdova‐Bahena, Fabiola Cortés‐Mendoza, Angel J Ruiz‐Moreno, Cecilia Mata‐Cruz, Mireya Velázquez‐Paniagua, Nohemí Salinas‐Jazmín, Martín González‐Andrade, Marco A Velasco‐Velázquez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCD36 is a transmembrane glycoprotein that facilitates the uptake of fatty acids and oxidized low‐density lipoproteins. CD36 is overexpressed in various types of cancer and CD36+ cancer cells display enhanced malignancy, including resistance to therapy and elevated stemness and metastatic ability. Thus, CD36 is a therapeutic target in cancer. This study aimed to discover new CD36 antagonists through virtual screening. We identified a druggable pocket in CD36 that is functionally relevant and serves as the binding site for reported antagonists. Consensus molecular docking of a chemical library containing over 25,000 drug‐like compounds identified 15 computational hits with structural diversity. Experimental evaluation of seven compounds revealed that compounds 8, 13, and 14 are novel inhibitors of CD36‐mediated palmitate and/or oxLDL uptake in cellular models. Compound 14 reduced the clonogenicity of HepG2 cells without impacting cell viability, showing that it modulates CD36‐triggered phenotypes associated with disease progression. Analysis of molecular interactions of compounds 8, 13, or 14 with CD36 by molecular dynamics simulations showed that the identified antagonists had stable binding and favorable binding energy, leading to distinct conformational states of the protein. These results support the use of the antagonists identified here as lead compounds for developing CD36‐targeted therapies.Keywords: CD36, computer‐aided drug discovery, consensus docking, hepatocellular carcinoma, palmitic acid

Introduction

CD36 is a class B scavenger receptor expressed in the membrane of various mammalian cell types (Silverstein and Febbraio 2009). CD36 is present in platelets, microvascular endothelium, erythroid precursors, hepatocytes, adipocytes, cardiac and skeletal myocytes, phagocytes (macrophages, dendritic cells, microglia, retinal pigment epithelium), and in the epithelia of the breast, kidney, and gut (Febbraio et al. 2001). CD36 consists of 472 amino acids, having two short cytoplasmic tails, two transmembrane domains, and one extracellular domain that mediates ligand binding (Hsieh et al. 2016; Pepino et al. 2014). CD36 has diverse functions in maintaining homeostasis, including participating in cell adhesion (van Schravendijk et al. 1992) and angiogenesis (Dawson et al. 1997), functioning as a pattern recognition receptor (Endemann et al. 1993), and mediating lipid uptake (Han et al. 1997). These functions may vary according to the ligand recognized. CD36 can bind large fatty acids (FA) (Abumrad et al. 1993), oxidized low density lipoproteins (oxLDL) (Endemann et al. 1993), thrombospondin‐1 (Silverstein et al. 1992), amyloid‐forming peptides (Bamberger et al. 2003), among others.

CD36 has a relevant role in diverse pathologies, including metabolic disorders (Karunakaran et al. 2021), atherosclerosis (Park 2014; Tian et al. 2020), Alzheimer's disease (Dobri et al. 2021), and several types of cancers (Guerrero‐Rodríguez et al. 2022; Xia et al. 2023). In cancer, CD36 expression has been associated with increased cell proliferation (Drury et al. 2020; Liang et al. 2018), a metabolic shift toward FA oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation (Farge et al. 2017; Gyamfi et al. 2021), metastasis initiation (Pascual et al. 2017), and the development of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (Xu et al. 2021). Accordingly, increased CD36 expression has been reported in human tumors from breast (Feng et al. 2019, 2020), colon and rectum (Drury et al. 2020, 2022), brain (Erhart et al. 2019; Hale et al. 2014), blood (Landberg et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2022), and ovary (Jayawardhana et al. 2020; Ladanyi et al. 2018). In the case of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), CD36 promotes the uptake of palmitate by cancer cells, inducing lipid accumulation (Joshi‐Barve et al. 2007) and promoting tumor growth and invasion (Luo et al. 2021; Nath et al. 2015). Accordingly, CD36 inhibition has been proposed as a strategy to slow tumor progression (Drury et al. 2020; Pascual et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2022).

Virtual screening (VS) is a computational strategy with high‐throughput capability for the identification of compounds likely to bind to a specific target (Shoichet 2004). Molecular docking is frequently included in VS, since it predicts the fitting of compounds into target cavities and estimates the energy produced by ligand‐target interaction (Meng et al. 2011). This strategy is cost‐effective as it reduces the number of candidates to be evaluated in experimental screening (Kitchen et al. 2004; McInnes 2007).

In this project, we aimed to identify new CD36 antagonists by using a structure‐based VS protocol. By consensus docking, we identified 15 drug‐like compounds that have the potential to bind CD36 in a pocket located within a FA channel. Experimental testing of seven of those compounds identified compounds 8, 13, and 14 as inhibitors of the CD36‐mediated uptake of palmitate in HCC cells and/or oxLDL transport in THP‐1 macrophage‐like cells. Compounds 13 and 14 reduced the clonogenicity of palmitate‐treated HCC cells, demonstrating that they can modulate cellular functions associated with disease progression. Molecular dynamics simulations (MDS) of the compounds indicated stable binding and favorable energies but with diverse CD36 conformational dynamics.

Materials and Methods

VS and Chemoinformatic Analysis

Pocket detection within the CD36 structure (PDB ID: 5LGD) (Hsieh et al. 2016) was performed using the MetaPocket 2.0 server (Huang 2009). The druggability of the identified pockets was assessed using the FTMap server (Kozakov et al. 2015) using the default settings. For molecular docking, the CD36 structure was prepared with AutoDockTools by removing water molecules, small ligands, and other proteins. Then polar hydrogens and Gasteiger charges were added. The previously reported CD36 ligands AP5055, AP5156, AP5258 (Geloen et al. 2012), and ursolic acid (Ikeda et al. 2007; Murakami et al. 2007; Wilkinson et al. 2011) were used as reference compounds. Ligands were protonated at a pH of 7.4 and optimized using OpenBabel (2.4.1 version) (O'Boyle et al. 2011) employing the MMFF94 force field and docked using AutoDock Vina (Nguyen et al. 2020) into the four largest pockets identified by MetaPocket. The best‐ranked pose of each ligand was preserved for analysis.

The ZINC Natural Derivatives (Znd) subgroup of the ZINC database, containing 30,793 compounds, was filtered using OpenEye software (OMEGA 6.0.0.1—FILTER Hawkins et al. 2010; OMEGA 6.0.0.1., n.d.) to retain only compounds complying with Lipinski's Rule of Five. The compounds were docked into the selected pocket of CD36 using four different docking protocols: (i) AutoDock Vina; (ii) FRED (McGann 2011) with the Chemgauss4 scoring function; (iii) GOLD (Nurisso et al. 2012) with ChemPLP scoring function; and (iv) and GOLD with GoldScore scoring function. For each protocol, the docking scores were normalized by calculating the Z‐score for the best‐ranked pose (Blanes‐Mira et al. 2022; Minai et al. 2008). Since GOLD returns positive values as scores, the Z‐scores from this method were multiplied by −1. The consensus score was the average of the Z‐scores from all protocols. The ligand efficiency (LE) was calculated as the ratio of the consensus score to the number of heavy atoms in each compound. Finally, blind docking was performed for the most promising compounds using QuickVina‐W (Hassan et al. 2017).

The physicochemical properties for compounds selected from the molecular docking were calculated using the SwissADME (Daina et al. 2017) and pkCSM‐ pharmacokinetics web servers (Pires et al. 2015). Structural comparison of the computational hits was performed using the ratio of the size of the maximum common substructure shared among compound pairs to their total size, calculated with the ChemMine Tools web server (Backman et al. 2011).

Cell Culture

The HCC HepG2 cell line was obtained from ATCC (HB‐8065) and cultured in EMEM (Sigma‐Aldrich, M0643) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Sigma‐Aldrich, F4135), penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) (Gibco, 15,070–063). The THP‐1 cell line from monocytic leukemia was obtained from ATCC (TIB‐202) and cultured in RPMI‐1640 (Gibco, 31800‐022) supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 μM β‐mercaptoethanol, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL). THP‐1 cells were differentiated into macrophage‐like cells by treating them with 5 nM of phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA, Sigma‐Aldrich, P1585) for 24 h as reported (Mol et al. 2024). All cell cultures were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2 unless otherwise indicated.

The expression of membrane CD36 in the above cells was verified by flow cytometry by incubating 100,000 cells for 45 min at 4°C with 1 μg/mL of the anti‐human CD36 antibody FA6‐152 (Stem Cell Technologies, 60084). Subsequently, anti‐mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A21202) was added to the samples, which were analyzed in an Attune NxT (Thermo Fisher) flow cytometer.

Cell‐Based Activity Assays

CD36‐mediated palmitate uptake was evaluated by exposing HepG2 cells to compounds for 1 h followed by incubation with 50 ng/mL BODIPY‐Palmitate (Cayman, 26749) for 15 min at RT. Then, cells were washed and stained with 7‐AAD (Cayman, 601181) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Internalization of BODIPY‐Palmitate was analyzed by flow cytometry. During assay standardization, anti‐human CD36 antibodies, clones JC63.1 (Abcam, ab23680) and FA6‐152 (Stem Cell Technologies, 60084), were employed as positive controls.

oxLDL uptake was assessed by incubation of macrophage‐like THP‐1 cells with the compounds (50 μM) or the anti‐human CD36 clone JC63.1 for 1 h at 37°C. Subsequently, oxLDL‐DyLight 488 (Cayman, 601181) was added according to the manufacturer's protocol and incubated for 16 h. Then, the cells were washed, detached with trypsin, and stained with 7‐AAD. Internalization of oxLDL‐DyLight 488 was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cell Viability Assays

HepG2 or macrophage‐like THP‐1 cells were treated with the compounds for 24 h. The effects on cell viability were estimated using the MTT (3‐(4,5‐dimethyltiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5,‐diphenyl tetrazolium bromide, from Sigma‐Aldrich, M5655) assay (Mosmann 1983). The reduced tetrazolium salt was dissolved in DMSO (Reactivos Meyer, 67‐68‐5) and measured spectrophotometrically at 570 nm. The experiments for these assays were performed in quintuplicates.

Clonogenicity Assays

The assay was performed as previously described (Mata‐Cruz et al. 2025). Briefly, 100 viable HepG2 cells were plated on a 96‐well ultra‐low attachment plate (Corning Costar, 3473) with MammoCult medium and growth factors (Stem Cell Technologies, 05620) and cultured in the presence of 50 μM palmitate prepared as previously reported (Pascual et al. 2017). The medium containing the corresponding treatments was replenished every 72 h for 7 d. At the end of the experiments, micrographs were taken (Eclipse Ti‐U microscopy, Nikon) and analyzed (NIS Elements Basic Research software, Nikon) to count the number of tumorspheres with a diameter > 60 μm. Sphere‐forming efficiency (SFE) was calculated as reported (Vásquez‐Bochm et al. 2019).

Real‐Time PCR

HepG2 cells were exposed for 24 h to palmitate and/or the compounds of interest. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat. 74134). Real‐time PCRs were performed in triplicate using the QuantiTec SYBR Green RT‐PCR kit (Qiagen, Cat. 204243) and the following primers: CD36 forward 5′GGACATACTTGGATATTGAACC3′, CD36 reverse 5′ACACCAACACTGAGTAAGAT3′, β‐actin forward 5′CACCATTGGCAATGAGCGGTTC3′, β‐actin reverse 5′AGGTCTTTGCGGATGTCCACGT3′. Relative mRNA expression was calculated by 2−ΔΔCt against β‐actin expression (Taylor et al. 2019).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were made using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett's or Sidak's multiple comparison tests, or a two‐tailed unpaired t‐test using Graph Pad Prism 8 (Version 8.0.2). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

MDS

The structural file of the protein was prepared with the pdb4amber script, which is part of AmberTools24, and the GAFF forcefield was applied for ligands (Wang et al. 2004). Then, the coordinates and topologies of the complexes were prepared using the tLEAP module of AMBER23 (Case et al. 2005, 2023; Walker et al. 2008). Subsequently, hydrogens and some missing atoms were added to the structure and the complex using the tLEAP module with the force field protein.ff19SB (Walker et al. 2008); after that, an optimization of the hydrogen bond network was performed to increase the stability of the solute and to be able to have a pKa prediction to adjust the protonation states of the protein residues at pH 7.4. At that point, Cl− or Na+ counterions were included to neutralize the system. The complexes were solvated in an octahedral box of water molecules using the TIP3P model; the box's boundaries were 12 Å from the protein surface.

MDS were performed at 1 atm and 310.15 K, maintained with the Berendsen barostat and thermostat. Periodic boundary conditions and Ewald particle mesh sums (1 Å spacing) were used to treat long‐range electrostatic interactions. To calculate direct interactions, a 10 Å cutoff was used. On the other hand, to satisfy the binding constraints, the SHAKE algorithm was used, thus allowing the use of a time step of 2 fs to integrate Newton's equations as mentioned in the Amber package. Amber force field parameters protein.ff19SB were also used for all residuals. The calculations were performed using a graphics processing unit‐accelerated MD engine in AMBER (pmemd.cuda), a program package running entirely on CUDA‐enabled GPUs (Krieger and Vriend 2015; Salomon‐Ferrer et al. 2013). Simulations were performed on an Ubuntu 22.04 Workstation with a Nvidia Gigabyte GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, yielding a max performance of 321 ns/day.

The protocol started with a structure minimization step before pressure equilibration for 500 ps at 315 K and 1.0 atm. Each complex produced 100 ns of MDS. The CPPTRAJ tool by AMBER23 utilities performed all analyses (Case et al. 2005; Roe and Cheatham 2013). The root mean square deviation (RMSD) calculations were performed considering C, CA, and N. Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD, 1.9.3 version), Maestro Schrödinger (version 14.2.118, release 2024–5, Platform Linux‐x86_64), Chimera (1.17.3 version), and PyMOL (Molecular Graphics System, version 2.5.0) were used to visualize and create the MDS images (Maruyama et al. 2023; Pettersen et al. 2004; Walker et al. 2008).

The binding free energies (ΔG bind) were calculated by molecular mechanics/Poisson Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) (Zhou and Madura 2004), which combines molecular mechanics energy with implicit solvation models. ΔE MM comprises ΔE Internal (bond, angle, and dihedral energies), ΔE Electrostatic (electrostatic energies), and ΔE Vdw (van der Waals energies). ΔG Solv is the sum of electrostatic solvation energy (polar contribution; ΔG PB) and non‐electrostatic solvation component (non‐polar contribution; ΔG SA). The polar contribution was calculated using the Poisson‐Boltzmann surface area model, while the non‐polar energy was estimated from the solvent‐accessible surface area (SASA). The conformational entropy change (−TΔS) was computed by normal mode analysis from a set of conformational snapshots taken from the MDS (Hou et al. 2011; Kollman et al. 2000).

For selected ligand‐CD36 complexes, we detected and characterized the pockets in 11 conformations, sampled at 1 ns interval from 90 to 100 ns of the MDS, by using the Fpocket server (Le Guilloux et al. 2009). Analyses were performed using default settings, with the exception that the minimum number of spheres per pocket was set to 40. The volume of the largest pocket in each conformation was then calculated.

Results

Several antagonists of CD36 have been reported in the literature (Åbacka et al. 2024; Geloen et al. 2012; Ikeda et al. 2007; Murakami et al. 2007; Wang et al. 2011; Wilkinson et al. 2011) but their binding sites remain undefined. Thus, we began by identifying all druggable pockets in CD36. We found six distinct pockets (Figure S1A), but only four were large enough (> 250 Å3) to accommodate drug‐like molecules (pockets 1, 2, 4, and 5). To determine the most likely binding site for known CD36 ligands, we docked four reported antagonists (Geloen et al. 2012; Wilkinson et al. 2011) into each pocket. The use of pocket 4, located within one of the reported FA channels of CD36, resulted in the highest docking scores (Figure S1B). Besides, probe binding with FTMap server corroborated that pocket 4 was a “hot spot” for ligand binding (Figure S1C). Thus, pocket 4 (Figure 1A) was selected as the target site for our VS strategy (Figure 1B). From the Znd database, we selected the 25,390 compounds that complied with Lipinski's rule of five. Docking of those compounds into pocket 4 using different protocols allowed the computation of a consensus docking score (Figure S1D). We selected the 18 compounds with the most favorable rankings on consensus score and LE (Figure 1C). However, blind docking of those compounds revealed that 16, 17, and 18 had a higher theoretical affinity for regions of CD36 outside pocket 4 (Figure 1D) and were therefore excluded from the subsequent analyses. The remaining 15 compounds were considered computational hits.

As expected from our selection criteria for the VS, the identified compounds (Figure 2A) had physicochemical properties adequate for oral administration (Figure 2B). The computational hits were structurally diverse among them and showed low structural similarity with the previously reported CD36 inhibitors AP5055 (Geloen et al. 2012), CHEMBL1761157 (Wang et al. 2011), and SMS121 (Åbacka et al. 2024) (Figure 2C). Additional analyses (Table S1) predicted gastrointestinal absorption above 85% for all compounds except compound 15. Most compounds were characterized as: (i) impermeable to the blood–brain barrier (BBB) (except compounds 8 and 9), (ii) susceptible to CYP‐mediated metabolism, and (iii) hepatotoxic. Compounds 4, 6, 11, and 13 were predicted to be mutagenic in the AMES test. Predicted clearance rates varied among the compounds: most exhibited low clearance (less than 40% of liver blood flow), compounds 9 and 13 showed medium clearance, and compounds 4 and 5 demonstrated high clearance (> 70% of liver blood flow).

From the computational hits, we obtained 10 commercially‐available compounds; however, the solubility of compounds 3, 5, and 6 was incompatible with the cellular systems employed for activity characterization. The effect of compounds 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13, and 14 on the function of CD36 was analyzed in the HepG2 cell line. HepG2 cells expressed membrane CD36+ (Figure 3A) and were therefore employed to analyze the uptake of fluorescent palmitate by flow cytometry (Figure 3B). We correctly reproduced the inhibition of FA uptake caused by the anti‐human CD36 clones JC63.1 and FA6‐152 (Figure 3C) (Gong et al. 2020; Pascual et al. 2017), corroborating that CD36 partially mediates palmitate uptake in HepG2 cells (Mata‐Cruz et al. 2025). In a primary screening, all compounds were evaluated at a single concentration (50 μM). Compounds 8, 13, 14 were identified as inhibitors of cellular uptake of palmitate (Figure 3D–F) and considered experimental hits. Noteworthy, compound 9 increased CD36 activity (Figure 3D), suggesting an allosteric activation of CD36. Importantly, all these effects were observed with mild to null cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells (Figure 3G–I) demonstrating cytotoxicity‐independent cellular activity.

Further biological characterization of the identified antagonist included a concentration‐response analysis (Figure 4A–C). The results revealed that (i) compound 8 caused a non‐significant reduction in palmitate uptake; (ii) compound 13 reduced palmitate uptake at 50 μM but showed no effect at lower concentrations; and (iii) compound 14 exhibited a concentration‐dependent effect, emerging as the most potent inhibitor. Since CD36 is transcriptionally activated by palmitate through a positive feedback loop (Glatz et al. 2010), we hypothesized that CD36 antagonists would reduce CD36 mRNA expression in HepG2 cells. In agreement with the above findings, compounds 13 and 14, but not compound 8, decreased CD36 expression (Figure 4D).

The palmitate and oxLDL‐binding sites on CD36 partially overlap (Kar et al. 2008; Pepino et al. 2014). Thus, we analyzed the effect of compounds 8, 13, and 14 on oxLDL uptake using CD36+ macrophage‐like THP‐1 cells (Figure 6A). Compounds 8 and 13 reduced oxLDL uptake in live macrophage‐like THP‐1 cells, with efficacy comparable to the anti‐human CD36 antibody clone JC63.1 (Figure 6B). In contrast, compound 14 did not alter oxLDL transport (Figure 6B). Cell viability analysis showed that compounds 13 and 14 reduced the viability of macrophage‐like THP‐1 cells by 18% and 27% respectively, whereas compound 8 exhibited high (> 80%) cytotoxicity (Figure 6C). These findings demonstrated that compounds 8, 13, and 14 exhibit differential effects on two CD36‐mediated functions and vary in cytotoxicity across different cell types.

To characterize the binding modes of the experimental hits, we conducted MDS. Representative conformations were selected from the trajectory based on the simultaneous presence of the most prevalent intermolecular interactions. By analyzing the occurrence percentages of non‐covalent interactions, we identified key residues involved in binding. Compound 8 primarily interacted with residues at the bottom of the CD36 cavity through hydrophobic interactions, with Asp250 standing out due to its high interaction frequency (95.6%). A salt bridge with Asp209 was also maintained for 99.9% of the simulation time. Hydrogen bonds were observed with Pro203, Ser269, Lys334, and Glu335, with frequencies ranging from 9.0% to 14.7% (Figures 7A and S2).

Compound 13 exhibited frequent hydrophobic interactions with Asp250, Asp251, and Phe266, occurring 99.8%, 79.1%, and 79.9% of the time, respectively. It also formed hydrogen bonds with Gln116, Asn118, and Asp270, with frequencies of 55.0%, 49.0%, and 29.5%, respectively (Figures 7B and S2). For compound 14, our analysis highlighted Asp270 as a critical residue, forming hydrogen bonds 98.9% of the time. Arg96 also participated in hydrogen bonding (18.5%), while Gln116, Phe201, Pro202, Tyr204, Thr207, and Asp209 engaged in hydrophobic interactions, with frequencies ranging from 32.6% to 90.9%. In contrast to its significant role in the binding of compounds 8 and 13, Asp250 contributed minimally to compound 14 binding, with a hydrophobic interaction occurrence of only 15.9% (Figures 7C and S2). In summary, the experimental hits exhibited distinct binding modes with only a few shared residues contributing to their interactions.

RMSD analysis indicated stable binding modes for all three evaluated compounds, with minimal conformational changes. Compound 8 had an RMSD of 1.37 Å ± 0.43, compound 13 had 0.91 Å ± 0.17, and compound 14 showed 1.79 Å ± 0.14 (Figure 7D). To assess the impact of ligand binding on protein flexibility, we computed root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) values (Figure 7E). Residues 46–58, located in the region proximal to the cell membrane, exhibited increased fluctuation in the presence of compound 14 compared to compounds 8, 13, or the apo form. The most pronounced fluctuations among systems were observed in residues 134–247 of the CD36‐compound 13 complex. Importantly, those residues form one of the FA channel entrances (Pepino et al. 2014). Residues 272–333, forming the second FA channel entrance, were highly flexible in the apo form, but compound 13 reduced their flexibility, while compounds 8 and 14 nearly eliminated it, suggesting that compound binding restricts the conformational dynamics necessary for CD36 function. Theoretical binding energy (ΔG bind) calculations revealed that compounds 8 and 14 had binding affinities comparable to AP5055, the positive control. However, compound 13 displayed a significantly stronger binding affinity than all other ligands. As expected, the negative control ligand, characterized by a poor docking score and low LE, exhibited low binding energy (Figure 7F).

Interestingly, compounds 9 and 14 display structural similarity (Figure 2C) but exert opposite biological effects (Figure 3D). Thus, we analyzed their corresponding CD36 complexes by MDS. Both compounds share an indole–oxadiazole moiety, which is buried deep within the CD36 pocket. However, compound 9 displayed a compact conformation, whereas compound 14 assumed an extended conformation, exposing to the solvent the region opposite to the indole–oxadiazole moiety (Figure S3A). The volumes of the largest cavity in CD36, computed over the last 10 ns of the MDS, were 3881 ± 652 and 2592 ± 566 Å3 for the complexes with compounds 9 and 14, respectively (Figure S3B,C). The partially occluded cavity observed with compound 14 is consistent with its antagonistic activity, whereas the largest cavity induced by compound 9 may be relevant for its function as an allosteric agonist.

Altogether, these results support the stable binding of the selected compounds to CD36, but with key differences in their binding modes and the impact on the conformational dynamics of the target.

Discussion and Conclusions

CD36 is a transmembrane glycoprotein involved in lipoprotein and FA metabolism. Recent studies have demonstrated that CD36 is overexpressed in human cancer cells, where it regulates key features driving tumor progression (Drury et al. 2020, 2022; Erhart et al. 2019; Feng et al. 2020, 2019; Hale et al. 2014; Jayawardhana et al. 2020; Joshi‐Barve et al. 2007; Ladanyi et al. 2018; Landberg et al. 2018; Luo et al. 2021; Nath et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2022). Aiming to discover new CD36 antagonists, we identified a pocket within the FA channel of CD36 that: (i) is druggable, (ii) contains “hot spots” for the binding of small molecules, and (iii) is likely the binding site for reported CD36 inhibitors. Similar physicochemical analyses have successfully identified ligand‐binding sites in target proteins (Jones et al. 2022). Notably, the selected pocket includes the residues Lys231, Lys334 and Glu335, which have been reported as key for the uptake of FA (Kuda et al. 2013).

We identified 15 potential CD36 binders with appropriate LE and predicted favorable oral bioavailability by using consensus docking and chemoinformatic analyses. The use of consensus docking addresses the limitations of individual docking algorithms, which rely on a scoring function that may not accurately predict binding affinities across all target‐ligand pairs (Jorgensen 2004; Warren et al. 2006). By integrating multiple docking results, this approach enhances reliability and reduces false positives (dos Santos Maia et al. 2020). Notably, a recent study employing consensus docking identified SMS121 as a CD36 antagonist capable of inhibiting FA uptake and reducing the viability of acute myeloid leukemia cells (Åbacka et al. 2024).

A subset of the identified compounds was tested in vitro using HepG2 cells, a widely used model for studying HCC (Arzumanian et al. 2021). The HepG2 transcriptome closely resembles that of human liver tumors from the TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas program) cohort (Jin et al. 2023; The human protein atlas 2023; Uhlen et al. 2017) and exhibits high CD36 expression (Bort et al. 2020; Luo et al. 2021; Mata‐Cruz et al. 2025). However, palmitate uptake in hepatocytes or in HepG2 cells is mediated by multiple transporters (Eynaudi et al. 2021; McGarry and Brown 1997; Shi et al. 2006). Our analysis using anti‐CD36 antibodies, consistent with previous reports (Mata‐Cruz et al. 2025; Pascual et al. 2017), confirmed that CD36 plays a primary role in palmitate uptake, validating our in vitro model for the intended use.

Compounds 13 and 14 consistently reduced palmitate uptake and decreased the expression of CD36, a transcriptional target of CD36‐activated signaling (Glatz et al. 2010). These findings suggest that these compounds could be lead candidates for developing CD36‐targeted therapies for diverse pathologies. The sphere‐forming assays estimate the proportion of stem/progenitor‐like cells, since those cells can survive and proliferate under adhesion‐independent conditions (Johnson et al. 2013). We demonstrated that compounds 13 and 14 impaired the HepG2 sphere‐forming efficiency, suggesting that compounds 13 and 14 block the reported CD36‐mediated promotion of stemness in cancer cells (Farge et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024).

The binding sites for palmitate and oxLDL on CD36 have not been fully characterized but multiple evidence shows that they partially overlap (Glatz et al. 2010; Pepino et al. 2014). As a result, antibodies that block palmitate binding and uptake also interfere with oxLDL transport (Feng et al. 2019; Mata‐Cruz et al. 2025; Pascual et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2021). Our analysis using PMA‐differentiated macrophage‐like THP‐1 cells revealed that compounds 8 and 13, but not compound 14, reduced oxLDL uptake, despite all three compounds exhibiting stable binding at overlapping CD36 regions in MDS. We hypothesize that these differences in biological activity arise from distinct effects on CD36 conformational dynamics. Notably, the varying impact of CD36 antagonists may have therapeutic implications. For instance, a selective oxLDL uptake inhibitor could be valuable in developing treatments for atherosclerosis, where CD36‐mediated oxLDL uptake drives macrophage differentiation into foam cells (reviewed by Febbraio et al. 2001; Park 2014). However, compound 8 exhibited cytotoxicity in macrophage‐like cells but not in hepatocarcinoma cells. Compound 8 is predicted to be a substrate of CYP2D6, an enzyme highly expressed in hepatocytes and enterocytes but absent in macrophages (Karlsson et al. 2021; The Human Protein Atlas 2024); consequently, HepG2 cells may more efficiently generate non‐cytotoxic metabolites of compound 8. Further biological characterization of the CD36 antagonists identified here should focus on their pharmacokinetics and safety profiles, as well as their efficacy in relevant disease models.

In conclusion, we report the discovery of a new set of CD36 antagonists. The bioactive compounds identified seem to be excellent leads for the development of therapeutics that target CD36.
