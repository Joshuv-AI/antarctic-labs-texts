Exploring adherence to iron and folic acid supplementation in pregnant women at rural health facilities in Waghimra zone, Northern Ethiopia
Authors: Mohammed Abdu Seid, Aster Shumet Gebru, Ali Yimer, Kedir Ali, Adem Yesuf, Nurye Sirage, Abubeker Seid, Natnael Amare Tesfa, Hassen Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIron deficiency anemia is a prevalent condition during pregnancy, particularly in resource-limited areas, and is associated with negative fetal and maternal outcomes. A key strategy to prevent anemia during pregnancy involves ensuring adequate iron and folic acid supplementation (IFAS), diversifying the diet, deworming and infections control, such as malaria. This study aimed to assess the extent to which pregnant women at rural health facilities adhere to IFAS and to identify the factors contributing to this adherence.MethodsA multi-center cross-sectional study was conducted from February to June 2024 at rural health facilities in Ethiopia. Systematic random sampling was used to recruit study participants, and data were collected through semi-structured questionnaires, client interviews, and document reviews. The data were entered into Epi-Data 4 and then exported to STATA-16 for analysis. To explore the association between outcome and independent variables, both bi-variable and multivariable logistic regression models were applied. After performing multivariable regression, variables with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.ResultsFour hundred and six pregnant women were study participants. The mean age of the pregnant women was 27.9 years (SD ± 7.1). Adherence to IFAS was 45.1% [95% CI: 40.3–49.9%]. Counseling on the importance of IFAS (AOR = 2.29; 95% CI: 1.21–4.34), number of antenatal care (ANC) contacts (AOR = 3.50; 95% CI: 1.54–7.92), good knowledge of IFAS (AOR = 4.22; 95% CI: 2.43–7.31), and anemia (AOR = 3.07; 95% CI: 1.36–6.89) were associated factors.ConclusionsThe adherence of pregnant women to IFAS was very low compared to the world health organization recommendation (45% vs. > 80%). Counseling regarding the importance of IFAS, the number of ANC contacts, good knowledge of IFAS, and anemia status were independent predictors. Creating awareness about IFAS, providing counseling during ANC contacts, and strengthening the monitoring and evaluation system are all critically important.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08119-5.Keywords: Adherence, Associated factors, IFAS, Pregnancy, Rural ethiopia

Introduction

Anemia remains a major global health burden, affecting 1.92 billion individuals (24% of the world’s population) [1]. Among pregnant women, the prevalence reaches 36.8% worldwide, with regional disparities showing the highest burden in Africa (41.7%) [2]. In Ethiopia, a recent systematic review reported that 26.4% of pregnant women were anemic [3]. This is attributed to the ongoing conflict in different parts of Ethiopia over the last five years [4]. This conflict yields undesirable consequences in women’s health due to damage to health facilities, and transport restrictions cause shortages of essential medical supplies, including iron and folic acid supplementation, malaria prophylaxis, tetanus vaccination, and deworming, that potentially reduce the likelihood of having anemia during pregnancy [5–9]. Daily supplementation with iron and folic acid throughout pregnancy reduces the incidence of iron deficiency anemia at term by 57% and lowers the overall prevalence of maternal anemia by 70% [10]. Common consequences of iron deficiency anemia during pregnancy include low birth weight, preterm birth, stillbirth, and higher perinatal mortality rates [11, 12]. A summary report by the World Health Organization (WHO) indicated that antenatal supplementation with IFA significantly reduces these associated risks [13].

Anemia during pregnancy also imposes significant economic and social burdens. It entails considerable financial and societal costs that affect both individual health and broader developmental goals. It perpetuates a cycle of poverty and inequality, leading to increased healthcare expenses, reduced productivity, and negative birth outcomes [14, 15]. The World Health Organization fact sheet states that for every US$1 invested in reducing anemia in women, there is a potential economic return of US$12, which can significantly decrease economic losses.

Throughout pregnancy, consistent iron and folic acid intake is necessary to ensure positive fetal health outcomes [13, 16]. Meeting the high demand for iron and folic acid through diet al.one is challenging, making supplementary tablet preparations essential to fulfill this requirement [17–19]. Inadequate intake of iron and folic acid during pregnancy, either from diet or supplements, results in iron deficiency anemia [20]. Iron deficiency anemia is highly prevalent during pregnancy and poses a substantial public health burden as the predominant nutritional deficiency disorder, linked to poor obstetrical and neonatal outcomes [21–23]. Iron deficiency anemia is diagnosed through hemoglobin (Hb) level assessment, with categories classified as follows: mild (Hb ≤ 11 g/dL), moderate (Hb 7–9.9 g/dL), and severe (Hb < 7 g/dL) [24, 25]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that pregnant women supplement with 60 mg elemental iron and 400 µg (0.4 mg) folic acid daily throughout pregnancy to prevent iron deficiency anemia [26]. Folic acid is the synthetic form of folate used in most supplements and fortified foods [27]. Its deficiency may be caused by genetic factors, insufficient dietary intake, or interactions between environmental and genetic factors [28, 29]. Due to inadequate compliance with these supplementation recommendations, the potential benefits of folic acid in preventing neural tube defects (NTDs) are not being fully realized [30, 31]. Likewise, Iron deficiency anemia in pregnancy arises from multiple etiological factors: inadequate dietary iron intake, impaired gastrointestinal absorption, suboptimal adherence to IFAS, significantly increased iron demands during gestation, and chronic iron depletion from conditions such as menorrhagia [32–35].

As per research findings, the degree of adherence to IFAS varies from country to country. As a result, India (63.8%) [36], Iran (71.6%) [37], Nepal (58%) [38], Sri lanka (80.1%) [39], Ghana (84.5%) [40], Uganda (22.4%) [41], Sudan (79.0%) [42] and Ethiopia (41.4%) [43] of pregnant women adhere to IFAS. Research from sub-Saharan African countries indicated significant variation in adherence to IFAS, ranging from a low of 1.4% in Burundi to a high of 73.0% in Senegal [44].

A pooled analysis of various studies conducted in Ethiopia revealed that the adherence rate to iron and IFAS among pregnant women was 41.38% [43]. However, some discrepancies were observed in both the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) data analysis and community-based studies, which showed that more than 80% of pregnant women who attended at least four antenatal care contacts failed to adhere to IFAS [33, 45]. Likewise, multiple individual studies have reported considerable variation in adherence to IFAS among pregnant women in Ethiopia, with rates ranging from 17.1 to 74.5% [43, 46]. These differences are attributed to variations in geographical contexts and study periods. Notably, a significant discrepancy exists between urban and rural communities in adherence rates. For instance, in the rural Soro District of Hadiya, only 18.8% of pregnant women adhered to IFAS, whereas in the urban city of Dire Dawa, the adherence rate was considerably higher at 71.8% [47, 48]. Moreover, in communities experiencing ongoing armed conflicts, such as the Amhara region, healthcare services and supply chains are severely compromised, leading to a significant reduction in adherence to IFAS [49].

Data from the 2019 EDHS revealed that only 11% of pregnant women took iron and folic acid tablets for at least 90 days, representing a 6% increase compared to the 2016 survey. In contrast, 58% of pregnant women did not consume any iron and folic acid tablets during pregnancy, reflecting a 2% increase from the 2016 findings [50]. Ethiopia has been implementing IFAS at both the community and facility levels for nearly three to four decades. However, the desired outcomes have not yet been achieved due to several factors, including concerns about forgetfulness, fear of side effects, drug shortages, knowledge gaps, and other unspecified reasons [20, 37, 51].

Adherence for iron and folic acid supplementation is influenced by several factors, including age [37, 52], maternal education [37], gravidity [35, 36], knowledge about IFAS [40–43, 47, 53], number of ANC contacts (≥ 4) [42, 47, 52, 54], early ANC registration [40, 43, 45, 54], counseling on IFAS [35, 41, 53, 54], and current anemia [35, 37, 39, 43, 47] (Fig. 1). This study aimed to determine the extent to which pregnant women at rural health facilities adhere to iron and IFAS, and identify the factors associated with adherence, despite varying levels of reported adherence in previous studies.

Methods

Study design, setting and time

We carried out a multi-center cross-sectional study at rural health facilities in Waghimra zone, northern Amhara, Ethiopia. The Waghimra zone is located approximately 861 km from Addis Ababa and is known for its hot climate, with maximum temperatures reaching 31.6 °C and minimum temperatures around 26.2 °C. Agriculture is the primary livelihood for over 90% of the population. The annual rainfall in this region ranges from 250 to 750 mm. There are over 15 healthcare facilities located in the area. Data was gathered from February through to June of 2024.

Study participants and eligibility

This study included pregnant women aged 18 to 42 years who had at least one ANC contact at health facilities and were on IFAS for at least one month, but excluded women who were non-verbal due to other health conditions such as common mental illness like schizophrenia and autism, developmental disorder or intellectual disability and emergency conditions like stroke and coma.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

Sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula with assumptions; p = 41.4% [43], 95% CI, and 5% margin of error (d = 0.05). The minimum sample size was 373 and the final sample size was 410 after adding 10% oversampling to account for non-response rate. Samples were recruited using systematic random sampling technique and allocated proportionally to the five health facilities based on the number of target population (ANC follow-up) at each facility.

Study variables

In this study, the dependent variable was adherence to iron and folic acid supplementation (IFAS), while the independent variables include socio-demographic characteristics (age, marital status, educational status, occupation, income level), as well as obstetric, and medical-related characteristics such as parity/gravidity, type of gestation, gestational age, number of ANC contacts, receipt of counseling, history of anemia, current anemia status, and history of hypertension.

Operational definition

Adherence

Pregnant women have taken iron and folic acid tablets, or their equivalent, at least four times per week during the month leading up to the survey or for a minimum of 90 days throughout their pregnancy; failure to do so constitutes non-adherence [42, 55].

Knowledge about IFAS: Individuals who scored at or above the average on a set of 40 Likert scale questions designed to evaluate respondents’ comprehensive knowledge of IFAS were considered good, while those who scored below the average were regarded as poor [56]. The 40-Likert scale includes several relevant questions, categorized as follows: 5 items address the benefits of IFAS, 7 items discuss potential side effects, 6 items focus on managing those side effects, 6 items relate to the impacts of iron and folate deficiency, 7 items examine the features of anemia, and 7 items identify dietary sources that can boost blood levels. Additionally, an item on frequency of taking IFAS and an item on duration of taking IFAS. For scoring, a correct answer was assigned a score of “1,” while an incorrect answer received a score of “0.” The total scores for each participant were calculated, and the knowledge levels were classified as either high or low based on the median score. Participants with scores above the median were categorized as having high IFAS knowledge, while those with scores below the median were categorized as having low IFAS knowledge.

Tertiary hospitals: Tertiary hospitals constitute the highest level in a three-tiered healthcare system, designated as Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals. These facilities serve large catchment populations (nearly 3.5-5 million people), providing specialized care for complex medical conditions and managing referred cases that primary and secondary facilities cannot adequately address (Ethiopian Hospital Accreditation Standards ES 7191:2025).

Data collection instrument, procedure and quality control

A semi-structured questionnaire containing socio-demographic variables and obstetric and medical-related variables, face-to-face interviews, and document review were used to collect data. The information gathered from clinic cards includes socio-demographic factors such as age, marital status, and occupation, as well as obstetric and clinical factors, including gravidity, parity, and type of gestation, number of ANC contacts, and any history of hypertension or anemia. The authors recorded gestational age (GA) directly from the review of women’s cards using ultrasound results. However, when an ultrasound report was not available, GA was calculated based on the last normal menstrual period (LNMP) indicated. On the other hand, the anemia status of pregnant mothers was evaluated through physical assessments and blood sample analyses, specifically hemoglobin tests requested by midwife nurses, with the results (laboratory findings) being recorded. Hemoglobin cutoff values for diagnosing anemia were as follows: Hb < 11 g/dL (mild anemia), Hb between 7 and 9.9 g/dL (moderate anemia), and Hb < 7 g/dL indicates severe anemia.

The training was given to data collectors and the supervisor by the principal investigator about the objectives of the study, data collection techniques, and ethical issues. Data collectors were two midwives and one supervisor. Strict supervision was taken during the data collection process and confidentiality was maintained during the study.

Data processing and statistical analysis

The data were coded, cleaned, entered into Epi-Data 4, and for analysis, exported to STATA 16. Frequency tables with percentages were utilized to present categorical variables, while means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables. To identify the relationship between adherence to IFAS and predictors, a logistic regression (bi-variable and multivariable) model was computed. The multivariable logistic regression analysis included variables with a p-value of ≤ 0.2 in the binary logistic regression. The strength of association was assessed using the crude odds ratio (COR) and adjusted odds ratio (AOR) at 95% CI. Hosmer and Lemeshow, for the corresponding model goodness of fit and variance inflation factor, verified the multicollinearity among predictor variables [57, 58]. Finally, p-values of less than 0.05 were used to determine which variables were statistically significant.

Discussion

Anemia during pregnancy is one of the most common public health problems, particularly in resource-limited settings, and can have adverse fetal and maternal consequences. Hence, this study aimed to determine the extent to which pregnant women adhere to IFAS and identify the factors associated with adherence in rural health facilities.

In this study, 45.1% [95% CI: 40.3–49.9%] of pregnant women adhered to IFAS, which is consistent with nationally reported pooled adherence 46.15% [46] and Debre Tabor, Ethiopia 44.0% [35]. However, this finding is higher than studies in Metema District, Ethiopia 34.9% [59] Uganda 22.3% [41] and sub-Saharan study 28.7% [52]. On the other hand, this proportion is lower than studies in Dire Dawa, Ethiopia 71.8% [47], Debre Markos, Ethiopia 52.8% [53], Sudan 79.0% [42], Ghana 84.5% [40], Sri Lanka 80.1% [39], India 63.0% [36], Iran 71.6% [37], and WHO target > 80% [60].

The differences are likely due to the ongoing conflicts in the Waghimra zone of Amhara, which have resulted in the destruction of healthcare facilities. This has a significant impact on both preventive and curative health services, including IFAS [61, 62]. In areas affected by armed conflict, disruptions in the medical supply chain are common. This is because conflict damages infrastructure, and transport restrictions and road blockades cause shortages of essential medical supplies, including medicines, supplements, vaccines, and diagnostic tools. Moreover, healthcare workers may face displacement or inability to reach their workplaces due to the destruction, looting, or forced shutdown of health facilities. Pregnant women often avoid seeking care due to fear of violence or lack of safe transportation [5, 6, 63]. As a result, their ANC follow-ups are compromised, resulting in a decrease in ANC attendance [62, 64]. This results in poor pregnancy, reproductive health, and fertility outcomes [7, 65]. The discrepancy is also partly related to the survey method, sample size estimation, and pre-existed awareness about IFAS across studies. This study was conducted in a rural health facilities, and it found a proportion that is higher than the results from West Tigray (urban: 37.2%; rural: 28.9%) [66], pooled EDHS (urban: 17.7%; rural: 11.2%) [67] and 22 Sub-Saharan African Countries (urban: 41.2%; rural: 23.3%) [68]. The disparities in the results could be caused by factors such as media exposure, education levels, employment status, and healthcare access, all of which are typically easier to obtain in urban settings [69]. Misconceptions regarding IFAS can arise from certain cultural beliefs, such as the idea that they cause excessive bleeding during childbirth or result in larger babies [70]. To address misconceptions, health care providers should dispel myths by giving precise information that is adapted to the needs and cultural background of clients. Counselors, for instance, can clarify possible side effects and how to manage them, stress the advantages of IFA adherence, and explain the significance of IFAS for the mother’s and the baby’s health.

Mothers who got counseling about IFAS were 2.3 times more likely to be adherent to IFAS than those who did not get counseling about IFAS. This is consistent with research conducted in Northwest Ethiopia [53], and Uganda [41]. It likely stems from counseling which enhances knowledge, corrects misconceptions about IFAS [71], addresses fear of side effects, and strengthens behavioral change and motivation [47]. Additionally, counseling improves follow-up care and builds trust between providers and patients, which facilitates adherence to IFAS [72].

The odds of adherence to IFAS for pregnant women whose preceded ANC contact at tertiary hospital was 2.6 times higher as compared to those mothers who had ANC contacts at health centers. A possible clarification is that when health facilities advance through the provision of materials, drugs, and services, along with the involvement of experts, clients are more likely to receive uninterrupted services. This, in turn, increases client satisfaction and encourages adherence to recommended health practices. Participants who had three and more ANC contacts was 3.5 times more likely to adhere to IFAS in contrary to pregnant who had one ANC contact. This result is supported by findings from Debre Markos, Ethiopia [53],, Dire Dawa, Ethiopia [47], and Sudan [42]. This is probably because pregnant women who have more antenatal visits are likely to be better informed about the benefits of Iron and IFAS for preventing anemia and improving pregnancy outcomes. These visits provide opportunities for counseling and education about the advantages of IFAS, as well as information on its common side effects [73]. Mothers who had good knowledge about IFAS were 4 times more likely to adhere to IFAS as compared to their counterparts. This is similar with results from Debre Tabor, Ethiopia [35], Sudan [42], Uganda [41] and Ghana [40]. The possible reasons could be that pregnant women with good knowledge about IFAS may have a positive attitude and practice toward IFAS and its undesirable consequences. Anemic pregnant women were 3 times more likely to adhere IFAS than non-anemic pregnant. This is consistent with Debre Tabor, Ethiopia [35], Dire Dawa, Ethiopia [47], Ghana [40], Iran [37] and Sri Lanka [39]. It is probably due to pregnant women with anemia may have a greater motivation to address their health condition, as anemia is associated with a higher risk of complications [54].

Strength of the study

This study’s main strength lies in its ability to assess the adherence to IFAS among pregnant women in rural communities of conflict-affected areas in Ethiopia. The findings reveal that adherence is low, which can be attributed to factors such as a lack of knowledge regarding the importance and potential side effects of IFAS, as well as the number of ANC contacts attended by pregnant women.

This study has limitations. A cross-sectional design restricts the ability to make causal inferences. Since the study focused on the Waghimra Zone, the findings may limit generalizability. Furthermore, the ongoing conflict in the area could serve as a potential confounder. Recall bias was an anticipated issue because adherence was predicated on the prior one-month intake of IFAS. In this study, participants with lower socioeconomic status had limited access to health information and education which possibly resulted in reduced adherence to IFAS. Moreover, the adherence to iron and folic acid supplementation was assessed based on self-reported data from pregnant women, which may not accurately reflect the actual compliance rate. Thus self-reported adherence potentially prone to social desirability bias and recall bias. To reduce recall bias, researchers should inquire about the number of iron and folic acid pills pregnant women took prior to the survey. To address social desirability bias, data collectors should guarantee anonymity and confidentiality, utilize self-administered questionnaires, and maintain a non-judgmental approach when addressing sensitive questions. More precisely, this study recommends that future research validate biomarkers, including serum ferritin, hemoglobin, serum folate, RBC folate, and homocysteine.

Conclusion

This study revealed that adherence to IFAS far below the standard of WHO recommendation (45% vs. > 80%). Key factors influencing adherence to IFAS include counselling on its importance, the number of antenatal care (ANC) contacts, a good understanding of IFAS, and the mother’s current anemia status.

Recommendations

To enhance IFAS adherence in rural areas, we recommend the following strategies:

Promote awareness among pregnant women about the importance of Iron and Folic Acid Supplementation and encourage more frequent antenatal care contacts.

Policy Implications: Since pregnant women are particularly vulnerable during armed conflict, ANC services should be promoted through mobile clinics and outreach initiatives in collaboration with partners. The strategies suggested by the authors for mobile clinics include flexible service delivery, community engagement, and integrated services. Moreover, community-based educational programs, like peer support groups, should be implemented. Integrating IFAS with other ANC key services, such as malaria prophylaxis, tetanus vaccination, and deworming, is highly desirable.
