Salivary Testosterone Concentration and Cognitive Impairment among Japanese Older Adults in the Toon Health Study
Authors: Juna Kamijima, Ai Ikeda, Hadrien Charvat, Koutatsu Maruyama, Kiyohide Tomooka, Ryoichi Kawamura, Yasunori Takata, Haruhiko Osawa, Madoka Tatsukawa, Miyuki Yoshida, Akiko Kooka, Tamami Shiba, Tomoko Nishiyama, Kazumi Fujimura, Keiko Suyama, Isao Saito, Takeshi Tanigawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAs the number of people with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) increases, modifiable risk factors, such as stress and an unhealthy lifestyle, and preventative measures are gaining interest. However, evidence for an association between potential biomarkers of such risk factors and MCI is limited. The study aimed to investigate the association between the salivary testosterone concentration and MCI in Japanese community-dwelling older adults.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 359 participants (159 men and 200 women) aged 60 years and over. MCI was evaluated using the Japanese version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment; a score <26 was deemed indicative of MCI. A saliva sample was collected after the patient chewed sugar-free gum for 5 min in the morning, and the salivary testosterone concentrations were assayed. A modified Poisson regression model was used to examine the association between the salivary testosterone concentration and MCI after adjusting for age, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, alcohol intake, smoking status, physical activity, sleep hours, and age at menopause (women only).ResultsFor women, the multivariable-adjusted prevalence ratio (PR; 95% confidence interval [CI]) of MCI for the highest-quartile group of salivary testosterone concentrations compared with the lowest-quartile group was 0.52 (0.26, 1.04), and the multivariable-adjusted PR (95% CI) for 1-standard deviation increments of log-transformed salivary testosterone concentrations was 0.74 (0.61, 0.89). A similar trend was found in men, but it was not statistically significant.ConclusionThis study of Japanese individuals aged 60 years or older provides important evidence that a higher salivary testosterone concentration is associated with lower prevalence of MCI and higher cognitive function in women.

Introduction

The number of people with dementia is projected to increase from 57.4 million cases globally in 2019 to 152.8 million cases in 2050 [1]. According to the 2024 report of the Lancet Commission, 14 modifiable risk factors for dementia include hypertension, physical inactivity, diabetes, excessive alcohol consumption, smoking, and obesity, among others, and their management may help reduce dementia risk, with a potential population attributable fraction of up to 45.3% [2]. People who have experienced mild cognitive impairment (MCI) are considered at high risk of dementia but appropriate interventions, such as lifestyle interventions (e.g., physical activity and diet) and cognitive training, can improve cognitive performance in those with MCI [3, 4]. In other words, early intervention via the reduction of lifestyle-related risk factors among MCI patients is important to delay or prevent the onset of dementia.

Testosterone may have a positive effect on cognitive function. Although not all studies have yielded consistent results [5, 6], many have shown that testosterone-replacement therapy exerts positive effects on healthy older men [6, 7]. Moreover, patients treated with androgen-deprivation therapy for prostate cancer have an increased risk of dementia according to one meta-analysis [8]. Two epidemiological studies of community-dwelling older women and men, respectively, showed that high or moderately high testosterone concentrations are associated with or predict better performance on cognitive-function tests [9, 10]. Animal models have suggested that testosterone influences cognitive function by enhancing synaptic plasticity [11, 12], increasing dendritic thorns of CA3 neurons in the hippocampus [13]. Furthermore, testosterone may play an inhibitory role in the prevention of Alzheimer’s disease by reducing beta-amyloid neurotoxicity [14] and beta-amyloid production and enhancing beta-amyloid degradation [15].

Another possibility is that levels of testosterone may be influenced by certain risk factors for dementia, including an unhealthy lifestyle (e.g., smoking and excessive alcohol consumption [2]) and chronic stress [16]. Epidemiological studies revealed substantially lower testosterone concentrations with certain lifestyles including a sedentary lifestyle [17], smoking history [18], and excessive alcohol intake [19]. Other studies have shown that the testosterone concentration is associated with stress-related disorders such as depression [20, 21] and anxiety disorder [21] as well as social stress (i.e., loss of spouse [22]). It is therefore possible that the detrimental effect of unhealthy lifestyle and chronic stress on the cognitive function is at least partly mediated by a decrease in testosterone concentration.

However, evidence on the association between testosterone and cognitive function in older men and women is inconsistent [5, 23], and studies on such an association in Asia are limited [24]. Therefore, we investigated the cross-sectional association between salivary testosterone concentration and cognitive function, especially MCI, in community-dwelling Japanese residents.

Methods

Participants

This study was based on data from the Toon Health Study, a longitudinal study established during 2009–2012 to reveal scientific evidence for the effective prevention of cardiovascular diseases. The 1st Toon Health Study involved 2,032 community-dwelling men and women from Toon City, Ehime Prefecture, Japan, who were aged 30–79 years at the time of enrollment. Participants had been asked to participate in follow-up surveys every 5 years. For the present study, only the 1,776 participants who took part in the 2nd study during 2014–2017 were considered eligible; therefore, our study population was slightly different from that of the 1st Toon Health Study.

We excluded participants younger than 60 years old who did not undergo the Japanese version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-J) (n = 633), and those who had a history of stroke (n = 21) or were using medication for stroke (n = 20). Our target population was the remaining 1,102 community-dwelling men and women from Toon City who were aged 60–84 years during 2014–2017 and had no history of stroke. Lastly, participants with missing data on the salivary testosterone concentration, either because of not being collected (n = 5) or because the remaining volume of saliva was insufficient to measure testosterone (saliva was used for another studies [25, 26]) (n = 724), and women with missing data on the age at menopause (n = 14) were excluded. Finally, 159 men and 200 women were included in this analysis, as illustrated in Figure 1.

Participant flow diagram.

Measurement of Salivary Testosterone Concentrations

Saliva was collected from each participant in the morning. Reception hours in the Toon Health Study were from 8:00 AM to 9:30 AM. After participants provided written informed consent, saliva samples were collected. Previous studies have shown that serum [27, 28] and salivary [28] testosterone concentrations peak in the morning, and the Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline for testosterone therapy in men with hypogonadism recommends that testosterone concentrations can be measured in the morning after an overnight fast [27]. At the time of sample collection in the Toon Health Study, all participants had been fasting for at least 10 h. Moreover, they had been prohibited from consuming more than 69 g of alcohol on the day before participation in the study and from engaging in vigorous exercise, including walking and heavy labor such as farm work, on the morning of participation. They also had been prohibited from tooth-brushing on the morning. Participants chewed 1 g of sugar-free gum (Tokyo Shizaisha, Tokyo, Japan) for 5 min, and the participants spat the produced saliva into a plastic collection tube. The samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C and stored at −80°C until they were assayed [26]. Approximately 50 samples, including a quality control sample, were measured per batch. To monitor assay performance, intra- and inter-day coefficients of variation were calculated. Salivary testosterone concentrations were measured via liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. The limit of detection for this assay is 1 pg/assay, and the numerical values for samples below the limit of detection were interpolated from the calibration curve. Intra- and inter-day coefficients of variation were 1.4–2.8% and 3.4–5.1%, respectively. Salivary testosterone concentrations were analyzed according to the standard protocol of ASKA Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Kanagawa Prefecture, Japan) [29].

Assessment of MCI

The cognitive function of each participant was assessed using the MoCA-J, which is used to assess cognitive function in multiple domains (e.g., memory, visuospatial abilities, executive function, attention, concentration, linguistic cognition, and orientation) and is generally used to screen for MCI [30]. The maximum MoCA-J score is 30, and participants with less than 13 years of education receive an additional 1 point to their score. Participants with a MoCA-J score less than 26 were defined as having MCI [30].

Assessment of Background Factors

Height and weight were measured while the patients were wearing stockings and light clothing, and weight was calculated by subtracting 500 g as the weight of the clothing from the measured value. The patient’s body mass index (BMI) was calculated as their weight (kg) divided by the square of their height (m2). Blood pressure was measured twice, after the patient had been seated for at least 5 min, by using an automatic sphygmomanometer (BP-103iII; OMRON Colin Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and the average value was recorded. Participants with a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, or currently taking medication were defined as having hypertension. An oral glucose-tolerance test was conducted after the patient had fasted for at least 10 h. Participants with a fasting blood glucose concentration ≥126 mg/dL, 2-h-postload glucose concentration ≥200 mg/dL, or currently taking medication were defined as having diabetes mellitus. We used a self-completed questionnaire to evaluate educational background; alcohol intake, smoking status (never smoker, ever smoker); social network; typical hours of sleep at night; and age at menopause (women only). The Berkman-Syme Social Network Index (SNI) [31, 32] was evaluated by totaling the scores for marital status (no = 0, yes = 1), close friends and relatives (0–2 friends and 0–2 relatives = 0, otherwise = 1), and frequency of group participation (never/almost never = 0, sometimes = 1, less than once a week/once or more per week = 2). Habitual physical activity was measured using the Japan Arteriosclerosis Longitudinal Study Physical Activity Questionnaire [33]. Physical activity (metabolic-equivalent hours/day) was calculated as the intensity and duration of each activity, except for the hours of sleep.

Statistical Analysis

To determine trends in baseline characteristics according to quartile groups of salivary testosterone concentration, linear regression analysis or binary logistic regression analysis was used. The sex-specific association between the salivary testosterone concentration and MCI status was assessed in terms of prevalence ratios (PRs) obtained through modified Poisson regression [34]. Analyses were first conducted with quartiles of salivary testosterone concentration; in a second step, we assessed the existence of a log-linear relationship between the logarithm of the salivary testosterone concentration and the prevalence of MCI, with results given in terms of a 1-standard deviation (SD) increase. To assess the relationship between 1-SD increments of the logarithm of the salivary testosterone concentration and the MoCA-J score, a beta regression model [35] was used because of the specific features of bounded outcome variables such as heteroscedasticity [36]. In each case, three different models were fitted. Model 1 was adjusted for age alone. Model 2 was adjusted for age plus the following background factors (based on subject-matter knowledge): BMI, hypertension (no/yes), diabetes mellitus (no/yes), alcohol intake, smoking status (never/ever), physical activity, and sleep hours. For women, age at menopause was also incorporated as a covariate. Educational background was not incorporated as a covariate because it was already reflected in the outcome (the MoCA-J score). In model 3, inverse probability weights obtained through a propensity score [37] were used to correct for possible selection bias in the analyses. Briefly, we compared the basic characteristics between participants who were and those who were not included in the final study analysis among the 1,102 target participants. The propensity score was then calculated from a logistic regression model for the probability of being included in the present study, incorporating all relevant factors that differed (p < 0.1) between individuals included and those not included in the analysis, namely, age, BMI, hypertension, age at menopause (women only), alcohol intake, sleep hours, social network, and MoCA-J score.

PRs were used in the main analyses because the prevalence of MCI was relatively high, interpreting odds ratios as approximations of relative risks might not be appropriate. Nonetheless, as part of the sensitivity analysis, we repeated the above modeling steps using logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios. Also, in the main analyses, we did not consider the rate of saliva flow, because the rate of diffusion through the acinar cells is so fast (as lipophilic compounds such as testosterone are highly membrane-permeable) that their concentrations can be considered independent of the rate of saliva flow [38]. In the sensitivity analysis, we repeated the modified Poisson model analyses using an adjusted salivary testosterone concentration (pg/[mL·g]), calculated as the salivary testosterone concentration (pg/mL) divided by the salivary mass (g) of each participant.

All analyses were stratified by sex because the salivary testosterone concentration greatly differed between men and women (median = 34.7 pg/mL for men; median = 2.3 pg/mL for women). For the participants whose salivary testosterone concentrations were below the limit of detection, we used the interpolated values. A p value <0.05 indicated statistical significance. All analyses were performed using the SAS statistical package version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

The 359 participants (159 men and 200 women) in this study sample were younger and had a lower likelihood of MCI than the 743 participants (255 men and 488 women) who were over 60 years of age and underwent the MoCA-J but were excluded from the final sample, both among men and among women. Furthermore, the prevalence of hypertension was lower among men and the age at menopause was higher among women in this study. In general, this study sample had a better risk profile and higher cognitive function than the excluded population (online suppl. The prevalence of MCI in the present study was 40.9% in men and 24.5% in women. The multivariable-adjusted PRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of MCI according to the quartiles of salivary testosterone are presented in Table 2. The salivary testosterone concentrations of 13 women were below the limit of detection, and all of them were included in the lowest quartile. After propensity score weighting, among women, the multivariable-adjusted PR (95% CI) of MCI for the highest-quartile group of salivary testosterone concentrations compared with the lowest-quartile group was 0.52 (0.26, 1.04), and that for 1-SD increments of log-transformed salivary testosterone concentrations was 0.74 (0.61, 0.89; model 3). Moreover, results of the beta regression model suggested that a 1-SD increment of the log-transformed salivary testosterone concentration was positively associated with the MoCA-J score for women (shown in Table 3, β [95% CI] = 0.10 [0.05, 0.15]). Similar results were found in men, but they were not statistically significant.

Discussion

In our cross-sectional analysis, the salivary testosterone concentration was related to cognitive function in women. The prevalence of MCI was lower among women who had a higher salivary testosterone concentration even after adjusting for various risk factors of dementia, suggesting that testosterone may be directly associated with cognitive function.

The results of this study are consistent with those of a previous study in which women with Alzheimer’s disease had a lower free androgenization index [39]. In another study of community-dwelling adults, women with higher Mini-Mental State Examination scores had higher total and bioavailable testosterone concentrations [9]. However, a systematic review of the association between testosterone and cognitive performance in postmenopausal women suggested a lack of precision for the discrimination of different concentrations of testosterone at lower concentrations in women [23]. To our knowledge, this is the first report of the relationship between the salivary testosterone concentration and cognitive function in which liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry was used; this technique can be used to measure low concentrations of steroid hormones that cannot be measured using an immunoassay [29, 40]. In addition, previous studies have indicated that salivary testosterone concentrations reflect the plasma bioavailable testosterone [40] and free testosterone [41], which may be more important for cognitive function as such testosterone can cross the blood-brain barrier [42].

Estrogens, such as estradiol, may have neuroprotective actions similar to those of testosterone, preventing the formation and accumulation of β-amyloid protein, which is one of the neuropathological characteristics of Alzheimer’s disease [43]. In postmenopausal women, the ovaries cease to produce estrogens, which may increase the risk of Alzheimer’s disease. Therefore, the formation of estradiol, primarily from the aromatization of testosterone in extra-gonadal tissue such as the adrenal cortex, may be important to maintain such women’s estrogen supply and prevent cognitive decline [6, 44]. Furthermore, testosterone reportedly increases the dendritic-spine density in pyramidal neurons in the CA1 region of the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex in ovariectomized rats independently of estrogen action; therefore, testosterone may have a protective effect on cognitive function in women by itself [45]. In addition, Lee et al. [46] reported that a 1-pmol/L increase in the free testosterone concentration in women but not in men was associated with a 7% decrease in the risk of being amyloid-positive (having a standardized uptake value ratio >1.21) [46]. This suggests that even a slight difference in the testosterone concentration has clinical significance for women. Moreover, the study [46] has pointed out that there was a hypothetical minimal testosterone threshold required for the neuroprotective effects, which may make women to be more vulnerable rather than men to the small decrease in testosterone, with regard to early pathological amyloid deposition in the brain.

A low testosterone concentration may be associated with chronic stress and an unhealthy, stress-related lifestyle, which are also major risk factors for dementia. In previous studies, a lower testosterone concentration was associated with chronic stress [47–49]. Chronic stress activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, promoting the secretion of cortisol; such secretion may inhibit the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, which regulates the production of testosterone [47, 48]. Moreover, chronic stress suppresses the secretion of gonadotropins such as luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone by the pituitary gland. Their decrease may negatively affect the testosterone concentration [49]. For older women in Japan, a major driver of chronic psychosocial stress is caregiving [50]. In the present study, women who had a higher social network in our study tended to have lower salivary testosterone concentrations, which may indicate that women tend to be susceptible to chronic stress related to traditional gender roles at home [51] or to interpersonal relationships [52]. Moreover, chronic psychosocial stress may be related to lifestyle factors such as smoking [53] and excessive drinking [54]. Although we observed no relationship between alcohol intake and salivary testosterone concentrations in this study, likely because it included the total of only 20 (18 men and 2 women) excessive drinkers (≥56.7 g/day of alcohol [19]), women with a history of smoking had lower salivary testosterone concentrations. Therefore, it is possible that the effect of chronic psychosocial stress or stress-related lifestyle factors on cognitive function might be at least partly mediated by modifications of the testosterone concentration. On the other hand, although we observed no differences in the association between salivary testosterone concentration and chronic psychosocial stress or the stress-related lifestyle factors in men, we found weak associations between salivary testosterone concentration and cognitive function for men in the present study.

The current study has several limitations. First, because of the cross-sectional study design, we cannot exclude the possibility of reverse causation. Second, as the salivary testosterone concentration was evaluated at one time point only, we could not account for diurnal variation in testosterone concentrations. However, we collected saliva samples at roughly the same time in the morning for all participants. In addition, previous studies have shown that diurnal variation in testosterone concentrations in older adults tends to be smaller than that in younger adults [27, 55]. As the participants in the present study were aged 60 years or older, the diurnal variation in their testosterone concentrations was likely relatively small. Third, some behaviors, such as smoking and caffeine intake, which may affect the testosterone concentrations in saliva, were not prohibited before saliva collection in this study. Fourth, no duplicate aliquots or reassay reliability checks were performed. Fifth, we cannot exclude the possibility of selection bias due to the large proportion of missing data regarding salivary testosterone concentration in this study. The included participants were healthier than the individuals who were excluded; hence, the association between salivary testosterone concentrations and MCI might have been underestimated. Therefore, extrapolation of the present results to the general population needs to be undertaken with care. Sixth, although we adjusted for various possible confounding factors in this study, we cannot exclude the possibility of residual confounding by unmeasured variables such as genetic influences, including the ε4 allele of apolipoprotein E that may affect the association between testosterone and Alzheimer’s disease [56]. Lastly, this study had a limited sample size, which may explain why we could not detect a significant association in the complete case analysis between salivary testosterone concentration and cognitive function in men.

In summary, we found that a higher salivary testosterone concentration was associated with a lower prevalence of MCI and higher cognitive function among Japanese community-dwelling older women. In future, a longitudinal study with a larger sample would be needed to have a better assessment of the potential association between salivary testosterone concentration and cognitive function.
