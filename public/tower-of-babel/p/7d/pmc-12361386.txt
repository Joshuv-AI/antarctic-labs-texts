A physics-informed Airy beam learning framework for blockage avoidance in sub-terahertz wireless networks
Authors: Haoze Chen, Atsutse Kludze, Yasaman Ghasempour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The line-of-sight blockage is one of the main challenges in sub-terahertz wireless networks. Interestingly, the extended near-field range of sub-terahertz nodes gives rise to near-field wavefront shaping as a feasible remedy to tackle this issue. Recently, Airy beams emerged as one promising solution that opens significant opportunities to circumvent blockers with unique self-accelerating properties and curved trajectories. Yet, to unleash the full potential of curved beams in practice, one fundamental challenge remains: How to find the best beam trajectory? In principle, an infinite number of trajectories can be engineered. To find the optimal trajectory, we develop a physics-informed machine-learning framework for Airy beam shaping based on a detailed understanding of near-field electromagnetics, ray optics, and wave optics. The experimental results indicate that Airy beams, when correctly configured, can substantially increase the link budget under high-blockage scenarios even compared to near-field beam focusing, providing insight into coverage expansion and blind-spot reduction.

Introduction

The sub-terahertz (sub-THz) band (broadly defined from 100 to 300 GHz) plays a critical role in next-generation wireless technologies1,2. In particular, the large amount of bandwidth available in this spectral regime can realize the vision of ultra-high-speed connectivity needed for wireless immersive augmented/virtual reality3 as well as the ultra-low-latency backhauling to bring broadband connectivity to remote and rural areas, which is the key to closing the digital divide4. Indeed, communication systems in the D-band regime (110–170 GHz) are already supporting multi-gigabit-per-second data rates with ranges extending beyond 2 km5, and similarly, data rates of beyond 80 Gbps have been reported for real-time uncompressed 8 K video transmission at 220 GHz in 1+ km ranges6. Despite these promising advances, realizing sub-THz communication in dynamic environments has been stalled by a key challenge—transient blockages that obstruct the line-of-sight between a transmitter and a receiver, yielding significant power drop and hindering the reliability of the communication link7–9. While non-line-of-sight paths, in the form of either natural specular reflections and scattering from the environment10,11 or engineered non-specular paths formed by intelligent reflecting surfaces12,13 may alleviate the problem to some extent, such paths are not always available and may not support high data rates.

More recently, an alternative class of solutions emerged, relying on the observation that a sub-THz wireless transmitter’s near-field (the Fresnel regime) may extend to a few tens of meters with reasonable aperture sizes14–16. For instance, the near-field of a 15-cm aperture at 140 GHz extends to 16.5 m, while in legacy sub-6 GHz bands, this range is only a few tens of centimeters. Hence, by exploiting the physics of near-field channels, sub-THz beams with interesting self-healing and self-accelerating properties can be created with unique advantages in mitigating the blockage issues17–20. Past studies have shown that such self-healing and self-accelerating can be theoretically and experimentally realized in the optics domain through multiple solutions to either the exact Helmholtz equation or its paraxial approximation, yielding Bessel19,20 and Pearcey17 beams that show promises in autofocusing and imaging21–23, as well as Mathieu24 and Weber25 beams that offer opportunities in structured light reconstruction and particle manipulation26,27. Additionally, non-diffractive beams generally show improved resilience when propagating through turbulent and scattering mediums28,29. These near-field beams bring unprecedented opportunities to tackle the decade-long problem of blockage in directional millimeter-wave and sub-THz wireless networks. Specifically, many of these beams are capable of generating curved trajectories30 that can be exploited to outfight or avoid the blocker. In this study, we specifically focus on curved-trajectory engineering based on Airy beams31, from which a parameterization and optimization framework can be established to overcome dynamic blockage in near-field communication scenarios. Notably, in Airy beams, waves asymmetrically accelerate in the transverse direction without the need for an external potential31. First introduced in 1979, the pioneering work of Berry and Balazs32 found a shape-preserving accelerating solution to the potential-free Schrödinger equation in the form of an ideal Airy function. Nevertheless, this result remained relatively unnoticed for decades. The first observation of Airy beams took place in 200733,34, almost 30 years after the original work. Since then, the generation and characterization of such beams have been studied mostly at optical frequencies35–38 with applications in communication39, imaging40, particle manipulation41, material processing42, and plasmons43. More recently, a few efforts have reported the generation of Airy beams in sub-THz regimes and explored the potential of their curved trajectory to go around obstruction44–46. Nonetheless, Airy beams, along with other self-accelerating beams, have yet to showcase their full potential for blockage recovery in practical wireless mediums, as optimizing the trajectory of such beams in a given obstruction scenario has remained unexplored.

In this work, we present a deep-learning-based framework to learn the optimal Airy beam configuration/trajectory that is able to deliver maximum power to a wireless receiver under line-of-sight obstruction. Indeed, deep learning has been shown as an instrumental means to conquer random scattering in optical wavefront shaping47,48 (e.g., in light-sheet fluorescence microscopy to improve imaging quality49). Here, to achieve our goal of adaptive Airy optimization in the sub-THz regime, the fundamental challenges that motivate the use of deep learning are rooted in the physics of near-field electromagnetics: First, the trajectory of a finite-energy Airy beam does not follow a closed-form propagation equation under obstruction50. Further, the design space is infinitely large in principle, as there are an infinite number of curved trajectories that can be engineered. Finally, characterizing the amount of received power under each candidate Airy beam requires iteratively solving the Rayleigh–Sommerfeld integral in inhomogeneous media (i.e., in the presence of blockers), which is considerably resource- and time-consuming. Therefore, an exhaustive search for the optimal Airy beam configuration is infeasible in any practical setting. Furthermore, conventional optimization methods also incur impractical computational and time-overheads due to a lack of gradient and closed-form representation of an objective function51. To offload such overheads, our solution is custom-tailored upon a detailed understanding of the physics of Airy beams, near-field channels, ray optics, and Fourier optics while leveraging advances in artificial intelligence. Our experimental results demonstrate that the proposed Airy trajectory optimization framework is substantially effective in learning the optimal trajectory to curve around blockages and delivering a link budget gain for mobile sub-THz wireless networks.

Max-power trajectory shaping

Exploiting the Airy near-field wavefront in practice demands fundamental changes in several aspects of wireless networking. In today’s wireless networks operating in mmWave bands, the transmitter has to align its directional beam toward the location of the receiver. Instead, here, a curved trajectory should be established between the transmitter and the receiver while taking into consideration the potential obstructions. Geometrically, an infinite number of trajectories can be engineered. Figure 1c depicts a few examples of such paths for a simple setting. Hence, merely finding a trajectory that satisfies the geometric requirements alone is not sufficient; it is, instead, important to find the optimal Airy beam that delivers maximum power to the receiver under a given wireless environment. Figure 1d shows that different curved trajectories between the transmitter and receiver may offer vastly different power levels, with and without obstruction.

In contrast, we introduce a data-driven approach to optimize Airy beam shape with zero-shot execution, where complexity and overheads are offloaded to the pre-training stage.

Airy trajectory learning

We exploit physics-driven insights to train a supervised neural network that solves Eq. (5) for the optimal Airy configuration. First, we use ray optics to define a blockage percentage (bl) that captures the ratio of blocked rays to the total emitted rays from the transmitter. This blockage parameter, which is purely a geometric feature, plays an important role in the potential link budget gain achieved by a curving beam, i.e., the gain offered by Airy beams (relative to a conventional focused beam) grows as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${bl}$$\end{document}bl increases. For the same reason, exploiting Airy self-accelerating beams can significantly reduce the shadow area of an obstruction (see Supplementary Note 4). Accordingly, we use \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${bl}$$\end{document}bl to threshold the activation of our neural network (i.e., reverting to conventional focused beams when the LOS path remains unblocked). Further, based on its relationship (though complicated as shown in Supplementary Note 4) to optimal trajectories, we also exploit bl as a direct input feature to the neural network that impacts its convergence to the optimal Airy parameters.

Experimental realization

We implement AI-learned self-accelerating Airy beams with over-the-air experiments in the D-band regime. We use a pre-trained neural network to learn the optimal Airy configuration for unseen experimental configurations. We emphasize that this approach requires a one-time training overhead, but the time and computational overhead of predicting the best Airy beam with a pre-trained neural network is minimal, opening the possibility for adoption in practical mobile wireless networks. The details of the experimental setup are provided in the “Methods” section and Supplementary Note 10. First, Fig. 3a, b shows that the calculated Airy beam trajectories can be physically realized in the experiments. In this experiment, we transmit a single tone at 120 GHz. We note that the slight discrepancy (if any) between measured and simulated patterns could be caused by the alignment of the setup, discretization of transmitting elements (as opposed to a continuous aperture), and imperfect metasurface fabrication. Interestingly, the half-power bandwidth supported by an Airy communication link depends on the exact trajectory of the beam, which itself varies with the receiver location and the blockage conditions. Hence, distinct from conventional wireless networks, the bandwidth for Airy beam communication is location and environment dependent (see Supplementary Note 11 for theoretical derivation and analysis). The root cause of such dependency is that the Airy beam phase profile at the transmitter (derived in Eq. (5)) is optimized for the center frequency; thus, different frequencies follow slightly different curving trajectories, which leads to power reduction at the receiver location. As discussed in Supplementary Note 11, beams with sharper curvatures cause larger spectral deviation and lower bandwidth, consequently. Similarly, a larger propagation distance between the receiver and the obstruction yields lower bandwidth. Nevertheless, we demonstrate the generation of wideband curved links, where high bandwidth can be achieved up to a certain distance limit. Figure 3c shows the measured radiation heatmaps of the same example setting in Fig. 3b, but this time transmitting at 115 GHz and 125 GHz. To quantitatively analyze the bandwidth of such a curved trajectory, we extract the main trajectory from these heatmaps and use typical polynomial fitting in Fig. 3d. As shown, the trajectories slightly deviate as we move away from the center frequencies. Regardless, for a small receiver aperture size of 5 mm (4-element antenna array at 120 GHz), a large bandwidth of 10+ GHz can be realized for propagation distances up to 12 cm and 5+ GHz up to 23 cm. The ability to create a stable wave trajectory for a wide bandwidth is important, as the communication link capacity grows linearly with bandwidth. We emphasize that our neural network framework is designed to learn the optimal beam with maximum deliverable power at the center frequency. Discussion

The results presented above demonstrate that adaptive trajectory shaping combined with unique properties of self-accelerating beams could be an important and unique asset in future mobile sub-THz wireless networks. We demonstrate that it is possible to accurately and efficiently learn the optimal near-field self-accelerating beam in dynamic sub-THz settings through a physics-informed neural network framework, which offers a meaningful benchmark for future wavefront optimization studies. While a brute force strategy to search over all possible Airy beam configurations is infeasible due to the infinitely large search space size and the lack of a closed-form computationally-efficient link budget calculation in the near-field, a pre-trained neural network offers the same communication performance while imposing negligible computation and time overhead for practical wireless transmitters. The generated near-field Airy beam can carry GHz-scale bandwidth without diverging from the optimized trajectory, allowing for high-speed communication rates. Trajectory shaping in real-time will obviously require rethinking the communication hardware and protocols across all layers of the stack. Importantly, the communication nodes should be equipped with reasonably-sized sub-THz phased arrays or tunable metasurfaces with the ability to be dynamically reconfigured, of which several small-scale prototypes have been recently reported in the literature57–64. Regardless, our results strongly suggest that mobile wireless networks operating above 100 GHz will gain substantial benefits from near-field wavefront shaping and adaptation.

Methods

Experimental setup

We conduct over-the-air experiments in the D-band using metasurfaces to generate Airy beams that curve around blockages. On the transmitter side, we use the Keysight PSG Analog Signal Generator (E8257D) with a maximum frequency of 42.5 GHz that feeds into a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4\times$$\end{document}4× up-convertor from VDI (WR6.5CCU-M4) that operates in the D-band. The resulting carrier sinusoid is mixed at the up-convertor with the data-modulated baseband input generated by a Keysight Arbitrary Waveform Generator (M8195A). The mixed waveforms are amplified with 20 dB gain (with VDI WR6.5AMP) and then transmitted through a horn antenna. A custom-fabricated metasurface with an optimized phase profile for Airy beam generation is placed close to the transmitting horn. For obstruction, we use a stone (completely blocking sub-THz frequencies) with the size of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$11{{\mathrm{cm}}}$$\end{document}11cm and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3{{\mathrm{cm}}}$$\end{document}3cm along transverse and propagation directions, respectively. On the receiver side, the corresponding down-convertor (WR6.5CCD-M4) and an identical but separate local oscillator are used to retrieve the received baseband signal, which is fed into a Keysight Real-Time Oscilloscope (UXR0254B) for processing. We measure the field propagation by collecting the received time-domain signal at various spatial points with a fine-grained resolution of 1.5 mm along both directions. For data modulation measurements, we use an intermediate frequency of 4 GHz at the baseband with varying bandwidth and modulation types. More illustrations on the experimental system can be found in the Supplementary Note 10.

Metasurface fabrication

We stress that the contribution of our work (i.e., the optimization system) is oblivious to metasurface implementations. Unfortunately, off-the-shelf, reasonably-sized, and electronically controllable metasurfaces at the sub-THz regime are not readily available. Recent developments are limited to small-scale prototypes, which typically exhibit very short near-field ranges–insufficient for generating Airy beams at standard experimental distances57–64. Hence, we offer a proof-of-concept verification of the effectiveness and practicality of our system model using passive surfaces, which are better suited for generating accurate yet static wavefronts over extended distances. Specifically, we choose C-shaped split-ring resonators as meta-elements to realize spatially varying phase profiles. By changing the geometry of such resonators (e.g., radius and opening angle), the phase of cross-polarization S21 can be fine-tuned across a wide passband. To accommodate D-band frequencies, our meta-elements range from 0.8 mm to 1.1 mm in diameter. For efficient and streamlined design and fabrication of metasurfaces, we conduct a parameter sweep on resonator geometries in CST Studio Suite to generate a look-up table for phase modulation. The sweep resolution for radius and opening angle is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10{{\mathrm{\mu m}}}$$\end{document}10μm and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${1}^{\circ }$$\end{document}1∘, respectively. We report an average phase error of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${1.16}^{\circ }$$\end{document}1.16∘ and an amplitude variance of 5.6% (artifact) across the full \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${360}^{\circ }$$\end{document}360∘ phase modulation range. In this work, we use the conventional half-wavelength spacing (1.25 mm grid size) to construct square-shaped \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20{{\mathrm{cm}}}\times 20{{\mathrm{cm}}}$$\end{document}20cm×20cm metasurfaces at 120 GHz. After the geometric optimization of the metasurface, they are fabricated on paper using the hot stamping technique65. Specifically, we first use a laser printer to print out the designed C-shaped resonator patterns. Then, we run the printed pattern together with aluminum foil through a standard office laminator. Under high temperatures, the aluminum powder on the foil adheres to the printed toner pattern to generate final resonating metasurfaces. We note that this fabrication process is scalable, cheap, and uses equipment that is available in typical home and office spaces.
