Added Value of Rotational Mechanics to Longitudinal Indices in the Risk Stratification of Patients With Left Ventricular Hypertrophy
Authors: Juan Geraldo, Celia Gil, Alejandro Queipo, Guillermo García‐Martín, Ester Galiana, Alberto Hidalgo, Paula Gramage, Hector Martinez‐Navarro, Jorge Lowenstein, Ildefonso Roldán, Vicente Mora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionIn the past, analysis of the rotational mechanics of the left ventricle (LV) has been complicated by a confusing codification system and also because it has been approached independently from myocardial function state. We hypothesized that, once global longitudinal strain (GLS) is altered as early sign of myocardial involvement, rotational mechanics is a determining factor in LV ejection fraction (LVEF) in patients with LV hypertrophy (LVH).Methods2D‐speckle tracking echocardiographic images were used to generate GLS and rotational parameters. Myocardial dysfunction was defined by GLS < 16%. We calculated the “Rotational Gradient” (RG), which is the result of the interaction of basal and apical rotations, whether twist (positive RG value) or rigid rotation pattern (negative RG value). The study included 67 healthy controls and 348 patients with LVH (173 with GLS ≥ 16%, and 175 with GLS < 16 %). We prospectively evaluated the subjects´ RG values according to GLS and LVEF state.ResultsUnlike patients with GLS ≥16% (all with LVEF ≥ 50%), in patients with myocardial dysfunction (GLS < 16%), RG showed a good relationship with LVEF (r = 0.73; p < 0.001). In these latter patients a smaller RG was associated with a probability of developing LVEF < 50% [OR = 1.25 (95% CI: 1.16–1.35; p < 0.001)], RG ≤ 13.4° predicted a LVEF < 50% [AUC 0.92 (95% CI: 0.87–0.96; p < 0.001), sensitivity 82.5% and specificity 92.5%], and a rigid rotation pattern was associated with the lowest LVEF values.ConclusionsRotational mechanics should be interpreted based on myocardial function state. In patients with GLS < 16% a smaller RG is associated with lower LVEF. RG can be used to stratify patients with LVH at risk of heart failure.Keywords: left ventricular hypertrophy, longitudinal strain, myocardial function, rotational gradient, rotational mechanics, speckle tracking echocardiography

Introduction

In a healthy heart, a systolic longitudinal ventricular shortening of fundamentally subendocardial origin occurs, as well as a simultaneous circumferential shortening that originates fundamentally at the transmural level. These normal orthogonal strains, along with the circumferential‐longitudinal shear strain, are the origin of the rotational movements of the heart [1, 2, 3]. As a rule, the base and apex rotate in opposite directions to produce the ventricular twist motion. Both longitudinal and circumferential contraction, as well as rotational movements, contribute to LV wall thickening.

Myocardial strain imaging, a speckle‐tracking method that measures myocardial contractility, is a sensitive early marker of myocardial dysfunction [4]. Most heart diseases are characterized by myocardial dysfunction in their initial phases due to subendocardial factors indicated by a reduction in global longitudinal strain (GLS), even in subclinical stages [4, 5, 6]. When longitudinal deformation is affected and disease develops, the predominant transmural circumferential fibers begin to deteriorate. This results in a progressive slowing down of rotational movements [7, 8].

In the past, the analysis of LV rotational mechanics has been hindered by several limitations; it has been carried out without taking into account whether or not there has been myocardial involvement and has relied on a rotational coding system that does not distinguish between rotational patterns (twist vs. rigid rotation) or quantify them correctly.

GLS—assessed by two‐dimensional speckle‐tracking echocardiography—has been shown to be an accurate predictor of the outcomes of patients with LVH and various cardiac diseases [9, 10, 11]. We hypothesized that, once the initial GLS becomes altered as a sign of early myocardial involvement, the subsequent alteration of rotational mechanics is a determining factor in the LVEF state of patients with LVH.

Methods

Study Population and Recruitment

The present prospective study included 415 participants, 67 healthy subjects recruited from individuals referred for an echocardiogram with normal LVEF and no established cardiovascular disease, and 348 patients with LV hypertrophy (LVH) which was confirmed when two‐dimensional echocardiographic images revealed an interventricular septal thickness ≥12 mm. Exclusion criteria were the following: (1) age <18 years, (2) inadequate image quality, (3) patients with prosthetic valves, (4) cardiac stimulation by pacemakers, (5) cardiac resynchronization devices, and (6) previous myocardial infarction. According to these criteria, 46 patients were excluded. After excluding patients all analyses were conducted on complete cases (Figure 1). The cardiopathies underlying LVH in each case were as follows: 58 amyloidosis, 137 severe aortic stenosis, 40 hypertrophic myocardiopathy, and 113 hypertensive cardiomyopathy.

The study was performed in agreement with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 and approved by local ethic committee. All participants gave informed written consent.

Conventional and Speckle Tracking Echocardiography Analysis

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed by trained and internally certified personnel using two ultrasound scanners (Vivid E9 and Vivid E95; GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, United Kingdom), each equipped with a 2.5‐MHz transducer, to obtain 2D echocardiographic images. All images were transferred to a workstation for computer analysis (EchoPAC version 203; GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, United Kingdom).

Only echocardiographic studies fulfilling the following conditions were included: (a) presence of apical 2‐chamber, 3‐chamber and 4‐chamber and short axis views at the level of mitral valve leaflet, papillary muscles and apical region distal to the papillary muscles and (b) frame rate above 50 frames/s. Echocardiography global and regional strain measurements were based on a 17 segment LV model. GLS was obtained from apical views. To determine strain, the investigator contoured the endocardial border of the LV. With the help of software, a second wider concentric circle is automatically generated next to the epicardium (verified by the operator). This generated a region of interest covering the entire transmural thickness of LV wall. The program provides automatized tracking confirmation and generates longitudinal strain values expressed as percentages (%) of shortening,

Rotation values were obtained from short axis views. For the measurement of LV rotations, 2D parasternal short‐axis images were recorded: one at the basal plane showing the middle level of the mitral valve using its diastolic opening as a reference, and the other at the apical plane traced below the insertion of the papillary muscles. The term “rotation” refers to the angular displacement (in degrees) of a myocardial segment in a transversal projection around the longitudinal axis of the LV, in either a clockwise or counterclockwise direction as viewed from the apex) [12]. GLS and rotations were assessed as the average of three cardiac cycles in patients in sinus rhythm and five in patients with atrial fibrillation. In patients with atrial fibrillation, cardiac cycles run with similar R‐R intervals were analyzed from the target echocardiographic loops.

We employed a coding system [13] in which basal and apical rotation angles are represented in absolute values that indicate the direction of the rotation (clockwise below the zero‐line vs. counterclockwise above the zero‐line, in both), without signs of positivity or negativity that can be interpreted, respectively, as beneficial or deleterious, when in fact they only indicate the direction of the rotation (clockwise vs. counterclockwise, in both). The term “Rotational Gradient” (RG) englobes the result of the interaction of basal and apical rotations, whether twist when base and apex rotate in opposite directions, or rigid rotation if they rotate in the same direction. The RG is calculated according to the following algorithm:

*When there is twist, RG° = (Minor rotation)° + (Major rotation)° [e.g., (5) + (10) = 15°]

*When there is rigid rotation, RG° = (Minor rotation)° – (Major rotation)° [e.g., (5) – (10) = –5°]

Thus, the existence of twist (normal or reverse) is always expressed as an RG with a positive value that is the sum of the basal and apical rotation angles (Figure 2A,B). Conversely, when there is rigid rotation (clockwise vs. counterclockwise), the resulting RG is always assigned a negative value that is the difference between the two rotation angles (Figure 2C,D). It is important to emphasize that the direction of apical or basal “rotation” (clockwise vs. counterclockwise) should not be confused with the “rotational gradient” value (twist‐positive vs. rigid rotation‐negative). “Torsion” is defined as RG (whether twist or rigid rotation pattern) divided by the longitudinal diameter of the LV.

Rotational patterns. “Twist” occurs when the base and apex rotate in opposite directions (A normal and B reverse). If they rotate in the same direction, the movement is described as “rigid rotation” (C clockwise and D counterclockwise). The RG curve is represented as a broken yellow line. Thus, the existence of twist (A, B) is always expressed as an RG with a positive value which is the sum of the basal and apical rotation angles. Conversely, when there is rigid rotation (C, D), the resulting RG is always assigned a negative value which is the difference between the two rotation angles.

For the sake of simplicity, all strain parameters are reported as absolute values, so that lower absolute strain values correspond to lower deformation values. GLS <16% is considered to represent myocardial dysfunction [14]. Two groups were established among the 348 patients with LVH: (1) Gr1, 173 with GLS ≥16% (normal myocardial function) and (2) Gr2, 175 with GLS <16% (myocardial dysfunction). LVEF was determined using Simpson's biplane method from the automatic ultrasound scanner software, verified by the operator, who was unaware of the strain/rotation results.

Statistical Analysis

All data analysis were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and MedCalc Statistical Software version 20.014 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium). Date is presented as frequency (percent), mean (±standard deviation), or median (quartiles), as appropriate. Agreement was evaluated using coefficient and Bland–Altman analysis, with calculation of bias and 95% limits of agreement (95% LoA). For reproducibility analyses, intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) estimates and their 95% confident intervals were calculated. We used a two‐way random‐effects model based on a mean rating (k = 2) and a two‐way mixed‐effects model (both with absolute agreement for individual assessments) to assess interobserver and intraobserver variability, respectively. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to check whether the parameters were normally distributed. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to compare groups, while the Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used to discern post hoc differences between pairs of means. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies with percentages and compared with Pearson's chi‐square test. The R values and the 95% confidence interval (95% CI) of the bivariate correlations between RG, torsion and LVEF were originated from the Pearson Correlation Coefficient. To assess potential multicollinearity among the independent variables and LVEF, a variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance values were calculated for each predictor (RG, torsion). VIF values ≥5 was considered to indicate moderate‐serious correlation among variables.

To test the independence of the predictive values of RG and torsion to determine the probability of showing a LVEF <50% (dependent variable) in patients with GLS <16%, we performed a multivariable binary logistic regression analysis including as key covariates (age, sex, LVH etiology) in the models. We analyzed RG and torsion separately and in a combined model to assess whether one remained significant over the other.

The RG and torsion cut‐off values that optimize the probability in each patient of showing LVEF <50% were obtained by ROC analysis, calculating the AUC (95% CI). The AUC curves, generated from RG and torsion, were compared. A bootstrap resampling procedure with 1000 iterations was implemented in R (version 4.5.2; rms package). p value <0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Reproducibility

We examined 10 randomly selected recordings that were measured by two observers using 2D‐speckle tracking echocardiography. In addition, we randomly selected 15 cases to assess the variability of the LVEF. The intra‐ and inter‐observer correlation coefficient and variability in GLS were 0.86 and –0.14 (95% LOA –2.51 to 2.23), and 0.87 and 0.50 (95% LoA –1.77 to 2.77), respectively. The intra‐ and inter‐observer correlation coefficient and variability in RG were 0.94 and –1.15 (95% LOA –3.81 to 1.51), and 0.85 and –1.13 (95% LOA –6.69 to 4.43), respectively. The intra‐ and inter‐observer correlation coefficient and variability in LVEF were 0.93 and –0.42 (95% LOA –3.39 to 2.55), and 0.91and –0.35 (95% LOA –3.68 to 2.97), respectively. Finally, when we analyzed LVEF grade categories (< and ≥50%) no cases were reclassified.

Discussion

The present study shows how 2D‐speckle tracking echocardiographic measures of myocardial rotational mechanics provide valuable information about the LVEF spectrum in patients with LVH. The primary outcome of interest is that RG is closely linked to GLS status. In patients with GLS <16% (myocardial dysfunction), RG values provide determinant information about LVEF status; specifically, a progressive decrease in RG is associated with lower LVEF, until LVrEF levels are eventually reached. In these patients a rigid rotation pattern of the LV is associated with a more severely impaired LVEF. In contrast, in patients without myocardial dysfunction (GLS ≥ 16%), LVEF status is independent of RG values and rotational pattern.

Until now, the analysis of LV rotational mechanics has been complicated by two factors: (a) it has been carried out without taking into account whether there has been myocardial involvement and (b) a confusing rotational coding system has been used to classify the LV. The system currently employed with available echocardiography and MRI devices does not distinguish between the different rotational patterns (twist vs. rigid rotation) [13], and therefore cannot quantify the RG.

To overcome the first drawback, we evaluate rotational mechanics according to the patient's myocardial function from the GLS values [14]. To address the second problem, we used a simple and recently created coding system [13] that allows us unequivocally differentiate LV rotational patterns (twist vs. rigid rotation), and correctly identify them from a distinctive quantification of RG. This allows establishing a gradual relationship between the RG and parameters such as LVEF, natriuretic peptides, functional class, or other factors.

Myocardial Involvement and Rotational Mechanics

In the setting of early myocardial dysfunction, the subendocardial layer is primarily affected. This leads to decreased GLS, even in subclinical stages [4, 15, 16]. In our community‐based cohort, abnormal GLS occurred in all patients with LVrEF and 64% with LVpEF. Thus, we can affirm that the information provided by GLS about LVEF is incomplete. GLS is only one of the components of myocardial deformation.

As the disease progresses, transmural insult results in concomitant mid‐wall and epicardial dysfunction, leading to GCS and rotational mechanics affectation [7]. The analysis of rotational mechanics, which is a result of the interaction between longitudinal and circumferential contraction, may detect abnormalities of the LV earlier than GCS [17, 18]. As can be seen in Figure 8, when we compared LVH patients with GLS ≥16% (Gr1, without criteria for myocardial dysfunction) to controls (CGr), a slight decrease in GLS (though still within normal limits) was observed during the initial stages of LVH (Gr1), along with a slight increase in RG. The same figure shows that, in patients with GLS < 16%, an unchanging RG (Gr2a) was associated with LVpEF, despite a significant decline in GLS. Finally, when transmural damage progressed and RG decreased (Gr2b), LVEF dropped to LVrEF levels. In this way, when GLS decreases, the maintaining or relative enhancing of transmural RG to preserve LVEF seems to be a compensatory mechanism.

Rotational Mechanics and LVEF

Several studies have shown increasing twist in patients with LVH of various origins [19, 20]. Minatoguchi et al. [21] observed an increase in LV twist in LVH and associated it with LVpEF, while a decrease in LV twist was associated with LVrEF, though the relationship between the two was only moderate (r = 0.50; p < 0.001). It is unknown whether any of the patients had rigid rotation. The coding system employed by the authors was applied as if all rotational patterns were twist, using the algebraic subtraction of apical and basal rotations [Twist = (apical rotation)—(basal rotation)] available on echocardiography devices. This approach does not distinguish twist from rigid rotation patterns [13], which makes it difficult to establish a reliable gradual relationship between rotational mechanics and LVEF.

Unlike previous coding system, the rotational mechanics coding system employed in the present study allows us to quantify RG while distinguishing between twist and rigid rotation. This quantification of RG reveals the importance of rotational mechanics in maintaining LVEF when myocardial dysfunction appears. A progressive decrease in GLS and stable RG were associated with LVEF preservation. In patients with GLS < 16%, for a similar reduction in GLS values maintaining an LVpEF ≥50% was related to higher RG values than LVrEF < 50% (Figure 6A and Table 3), and highlights the close relationship between decreasing RG values and lower LVEF [r = 0.73; p < 0.001] in this population. Twist was present in 84% of our patients with GLS < 16%, and rigid rotation pattern was present in 16%, which was associated with lowest LVrEF. As transmural involvement progresses, the RG decreases and the LVEF is reduced (Figure 8), which highlight their importance for the preservation of the LVEF once myocardial dysfunction develops. Most previous research [22, 23] has limited the evaluation of rigid rotation as a rotational pattern to patients with dilated cardiomyopathy. Consequently, findings in said patients—obtained using MRI tagging and 2D speckle tracking echocardiography—have supported a significant relationship between rigid rotation and more severe LV remodeling. In line with this, Guerra et al. [24] described how a rigid rotation pattern was predictive of heart failure in oncologic patients only when GLS <16%. Few clinical studies have evaluated LV rigid rotation in patients with LVH or explored its relationship with GLS [25]. In patients with GLS < 16% we report a rigid rotation pattern in the most severe cases of LVrEF; in fact, 67.6% of our patients with LVEF < 40% presented a rigid rotation pattern.

In contrast, subjects with GLS ≥ 16% (normal myocardial function, CGr and Gr1) presented a wide variability of RG values and rotational patterns (Figure 5) independent of LVEF (≥50% all), suggesting a basal behavior of rotational mechanics in a stand‐by mode while myocardial function is normal. Thus, lower GR values or rigid rotation pattern in the context of normal myocardial function (GLS ≥ 16%) have no impact on LVEF, while they are associated with lower LVEF values when there is myocardial dysfunction (GLS < 16%). The true interest of rotational mechanics analysis corresponds to patients with impaired longitudinal myocardial dysfunction by endocardium affectation, since the transmural involvement of rotational mechanics occurs after or simultaneously with GLS involvement, but no before. For this reason, the evaluation of rotational mechanics in patients without longitudinal myocardial dysfunction does not provide additional information on the state of the ventricular function.

To our knowledge, our results represent the first study to show that rotational mechanics should be interpreted in relation to the degree of longitudinal myocardial involvement. If GLS < 16% then evaluate RG; if RG < 20° the risk of developing LVEF < 50% increase, and is high when RG ≤ 13.4°. In this way, in patients with LVH, LV rotational mechanical position themselves as a marker of advanced myocardial dysfunction that may be helpful in stratifying individuals at risk of heart failure, or those in need of closer follow‐up or medical therapy [26], mainly in those with LVpEF. Sthor et al. [27] pointed out that “future work will benefit from a combined approach in which changes in LV rotational mechanics and GLS are considered and interpreted within the context of both altered physiology and disease state.” LVEF deterioration occurs when all compensatory mechanisms involved in maintaining ventricular function have failed. Identifying individuals with abnormalities in longitudinal myocardial function and rotational mechanics that precede the deterioration of LVEF can help define certain phenotypes that potentially benefit from therapeutic alternatives to reduce the risk of heart failure.

Limitations

Our results are based on patients recruited from one large academic medical center, and the findings may not be generalized to other settings. Strain analysis depends on two‐dimensional echocardiogram image quality, and we acknowledge that some variability may occur in the location of transversal planes measurements.

Study's implications apply to hypertrophic remodeling (regardless of cause) and that they may not translate to non‐LVH patients. Our cohort included a variety of LVH etiologies; results are intended to be general for LV hypertrophy, but individual diseases might show different rotational patterns that warrant further study.

Although 2D speckle‐tracking is clinically useful, it is restricted to the assessment of LV function in a single plane. Moreover, each segment must be evaluated sequentially and thus is subject to beat‐to‐beat variability. In the near future, 3D volume acquisition will undoubtedly become the method of choice for measuring strain and rotational mechanics. More time is needed to determine the precise role of 3D myocardial tracking in routine clinical practice.

Future studies with longitudinal data will help characterize the time course of the alteration of myocardial properties in LVEF patients. The validity of our results should be tested in prospective studies carried out in different clinical scenarios and thus improve the stratification of risk of heart failure.

Conclusion

The significance of rotational mechanics is closely linked to the state of myocardial function. LVEF is conditioned by RG values in patients with LVH and decreased longitudinal strain in such a way that a decrease in this rotational parameter is associated with lower LVEF.
