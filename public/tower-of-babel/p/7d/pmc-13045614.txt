Adolescent Athlete Returns to Competitive Basketball After Cardiac Arrest and Diagnosis of Probable Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Anusha Konduri, Melodie M. Lynn, Jordan E. Ezekian, Mansi Gaitonde, Benjamin D. Levine, Katherine Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReturn to play after cardiac arrest in young athletes with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) requires careful evaluation and shared decision-making.Case SummaryA 14-year-old male competitive basketball athlete survived an out-of-hospital ventricular fibrillation arrest and was diagnosed with probable HCM after comprehensive testing. He underwent implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement for secondary prevention. Sport-specific exercise testing and multidisciplinary shared decision-making with his family, clinicians, and athletic staff informed a graded return-to-play plan. He successfully returned to competitive basketball.DiscussionEmerging evidence suggests that selected athletes with HCM and implantable defibrillators may safely participate in competitive sports when evaluated with individualized, sport-specific assessment and managed through shared decision-making. This case illustrates how functional evaluation can inform exercise counseling and support participation in high-level athletics.Take-Home MessagesSport-specific evaluation can guide safe return to play in selected athletes with HCM. History of Presentation

A 14-year-old, previously healthy male competitive basketball player collapsed during a game and was found to be pulseless. No on-site automated external defibrillator (AED) was available. A physician bystander initiated cardiopulmonary resuscitation, which continued for approximately 5 minutes until the arrival of emergency medical services. On initial assessment, the cardiac rhythm was interpreted as polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (Figure 1). Spontaneous circulation was restored after two 200-J shocks. The patient was intubated and transported to a tertiary pediatric cardiac intensive care unit. On admission, he was intubated and sedated, with a normal cardiac and pulmonary examination and no signs of heart failure. He had a normal neurological examination after extubation on hospital day 1. A subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was placed for secondary prevention. Nadolol (20 mg daily) was initiated to suppress recurrent ventricular arrhythmias and to reduce the risk of inappropriate ICD shocks by limiting exercise-related sinus tachycardia. Past Medical History

The patient weighed 68 kg and was 181 cm tall. He had a history of migraines and mild intermittent asthma but had no prior cardiac symptoms, syncope, or abnormal preparticipation evaluations. He competed in basketball without restrictions, and there was no known family history of cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, or sudden cardiac arrest (SCA).

Investigations

On admission, laboratory studies and chest radiography were unremarkable. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm, left ventricular hypertrophy, anterior ST-segment elevation, and inferolateral T-wave inversions (Figure 2), consistent with abnormal rather than physiologic athletic repolarization. Echocardiography revealed mild concentric left ventricular hypertrophy with normal systolic function and no left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (Figure 3). Coronary computed tomography showed a normal origin and course of both the left and right coronary arteries. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed increased left ventricular mass (183 g, Z-score: +2.9), midseptal thickness of 15 to 16 mm, preserved ejection fraction (60%), and a small area of inferoseptal late gadolinium enhancement (Figure 4), consistent with evolving hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). Initial cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) after discharge using a standard Bruce treadmill protocol demonstrated a peak oxygen uptake (VO2) of 46.8 mL/kg/min (85% predicted) and peak heart rate of 192 beats/min (93% predicted) on nadolol. At the ventilatory threshold, VO2 was normal with a heart rate of 155 beats/min. The blood pressure response was appropriate, and there were no arrhythmia or signs of myocardial ischemia during exercise. The ICD demonstrated appropriate sensing.

Given the patient's desire to return to sport, an individualized, sport-specific exercise testing protocol was developed to better replicate competitive basketball. Six weeks after the initial CPET, a steady-state and incremental maximal CPET using a custom high-speed treadmill was performed at the Institute for Exercise and Environmental Medicine. The patient exercised at 2 submaximal work rates (4 and 6 mph [6.4 and 9.7 kph]) followed by an incremental test to exhaustion, achieving a peak VO2 of 52 mL/kg/min (91% predicted) and a peak heart rate of 179 beats/min (84% predicted) on nadolol. After the incremental test, a 12-minute simulation (1 quarter game) of competitive basketball was performed, incorporating walking/slow jogging, higher speed jogging, brief accelerations, and occasional breaks to mimic time-outs or free throws; metabolic assessment was not performed during this portion. A single premature ventricular contraction occurred during steady-state exercise, and no arrhythmias were observed during incremental or basketball-specific testing.

Management

Shared decision-making discussions included the patient, his mother (sole caregiver), pediatric cardiomyopathy cardiologist, electrophysiologist, sports cardiologist, team physician, and athletic trainer. Subcutaneous and transvenous ICD systems were considered and discussed with the patient and family. The subcutaneous ICD was preferred to preserve central venous access for future device needs, avoid lead fracture and other lead complications relevant to competitive contact sports, and reduce long-term infection risk. Preimplant electrocardiographic sensing screening met criteria, confirming suitability. A protective vest for ICD comfort was arranged for the patient; the vest has a shield placed in an athletic compression shirt, covering the ICD and acting as a shock absorber in case of sudden impact. Emergency preparedness measures were implemented, including an emergency action plan (EAP) and cardiopulmonary resuscitation training for coaching staff. The patient's school gym has been equipped with an AED, and the coaching staff carries a portable AED for away games to improve safety for all athletes and spectators present at events. A graded return-to-play program was recommended, emphasizing aerobic reconditioning, gradual reintroduction of basketball-specific drills, and limitation of sustained high-intensity efforts in the early phases of the program (Figure 5). Outcome and Follow-Up

Five months into follow-up since his SCA, the patient has been playing on his high school basketball team for 3 months without any syncopal events, cardiac symptoms, or ICD discharges. The ICD shock zone was programmed for rates exceeding 250 beats/min. He remains under the care of his pediatric cardiology team, including his primary cardiomyopathy cardiologist, electrophysiologist, and sports cardiologist. Device interrogations have not shown any sustained or nonsustained ventricular arrhythmias.

Discussion

HCM is an important cause of SCA in young athletes.1 Historically, competitive sports participation in individuals with HCM or ICDs was strictly contraindicated given concerns about ventricular arrhythmia, SCA, and potential for ICD malfunction during high-intensity exercise.2 However, emerging data have challenged these assumptions and prompted a paradigm shift toward evidence-based, individualized, and shared decision-making approaches.3

Evolving evidence on sports participation in HCM patients

Recent studies, including the ICD Sports Registry and LIVE-HCM study, show that carefully selected athletes with HCM and/or ICDs who return to competitive play do not have higher mortality or inappropriate ICD discharges compared with sedentary peers.3, 4, 5 In both registries, most adverse events occurred outside exercise, and ICDs functioned appropriately when required. These findings, alongside the recognized physical and psychosocial benefits of exercise and sport participation, have informed recommendations emphasizing individualized risk assessment, multidisciplinary evaluation, and shared decision-making.3,5 Athletes and families are counseled on potential ICD shocks, strategies to minimize risk, and implications for ongoing participation. Importantly, if arrhythmia or ICD shocks occur, medications, ICD settings, and exercise and sport participation would be re-evaluated.

Multidisciplinary shared decision-making is essential for sport participation

This case highlights the importance of multidisciplinary, family-centered shared decision-making in return-to-play decisions after cardiac arrest. As a high-level competitive basketball athlete, the patient and his family strongly valued maintaining his ability to compete, shaping the shared decision-making process. His care team included a cardiomyopathy cardiologist, electrophysiologist, sports cardiologist, team physician, and athletic trainers. The patient was initially evaluated by the pediatric cardiomyopathy specialist and electrophysiologist, and through shared decision-making conversations with those 2 providers, it was determined that the patient and his family wanted to return to competitive basketball, at which point the patient was referred to the pediatric exercise and sports cardiologist to discuss a specific return-to-play plan. The patient and family were counseled on the low risk of recurrent arrhythmia (<1%-2% per year) and potential defibrillator shocks, balanced against the psychological and social benefits of sport. Nonmedical stakeholders, including coaches, athletic trainers, and school administrators, were also engaged to ensure a safe athletic environment and optimize emergency preparedness. In general, EAPs should include a venue-specific (for both home and away scenarios) written plan with specified roles for on-site personnel, information and coordination plans for emergency medical services and first responders, locations of emergency equipment and confirmation that the equipment is readily accessible, and regular practice of the EAP to optimize response time and clarity of roles.6 While AEDs are less critical for patients with well-functioning ICDs, AEDs are an important part of the EAP, and discussions about EAPs for high-risk patients provide an important opportunity to improve SCA outcomes in the community.

Role of the exercise/sports cardiologist and individualized exercise testing

The exercise/sports cardiologist plays a key role in integrating diagnostic findings with the athlete's functional and competitive goals. Through exercise counseling and shared decision-making, they can develop an exercise plan that supports physical and psychosocial fitness while balancing risk.1,7 In this case, standard CPET was used to assess aerobic capacity, hemodynamic responses, and exertional arrhythmia risk, and a sport-specific CPET using an individualized treadmill protocol followed by an exercise test that simulated a single quarter of a basketball game was performed to replicate the physiological demands of the patient's sport and evaluate for exertional arrhythmias in a realistic context. Results from both tests informed the return-to-play plan codeveloped by the patient, his coaching staff, and his sports cardiologist.

Exercise testing protocols depend heavily on the clinical indication. In HCM, indications include distinguishing an athletic heart from evolving HCM, assessing mechanisms of exercise limitation, evaluating exertional symptoms, screening for arrhythmia or ischemia, optimizing ICD function, and informing exercise counseling and prescription. When the goal is arrhythmia screening or symptom evaluation in competitive athletes, it is essential that the protocol reproduce sport-specific activities. The commonly utilized Bruce protocol is often suboptimal owing to large workload increments and steep grades that fail to reflect typical sport demands, confound ventilatory threshold determination, and may lead to submaximal effort.8,9

In the current case, interpretation of peak VO2 was contextualized by the patient's recent SCA and period of restricted training. Peak VO2 was lower than expected for a competitive basketball athlete, consistent with patient's recent cardiac arrest and period of restricted exercise training. Serial testing demonstrated improvement in aerobic performance despite a lower peak heart rate on beta-blockade, supporting recovery of conditioning rather than intrinsic exercise limitation.

For basketball athletes, protocols that reproduce variable running speeds are ideal, requiring treadmills capable of high speeds and rapid acceleration and deceleration, features not available in most clinical settings. When only standard treadmills are available, alternative protocols such as Naughton, Balke, Astrand, or Astrand-Saltin may be used, and creative options such as alternating box jumps with interval sprints can further simulate basketball demands.8 Overall, individualized, sport-specific testing reflects current best practices in sports cardiology, providing nuanced, activity-relevant assessment that complements cardiac and exercise diagnostics.

Framework for return to play

A structured, graded return-to-play program can be safely implemented, promoting physical recovery, aerobic conditioning, confidence building, and community engagement after cardiac arrest and a period of deconditioning. For this patient, the return-to-play exercise prescription used the FITT (frequency, intensity, time, type) principle as well as a 5-zone exercise training framework derived from CPET data to guide gradual reintroduction to basketball-specific activity.10 This disciplined approach encourages functional rehabilitation and psychological readiness. Conclusions

This case highlights how evidence-based, multidisciplinary shared decision-making can facilitate safe return to play in motivated young athletes after SCA, a new diagnosis of HCM, and ICD placement. It highlights individualized, sport-specific exercise testing, exercise and sports cardiologists as important team members in developing return-to-play plans, and the potential utility of exercise prescriptions in HCM patients while underscoring the importance of balancing clinical risk, patient autonomy, and the psychosocial benefits of athletic participation.
