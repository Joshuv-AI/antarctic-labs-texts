Molecular characterization of rabbit hemorrhagic disease virus type 2 and long-lasting protection conferred by a VLP-based vaccine
Authors: Yanhui Wang, Peipei Qiao, Hongchao Wu, Xiaodan Tong, Tongyan Wang, Lu Wang, Yihang Yang, Xiaorui Su, Caihong Liu, Yalei Chen, Lulu Wang, Mengyue Wang, Xiangfeng Xi, Fang Wang, Wenqiang Pang, Yuxiu Liu, Kegong Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRabbit hemorrhagic disease virus (RHDV) type 2 (GI.2) is an emerging pathogen genetically distinct from classic RHDV (GI.1). It can infect young, wild, and even vaccinated rabbits, and may also contract RHD. This study investigated the prevalence of RHD in China. Furthermore, a virus-like particle (VLP) vaccine was developed based on the prevalent strains, and the vaccine’s protective efficacy against different strains as well as the duration of antibody persistence were evaluated.ResultsFrom 2020 to 2024, 205 clinical samples were collected from 16 cities in China. Among them, 86 samples tested positive for RHDV-2, while no classical RHDV-1 was detected. Furthermore, the VP60 gene of RHDV-2 was sequenced from 18 positive samples. These sequences showed greater than 95% nucleotide identity with the reference strain of RHDV-2 (GenBank accession number: MT383749). However, recombination analysis indicated a potential risk of recombination between RHDV-2 and RHDV-1. A recombinant baculovirus expressing the VP60 gene from the RHDV2 YC05 strain was constructed, and molecular characterization confirmed that the VP60 capsid protein was efficiently expressed in baculovirus-infected Sf9 cells. Hemagglutination activity reached a titer of 1:214 at 4 days post-infection, and the expressed protein self-assembled into virus-like particles (VLPs) in the infected Sf9 cells. Immunization with VLPs containing RHDV2-VP60 at an HA titer of 1:28 induced specific antibodies against RHDV2-VP60 in rabbits, with hemagglutination inhibition titers ensured at 1:25 or higher. All rabbits immunized with the VLP vaccine survived challenge with different clinical RHDV2 strains and remained fully protected for at least 180 days.ConclusionsOur results demonstrated that RHDV2 has become widely prevalent and is now the dominant pathogen in China. Recombinant RHDV-2 VP60 protein expressed in the baculovirus-insect system demonstrates robust immunogenicity and confers long-term protection, making them a promising vaccine candidate for preventing RHDV2 infection in rabbits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05374-2.Keywords: Rabbit hemorrhagic disease virus type 2, China, VP60, Virus-like particles, Immunogenicity

Background

Rabbit hemorrhagic disease (RHD), caused by RHD virus (RHDV), belongs to the family Caliciviridae and the genus Lagovirus. It is an acute and highly contagious infectious disease characterized by respiratory hemorrhage, hepatic necrosis, edema of parenchymal organs, and congestive hemorrhage, with a mortality rate exceeding 90% [1, 2]. The disease poses severe threats to the rabbit industry. In 1989, the World Organisation for Animal Health officially classified RHD as a list B disease, while China designated it as a category II notifiable infectious disease. The classic RHDV GI.1 strain primarily infects domestic rabbits older than 2 months, while wild rabbits exhibit partial resistance [3]. However, in 2010, a new variant, RHDV type 2 (GI.2), emerged in France [4]. This strain exhibits pronounced genetic divergence from classic RHDV, causing fatal infections in domestic rabbits even after vaccination with classic RHDV vaccines. Unlike GI.1, RHDV2 infects rabbits as young as 7–15 days and also demonstrates high pathogenicity in wild rabbits [5–7]. In 2020, China reported its first RHDV2 outbreak in Sichuan rabbit farms, causing massive mortality (> 70%) among domestic rabbits, and genetic sequencing confirmed the causative agent as RHDV GI.2 [8]. Subsequently, RHDV2 replaced classic RHDV as the predominant prevalent strain, causing substantial damage to the rabbit industry in China.

Due to the absence of susceptible cell lines for propagating RHDV, traditional RHD prevention relied primarily on tissue-derived inactivated vaccines. However, these vaccines cannot fully meet the demands of large-scale prevention and control due to the high production costs and susceptibility to inactivation interference. Moreover, they fail to offer effective protection against RHDV2 infection [9, 10]. Although genetically engineered vaccines have demonstrated strong immunogenicity, earlier versions primarily targeted classic RHDV strains and indicated limited cross-protection against RHDV2 [11]. Consequently, developing novel vaccines specifically designed to protect against RHDV2 has become an urgent priority. VP60 is the primary antigen of RHDV and is associated with its pathogenicity as well as the induction of immune responses after immunization. When expressed in a eukaryotic expression system, the VP60 protein can form virus-like particles similar to the natural viral capsid protein. These VLPs exhibit strong immunogenicity, making VP60 a leading candidate protein in the development of vaccines against RHD.

Therefore, we sequenced clinically prevalent RHDV2 strains and selected a representative strain that exhibited high genetic similarity to epidemic variants circulating in China and demonstrated strong pathogenicity in rabbits. Using this strain as a template, we successfully expressed the RHDV2-VP60 protein through the baculovirus expression system, and subsequently evaluated its immunogenicity in rabbits.

Results

Epidemiology and VP60 sequence analysis of classic RHDV and RHDV2

A total of 205 liver, spleen, or lung samples were collected from dead rabbits suspected of RHD across 16 cities in China between 2020 and 2024 (Table 1). Among these, classic RHDV was undetected (0/205), while RHDV2 was identified in 86 samples, yielding a prevalence rate of 41.95% (86/205) (Fig. 1B). These findings indicate that RHDV2 has become widely prevalent and is now the dominant pathogen in China.

We sequenced the VP60 gene of 18 clinical RHDV2-positive samples from different regions and seasons to further understand the genetic variation of RHDV2. Sequences alignment revealed that these isolates shared > 95% homology with the RHDV2 reference strain (GenBank accession number: MT383749), but only approximately 80% homology with the RHDV1 reference strain (GenBank accession number: FJ794180; Fig. 1D). Furthermore, phylogenetic tree analysis revealed that our isolates clustered with global RHDV2 strains, confirming their classification as genotype (Fig. 1C).

Recombination analysis using Simplot clarified the genetic relationships between clinical RHDV2 isolates and the classical RHDV1 and RHDV2 strains prevalent in China. Most clinical isolates exhibited high sequence similarity with the RHDV2 reference strain, with YC05 indicating the highest homology. Notably, the XJ01 strain displayed reduced similarity within the 250–450 nucleotide region, whereas the JY05 strain exhibited overall lower similarity to RHDV2, with further decline at the 3′ end of the VP60 gene. This region instead displayed increased similarity to RHDV1, suggesting recombination and a shift toward RHDV1-like sequences (Fig. 1E).

Pathogenicity analysis of RHDV2 clinical isolates in rabbits

To evaluate differences in pathogenicity among circulating RHDV2 strains, we conducted comparative virulence assays using the YC05 strain (highest similarity to the reference strain) and two strains with potential genetic recombination (XJ01 and JY05). Survival analysis demonstrated significantly higher mortality in all infected groups compared with the control group (Fig. 2A). Notably, YC05 and XJ01 infected groups exhibited 100% lethality, while the JY05 group exhibited 60% mortality. Body weight measurements further underscored strain-dependent pathogenicity (Fig. 2B). All infected rabbits experienced a progressive decline in body weight post-infection, while the control group maintained a steady increase. The most severe weight reduction was observed in the YC05 group. Gross pathological examination revealed pronounced lesions in all infected groups compared to uninfected controls, including organomegaly and congestion of major organs (Fig. 2C).

HE staining revealed non-significant pathological changes in control rabbits. In contrast, the challenged group exhibited marked pathological alterations across multiple organs. In the lungs, alveolar wall congestion, diffuse homogeneous pink-stained serous exudate, desquamated epithelial cells, and hemorrhage were observed. In the liver, dilated and congested hepatic sinusoids, hemosiderin deposits, and diffuse hepatocyte necrosis were evident. The spleen indicated reduced splenic nodule volumes, decreased lymphocyte counts, and extensive hemorrhage in the red pulp. In the kidneys, glomeruli were enlarged and filled with erythrocytes, diffuse necrosis of renal tubular epithelial cells was present, and diffuse hemorrhage was observed in the tubulointerstitial areas (Fig. 2D).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the YC05 isolate possesses the highest virulence among the tested strains and exhibits the closest homology to reference epidemic strains. These findings validate the selection of the RHDV2-VP60 sequence from YC05 as the template for virus-like particle (VLP) vaccine development.

Expression and characterization of RHDV2-VP60 protein in Sf9 cells

The expression strategy of RHDV2-VP60 is indicated in Fig. 3A. To express the RHDV2-VP60 in the baculovirus system, a recombinant donor plasmid, pFastBac-RHDV2-VP60, was initially constructed. Double-enzyme digestion confirmed the correct insertion of the target gene into the vector (Fig. 3B), indicating successful construction of the recombinant donor plasmid. Subsequently, the plasmid was transformed into the baculovirus host strain DH10Bac, and PCR analysis confirmed the expected recombinant bacmid fragment size (Fig. 3C), demonstrating successful integration of the RHDV2-VP60 gene into the baculoviral genome. The purified recombinant bacmid was transfected into Sf9 insect cells for baculoviral rescue. Distinct CPEs appeared 48–72 h post-transfection (Fig. 3D). To assess the expression kinetics and level of VP60 protein, cell supernatants were collected at various time points post-transfection. The HA titer peaked at 4 dpi (Fig. 4A). Concurrently, IFA and SDS-PAGE analyses demonstrated high-level expression of VP60 protein in infected Sf9 cells, peaking at day 4 dpi (Figs. 4B-C). Moreover, TEM analysis (Fig. 4D) revealed the presence of typical VLPs in infected cells, providing further evidence for the successful expression and assembly capability of the VP60 protein. In summary, RHDV2-VP60 was successfully expressed in the baculovirus system, with day 4 post-transfection identified as the optimal time point for protein production.

Immunogenicity evaluation of the VLP vaccine in rabbits

The immunogenicity of the VLP vaccine was next evaluated in rabbits. The vaccine demonstrated an acceptable safety profile during its administration to rabbits, with no observed effects throughout the observation period. Post-immunization, the antibody levels were significantly higher in the vaccinated group than the control group (p < 0.001; Fig. 5A). Following challenge with either the YC05 or XJ01 RHDV2 strain, all vaccinated rabbits survived, while mortality in the control groups reached 80% (YC05) and 100% (XJ01) (Fig. 5B). Additionally, the vaccinated rabbits maintained their body weight throughout the observation period, while the control group experienced a discernible decline (Fig. 5C). At necropsy, no pathological changes were observed in the liver or lung tissues of the immunized group. In contrast, the control group exhibited hepatomegaly and enlarged lungs with scattered hemorrhagic spots on the surface (Fig. 5D). HE staining demonstrated dilated and congested hepatic sinusoids in the liver, diffuse, homogeneous, pink-stained serous exudate within the alveolar spaces, and many desquamated alveolar epithelial cells in the lungs (Fig. 5E). These results collectively demonstrate the efficacy and safety of the VLP vaccine in protecting rabbits against RHDV2 infection.

Long-term antibody persistence and protective efficacy of the VLP vaccine against RHDV2 challenge in rabbits

The VLP vaccine induced a sustained humoral immune response, as evidenced by persistently high HI titers over 180 dpi (Fig. 6A). Notably, the vaccinated group consistently exhibited higher antibody levels than the control group. At 180 dpi, all vaccinated rabbits survived challenge with RHDV2; none of the control rabbits survived (Fig. 6B). These findings demonstrate that the VLP vaccine elicits durable immunity and provides long-term protection against RHDV2 infection in rabbits.

Discussion

RHD was first identified in China in 1984 as a highly fatal infectious disease affecting adult rabbits [12]. Currently, RHD has been reported in many countries, with increasingly complex epidemiological patterns. In 2010, a novel serotype (RHDV2) was identified in France, followed by a global epidemiological shift toward RHDV2 dominance [13]. China reported its first RHDV2 outbreak in 2020, which caused substantial losses to the domestic rabbit industry. Currently, RHDV2 (GI.2) remains the predominant pathogen in China, consistent with our epidemiological results (42.59% prevalence of RHDV2, no classic RHDV detected), and aligns with global reports on the replacement of the GI.2 genotype [14, 15]. Moreover, we analyzed the RHDV2-VP60 sequence of the prevalent RHDV2 strain in China from 2020 to 2024. In our research, the VP60 sequence of the currently prevalent RHDV2 strain in China indicated relatively low similarity to that of the classic RHDV strain, consistent with previous studies (< 85%) [16]. No recombination events were detected in the clinical RHDV2 VP60 sequences based on similarity and recombination analysis. Among the analyzed strains, the YC05 strain exhibited the highest similarity to indigenous Chinese RHDV2 strains, while XJ01 and JY05 strains indicated potential recombination trends. Further pathogenicity analysis revealed that YC05 and XJ01 strains demonstrated strong virulence in rabbits. Although RHDV2 and classic RHDV1 strains induce similar clinical manifestations, their genomic homology is < 85%, with significant antigenic divergence [10]. The distinct genetic and antigenic properties of RHDV2 prevent the RHDV1 vaccine from producing cross-protective immunity against RHDV2 infection. Notably, RHDV2 exhibits an expanded host range (wild rabbits and young domestic rabbits) and higher case fatality rates compared to classic RHDV [17, 18]. These findings underscore the urgent need for effective strategies to control and prevent the spread of RHDV2.

The prevention of classic RHDV was obtained by preparing tissue-inactivated vaccines through formaldehyde inactivation, which resulted in a satisfactory immune response. However, these conventional vaccines pose potential biosafety risks to both animals and the environment due to possible incomplete virus inactivation. As a member of the Caliciviridae family, the capsid protein VP60 of the RHDV remains the main structural component and primary protective antigen [19]. Employing baculovirus expression systems to produce capsid proteins within insect cells results in the spontaneous assembly of these proteins into VLPs. These VLPs are virtually identical to natural viruses regarding both their appearance and antigenic properties [20, 21]. These characteristics make VP60 a key focus for developing a genetic engineering subunit vaccine against RHD. Numerous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of recombinant baculovirus-expressed RHDV-VP60 vaccines, with documented protective immunity lasting approximately 7 months in vaccinated rabbits [22, 23]. Some researchers also prepared a bivalent inactivated vaccine by simultaneously expressing the VP60 proteins of RHDV and RHDV2 through recombinant baculovirus, which has indicated promising immunogenicity in rabbits [24, 30].

Unlike prior studies that predominantly used RHDV strains, we specifically selected the RHDV2 YC05 strain isolated from China, which is highly similar to the Sichuan strain isolated in China in 2020 and exhibits high pathogenicity in rabbits. The VP60 gene of the RHDV2 YC05 strain was expressed in the infected Sf9 cells and self-assembled into VLPs using the baculovirus expression system. Notably, it yielded exceptionally high levels of immunogenic VP60 protein, with HA titers reaching 1:214 at 4 dpi in Sf9 cells that persisted until 5 dpi, which indicates robust protein expression and efficient VLP assembly compared with the previous study [25]. To ensure consistent vaccine quality, the VLP vaccine was standardized to contain 28 HA units of effective antigen per dose. Following immunization, a robust and long-term humoral immune response was effectively induced. Rabbits vaccinated with the VLP vaccine generated specific antibodies against RHDV2-VP60, with HI titers maintained at 1:25 or higher. These titers were not only significantly higher than those in the control group but also surpassed the HI antibody levels previously reported in rabbits immunized with RHDV2-VP60 VLP vaccines from other Chinese native strains [26]. Notably, vaccination provided complete protection against infection with the RHDV2 YC05 strain in rabbits and protected them from infection by the XJ01 strain, which tends to recombine. These findings provide strong evidence of the protective efficacy of the vaccine. Furthermore, our research demonstrated that the protective immunity against RHDV2 induced by the VLP vaccine persisted for approximately 180 days post-vaccination. These findings further demonstrated that the VLP vaccine exhibits superior immunogenicity and long-term protective efficacy. Although a previous study also developed a vaccine using the Chinese RHDV2 strain (SC2020/0401) [27], our study provides the first evidence of long-term immunity (180 days) induced by a VLP vaccine derived from a local RHDV2-VP60 sequence. This addresses the urgent demand for vaccines with more durable efficacy and a reduced frequency of booster vaccinations.

Given that RHDV2 is an RNA virus with high potential for mutation and recombination, the emergence of new variants remains a significant concern. Since its first detection in 2010, RHDV2 has demonstrated considerable genetic plasticity, raising the possibility that further mutations or recombinant events could lead to novel strains with altered pathogenicity or antigenicity [28, 29]. Accordingly, continuous epidemiological surveillance is essential to monitor viral evolution and analyze and predict future trends in viral variation. Developing broadly protective vaccines remains an urgent priority to address the continuous emergence of novel RHDV2 variants. A promising strategy for multivalent vaccines incorporates tandem expression of key immunodominant regions from prevalent strains, which could elicit cross-neutralizing antibodies against both contemporary and future variants.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we successfully constructed a recombinant baculovirus vector containing the VP60 gene derived from a prevalent Chinese RHDV2 strain to limit the outbreak and spread of RHDV2. Molecular characterization confirmed efficient expression of the VP60 capsid protein in baculovirus-infected Sf9 cells, and it could self-assemble into VLPs in infected Sf9 cells. Subsequently, the VLP vaccine we developed effectively stimulated humoral immunity, maintained HI antibody titers at 1:25 or higher, and conferred immune protection against RHDV2 infection in rabbits for approximately 180 days.

Methods

Animals and care

A total of 50 8-week-old clean New Zealand white rabbits that were validated to be free of RHDV antibodies were obtained from Zhengzhou Tuerkang Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd (Animal Production License Number: SCXK 2021-0001).

Sample collection and identification

Tissue samples from 205 rabbits suspected of dying due to RHDV were collected from Zhengzhou, Kaifeng, Luoyang, Nanyang, Jiyuan, Jiaozuo, Dengfeng, Anyang, Puyang, Rizhao, Linyi, Taiyuan, Yulin, Sichuan, Xiangyang, and Xinjiang in China. All samples were analyzed for RHDV and screened using reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) for RHDV (GI.1) and RHDV2. The viral nucleotide samples were extracted following the manufacturer’s protocol (Geneaid Biotech Ltd., Taiwan, China). The primers for the RHDV1 RT-PCR test were as follows: Forward (5′-3′): GGT CAC TGC AGA RAA TTC ATC and reverse (5′-3′): TAC CAA AAC TGA RGC ACR TTG G, and for the RHDV2 RT-PCR test were as follows: Forward (5′-3′): CCC TGG AAG CAG TTC GTC AAA T and reverse (5′-3′): TCA GAC ATA AGA AAA RCC ATT. Reaction products underwent electrophoresis through 1.5% agarose gels, and the positive samples of RHDV1 indicated an 831 bp band, while the positive samples of RHDV2 indicated an 829 bp band (Fig. 1A).

Virus sequencing analysis

The viral nucleotide samples were reverse-transcribed to complementary DNA (cDNA) using the cDNA synthesis super mix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China, Cat No. AE301-02). The RHDV2 VP60 sequencing analysis was conducted as previously explained [30]. The RHDV2 VP60 gene similarity was analyzed using MegAlign software (DNASTAR Inc., Madison, WI, USA) and the ClustalW algorithm. The phylogenetic tree was constructed from the consistent nucleotide sequences using the neighbor-joining approach, and then subjected to 1,000 bootstrap replicates to evaluate the confidence level of each internal node. The tree was generated using the MEGA software (version 11.0). Simplot software (version 4.1) was used to detect the sequence similarity and homologous recombination [31].

Hemagglutination assay and hemagglutination inhibition assay

Hemagglutination (HA) assay was performed to detect the viral load of each tissue sample. A serial two-fold diluted tissue sample (50 µL) was added to an equal volume of 1% human type O erythrocytes, mixed thoroughly, and HA point titers were determined following incubation at 4 °C for 45 min. Meanwhile, positive (1% human type O erythrocytes) and negative controls (physiological saline, PBS) were included in the test samples. The hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay was based on previously described approaches [30].

Pathogenicity test of clinical isolates RHDV2 strains

Twenty rabbits were randomly divided into four groups, with five rabbits per in each group. Groups 1–3 were injected subcutaneously with 1 mL of the RHDV2 strains YC05, JY05, and XJ01 (each containing an HA titre of 211), respectively. Group 4 was injected with 1 mL of PBS using the same method. After the challenge, the rabbits were monitored daily for one week, and the mortality rate was recorded for each group. Weights were recorded either at the time of death or on day 7. All surviving rabbits in each group were humanely euthanized by intravenous injection of pentobarbital sodium. The lungs, livers, kidneys, and spleens of rabbits were collected 7 days after the RHDV2 challenge. All tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE staining) according to methods reported in a previous article [32].

Construction of recombinant vector and expression of recombinant baculovirus

The plasmid containing the RHDV2-VP60 gene of the YC05 strain was synthesized by GENEWIZ Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Suzhou, China) and cloned into the linearized pFastBacI vector after digestion with BamH I and Hind III (Invitrogen, USA, Cat No. FD0054, FD0504). The resulting plasmids were transformed into Trans-T1 competent cells (TransGen Biotech, China, Cat No. CT101-01) to generate the recombinant donor plasmid pFBI-RHDV2-VP60. Verification of recombinant plasmids was performed by BamH I and Hind III double digestion and DNA sequencing.

Subsequently, the recombinant donor plasmid pFBI-RHDV2-VP60 was transformed into DH10Bac Escherichia coli competent cells (Invitrogen, USA, Cat No. 10359016) to produce recombinant bacmids. The Sf9 cells were adjusted to 3 × 106 cells/mL, then the recombinant bacmids were transfected into Sf9 insect cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions of Cellfectin II transfection reagent (Invitrogen, USA, Cat No. 10359016) to promote the recombinant baculovirus, rAC-RHDV2-VP60, at the same time, a normal cell control was set up. The lesions were observed daily, and when the cytopathic effect (CPE) exceeded more than 80%, the supernatant was collected and employed as the P1 generation. Subsequent infections were performed by adding virus to cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1.

Expression and identification of recombinant proteins

Sf9 cells were infected with rAC-RHDV2-VP60 at an MOI of 1 and harvested at 2–5 days post-infection (dpi). Both extracellular supernatant and infected cells were collected, mixed, and subjected to one freeze-thaw cycle. The lysate was centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected for sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), HA assay, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of RHDV-VP60 protein. For SDS-PAGE, proteins were separated under reducing conditions using 12% SDS-PAGE and visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. For TEM, the supernatant was clarified using NaHCO3, and then stained following previously described methods [33].

For the indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA), infected Sf9 cells were fixed with 80% acetone for 20 min, washed with PBS, and incubated with an RHDV2 polyclonal antibody (laboratory-made) as the primary antibody. A fluorescein isothiocyanate-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen, USA, Cat No. 10359016 A-11008) was then applied. Samples were observed under a fluorescence inverted microscope (TS2-FL, Nikon).

Vaccine formulation and immunization in rabbits

The Sf9 cells were infected with recombinant baculovirus at an MOI of 1 for 72–96 h. Both extracellular supernatant and infected cells were collected, mixed, and subjected to one freeze-thaw cycle. The lysate was centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 10 min, and the clarified supernatant was collected for HA titer determination. The antigen preparation was then mixed with 10% aluminum hydroxide gel adjuvant to formulate the vaccine.

For immunogenicity evaluation, rabbits were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5 per group). Groups 1 and 3 received 1 mL of the vaccine subcutaneously (HA titer = 28), while groups 2 and 4 received an equal volume of PBS. Serum samples were collected on days 0 and 14 post-immunization. At 14 days post-immunization, groups 1–2 were challenged with the RHDV2 YC05 strain, while groups 3–4 were challenged with the RHDV2 XJ01 strain. Rabbits were monitored daily for one week to record mortality rates. Body weights were measured at the time of death or on day 7 post-challenge. All surviving rabbits were humanely euthanized at the study endpoint. As described above, on day 7 post-challenge, the livers and lungs were collected for pathological examination and HE staining.

For the antibody persistence study, rabbits were randomly divided into two groups (n = 5 per group). The immunized group received 1 mL of vaccine subcutaneously (HA titer = 28), while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. Serum samples were collected on days 0, 14, 28, 42, 56, 90, 120, 150, and 180 post-vaccination. On day 180 post-vaccination, all rabbits were infected with RHDV2 as described above and subsequently monitored.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance and independent-sample t-tests with GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0; GraphPad Software Corporation). A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
