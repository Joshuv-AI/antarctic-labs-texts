A single-cell atlas of intracranial aneurysm progression identifies a dysfunctional VSMC subpopulation associated with rupture risk
Authors: Xiangrong Chen, Bikun Liu, Xiaofeng Zhou, Zongtao Wu, Dongze Xu, Jingcheng Zhan, Jinliang Liu, Mao Ling, Fan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntracranial aneurysm (IA) rupture is a life-threatening event. While inflammation is implicated, the cellular mechanisms associated with progression from stable lesion to rupture remain poorly defined at single-cell resolution.MethodsWe performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on a mouse model spanning sham, formed, and ruptured IA stages. Computational analyses included trajectory inference, cell-cell communication analysis, and metabolic scoring. Key findings were validated using human IA bulk RNA-seq data and protein-level assays on matched clinical tissues.ResultsOur single-cell atlas revealed a progressive “inflammatory-lytic” microenvironment marked by immune cell infiltration and vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) depletion. We identified a metabolically specialized VSMC subpopulation (VSMC_0) that was selectively lost in formed and ruptured aneurysms. Pseudotime analysis indicated VSMC differentiation blockade, associated with downregulation of the structural genes actin alpha2, smooth muscle (ACTA2) and prolyl 4-hydroxylase subunit alpha2 (P4HA2), which correlated with irregular aneurysm shape a strong clinical predictor of rupture risk. Cell communication analysis positioned VSMC_0 as a signaling hub to macrophages, suggesting a feed-forward inflammatory loop. These findings were validated in human IAs tissues.ConclusionOur study identifies VSMC phenotypic dysregulation as a key correlate of IA instability, linking molecular deficits to clinical rupture predictors. This reframes IA as a disorder of vascular cellular identity and communication, revealing potential therapeutic targets for aneurysm stabilization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12868-026-01029-y.

Introduction

IAs are focal deformities frequently observed in cerebral artery branches, with an estimated prevalence of 5% to 10% in the general population [1]. More than 85% of human IAs occur in the anterior cerebral circulation, developing at arterial bifurcations along the circle of Willis [2]. IAs are typically stable but can rupture, with an annual incidence of approximately 1.2% per patient [3]. Subarachnoid hemorrhage resulting from a ruptured aneurysm causes serious complications and is often life-threatening [4, 5]. IAs are uncommon in children, representing only 0.5–4.6% of all aneurysms, and their prevalence increases with age [6]. In populations over 30 years of age, the prevalence of harboring an IAs ranges from 3.6% to 6.5% [1]. About 30% of IAs patients die from Subarachnoid hemorrhage and up to 20% of survivors experience permanent disability. While surgical clipping and endovascular techniques, such as coiling and stent-assisted coiling, remain the primary treatments for IAs, their invasive nature carries a risk of serious complications. A clearer understanding of the mechanisms driving aneurysm formation, progression, and rupture could reveal targets for safer, non-invasive therapeutic strategies. Although animal models are widely used to study aneurysm pathogenesis, the resulting lesions are induced rather than spontaneous [7–9]. Furthermore, obtaining sufficient tissue from human IAs for biochemical analysis is challenging, which has limited a complete definition of their specific pathogenic mechanisms. Pervious study [10] have demonstrated the primary histopathological features of IAs encompass immune infiltration, cell death, oxidative stress, lipid metabolism, iron accumulation, and proteolytic activity [11, 12]. Research into IAs pathogenesis has consistently identified the participation of diverse immune cell populations. Sajjad et al. [13] summarized the impact of macrophage-driven inflammation on the aneurysm wall and reviewed pharmacological approaches to modulate the macrophage response during internal carotid aneurysm formation and rupture. Hashimoto et al. [14], Chalouhi et al. [15], and Signorelli et al. [16] have demonstrated that inflammatory cell infiltration leads to VSMC apoptosis and migration, and that extracellular matrix (ECM) protein degradation subsequently promotes the progression and rupture of IAs.

While the link between inflammation and VSMC dysfunction is well-established, critical cellular and molecular mechanisms remain unclear. The precise compositional shifts and phenotypic states of VSMC during aneurysm formation versus rupture are still poorly defined. It remains unknown whether VSMC loss occurs uniformly or results from the selective depletion of specific functional subpopulations. The reciprocal signaling between infiltrating immune cells and heterogeneous VSMCs that drives pathogenesis is also not fully understood. Resolving these questions demands a high resolution, single-cell approach to deconvolute the aneurysm wall’s complex cellular ecosystem.

The scRNA-seq dataset GSE193533, generated by Martinez and colleagues using an elastase-induced mouse cerebral aneurysm model, has provided initial insights into the cellular dynamics of aneurysm formation [17]. Their work documented overall shifts in immune cell infiltration and VSMC loss, and identified dimethyl fumarate (DMF)-induced transcriptomic changes in VSMCs. However, the original analysis did not specifically focus on VSMC heterogeneity, metabolic reprogramming, or the cell-cell communication networks linking VSMC subpopulations to inflammatory cells during the progression from aneurysm formation to rupture. Furthermore, whether the molecular signatures identified in mice are conserved in human aneurysms and correlate with clinical rupture predictors remains unexplored.

Therefore, a systematic single-cell atlas that captures the full transition from aneurysm formation to rupture, integrates a focused analysis of VSMC heterogeneity with immune microenvironment crosstalk, and links these findings to human clinical morphology, is nevertheless lacking. Such a resource is crucial for identifying key cellular players, precise signaling pathways, and dysfunctional cellular states that may serve as therapeutic targets for stabilizing aneurysms.

To address these gaps, we performed a multi-stage re-analysis and extended integration of the GSE193533 scRNA-seq dataset across sham, formed, and ruptured stages, and supplemented this with human bulk transcriptomics (GSE75436), protein validation in matched clinical tissues, and a retrospective clinical cohort analysis (229 IAs) to establish cross-species and clinical relevance. This study aims to map the evolving cellular landscape during aneurysm progression and to delineate VSMC heterogeneity, identifying subpopulations vulnerable to depletion. We also seek to decipher specific cell-cell communication networks, particularly between VSMCs and inflammatory cells, and to validate key mechanistic insights in human aneurysm tissues. By integrating murine scRNA-seq with human bulk transcriptomics and clinical data, we provide a multidimensional understanding of IAs pathogenesis that reveals the cellular basis of destabilization and highlights novel therapeutic avenues.

Results

Clinical predictors of aneurysm rupture

To establish clinical relevance at the outset, we first examined whether molecular deficits identified in our mechanistic studies correspond to observable imaging predictors of rupture. We therefore analyzed a retrospective cohort of 229 aneurysms (91 ruptured, 138 unruptured) to identify which imaging parameters best discriminate ruptured from unruptured aneurysms. While our study cannot directly link molecular deficits to specific imaging features at the individual patient level, this analysis serves to establish the clinical relevance of our molecular findings by identifying morphological predictors that may reflect underlying wall vulnerability.

Single-cell atlas reveals inflammatory cell infiltration and VSMC loss

IAs were induced in adult male mice using an elastase model. scRNA-seq was then performed on circle of Willis samples. As detailed in the original study by Martinez et al. [17], cerebral blood vessels from the circle of Willis were harvested, and the connective and arachnoid tissues were removed. The circle of Willis tissues were pooled from five mice per group (Sham, Formed, and Ruptured) and digested into single-cell suspensions for scRNA-seq library preparation. The resulting sequencing data were deposited as one sample per condition, representing the pooled cellular material from five biological replicates. We analyzed scRNA-seq data from GSE193533 (Sham, Formed, Ruptured). After quality control, 14,809 cells were retained. Doublets and low-quality cells were removed, yielding 10,710 cells (Fig. 2A–C). Subcluster 0 highly expressed Ccn1, Prlr, Ogn, and Ccn3 (Fig. 3D). GO enrichment analysis showed enrichment of oxidative metabolic pathways including “energy derivation by oxidation of organic compounds” and “electron transport chain activity” (Fig. 3E). Subcluster 1 was enriched in “wound healing” and “extracellular matrix organization.” Specific markers of VSMC_1 included Fn1, Sparc, Thbs1, Col1a1, and Col3a1, suggesting a reparative or matrix-remodeling phenotype. Subcluster 2 was enriched in “negative regulation of phosphorus metabolic process” and “negative regulation of phosphate metabolic process.” Markers such as Pde5a and Prkg1 were enriched in this cluster, indicating potential involvement in contractile signaling modulation. Subcluster 3 was enriched in “chromosome segregation” and “mitotic sister chromatid segregation”. This cluster expressed proliferation-associated genes including Cdk1, Ccnb1, and Top2a, consistent with a proliferative/progenitor-like state. AUCell analysis demonstrated that subcluster 0 had higher Oxidative Phosphorylation (OxPhos) and Fatty Acid Oxidation (FAO) scores than subclusters 1–3 (Fig. 3F), indicating a greater reliance on oxidative metabolism in this subcluster relative to others. The top 50 marker genes for each VSMC subcluster are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Protein and transcriptional validation in clinical specimens

To experimentally validate our bioinformatic predictions, we performed Western blot analysis on matched pairs of human IAs tissue and CT samples. Western blot analysis confirmed that protein levels of both α-SMA (encoded by the ACTA2 gene) and P4HA2 were significantly reduced in IAs compared to CT (Fig. 7A). This finding was corroborated at the transcriptional level by qPCR, which demonstrated significant downregulation of MIR145, ACTA2, and P4HA2 mRNA in IAs (Fig. 7B). The consistent reduction of these markers across protein and RNA levels provides strong evidence for a substantial impairment of the contractile and matrix-synthetic functions of VSMC in human IAs. The concerted downregulation of MIR145, ACTA2, and P4HA2 represents a core molecular signature of VSMC pathology in IAs. This signature translates to a tangible loss of contractile proteins and collagen-modifying enzymes in the aneurysm wall. Discussion

This study provides a high-resolution, multi-modal dissection of the cellular and molecular dynamics underlying IAs progression. Our core findings illuminate a self-reinforcing pathological cycle centered on the phenotypic dysregulation of VSMC. By integrating murine single-cell atlases, trajectory inference, cell-cell communication networks, and validation in human tissues, we delineate a dynamic cascade from homeostatic imbalance and inflammatory amplification to ultimate structural failure. Our analysis extends prior scRNA-seq studies in IAs, notably the work of Martinez et al. [17], who generated the GSE193533 dataset and focused on DMF effects on VSMC mitochondria.

Firstly, our single-cell atlas confirms profound cellular remodeling within the IAs wall, characterized by progressive depletion of structural VSMC alongside massive infiltration of innate immune cells, notably macrophages and neutrophils. This “inflammatory-lytic” microenvironment aligns with established histopathological descriptions of IAs [18, 19]. However, our study advances beyond this macroscopic view by revealing, at single-cell resolution, that VSMC are not a homogeneous population. We identified four functionally distinct subclusters, among which the metabolically specialized VSMC _0 subcluster was selectively and severely diminished at the rupture stage. This subcluster, characterized by high OxPhos and FAO activity, likely represents a terminally differentiated, functionally competent VSMC state essential for maintaining vascular wall integrity. Its loss is not merely a quantitative decrease but a qualitative failure of the vascular wall to sustain its most crucial structural workforce [20]. Of note, VSMC_1 and VSMC_3 showed increased proportions in formed aneurysms, but pseudotime analysis indicated that these cells were arrested in early differentiation states and failed to progress into the mature VSMC_0 phenotype. Thus, while these clusters may represent reactive proliferation and attempted matrix repair, the selective loss of the terminally differentiated, metabolically specialized VSMC_0 subpopulation is the critical defect driving wall destabilization.

The trajectory and CytoTRACE analyses mechanistically explain this loss by revealing a blockade in VSMC differentiation during IAs progression. VSMCs in diseased samples were arrested in earlier, less differentiated pseudotime states, failing to progress into the mature state housing the VSMC _0 subcluster. This differentiation blockade is intrinsically linked to the downregulation of key contractile (ACTA2) and matrix-stabilizing (P4HA2) genes, a signature conserved from our murine model to human IAs bulk RNA-seq and protein validation. The suppression of MIR145, a master regulator of the VSMC contractile phenotype, further underscores this dysregulated differentiation program [21–24]. Therefore, the aneurysm wall suffers from an inability to generate or maintain the mature, functional VSMC necessary for structural resilience.

Crucially, our cell-cell communication analysis positions the vulnerable VSMC _0 subcluster not as a passive victim but as an active signaling hub within the vascular microenvironment. The specific, strong outgoing signals from VSMC to macrophages via the “Thbs1/Fn1-Sdc4” axis suggest a role in modulating immune cell behavior, potentially for containment or resolution. Conversely, robust incoming signals from macrophages (via Spp1) and neutrophils create a feed-forward inflammatory loop that may directly promote VSMC dysfunction and death [25]. The depletion of the VSMC _0 hub could thus disrupt this delicate communication balance, unleashing uncontrolled inflammation that exacerbates wall damage a concept supported by studies on vascular inflammation in other contexts [26].

The “Thbs1/Fn1-Sdc4” signaling axis: a potential driver of VSMC-macrophage crosstalk. Our cell-cell communication analysis identified “Thbs1/Fn1-Sdc4” as prominent ligand-receptor pairs between the vulnerable VSMC_0 subcluster and macrophages. Thbs1 is a matricellular protein that regulates cell-matrix interactions and inflammation. Its role in vascular pathology is well-established, as Thbs1 promotes macrophage recruitment and pro-inflammatory polarization in atherosclerosis [27], and its upregulation has been observed in experimental aneurysm models [28]. Fn1, an ECM glycoprotein, modulates VSMC adhesion, migration, and phenotype switching, with elevated expression reported in human IA tissues [29]. Sdc4, a transmembrane heparan sulfate proteoglycan, acts as a co-receptor for both Thbs1 and Fn1 and mediates mechanotransduction and inflammatory signaling in VSMCs [30]. The convergence of these signaling molecules in our analysis points to a coordinated “Thbs1/Fn1-Sdc4” axis that may facilitate VSMC-macrophage crosstalk. While its functional consequences in IA remain unknown, in other vascular contexts, Thbs1-Sdc4 signaling promotes VSMC apoptosis and matrix degradation [31]. This raises the possibility that the “Thbs1/Fn1-Sdc4” axis directly contributes to the selective depletion of the VSMC_0 subpopulation.

Ideally, Our integrated model posits that IA progression is driven by a vicious cycle of “differentiation blockade, inflammatory amplification, and structural decay.” This framework has significant translational implications. First, it provides a mechanistic link between our molecular findings and key clinical observations: the downregulation of structural genes like ACTA2 and P4HA2 validated in human tissues likely constitutes a core molecular event driving the “structural decay” arm of this cycle. This molecular deficiency in VSMC contractility and matrix stabilization may directly underlie the biomechanical weakening that manifests clinically as aneurysm wall irregularity, a potent morphological predictor of rupture identified in our clinical cohort. Several limitations warrant consideration. The mouse scRNA-seq data were derived from pooled samples (n = 5 mice per group), which precludes assessment of inter-individual variability. However, this pooling strategy is standard for cerebral aneurysm scRNA-seq studies due to the limited tissue yield from individual mouse circle of Willis. The use of a murine elastase-induced model, while valuable for studying rupture, involves an acute inflammatory insult that may not fully replicate the chronic, hemodynamically driven nature of human IAs [32]. Spatial validation of the predicted cellular interactions via multiplex imaging or spatial transcriptomics in human aneurysm tissues is a critical next step. Furthermore, the exact upstream triggers initiating the VSMC differentiation blockade and the precise functional role of VSMC _0 specific markers like Ccn1 require further investigation.

Additionally, the retrospective clinical cohort lacked histological tissue samples, precluding direct correlation between imaging features and cellular pathology. Future prospective studies with tissue collection will be needed to directly link morphological predictors to underlying histological changes.

Due to the scarcity of human IA tissue specimens, we were unable to perform immunohistochemical staining to localize these markers within the aneurysm wall. Future studies with larger tissue cohorts will be needed to validate the spatial distribution of VSMC marker expression.

Conclusion

In summary, our integrated multi-omics approach spanning murine single-cell transcriptomics, human tissue validation, and clinical cohort analysis redefines the pathogenesis of IAs progression. We identify VSMC phenotypic dysregulation, characterized by blocked differentiation and the selective loss of a metabolically specialized subpopulation VSMC_0, as a central mechanism of vascular wall destabilization. This cellular defect is linked to the downregulation of key structural genes and operates within a self-reinforcing cycle of inflammatory signaling and matrix decay. These molecular alterations provide a plausible biological basis for clinically observed high-risk morphological features, particularly aneurysm irregularity. Collectively, these findings shift the pathogenic paradigm of IAs from a primarily inflammatory condition toward a disorder of vascular cellular identity, communication, and metabolic fitness. The specific pathways, cell states, and ligand-receptor axes we uncovered deepen the understanding of aneurysm biology and chart a new roadmap for therapeutic development. Future strategies may therefore aim to preserve VSMC differentiation, restore metabolic homeostasis, or disrupt specific pathological cell-cell dialogues to fortify the vulnerable aneurysm wall and prevent rupture.

Methods

Data source

The scRNA-seq data from GSE193533 and bulk-seq data from GSE75436 were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE193533 comprises one Sham, one Formed, and one Ruptured sample. As described in the original study by Martinez and colleagues, cerebral blood vessels from the circle of Willis were harvested from adult male mice using an elastase-induced aneurysm model. The circle of Willis tissues were pooled from five mice per group (Sham, Formed, and Ruptured) and digested into single-cell suspensions for scRNA-seq library preparation. The resulting sequencing data were deposited as one sample per condition, representing the pooled cellular material from five biological replicates. GSE75436 contains 15 IAs and 15 CTs.

scRNA-seq data processing

Unsupervised clustering was performed using the Seurat R package (version 4.4.2) [33]. Low-quality cells and likely multiplets were removed by filtering out those with fewer than 200 genes. Cells exhibiting a haemoglobin gene ratio greater than 3% or a mitochondrial gene ratio exceeding 10% were also excluded. Potential doublets were identified with the scDblFinder package (version 1.20.2).

Gene expression counts were normalized using the NormalizeData’ function in Seurat, which applies the LogNormalize method to scale each cell’s expression by a factor of 10,000 followed by log transformation. The ‘FindVariableGenes’ function (mean.function = FastExpMean, dispersion.function = FastLogVMR) was employed to select the most variable genes. Batch effects in the sequencing data were removed by applying the mutual nearest neighbors method described by Haghverdi [34].

Cell clustering was conducted via graph-based methods using the ‘FindClusters’ function in Seurat. A two-dimensional uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was generated for visualization with the ‘RunUMAP’ function. Cluster-specific marker genes were detected by pairwise comparison of clusters using Seurat’s ‘FindAllMarkers’function (test.use = presto, logfc.threshold = 0.25, min.pct = 0.25). Differential expression between two populations was assessed with the ‘FindMarkers’ function (test.use = presto, group.by = contrasts, min.pct = 0), defining genes with a p value < 0.05 and |log2foldchange| >0.5 as significantly differentially expressed. Enrichment of these gene sets in Gene Ontology or KEGG pathway terms was evaluated using a hypergeometric test in R.

CellChat analysis

The CellChat R package (version 1.6.1) was employed to analyze intercellular communication [35]. A CellChat object was created from the normalized expression matrix using the ‘createCellChat’ function. Data preprocessing was performed with the ‘identifyOverExpressedGenes’, ‘identifyOverExpressedInteractions’, and ‘projectData’ functions under default parameters. Potential ligand-receptor interactions were then assessed using ‘computeCommunProb’, ‘filterCommunication (with min.cells = 10), and ‘computeCommunProbPathway’. The cell communication network was subsequently aggregated with the ‘aggregateNet’ function.

Metabolic scoring analysis

The AUCell package (version 1.28.0) was employed to compute activity scores for key metabolic pathways at the single-cell level. Specifically, gene sets representative of Fatty Acid Oxidation (FAO) and Oxidative Phosphorylation (OxPhos) were utilized. AUCell calculates the Area Under the Curve (AUC) for the recovery curve of each gene set within the ranked expression profile of each individual cell, providing a robust and normalized score that reflects the relative activity of the predefined gene set in each cell.

CytoTRACE analysis

To infer the differentiation state and developmental potential of individual VSMC, we applied CytoTRACE (version 0.3.3), a computational framework that predicts cellular differentiation status based on the dispersion of gene expression. CytoTRACE operates on the principle that less differentiated cells express a more diverse transcriptome, while terminally differentiated cells exhibit more restricted gene expression profiles. Cells were colored by their CytoTRACE scores on UMAP and Boxplot embeddings to visualize the distribution of differentiation potential across VSMC subclusters.

Pseudotime analysis

The Monocle2 package (version 2.9.0) was employed to calculate developmental pseudotime [36]. First, the raw count matrix from Seurat was converted to a CellDataSet object using Monocle’s ‘importCDS’ function. Ordering genes, defined as those with a q-value < 0.01, were then identified via the ‘differentialGeneTest` function to inform the placement of cells along the pseudotemporal trajectory. Dimensional reduction and clustering were performed using the ‘reduceDimension’ function with default parameters, after which cell ordering was inferred with the ‘orderCells’ function. Finally, gene expression dynamics across pseudotime were visualized using the ‘plot_genes_in_pseudotime’ function.

GO/KEGG enrichment analysis

The highly correlated genes were analyzed for functional enrichment using Gene Ontology (GO), Disease Ontology (DO), and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). This analysis was performed with the “GOplot”, “ClusterProfiler”, and “DOSE” packages in R.

Patients and samples

A total of 5 patients diagnosed with IAs were recruited for this study, and complete vascular wall tissue samples were collected from the IAs patients who underwent microsurgical clamping. 5 segments of the control were obtained during surgery for traumatic intracranial hemorrhage, The tissue samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and promptly transported to the laboratory for subsequent experiments.

Clinical cohort and imaging data collection

We retrospectively analyzed data from patients with IAs treated at the Department of Neurosurgery, Ankang Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Shaanxi Province, China, between January 2019 and December 2024. The study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee. Patients with saccular aneurysms confirmed by digital subtraction angiography were included, while those with previous treatment, incomplete data, or associated vascular malformations were excluded. A total of 229 IAs (91 ruptured, 138 unruptured) were analyzed. Clinical data (age, sex, hypertension, smoking) and imaging parameters (size, aspect ratio, bottleneck factor, shape regularity, inflow angle, branching angle) were extracted. Measurements were performed independently by two neurointerventionalists.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples with Trizol reagent (Carlsbad, CA), and its concentration was determined by ultraviolet spectrophotometry. Reverse transcription was performed using the Superscript RT-PCR First Strand Synthesis System Kit (Invitrogen) with random hexamers. We conducted real-time PCR of the resulting cDNA on an Mx-3000P quantitative PCR system (Stratagene). The primer sequences were as follows: for MIR145, forward TGCTGAGAACGAGCAGTAA, reverse CGAGGTCCGAAGACTCATTT; for ACTA2, forward CAGCCAAGCACTGTCAGGAAT and reverse CCATCACCCCCTGATGTCTG; for P4HA2, forward GGCCTGGTTTGGTGTCCTG and reverse GCCCAGCTCTTAATCTTGGAAAG, for GAPDH, forward CCCATCACCATCTTCCAGGA and reverse CTTCTCCATGGTGGTGAAGAC. Data were visualized using GraphPad Prism 5 software.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from snap-frozen human IAs and matched control control (CT) tissues (n = 5 pairs) using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ACTA2 (Abcam, 1:1000), P4HA2 (Abcam, 1:1000), and GAPDH (Abcam, 1:5000). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were used for detection with enhanced chemiluminescence. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to GAPDH. Statistical analysis was performed using paired Student’s t-test.

Statistical analysis

Differences in the data were analyzed using the unpaired t-test and the Wilcoxon rank sum test. The Pearson correlation test assessed the correlation of tissue expression levels. All statistical p-values were derived from two-tailed tests, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2.
