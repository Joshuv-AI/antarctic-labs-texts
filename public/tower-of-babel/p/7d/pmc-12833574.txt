Cancer cachexia-related monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells impair T-cell negative selection and predict immune-related adverse events
Authors: Li Wei, Ya-Qin Sun, Jian-Hua Ren, Ze-Xuan Huang, Yuan Zhang, Xiu-Qing Pang, Xiao-Tong Lv, Xiang-Yuan Wu, Yan-Fang Xing, Xing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsCancer cachexia is prevalent in various cancers and is associated with chemotherapy toxicity. However, limited data exist on the relationship between cachexia and immune-related adverse events (irAEs). The aim of this study is to explore the correlation between cancer cachexia and irAEs and its possible mechanism.MethodsA murine model of orthotopic hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) with cachexia was developed to evaluate the impact of T-cell infiltration into multiple tumor-free organs on the occurrence of irAEs. Single-cell RNA sequencing of thymic stromal cells was performed. Additionally, patients with advanced cancers receiving anti-programmed cell death protein 1/ligand 1 (PD-1/L1) antibody treatment were followed to investigate the relationship between cachexia and irAEs.ResultsInflammatory cell infiltration was observed in multiple tumor-free organs of cachexic HCC mice but not in non-cachexic controls. Immunofluorescence confirmed that the infiltrating cells included CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Morphological assessment and hematoxylin–eosin staining revealed thymic atrophy in cachexic HCC mice. Single-cell RNA sequencing of thymic stromal cells showed a reduction in medullary thymic epithelial cells (mTECs) II and III in cachexic mice. Autoimmune regulator (Aire) downregulation was accompanied by decreased expression of tissue-restricted antigens in mTECs. T cells from cachexic HCC mice induced organ-specific inflammation and T-cell infiltration in multiple organs of tumor-free mice. Following anti-mouse PD-1 antibody treatment, the incidence of inflammation in multiple organs markedly increased in cachexic HCC mice and in tumor-free mice that had received T cells from the cachexic HCC mice. Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence analyses revealed enrichment of thymic monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) in cachexic HCC mice. M-MDSCs infiltrated the thymus in cachexic mice with cancer, and they induced apoptosis of mTECs from tumor-free mice in vitro via nitric oxide production. Transfer of M-MDSCs led to inflammatory cell infiltration in multiple organs and thymic involution in tumor-free mice without affecting body weight. Sixty-four patients with advanced cancer receiving anti-PD-1/L1 therapy were enrolled. Patients who developed irAEs had higher levels of circulating M-MDSCs than those who did not. Moreover, patients with cachexia (body mass index (BMI) < 20 kg/m2 or ≥5% weight loss over the past 6 months) had elevated M-MDSC levels. Patients with both high M-MDSC levels and low BMI or weight loss ≥5% experienced more irAEs (hazard ratio: 2.333; 95% confidence interval: 1.231–4.423).ConclusionsM-MDSCs in cachexic mice induced mTEC apoptosis through nitric oxide production, impairing T-cell negative selection and promoting autoimmune T-cell infiltration into tumor-free organs. Cancer cachexia-related M-MDSCs may serve as predictive biomarkers for irAEs in patients with advanced cancer.

Introduction

Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including antibodies targeting programmed cell death protein 1/ligand 1 (PD-1/L1), effectively boost the immune system,1 they may also cause immune-related adverse events (irAEs).2 The occurrence, affected organs, and severity of irAEs are difficult to predict, as they can arise in any organ system at any point during the treatment cycle and may involve multiple organs simultaneously.3 Because irAEs can be life-threatening in some patients,4 the ability to predict them before onset is clinically important. However, reliable predictive biomarkers and risk factors for irAEs have not yet been established.5,6 Activated T-cell infiltration of tissues is a hallmark of irAEs,7,8 but it remains unclear whether this is a prerequisite for irAEs development or a downstream consequence.9

Cachexia occurs in up to 70% of patients with cancer and contributes to at least 20% of cancer-related deaths.10,11 Patients with pancreatic, gastroesophageal, liver, and bile duct cancers are at higher risk of developing cachexia.12 In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), cachexia is prevalent across all stages, occurring in 23.7% of patients at diagnosis and in 19.0%, 23.5%, 34.7%, and 34.0% of patients with Barcelona clinic liver cancer stage 0/A, B, C, and D, respectively.13,14 Pretreatment cachexia has been associated with chemotherapy toxicity in several cancer types, including esophageal, gastrointestinal, pancreatic, breast, and lung cancers.15 However, the relationship between cancer cachexia and irAEs has not been previously investigated. In cachexic cancer mouse models, the thymus has been shown to exhibit significant weight loss and atrophy.16

We hypothesized that thymic atrophy leads to a reduction in both the number and proportion of medullary thymic epithelial cells (mTECs), which in turn express lower levels of tissue-restricted antigens (TRAs). This may result in the infiltration of activated T cells into multiple organs, potentially contributing to the development of irAEs. In this study, we investigated the role of activated T-cell infiltration in the occurrence of irAEs in tumor-free organs and examined the causal relationship between thymic atrophy and cachexia in cancer-bearing mice. We also explored the underlying mechanisms and potential clinical implications.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (No. RG2023-034-01) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. All animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines and were performed in accordance with the National Research Council's Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Due to limited animal experimental facilities in our institution, the research team conducted careful investigations and decided to carry out the animal experiments at Ruiye model animal (Guangzhou) Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China), which possesses the qualifications for animal breeding. Therefore, animal protocols were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Ruiye model animal (Guangzhou) Biotechnology Co., Ltd (No. RYEth20220413-1).

Cell lines

The murine hepatoma cell lines Hepa1-6 (#CL-0105, RRID: CVCL_0327) and B16 (#CL-0029, RRID: CVCL_F936) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Hepa1-6 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and B16 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cell lines were authenticated by surface antigen profiling and routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Orthotopic HCC model in mice

Male C57BL/6J and C57L/J mice (6 weeks old, weighing 20–22 g) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, China) and housed under pathogen-free conditions. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental or control groups. Hepa1-6 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested and prepared as a mixture containing 1 × 106 cells in 10 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and 10 μL of Matrigel (BD BioCoat, Corning, NY, USA).

The mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 160 μL of 1% pentobarbital (Chenxue Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) per 25 g of body weight. Once a mouse exhibited slow, shallow breathing and no response to pain, it was secured to the lid of a 100-mm Petri dish (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and the surgical site was sterilized. A sagittal incision approximately 1 cm in length was made in the mid-abdomen using ophthalmic scissors (Zhongke Life Science Research Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). The median liver lobe was gently exteriorized using a cotton swab moistened with normal saline and placed on the outer surface of the peritoneum. Then, 20 μL of the Hepa1-6 cell suspension was slowly injected under the capsule into the left lobe of the liver using a 0.5 mL insulin syringe (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA). After injection, the needle was withdrawn, and pressure was applied with a cotton swab for 1 min to control bleeding. The liver was then returned to the abdominal cavity, and both the peritoneum and skin were closed with intermittent 5-0 sutures. The mice were placed in a lateral recumbent position on a thermal blanket (Zhongke Life Technology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) until recovery from anesthesia. Control group mice underwent the same surgical procedure but were injected with 10 μL of PBS and 10 μL of the Matrigel (BD BioCoat) mixture.

After the operation, both the experimental and control groups were fed normally, and body weights were recorded every 2–3 days. Based on changes in body weight, tumor-bearing mice were classified into two groups: those with <5% weight loss (HCC without cachexia; n = 6) and those with 5%–10% weight loss (HCC with cachexia; n = 6) compared with tumor-free mice (sham-operated group; n = 6).

Murine subcutaneous tumor models

Routinely cultured Hepa1-6 cells and B16 cells at the logarithmic growth stage were digested and prepared as a cell suspension containing 1 × 107 cells/mL PBS. The middle and posterior armpits were selected as the injection points and disinfected with 75% ethanol (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Before inoculation, the cell suspension was mixed well with a 200 μL pipette; 100 μL of the cell suspension was injected subcutaneously. Post-inoculation, the mice were fed normally, and the longest tumor was measured with a vernier caliper every 3–4 days. The shortest diameter line (mm), tumor volume (mm3), and mouse weight (g) were recorded.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and data analysis

Fresh CD45− thymic cells were isolated using the EasySep™ Mouse CD45 Positive Selection Kit (Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). scRNA-seq was performed using the Chromium Single Cell platform (10 × Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Cell viability was assessed using trypan blue exclusion and dual-fluorescence staining with acridine orange and propidium iodide.

Cell Ranger software (version 6.0.1; 10 × Genomics) was used for demultiplexing, genome alignment (GRCh38), barcode counting, and unique molecular identifier counting of the raw sequencing data. Seurat (version 4.0.0) was employed for quality control, normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and visualization of the raw count matrix. A total of 6531 single cells from six tumor-free mice and 7046 cells from six cachexic HCC mice were included in the final analysis.

Principal component analysis was performed to reduce feature dimensionality using the RunPCA function. The first 20 principal components, with a resolution parameter of 0.6, were used to identify clusters via the FindClusters function. Dimensionality reduction was then visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection with the RunUMAP function. Cytokine expression matrices were extracted for differential analysis between normal and tumor samples using the ggpubr package in R. Gene sets related to cell death pathways were obtained from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), MSigDB, and FerrDb V2 databases and analyzed using the clusterProfiler, msigdbr, and ggpubr packages in R, respectively.

Study participants

Patients with advanced cancer who received anti-PD-1/L1 antibody (Supplemental Table 1) treatment at The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between January 1 and December 31, 2022, were included in this study (Supplemental Table 1). Additional inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) age ≥18 years and <80 years; (ii) pathological confirmation of an advanced malignant tumor; and (iii) clinical indication for anti-PD-1/L1 antibody treatment.

Exclusion criteria were (i) pregnancy or lactation; (ii) mental illness; and (iii) inability to provide consent or adhere to treatment. Prior to initiating anti-PD-1/L1 antibody therapy, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from patient blood samples for flow cytometric analysis of monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs). Data on irAEs and related clinical information were collected, including time of onset, affected organs, and severity, with a minimum follow-up period of 2 months after the final dose of ICIs. Cancer cachexia was defined, according to an international consensus statement,17 as weight loss of >5% over the past 6 months or a body mass index (BMI) of <20 kg/m2.

Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining

Mouse organs were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with HE (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Images were acquired using TissueFAXS SL Spectra (TissueGnostics, Vienna, Austria) and Pannoramic MIDI (Budapest, Hungary).

Immunofluorescence analysis

Immunofluorescence analysis was performed after antigen retrieval. Sections were microwaved (800 W, low power) in 10 mmol/L citric acid (TTCA Co., Ltd, Anqiu, Shandong, China) for 2 min at high power, followed by 5 min at 50% power. The sections were then cooled for 60 min at 25 °C, washed in PBS, and blocked with 0.5% BSA/PBS for 30 min at 37 °C. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies (Supplemental Table 2) overnight for 12 h at 4 °C and then for 30 min at 37 °C. After washing in PBS, sections were incubated with 1:400 diluted goat/donkey anti-rat Alexa Fluor 488 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), goat/donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), or goat/donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) secondary antibodies for 45 min at 37 °C. Finally, sections were washed and mounted with DAPI (Mounting Medium with DAPI-Aqueous, #ab104139, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) and stored in the dark at −30 °C until imaging. Identical confocal settings were used to limit the intra-experimental variability. Fluorescent signals were detected using a laser scanning confocal microscope and automatic slide slice scan (Leica STELLARIS STED, Wetzlar, Germany). For quantification of the immunofluorescence results, images of the indicated tissues were analyzed using Leica Application Suite X 1.8.0.13313. Alternatively, the absolute number of positive cells/mm2 in nine fields of interest was statistically analyzed. The investigators were blinded to the experimental groups while conducting the studies.

Flow cytometry analysis and sorting

After anesthesia with pentobarbital sodium (Chensheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) solution and cervical dislocation, the thymus was isolated from the anterior mediastinum of each mouse. Thymic lobes were digested enzymatically using 0.25 mg/mL papain (Worthington Biochemical Corp., Lakewood, NJ, USA), 0.25 mg/mL collagenase IV (Roche Life Science, Penzberg, Germany), and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Roche Life Science). Single-cell suspensions were obtained from the thymus of mice as described previously.18,19 The cells were stained with monoclonal antibodies (Supplemental Table 2). For intracellular staining of FoxP3, a FoxP3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Kit (MultiSciences Biotech Co., Ltd, Hangzhou, China) was used following the manufacturer's instructions. Cell phenotypes were evaluated using CytoFLEXLX (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The data were analyzed with the CytExpert and FlowJo V10.0.7 (FlowJo, Ashland, OR, USA). A BD FACSAria III cell sorter (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was used for flow cytometry sorting.

CD3+ T lymphocyte transfer

CD3+ T lymphocytes were isolated via magnetically activated cell sorting (EasySep™ Cell Separation, Stemcell, Vancouver, Canada) from the tumors or spleens of tumor-bearing mice with cachexia or tumor-free mice. Next, 1.5 × 106 CD3+ T lymphocytes were transferred into tumor-free mice via caudal vein injection. After 12 h, the mice were sacrificed, and their lungs, liver, stomach, small intestine, thyroid, skin, salivary glands, pancreas, brain, and eyes were dissected and placed in 4% paraformaldehyde for preservation. These organs were cut into 4-mm sections and stained with HE for microscopic evaluation.

Caspase activity and nitric oxide (NO) analysis

M-MDSCs were collected to measure the production of NO in the indicated conditioned media and the activity of caspase-3 using a Nitric Oxide Content Assay Kit (Beijing Boxbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China) and Caspase-3 Activity Detection Kit (Beijing Boxbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China), respectively.

Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction of inducible NO synthase (iNOS)

M-MDSCs were collected, processed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) to extract total RNA, and then reverse transcribed from 1 μg of total RNA to cDNA using the PrimeScript®RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa Biomedical Technology, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. Premix Ex Taq™ (Probe qPCR, TaKaRa Biomedical Technology) was used to perform real-time quantitative assays. The relevant primer sequences used in the experiment are shown as follows: NOS2 (iNOS) forward, 5′-CACCTTGGAAGAGGAGCAAC-3′, NOS2 (iNOS) reverse, 5′-AAGGCCAAACACAGCATACC-3'; GAPDH forward, 5′-AACTTTGGCATTGTGGAAGG-3′, GAPDH reverse, 5′-ACACATTGGGGGTAGGAACA-3'.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Supernatants from the T-cell and M-MDSC co-culture systems and the plasma of patients and donors were collected. Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) quantification in the supernatants was determined through ELISA (Human IFN Gamma ELISA Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Color development is induced by adding a substrate solution. Optical density values are converted to concentrations using a standard curve. Each sample is analyzed in triplicate, and the mean value is used for data interpretation.

T-cell proliferation and activation assay

T-cell proliferation was determined using the 5,6-carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) dilution assay. Briefly, a freeze-dried powder of CFSE (500 μg, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was dissolved in 180 μL of anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (Yoshida Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and frozen at −30 °C. T-cells were sorted from patients with cancer and healthy donors, labeled with CFSE, and cultured alone or with M-MDSCs at different ratios (2:1, 4:1, 8:1) for 3 days. The cells were then stained for surface marker expression with CD4-FITC (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) or CD8-APC antibodies (BioLegend, Supplemental Table 2); T-cell proliferation was analyzed using a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Where indicated, 1 mmol/L l-arginine (Cayman Chemicals, Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA), 0.5 mmol/L nor-NOHA (arginase I specific inhibitor; Cayman Chemicals), or 100 μmol/L L-NMMA (inducible NO synthase (iNOS) inhibitor; Cayman Chemicals), was added to the culture on day 0.

Detection of apoptosis

Annexin V-APC/7-AAD Apoptosis Kit (MultiSciences Biotech Co., Ltd, Hangzhou, China) was used to evaluate the apoptotic death of cells induced by the eluate of test compounds. The assay was prepared in 12-well plates by adding 500 μL of complete medium (with a cell density of 1 × 105 cells/well) and 500 μL of the prepared eluate, followed by incubation for 24 h and 48 h. Cells treated with served as the positive control. The negative control consisted of cells suspended in complete medium and incubated for 24 h and 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with cold PBS, and then double-stained using Annexin V-APC (as a marker for early cell death) and 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD; as a marker for cell membrane disruption, necrosis, and late apoptosis). The percentage of apoptotic cells was calculated by flow cytometry using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used for data analysis. For comparisons between two groups, either the Student's t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was applied, depending on the normality of data distribution. Comparisons involving more than two groups were performed using the Friedman test, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA, with appropriate corrections for multiple comparisons. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Infiltration of activated T cells parallels thymic atrophy in cachexic HCC mice

To investigate the role of T-cell infiltration in multiple organs in the development of irAEs in advanced cancer, an orthotopic HCC model was established. T-cell infiltration was assessed in tumor-bearing mice exhibiting 5%–10% weight loss, skeletal muscle mass loss, and fat loss (cachexic) and compared with that in non-cachexic tumor-bearing mice and tumor-free controls (Supplemental Fig. 1). Pathological evaluation revealed inflammatory cell infiltration in multiple organs of cachexic HCC mice but not in non-cachexic or tumor-free mice (Fig. 1A and B). Thymic atrophy was also observed in cachexic HCC mice. Thymus volume, weight, and total cell count were lower in cachexic HCC mice than in non-cachexic HCC mice and tumor-free controls (Fig. 1D). HE staining revealed degradation of the thymic medulla in cachexic HCC mice (Fig. 1E).

Similar findings were observed in 1-year-old cachexic HCC mice (Supplemental Fig. 2). Additionally, because Hepa1-6 is a syngeneic tumor for the C57L/J background, thymic atrophy was further evaluated in an in situ HCC model using C57L/J mice, which yielded consistent results (Supplemental Fig. 3). Notably, Hepa1-6 cells formed tumors and spontaneous metastases after implantation in syngeneic C57L/J mice, making it difficult to assess inflammation in tumor-free organs.

To explore the relationship between tumor volume, T-cell infiltration, and thymic medulla integrity, subcutaneous HCC and malignant melanoma mouse models were constructed. Tumor growth was limited to a maximum diameter of 1.5 cm, in accordance with humane endpoint criteria, as weight loss was not significant compared with tumor-free mice. In these subcutaneous tumor models, neither T-cell infiltration nor thymic involution was observed (Supplemental Fig. 4).

mTEC abundance is decreased in the thymuses of cachexic HCC mice

Several cell types in the thymus were evaluated using flow cytometry (Supplemental Fig. 5A). HCC mice with or without cachexia exhibited similar proportions of thymocyte subpopulations, including double-negative (CD4−CD8−), double-positive (CD4+CD8+), single-positive (CD4+CD8− and CD4−CD8+), mature (CD3+CD4+CD8− and CD3+CD4−CD8+), and memory (CD4+CD8−CD44highCD62Llow and CD4−CD8+CD44highCD62Llow) T cells (Supplemental Fig. 5B). However, the absolute numbers of double-negative, double-positive, CD4 single-positive, CD8 single-positive, and CD3+CD4+ T cells were decreased in cachexic HCC mice (Supplemental Fig. 5C). Additionally, the proportion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) was higher in the thymuses of cachexic mice than in those of non-cachexic HCC mice, although the absolute cell numbers were similar (Supplemental Fig. 5B and C).

T-cell selection occurs through interactions between thymocytes and TECs.20 Cortical TECs (cTECs) mediate positive selection, whereas mTECs are responsible for negative selection.21 Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that Krt5, a marker of mTECs,22 was significantly less abundant in the thymuses of cachexic HCC mice, whereas β5T, a marker of cTECs,23 remained unaffected (Fig. 2A and Supplemental Fig. 6). Flow cytometry further confirmed a reduced proportion of mTECs among total TECs in cachexic HCC mice (Supplemental Fig. 5D). Additionally, the proportion of mature mTECs among the total mTECs was decreased (Supplemental Fig. 5D). scRNA-seq was performed on CD45− cells from the thymuses of tumor-free and cachexic HCC mice to investigate the mechanism underlying mTEC involution (Fig. 2B). The analysis focused on TEC subsets classified as cTECs and mTECs: mTEC I (putative TEC progenitors, characterized by high Itga6 and Sca1 expression), mTEC II (mature mTECs, with high expression of Aire, Fezf2, Cd40, H2-Aa, or Cd74), mTEC III (heterogeneous TECs including putative post-AIRE cells, expressing distinct genes such as Pigr, Ly6d, Spink5, Ivl, and Krt10), and mTEC IV (lacking classical mTEC or cTEC markers but expressing unique genes such as Lrmp, Avil, Trpm5, Dclk1, Gng13, L1cam, and Sox9) (Fig. 2C–E).24 mTECs ectopically express and cross-present a wide array of TRAs, whose expression is stochastic and AIRE-dependent. In cachexic HCC mice, levels of mTEC II and III were decreased, whereas the proportion of cTECs increased significantly (Fig. 2F). These findings indicate that mTEC-driven T-cell negative selection may be dysregulated in cachexic HCC mice.

Several chemokines and cytokines that may contribute to T-cell negative selection were also significantly altered in the mTECs of cachexic HCC mice compared with those of tumor-free mice, potentially limiting mTEC maturation (Fig. 3B and C). Thymic M-MDSC enrichment was observed in cachexic HCC mice, as demonstrated via flow cytometry (Fig. 5D) and immunofluorescence (Fig. 5E) analyses. Thymic M-MDSCs suppressed T-cell proliferation (Supplemental Fig. 14). Furthermore, M-MDSCs from cachexic HCC mice were co-cultured with mTECs isolated from tumor-free mice, and the survival rates of mTECs were recorded. M-MDSCs were found to promote mTEC death in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5F and Supplemental Fig. 15A). Additionally, caspase-3 activity was increased in the supernatant of the co-culture system in an M-MDSC dose-dependent manner (Supplemental Fig. 15B).

M-MDSCs induced early apoptosis in mTECs (Supplemental Fig. 15C). Furthermore, iNOS expression and NO production were elevated in M-MDSCs from cachexic HCC mice compared with those from tumor-free mice (Supplemental Fig. 15D and E). NO levels in the supernatant of the co-culture system increased in a dose-dependent manner with M-MDSC concentration (Supplemental Fig. 15F). The apoptosis-inducing effect of M-MDSCs on mTECs was inhibited by the iNOS inhibitor L-NMMA, as demonstrated by increased mTEC survival, reduced apoptosis, and decreased caspase-3 activity (Supplemental Fig. 15G–I). Overall, these findings indicate that M-MDSCs induce mTEC apoptosis via NO production.

Discussion

Activation of lymphocytes responsive to TRAs is a key mechanism in the development of autoimmune diseases and irAEs.8,9 mTECs play a central role in the negative selection of T cells, which is primarily responsible for eliminating autoreactive T cells.28,29 Tregs and lymphatic endothelial cells in lymph nodes also contribute to the negative selection process.30 Thymic involution, which begins in adulthood, increases the likelihood of dysfunction in negative selection.31 However, under normal physiological and certain pathological conditions, immune checkpoints help restrain the activation of autoreactive lymphocytes, thereby preventing the onset of autoimmune diseases. T-cell infiltration of normal organs has been observed in the context of thymic involution, particularly in elderly individuals.32 Despite this, predicting the occurrence, affected organs, and severity of irAEs remains challenging and often appears stochastic.2 Importantly, TRA expression in mTECs follows a stochastic pattern and is primarily regulated by the transcription factors AIRE and FEZF2.22,25 Given that administration of ICIs to patients with thymic tumors is associated with severe irAEs,33 thymic dysfunction may be a contributing factor in irAE development. In the present study, we observed thymic atrophy in cachexic tumor-bearing mice. Furthermore, mTEC dysfunction impaired the negative selection of T cells, resulting in autoimmune T-cell infiltration into normal, tumor-free organs.

Cachexia-associated thymic atrophy contributed to the development of irAEs. Infiltration of tissues by activated T cells is widely recognized as a hallmark of irAEs.34 Additionally, activation of B cells, humoral immune responses, and the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures have also been implicated in irAEs pathogenesis.35 The activation of autoreactive lymphocytes represents a central mechanism underlying irAEs. Tumor antigens and undiagnosed pre-existing autoimmune conditions may also contribute to their development.36 Peripheral tolerance mechanisms mediated by Tregs and extrathymic AIRE-expressing cells may help control irAEs onset and severity.37 In the present study, we demonstrated that expansion of the autoreactive T-cell pool due to thymic atrophy in cachexic cancer-bearing hosts does not fully account for the development of irAEs. Additionally, several clinical features apart from cachexia-associated thymic atrophy are likely to be involved in irAEs susceptibility.

The involuted thymus leads to reduced output of naïve T cells, a limited T-cell receptor repertoire, relatively enhanced Treg cell generation, induction of immunosenescence, and potential suppression of immune surveillance of neoplasia. Aside from age, acute thymic atrophy can also be induced by several infectious agents (including bacteria, viruses, parasites, and fungi) as well as other factors such as stress, pregnancy, malnutrition, chemotherapy, and cancer.38,39 This study revealed that cancer-related cachexia causes thymic atrophy, which leads to dysfunction in T-cell negative selection. In particular, during ICI treatment, several factors, including chemotherapy, infection, and unresolved malnutrition, can induce thymic atrophy and increase the incidence of irAEs. Although this study demonstrated the predictive value of pretreatment M-MDSC levels and weight loss for irAEs occurrence, dynamic predictive models incorporating weight loss, M-MDSCs, and other thymic insults are needed to improve the accuracy of irAEs prediction.

MDSCs and certain cytokines may contribute to cancer cachexia–associated thymic atrophy. In mTECs from cachexic tumor-bearing mice, levels of CCL25, CX3CL1, and CXCL13 were elevated, whereas those of CXCL16, CCL22, CCL17, CXCL2, and CCL5 were decreased. CCL25 in the thymus is known to chemoattract dendritic cells (DCs).40 CD3+NK1.1−TCRβ+CD4−CD8− double-negative T cells enhance Treg cell recruitment via CCL5 in ischemic stroke lesions.41 Transendothelial DCs chemoattracted by the endothelial ligand CX3CL1 may facilitate the thymus in acquiring blood-borne antigens to induce and maintain central tolerance.42 However, the precise roles of these altered cytokines and chemokines in T-cell negative selection remain unclear and warrant further investigation. MDSCs are immunosuppressive and tumor-promoting cells that also play roles in various inflammatory diseases.43,44 Indeed, MDSC accumulation is associated with the severity of inflammatory diseases, whereas MDSC transfer has been reported to alleviate several autoimmune diseases.45,46 In the present study, however, M-MDSC transfer did not alleviate T-cell infiltration in normal organs. In contrast, Fang et al.26 reported that M-MDSC chemoattraction to the thymus in chronic hepatitis B induces central tolerance to HBV by presenting HBV antigens to thymocytes. We confirmed infiltration of tumor-associated M-MDSCs into the corticomedullary junction of the thymus and demonstrated their novel role in inducing mTEC apoptosis through NO production, a mechanism also observed in various other cell types.47 Nonetheless, the impact of limited M-MDSC production on the efficacy of ICI treatments in elderly patients requires further investigation.

The causes of cancer-associated cachexia include, at a minimum, tumor progression and systemic inflammation. Several murine tumor models, both spontaneous and experimentally induced, have been used to study cancer cachexia; however, a single tumor model cannot fully reproduce the heterogeneity of human cancers. The primary hallmark of cancer cachexia is the loss of skeletal muscle mass, accompanied by progressive functional impairment, reduced food intake (anorexia), and body weight loss. In the present study, we employed in situ HCC-related cachexia models, which exhibited skeletal muscle and body weight loss. Reduced physical activity and food intake were also observed. Thus, the variation in cachexia development among in situ HCC mice was attributed to differences in tumor progression and systemic inflammation. Cachexic HCC mice consistently exhibited a greater tumor burden than non-cachexic counterparts. Furthermore, previous studies on the role of MDSCs in cancer have shown that M-MDSC transfer generally promotes tumor progression. Based on this, we speculated that the transfer of cancer cachexia–associated M-MDSCs could also contribute to tumor progression. Notably, subcutaneous models of HCC and melanoma, in which tumors reached the ethical limit of 1.5 cm in diameter, did not result in cachexia. In contrast, in situ HCC tumors of similar size consistently led to cachexia. These findings indicate that thymic atrophy in cachexic HCC mice may result from both tumor progression and cachexia. However, our study does not rule out the possibility that thymic destruction could be mediated by autoimmune attack during the process of thymic atrophy. Additionally, whether cachexia resulting from other causes follows a similar biological mechanism warrants further investigation.

In the current study, mTEC dysfunction during thymic atrophy induced by cancer-associated cachexia was characterized by a decreased total number of mTECs, a relatively lower proportion of mature mTECs, and reduced expression of Aire within mTECs. M-MDSCs induced apoptosis of mTECs, which may be a critical factor contributing to their dysfunction. Accordingly, we speculate that M-MDSCs play a fundamental role in thymic atrophy associated with cancer cachexia. Although mTECs primarily direct the negative selection of T cells, Tregs and fibroblasts also contribute to this process to some extent.48 The number of Tregs increased during thymic involution, which may have further contributed to impaired T-cell negative selection and warrants additional investigation.

In this study, the Hepa1-6 cell line-based model effectively simulates orthotopic HCC. The Hepa1-6 model, serving as a homologous transplantation model, exhibits rapid tumor growth over a matter of weeks, with cachexia manifesting in its advanced stages. It primarily mimics the consumption resulting from tumor burden alone, but falls short in replicating the backdrop of chronic liver injury, persistent inflammation, and metabolic disturbances. Virus models, such as those involving HBV transgenic animals, boast a pathological progression that aligns more closely with human conditions, potentially offering a superior simulation of metabolic disturbances and systemic inflammation. Nevertheless, these models are encumbered by extended construction cycles, high costs, and potentially insignificant cachexia phenotypes. The chemical induction model, utilizing diethylnitrosamine (DEN)/CCL4, stands out for its prominent chronic liver injury characteristics. However, it is plagued by significant interindividual variability and random tumor localization, which may compromise experimental consistency. Furthermore, the chemicals employed may harbor systemic toxicity, necessitating careful differentiation between drug toxicity and cachexia.

This study also has some limitations. The causal relationship between thymic atrophy and infiltration of TRA-responsive T cells requires further validation, potentially through transplantation of atrophied thymuses into healthy recipients. Such a procedure may reverse thymic involution and result in minimal infiltration of TRA-responsive T cells into normal organs. Additionally, the timing and severity of irAEs could not be predicted based on the findings of this study. Therefore, further research, particularly involving human samples, is needed to clarify the role of thymic atrophy in the development of irAEs.

Conclusions

In conclusion, thymic atrophy was observed in cachexic tumor-bearing mice. Impaired thymic T-cell negative selection, caused by mTEC dysfunction, led to autoimmune T-cell infiltration into normal organs, serving as a prerequisite for the development of irAEs. Furthermore, cancer cachexia–associated M-MDSCs were found to induce mTEC apoptosis during thymic atrophy and may serve as predictive biomarkers for irAE development.
