Changes in Purine Metabolism During Differentiation of Dopamine Neurons from Human Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells
Authors: Lauren Grychowski, Fatemeh Seifar, Erkin Ozel, Anika Thite, Diane J Sutcliffe, Ashok R Dinasarapu, Ellen J Hess, H A Jinnah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurines are a class of ubiquitous molecules required for fundamental processes in all cells. Purines are derived from two major sources: de novo synthesis, and salvage of preformed purine bases. The current studies provide evidence that the relative contributions of these two pathways change substantially as human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) differentiate into neurons. Expression of all genes in the de novo synthesis pathway decreases as pluripotent cells differentiate into neurons, but expression of the salvage pathway gene HPRT1 increases. This selective rise in HPRT1 gene expression corresponds with increased activity of its associated enzyme, hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGprt). Similar changes in the expression of genes for the de novo pathway genes and for HPRT1 were found in a public database of gene expression for human brain development. The consequences of eliminating HGprt-mediated recycling were also evaluated in human-derived iPSCs with null HPRT1 mutations and stock iPSC lines that have been gene-edited to contain a null HPRT1 mutation. The absence of HGprt had no apparent impact on early neuronal differentiation, through 60 days of in vitro differentiation. Biochemical studies of purine content showed that that the absence of HGprt had little impact on intracellular purines, although large amounts of its substrate (hypoxanthine) accumulated in the tissue culture medium. Neurons derived from iPSCs without HGprt appeared morphologically and neurochemically indistinguishable from neurons derived from iPSCs where HGprt was intact. Interrogation of the transcriptome using RNA sequencing (RNAseq) indicated that the absence of HGprt had no consistent impact on gene expression during differentiation. Introduction

Purines are a class of molecules that play essential roles in many cellular functions including energy-requiring reactions, cofactor synthesis, and nucleic acid formation. In neurons, purines support development and differentiation and contribute to both intracellular and intercellular signaling (Garcia-Gil et al. 2018; Rodrigues et al. 2019). Purines are derived from two major sources. The first is de novo synthesis, a complex pathway that involves 10 steps and 6 different enzymes (Pareek et al. 2021). The second is the salvage of preformed purine bases (Murray 1971). Because there are fewer steps, purine recycling is more efficient than de novo synthesis. Making a single molecule of the central purine inosine monophosphate (IMP) costs 6 high-energy phosphate bonds of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) in the de novo pathway, while it costs only 1 high-energy molecule of phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate in the recycling pathway. Most cells obtain their purines from both de novo and recycling pathways, but the relative importance of each varies according to cell type. Rapidly dividing cells often rely heavily on the de novo pathway, while post-mitotic cells rely more on recycling. Regarding the nervous system, the relative activity of the de novo and salvage pathways during neuronal growth and differentiation is not well delineated. Studies in rodents have implied that the de novo pathway predominates in the earliest stages of development when neural precursors proliferate, while the salvage pathway becomes increasingly active as neurons mature (Mizukoshi et al. 2023).

The critical importance of purine recycling for neural function is highlighted by Lesch-Nyhan disease (LND), a rare disorder caused by mutations in the HPRT1 gene, which encodes the enzyme hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGprt). Affected individuals have severe neurological and behavioral problems (Fu et al. 2014). The loss of HGprt has a disproportionate impact on dopamine neurons, leaving most other neurons largely spared (Visser et al. 2000; Goettle et al. 2014). Prior studies of mouse models lacking HGprt have revealed a relatively selective reduction of dopamine content in the basal ganglia (Jinnah et al. 1992, 1994, 1999; Smith and Friedmann 2000; Egami et al. 2007; Tschirner et al. 2016). These findings have been replicated in rats (Meek et al. 2016) and validated by human studies showing low dopamine levels and tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) in post-mortem human LND brain tissue (Lloyd et al. 1981; Goettle et al. 2014), and reduced dopamine transporters in positron emission tomography scans of LND individuals (Ernst et al. 1996; Wong et al. 1996). Despite these changes in biochemical markers of dopamine neurons, there is no evidence that HGprt deficiency is associated with dopamine cell loss (Egami et al. 2007; Goettle et al. 2014). These observations have led to the hypothesis that the pathogenesis in LND involves a defect in dopamine neuron development or differentiation (Shirley et al. 2007; Ceballos-Picot et al. 2009; Kang et al. 2011, 2013; Guibinga et al. 2012; Torres and Puig 2015).

Although prior studies have implicated a developmental process in LND, mechanistic understanding has been hindered by the lack of experimental models to study human dopamine neuron development. Mouse models do not exhibit the neurobehavioral abnormalities of LND, and most cell models such as fibroblasts or blood cells have limited potential to model neuronal development. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) derived from human sources provide promising models to address some of these limitations. So far, only a few studies have been conducted on iPSCs relevant to LND. Unbiased transcriptomic and proteomic studies have revealed changes in gene and protein expression associated with neurogenesis and early development (Dinasarapu et al. 2022; Sutcliffe et al. 2021). One study attributed LND pathogenesis to a purine deficiency (Ruillier et al. 2020), and another suggested energy limitation (Bell et al. 2021). The current study used both iPSCs derived directly from humans (HD-iPSC) and commonly used iPSC stock lines (SL-iPSC) that were gene edited to contain a null HPRT1 mutation to investigate normal purine metabolism during neuronal development, as well as the consequences of HGprt deficiency.

Methods

Cell cultures. The HD-iPSC lines were derived from 4 different individuals with LND and 4 different normal controls (CON) as previously described (Sutcliffe et al. 2021; Dinasarapu et al. 2025a). Additionally, 3 widely available stock iPSC lines (KOLF2.0, NCRM1, PGP1) were each gene-edited to carry common c.508C>T early stop codon mutation associated with LND using the CRISPR-Cas9 method (Dinasarapu et al. 2025b). All iPSC lines were de-identified and previously published (Sutcliffe et al. 2021; Dinasarapu et al. 2025a, b), so additional ethical approvals for the current studies were not required. All iPSCs were verified to have a normal karyotype, and the expected mutations in HPRT1 were confirmed by DNA sequencing.

For neuronal differentiation, iPSC colonies were dissociated using Accutase (StemPro™) and seeded at 100,000 cells/mL on Matrigel coated 6-well dishes. Cells were fed with mTeSR™ Plus Medium (Stem Cell Technologies). At 90–100% confluency, a widely used floor-plate neuronal induction method to differentiate dopamine neuron cultures was used with slight modification (Kriks et al. 2011). Differentiation was initiated in medium containing SB431542 (20 mM) and LDN193189 (2 mM). At days 1–2, medium was exchanged every day with SHH (100 µg/ml), purmorphamine (10 mM) and FGF8a (500 µg/mL). At days 3–4, CHIR99021 (15 mM) was included. At days 5–6, 25% of N2 media was mixed with 75% neuralization media. The ratio of N2 to neuralization media was gradually increased to 50% and 75% over the next 2 days with LDN 193,189 (2 mM) and CHIR 99,021 (15 mM). At day 11, the medium was changed to neurobasal (ThermoFisher scientific) with addition of B27, BDNF (100 µg/ml), GDNF (100 µg/ml), TGF-β3 (20 µg/ml), dcAMP (250 mM), DAPT (10 mM), ascorbic acid (200 mM) and CHIR99021 (15 mM). This medium was exchanged daily for 3 days. At day 14, the same neurobasal medium without addition of CHIR99021 was exchanged every other day until day 60. Neurons were replated at 500,000/mL on Matrigel coated 6-well plates at day 21 and fed every other day using neurobasal media. Cells were studied at 4 stages; undifferentiated iPSCs on day 0 (D0), neural progenitor stage at day 11 (D11), early neurons at day 25 (D25), and later neurons at day 60 (D60) as indicated in the Graphical Abstract.

Immunostains of iPSCs and their neuronal derivatives were conducted as previously described (Sutcliffe et al. 2021; Dinasarapu et al. 2022). Neurons were stained using a generic neuronal marker (beta III-tubulin, Cell signaling, #4466S) and a more specific marker for monoaminergic neurons (TH, Pel-Freez, #P40101-150).

Biochemical studies. Purines were measured by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with photodiode array UV detection as previously described (Shirley et al. 2007; Goettle et al. 2013; Sutcliffe et al. 2021). The levels of dopamine and related metabolites were measured by HPLC with electrochemical detection as previously described (Shirley et al. 2007; Goettle et al. 2013). Biochemical measures were performed for both cell extracts and conditioned medium, 24 h after medium exchange. Results were normalized to total cell protein with a BCA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL).

Results for biochemical studies are presented as group averages (± SEM) or as box plots showing the median, interquartile ranges, and individual data points. Statistical analyses were conducted using single or multivariate ANOVA. Depending on the dataset, explanatory variables included Disease Effect (HD-iPSC CON vs. LND or SL-IPSC parent line vs. gene-edited counterpart), Day of Differentiation (D0, D11, D25, D60), Type of iPSC (HD-iPSC vs. SL-iPSC), and sometimes Sample Source (intracellular vs. extracellular). The individual analytes (purine analytes, monoamine metabolites, and individual RNAs quantified) served as the measurement outcomes.

Gene expression profiling. Gene expression profiles were assessed by RNA sequencing (RNAseq) as previously described (Dinasarapu et al. 2022; Sutcliffe et al. 2021). The number of sequenced read pairs per sample (in millions) ranged from 20.3 to 49.7 million, with a median of 28.8 million. After quality control checks and filtering, a total of 24,572 genes were retained for analyses. A list of specific RNAs was chosen to evaluate changes in expression related to the four stages of differentiation or different aspects of purine metabolism. To compare the relative levels of transcripts both across differentiation stages within each gene and between genes, expression levels were estimated by normalizing the read counts using the Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (fpkm) method from countToFPKM R-package (https://github.com/AAlhendi1707/countToFPKM) and then visualized as line plots. Results for each of these genes were independently verified by quantitative PCR.

Differential gene expression was performed using edgeR as previously described (Dinasarapu et al. 2022, 2025a, b; Sutcliffe et al. 2021). The analysis was implemented using glmFit and glmLRT functions for likelihood ratio testing, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons conducted with the exactTest function. For the HD-iPSC lines and their derivatives, the statistical model addressed the Disease Effect as a group (CON vs. LND) while adjusting for Day of Differentiation. For the SL-iPSCs, a paired analysis was performed by also including the parent line as a factor in the design matrix to account for baseline differences among the 3 stock lines. The Benjamini-Hochberg method for the False Discovery Rate (FDR) was used to correct for multiple comparisons.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) applied to gene expression profiles quantified as counts per million (CPM) was used to identify biological pathways (Subramanian et al. 2005). Gene expression profiles were ranked based on their Signal-to-Noise ratio, and GSEA was conducted using v4.3.3 software (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/login.jsp) to evaluate the enrichment of curated Gene Ontology (GO) gene sets from The Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). GO terms ranked by their normalized enrichment scores (NES) and statistical significance were assessed using nominal p-values.

Results

Neuronal differentiation of HD-iPSCs. At D0 before differentiation, all HD-iPSC CON (n = 4) and LND (n = 4) lines had a similar morphology characteristic of undifferentiated iPSCs (Sutcliffe et al. 2021). They grew as small round cells in discrete colonies, with a scant cytoplasm surrounding a large nucleus, and positive immunostaining for typical stem cell markers. Gene expression profiling revealed the expected pluripotency markers, with no apparent differences in expression levels between CON and LND lines (Fig. 1A).

At D11 of the differentiation protocol corresponding to the neural progenitor cell stage, all CON and LND lines were morphologically larger and more angular, with positive immunostaining for immature neuronal markers (Dinasarapu et al. 2022). They expressed lower levels of most pluripotency genes (Fig. 1A) with higher expression of genes associated with neuronal development (Fig. 1B). There were no obvious differences in the morphologies or expression of these genes between the CON and LND lines.

At D25 and D60 of the differentiation protocol, there was progressive elaboration of neurites with positive immunostaining for neuronal markers. Although there were some morphological differences among individual lines, there were no consistent differences between the CON and LND groups. These cells expressed low or absent levels of pluripotency genes (Fig. 1A), and higher levels of genes associated with neuronal maturation (Fig. 1B), again with no obvious differences between the CON and LND groups.

Neuronal differentiation for SL-iPSCs. Morphological and gene expression studies similar to those described for HD-iPSCs were repeated during neuronal differentiation of 3 independent SL-iPSCs (KOLF2.0, NCRM1, PGP1) with one HPRT1 c.508C>T gene-edited subline for each. Results for the SL-iPSCs were similar to those described for the HD-iPSCs. The differentiation protocol was associated with morphological and molecular evidence for neuronal differentiation, with no obvious differences between the parent stock lines and their corresponding gene-edited sublines (Fig. 1A, B).

One observation when comparing SL-iPSCs (with or without HPRT1 edits) to HD-iPSCs (with or without HPRT1 mutations) was that SL-iPSCs had relatively lower levels of some genes (NANOG, POU5F1, ZFP42) associated with pluripotency (Fig. 1A) and relatively blunted changes in some genes (NES, VIM) associated with early neuronal development (Fig. 1B). Overall, these morphological and molecular results confirm that the differentiation protocol produced neuron-like cells, with no obvious impact of HPRT1 mutations in SL-iPSCs, and small differences between SL-iPSCs and HD-iPSCs.

Purine enzyme genes in HD-iPSCs. Genes encoding all enzymes of the de novo pathway (PPAT, GART, PFAS, PAICS, ADSL, ATIC) showed a progressive decrease in expression during differentiation from D0 to D60 (Fig. 1C). Also shown are progressive declines in two genes (PRPS1 and PRPS2) which encode enzymes that produce the phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate substrate that is required to drive the de novo pathway (Fig. 1C). There were no apparent differences in the expression of any of these genes at any time point between the CON and LND groups.

The salvage enzyme gene APRT showed changes very similar to the de novo enzyme genes. However, the salvage enzyme gene HPRT1 showed more complex changes, with the highest levels at D0, a drop between D11 and D25, and a subsequent increase at D60 (Fig. 1C). There was less HPRT1 gene expression in the LND group, because most of the HPRT1 mutations studied are subject to nonsense-mediated decay and thereby lower steady state levels of its mRNA transcript (Sutcliffe et al. 2021). This pattern of HPRT1 mRNA expression was mirrored by HGprt enzymatic activity (Fig. 2). For lines from the CON group, enzyme activity tended to decline during early differentiation but then increased at the latest time point. No enzyme activity could be detected in any of the LND lines, as expected.

The most abundant purine released into the extracellular space was hypoxanthine (Fig. 3), which was below limits of detection in the cell pellets. High levels were released at D0 and D11, but lower levels were released at D25 and D60. At all time points, the LND lines released larger amounts of hypoxanthine than the CON lines. As a substrate for HGprt, extracellular hypoxanthine accumulates due to HPRT1 mutations that eliminate activity. Guanine is also a substrate for HGprt, but levels were not detectable, even in lines with HPRT1 mutations. Uric acid was not detected, confirming prior reports that these cells do not express xanthine oxidoreductase, the enzyme responsible for converting hypoxanthine into uric acid (Seifar et al. 2022).

Purine enzyme genes in SL-iPSCs. De novo pathway genes for the 3 SL-iPSCs and their gene-edited counterparts showed gene expression changes similar to those described above for HD-iPSCs (Fig. 1C). All genes of de novo synthesis and the salvage enzyme gene APRT showed a progressive decrements during differentiation from D0 to D60.

Changes in HPRT1 gene expression also followed a pattern similar to the HD-iPSCs during differentiation, with high levels at D0, low levels at D11 and D25, and a subsequent increase at D60. There were no apparent differences between the starting SL-iPSCs and their corresponding HPRT1 gene-edited counterparts for any of the de novo pathway genes. However, HPRT1 expression was significantly lower in all gene-edited lines. The pattern of HPRT1 gene expression was partly paralleled by changes in HGprt enzyme activity, with some variations between lines (Fig. 2). The un-edited NCRM1 line showed a similar pattern to the HD-iPSCs with differentiation, with low levels until a sharp rise at D60. In contrast, the unedited KOLF2.0 and PGP1 did not show a rise in activity at D60. As a result, the group average for the unedited SL-IPSCs showed less obvious changes during development. As expected, the gene-edited counterparts had no detectable HGprt enzyme activity.

These results confirm that neuronal differentiation is associated with comparable changes in expression of genes encoding purine metabolic enzymes across both HD-iPSCs and SL-iPSCs with the only notable Disease Effect being HPRT1 itself.

Purine biochemistry in HD-iPCSs. The most abundant intracellular purines in mammalian cells include ATP and guanosine triphosphate (GTP) and their diphosphate (ADP, GDP) or monophosphate derivates (AMP, GMP). Neuronal differentiation of HD-iPSCs was associated with a reduction in ATP and GTP from D0 to D60 (Fig. 3). Intracellular levels of ADP, AMP, and GDP showed less obvious changes over time. When comparing the CON and LND groups, there were no apparent differences for any of these intracellular purines at any time point.

Purine biochemistry in SL-iPSCs. Intracellular and extracellular purines in the SL-iPSCs and their gene-edited counterparts showed trends similar to those of the HD-iPSCs (Fig. 3). There were no obvious differences between the SL-iPSCs and their HPRT1 gene-edited counterparts, except that extracellular hypoxanthine levels were again elevated in all gene-edited lines. Also, the SL-iPSCs and their edited counterparts contained higher levels of some purines than the HD-iPSCs.

These results confirm that neuronal differentiation is associated with comparable changes in purine metabolism across both HD-iPSCs and SL-iPSCs. Both types of iPSCs showed obvious increases in hypoxanthine released into the culture medium, and no measurable impact on intracellular purines. There were again some differences between the HD-iPSCs and the SL-iPSCs, and even between the SL-iPSC pairs.

Purine metabolism in the developing human brain. To determine if the patterns of gene expression seen here with iPSCs differentiating into neurons was mirrored by similar changes in the developing human brain, a publicly available gene expression database was interrogated. The Human Brain Transcriptome is a public resource that provides genome-wide, exon-level transcriptome data generated using the Affymetrix GeneChip Human Exon 1.0 ST Arrays from over 1,340 brain tissue samples from postmortem human brains, spanning the prenatal period through adulthood (https:/hbatlas.org).

Across 6 different brain regions, the expression of nearly all genes involved in the de novo pathway (PPAT, GART, PFAS, PAICS, ATIC) dropped substantially in the pre-natal period, and many continued to drop after birth through early childhood (Fig. 4). Expression of some genes involved in purine metabolism (PRPS1, APRT) dropped mainly in the pre-natal period and then remained relatively stable after birth, while one (PRPS2) revealed changes that appeared to vary according to brain region. The only gene for which expression increased both in the prenatal period and through early childhood was HPRT1. These results are consistent with results found here for iPSCs differentiating into neurons.

Impact of impaired purine recycling for dopamine neurons in HD-iPSCs. Numerous prior studies have indicated that HPRT1 mutations significantly impact the function of dopamine neurons. Therefore, the development of dopamine neurons was evaluated in detail (Fig. 1D). During differentiation, all cell lines expressed patterns of genes expected of early neuronal development (e.g. SHH, FGF8, FOXA2, OTX2), later neuronal development (WNT1, LMX1A and B, MSX1, PITX3, NR4A2 or NURR1, EN2), and more mature dopamine neurons (TH, DDC, VMAT or SLC18A2, DAT1 or SLC6A3, KCNJ6). There were no obvious differences between the CON and LND lines at any time point.

Morphologically, the differentiation protocol applied to HD-iPSCs generated numerous cells with neuronal morphology that were also immunopositive for TH at D25 and D60. In keeping with prior studies using this protocol, there was a wide range of neuron-like profiles with varying neuritic lengths and branch patterns, and varying TH staining intensity. However, there were no obvious morphological differences between CON and LND lines at either D25 or D60 (Fig. 5A).

Neurotransmitter measurements by HPLC confirmed all cultures contained dopamine, and they released a significant proportion into the culture medium over 24 h (Fig. 5C). The cells retained only small amounts of the dopamine metabolites dihydroxyphenyl acetic acid (DOPAC) and homovanillic acid (HVA), but large amounts of both were released into the culture medium (Fig. 5C). The relatively high levels of DOPAC and HVA may reflect relatively low expression of SLC18A2, encoding the vesicular monoamine transporter VMAT2 (Fig. 1D), which sequesters and protects dopamine in storage vesicles where it is less vulnerable to metabolism. There were no consistent Disease Effects when comparing the CON and LND lines at either D25 or D60.

Impact of impaired purine recycling for dopamine neurons in SL-iPSCs. Patterns of gene expression related to dopamine neuron development among cultures differentiated from the SL-iPSCs and their gene-edited counterparts were similar to those observed for HD-iPSCs (Fig. 1D), with no obvious Disease Effect. However, there were some differences for starting Type of iPSC (SL-iPSCs vs. HD-iPSCs). For example, EN2 and SLC18A2 were expressed at higher levels in neurons from HD-iPSCs compared to neurons from SL-iPSCs at D60. Conversely, WNT1 was expressed at lower levels in neurons from HD-iPSCs compared to neurons from SL-iPSCs at D25.

Neurons derived from the SL-iPSCs also exhibited morphological and neurotransmitter features similar to those of the HD-iPSCs, with similar results at both D25 and D60 and no obvious Disease Effect at either time point (Fig. 5B, C).

Differential gene expression. The inclusion of all samples in a single RNAseq batch enabled comparison of gene expression according to several variables, including Day of Differentiation (D0, D11, D25, D60), Disease Effect (LND vs. CON), and Type of iPSC (HD-iPSC vs. SL-iPSC). Principal component analyses revealed a clear segregation of samples according to Day of Differentiation (Fig. 6A). For Day of Differentiation, there was additional minor segregation by Type of iPSC at D0 and D11, but not at later time points. There was no apparent segregation according to Disease Effect at any time point. These results suggest that the molecular signatures were largely determined by Day of Differentiation, modestly impacted by Type of iPSC at early stages, and not impacted by Disease Effect.

Analysis of Disease Effect at each Day of Differentiation separately revealed few significant findings, with limited consistency across the Type of iPSC (not shown)

The Disease Effect for differentially expressed gene was evaluated separately for the two Types of iPSCs at each time point, because the HD-iPSCs required a group analysis, whereas the SL-iPSCs required a paired analysis. The edgeR-based analyses revealed variable numbers of differentially expressed genes for Disease Effect at each Day of Differentiation, with little overlap for individual genes found for HD-IPSCs and SL-iPSCs. These results suggest a relatively minor Disease Effect that was not consistent across different Day of Differentiation (Figure S1A).

When the results were evaluated by edgeR with a model design that isolated Day of Differentiation (regardless of Disease Effect), a very large number of genes were differentially expressed across different days of differentiation for both HD-iPSCs and SL-iPSCs (Fig. 6B). The majority of these genes overlapped for both Types of iPSCs. This result confirms what could be seen in the PCA plot. When the model design focused on the Disease Effect regardless of Day of Differentiation, there were 23 differentially expressed genes for the HD-iPSCs and 10 for the SL-iPSCs (Fig. 6C-D). However, there was limited overlap in the lists of differentially expressed genes between the Types of iPSCs, except for HPRT1. When the model design focused on Disease Effect regardless of Type of iPSC or Day of Differentiation, only 4 genes were differentially expressed (Fig. 6E). These results imply a relatively minor impact of HPRT1 mutations on the expression of genes other than HPRT1 itself, and this impact was not consistent across HD-iPSCs and SL-iPSCs.

One potential reason for the apparently limited Disease Effect on gene expression at any Day of Differentiation might be that statistical power to detect differences was reduced by the high overall variance contributed by combining results across developmental time points, which had very different gene expression profiles. To address this possibility, each Day of Differentiation was evaluated separately. To identify the Disease Effect at each Day of Differentiation in the HD-iPSCs, time point specific Disease Effect contrasts were designed in edgeR. This analysis revealed few significant changes at each Day of Differentiation, except at D60, where there were 337 significantly altered genes for the HD-iPSCs (Figure S1A). Using analogous contrasts at each Day of Differentiation for the SL-iPSCs, pairwise analysis revealed few significant changes at any Day of Differentiation. These results suggest that the Disease Effect was greatest at D60 for the HD-iPSC comparisons, but this Disease Effect could not be replicated in the SL-iPSCs (not shown).

Because the Disease Effect was associated with so few consistent gene expression changes, pathway analyses to identify any Disease Effect were conducted with GSEA, which considers all genes according to rank instead of those identified as significantly affected in edgeR analyses. With this strategy, the biological pathways identified showed little consistency across Type of iPSC or Day of Differentiation (Figure S1B).

Discussion

The current studies delineate changes in purine metabolism during the differentiation of human iPSCs into dopamine neurons, as well as what happens when HGprt-mediated purine recycling is absent. There are several novel findings. First, neuronal differentiation is associated with a reduction in the total amount of intracellular purines. Second, neuronal differentiation is associated with marked changes in expression of genes in the de novo synthesis and salvage pathways, implying a shift in the source of purines during neuronal differentiation. Third, HD-iPSCs and SL-iPSCs appear to differ in certain respects including their purine metabolism and the consequences of HGprt deficiency. Finally, the absence of HGprt-mediated purine recycling has readily detectable metabolic consequences, but these consequences do not appear to impact cell viability or prevent neuronal differentiation up to D60 in this tissue culture model.

Purine metabolism during neuronal differentiation. Some cells in the body require more purines than others (Murray 1971; Garcia-Gil et al. 2018; Rodrigues et al. 2019; Pareek et al. 2021). Rapidly dividing cells need large amounts of purines to sustain DNA synthesis required for cell division and to sustain RNA synthesis necessary for protein translation and cell growth. By comparison, these demands are both lower in post-mitotic cells. This difference in need for purines may explain the lower levels of purines found in the most mature neurons compared to undifferentiated iPSCs (Fig. 3).

This change in purine content appears to come with a change in the sources of purines. All genes encoding enzymes in the de novo synthesis pathway dropped substantially, while HPRT1 increased. These changes are consistent with prior studies indicating that the de novo pathway is more active in rapidly dividing cells whereas the recycling pathway becomes more prominent in the post-mitotic state (Murray 1971; Garcia-Gil et al. 2018; Rodrigues et al. 2019; Pareek et al. 2021). The current findings in human iPSCs are consistent with recent studies showing a drop in de novo purine synthesis with a simultaneous rise in HGprt during early mouse brain development (Mizukoshi et al. 2023). They are also consistent with data regarding gene expression in the Human Brain Transcriptome, which show that expression of genes encoding enzymes of the de novo pathway are highest during early embryogenesis and decline after birth, while expression of HPRT1 is low early in embryogenesis and continues to rise for at least 2 years after birth (Fig. 4). These observations may explain why neurons are particularly vulnerable to the loss of HGprt-mediated purine recycling in LND.

HD-iPSCs vs. SL-iPSCs for disease modeling. In principle, the Disease Effect should be similar when using different Types of iPSCs. It has often been suggested that the SL-iPSC strategy may be preferable for disease modeling, because it limits inter-individual variance associated with different genetic backgrounds in HD-iPSCs. While variance may indeed be lower when comparing a gene-edited line to its starting parent line, SL-IPSCs from different starting stock lines have similar differences in genetic background as HD-iPSCs (Dinasarapu et al. 2025b).

In the current studies, certain pluripotency genes (NANOG, POU5F1 and ZFP42) were expressed at higher levels at D0 in the HD-iPSCs compared to the SL-iPSCs (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, the differentiation protocol produced different patterns for particular genes (e.g. WNT1, SHH) at different time points during differentiation of HD-iPSCs and SL-iPSCs (Fig. 1D). Perhaps the most notable difference was the purine content, which was higher in SL-iPSCs than in HD-iPSCs at certain time points. For example, ATP and GTP levels at D11 and D25 were approximately 2-fold higher when starting with SL-iPSCs compared to HD-iPSCs, with or without HPRT1 mutations (Fig. 3). Similarly, extracellular hypoxanthine at D0 and D11 was approximately 2-fold higher for SL-iPSCs compared to the HD-iPSCs. Differences between the HD-iPSCs and SL-iPSCs were also apparent for HGprt enzyme measurements at different time points. Enzyme activity increased at D60 of differentiation for the HD-iPSCs, but not for all SL-iPSCs (Fig. 2). Finally, transcriptome analyses revealed substantial differences in gene expression between the HD-iPSCs and SL-iPSCs for Day of Differentiation (Figs. 1 and 6).

These results confirm recent suggestions that disease modeling using the HD-iPSC and SL-iPSC strategies may not always yield the same result (Dinasarapu et al. 2025b). Some of the differences between the Type of iPSC may arise from batch effects, because it is technically challenging to differentiate multiple cell lines at the same time (8 HD-iPSCs and 6 SL-iPSCs) and then subject them to RNAseq or HPLC in a single batch. This technical limitation means that any Disease Effect must be sufficiently robust to be detectable despite the potential contributions to technical batch effects. For the Disease Effect, the loss of HGprt enzyme activity and elevations of hypoxanthine in tissue culture medium were clearly robust enough to be able to detect under nearly all conditions. Similarly robust changes for Disease Effect on dopamine or its metabolites could not be demonstrated. The absence of such an effect may mean that there is no Disease Effect in this model through D60, or that the Disease Effect was small in relation to technical variance.

Careful review of the data suggested that many of the differences between neurons derived from HD-iPSCs and SL-iPSCs might reflect the latter being slower to differentiate, even though the same protocol was used and the same time points were selected for analyses. For example, the HPRT1 gene and enzyme did not increase as much the latest time point (D60) in the SL-iPSC, compared to the HD-iPSCs (Figs. 1C and 2) and in the developing human brain (Fig. 4). In addition, genes associated with the most mature neuronal state (SLC18A2 and KCNJ6) were lower in the SL-iPSCs than the HD-iPSCs (regardless of Disease Effect) at D60. Finally, immunostains suggested that HD-iPSCs had more varicosities than the SL-iPSCs, a marker of potential synapse development (Fig. 5A, B). These differences may reflect differences in the abilities of these two types of iPSC lines to differentiate into neurons.

Consequences of HGprt deficiency. The lack of HGprt had apparently little impact on neuronal differentiation in this tissue culture model. Viability appeared to be normal for HGprt-deficient iPSCs, neural precursor cells, and neurons. There was no obvious Disease Effect in morphology or neurotransmitter content at D30 or D60. The HGprt-deficient neurons exhibited impaired hypoxanthine recycling as expected, but intracellular purines appeared unaffected. These results are consistent with prior studies of HGprt-deficient cells in culture. Similar to the current studies, several other studies have reported high extracellular hypoxanthine but normal intracellular purines in HGprt-deficient cells (Shirley et al. 2007). Since there are only two sources of purines for cells and purines are essential for cell survival, these observations imply that de novo purine synthesis must be sufficient to compensate for impairments in purine recycling in the current tissue culture model.

The transcriptome studies suggested that HPRT1 mutations had little impact on gene expression during neuronal differentiation in this model, at least through D60. One potential explanation is that the Disease Effect does not occur at the level of gene expression, but is mostly post-translational or metabolic. This explanation seems unlikely because post-translational and metabolic abnormalities often cause numerous secondary consequences that may impact gene expression, even when HPRT1 does not directly regulate gene expression. Another potential explanation is that there was insufficient statistical power to detect a Disease Effect because combining data across developmental time points introduces too much variance into the dataset, making it difficult to detect changes at specific time points. However, even when each time point was studied separately, there was little consistency in Disease Effect across HD-iPSCs and SL-iPSCs. A third explanation is that neurons produced in this model are still too immature to show any relevant disease effect, and that durations of differentiation longer than D60 may be needed to detect any Disease Effect. This possibility seems feasible, since some markers of neuronal maturation remained low even at D60 (e.g. SLC18A2 and KCNJ6), suggesting somewhat immature neurons (Fig. 1D). Further, the rise in HGprt even at D60 (Fig. 1C) in both Types of iPSCs was small in comparison to the rise seen in the human brain itself (Fig. 4). Finally, the abnormal dopamine phenotype does not emerge in knockout mice until postnatal day 14–30, suggesting that it may be a relatively late event. If this is the case, then it may be difficult to model the Disease Effect for dopamine neurons without very long periods of differentiation. While it is feasible to differentiate neurons in this model for longer periods, most studies using this model stop at D25-D60 because it is technically challenging to study very long durations, and there is progressive cell loss suggesting reduced cell viability. One final possible explanation for the lack of a Disease Effect on gene expression is that cultures are heterogeneous, with varying proportions of dopamine neurons, depending on how a “dopamine neuron” is defined. In this case, any abnormality restricted to dopamine neurons could be masked by the absence of changes in other cells.

On the other hand, the current studies also indicate that there was little apparent Disease Effect for more specific measures of dopamine neurons. This finding was surprising, since numerous prior studies have documented reductions in dopamine content for many HGprt deficient cells including rat PC12 pheochromocytoma cells (Bitler and Howard 1986), mouse MN9D neurohybridoma cells (Egami et al. 2007; Lewers et al. 2008), mouse primary neurons in culture (Smith and Friedmann 2000), and human neural stem cells from an aborted human fetus (Cristini et al. 2010). There are several potential explanations why the HGprt-deficient iPSC-derived neurons had an apparently normal dopamine neurotransmitter phenotype, and these reasons are similar to those outlined above for the transcriptome. Additionally, another potential reason for absence of any apparent Disease Effect might be that the wrong target outcome measures were selected for dopamine neurons, and there might be abnormalities in neuronal physiology, calcium homeostasis, or synaptic microstructure. This explanation seems unlikely, because dopamine levels have been most prominently affected in other studies and the transcriptome covers many additional functions, while there is no evidence from prior studies that HGprt deficiency has any effect on neuronal physiology, calcium, homeostasis, or synapse structure. Another reason could be that HGprt only affects certain subtypes of dopamine neurons (Jinnah et al. 1994, 1999), and the differentiation protocol used may have generated a subtype of dopamine neurons (Tiklova et al. 2019; Cardoso and Levesque 2020; Garritsen et al. 2023) that is unaffected. A fourth reason is that the artificial tissue culture environment compensates for whatever is happening to dopamine neuron differentiation in vivo, a phenomenon established for other types of cells in culture (Lopez et al. 2020; Torres et al. 2025). A final reason for apparently normal dopamine differentiation could be that iPSC-derived dopamine neurons are not mature enough, as noted above for the transcriptome. Further work involving longer culture periods or other more suitable models for studying later stages of development, or changes to the tissue culture environment may be required to determine if a relevant Disease Effect can be replicated in iPSC-derived dopamine neurons.
