Inner-Bond-Cleavage Approach to Figure-Eight Macrocycles from Planar Aromatic Hydrocarbons
Authors: Reiji Yoshina, Junichiro Hirano, Emiko Nishimoto, Yuki Sakamoto, Keita Tajima, Shunsuke Minabe, Muhammet Uyanik, Kazuaki Ishihara, Tomoyuki Ikai, Eiji Yashima, Takuya Omine, Fumitaka Ishiwari, Akinori Saeki, Jinseok Kim, Juwon Oh, Dongho Kim, Guanting Liu, Takuma Yasuda, Hiroshi Shinokubo, Norihito Fukui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Figure-eight-shaped nonplanar π-systems adopt distinctive chiral D2-symmetric structures, which are ideal for realizing efficient circularly polarized luminescence (CPL). However, the short-step and enantioselective synthesis of figure-eight π-systems represents a considerable challenge for the conventional bottom-up synthetic strategy. Herein, we report that the oxidative cleavage of the internal double bond of a commercially available polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon, i.e., dibenzo[g,p]chrysene (DBC), catalytically affords a figure-eight electron-accepting macrocycle, i.e., cyclobisbiphenylenecarbonyl (CBBC), with high scalability (up to 3.3 g) and excellent enantioselectivity (94% ee). This inner-bond-cleavage approach also applies to larger PAHs, affording highly distorted molecular frameworks that comprise two figure-eight subunits. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the peripheral functionalization of CBBC with carbazole afforded donor–acceptor-type emitter, which shows thermally activated delayed fluorescence and emits CPL with a g value of 1.0 × 10–2. This g value is ten times higher than those of previously reported chiral TADF-active emitters for circularly polarized organic light-emitting diodes. These results demonstrate that oxidative inner-bond cleavage is a powerful synthetic strategy for creating innovative materials that incorporate molecules with figure-eight structures.

Introduction

Most organic molecules have been synthesized via the stepwise formation of bonds between constituent fragments of the target compound, namely the bottom-up approach. Among organic molecules, sp2-carbon-rich compounds with distinct three-dimensional shapes, such as spheres, bowls, helices, and hoops, represent an important class of compounds due to the characteristic functionalities derived from their inherent structural features (Figure 1).1−6 However, creating these nonplanar molecules via the bottom-up approach is intrinsically challenging as sp2-hybridized carbon atoms usually adopt a planar geometry. Several elegant strategies have already been developed to circumvent this obstacle, including template-directed synthesis,7 reductive elimination from planar organometallic intermediates,8 and preconstruction of the main framework using sp3-hybridized carbon atoms.9−11 Nevertheless, several classes of complex molecules remain difficult to access via the bottom-up approach.

Known examples of readily available sp2-carbon-rich compounds with nonplanar structures.

Nonplanar aromatic macrocycles with a figure-eight conformation are conformationally different from conventional nontwisted cyclic molecules12 due to their characteristic 360°-twisted macrocyclic conjugation.13−24 Their highly symmetric chiral structures with D2-symmetry are distinctive compared to common chiral nonplanar π-systems such as binaphthyls and helicenes. Furthermore, this symmetry is ideal for realizing efficient circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) due to the parallel arrangements of the electric and magnetic transition dipole moments.22−24 Nevertheless, the availability of figure-eight π-systems has been limited by the complexity of their synthesis, as the construction of the central cross-linkage through bottom-up approaches often requires multistep transformations to avoid undesired side reactions (Figure 2a). Moreover, the enantioselective synthesis of figure-eight π-systems remains unprecedented except for two examples based on the rhodium-catalyzed [2 + 2 + 2] cyclization of precisely designed precursors.25,26

Synthesis of figure-eight aromatic macrocycles.

Herein, we demonstrate that oxidative inner-bond cleavage represents an effective strategy for the synthesis of figure-eight π-systems (Figure 2b). The catalytic oxidative cleavage of an internal double bond of the commercially available polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH), dibenzo[g,p]chrysene (DBC; 1a, R = H), affords the figure-eight electron-accepting aromatic macrocycle, cyclobisbiphenylenecarbonyl (CBBC; 2a, R = H), with high scalability (up to 3.3 g) and excellent enantioselectivity (94% ee). The development of internal-modification strategies is an emerging research area in organic synthesis; representative examples of such strategies are molecular surgery27 and skeletal editing.28 The construction of the central ten-membered ring of CBBC via a conventional bottom-up approach would be difficult, as the intramolecular reductive homocoupling of 2,2′-dihalobenzophenone tends to occur instead of the desired intermolecular dimerization.29 Although 2a was first reported in 1934, its synthesis required a stoichiometric amount of toxic sodium dichromate in refluxing acetic acid.30 To date, only two CBBC derivatives have been reported over the last 90 years.31,32 Furthermore, the fundamental physical properties and functionalization of CBBC 2a and its derivatives have not been investigated so far.

Results and Discussion

Catalytic Oxidative Inner-Bond Cleavage

We began our study by establishing a rational strategy to synthesize CBBC 2a from DBC 1a under mild conditions without the use of toxic heavy metals. This goal was achieved by employing internally hydroxy-substituted DBC derivative 3a (R = H) as an intermediate (Scheme 1; reactions A and B). The ruthenium-catalyzed cis-dihydroxylation33 of DBC 1a with NaIO4 as a terminal oxidant proceeded smoothly at room temperature to afford 3a in 95% yield (reaction A). The organoiodine(III)-catalyzed oxidative glycol cleavage34 of 3a proceeded smoothly using a catalytic amount of iodobenzene and a stoichiometric amount of m-chloroperoxybenzoic acid (m-CPBA) to furnish CBBC 2a in 92% yield (reaction B). Importantly, both reactions A and B can be readily scaled up to provide 8.7 g of 3a and 3.3 g of 2a, respectively, without decreasing the chemical yield.

With this new strategy in hand, we explored the scope using peripherally functionalized DBC derivatives (Scheme 1). Alkyl (2b, 2c) and aryl (2d) substituents are compatible (43–80% over two steps). The introduction of electron-withdrawing carbonyl groups does not retard the reactions, and 2e was obtained in 86% yield over two steps. Various brominated DBC derivatives also engaged in this transformation and furnished the corresponding CBBCs 2f–2i in 63–86% yield over two steps. These brominated CBBC derivatives are valuable precursors for the construction of elaborate frameworks. To our delight, the inner-bond-cleavage strategy is also applicable to the larger PAHs 4 and 5. The oxidative bond cleavage of 4 and 5 proceeded selectively at two internal C–C bonds to furnish 6 and 7 in 63% and 59% yield, respectively. We further confirmed that the treatment of substrates 1g, 1h, and 5 under the previously reported conditions using sodium dichromate in refluxing acetic acid did not afford the corresponding bond-cleaved products, which emphasizes the utility of the present procedure. However, the present protocol was not applicable to electron-rich DBC derivatives with methoxy or N-carbazolyl groups. Benzothiophene-substituted DBC and its S,S-dioxide derivative also did not participate in this protocol. In these cases, the desired diols were not obtained in the ruthenium-catalyzed cis-dihydroxylation.

An X-ray diffraction analysis of single crystals of 6 and 7 revealed their remarkably distorted structures with two CBBC subunits (Figure 3). Compound 6 adopts a chiral S-shaped structure, in which a pair of CBBC units with the same configuration share one benzene ring. Compound 7 exhibits a chiral helical structure, in which two CBBC subunits with the same configuration share a biphenyl unit. The twisting angle between the two phenyl–phenyl linkages at both ends is very large (270°) despite their short distance (6.2 Å), thus creating a short pitch (8.3 Å). The highly distorted structures of 6 and 7 would be difficult to construct via conventional bottom-up approaches.

The selectivity of the inner-bond-cleavage reactions of 4 and 5 can be explained by Clar’s rule,35 which predicts higher double-bond character for the oxidatively cleaved inner C–C bonds of 4 and 5. We corroborated this empirical assumption by conducting density functional theory (DFT) calculations for 4 and 5. A relatively large bond-length alternation in the six-membered rings that contain the cleaved C–C bonds was observed, indicating weak local aromaticity (Figure S77).36

The intermediate cis-diol 3a adopts a chiral double-helicene structure (Figure 4),37 which prompted us to examine the oxidative kinetic resolution of 3a. Treatment of racemic 3a with 0.5 equiv of m-CPBA in the presence of chiral iodoarene Ar*I(38) at 0 °C afforded (P,P)-2a in 41% isolated yield with an excellent enantiomeric excess (94% ee) and 54% recovery of diol 3a (Scheme 1, reaction C). The absolute configuration of (P,P)-2a was determined as described in the Supporting Information. Importantly, heating of the recovered diol 3a can revert it to a racemic mixture due to the small racemization barrier (t1/2 = 36 h at 25 °C),37 indicating that repeating the oxidative kinetic resolution of the racemic diol and the racemization of the recovered diol can eventually convert 100% of racemic substrate 3a into the enantio-enriched CBBC 2a. Subsequently, we explored the scope of this oxidative kinetic resolution. Diols with alkyl (3b, 3c), aryl (3d), or carbonyl (3e) substituents were transformed into the corresponding CBBC derivatives in moderate-to-good yield (23%–38%) with good-to-excellent enantioselectivity (85%–96% ee). The reactions of brominated diols (3f–3i) also proceeded with moderate-to-high enantioselectivity (up to 80% ee), albeit in low yield (3%–21%), due to the low solubility of the diols under these conditions. Furthermore, this protocol is applicable to the larger PAH 5, which afforded compound 7 in 15% yield over two steps with good enantioselectivity (71% ee). Although the enantioselective oxidation of diols to hydroxyketones using a chiral hypervalent iodine has already been reported,39−41 the enantioselective glycol cleavage has not been disclosed. We have also attempted the dynamic kinetic resolution of 3a with 1.2 equiv of m-CPBA at 60 °C because the racemization barrier of 3a is not high. However, the enantiomeric excess of the obtained CBBC 2a decreased to be less than 30% ee.

Properties of CBBC

A single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis revealed that 2a exhibits a figure-eight structure identical to that in a previous report (Figure S55).42 In the single crystal, 2a crystallizes in the C2221 (no. 20) space group, which is a Sohncke group. We randomly chose four single crystals and determined their enantiomeric excesses to be >99% ee. These results indicate that spontaneous resolution occurs during the crystallization of 2a, thus representing a rare example of conglomerate formation. The strain energy of 2a was estimated to be 15.5 kcal mol–1 using DFT calculations at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) level based on a hypothetical homodesmotic reaction (Figure S76).

The activation barrier of the racemization of 2a was evaluated by monitoring the decrease in the circular dichroism (CD) signals upon heating enantiomerically pure 2a. This analysis afforded an activation enthalpy (ΔH) of 29.8 kcal mol–1 and an activation entropy (ΔS) of −11 cal K–1 mol–1 (Figures S65 and S66). The racemization of 2a was further simulated using DFT calculations (Figure 5; Movie S1). The figure-eight conformer A untwists to adopt the distorted ring conformation B with the two carbonyl groups rotated inward in opposite directions. The chiral intermediate B further isomerizes to achiral conformer C via a small activation barrier (1.9 kcal mol–1). Subsequent isomerization finally affords the opposite enantiomer with a figure-eight conformation. The calculated overall activation barrier for the racemization (33.7 kcal mol–1) agrees closely with the experimentally determined value. These results indicate that 2a is configurationally stable at ambient temperature.

The UV/vis absorption of CBBC 2a in the 300–380 nm range is stronger than the absorption corresponding to the n−π* transition in benzophenone (Figure S67). This is due to the contribution of the π–π* transition in 2a that arises from the π-extension along the biphenyl subunits (Figure S74). While 2a is virtually nonemissive in CH2Cl2 at room temperature, it exhibits phosphorescence with a lifetime of 1.3 × 102 ms in 2-methyltetrahydrofuran (2-MeTHF) at 77 K (Figure S67). Transient absorption spectroscopy measurements indicated that intersystem crossing (ISC) proceeds rapidly with a time constant of 3 ps (Figure S70). The CD and circularly polarized phosphorescence (CP-Phos) spectra of (M,M)-2a and (P,P)-2a are shown in Figure 6. The |g| value of CP-Phos reaches 3.3 × 10–2 at 447 nm, which is higher than those of previously reported chiral organic phosphors.43 2,7,10,15-Tetra(p-tolyl)CBBC 2j, which was synthesized via the Suzuki–Miyaura cross-coupling reaction of 2h with 4-methylphenylboronic acid, exhibited fluorescence at 482 nm in CH2Cl2 with a quantum yield of 3.7% and a lifetime of 6.4 ns (Figure S68). Its enantiomers display intense circularly polarized fluorescence (CP-Flu) with a high |g| value of 1.4 × 10–2 (Figure 7), which is comparable to those of previously reported efficient organic CP-Flu emitters.44 TD-DFT calculations of 2j revealed that their electric and magnetic transition dipole moments adopt parallel arrangements due to their D2-symmetric structures (Figure S75).22−24 These results demonstrate that CBBC is an ideal skeleton for CPL-active materials.

Functionalization of CBBC

Research on organic CPL-emitters that show thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) has actively been pursued in response to the growing demand for low-cost, metal-free, and efficient circularly polarized organic light-emitting diodes (CP-OLEDs), which are useful for cutting-edge applications including 3D displays and security printing.45 To date, various chiral elements have been examined for the design of TADF-active CPL-emitters, including point chirality, axial chirality, and planar chirality.46−48 However, the CPL g values of the resulting molecules in solution, which is preferable to the solid-state study due to the suppressed overestimation,49 remain low (typically 1 × 10–3–3 × 10–3). On the other hand, carbazole-substituted donor–acceptor-type (D–A-type) benzophenone derivatives exhibit TADF-activity,50 which naturally led us to envision a carbazole-substituted D–A-type CBBC derivative.

Treatment of 2a with N-iodosuccinimide (NIS) in conc. H2SO4 afforded 2,7,10,15-tetraiodo derivative 8 in 58% yield (Figure 8a). A Buchwald–Hartwig amination of 8 with carbazole at 120 °C afforded 2,7,10,15-tetra(N-carbazolyl)CBBC 9 in 59% yield. The enantiomerically pure starting material (P,P)-2a was also converted to (P,P)-9 via the same transformations without loss of enantiomeric excess. The enantiomers of rac-9 were separated using HPLC. A single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis unambiguously determined the structure of 9, which adopts a figure-eight core conformation (Figure S62).

Synthesis and properties of D–A-type CBBC derivative 9. (a) Synthesis of 9. (b) CD (solid) and CPL (dashed) spectra of (M,M)-9 and (P,P)-9. (c) CPL g values of (M,M)-9 and (P,P)-9. (d) Electroluminescence characteristics of OLED devices based on rac-9.

The UV/vis absorption spectrum of rac-9 in toluene exhibits a broad peak at 413 nm tailing to approximately 480 nm (Figure S69). Photoexcitation afforded green emission at 515 nm with a quantum yield of 22%. The CPL spectra of the enantiomers of 9 in toluene exhibited clear CPL with a maximum |g| value of 1.0 × 10–2 (Figure 8b,c). Drop-cast films of the enantiomers of 9 in 3,3′-di(9H-carbazol-9-yl)-1,1′-biphenyl (mCBP) also showed clear CPL spectra with a high |g| value of 8.4 × 10–3 (Figure S69). The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry indicate that the heating of rac-9 results in decomposition around 400 °C without phase transition (Figure S80). We then fabricated vacuum-deposited films consisting of 5 wt % rac-9 in mCBP (Figures S76 and S77). Photoexcitation of the obtained film resulted in emission at 517 nm with a high quantum yield of 63%. This film showed TADF activity, as corroborated by the small singlet–triplet energy gap (ΔEST = 0.15 eV) and a delayed emission component with a lifetime of 40.9 μs. We also fabricated OLED devices using rac-9 (Figure 8d). Electrical excitation of the film afforded green emission (CIE: x, y = 0.23, 0.58) with a maximum external quantum efficiency (EQEmax) of 5.4%. High EQE values, i.e., beyond 5%, are characteristic of TADF-active emitters. The g value of the CPL of 9 in solution is higher than those of previously reported TADF-active CPL-emitters,46−49 which emphasizes the advantage of employing a figure-eight structure for the design of practical CP-OLED materials.

Conclusions

We have developed a catalytic enantioselective synthesis to obtain CBBC 2a from DBC 1a via internally hydroxy-substituted DBC derivative 3a. This oxidative inner-bond-cleavage strategy is applicable to peripherally functionalized DBC derivatives and PAHs. This new synthetic strategy has enabled detailed systematic investigations into the chemistry of CBBC, including an exploration of its fundamental physical properties and functionalization. This exploration revealed that CBBC is configurationally stable even at high temperature and emits efficient CPL with high g values. The peripheral functionalization of CBBC with carbazole afforded D–A-type emitter 9, which shows TADF activity and emits CPL with a high g value of 1.0 × 10–2. These results demonstrate that oxidative inner-bond cleavage is a powerful strategy for creating innovative materials that incorporate molecules with figure-eight structures.
