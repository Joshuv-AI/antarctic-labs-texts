LIKE EARLY STARVATION is involved in the regulation of starch initiation in potato tubers (Solanum tuberosum cv. Désirée)
Authors: Camille Locquet, Mara Berti, Fabrice Bray, Hyazann Hulin, Gregory Stoclet, David Seung, Denis Lourdin, Christophe D’Hulst, Fabrice Wattebled, Nicolas Szydlowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStarch initiation has been intensively investigated, and in tubers of Solanum tuberosum it is controlled by a heteromultimeric isoamylase (ISA) complex composed of ISA1 and ISA2. In Arabidopsis, two non-catalytic starch binding proteins regulate leaf starch metabolism, namely EARLY STARVATION 1 (ESV1) and LIKE EARLY STARVATION 1 (LESV). These proteins both interact with starch glucans through a C-terminal antiparallel β-sheet domain and differ by the presence of a 115 amino acid N-terminal overhang in LESV. Here, we used CRIPSR/Cas9 to inactivate LESV or ESV1 in potato and characterized the resulting tuber starches. Starch from esv1 mutants was unaffected whereas granule diameter was severely reduced in tubers of lesv plants. This was negatively correlated with the number of granules, consistent with an altered starch-initiation phenotype. Strikingly, SEM of purified starches revealed that lesv plants phenocopy isa1 and isa2 antisense lines described 20 years ago. Using yeast two-hybrid assays, we confirmed that LESV interacts with ISA1 in potato, as previously demonstrated in rice, providing molecular evidence for their functional association in potato. This interaction supports the hypothesis that LESV acts in concert with the ISA1/ISA2 complex, probably regulating the early steps of glucan organization required for proper starch granule formation.

Introduction

Starch is composed of two glucose polymers, amylose and amylopectin, with the latter representing about 80% of the starch weight in potato (Solanum tuberosum; Fredriksson et al., 1998). Amylose, which is essentially linear, consists of glucose residues linked by α-1,4 O-glycosidic bonds whereas amylopectin is a branched glucan characterized by glucose residues linked by α-1,4 O-glycosidic bonds with branching points formed by α-1,6 O-glycosidic bonds connecting the linear chains together. In potato tubers, amylose and amylopectin are arranged to form a single, large semicrystalline granule within the amyloplast (Kram et al., 1993). Despite the apparent simplicity of its structure, only consisting of glucose residues and two types of bonds, starch metabolism is in fact a complex process involving a wide range of proteins. Amylose is mainly synthesized by Granule Bound Starch Synthase (GBSS), which is the most abundant starch-bound protein (Vos-Scheperkeuter et al., 1986; Ball et al., 1998). In contrast, the synthesis of amylopectin involves a diverse array of enzymes that, beyond the ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase) that is shared with amylose synthesis for generating the ADP-glucose precursor, includes soluble starch synthases (SSs), starch branching enzymes (BEs), starch debranching enzymes (DBEs), and the starch dikinases Glucan Water Dikinase (GWD) and Phosphoglucan Water Dikinase (PWD) (Reyniers et al., 2020).

Starch initiation in plants has been intensively investigated in recent years. Due to the availability of efficient molecular toolboxes to study this process, a particular focus has been on transitory starch from the model plant Arabidopsis (Seung and Smith, 2019). In the last two decades, several proteins have been demonstrated to participate in the granule initiation process in chloroplasts (Seung and Smith, 2019; Mérida and Fettke, 2021; Vandromme et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2024). Protein Targeting to Starch 2 (PTST2) binds both to soluble maltooligosaccharide (MOS) primers and Starch Synthase 4 (SS4) (Seung et al., 2017). The latter elongates MOSs further, prior to the action of the other synthesizing enzymes (i.e. SSs, Bes, and DBEs) (Roldán et al., 2007; Seung et al., 2016; Lu et al., 2018). This protein–glucan initiation complex also includes MRC/Pii1 (Myosin-Resembling Chloroplast Protein/Protein Involved In Starch Initiation 1). PTST2 is recruited by the thylakoid-bound protein Mar-binding Filament-Like Protein 1 (MFP1) to specific areas of the stroma in the vicinity of thylakoids, where it develops starch granules (Seung et al., 2018; Vandromme et al., 2019; Sharma et al., 2024). Starch initiation in amyloplasts might involve some of the aforementioned proteins, although the function of SS4 remains unclear and the concentration/location of the initiation components most likely differs from chloroplasts due to the absence of thylakoids (Satoh et al., 2008; Toyosawa et al., 2016; Jung et al., 2018; Seung and Smith, 2019).

For potato tubers, altered starch initiation has only been reported for lines with antisense inhibition of the debranching enzymes ISOAMYLASE 1 (ISA1) or 2 (ISA2) (Bustos et al., 2004). Both proteins form a heteromultimeric complex in all species, although ISA1 can also act alone as a homomultimer in some species, such as maize and rice (Hussain et al., 2003; Utsumi and Nakamura, 2006; Kubo et al., 2010; Facon et al., 2013). The ISA1–ISA2 complex is responsible for hydrolysing some of the α-1,6 linkages to organize a distribution of branch points compatible with amylopectin phase transition (Ball et al., 1996). Inactivation of the complex in Arabidopsis leaves and cereal endosperm leads to the accumulation of branched soluble glucans called ‘phytoglycogen’ (Delatte et al., 2005; Wattebled et al., 2005, 2008; Streb et al., 2009). In potato, reduced ISA1/ISA2 activity leads to more limited phytoglycogen accumulation in some antisense lines, but a large increase in granule numbers per amyloplast and a strong decrease in starch granule diameter, suggesting an impaired regulation of starch initiation (Bustos et al., 2004). The proposed mechanism relies on the capacity of ISA1–ISA2 to regulate the number of starch granules per amyloplast by limiting the initiation of new glucans precursors.

More recent studies in Arabidopsis have revealed the presence of two non-catalytic proteins, EARLY STARVATION 1 (ESV1) and LIKE EARLY STARVATION 1 (LESV). Despite the lack of known catalytic domains, ESV1 plays a crucial role in regulating transitory starch degradation in Arabidopsis leaves (Feike et al., 2016). Starch turnover is impaired in the rosettes of the esv1 mutant with faster starch degradation at night, whereas no modifications of the starch content over the day/night cycle are observed in lesv mutants. On the other hand, LESV-overexpressing plants exhibit a 3-fold decrease in the starch content during the night. Granules from esv1 and lesv mutants display irregular shapes compared to wild-type starch, whilst granule size is decreased and increased in LESV- and ESV1-overexpressors, respectively. The number of starch granules per chloroplast is also increased in LESV-overexpressing plants. Furthermore, recombinant Arabidopsis ESV1 and LESV proteins have been shown to decrease the activity of GWD and Starch Phosphorylase (PHS1) and enhance the activity of SS1, SS3, β-amylase, and isoamylase on starch granules in vitro (Singh et al., 2022). LESV and ESV1 share a highly similar C-terminal region organized in an antiparallel β-sheet domain that harbors alignments of Trp residues. To date, all the studies on these proteins have shown physical interactions with starch granules, which are proposed to rely on these aligned residues (Feike et al., 2016; Singh et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Osman et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2024). Both recombinant proteins from Arabidopsis have been shown to interact with amylopectin in EMSAs and synchrotron radiation circular dichroism analysis (Osman et al., 2024). Interestingly, the LESV amino-acid sequence contains an N-terminal extension that is absent from ESV1, and the conformation of this extension is influenced by amylopectin binding in vitro (Osman et al., 2024). By combining synthetic biology in yeast, and structural biology and functional genomics in Arabidopsis, it has recently been proposed that LESV and ESV1 promote and stabilize the amylopectin phase transition, respectively (Liu et al., 2023). The authors established a model in which LESV promotes the amylopectin phase transition by helping align double-helices, and ESV1 stabilizes the semi-crystalline lamellae that are formed.

More recently, the rice (Oryza sativa) floury endosperm9 (flo9) mutant, which harbors a phenotype similar to that of isa1 mutants (i.e. defective in amylopectin synthesis and having a floury endosperm), has been shown to be deficient in the ortholog of Arabidopsis LESV (Yan et al., 2024). Yeast two-hybrid, in vitro pulldown, and in vivo firefly luciferase complementation assays revealed direct interaction between the rice LESV and ISA1 polypeptides, which domain-truncation analysis attributed to the N-terminus region of LESV. Notably, the interaction between rice ESV1 and ISA1 was not tested in this study; however, interaction was also reported between LESV and PTST2, and both proteins were proposed to act synergistically as cargos for controlling starch biosynthesis and endosperm development by targeting ISA1 to starch. In a previous study using nanoLC-nanoESI-MS/MS, we identified LESV as one of the major starch-binding proteins after GBSS in potato tubers (Helle et al., 2018). ESV1 was also observed in this analysis, but with a much lower abundance. Interestingly, both proteins were unaffected by extended detergent treatment or proteolytic shaving of the starch granule surface.

Postulating that the presence of LESV and ESV1 within the granule indicates a role in tuber starch metabolism, in this current study we used CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing to investigate their function in potato. We found that while esv1 mutants did not show any change in granule size and shape, lesv lines displayed a dramatic decrease of granule diameter and the accumulation of small granule aggregates, indicating deregulated starch initiation. Detailed characterization of the starch ultrastructure did not reveal any change in either mutant apart from an increase in phosphate content and a propensity to amylase digestibility in the lesv mutant, probably as a consequence of reduced mean granule diameter. Strikingly, the lesv phenotype is a phenocopy of isa1 and isa2 antisense potato lines described over 20 years ago by Bustos et al. (2004). In the present study, we confirmed by yeast two-hybrid assays that potato LESV and ISA1 physically interact, similar to the previous findings for the rice proteins (Yan et al., 2024). Finally, the absence of the phenotype in the esv1 mutants highlights the critical role of the LESV-specific interaction in the regulation of starch initiation. Our results suggest that LESV regulates starch initiation in potato tubers by targeting ISA1 to new granule precursors, which in turn controls the number of starch granules per amyloplast by restricting the number of initiation events.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

The potato cultivar used in this study was Solanum tuberosum cv. Désirée. The genome of this cultivar has been resequenced by our laboratory, and a polymorphism map was generated at the genome scale (Sevestre et al., 2020). Plants were grown in vitro on Murashig and Skoog (MS) medium including vitamins and supplemented with 2% sucrose and 0.8% plant agar (Duchefa Biochemie) in a growth cabinet under a 16/8 h light/dark (80 µmol m−2 s−1) at 22/20 °C. Plants were maintained and propagated in vitro by cuttings every 6 weeks.

Selection of targets and plasmid constructs

Target sites adjacent to NGG protospacer adjacent motifs were identified for LESV using the Geneious software (https://www.geneious.com/) and the CRISPOR online tool (http://crispor.tefor.net/; Concordet and Haeussler, 2018). Single-guide RNA (sgRNA) was selected according to predicted specificity, efficiency, out-of-frame scores, and a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) map (Sevestre et al., 2020). The vector used for mutagenesis was pDIRECT_22A (Addgene, plasmid #91133), which contains a kanamycin resistance gene. Plasmid vectors were constructed according to Čermák et al. (2017). Briefly, oligonucleotides containing the sgRNA sequence were phosphorylated (see Supplementary Table S1 for primer sequences). The oligonucleotides were hybridized using a Golden Gate reaction, creating a double-strand DNA fragment with sticky ends complementary to the AarI restriction site, and introduced into the pDIRECT_22A vector digested by AarI. The plasmid construct was then multiplied by production in a chemically competent Escherichia coli TOP10 strain [genotype FmcrA Δ(mrr-hsdRMS-mcrBC) Φ80lacZΔM15 ΔlacX74 recA1 araD139 Δ(ara-leu) 7697 galU galK rpsL (StrR) endA1 nupG λ-) (Invitrogen)] and purified using a NucleoSpin Plasmid Kit (Macherey-Nagel).

Agrobacterium-mediated transformation

Agro-infection of potato was performed as described by Sevestre et al. (2020). Briefly, A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 was transformed with 1 µg of the plasmid construct containing the sgRNA prior to selection in Petri dishes on solid LB medium containing 50 mg l−1 of kanamycin as the selection agent. Potato explants between 3–4 mm in length were prepared from stems and petioles of 3–4-week-old plants cultivated in vitro. They were placed for 48 h on Petri dishes containing MS medium as described above but with the addition of 1 mg l−1 each of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), gibberellic acid (GA3), and zeatin-riboside (ZR) (Sigma Aldrich). The A. tumefaciens culture was adjusted to OD600=0.2 using MS medium supplemented by 2% sucrose, the explants were dipped into it for 30 min, and then placed back onto the same Petri dish and left in the dark for 48 h at 25 °C. Then, residual bacteria were removed and the explants were transferred onto Petri dishes containing MS medium including vitamins, 2% sucrose, and 0.8% plant agar supplemented with 1 mg l−1 each of IAA, GA3, and ZR, plus 500 mg l−1 of timentin and cefotaxime (Duchefa) and 50 mg l−1 of kanamycin. After 2 weeks in the growth cabinet under the conditions described above to allow the development of calli, the explants were then transferred onto Petri dishes containing the same medium with the IAA concentration reduced to 0.1 mg l−1 to allow the differentiation of the callus in stems. These stems were taken and placed in tubes containing MS medium including vitamins, 2% sucrose, and 0.8% plant agar plus 50 mg l−1 of kanamycin to allow the development of roots.

Characterization of induced mutations

To assess the mutation caused by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, DNA was extracted from leaves of 3-week-old regenerated plants following the method of Porebski et al. (1997). The presence of the Cas9 gene was checked by PCR using specific primers with GoTaq® G2 Flexi DNA Polymerase (Promega). Primers specific to the targeted sequence were used to assess the Cas9-induced mutations and PCR products were analysed by Sanger sequencing by Eurofins Genomics (https://eurofinsgenomics.eu/). All primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Genotype was determined by analysing the sequencing results using the Synthego ICE analysis tool (https://ice.editco.bio/#/). Knockout mutants were selected according to R2 and K.O. scores determined by the ICE analysis. The K.O. score is the proportion of deletions that led to a frameshift, while the R2 value indicates how well the proposed deletions fit the Sanger sequence data of the edited sample.

Tuber production and starch extraction

At 3 weeks old, in vitro cultured plants were transplanted into 3 l pots containing commercial soil, and placed in the growth cabinet under the conditions described above for ∼12 weeks. After 4 weeks of culture, ∼5 cm of soil was added to cover the bases of the stems. Plants were watered twice a week for 11 weeks and tubers were harvested 1 week after the last watering, during plant senescence. Images of the plants were taken every week. Three replicate plants were used for the following experiments, each from independent cultures. After washing with tap water, tubers (100–120 g) were crushed on ice with deionized water using a Polytron homogenizer. Starch was filtered through a nylon net with a 100 µm mesh (Sefar, Bury, UK). Two or three centrifugation steps were performed at 4000 g and 4 °C using deionized water until the water appeared clean. The supernatant obtained from the first centrifugation was collected, boiled at 100 °C for 10 min and then stored at −20 °C for subsequent measurement of the contents of glucose and water-soluble polysaccharides. Cellular debris were removed from the starch through a centrifugation step at 4000 g and 4 °C for 45 min in a Percoll density gradient (Cytiva). The starch suspensions were then stored in 20% ethanol at 4 °C for further analysis.

Starch, glucose, and water-soluble polysaccharide contents

Starch suspensions were subjected to in vitro digestion with amyloglucosidase (AGS) from Aspergillus niger (Megazyme) for 30 min at 58 °C, after which the starch content was measured using an Enzytec™ Starch kit according to the manufacturer’s specifications. The content of the soluble extracts was also analyzed using the same kit, both before and after digestion with AGS, to measure the glucose and water-soluble polysaccharides contents, respectively.

Label-free quantitative proteomics

Briefly, 15 µg of proteins from starch were digested with enhanced Filter Aided Sample Preparation (eFASP) and analysis of peptides was performed on a Hybrid Quadrupole-OrbitrapTM Q ExactiveTM Plus Mass Spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) as previously described (Helle et al., 2018, 2019). Proteins were quantified with the Maxquant software 2.0.3.0 (https://maxquant.org/) with a custom Solanum tuberosum database from the Potato Genome Sequencing Consortium Public Data Release (52 570 sequences; https://spuddb.uga.edu/pgsc_download.shtml). The restriction method was Trypsin/P and allowed up to two missing cleavages; the minimum length of the peptide was set to six amino acid residues; the mass error tolerance of the primary precursor ion of the first search and the main search was set to 20 ppm and 5 ppm, respectively. The mass error tolerance of the secondary fragment ion was 0.02 Da. Cysteine alkylation was set as a fixed modification, variable modifications were methionine oxidation, protein N-terminal acetylation, deamidation (NQ).

Granulomorphometry

Starch granule size and shape were determined using a Flow-Cell FC200S+granulomorphometer (Occhio). A filter was applied to exclude particles with a diameter smaller than 4 µm because of the limits of the camera resolution and a roundness less than 0.5 to exclude cellular debris. An average of 3000 granules after filtration was analysed for each sample. Size and shape were recorded as the ISO area diameter and ellipsoid roundness parameters.

SEM and optical microscopy

Purified starch granules were fixed on an aluminum support with carbon double-sided tape and observed at 1 kV accelerating voltage with a Zeiss Merlin VP SEM.

For optical microscopy, transverse tuber slices of ∼1 mm thickness were cut with a kitchen slicer, placed on a microscope slide, and observed using an upright A1R Nikon confocal microscope with a Plan Apo ×60 Oil (NA=1.4) objective, using bright-field illumination.

For observation of purified starch granules, suspensions were first subjected to five washes with 20% sodium dodecylsulphate (SDS) or four sessions of sonication at 60% for 10 s, using a Sonifier Cell Disruptor B-30 (Branson). Purified starch granules were observed under transmitted light microscopy using a Leica AF6000 LX microscope with a Plan Apo ×100 Oil (NA=1.4) objective with differential interference contrast. The birefringence of purified starch granules was assessed under polarized light using a Nikon Eclipse TE2000-E microscope with the Plan Apo ×60 Oil objective.

Assay of phosphorylated glucose residues in starch

The quantification of phosphorylated glucose residues was carried out according to Verbeke et al. (2016). Briefly, 1 mg of starch was hydrolysed in 100 µl of 2 M trifluoroacetic acid at 95 °C for 80 min, after which 20 µl of the hydrolysate was added to 200 µl of ultrapure water and dried at room temperature in a speed-vac. After the addition of 200 µl of ultrapure water, the drying step was repeated. The samples were labeled using tetrahydrofuran and 8-aminopyrene-13,6-trisulfonic acid as described below, resulting in a sample volume of 4 µl. Then, 96 µl of ultrapure water was added and 15 µl of the resulting solution was further diluted in 185 µl of ultrapure water and injected into a Beckman Coulter PA800-plus Pharmaceutical Analysis System equipped with a laser-induced fluorescence detector (Verbeke et al., 2016).

Wide-angle X-ray scattering

X-ray diffraction was used to identify the type of crystalline structure and to quantify the crystalline/amorphous structure ratio in the starch samples. Before analysis, the starch water content was stabilized in a desiccator containing a saturated NaCl solution with a relative humidity of 75% for 1 week at room temperature. Then, 50 mg of sample was sealed in a copper ring between two sheets of adhesive tape to prevent any change in water content during analysis. Two-dimensional diffraction diagrams were recorded using a D8 X-ray diffractometer (Bruker) equipped with a GADDS 2D detector. X-ray radiation of Cu Kα1 (λ=0.15406 nm), produced in a sealed tube at 40 kV and 40 mA, was selected and parallelized using crossed Göbel mirrors and collimated to produce a 500 µm beam diameter. Data were monitored by the GADDS detector for 10 min and normalized.

Small-angle X-ray scattering

Measurements of small-angle X-ray scattering were performed on a Xeuss 2.0 apparatus (Xenocs) equipped with point collimation (beam size: 300×300 μm2) and a micro source using Cu Kα radiation (λ=0.15406 nm). The sample-to-detector distance was calibrated using silver behenate as a standard and was ∼150 cm for the measurements, and 2D patterns were recorded on a Pilatus 200k detector (DECTRIS). Integrated intensity profiles were computed from the 2D patterns using the Foxtrot® software. The lamellar repeat distance, d, representing the alternative crystalline and amorphous lamellae, was calculated by the Bragg equation: d=2π/qmax.

Maximum wavelength of starch–iodine complex

Samples of 2 mg of starch were solubilized in 100% DMSO by heating for 15 min at 100 °C. After cooling down, they were diluted 100 times and 80 µl of the dilution was added to 20 µl of iodine solution [I2 0.2% (w/v) and KI 2% (w/v)] and the absorbance was measured between 500–700 nm using a SPECTROstar Nano spectrometer (BMG LABTECH) to determine the wavelength at the maximum absorbance of the starch–iodine complex.

Determination of amylose content

To assess the amylose content in the samples, starch was fractionated using exclusion size chromatography to separate amylose and amylopectin molecules. A sample of 1.5 mg of starch was first gelatinized in 200 µl of DMSO by heating for 15 min at 100 °C, and then precipitated in 800 µl of 100% ethanol to remove lipids. After 10 min of centrifugation at 10 000 g and 4 °C, the starch was resolubilized in 300 µl of 10 mM sodium hydroxide, and then loaded on a column (0.5×65 cm) containing Sepharose CL-2B resin (Cytiva) equilibrated with 10 mM sodium hydroxide. The flow rate was equilibrated at 12 ml h−1 and fractions of 300 µl were collected. Then, 80 µl of each fraction was mixed with 20 µl of iodine solution [I2 0.2% (w/v) and KI 2% (w/v) and analysed by spectrophotometry between 500–700 nm. The fractions containing either amylose or amylopectin were pooled and the amount of each glucan was measured using an Enzytec™ Starch kit.

Chain-length distribution

The starch chain length distribution was measured according to Helle et al. (2018). Briefly, 2 mg of starch were gelatinized in 250 µl of ultrapure water by heating at 100 °C with regular stirring and it was then debranched at 42 °C overnight with 20 U of isoamylase and 1 U of pullulanase (Sigma). The samples were then desalted on Alltech Carbograph columns and freeze-dried. The dried samples were labeled using 2 µl of cyanoborohydride in tetrahydrofuran and 2 µl of 200 mM 8-aminopyrene-13,6-trisulfonic acid in 15% acetic acid and incubated at 42 °C overnight. Then, 46 µl of ultrapure water was added and the samples were diluted 50 times with ultrapure water before being analysed in a Beckman Coulter PA800-plus Pharmaceutical Analysis System equipped with a laser-induced fluorescence detector, according to Fermont et al. (2022).

In vitro enzymatic hydrolysis of starch

The enzymatic degradation of starch from the lesv lines was assayed by measuring the production of reducing glucose using 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS). The enzyme used to hydrolyse starch was porcine pancreatic α-amylase (PPA) (Megazyme), with 128 U/ml of PPA being suspended in a buffer consisting of 20 mM phosphate, 0.02% sodium azide, 2 mM NaCl, and 0.25 mM CaCl2, pH 7.0. Then, 1 ml of the enzyme suspension was added to 10 mg of starch in a 1.5 ml Eppendorf tube. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C in a thermomixer and stirred. Estimation of hydrolysed starch was performed by taking 15 µl aliquots after 0, 20, 60, 360, 1440, 1800, and 2880 min and mixing it with a DNS solution, according to Miller (1959). After heating for 10 min at 37 °C, the absorbance was read at 540 nm in the SPECTROstar Nano spectrometer. Standard curves of glucose were constructed (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 g l−1), and a negative control containing no PPA was used for each sample and subtracted from the measured values.

Yeast two-hybrid assays

The coding sequences of LESV, ESV1, and ISA1 were obtained as gBlocks™ DNA fragments flanked by attB Gateway recombination sites. These sequences were cloned into the Gateway entry vector pDONR221 using a BP Clonase II Enzyme kit (Thermo Scientific). The resulting entry clones were subsequently recombined into the destination vectors pGADT7 (containing the activation domain) or pGBKT7 (containing the DNA-binding domain) via LR Clonase II-mediated recombination (Thermo Scientific). The various plasmid combinations were co-transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain AH109 (MATa, trp1-901, leu2-3, 112, ura3-52, his3-200, gal4Δ, gal80Δ, LYS2::GAL1UAS-GAL1TATA-HIS3, MEL1 GAL2UAS- GAL2TATA-ADE2, URA3::MEL1UAS-MEL1TATA-lacZ). Transformed yeast cells were selected on double-dropout medium (DDO; SD/–Leu–Trp) (TaKaRa) to confirm the presence of both plasmids and screened for protein–protein interactions on quadruple-dropout medium (QDO; SD/–Leu–Trp–Ade–His) (TaKaRa), where growth indicated a positive interaction.

Protein alignment and modelling

Protein sequences were retrieved from the potato genome assembly dataset (v6.1) for the doubled monoploid potato DM 1-3 516 R44 (Pham et al., 2020). Plastid transit peptides were predicted using Localizer (https://localizer.csiro.au). Protein sequences were aligned using Clustal Omega (Madeira et al., 2024). ESV1 or LESV sequences without transit peptides were then computed with the Colabfold v1.5.5 software based on AlphaFold2 and MMseqs2 (Jumper et al., 2021; Mirdita et al., 2022). Colabfold was executed with default parameters, and five structural models were generated per input sequence. Models were ranked based on AlphaFold’s predicted local distance difference test score.

Results

CRISPR/Cas9 inactivation of LESV and ESV1 has no impact on plant development, or starch and phytoglycogen contents

Targeted mutagenesis in potato is challenging due to its tetraploid complex genomic structure. To overcome this, we used the CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing approach, which offers the ability to target all four alleles simultaneously with high efficiency and specificity. Guide RNAs (gRNAs) specific to the four copies of ESV1 and LESV in Désirée were designed using the Geneious software, together with the CRISPR-P and CRISPOR online tools in conjunction with the polymorphism map described by Sevestre et al. (2020). All four copies of ESV1 and LESV shared identical sequences at the selected gRNAs, which targeted the third exon in both genes (Fig. 1A, B). Subsequently, the gRNAs were cloned by Golden Gate reaction into the pDIRECT_22A plasmid, which carries a kanamycin resistance gene (Čermák et al., 2017) (Supplementary Fig. S1). This construct was then delivered into potato plants via A. tumefaciens nuclear genome transformation. To confirm that transformation was successful, the presence of the CAS9 gene in the genome was screened by PCR amplification using specific primers (Supplementary Table S1; Fig. 1C). Genotyping of the regenerated kanamycin-resistant plants was achieved by sequencing the targeted regions of LESV and ESV1 after amplification by PCR. Sequence chromatograms were analysed with the ICE analysis tool (Supplementary Figs S2, S3), which separates overlapping signals corresponding to different alleles. The number of edited alleles was deducted from the inferred sequences and their relative proportions, considering that one allele corresponds to ∼25% of the signal. Among the 35 LESV and 31 ESV1 transformants confirmed by PCR amplification of the CAS9 gene, 28 and 21 had at least one mutated allele, respectively. Nine independent full knock-out lines were selected for LESV and five for ESV1 (Fig. 1D), corresponding to 32.2% and 23.8% of the transformants, respectively, and all four alleles in the selected lines contained frameshift mutations. Granule morphology was investigated in all the generated lines and subsequent analyses were performed on three and two selected lesv and esv1 lines, respectively. The absence of the corresponding proteins in the starches of the mutants was confirmed by label-free quantitative proteomics following starch gelatinization and protein isolation (Supplementary Fig. S4). Mutants were cultivated in soil for 3 months and various parameters were assessed. No significant effects were observed in the overall appearance of plants, in the stem height, or in tuber morphology for either of the mutants (Fig. 2A–F). Furthermore, both tuber yield and number per plant were unaffected by the mutations (Fig. 2G, H), and the contents of starch, glucose, and water-soluble polysaccharides (WSPs), which include phytoglycogen, showed no consistent effects among the lesv lines (Fig. 2I, J).

Starch granule morphology is severely affected in lesv mutants

Starch granule morphology (diameter and roundness) was analysed using a granulomorphometer. A strong reduction in size was observed in the lesv knockout mutant lines compared with the wild type (WT) plants whereas no difference was observed in the esv1 mutants (Fig. 3A, B). On average, a two-fold reduction in diameter was observed in the lesv lines, with 50% of granules exhibiting a diameter between 4–5 µm in the mutants, compared with 13% in the WT. This phenotype was consistently observed among all nine of the independent knockout mutants. The ellipsoid roundness of the granules was determined by measuring the ratio between the mean radius of peripheral circles and the radius of the maximal circle of the examined particle: this ratio ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 corresponding to a perfect circle. The ellipsoid roundness was significantly decreased in the lesv mutant lines, with a mean roundness of 0.83 compared with 0.87 in the WT (Fig. 3C); again, no difference was observed in the esv1 mutants (Fig. 3D). To further investigate the reduction in diameter observed in lesv, purified starch granules or starch granules in situ were examined by optical microscopy or SEM. Large and regularly shaped granules were present in the WT, and some granules of normal size could also be observed in the lesv lines (Fig. 4; and Supplementary Fig. S5); however, a strong accumulation of tiny starch granules was consistently seen in the lesv samples. In these lines, atypical structures were also seen in the form of aggregates of small granules that were fused or that formed composite structures that exhibited deep fissures (Fig. 4). Additionally, some of the small granules showed flat faces with sharp edges. Suspensions of purified starch granules were subjected to ultrasonication and SDS washes to try separating the aggregates, but subsequent differential interference contrast microscopy indicated that this did not disrupt the aggregates (Supplementary Fig. S5), suggesting glucan entanglement between granules.

The lesv and esv1 mutations do not affect starch ultrastructure

Starch composition and ultrastructure were investigated using several approaches. Crystallinity was determined by wide-angle X-ray scattering, and all the starch samples from both the WT and the mutants exhibited the same B-type structure showing a characteristic diffraction peak at 2θ=7.4°. A small and non-significant decrease in crystallinity degree was observed in all the examined mutant lines (Figs 5A, 6A). In addition, the lamellar repeat distance was determined by small-angle X-ray scattering and consistent values of 7.2–7.4 were found for all the samples (Figs 5B, 6B). Radial orientation of starch glucan was confirmed by polarized light microscopy of starch suspensions and the birefringence cross was easily observed in both the WT and the largest granules of lesv mutants (Supplementary Fig. S5C). Detecting it in the tiny granule aggregates was more challenging, but it was still discernible. In addition, the maximum wavelength of the starch–iodine complex (Figs 5C, 6C) and the amylose content (Figs 5D, 6D) were not significantly altered in the mutants compared with the WT. Moreover, determination of the chain length distribution of amylopectin revealed no notable differences between the mutants and the WT (Figs 5E, F, 6E, F).

Lesv and esv1 mutations affect starch phosphate content and α-amylolysis in vitro

To investigate the impact of the lesv and esv1 mutations on starch phosphorylation in vivo, phosphoester contents were determined by fluorescence-assisted capillary electrophoresis after acid hydrolysis. A significant increase of starch phosphorylation ranging from 19–28% was measured in the lesv mutants (Fig. 7A), which was primarily attributable to an enrichment in O-6-phosphorylation, while the amount of O-3-phosphorylation was unchanged. This increase was consistent with that previously observed in fractionated potato starch granules of <10 µm diameter (Helle et al., 2019). In the esv1 mutant lines, a slight decrease of the phosphate content in the range 18–20% was measured (Fig. 7B). The hydrolysis rate of starch was assayed in vitro by measuring the increase of reducing ends after porcine pancreatic α-amylase treatment, and the degradability of lesv starch was significantly and consistently higher compared with the WT for all three mutant lines that were tested (Fig. 8A). The mean hydrolysis rate of lesv starch was 26% after 48 h, whereas that of the WT starch was only 17%, representing an increase of the hydrolysis rate of 53%. This was consistent with previous reports of the increased enzyme susceptibility of small granules due the higher amount of enzyme adsorption sites for a given starch weight (Noda et al., 2005; Dhital et al., 2010; Naguleswaran et al., 2012). A similar trend of increased starch degradability was observed for the two esv1 mutants, with hydrolysis rates of 26% and 30% after 48 h, compared with 21% in the WT (Fig. 8B).

LESV structural features and interaction with ISA1 support its role in regulating starch granule initiation

The alterations observed in the lesv mutants suggested that LESV plays a key role in the initiation and growth of starch granules. Notably, the lesv phenotype closely resembled that of isa1 and isa2 antisense lines, which also exhibit a marked reduction in granule size, and the formation of aggregates (Bustos et al., 2004). An interaction between LESV and ISA1 has previously been demonstrated in rice by Yan et al. (2024), but they did not test the interaction between ESV1 and ISA1. Hence, we conducted yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays in potato to assess both potential interactions. For each protein pair (LESV–ISA1 and ESV1–ISA1), two independent Y2H experiments were performed using reciprocal BD/AD configurations, to ensure reproducibility and to validate the specificity of the observed interactions. These experiments consistently confirmed an interaction between potato LESV and ISA (Fig. 9A), and in addition also revealed an unexpected interaction between ESV1 and ISA1 (Fig. 9B). The Y2H results also indicated that ISA1 monomers can interact with each other, which is consistent with previous findings in maize and rice (Hussain et al., 2003; Utsumi and Nakamura, 2006; Kubo et al., 2010; Facon et al., 2013). To investigate potential structural determinants underlying the distinct phenotypes of the lesv and esv1 mutants despite the shared ability of the proteins to bind with ISA1, we performed a protein sequence alignment using Clustal Omega, which revealed that both LESV and ESV1 contain N-terminal extensions, but with differing sequences and predicted structures (Fig. 10A). Structural models generated with AlphaFold2 showed that both proteins share a conserved C-terminal domain composed of stacked antiparallel β-sheets (Fig. 10B), a feature also observed in Arabidopsis homologs (Osman et al., 2024). These β-sheets align tryptophan residues that are thought to mediate interactions with amylopectin. In contrast to ESV1, which possesses a short and unstructured N-terminal region, the N-terminal extension of LESV contains three predicted α-helices (Fig. 10B), supporting a specific role in mediating interactions with other protein partners or in modulating the spatial and temporal dynamics of ISA1 recruitment.

In wild type (WT) plants, LESV interacts with ISA1 to regulate the number of soluble starch granule precursors. On the other hand, LESV and ESV1 interact with glucans via their C-terminal domains to promote and stabilize phase transition, respectively, as proposed by Liu et al. Discussion

The lesv mutation alters the regulation of starch initiation in potato

The aim of this study was to investigate the function of the non-catalytic proteins LESV and ESV1 in the metabolism of storage starch in potato tubers. To this end, knockout mutants were generated by targeted genome editing using the CRISPR/Cas9 approach. Starch morphology was determined in nine and five independent mutated lines for LESV or ESV1, respectively (Fig. 1), whilst three and two lines, respectively were used for further in-depth phenotypic characterization. Interestingly, key agronomic traits, including plant growth, and tuber and starch yield per plant were unaffected by the mutations (Fig. 2). However, the lesv mutation induced significant alterations in starch granule morphology, characterized by significant reductions in diameter and in roundness as determined by granulomorphometric analysis (Fig. 3). Optical and electron microscopy confirmed the accumulation of numerous tiny granules, many of which formed aggregated or fused structures (Fig. 4; Supplementary Fig. S5). These aggregates were resistant to degradation by SDS and ultrasonication, suggesting the presence of tight glucan entanglement and the occurrence of multiple initiation events per amyloplast, characteristic of altered regulation of starch initiation. Strikingly, this phenotype closely mirrors that of isa1 antisense lines previously described in potato (Bustos et al., 2004), where down-regulation of isoamylase also leads to small, aggregated starch granules. Accumulation of phytoglycogen is a hallmark of isoamylase deficiency in Arabidopsis and cereals, and is measurable in the isa1 potato lines, but to a lesser extent, indicating species-specific differences in glucan fate following ISA1 deregulation. In addition to its function in starch deposition through amylopectin trimming, Bustos et al. (2004) proposed that ISA1 regulates starch initiation in potato tubers by limiting the number of initiation events.

Given the strong phenotypic overlap between lesv and isa1 mutants, we hypothesized that there is a functional interaction between LESV and ISA1. Indeed, our Y2H assays confirmed that potato LESV physically interacts with ISA1 (Fig. 9), in line with recent findings in rice, where LESV (FLO9) has been shown to bind to ISA1 through its N-terminal domain (Yan et al., 2024). Furthermore, ISA1 self-interaction was also observed in our system, consistent with previous studies of maize and rice (Utsumi and Nakamura, 2006; Kubo et al., 2010; Facon et al., 2013).

Despite high sequence similarity in their C-terminal regions (Fig. 10), ESV1 and LESV seem to differ significantly in function. Both proteins possess a conserved C-terminal β-sheet domain with aligned tryptophan residues, which is known to bind starch (Feike et al., 2016; Osman et al., 2024). However, LESV has a unique N-terminal overhang, predicted to form α-helices and to undergo conformational changes upon binding with amylopectin. ESV1 also possesses an N-terminal extension, but it is markedly shorter and predicted to be disordered rather than coiled. In rice, Yan et al. (2024) did not test the interaction between ESV1 and ISA1, and the requirement of the LESV N-terminus for ISA1 binding was inferred from truncation assays. Our results revise this view: potato ESV1 and ISA1 did interact in Y2H assays (Fig. 10), despite the absence of a starch granule initiation phenotype in the esv1 mutants. This suggests that the ISA1 interaction alone is not sufficient for granule initiation and that additional structural features or regulatory contexts unique to LESV confer its specific role. A similar lack of phenotype has been reported in Arabidopsis esv1 mutants, although some irregularities in starch turnover were observed (Feike et al., 2016). Interestingly, the lesv phenotype in potato differed from the lesv phenotype in rice, as rice lesv mutants accumulate substantial amounts of phytoglycogen (Yan et al., 2024), in contrast to potato lesv mutants that did present modifications in WSP, which include phytoglycogen (Fig. 2I). This reflects the difference in the phenotype of the respective isa mutants: while rice isa mutants accumulate large amounts of phytoglycogen, potato mutants only show moderate phytoglycogen accumulation (Bustos et al., 2004). Thus, although the nature of the phenotype differs between species, the fact that lesv mutants closely mimic isa mutants is conserved. This strengthens the case for a conserved functional interaction between LESV and ISA1 across species, despite divergent downstream effects.

We propose that LESV in potato plays an essential role in regulating starch granule initiation through physical interaction with ISA1 and early glucan precursors (Fig. 10C). This recruitment is lost in lesv mutants, leading to deregulated initiation and to formation of multiple granules per amyloplast. Although ESV1 was also found to interact with ISA1 in our Y2H assays, its inactivation did not alter granule number or morphology, suggesting that this interaction alone is insufficient to regulate initiation. This implies that LESV confers additional regulatory specificity (possibly through its unique N-terminal α-helices) that might modulate the timing, localization, or efficiency of ISA1 recruitment. Our findings support a revised model in which LESV and ESV1 both contribute to starch granule biogenesis but with distinct and only partially overlapping functions (Fig. 10C). In this model, LESV interacts with ISA1 and soluble glucan precursors to restrict nucleation events and ensure proper granule initiation. In parallel, both LESV and ESV1 probably bind growing glucans through their conserved C-terminal domains to promote or stabilize the phase transition from soluble to semi-crystalline starch (Liu et al., 2023), potentially aided by transient interactions with ISA1. In esv1 mutants, the starch structure remains unchanged, suggesting that LESV compensates for loss of ESV1 under normal conditions. Conversely, in lesv mutants, granule initiation becomes deregulated, leading to multiple smaller granules and granule aggregates, while ESV1 might still maintain phase transition. Although no major ultrastructural defects were detected in either mutant using current imaging and diffraction methods, subtle changes in glucan organization might have gone unnoticed. Overall, despite their shared starch-binding domains and capacity to interact with ISA1 in yeast, LESV and ESV1 appear to fulfill non-redundant roles shaped by differences in their N-terminal regions and context-dependent regulatory mechanisms.

In our previous work (Helle et al., 2018), LESV was identified as one of the most abundant starch-associated proteins in potato tubers, remaining bound to granules even after extensive washing and protease treatment, suggesting a strong or stable interaction with starch. In contrast, ISA1 was not detected under the same conditions, indicating that it might not stably associate with mature granules or is present at levels below detection. This supports the idea that the LESV–ISA1 interaction is transient, occurring during early stages of granule initiation before mature structures are formed. In this model, LESV could act as a scaffolding protein, recruiting ISA1 to nascent glucan chains to regulate initiation. Once ISA1 has completed its role, it might return to the soluble phase, explaining its low abundance in the starch-bound proteome. Although Y2H assays confirm direct interaction, they do not reflect the strength or timing of this association in vivo. Future co-localization or time-resolved proteomics would help to clarifying the dynamics of this interaction during granule development.

The lesv mutation affects starch phosphate content and α-amylolysis in vitro

The lesv mutation resulted in an increase in phosphate content, particularly due to elevated levels of O-6-phosphoester residues (Fig. 7), and this is consistent with two previous studies. First, higher phosphate content mainly attributable to increased O-6-phosphoester residues was also found in small starch granules in potato tubers by Helle et al. (2019), and second, LESV in Arabidopsis has been found to regulate the phosphorylation activity of starch-related dikinases, which are key enzymes of the regulation of starch degradation (Singh et al., 2022). In vitro experiments in the latter study revealed that LESV enhances the activity of PWD, an enzyme bound to starch granules that is involved in phosphorylating glucose at position C3, while reducing the activity of GWD that phosphorylates glucose at position C6—a prerequisite step for PWD activity. We found that the hydrolysis rate of the lesv mutant starch was significantly increased compared with the WT, by 53% (Fig. 8). This is consistent with previous findings that smaller granules, due to their increased surface area per unit mass, facilitate enzyme diffusion and adsorption, thereby accelerating catalytic action (Dhital et al., 2010). This modification is particularly interesting from the perspective of industrial applications. The alteration of the digestibility is of economic interest as it enables the production of the same amount of hydrolysate in less time or with fewer enzymes. Additionally, small granules are valuable in composite materials, packaging components, fat-replacers in low-fat food, waste-water treatment adsorbents, binders in the paper industry, and as carriers for flavors, medicines, or vitamins (Kim et al., 2015). While natural small granules from sources such as quinoa, rice, amaranth, and taro are typically used, producing them from potato could allow higher yield production.

Whilst esv1 mutant starch granules displayed a size and morphology similar to the wild type, they show a moderate but consistent increase in the rate of hydrolysis by α-amylase (Figs 3, 8). Several hypotheses could account for this increased degradability. First, the esv1 mutants showed a slight decrease in starch phosphate content, particularly at the O-6 position (Fig. 7). Phosphorylation is known to influence crystallinity and granule hydration (Blennow et al., 2002). A decrease in phosphorylation might subtly alter the internal structure or organization of the granule, thereby indirectly affecting its digestibility. Such changes could remain undetected by small- and wide-angle X-ray scattering, and by morphological analysis, and yet still influence the enzyme interaction with starch. Second, based on previous findings in Arabidopsis (Liu et al., 2023), ESV1 is proposed to stabilize the semi-crystalline lamellar structure of amylopectin, and its absence might lead to a subtle disorganization of this structure that results in increased enzyme access to glucan chains. Taken together, these observations suggest that ESV1 might contribute to a fine-adjustment of starch granule integrity, and that its absence can result in granules that are more easily hydrolysed despite appearing structurally normal under standard imaging and diffraction methods.
