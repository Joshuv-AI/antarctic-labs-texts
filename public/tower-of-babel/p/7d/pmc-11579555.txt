Heterogeneous Electrochemical Carbon Dioxide Reduction in Aqueous Medium Using a Novel N4‐Macrocyclic Cobalt Complex
Authors: Libo Sun, Tan Su, Adrian C. Fisher, Xin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMolecular catalysts represent an exceptional class of materials in the realm of electrochemical carbon dioxide reduction (CO2RR), offering distinct advantages owing to their adaptable structure, which enables precise control of electronic configurations and outstanding performance in CO2RR. This study introduces an innovative approach to heterogeneous electrochemical CO2RR in an aqueous environment, utilizing a newly synthesized N4‐macrocyclic cobalt complex generated through a dimerization coupling reaction. By incorporating the quaterpyridine moiety, this cobalt complex exhibits the capability to catalyze CO2RR at low overpotentials and reaches near‐unity CO production across a wide potential range, as verified by the online mass spectrometry and in situ attenuated total reflectance‐Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy. Comprehensive computational models demonstrate the superiority of utilizing quarterpyridine moiety in mediating CO2 conversion compared to the counterpart. This work not only propels the field of electrochemical CO2RR but also underscores the promising potential of cobalt complexes featuring quaterpyridine moieties in advancing sustainable CO2 conversion technologies within aqueous environments.

Introduction

Carbon dioxide (CO2) stands as one major kind of greenhouse gas, and its substantial role in global warming is a pressing concern nowadays.[ 1 ] Global warming has significant and far‐reaching impacts on weather patterns, sea levels, ecosystems, and human societies.[ 2 ] It is imperative to address this issue by reducing greenhouse gas emissions through sustainable technologies.[ 3 ] One promising avenue for mitigation technologies involves carbon dioxide reduction, particularly through electrochemical methods.[ 4 ] Electrochemical CO2 reduction holds immense potential for the transformation of CO2 into valuable hydrocarbon fuels and chemicals, presenting a promising pathway toward renewable energy and resource utilization.[ 5 ]

Molecular catalysts tailored for electrochemical CO2 reduction play a pivotal role in advancing sustainable technology in this field.[ 6 ] Their unique molecular structures offer precise control over reaction pathways and product selectivity, opening up new avenues for chemical production from CO2.[ 7 ] To improve their conductivity and facilitate effective charge transfer when interacting with electrodes, the immobilization of molecular catalysts onto solid substrates to create heterogeneous molecular catalysts has been explored.[ 8 ] This approach may also contribute to improving material durability through posttreatment considerations.[ 9 ] Nevertheless, challenges persist in optimizing the performance of these catalysts towards CO2 reduction, particularly concerning selectivity, activity, and durability. Addressing these challenges necessitates further efforts focusing on fine‐tuning the coordination environment of molecular catalysts, which could regulate the electronic structures of the active center, enhance binding affinity to reaction intermediates and ultimately boost activity in the CO2 reduction process.

Metal porphyrins,[ 10 ] phthalocyanines,[ 11 ] cyclams,[ 12 ] complexes incorporating pyridine‐subunits,[ 13 ] and others have garnered significant attention for their potential in CO2 reduction.[ 14 ] Among these molecular catalysts, N4‐macrocyclic cobalt complexes (porphyrin, phthalocyanine derivatives) with well‐defined precise active site structures and easy‐to‐decorate electronic structures by customizing the coordinating ligand make them promising electrocatalysts for CO2 reduction.[ 10 , 11 ] To obtain suitable electrocatalysts with good selectivity, activity, and stability, tuning the coordination environment within N4‐macrocyclic cobalt complexes at the molecular level can be an ideal strategy. Efforts to date have mainly focused on tuning the ligand structures of porphyrins and phthalocyanines with decorative functional groups for CO2 reduction. Notably, these efforts predominantly involved porphyrins and phthalocyanines composed of pyrrole‐subunits, leading to studies of altering the electronic structure of the active site using various coordinating ligands. Recent advancements in ligands based on quaterpyridine moieties derived from pyridine‐subunits have demonstrated exceptional selectivity and activity in CO2 reduction.[ 15 ] For instance, CoCPY has been reported for heterogeneous CO2 to CO conversion with a high selectivity of 96%.[ 16 ] Moreover, CoQPyPhen exhibited a near‐unity selectivity and displayed high current densities when investigated in a flow cell setup and pertaining neat 100% selectivity.[ 17 ] Building upon these successes, this study introduced a novel N4‐macrocyclic cobalt complex containing quaterpyridine moiety, namely CoBQPyPhen, for heterogeneous CO2 reduction. The electrochemical results, as well as the online mass spectrometry and in‐situ attenuated total reflectance‐Fourier transform infrared (ATR‐FTIR) spectroscopy experiments proved its outstanding selectivity and activity for CO2 reduction.

Results and Discussion

In our previous research, we embarked on the design and synthesis of a series of ligands derived from quaterpyridine moiety.[ 16 , 17 , 18 ] When these ligands were coordinated with cobalt atoms, the resulting molecular catalysts, exhibited high activity towards electrocatalytic CO2 reduction to produce CO. Building on our previous findings that enhanced activity was found when quaterpyridine moiety containing molecular catalysts were adorned with a piperidine functional group, we developed a new molecular catalyst (CoBQPyPhen) by combining these two functional groups, as shown in Figure 1a. The synthesis of this molecular catalyst first involved a dimerization process of aromatic bromo‐ and piperidine‐substituted 1,10‐phenanthroline derivatives and subsequent coordination with cobalt acetate.[ 19 ] The successful preparation of such a metal complex was confirmed through high‐resolution mass spectrometry measurement, yielding a tested molecular weight of 521.0680. For comparison, the calculated value for such a metal complex stands at 521.0673, as depicted in Figure S1 (Supporting Information). The molecular orbital properties of these metal complexes were first calculated. As exhibited in Figure 1b, the calculated gaps between the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of CoTPP, CoPC, and CoBQPyPhen were determined to be 3.00, 2.22, and 2.11 eV, respectively, assuring a better redox ability for CoBQPyPhen than the others.

a) Illustration of molecular catalysts with different subunits. b) Calculations of frontier HOMO‐LUMO orbitals.

The Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR) of CoBQPyPhen before and after chelation were taken, as illustrated in Figure S2 (Supporting Information). Notably, discernible differences in the peaks within the 1400 to 1750 cm−1 region were found, signifying the alteration in the resonance of the aromatic phenyl groups within the molecular catalyst. Additionally, the UV–vis spectrum of CoBQPyPhen was further taken, which displayed a similar characteristic peak at ≈250 to 400 nm to that of molecular catalysts prepared in previous research and affirmed the successful synthesis of the metal complex.[ 17 ] To delve deeper into the electronic structure of CoBQPyPhen, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) was employed to measure its work function. For a comprehensive understanding, the UPS data for 5,10,15,20‐Tetraphenyl‐21H,23H‐porphine cobalt(II) (CoTPP) and cobalt(II) phthalocyanine (CoPC) were also collected for comparison purposes. As illustrated in Figure 2a–c, the work function of CoBQPyPhen was measured to be 2.82 eV, notably lower than the values of 3.08 eV for CoTPP and 2.96 eV for CoPC, respectively. This lower work function of CoBQPyPhen is typically associated with a higher electron‐donating behavior of the metal complex from lower orbital levels to higher ones.[ 20 ] This is advantageous for facilitating electron transfer and enhancing the catalytic activity of the metal complexes. Furthermore, the measured work functions are intricately related to the gap of HOMO and LUMO of the metal complexes.

As pyrrole subunits and pyridine subunits may lead to different coordinating environments of cobalt, the electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) tests were taken (Figure S4, Supporting Information), where CoTPP and CoPC with unpaired electrons could exhibit their signals, while that of CoBQPyPhen is almost absent, suggesting a different electronic structure of cobalt‐based on pyrrole subunits and pyridine subunits. This difference can be observed from the density of state (DOS) calculation of cobalt, as shown in Figure S12 (Supporting Information), where that of CoBQPyPhen is paired with the calculated magnetic moment to be zero. X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements of CoBQPyPhen, CoTPP, and CoPC were further taken to investigate the oxidation state of the cobalt element. As shown in Figure 2d, the 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 high‐resolution peaks for the cobalt element of CoTPP saturated ≈780.3 and 796.0 eV confirm a valence state of +2. Similarly, the 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 peaks for the cobalt element of CoPC at ≈780.9 and 796.4 eV also confirm a valence state of +2. Figure 2d further reveals the peak binding energy positions for the cobalt element of CoBQPyPhen are almost the same as that of CoPC with the observed satellite peaks, affirming the valence state of +2 for cobalt in CoBQPyPhen. Further, the X‐ray absorption near‐edge structure (XANES) was conducted for CoBQPyPhen (Figure 2e), where a valance state between 0 and +2 could be found. The extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectroscopic analyses (Figure S5, Supporting Information) disclosed a coordination number of ≈3.94, which indicates the formation of N4‐macrocyclic cobalt complex and follows the result of high‐resolution mass data in Figure S1 Supporting Information.

Subsequently, these molecular catalysts were immobilized onto multi‐wall carbon nanotubes (CNT) to form heterogeneous molecular catalysts (CoTPP/CNT, CoPC/CNT, and CoBQPyPhen/CNT). The successful immobilization of these molecular catalysts could be confirmed through Raman spectra analysis (Figure 2f). Following immobilization, the ID/IG ratios for CoTPP/CNT, CoPC/CNT, and CoBQPyPhen/CNT were found to be 1.10, 1.07, and 1.05, respectively. These variations in ID/IG ratios indicate differing degrees of interaction between the metal complexes and the substrate, with a decreased I D/I G ratio of 1.05 indicating an improved graphene structure from better interaction between the coplanar structure of CoBQPyPhen. Conversely, the twisted structure of CoTPP may lead to the least decrease in I D/I G ratios among these three. Subsequently, the high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR‐TEM) images of CoBQPyPhen/CNT were obtained, as illustrated in Figure 2g, which revealed the absence of nanoparticles. The atomic dispersion of CoBQPyPhen on the substrate can be directly observed using aberration‐corrected high‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC HAADF‐STEM) in Figure 2h, indicating that the electrocatalyst retained its molecular dispersion after immobilization. Additionally, the energy dispersive spectrometry mapping (EDS) images were obtained in Figure 2i–l, showing that the elements were uniformly dispersed on the CNT substrate.

The electrochemical CO2 reduction performance was assessed using an H‐cell system, where these electrocatalysts (CoTPP/CNT, CoPC/CNT, and CoBQPyPhen/CNT) were initially drop‐casted onto carbon paper stripes as working electrodes before use. The polarization behavior of these electrocatalysts was measured in a CO2‐saturated 0.1 m KHCO3 electrolyte to compare the activity of them. As illustrated in Figure 3a, the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curve of CoBQPyPhen/CNT demonstrated a catalytic activity at significantly lower applied bias potential than the others. Moreover, CoBQPyPhen/CNT displays the highest current density, followed by CoPC/CNT, while CoTPP/CNT exhibited the poorest performance at the same potential. This trend aligns with the UPS and Raman results, highlighting the pivotal role of a lower intrinsic work function of the molecular catalyst in enhancing electrocatalytic reactions. Furthermore, effective interaction between the molecular catalyst and the substrate was shown to be crucial for boosting the activity of CO2 reduction.

a) LSV curves recorded at 10 mV s−1 in CO2 saturated 0.1 M KHCO3. b) FECO (electrolysis time: 1 h). c) Calculated j CO (electrolysis time: 1 h). d) Turnover frequency (TOF) (electrolysis time: 1 h). e) Stability test of CoBQPyPhen at −0.45 V vs RHE.

Subsequently, the electrocatalytic CO2 reduction activities of these electrocatalysts were further investigated through chronoamperometry measurements within an applied potentials range of −0.25 to −0.60 V vs RHE (reversible hydrogen electrode), with their Faradaic efficiencies (FECO) for CO2 reduction to CO presented in Figure 3b. Remarkably, it could be observed that all these electrocatalysts displayed activity towards CO2 reduction, with CoBQPyPhen/CNT standing out with a superior FECO compared to the others. Specifically, CoBQPyPhen/CNT could achieve a FECO of ≈88% at a modest potential of −0.25 V vs RHE, which increased to ≈95% at −0.30 V vs RHE, and reached near‐unity CO production within the potential range of −0.35 V to −0.60 V vs RHE. In contrast, CoPC/CNT can only produce CO at a potential of −0.35 V vs RHE with a FECO of ≈ 58%, and only surpassing 90% FECO when the potential applied reached −0.60 V vs RHE. CoTPP/CNT exhibited the least favorable performance among them, generating CO at a potential of −0.40 V vs RHE, with the FECO below 90% at the testing potentials.

The partial current densities (j CO) of these electrocatalysts were further calculated and presented in Figure 3c. Notably, at a potential of −0.60 V vs RHE, CoBQPyPhen/CNT exhibited a j CO value of ≈2.2 and 5.2 times greater than those of CoPC/CNT and CoTPP/CNT, respectively, establishing a performance trend of CoBQPyPhen/CNT > CoPC/CNT > CoTPP/CNT. In Figure 3d, CoBQPyPhen demonstrated the highest turnover frequency (TOF) of 4.2 s−1 among these samples at −0.6 V vs RHE. All these reinforced the exceptional performance of CoBQPyPhen/CNT among them. Additionally, the Tafel slope values for these electrocatalysts were determined and are displayed in Figure S6 (Supporting Information), with all three electrocatalysts exhibiting similar values, close to 120 mV dec−1, indicative of a kinetic one‐electron rate‐determining step for CO2 reduction. To evaluate the superior performance of CoBQPyPhen/CNT, its charge transfer behavior was investigated with electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). As provided in Figure S7 (Supporting Information), through the establishment of an equivalent circuit, the charge‐transfer resistance (Rct) values for CoTPP/CNT, CoPC/CNT, and CoBQPyPhen/CNT were fitted to be 3845, 1547, and 426, respectively, revealing significantly lower charge transfer resistance compared to the others. This low resistance facilitates the rapid conversion of CO2 to CO. Furthermore, the stability of CoBQPyPhen/CNT was assessed at −0.45 V vs RHE, as depicted in Figure 3e, CoBQPyPhen/CNT maintained a consistent current density ranging between −9.0 and −11.0 mA cm−2, with FECO fluctuating between 90% to 100% over a 10 h period. The XPS and SEM data before and after the stability test remain almost unchanged (Figures S9–S11, Supporting Information), underscoring its stability for extended electrocatalytic CO2 reduction.

When compared to another series of molecular catalysts published previously (CoQPyPhen),[ 17 ] both two types of molecular catalysts (CoQPyPhen and CoBQPyPhen) demonstrate exceptional performance superior to that of CoTPP and CoPC in the context of CO2 reduction, thus corroborating our earlier statement on the enhanced activity of molecular catalysts with pyridine subunits compared to pyrrole subunits. Besides, other kinds of molecular catalysts based on quaterpyridine moieties also exhibit good selectivity for CO2 reduction (Table S2, Supporting Information),[ 15 , 21 ] thus validating the effectiveness of the strategy of employing the quaterpyridine moieties in designing molecular catalysts for CO2 reduction.

Moreover, the products of CO and H2 during reactionwere detected by online mass spectrometry. As shown in Figure 4a–c, when the potentials were applied from −0.4 to −1.5 V vs RHE, CoBQPyPhen/CNT exhibits obviously lower H2 signals than that of CoTPP/CNT and CoPC/CNT, indicating good selectivity of CoBQPyPhen/CNT than the latter for CO2 reduction. To gain deeper insights into the reaction mechanism and the advantage of utilizing the quaterpyridine moiety‐containing CoBQPyPhen for CO2 reduction, the in‐situ attenuated total reflectance‐Fourier transform infrared (ATR‐FTIR) spectroscopy technique was employed to investigate the surface adsorption behaviors of these electrocatalysts during reaction.[ 22 ] The in‐situ ATR‐FTIR spectra (Figure 4d–f) of CoTPP/CNT, CoPC/CNT, and CoBQPyPhen/CNT were analyzed for comparison. As shown in Figure 4f, CoBQPyPhen/CNT displays a pronounced peak response at considerably lower overpotentials, revealing several discernible peaks. Specifically, the peak at ≈ 1238 cm−1 was attributed to the vibration of adsorbed bicarbonate,[ 23 ] while the peak at ≈1445 cm−1 was assigned to the vibration of adsorbed carbonate.[ 24 ] Additionally, the peak at ≈1640 cm−1 denoted the vibration of unassociated H2O,[ 23 ] concealing a distinct shoulder peak of adsorbed COO* intermediate.[ 24 , 25 ] Furthermore, the peak at ≈1394 cm−1 was indicative of the vibration of adsorbed COOH* intermediate.[ 26 ] While these peaks could also be observed in the other electrocatalysts tested, their response occurred at larger overpotentials, and the peak intensities were comparatively weak. Nevertheless, the presence of these peaks suggests that they may follow a similar reaction pathway involving the formation of COO*, COOH*, a consideration that will be further explored in subsequent computational calculations.

To gain a computational understanding of the reaction mechanism for these molecular catalysts, density functional theory (DFT) was employed to obtain the reaction path of these molecular catalysts.[ 27 ] While the reaction path of CoTPP and CoPC had been reported in our previous work,[ 17 ] the reaction path of CoBQPyPhen was obtained considering the same reaction path of CoTPP and CoPC (Figures S14–S16, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure 5a, CoBQPyPhen exhibited the lowest Gibbs free energy difference along the reaction path among the three, where the desorption of adsorbed CO* was identified as the key rate‐determining step (RDS) with a Gibbs free energy difference of 0.363 eV for CoBQPyPhen, which is smaller than the corresponding values for CoTPP and CoPC, suggesting better activity for CoBQPyPhen than the others with pyrrole subunits.[ 28 ] Furthermore, from the partial density of states (PDOS) analysis (Figure S13, Supporting Information), CoBQPyPhen exhibits a relatively large overlapping with the intermediates of CO and COOH during the reaction when compared with that of CoTPP and CoPC. The d‐band‐center of CoBQPyPhen during the reactions is also close to the Fermi level and binds more effectively with the intermediates when compared with the others. Subsequently, from the Co─C bond order of these electrocatalysts during the reactions for the formation of COOH and CO intermediates, the projected crystal orbital Hamilton population (pCOHP; Figure 5b,c) suggested that the bond order for the formation of COOH for CoBQPyPhen is weaker than the others, while that for the formation of CO is relatively stronger. The charge density difference calculations for CoBQPyPhen suggested that the formation of CO intermediate could form a stronger bond than that of COOH intermediate.

Isosurface of charge density difference for the formation of COOH (d) and CO (e) intermediates configuration with CoBQPyPhen (yellow: charge accumulation, cyan: charge depletion).

Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully synthesized a novel N4‐macrocyclic cobalt complex (CoBQPyPhen) through the dimerization coupling reaction method. The prepared molecular catalyst could incorporate quaterpyridine moiety, leading to enhanced catalytic activity and selectivity of CO2 reduction to CO when compared to CoTPP and CoPC. Notably, such a catalyst could achieve a near‐unity CO production over a wide potential range and exhibit CO2 reduction activity at a low overpotential. Further investigation into its surface adsorption behavior using in‐situ ATR‐FTIR reveals pronounced responses at lower overpotentials, yielding intense peak signals compared to counterpart molecular catalysts. Theoretical calculations verify that CoBQPyPhen exhibits a smaller energy barrier for the rate‐determining step (RDS) compared to CoTPP and CoPC. These findings provide new insights for guiding the future development of molecular catalysts for CO2 conversion.

Experimental Section

Materials

5,10,15,20‐Tetraphenyl‐21H,23H‐porphine cobalt(II) (CoTPP, 85%), cobalt(II) phthalocyanine (CoPC, 97%), K2CO3 (98%), Nafion 117 membrane, Nafion perfluorinated resin solution (5 wt.% in lower aliphatic alcohols and water, contains 15–20% water), Ethylenediamine (99%), KBr (99%), and Co(OAc)2·4H2O (98%) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. 2‐Bromo‐1,10‐phenanthroline, bis(acetonitrile)palladium(II) dichloride, and 4,4,4′,4′,5,5,5′,5′‐Octamethyl‐2,2′‐bi(1,3,2‐dioxaborolane) were purchased from Bidepharm. Ltd. KHCO3 (99.99%) was purchased from Maclin Inc. Multiwalled carbon nanotube (CNT, 5–15 nm, >99 wt.%) was purchased from Chengdu Organic Chemicals Co., Ltd. H2SO4 (98%), HNO3 (60%), and other solvents were bought from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of CoBQPyPhen

2‐Bromo‐1,10‐phenanthroline‐5,6‐dione and 7‐bromopyrazino[2,3‐f][1,10]phenanthroline were prepared according to previous research.[ 17 ] The obtained 7‐bromopyrazino[2,3‐f][1,10]phenanthroline (0.1 mmol) was mixed with 4,4,4′,4′,5,5,5′,5′‐Octamethyl‐2,2′‐bi(1,3,2‐dioxaborolane) (0.2 mmol), K2CO3 (0.3 mmol), and bis(acetonitrile)palladium(II) dichloride (0.0025 mmol). After the addition of DMF (5 mL), the system was evacuated to remove oxygen and reacted at 120 °C under Ar atmosphere protection for 24 h. Finally, the system was quenched with DI water, filtered, and washed with acetone, MeOH, and diethyl ether. After dried, the yellow product (0.01 mmol) was mixed with Co(OAc)2·4H2O (0.012 mmol) and DMF (4 mL), the system was evacuated to remove oxygen and reacted at 120 °C under Ar atmosphere for 2 h. After it was cooled down to room temperature, diethyl ether (35 mL) was added to precipitate the solid product. The product was obtained by centrifugation, then washed with methanol and diethyl ether three times to remove metal salts. Later, the solid material was dried in a vacuum oven to obtain the final product. The high‐resolution mass spectrometry data of the final product was taken to prove the successful synthesis of the metal complex, where CoBQPyPhen: 521.0673, measured: 521.0680 (M)+, as given in Figure S1 (Supporting Information).

Electrochemical Tests

The electrolysis was conducted with an H‐cell system, where 10 mg CNT was first dispersed in a glass vial with 3.7 mL DMF with sonication. Then, a calculated amount of metal complex was added into the vial with the addition of 0.05 mL Nafion perfluorinated resin solution (5%). The mixture was further sonicated for 3 h to make the electrocatalyst uniformly dispersed. 200 µL of the as‐prepared ink was dropped casted onto a carbon paper. For CO2 reduction tests, Pt plate and Ag/AgCl (sat.) was used as counter and reference electrodes, respectively. 0.1 m KHCO3 saturated with high‐purity CO2 (99.999%) was used as the electrolyte. The flow rate of CO2 was controlled to be 20 sccm, where the gas‐phase products were channeled into online GC for analysis. iR correction (85%) was used to compensate for the liquid electrolyte resistance. The impedance measurements were taken at −0.4 V vs RHE in 0.1 m KHCO3 on a 1 MHz EIS module of Metrom Autolab 302N potentiostat. The data obtained was fitted with Zview software, The equivalent circuit model includes Rs, Rct, and CPE, which represent the solution resistance, the charge transfer resistance, and double layer resistance, respectively.

Online Mass Tests

The online mass data was measured using a Hiden analytical QGA system in a traditional H‐cell. CO2 gas was introduced into the system with a flow rate of 30 sccm and the outlet was conducted into the mass. For each electrocatalyst, the measurement was taken from −0.4 to −1.5 V vs RHE.

In Situ ATR‐FTIR Tests

The in situ ATR‐FTIR data were given using an electrochemical apparatus (VeeMax III) from Pike. A Ge prism evaporated with 3 nm Ti, 25 nm Au, and 2 nm Ti layer was used for measurements. After drop‐casting the electrocatalysts onto the surface of the prism, the electrolyte was saturated with Ar, and the working electrode was activated by successive LSV scanning, then the system was saturated with CO2, and in situ signals under different potentials were taken.

DFT Calculation Details

To clarify the electronic information of CoTPP, CoPC, and CoBQPyPhen, their HOMO‐LUMO orbitals were calculated with Gaussian software.[ 29 ] The geometry of models was optimized on a B3LYP hybrid functional level.[ 30 ] The pseudopotential LANL2DZ basis set was considered for cobalt atoms, and the 6–311+G(d) basis set was employed for other elements[ 31 ] with the use of PCM model.[ 32 ] The van der Waals’ interaction was used by Grimme's DFT‐D3 with the Becke–Jonson damping method. HOMO‐LUMO orbitals were plotted using MULTIWFN 3.8 and VMD software.[ 33 ] To clarify the reaction path of these models, density functional theory was considered using Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP) code with the consideration of spin‐polarization.[ 34 ] For all the calculations, the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the description of atomic potential by the projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudo‐potential, and Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional were utilized to describe the exchange‐correlation interactions.[ 35 ] The Grimme's DFT‐D3 with Becke–Jonson damping method was considered for van der Waals’ interaction.[ 36 ] The models were established with locating molecules on the graphene layer and the vacuum layer thickness was set to 25 Å was set along the z‐axis. The convergence threshold force and energy during optimization were set to be 0.02 eV Å−1 and 10−5 eV, respectively. The plane‐wave cut‐off energy was set to be 520 eV. The k‐point grids were used for the models. The Gibbs free energy difference (G) for each elementary reaction step was calculated and analyzed with the assistance of vaspkit 1.02 software,[ 37 ] with the use of the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) model.[ 38 ]

Material Characterization

UV–vis data was collected on a Cary 60 UV–vis Spectrophotometer. FTIR spectra were measured using a Vacuum Bruker 80 ATR‐FTIR spectrometer in the range of 400–4000 cm−1. X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data were collected on a Thermo Scientific K‐Alpha instrument. The Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) data were taken on a ThermoFisher Nexsa equipment. The Raman spectra were measured on a WITec alpha300R Raman microscope. High‐resolution mass (HRMS) data was taken on a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive with ESI scanning mode. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and EDS data were obtained on FEI Talos F200x equipment. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were taken on a TESCAN MIRA LMS equipment. Gas‐phase products were analyzed using an online Agilent 7890B Gas Chromatograph equipped with Parapak Q and Molecular Sieve 5 Å column. Evaporatings of Ti and Au were operated on the HHV 306 E‐beam evaporator. XAS experiments were carried out with a Benchtop X‐ray Absorption Fine Structure Spectrometer (RapidXAFS 2 m) manufactured by Anhui Absorption Spectroscopy Analysis Instrument Co., Ltd in China. A Si(533) prism (radius of curvature≤ 0.5 m) was used as the monochromator crystal to collect the signal from the cobalt element. A total of 100 mg sample was prepared for testing and the test was taken in a transmission mode at 20 kV and 20 mA for 2 h to accumulate the signal. Athena software was used to read the original data, compare, and plot the XANES Figure. Artemis software was used to fit the EXAFS data. The molecule model was optimized with Gaussian software before constructing the first‐shell Co─N bonding path. The aberration‐corrected high angle‐annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy images were obtained on the FEI Themis Z instrument.
