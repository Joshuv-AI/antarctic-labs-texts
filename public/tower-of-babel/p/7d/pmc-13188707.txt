The relationship between compassionate love, burnout levels and caring behaviors of intensive care nurses: the mediating role of compassion fatigue
Authors: Gülsün Özdemir Aydın, Halide Aksu, Nuray Turan, Eylem Toğluk Yiğitoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntensive care nurses are frequently exposed to emotionally demanding environments that challenge their ability to sustain compassionate care. These stressors may compromise their emotional well-being, leading to compassion fatigue, burnout, and reduced quality of caring behaviors. This study aimed to examine the relationship between compassion fatigue, burnout, and caring behaviors among intensive care nurses.MethodThis correlational and cross-sectional study was conducted in adult ICUs of several public and private hospitals across Türkiye between October 2024 and April 2025. A total of 323 ICU nurses who met the inclusion criteria voluntarily completed an online Google Forms survey. Data were collected using a sociodemographic questionnaire, the Compassionate Love Scale, the Short Compassion Fatigue Scale, the Burnout Syndrome Assessment Scale for ICU Nurses, and the Caring Behaviors Inventory-24. Descriptive statistics, correlation analyses, and partial mediation analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27.0). The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.ResultsAll nurses who were enrolled in the study provided complete responses. Most participants were female (81.1%) and held a bachelor’s degree (90.4%). The mean age was 30.4 ± 5.68 years, and the mean duration of professional experience was 8.5 ± 8.3 years. The mean scores for compassionate love, compassion fatigue, burnout, and caring behaviors were 99.7 ± 14.8, 68.7 ± 21.7, 44.5 ± 18.6, and 4.74 ± 0.94, respectively. Compassionate love was negatively correlated with compassion fatigue (r = −0.36, p < 0.001) and burnout (r = −0.30, p < 0.001), and positively correlated with caring behaviors (r = 0.30, p < 0.001). Compassion fatigue was positively correlated with burnout (r = 0.42, p < 0.001) and negatively with caring behaviors (r = −0.26, p < 0.001). Partial mediation analyses showed that compassion fatigue partially mediated the relationship between compassionate love and both burnout (β = −0.127, 95% CI = −0.188, −0.069) and caring behaviors (β = 0.062, 95% CI = 0.018, 0.108). Given the cross-sectional design of the study, causal inferences could not be established.ConclusionCompassion fatigue partially mediates the relationships between compassionate love, burnout, and caring behaviors among intensive care nurses. These findings indicate that compassion fatigue is a key factor linking the emotional capacities of nurses to burnout and caring behaviors in clinical practice, while also highlighting the relevance of compassion-related constructs for nursing education, research, and management in intensive care settings.Clinical ImplicationsCompassion fatigue is a clinically relevant factor associated with burnout and caring behaviors, indicating its importance in identifying nurses at risk for reduced quality of care in intensive care settings.Keywords: Empathy, Compassion fatigue, Burnout, Professional, Patient care, Intensive care units, Nurses, Statistical models

Introduction

Nurses working in intensive care units (ICUs) provide continuous, highly specialized, and individualized care to critically ill patients, often under conditions characterized by high clinical complexity, unpredictable disease trajectories, and frequent exposure to patient suffering and death. These demanding working conditions place ICU nurses at a heightened risk for psychological strain and emotional exhaustion compared to nurses in other clinical settings [1, 2–4]. Previous studies have consistently reported elevated levels of compassion fatigue and burnout among ICU nurses, highlighting the vulnerability of this population to adverse emotional and professional outcomes [1, 2, 4, 5]. Compassionate love is defined as a stable form of prosocial motivation characterized by deep concern for others’ well-being, a willingness to prioritize others’ needs, and sustained caring engagement that extends beyond situational empathy or altruistic acts [6]. Within nursing, compassionate love has been identified as a key personal resource that supports professional commitment, job satisfaction, and the delivery of caring behaviors [7, 8]. Empirical evidence suggests that nurses with higher levels of compassionate love demonstrate stronger patient-centered care while reporting lower levels of burnout and emotional distress [7, 8]. However, maintaining compassionate love in emotionally intense environments such as ICUs can be challenging due to persistent exposure to trauma, suffering, and ethical stressors. One of the primary factors that may erode compassionate engagement in nursing is compassion fatigue, which refers to a state of emotional, physical, and psychological exhaustion resulting from prolonged exposure to patients’ suffering and the continuous provision of empathic care [2, 5, 9]. Compassion fatigue has been widely documented among ICU nurses and is associated with increased burnout, reduced compassion competence, and diminished caring behaviors [1–5, 10, 11]. High workload demands, frequent encounters with death, and limited organizational resources further intensify compassion fatigue in critical care settings [3, 4]. Although the positive feelings derived from helping others may serve as a protective factor, evidence indicates that it does not fully offset the cumulative emotional burden experienced by nurses working in high-intensity clinical environments [9, 12].

Burnout is a psychological syndrome arising from chronic occupational stress and is characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment [4, 9]. Among ICU nurses, burnout has been linked to both individual emotional factors and organizational stressors, and has been shown to negatively affect caring behaviors, professional performance, and overall well-being [1, 4, 9, 11]. In addition, caring behaviors, which reflect nurses’ ability to provide attentive, empathetic, and patient-centered care, are influenced by emotional capacity, compassion, competence, resilience, and perceived organizational support [10, 11, 13]. Studies conducted in intensive care and other nursing contexts demonstrate that higher levels of compassion fatigue and burnout are associated with lower levels of caring behaviors [10, 11, 13]. Recent ICU nursing studies have predominantly examined relationships among compassion-related constructs and professional outcomes using cross-sectional and pairwise approaches. Evidence indicates negative associations between compassion and compassion fatigue, while stress, organizational demands, and emotional burden have been positively associated with compassion fatigue. Caring behaviors have also been linked to compassion competence and organizational factors, whereas burnout has been associated with both personal and contextual characteristics [2–4, 9–11]. Although these findings provide important insight into direct relationships, they largely reflect fragmented evidence derived from bivariate analyses rather than integrated explanatory models.

Furthermore, existing ICU research has not adequately clarified the mechanisms through which compassionate dispositions translate into professional outcomes. Notably, although compassion fatigue is widely recognized within the professional quality of life framework, empirical studies have rarely examined its role within partial mediational pathways linking compassionate resources to burnout and caring behaviors. The predominance of cross-sectional designs and the reliance on bivariate analyses limit understanding of whether compassion fatigue represents a transitional emotional process connecting compassionate love to professional well-being and care delivery, or whether these variables operate independently. This limitation is particularly relevant in intensive care settings, where repeated exposure to suffering, death, and ethical dilemmas may gradually transform compassionate engagement into emotional exhaustion. The cumulative empathic demands inherent in ICU care may therefore position compassion fatigue as a potential intermediary mechanism through which compassionate love is associated with both burnout and caring behaviors. Accordingly, the present study adopts an integrative perspective by examining compassion fatigue as a potential mediating mechanism within this relationship pattern. Exploring indirect pathways may contribute to a better understanding of how compassionate dispositions are associated with professional outcomes in emotionally demanding ICU contexts.The hypotheses of the study are listed below (Fig. 1).

Methods

Aims

The objective of this study was to explore whether compassion fatigue serves as a mediator in the relationship between compassionate love, burnout, and caring behaviors among nurses working in ICUs.

Design

A cross-sectional, correlational research design was employed, and the study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Data were gathered between October 2024 and April 2025 through an online survey created with Google Forms. This method was selected for its convenience and its ability to reach a large number of nurses from different hospitals efficiently. Before participation, all nurses received detailed information regarding the aim of the study, voluntary participation, and confidentiality. Informed consent was obtained electronically, and responses remained anonymous.

Settings

The study was conducted in adult ICUs of several public and private hospitals across Türkiye. These hospitals are characterized by a high patient volume and a large workforce of intensive care nurses, making them suitable settings for investigating emotional and behavioral aspects of critical care practice. Data collection was conducted using an online self-administered questionnaire administered via Google Forms.

Participants

Eligible participants were ICU nurses who

(1) were currently employed in an adult intensive care unit, (2) had at least three years of ICU experience, and (3) held a bachelor’s degree or higher in nursing. A minimum of three years of ICU experience was required to ensure sustained exposure to the emotional, ethical, and clinical demands inherent in intensive care practice, which are considered necessary for the development and stabilization of compassion fatigue and burnout-related processes. This criterion also allowed for a more reliable assessment of caring behaviors within the context of prolonged professional experience [2, 4, 9].

Exclusion criteria included

(1) being on unpaid or maternity leave during the data collection period, and (2) self-reporting a current or past psychiatric diagnosis made by a physician, which could potentially influence emotional responses and study outcomes.

Sample size

The sample size was estimated using G*Power (version 3.1.9) [14]. A small effect size was assumed (r = 0.15), which falls within the “small” range for correlations proposed by Cohen (1988). With α = 0.05 and power = 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 323. As this cross-sectional online survey yielded 323 complete responses, no additional inflation for non-response or incomplete questionnaires was applied. Although participants were recruited from multiple public and private hospitals, no cluster-based sampling or institutional stratification was employed. A convenience sampling method was used, and all eligible ICU nurses who met the inclusion criteria and voluntarily agreed to participate were included.

Measures

Sociodemographic Information Form

This form was developed based on relevant literature [4, 5, 10, 15], and consisted of 10 items related to the participants’ clinical unit, length of service in the current ICU, total professional experience, shift type, weekly working hours, history of psychiatric diagnosis, and psychiatric medication use.

Compassionate Love Scale

The Compassionate Love Scale was originally developed by Sprecher and Fehr (2005) [16], and later adapted into Turkish by Akın and Eker (2011) [17]. It consists of 21 items without any sub-dimensions, and the responses are given on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not at all true of me”) to 7 (“very true of me”). The total score range of the scale is 21–147, with higher scores indicating higher levels of compassionate love. Item-total correlations were reported between 0.28 and 0.70. The instrument demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.89) as well as satisfactory test-retest reliability (r = 0.82).

Short Compassion Fatigue Scale

The Short Compassion Fatigue Scale was created by Adams et al. (2006) [18], and later translated and validated for Turkish use by Dinç and Ekinci (2019). The instrument contains 13 items and evaluates compassion fatigue across two dimensions: secondary trauma (items c, e, h, j, l) and burnout (items a, b, d, f, g, i, k, m). Each item is scored on a 10-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“rarely/never”) to 10 (“very often”), yielding a possible total score range of 13 to 130 [19]. While the scale does not specify a cut-off value, higher overall scores reflect greater compassion fatigue. In the Turkish adaptation, internal consistency was acceptable, with Cronbach’s alpha reported as 0.88.

Burnout Syndrome Assessment Scale for ICU Nurses (BOSAS-ICU)

The BOSAS-ICU was originally designed by Choudhary et al. [20], and later adapted into Turkish by Dağ, Aydın, and Turan [21]. The Turkish adaptation includes 20 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always), resulting in a possible total score between 0 and 80. Psychometric testing demonstrated strong reliability and validity: internal consistency was very high (Cronbach’s α ≈ 0.95), test-retest reliability showed robust intraclass correlations, and confirmatory factor analysis indicated a unidimensional model fit (χ2/df = 2.23, CFI = 0.98, NNFI = 0.98, NFI = 0.97, SRMR = 0.067, RMSEA = 0.079). Content validity was also satisfactory, with item-level CVIs ranging from 0.833 to 1.000. These findings confirm that the Turkish BOSAS-ICU is a psychometrically sound instrument for assessing burnout in ICU nurses [21].

Although the Short Compassion Fatigue Scale includes a burnout-related component, compassion fatigue and burnout were treated as theoretically distinct constructs in the present study. Compassion fatigue reflects an acute, exposure-related response arising from sustained empathic engagement with patient suffering, whereas burnout represents a broader, work-related syndrome characterized by emotional exhaustion and disengagement over time. To minimize conceptual overlap, the total compassion fatigue score was used to capture overall secondary stress, while burnout was independently assessed using the BOSAS-ICU, which was specifically developed for this purpose. This approach allowed for a clearer examination of compassion fatigue as an intermediary process rather than a redundant indicator of burnout. In addition, multicollinearity diagnostics indicated acceptable independence between the constructs, further supporting the distinction between compassion fatigue and burnout in the present model.

Caring Behaviors Inventory-24 (CBI-24)

The Caring Behaviors Inventory-24 (CBI-24) is a shortened version of the original CBI-42 (Wolf et al., 1994; Wu & Putnam, 2006) [22, 23], and was adapted into Turkish by Kurşun and Kanan (2012). The instrument consists of 24 items distributed across four dimensions: assurance (8 items), knowledge and skill (5 items), respectfulness (6 items), and connectedness (5 items). Each item is rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 6 (always) [24]. Scores for each subscale and the overall scale are calculated by averaging item responses, yielding a possible score range of 1 to 6, with higher scores reflecting stronger perceptions of caring behaviors. The Turkish version demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91.

Data collection

Data were collected using an online questionnaire administered via Google Forms. Before participation, all eligible nurses were presented with an electronic informed consent form outlining the purpose of the study, its voluntary nature, and confidentiality assurances. Only those who provided informed consent were able to access the questionnaire. The survey link was distributed via institutional email networks and professional nursing communication groups to reach ICU nurses working in multiple public and private hospitals, across different shifts and geographic locations in Türkiye. The use of multiple dissemination channels aimed to enhance accessibility and sample diversity, and to reduce the overrepresentation of a single institution or work setting. Participants completed the questionnaire anonymously, and no personally identifiable information was collected. To ensure anonymity, IP addresses and other identifying metadata were not collected. To minimize the risk of duplicate responses, participants were instructed to complete the survey only once. The study settings were designed to restrict multiple submissions from the same Google account, and response timestamps were reviewed during data screening to identify potential duplicate entries. All data were stored on a password-protected account accessible only to the research team, and the final dataset was downloaded and stored on an encrypted personal computer. No third-party access to the data was permitted. Although probability sampling was not feasible, broad outreach across diverse institutions was employed to mitigate potential selection bias associated with online data collection. The possibility of residual selection bias is acknowledged as a study limitation.

Ethical considerations

Prior to data collection, ethical approval was secured from the Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee (Approval Date: 05.08.2024; Approval No: 2763785). In addition, formal written permissions were obtained from the administration of the participating hospitals. Authorization to use or adapt the measurement instruments was also received via email correspondence from the original developers or from the researchers who had performed the Turkish validation studies.

Participation was strictly voluntary. All nurses were fully informed about the objectives and scope of the study, and confidentiality procedures before enrollment. Electronic informed consent was obtained on the opening page of the online survey, and only those who confirmed consent could continue to the questionnaire. No personal identifiers were collected, and all information was treated as confidential, being used solely for scientific purposes.

Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize demographic characteristics and primary study variables. Prior to inferential analyses, key statistical assumptions were assessed. Normality of data distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and by examining skewness and kurtosis values, with values between −1 and +1 considered indicative of approximate normality. Linearity was assessed through visual inspection of scatterplots, and multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF) values. VIF values were low (VIF = 1.146), indicating no multicollinearity concerns. Based on these assessments, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to examine associations among continuous variables. Simple linear regression and partial mediation analyses were conducted using the PROCESS macro for SPSS (Model 4), developed by Hayes (2013) [25]. This approach enabled the estimation of total, direct, and indirect effects to assess whether compassion fatigue mediated the relationships between compassionate love, burnout, and caring behaviors. The significance of indirect effects was tested using bootstrapping with 5000 resamples and a 95% confidence interval; partial mediation was considered statistically significant when the confidence interval did not include zero. Before partial mediation analyses, selected demographic and occupational variables (age, gender, educational level, years of professional experience, ICU type, and ICU training status) were examined in relation to the main study variables. As these variables did not demonstrate consistent or theoretically meaningful associations with both predictors and outcomes, they were not included as covariates, and partial mediation analyses were conducted without covariate adjustment.

Results

Although a significant number of nurses accessed the survey link, only the fully completed questionnaires were included in the analysis. As a result, the final analytical sample consisted of 323 participants with complete data. The sociodemographic and professional characteristics of the participating nurses are summarized in Table 1. The majority of the participants were female (81.1%) and held a bachelor’s degree (90.4%), while 9.6% had postgraduate education. Most nurses worked in general ICUs (60.7%), followed by the coronary ICU (22.9%) and the neurology ICU (8%). A smaller proportion were employed in neonatal/pediatric (2.5%), emergency (1.9%), neurosurgical (0.9%), and anesthesiology (0.6%) ICUs. Approximately 35.6% of the nurses had completed an official ICU nursing certification, and 43.3% had attended an ICU-related training program. Nearly two-thirds (65.6%) of participants reported that working in the ICU was their personal choice. In terms of mental health, 6.2% had a diagnosed psychiatric condition, and 4.6% reported current use of psychotropic medications. The mean age of participants was 30.39 ± 5.68 years (range = 21–50 years), and the mean duration of professional experience was 8.70 ± 8.15 years (range = 3–35 years). Overall, the sample primarily consisted of young, female nurses with bachelor’s degrees, who were working in general ICUs.

Discussion

This study examined the relationships among compassionate love, compassion fatigue, burnout, and caring behaviors in ICU nurses and tested the mediating role of compassion fatigue. The findings demonstrated that higher levels of compassionate love were associated with lower compassion fatigue and burnout, and with higher caring behaviors. In addition, compassion fatigue partially mediated the relationship between compassionate love and both burnout and caring behaviors, highlighting its role as a key emotional mechanism in intensive care nursing. Given the unique characteristics of intensive care units, including continuous exposure to life-threatening conditions, frequent end-of-life care, and technologically complex interventions, the emotional processes identified in this study may be particularly pronounced among ICU nurses. From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore the importance of supporting nurses’ emotional resources to sustain caring behaviors and mitigate burnout in high-acuity care environments.

The inverse relationship between compassionate love and both compassion fatigue and burnout may be explained by the role of compassionate love as a relatively stable emotional resource that supports adaptive emotional regulation. Conceptual analyses describe compassionate love as extending beyond momentary empathy to include enduring concern, emotional commitment, and prosocial motivation, which strengthen nurses’ psychological resilience and professional identity [6–8]. In intensive care settings, where nurses are repeatedly exposed to suffering, ethical dilemmas, and end-of-life care, such emotional resources may buffer the cumulative effects of emotional strain. In ICUs, where emotional engagement must be sustained alongside rapid clinical decision-making and high mortality exposure, compassionate love may serve as a stabilizing emotional resource that supports both resilience and continuity of caring behaviors.This interpretation is consistent with research emphasizing the protective function of self-compassion and related compassionate capacities against empathy-based stress and emotional exhaustion. Wright and Pendry (2016) highlighted that self-compassion mitigates the negative effects of sustained empathic engagement [26]. Similarly, Özparlak et al. (2024) demonstrated that higher self-compassion is associated with greater psychological well-being and lower emotional exhaustion among nurses [27], while Lingyu and Rosa (2021) reported a significant burnout-reducing effect of self-compassion [28]. Together, these findings support the view that nurses’ emotional capacity is a key determinant not only of individual well-being but also of professional performance and care delivery.

It is also important to consider that compassionate love may not represent solely a stable individual disposition but may be influenced by contextual and professional factors. Supportive organizational cultures, leadership approaches, and ethical climates that emphasize compassionate care may facilitate the development and maintenance of compassionate orientations among nurses. Additionally, professional socialization processes and prolonged exposure to intensive care environments may shape how compassionate love is experienced and expressed over time. In particular, ICU tenure may simultaneously strengthen compassionate commitment while also increasing vulnerability to emotional strain, suggesting a dynamic interaction between individual emotional resources and the workplace context.

The negative association between compassion fatigue and caring behaviors suggests that prolonged exposure to patient suffering may gradually diminish nurses’ capacity to sustain attentive and patient-centered care. Compassion fatigue has been widely documented among ICU nurses and is consistently linked to emotional exhaustion, reduced compassion competence, and impaired professional functioning [1–5, 10]. Previous studies have also shown that compassion fatigue is associated with decreased caring behaviors and professional functioning [29, 30], and that stressors such as role conflict and excessive workload significantly contribute to compassion fatigue and burnout [31]. However, some studies have reported a lack of any significant association between compassion fatigue and caring behaviors, which may be attributed to contextual factors such as stronger ethical commitment, organizational support, or variations in workload and staffing patterns [32, 33]. In such contexts, professional values and institutional resources may buffer the negative effects of emotional exhaustion on care delivery. By contrast, in high-acuity environments such as ICUs, sustained exposure to critically ill patients may amplify the impact of compassion fatigue on caring behaviors. This effect may be intensified by the need to balance technological vigilance with relational care, a characteristic tension frequently reported in intensive care nursing.

The identification of compassion fatigue as a partial mediator highlights that compassionate love alone may not be sufficient to protect nurses from adverse professional outcomes. Even nurses with strong, compassionate dispositions may experience burnout and diminished caring behaviors when compassion fatigue accumulates over time. This finding helps reconcile inconsistencies in the literature by demonstrating that compassion fatigue functions as a critical explanatory mechanism linking emotional capacity to both burnout and care quality. Such mechanisms may be especially relevant in ICU environments, where sustained empathic engagement occurs under conditions of persistent clinical uncertainty and secondary traumatic exposure. Similar partial mediation effects have been reported in studies examining the role of compassion fatigue between organizational or personal resources and professional outcomes in nursing populations [5, 13]. Supporting this interpretation, Arimon-Pagès et al. (2023) showed that higher compassion fatigue among nurses was associated with poorer professional outcomes and reduced care quality [34]. Likewise, Mason et al. (2014) reported that compassion fatigue contributes to impaired clinical functioning, reduced attentiveness, and diminished care quality among trauma nurses [35]. These findings reinforce the view that compassion fatigue represents not only an emotional burden but also a serious professional risk threatening the sustainability of caring behaviors.

The intensive care environment is characterized by high emotional labor, exposure to secondary trauma, and sustained psychological stress, all of which increase vulnerability to compassion fatigue and burnout [2–4, 36–38]. Empirical evidence indicates that factors such as long working hours, inadequate institutional support, and high patient acuity intensify compassion fatigue among ICU nurses [3, 4, 39, 40]. The present findings suggest that subjective emotional resources, such as compassionate love, influence not only nurses’ emotional well-being but also their ability to maintain caring behaviors, particularly when shaped by compassion fatigue. Therefore, compassion fatigue should be viewed not merely as an individual emotional response but as a significant occupational risk that threatens the sustainability of high-quality care in intensive care settings [30, 36, 41].

From a practical perspective, the findings suggest several institutional strategies to foster compassionate love and mitigate compassion fatigue among ICU nurses. At the organizational level, structured psychosocial support programs such as regular reflective debriefing sessions, peer support groups, and access to counseling services may help nurses process emotionally demanding experiences and sustain compassionate engagement. Leadership practices that promote supportive supervision, recognition of emotional labor, and ethical climates valuing compassionate care may further strengthen nurses’ internal resources. In addition, workload regulation, adequate staffing, and rotation policies that limit prolonged exposure to high-intensity care environments may reduce cumulative emotional exhaustion. Training interventions focused on emotional awareness, self-compassion, and resilience-building have also been suggested as effective approaches to enhancing compassionate capacities while buffering against compassion fatigue. Together, these strategies may support the sustainability of caring behaviors and protect nurses’ professional well-being in intensive care settings. Although partial mediation analyses were conducted to examine indirect associations among variables, the cross-sectional design of the study does not allow for causal inference; therefore, the partial mediation findings should be interpreted as statistical relationships rather than causal mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that compassionate love is both directly and indirectly associated with burnout and caring behaviors among intensive care nurses, with compassion fatigue playing a partial mediating role. These findings indicate that compassionate love functions as a key emotional resource that supports nurses’ professional well-being and care capacity, while compassion fatigue represents a critical factor that can weaken these positive effects. From the perspective of nursing practice, these findings highlight the importance of recognizing compassion fatigue as a clinically relevant condition that may compromise nurses’ ability to sustain caring behaviors in intensive care settings. Interventions aimed at strengthening compassionate resources may help nurses maintain patient-centered care despite emotional strain. In terms of nursing management, the results emphasize the need for organizational strategies that reduce compassion fatigue, such as workload regulation, psychosocial support systems, and supportive leadership practices. Addressing compassion fatigue at the institutional level may contribute to reducing burnout and promoting sustainable care quality. Regarding nursing research, this study provides empirical evidence supporting a partial mediation model that explains how emotional resources influence professional outcomes in intensive care nursing. The findings underscore the need for future longitudinal and interventional studies to further examine causal pathways and evaluate the effectiveness of compassion-focused interventions. Finally, integrating compassion-related concepts, emotional self-regulation, and resilience-building strategies into undergraduate and subsequent nursing education programs may help prepare nurses to cope with the emotional demands of intensive care practice.

Clinical and research implications

The findings of this study have important implications for clinical practice, nursing education, and future research. From a clinical perspective, compassion fatigue emerges as a clinically relevant risk factor associated with increased burnout and reduced caring behaviors among intensive care nurses. Early identification of nurses experiencing high levels of compassion fatigue may help guide timely psychosocial support interventions aimed at preserving caring behaviors and preventing professional exhaustion in high-acuity care settings. From an educational perspective, integrating concepts such as compassionate love, emotional awareness, and compassion fatigue into undergraduate and in-service nursing education programs may enhance nurses’ capacity to sustain compassionate care while managing emotional demands. Training initiatives that promote self-reflection, emotional regulation, and resilience may further strengthen nurses’ emotional resources in intensive care environments. In terms of future research, longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to clarify causal pathways among compassionate love, compassion fatigue, and burnout, and to evaluate the effectiveness of strategies designed to strengthen compassionate resources while reducing compassion fatigue. Additionally, future studies should explore the role of organizational support, ethical climate, and cultural context in shaping these emotional processes and their impact on caring behaviors.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences regarding the relationships among compassionate love, compassion fatigue, burnout, and caring behaviors. Although partial mediation analyses were conducted to examine indirect associations, the temporal ordering of variables could not be established; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Second, all data were collected using self-reporting measures, which may be subject to response biases such as social desirability, recall bias, or common method variance. Participants may have overestimated positive attributes (e.g., caring behaviors or compassionate love) or underestimated negative experiences (e.g., burnout or compassion fatigue). Additionally, the use of convenience sampling and exclusive reliance on electronic questionnaires may limit the generalizability of the findings, as nurses less comfortable with digital tools may have been underrepresented. Future studies employing longitudinal designs, multi-source data, and objective or observational measures are recommended to strengthen causal interpretation and reduce potential reporting bias.
