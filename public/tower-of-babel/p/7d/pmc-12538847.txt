Impact of diastolic blood pressure time under range on mortality and acute kidney injury in septic patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jian Zhao, Si Tong Lin, Ai Hua Qin, Cheng Rui Zhou, Xiang Dong Huang, Hua Guo Chen, Shu Qin Zhou, Hu Peng, Yuan Zhuo Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent guidelines for sepsis management focus on maintaining mean arterial pressure, while the impact of low diastolic blood pressure (DBP) exposure remains unclear. This study investigated whether the time under range of DBP (DBP-TUR) is associated with clinical outcomes in septic patients who achieved conventional blood pressure targets.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC-IV database, we included 12,114 adult patients with sepsis. DBP-TUR was defined as the proportion of time with DBP < 50 mmHg while maintaining systolic blood pressure > 90 mmHg or mean arterial pressure > 65 mmHg during the first 48 h after ICU admission. Primary outcome was 28-day mortality, and secondary outcome was acute kidney injury (AKI).ResultsAmong the cohort, 6,192 patients (51.1%) experienced low DBP exposure. Patients were stratified into quartiles based on DBP-TUR (Q1: ≤5%, Q2: 5–15%, Q3: 15–50%, Q4: >50%). After adjusting for confounders, compared with Q1, both Q3 (OR:1.25, 95% CI:1.02–1.54) and Q4 (OR:1.27, 95% CI:1.02–1.57) showed significantly higher 28-day mortality. Similarly, AKI risk increased in Q3 (OR:1.47, 95% CI:1.14–1.91) and Q4 (OR:1.60, 95% CI:1.20–2.14). DBP-TUR demonstrated moderate predictive value for both mortality (AUC:0.73) and AKI (AUC:0.71).ConclusionLow DBP exposure, despite achieving conventional blood pressure targets, was independently associated with increased mortality and AKI risk in septic patients. Monitoring DBP-TUR might provide additional value in hemodynamic management of sepsis.Keywords: Sepsis, Diastolic blood pressure, Time under range, Mortality, Acute kidney injury

Introduction

Sepsis is a syndrome of organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Epidemiological surveys show that in 2017, there were approximately 48.9 million cases of sepsis and 11 million sepsis-related deaths globally, with a worldwide incidence of 677.5 cases per 100,000 population. In developed countries, the mortality rate of sepsis ranges from 15% to 25%, while in developing countries, it is higher, exceeding 40% [2–4]. For sepsis patients, early identification and intervention can improve prognosis, particularly precise management of hemodynamic parameters. Among these, diastolic blood pressure, an underestimated but important indicator, plays a crucial role in tissue perfusion and organ function protection.

Current sepsis guidelines focus on correcting tissue and organ hypoperfusion caused by hypotension as the core therapeutic goal. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines recommend maintaining mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≥ 65mmHg as the target parameter for hemodynamic resuscitation [5].However, this single indicator may mask important hemodynamic abnormalities. Clinical observations show that some patients maintain low diastolic blood pressure (DBP) despite achieving target MAP levels [6, 7], Reduced DBP leads to decreased coronary perfusion pressure, increasing the risk of adverse cardiovascular events and organ dysfunction [8, 9]. Other research indicates an association between reduced DBP and septic AKI, while no such association exists with MAP [10]. Therefore, in sepsis hemodynamic management, DBP should be considered a monitoring and intervention parameter of equal importance to MAP.

Blood pressure homeostasis depends on blood volume, cardiac output, and peripheral vascular resistance, with the latter being the determinant factor for DBP. Reduced DBP in sepsis patients stems from multiple mechanisms of vascular regulatory dysfunction: ATP-sensitive potassium channel activation, upregulation of iNOS expression, insufficient antidiuretic hormone secretion [11], and release of vasoactive substances such as pro-inflammatory cytokines and prostaglandins. Clinical research confirms that vascular responsiveness is an early predictor of ICU survival in sepsis patients. DBP >50mmHg and norepinephrine dosage < 0.5 µg/kg/min, as sensitive indicators of vascular responsiveness, more accurately predict early ICU survival than MAP and SAP [12], highlighting the unique clinical value of DBP monitoring in sepsis management.

Despite the significant physiological importance and clinical value of DBP, current sepsis guidelines primarily focus on target MAP values, lacking explicit recommendations for DBP. This practice underestimates the unique role of DBP in assessing tissue perfusion status. Clinical monitoring often employs single-point measurements or mean analyses, which fail to reflect the cumulative effects of dynamic blood pressure changes. Although the time-in-range concept has proven effective in chronic disease management, it has not yet been applied to sepsis hemodynamic assessment. Currently, systematic studies on the relationship between low DBP exposure duration and sepsis prognosis are lacking, and this gap limits the development of relevant clinical strategies.

Based on the above theoretical foundation, this study proposes a new parameter, Time Under Range (TUR) for diastolic blood pressure, and explores the relationship between DBP-TUR in the first 48 h of ICU admission and patient outcomes by analyzing clinical data from sepsis patients in the MIMIC-IV database. We hypothesize that higher DBP-TUR is associated with increased risk of adverse outcomes. This study will provide a new monitoring perspective for sepsis hemodynamic management, potentially improving risk assessment and individualized treatment strategies for patients.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, which contains comprehensive clinical data from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2023. These data include patient demographics, physiological variables, treatment methods, and post-discharge mortality outcomes up to one year [13, 14]. The study strictly adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and followed expert recommendations on the standardized use of critical care big data [15].

Study population

The study cohort was selected through a multi-step process. Initially, we identified patients with first-time ICU admissions in the MIMIC-IV database to eliminate confounding factors associated with readmissions. Subsequently, sepsis patients were identified using Sepsis-3 criteria, defined as suspected infection with a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score ≥ 2 at or before ICU admission. We excluded patients who were: (1) under 18 years of age; (2) had ICU length of stay < 48 h; or (3) had > 20% missing blood pressure monitoring data within the first 48 h of ICU admission.

Exposure variables

The primary exposure variable was Diastolic Blood Pressure Time Under Range (DBP-TUR), defined as the cumulative proportion of time with DBP < 50 mmHg while maintaining standard blood pressure targets (SBP > 90 mmHg or MAP > 65 mmHg). All DBP-TUR measurements were derived exclusively from ICU monitoring data, excluding any pre-ICU or emergency department blood pressure recordings.Blood pressure data were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database, including invasive arterial measurements (itemid: 220050–220052) and non-invasive measurements (itemid: 220179–220181).

We implemented a rigorous data cleaning protocol: (1) excluding physiologically implausible values (SBP: 60–250 mmHg, DBP: 20–150 mmHg, MAP: 30–180 mmHg); (2) ensuring SBP > DBP and MAP between DBP and SBP; and (3) calculating time intervals between consecutive measurements to determine DBP-TUR using a time-weighted approach.Specifically, DBP-TUR was calculated exclusively from blood pressure measurements obtained during the first 48 h following ICU admission, with no inclusion of pre-admission monitoring data. Time periods were counted toward the cumulative exposure when DBP < 50 mmHg while simultaneously achieving SBP > 90 mmHg or MAP > 65 mmHg. The final DBP-TUR was calculated as the ratio of cumulative low diastolic pressure duration to total effective monitoring time (periods meeting SBP > 90 mmHg or MAP > 65 mmHg criteria) multiplied by 100%.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality, obtained from discharge records and death registry information in the MIMIC-IV database. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) during ICU stay, defined according to KDIGO criteria as an increase in serum creatinine by ≥ 0.3 mg/dL within 48 h or ≥ 1.5 times baseline within 7 days, or urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h for > 6 consecutive hours.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) based on their distribution, while categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages). Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, or chi-square test as appropriate. DBP-TUR was categorized into quartiles (Q1-Q4), and survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank test to assess between-group differences. Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the association between DBP-TUR and 28-day mortality and AKI, adjusting for confounding factors including age, sex, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, chronic kidney disease), SOFA score, APACHE III score, and laboratory parameters. DBP-TUR was analyzed both as a categorical variable (quartiles) and as a continuous variable. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the predictive value of DBP-TUR for outcomes and to determine optimal predictive thresholds. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.1, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

This study included 12,114 patients who met the inclusion criteria, with 6192 patients (51.1%) experiencing low diastolic blood pressure exposure while maintaining standard blood pressure targets (DBP-TUR group) and 5922 patients (48.9%) not experiencing low diastolic blood pressure (non-DBP-TUR group), as shown in Table 1. The DBP-TUR group was characterized by older age, higher proportion of females, greater comorbidity burden, and higher disease severity scores (SOFA, APACHE III, Charlson Comorbidity Index, all P < 0.001). Additionally, the DBP-TUR group more frequently required vasopressor support (52.6% vs. 37.1%), and demonstrated higher rates of acute kidney injury (83.9% vs. 74.2%) and 28-day mortality (23.0% vs. 17.6%, all P < 0.001).

Distribution and grouping of DBP-TUR

Among the 6192 patients with low diastolic blood pressure exposure, DBP-TUR showed a right-skewed distribution with a median of 16.6% (interquartile range: 4.9%−49.8%), as illustrated in Fig. 1. Patients were divided into quartiles for analysis: Q1 (DBP-TUR ≤ 5%, n = 1578), Q2 (DBP-TUR 5%−15%, n = 1426), Q3 (DBP-TUR 15%−50%, n = 1688), and Q4 (DBP-TUR > 50%, n = 1500).

Clinical characteristics across quartiles

A progressive increase in age, female proportion, and comorbidity burden was observed with ascending DBP-TUR quartiles (Table 2). Disease severity markers showed a similar trend, with APACHE III scores increasing from 51.7 ± 21.0 in Q1 to 57.2 ± 22.5 in Q4 (P < 0.001). The highest DBP-TUR quartile (Q4) demonstrated the greatest vasopressor requirement (58.8%), acute kidney injury incidence (85.9%), and 28-day mortality (26.9%).

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed significant differences in 28-day survival across DBP-TUR quartiles (log-rank test, P < 0.001; Fig. 2). Survival curves began separating approximately 5 days after ICU admission and maintained differences throughout the follow-up period. The highest DBP-TUR groups (Q3 and Q4) exhibited markedly lower survival probabilities compared to lower exposure groups (Q1 and Q2).

Kaplan-Meier Survival Curves Stratified by DBP-TUR Quartiles

Dose-Response relationship and multivariable analysis

A clear dose-dependent relationship was observed between DBP-TUR and 28-day mortality (Fig. 3). Both observed mortality (shown as bars) and predicted mortality (shown as curve) demonstrated a progressive increase with rising DBP-TUR values. After adjusting for potential confounders, the relationship between DBP-TUR and clinical outcomes remained significant, as detailed in Table 3. Using Q1 as reference, the highest DBP-TUR quartile (Q4) was associated with a 27% increased risk of 28-day mortality (adjusted OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.02–1.57, P = 0.031) and a 60% increased risk of AKI (adjusted OR 1.60, 95% CI 1.20–2.14, P = 0.001). Trend tests confirmed significant increasing risks for both mortality and AKI across ascending quartiles (P for trend = 0.01 and 0.001, respectively).

When analyzed as a continuous variable, each percentage point increase in DBP-TUR was associated with a 0.3% increase in 28-day mortality risk (adjusted OR 1.003, 95% CI 1.001–1.006, P = 0.0123) and a 0.5% increase in AKI risk (adjusted OR 1.005, 95% CI 1.001–1.008, P = 0.0151).

Predictive value analysis

ROC curve analysis demonstrated moderate predictive capacity of DBP-TUR for clinical outcomes. For 28-day mortality prediction (Fig. 4), the area under the curve was 0.73, with an optimal threshold of 20.8% yielding sensitivity and specificity of 0.75 and 0.61, respectively. For AKI prediction (Fig. 5), the AUC was 0.71, with the optimal threshold identified at 81.8% (sensitivity: 0.70, specificity: 0.65).

Norepinephrine analysis across DBP-TUR quartiles

To address potential confounding by vasopressor therapy, we analyzed norepinephrine usage patterns across DBP-TUR quartiles. Norepinephrine usage demonstrated a progressive increase across DBP-TUR quartiles: Q1 (25.4%, n = 401), Q2 (31.1%, n = 443), Q3 (39.3%, n = 664), and Q4 (31.5%, n = 473) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6A). Among patients receiving norepinephrine therapy, maximum dose rates showed an ascending trend across quartiles: Q1 (0.178 µg/kg/min), Q2 (0.2 µg/kg/min), Q3 (0.2 µg/kg/min), and Q4 (0.23 µg/kg/min), indicating progressively higher vasopressor requirements in patients with greater low diastolic blood pressure exposure (Fig. 6B).

Norepinephrine Analysis Across DBP-TUR Quartiles

Critically, elevated mortality in higher DBP-TUR quartiles persisted even among patients receiving norepinephrine therapy: Q3 (34.3%) and Q4 (36.6%) versus Q1 and Q2 groups (Fig. 6C). Among patients in Q3 and Q4 quartiles who received norepinephrine therapy, detailed survival analysis demonstrated clinically significant outcomes: Q3 group had 436 survivors out of 664 treated patients (65.7%), while Q4 group had 300 survivors out of 473 treated patients (63.4%). Combined analysis revealed that 736 out of 1,137 high DBP-TUR patients (64.6%) survived despite intensive vasopressor support (Fig. 6D). This finding highlights the clinical relevance that nearly two-thirds of patients in the higher DBP-TUR groups achieved survival despite requiring intensive hemodynamic support, demonstrating that DBP-TUR’s prognostic significance remains independent of treatment intensity.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that low diastolic blood pressure exposure, despite achieving standard blood pressure targets (SBP > 90 or MAP > 65 mmHg), is independently associated with adverse outcomes in septic patients. This relationship exhibits dose-dependency, with higher DBP-TUR correlating with increased 28-day mortality and AKI risk. ROC curve analysis identified an optimal predictive threshold of 20.8% for DBP-TUR, providing a specific monitoring target for clinical practice.

Current sepsis guidelines emphasize MAP ≥ 65 mmHg as a resuscitation target but lack specific recommendations for DBP thresholds. However, recent expert consensus has begun to address this gap, with the 2023 Chinese expert consensus on blood pressure management in critically ill patients specifically recommending that diastolic arterial pressure should be maintained at >50 mmHg in patients with septic shock [16].This study quantifies the clinical significance of low DBP exposure through large-sample data, addressing this knowledge gap and providing temporal dimension analysis that supports these emerging recommendations.Previous research demonstrated that incorporating diastolic pressure into early warning scores improves sepsis identification accuracy [17], and DBP < 52 mmHg in emergency settings independently predicts progression to septic shock [18]. Our study extends these observations to the ICU environment and innovatively focuses on the temporal dimension.

The relationship between diastolic pressure and organ function preservation has preliminary evidence, though research data remain limited [10]. Studies have shown significantly lower diastolic pressure in patients who develop AKI [19], and pediatric data support the association between reduced diastolic pressure and severe AKI [20].Recent evidence from a large Chinese cohort further supports our findings, demonstrating that mean DBP during the first 24 h < 59 mmHg was independently associated with increased 28-day mortality in septic shock patients [21]. Our research not only confirms this association but also quantifies the cumulative effect of low diastolic pressure exposure, revealing a dose-dependent relationship between DBP-TUR and AKI risk. This may be related to diastole being the primary perfusion period for coronary arteries, kidneys, and other vital organs, with sustained low diastolic pressure leading to inadequate perfusion, tissue hypoxia, and organ dysfunction.This finding is supported by Liu et al., who showed that initial 24-hour perfusion index at ICU admission was independently associated with AKI in critically ill patients [22]. This underscores the complementary value of perfusion-related indices like PI and DBP-TUR in AKI risk assessment.

Regarding mortality risk prediction, previous studies identified admission diastolic pressure as an independent predictor of mortality in non-critically ill AKI patients [23], with the predictive value of DBP >50 mmHg on day 3 even exceeding SAPSII scores [12]. Our study, by introducing the DBP-TUR concept, provides a more dynamic assessment tool superior to traditional single-point measurements. This time-cumulative exposure assessment method more comprehensively reflects the impact of low diastolic pressure on patient outcomes, particularly for septic patients with significant blood pressure fluctuations.

The innovation of this study lies in the first application of the TUR concept to sepsis diastolic pressure management. While the TUR/TIR concept has proven effective in chronic disease management [24], sepsis-related research is rare. The ongoing ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 trial, a multicenter study of 1,500 patients, represents the first major randomized controlled trial to formally incorporate diastolic blood pressure into septic shock management algorithms, potentially establishing the first evidence-based framework for diastolic pressure-targeted therapy in sepsis [25].Recent trends in critical care emphasize the need for individualized blood pressure targets rather than a “one-size-fits-all” approach [26]. The OPTPRESS trial demonstrated that targeting higher MAP (80–85 mmHg) in elderly septic shock patients actually increased mortality compared to standard care [27], highlighting the complexity of hemodynamic management and supporting the need for more nuanced approaches like DBP-TUR monitoring. A pilot randomized controlled trial on individualized MAP targets showed that minimizing MAP deficit by targeting patients’ pre-illness MAP was feasible and safe [28], further supporting the concept of personalized hemodynamic management that our DBP-TUR approach represents. Methodologically, we employed direct measurement of time proportion rather than the traditional Rosendaal interpolation method [29], which is more suitable for high-frequency ICU monitoring scenarios. We believe this method is particularly important for capturing rapid hemodynamic fluctuations in septic patients.

Although research suggests increased cardiac output may compensate for low DBP effects [30], we found that approximately half of the patients still experienced low DBP exposure associated with adverse outcomes, indicating limitations in compensatory mechanisms. This finding supports the “dual threshold management” concept, which emphasizes monitoring dynamic changes in DBP while ensuring MAP targets are met [30]. The recent expert consensus recommendation of maintaining DBP >50 mmHg in septic shock patients aligns with our findings, as our optimal threshold of 20.8% DBP-TUR corresponds to patients spending approximately one-fifth of their time below this critical threshold [16].In clinical practice where direct microcirculation monitoring is unavailable, DBP-TUR may serve as a surrogate indicator of microcirculatory perfusion.

Based on our findings and supported by recent evidence, we recommend implementing DBP-TUR monitoring strategies in sepsis management. Specifically, setting a diastolic pressure alert threshold of 50 mmHg in ICU monitoring systems and calculating the cumulative proportion of time with DBP < 50 mmHg. When DBP-TUR exceeds 20.8%, clinicians should consider adjusting treatment strategies, such as reassessing volume status, modifying vasopressor types or dosages, or considering inotropic agents to increase diastolic pressure and improve tissue perfusion. This recommendation is further supported by recent evidence showing that patients with mean DBP < 59 mmHg during the first 24 h have significantly worse outcomes [21].Additionally, enhanced monitoring of organ function, particularly renal function, is warranted for patients with higher DBP-TUR.

The strengths of this study include its large sample size and rigorous statistical controls, though inherent limitations exist as a retrospective study. The single-center nature of the MIMIC database may affect external validity, and there is a lack of long-term outcome data and detailed cardiac function information.Additionally, this study does not capture supportive or life-saving interventions that patients may have received prior to ICU admission, such as fluid resuscitation, vasopressor therapy, or other hemodynamic interventions administered in the ambulance or emergency department. Since these pre-ICU interventions could potentially influence early hemodynamic parameters and patient outcomes, this represents a source of unmeasured confounding that may affect our findings.While these limitations do not undermine our main conclusions, they suggest future research should address the relationship between low DBP and long-term outcomes and cardiac function.

This study identifies DBP-TUR as a novel indicator for prognostic assessment in sepsis, emphasizing the temporal dimension of blood pressure monitoring. Future research directions include prospective validation of DBP-TUR’s predictive value and its applicability in different clinical settings; exploration of targeted intervention strategies to assess the impact of actively increasing diastolic pressure on outcomes; investigation of the correlation between DBP-TUR and microcirculatory changes for deeper insights into the mechanisms by which low diastolic pressure exposure affects prognosis; validation of optimal DBP-TUR thresholds across different patient subgroups (such as hypertensive patients, elderly patients, and those with cardiac dysfunction); and exploration of interactions between diastolic pressure and other hemodynamic parameters (such as pulse pressure and cardiac output) on outcomes. In conclusion, DBP-TUR, as a novel blood pressure management indicator, provides new perspectives and tools for hemodynamic monitoring and treatment strategy optimization in septic patients, potentially improving clinical outcomes in this high-risk population.

Conclusion

Low diastolic blood pressure exposure, despite achieving conventional blood pressure targets, was independently associated with increased mortality and acute kidney injury in septic patients. The dose-dependent relationship between DBP-TUR and adverse outcomes suggests the importance of monitoring diastolic blood pressure exposure duration. These findings indicate that DBP-TUR might serve as a potential predictor for risk stratification in septic patients.
