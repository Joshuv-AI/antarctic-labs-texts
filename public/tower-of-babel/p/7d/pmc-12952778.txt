Comprehensive analysis of subtype-specific outcomes and management in Castleman disease: a 20-year cohort study
Authors: Yoshito Nishimura, Thomas Habermann, Morie Gertz, Luis Porrata, Patrick Johnston, Yi Lin, Francis Buadi, David Dingli, Yucai Wang, Nelson Leung, Taxiarchis Kourelis, Thomas Witzig, Rahma Warsame, Joselle Cook, Allison C Rosenthal, Taimur Sher, Han W Tun, Angela Dispenzieri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Key Points•OligoCD was validated as a distinct subtype with intermediate outcomes and management implications.•iMCD-IPL was confirmed as a favorable Western subtype with unique responses, supporting subtype-specific treatment strategies.

Introduction

Castleman disease (CD) is a rare lymphoproliferative disorder characterized by histological findings, including lymphoid follicle hyperplasia, marked capillary proliferation with endothelial hyperplasia, and regressed germinal centers.1 CD is further classified into different subtypes by the number of lymph node affected and their distributions, and histology.2 Unicentric CD (UCD) is characterized by a single lymph node or a few lymph nodes in a single station often without systemic symptoms, and multicentric CD (MCD) that often presents with aggressive systemic features. Recently, oligocentric CD (oligoCD) has been proposed as an intermediate entity characterized by involvement of a few contiguous lymph node stations without MCD-defining symptoms.3 To date, the clinical behaviors of oligoCD are not yet clearly understood.

Among MCD, ∼50% of cases are associated with human herpes virus 8 (HHV-8) infections, often in immunocompromised hosts such as patients with HIV. The other 50% have negative HIV or HHV-8 status, namely idiopathic MCD (iMCD).4 Given its aggressive clinical behaviors, studies have been performed to elucidate iMCD subtypes to describe the optimal treatment strategies based on their clinicopathological phenotypes. iMCD has multiple clinical subtypes: iMCD with thrombocytopenia, anasarca, fever, renal dysfunction and/or reticulin fibrosis, and organomegaly (iMCD-TAFRO)5; iMCD with idiopathic plasmacytic lymphadenopathy (iMCD-IPL); and iMCD that does not fit into the existing categories (iMCD not otherwise specified [NOS]).6,7 Patients with polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, monoclonal protein, and skin changes (POEMS) syndrome may also present with iMCD-like symptoms and lymph nodes with CD histology. Histopathologically, there are at least 3 subtypes; hyaline vascular (HV)/hypervascular, plasma cell (PC), and mixed. UCD and oligoCD are often associated with the HV type, which has regressed germinal centers and marked capillary proliferation. Patients with iMCD-TAFRO often have hypervascular lymph node features, and iMCD-IPL generally corresponds to PC type and has been reported to be more responsive to interleukin-6 (IL-6)–directed therapy than other subtypes.8

Among the aforementioned CD subtypes, the anatomic subtype of oligoCD and the histopathological subtype of iMCD-IPL have recently been described3,7 and have been proposed by members of the Castleman Disease Collaborative Network (CDCN) to be bona fide new subtypes. We performed a comprehensive analysis of our CD data set over the past 20 years based on the new classification to elucidate clinicopathological characteristics, outcomes, and responses to treatment.

Methods

Study design and data collection

Patients with UCD, oligoCD, and iMCD, from 1 January 2004 to 4 August 2024, were included in this retrospective cohort study conducted at the Mayo Clinic. Diagnosis was based on reclassification upon data review. Patients who met criteria for POEMS syndrome (n = 30) and HIV and/or HHV-8–associated CD (n = 18) were excluded from this multisite Mayo Clinic study. Patients with UCD and iMCD have been described, in part, in a previous study.9 Those who had no pathology confirmation of CD diagnosis were excluded. All cases were independently reviewed by at least 2 hematopathologists as part of our institutional diagnostic workflow with expertise in CD. No formal central pathology review was performed. Data were extracted from the electronic health record system.

Definitions

UCD was defined as involvement of a single lymph node region. OligoCD was defined as involvement of ≥2 adjacent lymph node stations without meeting iMCD consensus diagnostic criteria.3,4 MCD required multicentric lymphadenopathy; iMCD was distinguished from HHV-8–associated MCD by negative HIV status and absence of HHV-8 on pathology or serology. iMCD cases were further categorized as TAFRO or IPL if diagnostic criteria for those subtypes were met, or as NOS if not. Briefly, patients were classified as iMCD-TAFRO if their lymph nodes met histopathological features of the International iMCD Diagnostic Criteria for CD4 and had all the following clinical features without another cause: (1) thrombocytopenia, defined as pretreatment nadir platelet level of ≤100 × 103/μL; (2) anasarca, which included pleural effusion, ascites, or subcutaneous edema; (3) fever of ≥37.5°C of unknown etiology or hyperinflammatory state, defined as C-reactive protein (CRP) of ≥2.0 mg/dL; and (4) organomegaly, defined as small volume lymphadenopathy in ≥2 regions, hepatomegaly, or splenomegaly on computed tomography scan.5 Patients were classified as iMCD-IPL if they met all the following criteria: (1) prominent polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia (γ-globulin of >4.0 g/dL or serum immunoglobulin G level of >3500 mg/dL), (2) multicentric lymphadenopathy, (3) an absence of active autoimmune disease leading to CD-like lymph node histopathology, and (4) normal germinal centers and a sheet-like infiltration of polyclonal PCs in the lymph node lesion.7,10 Patients who met criteria for POEMS syndrome were recorded but excluded from the primary analysis, as were those with positive HHV-8 status.

Data and statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics of patients with CD. Comparative analyses were conducted using Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, based on data distribution, and χ2 tests or Fisher exact tests when appropriate, for categorical variables. Primary outcomes were overall survival (OS) of each CD subtype and iMCD subtypes (oligoCD, UCD, and iMCD; and iMCD-IPL, iMCD-TAFRO, iMCD-NOS). Secondary outcomes included event-free survival (EFS) and time to next treatment (TTNT) after the first line of treatment. Clinical characteristics across CD subtypes were also explored. Time-to-event outcomes (OS, EFS, TTNT) were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and survival curves were compared by the log-rank test. Patients without an event were censored at last follow-up. TTNT was defined as the time from a previous line of therapy to the start of the next line of treatment; patients who died were censored at time of death for this analysis. For EFS, events were defined as either death, progression, or a change of treatment regimen. For comparison of categorical variables, a P value of < .01 was considered statistically significant. For survival and logistic regression analyses, a P value of < .05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP version 15.1.

The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic institutional review board as a retrospective medical records study (24-008536).

Results

Anatomic subtypes of CD: baseline characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the cohort by anatomic subtype. Among 217 patients, 20% had oligoCD, 57% had UCD, and 23% had iMCD. Specifically, 32 patients with oligoCD had 1 minor iMCD diagnostic criterion, 9 had 2 minor diagnostic criteria without laboratory abnormalities, and 2 had 3 minor diagnostic criteria without laboratory abnormalities. UCD had a higher proportion of females than oligoCD and iMCD. Median age was 43.0 years overall without differences among the CD anatomic subtypes. OligoCD, UCD, and iMCD had similar racial distributions, with overall 86% being White, although there was a slightly higher proportion of Asian patients in the iMCD group relative to others.

Most patients with oligoCD and UCD had the HV histological type (91% and 84%, respectively), whereas the PC histological type was the most predominant in iMCD (61%). Although autoimmune diseases were noted in 17% without differences among the groups, none had active diseases leading to CD-like lymph node histopathology. After the diagnosis of CD, 9% with UCD had solid tumors, most commonly sarcoma (55%). Specifically, there were 4 cases for follicular dendritic cell sarcoma, a known associated malignancy with UCD; 2 with non–small cell lung cancer; 1 with leiomyosarcoma, tenosynovial giant cell tumor, squamous cell carcinoma of tongue, prostate cancer, and Warthin tumor; respectively.

Main symptoms are summarized in supplemental Table 1. TAFRO-defining systemic symptoms, and fluid retention were more frequently observed in iMCD compared with UCD or oligoCD (P < .0001), whereas patients with oligoCD also exhibited significantly more fatigue, night sweats, fever, weight loss, tumor pain, and dyspnea than patients with UCD (P < .0001).

Patients with oligoCD had significantly lower albumin and higher CRP than those with UCD. Patients with iMCD had significantly lower hemoglobin and albumin and higher inflammatory markers and cytokines than those with oligoCD (supplemental Table 2). No differences in plasma or serum IL-6 and plasma vascular endothelial growth factor levels were observed between patients with UCD and oligoCD among patients tested.

Table 2 summarizes the demographics of patients with iMCD stratified based on clinical/histological subtypes. Patients with iMCD-IPL were more commonly Asian (44.4%) compared with iMCD-TAFRO (0%) and iMCD-NOS (8%). Anatomic subtypes of CD: treatment and outcomes

Indications for treatment choices and responses based on anatomical CD subtypes are summarized in Table 3. For patients with UCD, lymphadenopathy was the most common indication for treatment (88%), and surgery was the most common first-line intervention. For oligoCD, the most common indications for therapy were lymphadenopathy (49%), anemia (median hemoglobin, 13.8 g/dL), and the presence of B symptoms (26%); surgery was the most common first-line intervention. Of note, 27 (63%) were originally labeled as UCD and 16 (37%) as iMCD. The presence of anemia was associated with institution of systemic therapy (odds ratio for every 1 g/dL hemoglobin increase was 0.53 [95% confidence interval, 0.29-0.99], P < .01). Patients with iMCD had a variety of systemic symptoms, which led to the treatment initiation, and the most used first-line therapies included with rituximab (33%), followed by siltuximab (29%) and glucocorticoid monotherapy (16%); 69% had symptomatic improvement.

Treatment sequence is summarized in supplemental Figure 2. For UCD, surgery was the initial therapy for 89% of patients, with observation in another 7%. Second-line therapy was needed in 9 patients (7%); 3 of 9 (33%) patients had local recurrence but none progressed to iMCD. Interestingly, although 2 were lost to follow-up, none of those who were initially observed received further treatment. Of 10 patients with oligoCD who received rituximab as a first-line therapy, 5 required additional systemic therapy or a surgical intervention afterward. For 7 patients with oligoCD receiving glucocorticoid monotherapy as a first-line therapy, 3 (43%) required additional lines of treatment with either siltuximab, rituximab, or chemotherapy.

Subtypes of iMCD: treatment and outcomes

The most common treatments used for iMCD were anti–IL-6 antibodies (31%), rituximab (33%), and glucocorticoid monotherapy (16%). Initial response rate in terms of symptomatic improvement was high (89%) but short-lived. With the median follow-up periods of 3.8 years, 4.3 years, and 5.4 years for iMCD-TAFRO, iMCD-IPL, and iMCD-NOS, respectively, respective 5-year OS rates were 80.4%, 100%, and 91.0% (Figure 3). Compared with iMCD-TAFRO, iMCD-NOS had a lower hazard ratio for death of 0.39, but the result was not significant. During the follow-up periods, patients with iMCD-IPL had a 100% survival in our cohort. Respective 1-year EFS rates were 40.2%, 100%, and 65.6% for patients with iMCD-TAFRO, iMCD-IPL, and iMCD-NOS (Figure 4). Of patients with iMCD, 61% required second-line therapy (Table 4; supplemental Figure 3).

We analyzed treatment by clinical/histological subtype and found that a higher percent of patients with IPL received rituximab than those with TAFRO or NOS; IL-6 antibody therapy usage first-line was similar between the 3 groups, at 22% to 31% (Table 4). Although analysis was limited by small sample size, the IPL subtype was not only less likely to receive second-line therapy compared with the other 2 subtypes, but TTNT was longer. Patients with iMCD-TAFRO and iMCD-NOS were more likely to receive either siltuximab, rituximab, or glucocorticoid monotherapy as the first-line treatment (Table 4), whereas rituximab was most commonly used for iMCD-IPL with a high rate of symptomatic improvement (89%). TTNT for each iMCD subtype after the first-line therapy is summarized in supplemental Figure 3. iMCD-IPL had longer TTNT than iMCD-TAFRO and iMCD-NOS, but the differences were not statistically significant. Of patients with iMCD-TAFRO, with iMCD-NOS, and with iMCD-IPL, 75%, 58%, and 44%, respectively, required second-line treatment, most commonly either with siltuximab or rituximab (supplemental Figure 4). Patients with iMCD-TAFRO continued to have short TTNTs compared with the other subtypes beyond the second-line. Of patients with TAFRO, with IPL, and with NOS, 19%, 11%, and 19%, respectively, required fourth line or later.

Discussion

This cohort of 217 patients with CD, representing, to our knowledge, the largest single-institution study, provides a retrospective comprehensive subtype-specific analysis of clinical features, laboratory findings, treatment responses, and survival outcomes. Despite the recent advancements, gaps remain in the understanding of how the new subtypes, oligoCD and iMCD-IPL in particular, affect patient outcomes and treatment responses. iMCD-IPL was originally proposed in Japan as a separate iMCD subtype and is now reported to be the most common among iMCD in Asian countries. In the United States, iMCD-TAFRO was considered the most common subtype based on the CDCN registry. However, the registry data have their own limitations with the challenges in subtype validation due to the deficiency in critical data and reporting bias. It is not currently known whether geographical differences in iMCD epidemiology exist.11

Our study uniquely highlights 2 critical areas of novelty: the characterization of oligoCD as an emerging entity; and in-depth comparison of iMCD subtypes, including the newly proposed iMCD-IPL. Two of the most notable observations are (1) although patients with oligoCD may present with more symptoms than UCD, they can be treated very effectively with local therapy rather than systemic therapy; and (2) albeit based on low numbers of patients, we confirmed that iMCD-IPL appears to be a distinct entity that not only has a more benign course compared with iMCD-NOS and iMCD-TAFRO, but also seems to be sensitive to therapy with rituximab.

Distinguishing oligoCD from iMCD dramatically affects the management of patients. We demonstrated that except for organomegaly and fatigue, the clinical presentation of oligoCD is very similar to that of UCD. Although patients with oligoCD more frequently exhibited constitutional symptoms than those with UCD, this was significantly lower than what was seen in patients with iMCD. Moreover, 42% of patients with oligoCD were treated with local therapy (surgery or radiation); the remaining patients with oligoCD were treated with systemic therapy, which resulted in a need for second-line therapy in 63%. A factor associated with the use of systemic therapy as first-line therapy in oligoCD was anemia. The presence of B-symptoms showed a trend toward the use of systemic therapy but it was not statistically significant. We, and others, have shown that the presence of B-symptoms in UCD does not predict for failure, so, theoretically, it might not predict for failure in oligoCD, but more work will be required to verify our findings and to distinguish how often this entity can be cured by resection of an index lymph node akin to UCD vs an early or limited form of multicentric disease that will eventually require iMCD-directed systemic therapy. Careful long-term monitoring of patients with oligoCD is warranted, but given how similar the OS rates were for UCD and oligoCD (99.0% and 97.7% at 5 years), surgical resection (or other nonsurgical localized therapies such as nodal cryoablation or involved-field radiation, which have been used successfully in UCD) is feasible.12 Our results are similar to the recent report by Pierson et al describing oligoCD as undefined CD consisting of 14 patients having intermediate phenotype with a generally mild course similar to UCD without significant differences in index laboratory values including albumin, creatinine, CRP, and hemoglobin.3 However, it was noted that 5 of 14 patients with oligoCD in their cohort (35.7%) compared with 19.6% in the UCD and 40.3% in the iMCD cohorts experienced a subsequent disease flare, suggesting that patients with oligoCD might have UCD-like phenotype but similar outcomes such as those with iMCD. Of note, the higher oligoCD proportion in our cohort than in the existing literature likely reflects referral bias to a tertiary hematology center, and that the CDCN registry used voluntary submissions with a high volume of iMCD cases. It is notable how well our 123 patients with UCD did, with a 5-year EFS of 93.9% from the start of initial therapy. Although UCD is generally considered benign, paraneoplastic pemphigus, bronchiolitis obliterans, or follicular dendritic cell sarcoma, are known serious manifestations that can occur in the context.13 Although our cases rarely experienced such complications, it may be due to early surgical resections, which might have prevented them. Continued long-term follow-up of those cases is necessary to examine whether UCD might increase the lifetime risk of the complications or whether the risk is reduced after resection.

In contrast to the outstanding outcomes seen in UCD and oligoCD, patients with iMCD fared less well. iMCD is recognized as a heterogeneous entity with at least 3 distinct subtypes: the newly described iMCD-IPL, and iMCD-TAFRO and iMCD-NOS. Each subtype exhibits characteristic patterns of systemic involvement, laboratory abnormalities, and clinical trajectories. As previously reported from non-US countries, iMCD-TAFRO had the worst prognosis with the most aggressive disease course compared with iMCD-IPL and iMCD-NOS, with iMCD-NOS having intermediate outcomes, in between those of the other 2 clinical subtypes.14 Historically, although the dogma was that patients with PC type histology had inferior prognosis,15 in this series, we demonstrate that patients with iMCD-IPL had favorable outcomes. The IPL subtype was originally described in Japanese cohorts, and subsequently in Chinese cohorts, in which it represents the predominant iMCD phenotype.16,17 However, its significance in Western populations has remained uncertain. In our US cohort, patients with iMCD-IPL were more frequently treated with rituximab, which led to a long-term remission without the need for subsequent interventions. Although iMCD-IPL was reported to have favorable responses to anti–IL-6 therapy, likely related to its high plasma IL-6 levels as described in non-Western literature with a distinct clinicopathological phenotype,18 the utility of rituximab, which enables patients to have a break from treatment compared with anti–IL-6 therapy requiring long-term treatment, should be studied further.19,20 Also, there was a recent effort to improve diagnostic accuracy of iMCD-IPL using machine learning analysis.18 Future studies are warranted to validate its utility in non-Asian cohorts.

Not surprisingly, patients with iMCD-TAFRO experienced a higher early mortality and shorter TTNT than patients with the other subtypes. With the early disease-related mortality primarily occurring within the first 2 years, iMCD-TAFRO may require particularly aggressive upfront therapy to counter its fulminant systemic features. Currently, siltuximab combined with glucocorticoids or lymphoma- or myeloma-based chemotherapy are recommended for severe iMCD in the United States.21 Given that siltuximab requires a few infusions to reach a steady state,22 potential future considerations include possible upfront loading doses of siltuximab in these cohorts to confer early disease control.

Treatment response patterns differed among iMCD subtypes in this cohort. Although the benefits of using siltuximab as a first-line therapy was previously shown to provide durable disease remissions,20 siltuximab was administered as first-line therapy only to 31% of patients with iMCD-TAFRO, 22% of patients with iMCD-IPL, and 31% of patients with iMCD-NOS, leading to early need for the next line of treatment in iMCD-TAFRO and iMCD-NOS. Unlike siltuximab, which was approved after a phase 2 trial including 79 patients, rituximab outcomes are based on retrospective data from small numbers of patients.22,23

Response rates do appear comparable with respective overall response rates of 70% with siltuximab vs 56% with rituximab. The systematic review that reported 63% complete response rates with rituximab monotherapy only included 9 patients with iMCD.24 Given the rarity of the diseases and frequent relapses, patients with iMCD often get referred to tertiary academic centers after failure of first-line therapy. Overall, to improve the outcomes of patients with iMCD in the United States as a whole, we might need 2-faceted approaches; development of subtype-specific therapy and strategies for anti–IL-6-refractory cases using advanced technology such as next-generation sequencing and single-cell analysis of each subtype to identify target molecules (ie, JAK/STAT signaling, C-X-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 13, the mammalian target of rapamycin, and tumor necrosis factor),25, 26, 27, 28 advocacy for community providers who are not familiar with the management of CD to follow the standard treatment sequence, and for an early referral to academic centers for severe cases or in cases with diagnostic uncertainty.29

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective, single-center (albeit 3 geographical Mayo Clinic sites across the United States) analysis, selection bias and referral bias may have influenced the findings. Second, the relatively small sample size within iMCD subtypes, particularly iMCD-IPL, limited the ability to perform multivariate analyses or to draw definitive conclusions about treatment efficacy. Third, the classification of oligoCD remains inherently subjective without universally accepted diagnostic criteria. Fourth, a separate central pathology review was not performed in this study. However, this series represents, to our knowledge, the largest single-institution data set to compare CD subtypes based on the disease distribution including oligoCD, and iMCD subtypes using standardized definitions in detail. The systematic collection of laboratory parameters, treatment histories, and survival outcomes provides robust, clinically relevant insights that can inform future research.

In conclusion, this study contributes novel insights into the clinical, laboratory, and treatment response profiles of oligoCD compared with UCD and iMCD, and may validate iMCD-IPL as a new subtype in the Western cohort. The findings underscore that oligoCD, although often amenable to local therapies, may show features suggestive of systemic progression in a few cases. This finding requires prospective, multicenter validation, with particular attention to the see whether patients with symptomatic oligoCD benefit the most from surgery or other types of localized therapy used for symptomatic UCD, or systematic therapy used for iMCD. Also, it is crucial to accurately distinguish between iMCD-IPL and iMCD-TAFRO or iMCD-NOS, because these subtypes exhibit distinct patterns of disease progression and treatment response. Future research may further elucidate and define various subtypes and subtype-specific treatments to confer better long-term outcomes for this rare disease.
