MCPPIR promotes cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac repair via o8G oxidation of POC1B mRNA
Authors: Tao Wang, Xin-Zhe Chen, Hong-Fei Xu, Jin-Xi Wang, Jia-Hao Ren, Lu-Yu Zhou, Cui-Yun Liu, Xin-Min Li, Yu-Qin Wang, Su-Min Yang, Mei-Hua Zhang, Yu-Hui Zhang, Kai-Yang Lin, Jin-Wei Tian, Kun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe adult mammalian heart possesses severely limited regenerative capacity. Cardiomyocyte loss during cardiac injury, coupled with this restricted regenerative potential, represents a fundamental cause of heart failure and associated mortality. While PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) are abundantly expressed in cardiac tissue, their functional roles and molecular mechanisms in cardiomyocyte proliferation and heart regeneration remain largely undefined. In this study, we systematically investigated piRNA-mediated regulation of cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac repair processes.Methods and resultsUsing piRNA microarray analysis, we identified a novel piRNA regulating cardiomyocyte proliferation, which we named MCPPIR (myocardial cell proliferation-promoting piRNA). Genetic ablation of MCPPIR in mice attenuated cardiomyocyte proliferation and impaired neonatal heart regeneration, while MCPPIR overexpression enhanced proliferation, reduced fibrosis, and improved cardiac function post-myocardial infarction. Through mass spectrometry and RNA pull-down assays, we identified HNRNPH1 as a key binding partner. Cardiomyocyte-specific HNRNPH1 knockout mice displayed enhanced proliferative capacity. o8G-RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing revealed POC1B as the downstream target, with MCPPIR preventing HNRNPH1-mediated repression of POC1B mRNA. Mechanistically, the MCPPIR-HNRNPH1-POC1B axis maintains centrosome integrity, thereby promoting cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac repair.ConclusionOur study reveals a previously unrecognized role of piRNAs in regulating cardiomyocyte proliferation. We demonstrate that MCPPIR drives cardiomyocyte proliferation and promotes cardiac repair in adult hearts through o8G-mediated post-transcriptional regulation of POC1B mRNA. These findings establish the MCPPIR/POC1B axis as a promising therapeutic target for ischaemic heart diseases and a novel paradigm for developing regenerative therapies against myocardial injury.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) is one of the major contributors to global morbidity and mortality, characterized by the irreversible loss of cardiomyocyte function and the eventual development of heart failure, which severely impairs patients’ quality of life.1,2 During mammalian heart development, cardiomyocytes possess a strong regenerative capacity during the embryonic period, whereas postnatal cell division gradually ceases after a short proliferative window, resulting in impaired proliferation and greatly limiting the ability of the heart to regenerate and repair. The loss or permanent depletion of a large number of cardiomyocytes ultimately triggers pathological cardiac remodelling due to the insufficient natural renewal capacity and limited pool of adult cardiomyocytes.3,4 Given the unique developmental pattern of cardiomyocytes, active restoration or functional compensation of cardiomyocytes following myocardial injury represents a key strategy for cardiac repair.

Previous studies have shown that pre-existing cardiomyocyte proliferation is a key driver of cardiac regeneration.5 Cardiomyocyte proliferation enhances cardiac regeneration through multiple and complex signalling regulations of dedifferentiation, proliferation, and redifferentiation. During cardiac development, cardiomyocyte proliferation will be tightly controlled by a number of specific regulatory signalling factors. In myocardial injury, the extracellular matrix component versican activates integrin β1 and extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 and Akt, thereby promoting cardiomyocyte proliferation, reducing fibrosis, and improving cardiac function.6 Studies have shown that Oncostatin M derived from cardiac macrophages binds to the type II receptor glycoprotein 130 and enhances Yap expression through stimulation of Src, promoting cardiomyocyte proliferation and myocardial repair independent of the Hippo pathway.7 Overall, many studies have shown that the Hippo–YAP pathway plays a key role in regulating cardiac development, growth and homeostasis. YAP/TAZ is a promising therapeutic target in cardiac regenerative medicine as a potential regulator of tissue and organ regeneration.8 ,9 Furthermore, both the Notch and Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathways promote myocardial proliferation and tissue repair through transcriptional upregulation of key target genes, including c-MYC, cyclin D1, and CDKN1A.10–13 In summary, the synergistic interplay among multiple signalling pathways activates the proliferative capacity of cardiomyocytes, offering novel therapeutic avenues for cardiovascular disease intervention.

PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) are a novel class of 21–35 nucleotide noncoding RNAs, predominantly expressed in mammalian germline and stem cells. Characterized by 3′ terminal 2'-O-methylation, they play important regulatory roles in both developmental and pathophysiological processes.14,15 Recent studies have reported that piRNAs are also widely expressed in the cardiovascular system and participate in regulating cardiovascular disease development.16,17 For example, piRNAs are abundantly expressed during cardiac hypertrophy and promote pathological hypertrophy and cardiac remodelling.16 Furthermore, piRNAs promote N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) acetylation of Tfec mRNA through direct interaction with NAT10. This acetylation enhances TFEC expression, subsequently inducing BCL2-interacting killer accumulation and promoting cardiomyocyte apoptosis.17 In recent years, noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) have been extensively studied as a new regulator of cardiac proliferation in the cardiovascular field. ncRNAs, including microRNAs, long noncoding RNAs, and circular RNAs, are increasingly recognized as important regulators of cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac regeneration.18–20 Multiple classes of noncoding RNAs enhance cardiomyocyte proliferation through coordinated regulation of cellular processes and molecular mechanisms that overcome cell cycle arrest, thereby facilitating structural and functional recovery of injured cardiac tissue. However, the functional role of piRNAs in cardiomyocyte proliferation and their underlying molecular mechanisms remain largely unexplored.

8-Oxoguanine (o8G) is an oxidative modification of guanine present within DNA or RNA that induces a guanine to thymine (G > T) mutation followed by pairing with adenine, and plays an important role in mutagenesis, tumour proliferation, and aging processes.21,22 Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production triggered by environmental stress or pathophysiological conditions disrupts homeostatic regulation, leading to oxidative stress. This oxidizes biomolecules (nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids), producing dual effects: serving as redox signalling mediators when functioning as second messengers, or causing oxidative damage when affecting cellular components.23 As an essential biomolecule, o8G is usually responsible for the release of excessive intracellular oxygen free radicals, causing impairment of genetic integrity and stability, and is widely considered as one of the markers of oxidative damage to DNA and RNA. Upon exposure to oxidative stress, the guanine of nucleic acids is susceptible to form 8-oxoguanine, which can be either produced directly at the DNA (8-oxo-dG) and RNA (o8G) levels.21 Interestingly, o8G oxidative modification is abundant in RNAs, and position-specific oxidation of miRNAs could serve as an epitranscriptional mechanism to coordinate pathophysiological redox-mediated gene expression.22,24 Specific inhibition of o8G oxidation-modified miR-1 in the seed region attenuates the progression of cardiac hypertrophy, and o8G-miR-1 redirects target repression via o8G-A base pairing.22 Widespread 8-oxoguanine modifications in miRNA seed regions differentially regulate redox-dependent cancer progression.24 As the postnatal heart transitions its mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, the resulting increase in ROS induces DNA damage that triggers cardiomyocyte cell cycle arrest.25 However, while the regulatory role of o8G modifications in miRNAs has been established, it remains unknown whether oxidative mRNA modifications similarly function as epigenetic regulators in cardiac pathophysiology, particularly in cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac regeneration.

In this study, we demonstrated that MCPPIR expression is down-regulated during both postnatal development and adulthood. Genetic ablation of MCPPIR attenuated cardiomyocyte proliferation and impaired neonatal heart regeneration. Conversely, MCPPIR overexpression enhanced cardiomyocyte proliferation, reduced fibrosis, and improved cardiac function following myocardial infarction. Furthermore, we identified POC1B as a direct downstream target of MCPPIR. Mechanistically, MCPPIR promotes o8G modification and upregulates POC1B expression by preventing HNRNPH1 binding to POC1B mRNA. The resulting increase in POC1B enhances centrosome integrity, thereby stimulating cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac repair. Our study demonstrates that MCPPIR regulates cardiomyocyte proliferation through oxidative modification of POC1B mRNA. These findings provide novel mechanistic insights into cardiomyocyte proliferation and reveal potential therapeutic targets for ischaemic heart disease.

Methods

All procedures with animals were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Qingdao University (Approval No.20210512C574520241115136) and performed in accordance with the guidelines from Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament. The animals used in this study were C57BL/6J mice (purchased from Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd., Jinan, Shandong Province, China). All animal experiments were performed in mice of similar age and weight, and mice with normal physiological parameters during surgery were used for analyses. Mice were anaesthetized continuously with 1.5–2% isoflurane via a respiratory device and body temperature was maintained at 37°C throughout the procedure. After the experiment, all animals were anaesthetized by isoflurane inhalation (1.5–2%) and then euthanized by cervical dislocation. Detailed methods are provided in the Supplementary material online, Supplemental Material.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD. Graphpad Prism 8 software package was used for statistical analyses. Student’s t-test was used for the comparison of statistical significance between two groups, and all tests were determined by unpaired two-sided tests. Comparisons between multiple groups were assessed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test.

Results

MCPPIR promotes cardiomyocyte proliferation in vitro

To investigate the role of piRNAs in cardiomyocyte proliferation and myocardial regeneration, we first analysed piRNA expression patterns in mouse hearts during postnatal development under physiological conditions. Using piRNA microarray analysis, we examined expression profiles in ventricular tissue at embryonic day 13.5 (E13.5), E18.5, and postnatal days 1 (P1), 4 (P4), 14 (P14), and 60 (P60) (Figure 1A and B, Supplementary material online, Figure 1A-C and Supplementary material online, Table S1). From the significantly differentially expressed piRNAs, we selected the top 10 most up-regulated and down-regulated piRNAs for experimental validation. Among these candidates, DQ719164 (piR-134486) demonstrated the largest fold suppression during both postnatal development and adulthood (Figure 1C), warranting its selection for further investigation. piR-134486 showed abundant expression in the heart compared to other organs in adult mice (Figure 1D), with significantly higher levels in cardiomyocytes than in additional cardiac cell types (see Supplementary material online, Figure 1D). Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) revealed that piR-134486 localized to both nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments of cardiomyocytes (see Supplementary material online, Figure 1E). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that piR-134486 regulates cardiomyocyte proliferation. We therefore selected it for further study, designating it as MCPPIR (myocardial cell proliferation-promoting piRNA). Subsequent observation revealed that circulating MCPPIR concentrations were markedly decreased in P60 mice compared to P7 mice (see Supplementary material online, Figure 1F).

Next, we investigated the functional role of MCPPIR in cardiomyocyte proliferation using in vitro assays. MCPPIR mimic (MCPPIR) or its negative control (NC) was purchased from GenePharma and used for MCPPIR overexpression. Transfection of neonatal mouse cardiomyocytes with MCPPIR overexpression (see Supplementary material online, Figure 2A) significantly increased the number of proliferating cardiomyocytes, as evidenced by elevated EdU- and Ki67-positive cells (Figure 1E and F). Moreover, MCPPIR-overexpressing cardiomyocytes showed markedly higher numbers of cells positive for the mitotic marker pH3 and the cytokinesis marker Aurora B (Figure 1G and H). Furthermore, MCPPIR antagomir (anta) or its negative control (anta-NC) was purchased from GenePharma and used for MCPPIR knockdown. MCPPIR knockdown (see Supplementary material online, Figure 2B) substantially decreased cardiomyocyte proliferation (Figure 1I and J and Supplementary material online, Figure 2C and D). These results collectively indicate that MCPPIR overexpression enhances cardiomyocyte proliferation, whereas MCPPIR depletion inhibits cell cycle progression and proliferation. Importantly, MCPPIR did not significantly affect the proliferation of additional cardiac cell types, including cardiac fibroblasts (see Supplementary material online, Figure 2E-G), endothelial cells (see Supplementary material online, Figure 2H-J), and smooth muscle cells (see Supplementary material online, Figure 2K-M).

Deletion of MCPPIR inhibits cardiomyocyte proliferation in adult hearts

To examine the in vivo function of MCPPIR in cardiomyocyte proliferation and myocardial regeneration, we created MCPPIR knockout mice using the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Figure 2A and B). Successful deletion was confirmed by PCR analysis (Figure 2C). While MCPPIR knockout did not affect baseline cardiac phenotype (Figure 2D), heart-to-body weight ratio (Figure 2E), survival rate (see Supplementary material online, Figure 3A), or cardiac function (Figure 2F and Supplementary material online, Figure 3B-D) in adult mice, MCPPIR-deficient hearts exhibited increased cardiomyocyte size (Figure 2G), suggesting reduced cardiomyocyte numbers compared to wild-type controls. Furthermore, proliferating cardiomyocytes were significantly decreased in knockout hearts, as demonstrated by fewer EdU-positive (Figure 2H and I), pH3-positive (Figure 2J), Ki67-positive (see Supplementary material online, Figure 3E and F), and Aurora B-positive cardiomyocytes (see Supplementary material online, Figure 3G). These findings indicate that MCPPIR deficiency impairs cardiomyocyte proliferation during cardiac development.

Deletion of MCPPIR hinders cardiac regeneration and repair after cardiac injury in neonatal mice

In contrast to adult mice, neonatal mice maintain transient cardiac regenerative capacity that is crucial for cardiac homeostasis. To assess the role of MCPPIR in this process, we examined cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac regeneration following apical resection (AR) in 1-day-old (P1) wild-type (WT) and MCPPIR knockout (KO) mice. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed significantly fewer Ki67-, pH3-, and Aurora B-positive cardiomyocytes in the resection border zone of MCPPIR KO mice at day 7 (P7) compared to WT controls (see Supplementary material online, Figure 4A-D). Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated complete myocardial regeneration in WT mice by P21, while MCPPIR KO mice exhibited extensive fibrotic scarring (see Supplementary material online, Figure 4E). To further investigate the effect of MCPPIR deletion on the regenerative capacity of neonatal hearts, P1 WT and MCPPIR KO mice were subjected to MI. The number of Ki67-positive (see Supplementary material online, Figure 5A), pH3-positive (see Supplementary material online, Figure 5B and C), and Aurora B-positive cardiomyocytes (see Supplementary material online, Figure 5D) decreased while the fibrotic area increased in MCPPIR KO mice compared with that in WT mice (see Supplementary material online, Figure 5E). In addition, the cardiac function in MCPPIR KO mice was impaired compared to that of WT controls following MI injury (see Supplementary material online, Figure 5F and G). Together, these results indicate that MCPPIR deletion suppresses cardiomyocyte cell cycle activation and abrogates the proliferative and regenerative responses in neonatal mouse hearts.

MCPPIR promotes cardiomyocyte proliferation and heart repair after injury

Due to the limited regenerative capacity of adult cardiomyocytes, myocardial injury typically leads to progressive fibrotic scarring and pathological remodelling. Therefore, promoting cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac regeneration has emerged as a crucial therapeutic strategy for myocardial repair. To evaluate MCPPIR’s potential in enhancing cardiomyocyte proliferation, we tested the effects of MCPPIR in neonatal hearts at postnatal day 6 (P6), when the regenerative window is closing. P6 mice were injected intraperitoneally with MCPPIR or its NC for a week. MCPPIR overexpression in cardiomyocytes (Figure 3A) did not alter cardiac morphology (Figure 3B), heart mass (Figure 3C), or left ventricular function (Figure 3D). However, we observed a significant decrease in myocardial cross-sectional area in MCPPIR-overexpressing hearts (Figure 3E), suggesting increased cardiomyocyte numbers. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that MCPPIR overexpression markedly enhanced the expression of proliferation markers Ki67 (Figure 3F and G), pH3 (see Supplementary material online, Figure 6A), and Aurora B (see Supplementary material online, Figure 6B and C). These findings demonstrate that MCPPIR activation effectively promotes cardiomyocyte proliferation.

To better understand MCPPIR’s role in cardiac regeneration, we injected MCPPIR into adult mice following myocardial infarction (MI) induced by left anterior descending coronary artery ligation. Two weeks post-MI, MCPPIR treatment significantly reduced infarct size (Figure 3H) and improved cardiac contractile function (Figure 3I), demonstrating its reparative potential after cardiac injury. Histological analysis revealed increased numbers of pH3-positive (Figure 3J), Ki67-positive (see Supplementary material online, Figure 6D), and Aurora B-positive (see Supplementary material online, Figure 6E) cardiomyocytes in MCPPIR-treated hearts. Stereological quantification confirmed a significant increase in total cardiomyocyte numbers (Figure 3K), indicating that MCPPIR enhances myocardial repair through cardiomyocyte proliferation. Importantly, MCPPIR injection showed no detectable effects on liver or kidney morphology and function (see Supplementary material online, Figure 7A-F). These findings collectively demonstrate that MCPPIR promotes cardiac regeneration and functional recovery after ischaemic injury.

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of MCPPIR-mediated cardiac regeneration, we employed a transverse aortic constriction (TAC)-induced heart failure model (see Supplementary material online, Figure 8A). Compared to negative controls, MCPPIR administration significantly enhanced cardiomyocyte proliferation, as evidenced by increased pH3-positive cardiomyocytes (see Supplementary material online, Figure 8B), reduced cardiac fibrosis (see Supplementary material online, Figure 8C), and improved cardiac function (see Supplementary material online, Figure 8D). These findings identify MCPPIR as a promising therapeutic target for heart failure treatment.

Transcriptome-wide o8g oxidation analysis to identify targets of MCPPIR

The physiological transition from intrauterine hypoxia to postnatal oxygenation triggers cardiomyocyte cell cycle arrest via mitochondrial ROS-mediated oxidative DNA damage,26,27 indicating that postnatal oxidative nucleic acid modifications may regulate cardiomyocyte proliferation. Beyond the established role of o8G modifications in miRNAs, we investigated whether mRNA oxidative modifications might also function as epigenetic regulators in cardiac pathophysiology, particularly in cardiomyocyte proliferation. To determine MCPPIR’s potential role in mRNA o8G modification, we performed o8G-RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (o8G-seq) in WT and MCPPIR KO hearts (Figure 4A, Supplementary material online, Figure 9A and B and Supplementary material online, Table S2). Over 50% of detected o8G-modified mRNA transcripts contained at least one o8G peak (Figure 4B). Sequence motif analysis identified two significantly enriched o8G-binding motifs (Figure 4C). In both genotypes, o8G peaks were primarily localized to coding sequences (CDSs), with notable enrichment near start codons (Figure 4D). Parallel RNA-seq analysis of WT and KO hearts (Figure 4E) revealed correlations between o8G modification levels and gene expression (Figure 4F and Supplementary material online, Table S3). We classified o8G peaks as: Hyper-oxidized (increased o8G): Hyper-down (increased o8G with decreased expression) and Hyper-up (increased o8G with increased expression). Hypo-oxidized (decreased o8G): Hypo-down (decreased o8G with decreased expression) and Hypo-up (decreased o8G with increased expression).

To identify potential downstream targets of MCPPIR, we focused on differentially oxidized genes with oxidation scores >10 (see Supplementary material online, Table S3). o8G RIP-qPCR analysis revealed that POC1B showed the most significant reduction in o8G modification in MCPPIR KO hearts compared to wild-type controls (Figure 4G and Supplementary material online, Figure 9C). These findings suggest that POC1B may function as a key downstream effector of MCPPIR in regulating cardiomyocyte proliferation.

MCPPIR promotes o8g modification of POC1B mRNA and increases its stability

To elucidate the mechanism by which MCPPIR regulates POC1B expression, we performed integrative genomics viewer (IGV) analysis, which revealed significantly reduced o8G peaks in POC1B mRNA from MCPPIR KO hearts compared to WT controls (Figure 5A). Consistent with this finding, both POC1B mRNA (Figure 5B) and protein (Figure 5C) levels were markedly decreased in MCPPIR KO hearts. Conversely, MCPPIR overexpression in cardiomyocytes increased o8G modification of POC1B mRNA (Figure 5D), accompanied by elevated POC1B mRNA and protein expression (Figure 5E and F). Furthermore, developmental analysis showed that both o8G modification and POC1B expression were substantially higher in early postnatal WT hearts than in mature hearts (see Supplementary material online, Figure 9D and E). Clinically relevant, POC1B expression was significantly reduced in heart failure (HF) patients compared to healthy controls (see Supplementary material online, Figure 9F).

Given that MCPPIR upregulates POC1B expression while its knockout downregulates POC1B, we performed actinomycin D-based mRNA decay assays to assess MCPPIR’s effect on POC1B mRNA stability. MCPPIR overexpression significantly increased POC1B mRNA levels (Figure 5G), demonstrating its role in enhancing POC1B mRNA stability. These findings established POC1B as a key o8G-modified target of MCPPIR in cardiomyocyte proliferation regulation. To investigate the relationship between o8G modification and mRNA stability, we knocked down antioxidant enzymes (catalase and SOD2) to elevate intracellular ROS levels. Antioxidase depletion markedly increased POC1B mRNA stability (see Supplementary material online, Figure 9G and H), confirming that oxidative modification enhances POC1B mRNA stability.

We further evaluated the expression and functional roles of Cyp26b1 and Cyp2e1. Compared to WT controls, MCPPIR KO hearts exhibited reduced o8G modification in Cyp26b1 and Cyp2e1 mRNA (see Supplementary material online, Figure 10A and B), along with decreased mRNA and protein expression levels (see Supplementary material online, Figure 10C-F). Functional assays revealed that neither Cyp26b1 nor Cyp2e1 affected cardiomyocyte proliferation (see Supplementary material online, Figure 10G-J), indicating these genes are not involved in MCPPIR-mediated regulation of cardiomyocyte proliferation. Future studies will investigate whether additional MCPPIR downstream targets contribute to cardiomyocyte proliferation regulation.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which MCPPIR regulates cardiomyocyte proliferation, we first identified MCPPIR-binding proteins using biotinylated MCPPIR for streptavidin pull-down assays in cardiomyocytes followed by mass spectrometry (see Supplementary material online, Table S4). Among the enriched proteins, HNRNPH1 (Figure 5H), which showed the highest peptide spectrum match count, was selected for further investigation. As an RNA-binding protein, HNRNPH1 plays crucial roles in transcriptional regulation during development and participates in fundamental biological processes including cell cycle progression and transcriptional activation, though its function in cardiomyocyte proliferation remains unexplored. RNA pull-down (Figure 5I) and RIP (Figure 5J) assays confirmed the specific interaction between MCPPIR and HNRNPH1. Notably, neither MCPPIR knockout nor overexpression altered HNRNPH1 expression levels (Figure 5K and L), indicating that MCPPIR interacts with HNRNPH1 without affecting its abundance. These results suggested that MCPPIR interacts with HNRNPH1 and may be involved in the regulation of o8G oxidative modification.

MCPPIR regulates o8g modification and expression of POC1B by blocking HNRNPH1 binding to POC1B mRNA

To investigate HNRNPH1’s role in cardiomyocyte proliferation regulation, we generated cardiomyocyte-specific HNRNPH1 knockout mice by crossing HNRNPH1-floxed (HNRNPH1fl/fl) mice with Myh6-MerCreMer mice, enabling tamoxifen (TAM)-inducible HNRNPH1 deletion (HNRNPH1 cKO) in cardiomyocytes (Figure 6A and Supplementary material online, Figure 11A). Cardiac tissue analysis revealed significantly reduced HNRNPH1 levels in HNRNPH1 cKO mice compared to HNRNPH1fl/fl controls (Figure 6B). Although no morphological or functional differences were observed between genotypes (see Supplementary material online, Figure 11B), immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated significantly increased numbers of pH3-, Aurora B- and Ki67-positive cardiomyocytes in HNRNPH1 cKO mice (Figure 6C-E and Supplementary material online, Figure 11C), indicating enhanced proliferative capacity following HNRNPH1 deletion. To determine whether HNRNPH1 mediates MCPPIR’s regulation of POC1B mRNA o8G modification, we performed RIP-qPCR with anti-HNRNPH1 antibody. HNRNPH1 cKO hearts showed significantly reduced HNRNPH1 binding to POC1B mRNA (Figure 6F), accompanied by increased POC1B o8G modification and elevated expression levels (see Supplementary material online, Figure 12A-C) compared to HNRNPH1fl/fl controls. We further verified enhanced POC1B mRNA stability in HNRNPH1 cKO hearts (see Supplementary material online, Figure 12D). Conversely, MCPPIR KO hearts exhibited markedly increased HNRNPH1 binding to POC1B mRNA relative to WT controls (Figure 6G). These results demonstrate that MCPPIR promotes POC1B o8G modification and expression by competitively inhibiting HNRNPH1 binding to POC1B mRNA transcripts. Given that FAM161A serves as a scaffold protein for POC1B binding,28,29 we investigated whether MCPPIR-mediated HNRNPH1 dissociation affects FAM161A-POC1B mRNA interactions. Our data showed that FAM161A binding to POC1B mRNA was unaltered in both HNRNPH1 cKO hearts (see Supplementary material online, Figure 12E) and MCPPIR KO hearts (see Supplementary material online, Figure 12F) compared to their respective controls.

To determine POC1B’s role in cardiomyocyte proliferation, we first performed gain-of-function experiments. POC1B overexpression significantly enhanced cardiomyocyte proliferation, as evidenced by increased numbers of pH3-, Ki67-, and Aurora B-positive cardiomyocytes (Figure 6H, Supplementary material online, Figure 13A and B). For in vivo validation, adenovirus-mediated POC1B overexpression in adult mouse hearts improved cardiac function (Figure 6I), reduced infarct size (Figure 6J), and enhanced cardiomyocyte proliferation (Figure 6K and Supplementary material online, Figure 13C-E) following myocardial infarction, demonstrating POC1B’s critical role in regulating cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac repair. Nuclear content analysis of isolated adult cardiomyocytes revealed that POC1B-overexpressing hearts contained a higher percentage of mononuclear cardiomyocytes (see Supplementary material online, Figure 13F and G) and an increased diploid subpopulation among mononuclear cardiomyocytes (see Supplementary material online, Figure 13H), indicating that POC1B promotes karyokinesis during cardiomyocyte proliferation.

MCPPIR regulates centrosome integrity and cardiomyocyte proliferation by targeting POC1B

The centrosome comprises two barrel-shaped centrioles connected by proteinaceous linkers and surrounded by a dense pericentriolar matrix (PCM).30 As a crucial signalling hub for cell cycle regulation, the centrosome governs cell morphology, division, polarity, and differentiation.31 Previous studies have demonstrated that neonatal rat cardiomyocytes undergo irreversible centriole splitting during late foetal and early postnatal development, with concomitant loss of centrosome integrity shortly after birth, leading to G0/G1 cell cycle arrest.32 To investigate centrosome dynamics in mouse cardiomyocytes, we analysed centriole configuration using pericentrin (PCNT) immunostaining as a PCM marker. In cultured cardiomyocytes from embryonic day 15 (E15) and postnatal day 0 (P0) mice, centrioles predominantly maintained a typical paired configuration. Consistent with postnatal centrosome disassembly, the majority of cardiomyocytes from P3 and P7 mice displayed split centrioles (Figure 7A and B), confirming the loss of centrosome integrity during postnatal heart development. In addition, a previous report has also shown that neonatal rat cardiomyocytes undergo centrosome reduction that coincides with lost proliferation capacity and terminal differentiation, and the endogenous process of centrosome reduction involves PCM redistribution from the centrioles to the nuclear envelope.32 We observed that POC1B overexpression significantly altered PCM distribution. While control cardiomyocytes showed predominant PCNT localization at perinuclear regions, POC1B-treated cardiomyocytes maintained substantial centrosomal PCNT localization (Figure 7C). These findings indicate that POC1B overexpression inhibits centrosome disassembly (Figure 7D), prevents PCNT redistribution from centrosomes to perinuclear regions (Figure 7E), and correlates with enhanced proliferation capacity.

To determine whether MCPPIR regulates centrosome integrity and cardiomyocyte proliferation through POC1B, we first assessed centrosome dynamics. Genetic ablation of MCPPIR in mice significantly enhanced centrosome disassembly in cardiac tissue in vivo (Figure 7F), while MCPPIR overexpression in cultured cardiomyocytes potently inhibited this process in vitro (see Supplementary material online, Figure 14A). Furthermore, MCPPIR-mediated effects including POC1B upregulation (Figure 7G), reduced centrosome disassembly (Figure 7H), and enhanced cardiomyocyte proliferation (Figure 7I and Supplementary material online, Figure 14B) were all significantly attenuated by POC1B silencing. These findings demonstrated that POC1B serves as a direct downstream effector of MCPPIR in maintaining centrosome integrity and promoting cardiomyocyte proliferation. MCPPIR enhances centrosome stability and proliferative capacity by increasing o8G modification and expression of POC1B. To investigate potential crosstalk between the MCPPIR pathway and established cardiac regeneration pathways, including Hippo/YAP, Wnt/β-catenin, ErbB2, and neuregulin-1, we analysed the expression of key pathway components, such as YAP, β-catenin, ErbB2, and neuregulin-1. Our data revealed no significant alterations in these proteins following MCPPIR modulation (see Supplementary material online, Figure 14C), demonstrating that the MCPPIR pathway functions independently of these canonical cardiac regeneration pathways.

To evaluate MCPPIR’s function in human cardiomyocytes, we utilized the human induced pluripotent stem cell-induced cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs). MCPPIR treatment significantly enhanced hiPSC-CMs proliferation (see Supplementary material online, Figure 15A-C) and increased o8G modification (see Supplementary material online, Figure 15D) and expression (see Supplementary material online, Figure 15E and F) of POC1B. POC1B knockdown abolished MCPPIR-induced proliferative effects (see Supplementary material online, Figure 15G), establishing POC1B as a crucial downstream mediator of MCPPIR’s pro-proliferative activity in human cardiomyocytes. Furthermore, we identified a potential human homologue of MCPPIR (hsa-piR-2147). We examined the function of hsa-piR-2147 in hiPSC-CMs and observed that, similar to MCPPIR, it significantly promoted hiPSC-CMs proliferation (see Supplementary material online, Figure 15H-J), which strengthens the therapeutic potential and relevance of the piRNA-based strategy.

Discussion

In adult mammals, the premature loss of cardiomyocyte regenerative capacity ultimately results in pathological cardiac remodelling following myocardial injury. Promoting adult cardiomyocyte proliferation in situ has emerged as a crucial strategy for preserving myocardial function. The researchers discovered a previously unknown type of ncRNAs, called ‘PIWI-interacting RNAs’, in Drosophila, mouse, and rat germ cells in 2006. Unlike miRNAs and siRNAs, piRNAs are produced by the RNase III enzyme Dicer from hairpin precursors and long double-stranded RNA precursor transcripts, respectively. The piRNA precursor transcript is localized to ribonucleoproteins close to the nucleus and is involved in piRNA biogenesis and transposon silencing.33 It was shown that piRNA biogenesis is significantly conserved across species, and a unified model of piRNA biogenesis was proposed. In Drosophila melanogaster, piRNA biogenesis in germ cells exemplifies this unified model. The ‘ping-pong cycle’ relies on the exchange of two cytoplasmic PIWI proteins: Aub and Ago3.34 In conclusion, further investigation into the transcriptional regulation and biogenesis of piRNA clusters, as well as the functional characterization of PIWI-piRNA complexes, is essential for elucidating their roles in gene expression regulation and developmental processes.

piRNAs exhibit a striking degree of species-specificity, meaning they are generally not highly conserved in sequence or even in their target repertoire across broad evolutionary distances. Unlike many other small noncoding RNAs like microRNAs, which often show significant conservation, piRNAs evolve much more rapidly. The species-specific nature of piRNAs has significant implications for their translational relevance, particularly in the context of human health and therapeutic development. To overcome the challenges posed by piRNA species-specificity for translational relevance, researchers are employing several strategies to address these challenges. Developing sophisticated bioinformatics tools to identify novel piRNAs, predict their targets, and analyse their evolutionary dynamics in a species-specific manner. For therapeutic development, it is crucial to create tools that specifically interact with human piRNAs and their targets. While sequences differ, understanding the conserved underlying mechanisms of piRNA biogenesis, loading, and silencing can provide valuable clues for translational research. Researchers can navigate these complexities and pave the way for novel therapeutic and diagnostic applications related to piRNA biology in humans. In this study, we identified a potential human homologue of MCPPIR (hsa-piR-2147). Similar to MCPPIR, it significantly promoted hiPSC-CM proliferation, strengthening the therapeutic potential and relevance of the piRNA-based strategy.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated as by-products of aerobic metabolism, and its concentration must be balanced to maintain a normal redox state. However, increasing ROS production induced by environmental stress or pathophysiological conditions overwhelms homeostatic regulation, thereby imposing oxidative stress, and then oxidative stress oxidizes RNA to form 8-oxoguanine (o8G). In cardiac hypertrophy models, site-specific o8G modifications occur within the seed regions of selected miRNAs, regulating other mRNAs via o8G•A base pairing.22 Consequently, site-specific oxidation of microRNAs may serve as an epigenetic mechanism coordinating pathophysiological redox-mediated gene expression. In patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension, the oxidative stress-induced o8G-modified tRF-1-AspGTC is significantly up-regulated, exhibiting regulatory capacity over smooth muscle cell proliferation and anti-apoptotic activity.35 Moreover, recent research has revealed that ROS can induce o8G modifications in circPLCE1 within lung cancer cells. This o8G-modified circPLCE1 subsequently suppresses lung cancer progression by inhibiting macroautophagy via chaperone-mediated autophagy and altering cellular fate.36 This indicates that modification of o8G may play a crucial role in ROS-induced cancer cell progression, thereby providing a potential therapeutic target for subsequent cancer treatments. Collectively, RNA oxidation can critically lead to miscellaneous dysfunctions and regulation of both coding and noncoding RNAs, which are related to pathophysiological consequences under oxidative stress. Therefore, o8G is not only involved in oxidative damage but also serves as a general regulatory mechanism to mediate epitranscriptional modification. Our study demonstrates that MCPPIR-dependent o8G modification enhances POC1B mRNA expression, thereby promoting centrosome integrity and cardiomyocyte proliferation. These findings establish a direct link between o8G-mediated post-transcriptional regulation and cardiomyocyte proliferation. Importantly, we identify o8G mRNA modification as a novel epigenetic mechanism governing cardiac pathophysiology, specifically in cardiomyocyte proliferation and heart regeneration.

piRNAs technology holds immense promise for therapeutic interventions, leveraging their natural roles in gene silencing, genome defence, and developmental regulation. However, like any burgeoning gene therapy approach, piRNA-based interventions are not without potential risks, particularly concerning off-target effects and long-term consequences. While piRNAs are designed to target specific sequences, particularly those of transposons, partial sequence homology with other endogenous transcripts can lead to unintended binding by the piRNA–PIWI complex. This interaction might result in the cleavage or translational repression of these non-target transcripts, altering protein expression and cellular function. In addition, piRNAs are critical for maintaining genomic stability by suppressing the activity of transposable elements (TEs). TEs can cause mutations, gene rearrangements, and genomic instability, all of which are hallmarks of cancer. If piRNA-based interventions, particularly those aimed at silencing TEs, are not precisely controlled or if they inadvertently interfere with tumour suppressor pathways or activate oncogenes, they could paradoxically promote uncontrolled cell proliferation and increase the risk of cancer. Off-target piRNAs could inadvertently bind to and regulate transcripts involved in ion channel function, cardiac muscle contraction, or vascular development. This could disrupt normal cardiac electrophysiology, leading to arrhythmias, or affect blood flow regulation. Despite the significant risks associated with piRNA intervention, the scientific community is actively developing strategies to address these challenges. Rigorous sequence design and selection are paramount to minimize off-target binding. Developing delivery systems that allow for precise targeting to specific cell types and controlled expression levels are also vital. Extensive testing in cell lines, animal models, and carefully designed clinical trials is essential to monitor for off-target effects, genomic instability, and long-term health outcomes. Through meticulous design, comprehensive safety assessments, and continued scientific exploration, the field can strive to unlock the therapeutic potential of piRNAs while ensuring patient safety and minimizing unforeseen risks.

Polynucleotide phosphorylase (PNPase) was isolated as an interacting partner of o8G-RNA; and protected E. coli from oxidative stress.37 Human PNPase (PNPT1) can directly recognize o8G-RNA and trigger its degradation to tolerate oxidative stress.38,39 A DNA repair enzyme for base excision repair, APE1, has been shown to function in o8G degradation.40 In addition, Several RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), including Y-box binding protein 1 (YB-1) and HNRNPD can bind and destabilize o8G-containing RNAs.41,42 In our study, mass spectrometry analysis identified HNRNPH1 as a specific binding partner of MCPPIR, and subsequent validation confirmed HNRNPH1’s role in binding and destabilizing o8G-modified POC1B mRNA. However, whether HNRNPH1 directly recognizes o8G sites on POC1B mRNA, and which specific polynucleotide phosphorylases (PNPases) or RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) cooperate with HNRNPH1 to regulate o8G modification and expression of POC1B mRNA, warrants further investigation.

The centrosome is the microtubular organizing centre of higher eukaryotic cells composed of centrioles and pericentric matrix (PCM), and its structure and dysfunction are involved in a variety of tumour progression.43,44 POC1 centriolar protein B (POC1B) is involved in centriole replication as well as in the later steps of centriole length control, and plays an important role in centriole assembly, stability, and centrosome transformation.45,46,47 Co-depletion of POC1A and POC1B interferes with mitotic spindle formation and leads to loss of centriole integrity.46 Previous studies showed that cells undergo G0/G1 arrest when centrosome integrity is disrupted via knockdown of centrosome proteins.48,49 During the maturation of rodent CMs, there is an increase in irreversible centriole splitting and loss of centrosome cohesion during late foetal and early postnatal stages, and the PCM relocates from the centrioles to the nuclear membrane.32,50 Our results showed that an o8G oxidative modification-mediated increase in POC1B expression enhanced centrosome integrity, inhibited centrosome disassembly and PCM redistribution from centrosomes to perinuclear regions, and thus promoted cardiomyocyte proliferation. Collectively, our study suggests that the regulation of centrosome integrity may serve as a promising therapeutic target for treating myocardial injury.

Our findings demonstrate that MCPPIR enhances cardiomyocyte proliferation and promotes cardiac repair in adult hearts through o8G-mediated post-transcriptional regulation of POC1B mRNA (Figure 7J). Our study provides new insights into the previously unrecognized role of piRNAs on cardiomyocyte proliferation, and enhances our understanding of o8G oxidation modification in cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac regeneration, which represents potential novel therapeutic targets for myocardial injury and ischaemic heart diseases.

The adult mammalian heart’s limited regenerative capacity is a fundamental barrier to recovery after injury. This inherent constraint, coupled with cardiomyocyte loss, drives progression towards heart failure, presenting a major unmet clinical need. Our study reveals that piRNA MCPPIR promotes cardiomyocyte proliferation, reduces fibrosis, and improves cardiac function after myocardial infarction. MCPPIR promotes cardiomyocyte renewal and cardiac repair through interacting with HNRNPH1 to regulate o8G modification of POC1B mRNA. MCPPIR/POC1B pathway thus might represent a novel therapeutic target for ischaemic heart diseases.
