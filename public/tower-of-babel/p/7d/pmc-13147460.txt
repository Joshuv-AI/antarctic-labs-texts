Evidence for ecological adaptation of reductive citric acid and reverse oxidative citric acid cycles based on a survey of genomes from autotrophic Bacteria
Authors: Kathleen M Scott, Daniel P Walsh, Lydia Napier, Maryna Bohdanovska, Quinn Conde, Ana C Machado-Olavarria, Sanjay Madala, Samantha M Meade, Kim T Nguyen, Tammy Nguyen, Daryn O’Malley, Haley Palmer, Kavita J Parbhoo, Alexandra Pfent, Riley Phillips, Victoria Pontes Busato, Madina Rakhmonova, Desiree Ruiz, Erin Small, Shaniah S Spence, Anjali Thakur, Biswarup Mukhopadhyay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe reductive citric acid (rTCA) and reverse oxidative TCA (roTCA) cycles are used by autotrophic Bacteria to fix dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) at deep-sea and terrestrial hot springs, water treatment facilities, acid rock drainage, sediments and aquatic habitats including lake redoxclines, and more. rTCA and roTCA cycles operating in these contexts likely reflect selective pressures present in them. This genome survey examines the versions of the rTCA and roTCA cycles present in the completed genome sequences of several phyla of Bacteria. The versions of these cycles correlate in part with the phyla to which the organisms belong, as well as with their habitats. There are many potential mechanisms for navigating the carboxylation of C3 compounds, which may diminish its metabolic cost. There are variations in electron donors for reductive steps in these cycles that could push them in a reductive direction. The predicted O2 sensitivity of several enzymes correlates with organisms’ lifestyles. The presence of genes encoding DIC transporters and carbonic anhydrases correlates with habitat pH. Understanding how these cycles have responded to billions of years of selective pressure in a variety of habitats may provide insights into engineering autotrophic biochemistries to function well in a variety of industrial contexts.Keywords: reductive citric acid cycle, reverse oxidative citric acid cycle, autotroph, rTCA, roTCA, carbon fixation

Introduction

Autotrophic organisms introduce HCO3− and CO2 into the biological portion of the carbon cycle using a multitude of pathways [eight currently described; (Berg et al. 2010, Berg 2011, Hanson et al. 2012, Mall et al. 2018, Nunoura et al. 2018, Sánchez-Andrea et al. 2020)]. These organisms consume dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC; = CO2, HCO3−, and CO32−) in every possible habitat with a light source or redox pair that can power carbon fixation. It has been noted that different pathways are tailored to different habitats (e.g. O2-resistant pathways, like the Calvin–Benson–Bassham (CBB) cycle, are favored by oxic habitats [reviewed in (Berg 2011)]. The ways in which the environment has shaped the CBB cycle are plentiful and have been extremely well-explored. Autotrophs that use the CBB cycle, including Cyanobacteria and certain other autotrophic Bacteria, phytoplankton, and plants, thrive in a huge variety of habitats, from deep-sea hydrothermal vents (Muyzer 1995) to the ocean surface (Goericke and Welschmeyer 1993) and landmasses, where they are the predominant biomass (Bar-On et al. 2018). Accordingly, they have many adaptations to sustain carbon fixation in these habitats, including CO2 concentrating mechanisms to boost internal dissolved inorganic carbon concentrations (DIC; CO2 + HCO3− + CO3−2) to speed carbon fixation (Badger et al. 2005), as well as C4 pathways commonly found in plants that act as a delivery mechanism to facilitate carbon fixation when water is scarce (Hatch 1987).

However, other autotrophic pathways also must operate in a variety of habitats and must also accommodate the selective pressures present in these habitats. This is particularly true for the reductive citric acid (rTCA) and reverse oxidative (roTCA) cycles, given the diversity of habitats where these pathways operate (see below). These pathways consist of reductive, carboxylating versions of the citric acid cycle instead of the oxidative, decarboxylating version familiar from its presence in mitochondria and elsewhere [Fig. 1; (Evans et al. 1966, Buchanan and Arnon 1990)]. For both rTCA and roTCA cycles, cells must overcome the substantial energy barrier presented by reversing the citrate-forming enzyme, citrate synthase (Table 1). In the roTCA cycle, citrate synthase is pushed to operate in the citrate cleaving direction by the accumulation of citrate and diminishment of acetyl-CoA via its reduction and carboxylation, which in turn is driven by high concentrations of CO2 (Mall et al. 2018, Nunoura et al. 2018, Steffens et al. 2021). For the rTCA cycle, citrate synthase is replaced either by ATP citrate lyase, or the sequential activities of citryl-CoA synthetase and citryl-CoA lyase [Fig. 1 and Table 1; (Aoshima et al. 2004b,c, Ivanovsky et al. 1980)]. The operation of the rTCA and roTCA cycles also requires replacement of pyruvate and 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenases with ferredoxin-dependent enzymes that are also found in anaerobic heterotrophs [pyruvate and 2-oxoglutarate: ferredoxin oxidoreductases; Fig. 1 and Table 1 (Evans et al. 1966)].

Organisms using the rTCA and roTCA cycles respond differently to the availability of organic acids in their growth media. While organisms using the rTCA continue to operate the rTCA in the reductive direction when organic acids are available (Feng et al. 2010, Chiba et al. 2025), those using the roTCA either reverse the cycle to its oxidative direction or use the wishbone-shaped citric acid pathway, which lacks interconversion of 2-oxoglutarate and succinyl-CoA (Mall et al. 2018, Nunoura et al. 2018, Mollaei et al. 2021).

The rTCA cycle is widely distributed among autotrophic Bacteria, including members of Pseudomonadota and Nitrospinota, and is particularly well-represented in phyla Chlorobiota, Nitrospirota, Aquificota, and Campylobacterota (Supplementary Table S1). Phylum Chlorobiota consists primarily of anoxygenic photosynthetic organisms adapted for low-light habitats, including lakes, sediments, endolithic habitats, and terrestrial hot springs [(Overmann 2001), and references in Supplementary Table S2]. Phylum Nitrospirota includes chemolithoautotrophs that use Fe+2 or NO2− as electron donors in many habitats, including soil, groundwater, acid mine drainage, and water treatment facilities (Mosley et al. 2024). Thermophilic and hyperthermophilic members of phylum Aquificota power carbon fixation using H2 or reduced sulfur compounds provided from deep-sea hydrothermal vents and terrestrial hot springs (Reysenbach et al. 2001). Mesophilic and thermophilic autotrophic members of Campylobacterota use similar electron donors from comparatively cooler habitats at deep-sea hydrothermal vents, marine sediments, redoxclines and elsewhere (Campbell et al. 2006).

The roTCA cycle has been observed in five organisms so far, though it is likely present in many others. These five organisms are broadly distributed taxonomically (two members of Campylobacterota, one member of Thermodesulfobacteriota, one member of Thermosulfidibacterota, and one member of Actinomycetota), and also with respect to habitats (terrestrial hot springs, deep sea hydrothermal vents, saline lakes, and a ditch (Mall et al. 2018, Nunoura et al. 2018, Mollaei et al. 2021, Steffens et al. 2021, Gao et al. 2024). The huge span of habitats for organisms using the rTCA or roTCA cyles has a broad range of energy availabilities and variabilities, and O2 and DIC concentrations.

To provide evidence for ecological adaptation of the rTCA and roTCA cycles, genes encoding enzymes from these cycles were collected from complete genomes to observe whether there was any correlation with environmental energy supply, O2, and DIC concentration. We included organisms shown to use the roTCA cycle, though only two of them had completed genomes available at the time from IMG (Desulfurella acetivorans and Hippea maritima), because they use the same complement of enzymes as the rTCA cycle for all of the steps besides citrate cleavage, and it is possible that microorganisms using the rTCA or roTCA cycles for autotrophic growth would respond similarly to selective pressures from environmental factors.

Gene collection and verification

Genomes that were complete and publicly available January 2025 were selected from the Integrated Microbial Genomes and Microbiomes database (https://img.jgi.doe.gov/) from organisms known to grow autotrophically via the rTCA or roTCA cycles (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, and references therein). Completed genomes from autotrophic organisms were queried via BLASTp for homologs of ATP citrate lyase or citryl-CoA synthetase and citryl-CoA lyase (Fig. 1 and Table 1), enzymes considered to be indicative of the operation of the rTCA cycle (Hugler and Sievert 2011). While the Thermovibrio ammonificans carries genes encoding both the rTCA cycle and Wood-Ljungdahl pathways in its genome, proteomic and metabolomic analyses both indicate a reliance on the rTCA cycle (Giovannelli et al. 2017, Chiba et al. 2025). The organisms selected for this study are listed with their taxonomies in Supplementary Table S1.

Genes potentially encoding enzymes from the rTCA and roTCA cycles (referred to below as “rTCA and roTCA cycle genes”) were gathered by their memberships in PFAM, COG, KO, or EC families, and their predicted amino acid sequences were aligned and examined for active site and other strictly conserved residues (Table 2). Genes encoding oxoacid: ferredoxin oxidoreductases (OFOR) proved to be difficult to sort by substrate based on their sequences. Two OFOR enzymes (pyruvate: ferredoxin oxidoreductase; 2-oxoacid: ferredoxin oxidoreductase (Fig. 1 andTable 1) are necessary for the rTCA and roTCA cycles. When OFOR sequences were gathered from genomes, aligned, and subjected to phylogenetic analysis as described in (Gibson et al. 2016), some of the sequences were too divergent to cluster with the others, and some fell within classes that had members that metabolized multiple substrates. Further, the divergence among the sequences was substantial enough to compromise the alignment, diminishing the robustness of the clades generated by phylogenetic analysis to sort them by substrate. As a result, OFOR sequences were not distinguished here as pyruvate: ferredoxin- or 2-oxoglutarate: ferredoxin oxidoreductases. All sequences gathered from IMG and vetted for use in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Overall trends

The types of enzymes responsible for each step in the rTCA and roTCA cycles are distributed with some taxonomic coherence (Fig. 2). For example, the subunit compositions of OFOR enzymes, the presence of genes encoding enzymes that can interconvert C3 to C4 compounds, as well as the types of citrate-metabolizing enzymes, are all distributed among genomes in a manner largely consistent with the organisms’ taxonomies (Fig. 2).

For some steps, most organisms carry a single gene (i.e. malate dehydrogenase, fumarase, succinyl-CoA synthetase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, aconitase), while for other steps, within a single organism, an array of genes encoding a variety of enzymes are present (i.e. carbonic anhydrase, DIC transporters, oxoacid: ferredoxin oxidoreductase, C3-C4 metabolism, fumarate reductase). A few taxa are noteworthy for the abundance of rTCA or roTCA enzyme-encoding genes that they carry (Ca. Endoriftia persephone, Desulfurella acetivorans, Sulfurovum mangrovi).

In the case of S. mangrovi (Li et al. 2023), this abundance is due to the duplication of a ∼83 kb region of the genome from phage insertion into the chromosome (first copy DNA coordinates 31542–115110; duplication DNA coordinates 514953–598632, IMG gene object IDs 8081764954–5053; phage DNA coordinates 599607–634065, IMG gene object IDs 8081 765068–126). This duplicated segment of chromosome includes genes encoding malate dehydrogenase (NADH and NADPH), fumarase, succinyl-CoA synthetase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, and ATP citrate lyase. Nucleotide sequences of these gene copies are virtually identical, with only three substitutions in 25 632 nucleotides, suggesting that this phage insertion and genome region duplication are a recent event.

For the other organisms, this diversity of enzymes for a particular step may reflect an ability to grow as a facultative autotroph, both lithoautotrophically and organoheterotrophically, beyond mixotrophic, simultaneous use of both DIC and organic carbon sources. This ability to grow as facultative autotrophs, either exclusively on DIC, or predominantly organic carbon (using DIC only for anapleurotic reactions), has been demonstrated in Desulfurella acetivorans (Bonch-Osmolovskaya et al. 1990), and suggested for Ca. Endoriftia persephone [(Robidart et al. 2008); Supplementary Table S2]. It has been shown for organisms that fix DIC via the CBB cycle that facultative autotrophs are more likely to have multiple enzymes catalyzing steps of central carbon metabolism than obligate autotrophs are, perhaps to provide more versatility when using multiple carbon sources (Quasem et al. 2017), and this may also be true for facultative autotrophs using the rTCA cycle. The abundance of genes encoding steps for the rTCA cycle in Ca. Endoriftia persephone may also reflect the versatility needed to survive both the symbiotic and free-living lifestyles employed by this microorganism (Harmer et al. 2008). This versatility may be particularly helpful for organisms using the roTCA cycle, which, when growing heterotrophically, reverse the cycle to its oxidative direction or modify it to form the wishbone-shaped citric acid pathway (Nunoura et al. 2018, Mollaei et al. 2021). It may be helpful to have multiple genes encoding similar enzymes to facilitate the regulation of the pathways’ expression, and also to express alternatives that may work better in one direction than the other.

Co-location of rTCA and roTCA genes in genomes

As is typical for other organisms, genes encoding the subunits of multisubunit enzymes are almost always co-located on the chromosome (Supplementary Table S4). Within phyla, genes encoding different enzymes of the rTCA cycle often share patterns of co-location on the chromosome (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S4).

For members of phylum Chlorobiota, few genes encoding steps of the rTCA are co-located in their genomes. Four of the five examples of co-location include genes encoding fumarase, and three of the five include genes encoding sodium pump oxaloacetate decarboxylase. This decarboxylase is widespread in Bacteria, and though it has been studied in the decarboxylating direction, it is reversible. Decarboxylation is coupled to Na+ expulsion from the cytoplasm (Dimroth and Hilpert 1984, Dimroth et al. 2001). Since it is reversible, it could theoretically act as a carboxylase in the rTCA cycle, coupled to Na+ entry. Its presence in the genomes of many obligately autotrophic members of Campylobacterota (Fig. 2), where it seems likely operate in a carboxylating direction, argues for this possibility (Scott et al. 2008).

For members of phyla Nitrospirota, Aquificota, and Campylobacterota, genes that are present in single copies are often co-located (malate dehydrogenase, fumarase, succinyl-CoA synthetase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, aconitase; Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S4). These co-located genes often include genes encoding pyruvate or 2-oxoglutarate: ferredoxin oxidoreductase. In phyla Nitrospirota and Aquificota, the A subunit of fumarate reductase is also found among these co-located genes, and members of Nitrospirota also include genes encoding citrate cleaving enzymes in these clusters. For all three phyla, it is also common for genes encoding DIC transporters and carbonic anhydrase enzymes to be co-located with genes encoding carboxylases. This is particularly common for the oxoacid: ferredoxin oxidoreductases, as well as isocitrate dehydrogenase. Carboxylase coexpression with DIC transporters or carbonic anhydrase enzymes could facilitate carboxylase activity (see “DIC toolkit”, below).

Energy investment to push the rTCA and roTCA cycles in a reducing direction

One of the ecological advantages touted for the rTCA and roTCA cycles is that they are less energetically expensive than other autotrophic pathways for DIC fixation, requiring less ATP hydrolysis for the formation of central carbon intermediates from DIC than other pathways do (Berg 2011, Mangiapia and Scott 2016). Most steps of these cycles have reaction Gibbs energies that are near zero (Table 1), and pushing the cycles in a reducing, carboxylating direction is favored by the presence of a few steps that are pushed forward by nucleotide triphosphate hydrolysis, or the oxidation of cellular electron carriers with more negative redox potentials.

Nucleotide triphosphate hydrolysis

There are many mechanisms for the synthesis of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) and oxaloacetate from pyruvate, most of which require ATP hydrolysis [Fig. 1 and Table 1; (Sauer and Eikmanns 2005)]. Two of the enzymes, PEP carboxylase and pyruvate carboxylase, are irreversible (Table 1; irreversibility of pyruvate carboxylase is due to high cytoplasmic ATP: ADP ratios (Sauer and Eikmanns 2005). Genome data suggest that some organisms may have options to use reversible enzymes instead (Fig. 2).

Synthesizing oxaloacetate from pyruvate via pyruvate phosphate dikinase or pyruvate water dikinase, and then carboxylating PEP via PEP carboxylase is expensive, since use of either dikinase results in the hydrolysis of two phosphoanhydride bonds (Fig. 1 and Table 1). If pyruvate phosphate dikinase is used, the loss of one phosphoanhydride bond can be recovered from the pyrophosphate produced, e.g. via membrane-integral pyrophosphatase-mediated proton pumping (Luoto et al. 2013). The expense of this pathway for oxaloacetate synthesis may explain why genes encoding PEP carboxylase are not widely distributed among the organisms studied here, found only in Chlorobi (Fig. 2). Given that Chlorobi tend to be found in low-light habitats and have adaptations to low-energy conditions (Ormerod et al. 1992), the presence of genes encoding PEP carboxylase, and measurable activity of this enzyme in cell extracts (Ivanovsky et al. 2022) is surprising, but these organisms have alternative pathways for oxaloacetate synthesis (see below). Chlorobi have been shown to assimilate acetate via acetyl-CoA synthetase and PEP carboxylase (Tang and Blankenship 2010); the presence of this enzyme could facilitate mixotrophic growth on acetate (simultaneous assimilation of DIC and acetate) by these organisms and others whose genomes encode it.

Less expensive options do not appear to be available to members of Leptospirillum, whose genome data suggest a reliance on pyruvate water dikinase and PEP carboxylase; this expensive option does not appear to be the best fit for organisms relying on Fe2+ oxidation for growth (see below). PEP can be carboxylated in ways that conserve some of the energy of phosphoryl removal from this molecule. PEP carboxykinase (ATP, GTP, PPi) can couple carboxylation to the formation of a phosphoanhydride bond. This energy recapture may explain the co-occurrence of PEP carboxykinase and pyruvate water or phosphate dikinase in Chlorobiota and Campylobacterota (Fig. 2). Magnetococcus marinus distinguishes itself from the others by relying entirely on this reversible C3→C4 pathway (Fig. 2).

Additionally, pyruvate itself can be carboxylated to form oxaloacetate or malate directly. Pyruvate carboxylase couples synthesis of oxaloacetate to ATP hydrolysis (Fig. 1), and is common in Nitrospirota and Aquificota (Fig. 2). Carboxylation of pyruvate can also be catalyzed by sodium pump oxaloacetate decarboxylase, which can power the synthesis of oxaloacetate with cellular membrane potential by coupling pyruvate carboxylation to Na+ import (Dimroth and Hilpert 1984). These pumps are particularly well-represented in organisms isolated from marine habitats [Chlorobiota, Campylobacterota; Fig. 2; (Scott et al. 2008)], in which Na+ import could be facilitated by the presence of high environmental concentrations of this cation (Table 1). By coupling pyruvate carboxylation to the import of Na+ ions to the cytoplasm, sodium pump oxaloacetate expends some of the membrane potential that powers ATP synthase, which makes the effective cost of pyruvate carboxylation by this mechanism similar in magnitude to pyruvate carboxylase (1 ATP). Malic enzyme, which synthesizes malate by reductively carboxylating pyruvate without ATP hydrolysis or expending membrane potential, is a less expensive option, which may explain why it is well-represented in phyla Nitrospirota and Campylobacterota.

Organisms that synthesize oxaloacetate or malate directly from pyruvate still need to produce PEP as a precursor for amino acid, nucleic acid, and peptidoglycan synthesis, which may explain why organisms with genes encoding pyruvate carboxylase or sodium pump oxaloacetate decarboxylase, but lacking PEP carboxylase or PEP carboxykinase, also encode pyruvate water or phosphate dikinase (Nitrospirota, Aquificota, Campylobacterota), and those organisms lacking pyruvate water or phosphate dikinase genes also encode PEP carboxykinase (members of families Nitratiruptoraceae and Hydrogenimonadaceae; Fig. 2). Co-location of genes encoding sodium pump oxaloacetate decarboxylase and PEP carboxykinase in many of these organisms suggests their concerted activities in vivo (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S4).

In order to accomplish both objectives of the C3→C4 node of the rTCA and roTCA cycles, which are to synthesize PEP for biosynthesis, and to synthesize oxaloacetate or malate to continue the cycle, the equivalent of ATP hydrolysis is required. The abundance of choices for navigating this step are quite puzzling (Fig. 1). Organisms with the most ‘choices’ skew towards those that can use organic compounds as carbon and/or energy sources (e.g. Chlorobi, Magnetococcus marinus, as well as roTCA organisms Desulfurella acetivorans and Hippea maritima; Supplementary Table S2), which may provide these organisms with a variety of options to direct carbon metabolism when using it for a variety of purposes. However, many obligately lithoautotrophic rTCA autotrophs (e.g, members of Campylobacterales) have multiple options as well, suggesting that other factors are at work. Determining what these other factors might be, could also clarify the variability of these enzymes between organisms as well.

Another step that requires nucleotide triphosphate hydrolysis is the activation of succinate by succinyl-CoA synthetase (Fig. 1). Desulfurella acetivorans, which uses the roTCA, can synthesize succinyl-CoA with acetyl-CoA: succinate CoA transferase or propionyl-CoA: succinate CoA transferase (Pettinato et al. 2022), which removes the requirement for nucleotide triphosphate hydrolysis to synthesize succinyl-CoA, and could potentially diminish the expense of this step. However, it is difficult to reconcile use of CoA transferase enzymes in the rTCA cycle, since they would consume the acetyl-CoA necessary to start the cycle.

Some thermophilic and hyperthermophilic members of Aquificota introduce a requirement for ATP hydrolysis in the conversion of succinyl-CoA to isocitrate. For these organisms, oxoglutarate carboxylase and isocitrate dehydrogenase work together to form isocitrate from 2-oxoglutarate (Aoshima and Igarashi 2006, Aoshima and Igarashi 2008). This added expense is suggested to prevent accumulation of succinyl-CoA, which is heat-labile (Berg 2011), though it is important to note that the oxalosuccinate formed from 2-oxoglutarate carboxylation is also heat-labile (Aoshima and Igarashi 2006). Nonetheless, the restricted distribution of oxoglutarate decarboxylase to thermophiles and hyperthermophiles does suggest a link to temperature.

Citrate cleavage, the final step of the rTCA and roTCA cycles (Fig. 1 and Table 1), also consumes ATP in autotrophs using the rTCA cycle. The two ATP-dependent citrate-cleaving enzyme systems are ATP-citrate lyase (Kanao et al. 2001), and a system consisting of the sequential activities of citryl-CoA synthetase (which requires ATP) and citryl-CoA lyase [CCS/CCL; (Aoshima 2007, Hügler et al. 2007)]. A previous study had shown that ATP citrate lyase is broadly distributed among rTCA autotrophs, while the CCS/CCL system is found in members of Aquificacea (Hügler et al. 2007), and the results from this genome survey are consistent with these prior observations (Fig. 2). This survey also identifies CCS/CCL systems in Leptospirillum spp. (Fig. 2).

Organisms using the roTCA cycle circumvent the ATP requirement for acetyl-CoA formation from citrate cleavage by using citrate synthase to catalyze this reaction (Mall et al. 2018, Nunoura et al. 2018, Steffens et al. 2021). However, since thermodynamics favor the formation of citrate by citrate synthase (Table 1), the enzyme must be forced in a citrate cleaving direction with high concentrations of citrate and low concentrations of acetyl-CoA. Even under these circumstances, citrate formation is slow, and organisms compensate by expressing extremely high levels of this enzyme, which diminishes the energetic advantage of using this enzyme (Mall et al. 2018, Nunoura et al. 2018, Steffens et al. 2021).

Interestingly, citrate synthase genes are also common in members of Chlorobiota and Campylobacterota, and are also present in Aquificota (Fig. 2). Citrate synthase activity is sometimes measurable in cell extracts from members of Chlrobiota [yes: Chlorobaculum limnaeum (Ivanovsky et al. 2022); no: Chlorobium limicola (Tang and Blankenship 2010)], and has been proposed to facilitate the assimilation and metabolism of acetate (Tang and Blankenship 2010), or replenish 2-oxoglutarate used for amino acid synthesis (Ivanovsky et al. 2022). It is possible that in addition to these roles, under the correct conditions, citrate synthase could catalyze the cleavage of citrate in these organisms.

Electron carriers with more negative redox potentials

Two reductive carboxylation steps in the rTCA and roTCA cycles use ferredoxin as an electron donor and are catalyzed by oxoacid: ferredoxin oxidoreductases (OFOR). The carboxylation of acetyl-CoA to form pyruvate, and succinyl-CoA to form 2-oxoglutarate, is catalyzed by pyruvate: ferredoxin oxidoreductase (PFOR) and 2-oxoglutarate: ferredoxin oxidoreductase (OGOR), respectively [Fig. 1 and Table 1; reviewed in (Berg 2011, Hanson et al. 2012)]. These enzymes are reversible, and whether they catalyze carboxylations or decarboxylations can be influenced by the reductive potential of the ferredoxin acting as an electron donor or acceptor. For example, when using a ‘typical’ ferredoxin with a redox potential of ∼−400 mV (Li and Elliott 2016), carboxylation of acetyl-CoA is endergonic [23.2 kJ/mol, calculated via eQuilibrator; (Flamholz et al. 2011)], and the decarboxylation of pyruvate is favored. Indeed, PFOR acts as a decarboxylase to facilitate growth by many anaerobic heterotrophs (Bock et al. 1996, Hughes et al. 1998, Baughn et al. 2009). The ferredoxins that deliver electrons to OFOR enzymes in organisms that use the rTCA cycle have been found to have especially negative redox potentials. Ferredoxins that deliver electrons to OGOR from Hydrogenobacter thermophilus have a redox potential of −480 mV (Li and Elliott 2016), those delivering electrons to PFOR from Chlorobium tepidum have a redox potential of −514 and −584 mV, and those delivering electrons to both PFOR and OGOR in Aquifex aeolicus have a redox potential of −440 and −460 mV (Prioretti et al. 2023). As a result, depending on the ferredoxin used, the carboxylation of acetyl-CoA by PFOR has free energies ranging from 15.5 to −12.3 kJ/mol (Table 1), more favorable to carboxylation than when using ‘typical’ ferredoxins.

Several enzymes in the rTCA and roTCA cycles rely on nicotinamide dinucleotide coenzymes (NADH, NADPH) to provide electrons (malic enzyme, malate dehydrogenase, isocitrate dehydrogenase; Fig. 1 andTable 1). A reducing direction for these enzymes can be favored by using electron donors with more negative redox potentials. In the cytoplasm, under physiological conditions, NADP+/NADPH has a more negative redox potential (−380 mV) than NAD+/NADH (−280 mV), due to NADP+/NADPH ratios being smaller, and NAD+/NADH being larger (Buckel and Thauer 2013). There are NADH and NADPH-utilizing variants of malic enzyme, malate dehydrogenase, and isocitrate dehydrogenase. Relying on enzymes that preferentially use NADPH could help push these reactions in a reductive direction. Indeed, most of the isocitrate dehydrogenase genes found in these organisms encode enzymes predicted to be specific to NADPH, based on the presence of conserved residues from NADPH-specific enzymes (Table 2 and Fig. 2). This predicted use of NADPH by isocitrate dehydrogenase is consistent with enzyme assays from Chlorobium limicola, Sulfurimonas denitrificans, and Thermovibrio ammonificans (Kanao et al. 2002, Hügler et al. 2005, Hügler et al. 2007). The few IDH genes that encode enzymes that are predicted to use NADH [also based on conserved residues; Table 2; confirmed by enzyme assays in Aquifex aeolicus and Thermocrinis ruber; (Hügler et al. 2007)] are found in organisms that also encode 2-oxoglutarate carboxylase to help push the reaction in the reducing, carboxylating direction (see above; Fig. 2).

The situation is mixed for the other two steps of the rTCA and roTCA cycles that can use nicotinamide coenzymes. Malic enzyme from Campylobacterota is predicted to use NADPH, while predicted to use NADH by members of Nitrospirota (Table 2 and Fig. 2). Almost all of the malate dehydrogenase genes are predicted to encode NADH-specific enzymes (Table 2 and Fig. 2). This result is consistent with enzyme assays on cell extracts from members of Chlorobiota (Evans et al. 1966, Naterstad et al. 1996), and Aquificota (Hügler et al. 2007), though some activity with NADPH was apparent. Cell extracts from members of Campylobacterota (Sulfurimonas denitrificans and Arcobacter sulfidicus) could use either cofactor with similar activity (Hügler et al. 2005).

The reduction of fumarate to succinate can use a variety of coenzymes to donate electrons (Fig. 1 and Table 1). Fumarate reduction by quinols is catalyzed by multiple-subunit (ABC or ABCD) complexes in which the C and D subunits span the membrane and interact with the quinol pool (Jardim-Messeder et al. 2017). Genes encoding these complexes [fumarate reductase/succinate dehydrogenase (quinol)], which are generally co-located on the chromosome, are very common among rTCA and roTCA autotrophs (Fig. 2). The reactions catalyzed by these complexes are reversible; they can catalyze both the reduction of fumarate by quinols, or the oxidation of succinate by quinones [reviewed in (Jardim-Messeder et al. 2017)]. Whether a complex operates primarily in an oxidative or reductive direction cannot be distinguished by amino acid sequence (Cecchini et al. 2002, Cecchini 2003). As for the enzymes described above, the direction the complex takes can be skewed by the electron carriers used by the complex, in this case quinols/quinones synthesized by its host organism. The presence of ubiquinone, which has a redox potential (Q/Q·−) of ∼ −160 mV, skews complexes toward succinate oxidation, while the presence of menaquinol, which has a redox potential of −260 mV, skews complexes toward fumarate reduction [Table 1; (Hägerhäll 1997, Kishi et al. 2017)[. Menaquinol/menaquinone is the predominant quinol used by the organisms studied here, based on the presence of genes necessary to synthesize this coenzyme in their genomes (Fig. 4), as well as the detection of menaquinone in their membranes [Chlorobiota, (Frigaard et al. 1997); Nitrospinota, Aquificota, Campylobacterota (Elling et al. 2025) and some members of Pseudomonadota (Sharma et al. 2012)].

A different mechanism for fumarate reduction must take place in members of Nitrospirota and Aquificota (Fig. 4). Within Nitrospirota, two organisms have genes encoding both menaquinone biosynthesis as well as fumarate reductase/succinate dehydrogenase (quinone) (Nitrospira defluvii and moscovensis); other members lack the genes necessary to synthesize menaquinone (Leptospirillum ferrooxidans and L. ferriphilum; Nitrospira tepida, KM1, and N. inopinata). In addition, members of Leptospirillum lack genes encoding B, C, and D subunits (Fig. 4). Some members of Nitrospirota synthesize a novel quinone, methyl-plastoquinone, but its redox potential is anticipated to have a value even more positive than ubiquinone (Elling et al. 2025), which makes it an unsuitable for fumarate reduction.

Soluble (cytoplasmic) fumarate reductase (NADH) can catalyze this step of the rTCA cycle. These enzymes are widespread [e.g. Klebsiella,(Bertsova et al. 2020); Saccharomyces, (Enomoto et al. 1996, Muratsubaki and Enomoto 1998)]. This enzyme has been well-characterized in Hydrogenobacter thermophilus, in which it has five subunits (ABCDE), with the A subunit homologous to the A subunit from fumarate reductase/succinate dehydrogenase (quinol). Fumarate reductase (NADH) is irreversible [Table 1; (Miura et al. 2008)]. Some members of Aquificaceae have genes encoding all of the subunits of this enzyme (Fig. 4). Other members of Aquificota have A and B subunits that are good matches to those present in H. thermophilus, but lack close homologs to the C, D, and E subunits. This is particularly problematic for members of genus Sulfurihydrogenibium, as they also lack genes encoding fumarate dehydrogenase/succinate dehydrogenase (quinol) (Fig. 4).

‘Incomplete’ fumarate reductases (AB or A subunits) are often encoded by genes co-located with those encoding other steps of the rTCA cycle (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S4), which implicates their involvement in this cycle. For those organisms that encode a complete (ABC or ABCD) fumarate reductase/succinate dehydrogenase (quinol) elsewhere (e.g. members of Campylobacterota), perhaps these extra A and B subunits can associate with the C and D subunits encoded co-located with the other copies of A and B. For members of Leptospirillum and Sulfurihydrogenibium, which lack another locus encoding a complete fumarate reductase/succinate dehydrogenase (quinol), as well as the several members of Nitrospira that do not appear to be able to synthesize a quinol with sufficiently negative redox potential to reduce fumarate, it seems likely that the A or AB subunit versions of these enzymes encoded by their genomes catalyze fumarate reduction. Alternatives to ABCD or ABCDE fumarate reductase exist in other organisms. The genes encoding the AB-subunit version in the genome of Ca. Endoriftia persephone are co-located with those encoding 2-oxoglutarate: ferredoxin oxidoreductase as well as an electron bifurcating NADH dehydrogenase/heterodisulfide reductase complex. It has been suggested that these proteins aggregate to function as an electron-bifurcating complex, which could use the electrons from NADH to simultaneously reduce fumarate and ferredoxin, which could in turn reduce 2-oxoglutarate (Rubin-Blum et al. 2019). The observation that these genes are co-expressed makes this possibility seem more likely (Mitchell et al. 2024). However, the A and AB subunits from the other organisms studied here are not co-located with homologs of this potential complex. A periplasmic single subunit fumarate reductase has been characterized from Shewanella frigidimarina that accepts electrons from cytochrome c (Taylor et al. 1999), but this enzyme amino acid sequence is only 30% identical to the A subunits described here. This absence of homologs beyond A or AB subunits for ABCD or ABCDE-type fumarate reductases, and distinctness from electron-bifurcating or cytochrome c-dependent forms of fumarate reducatse, as well as their co-location with genes encoding other steps of the rTCA cycle, strongly suggest the presence of novel forms of fumarate reductase in phylum Nitrospirota, genus Sulfurihydrogenibium, and likely other organisms included in this study.

Correlations of rTCA and roTCA genes with O2 requirement or tolerance for growth

The majority of organisms that grow autotrophically by using the rTCA or roTCA cycles are anaerobes, facultative anaerobes, or microaerophiles, while a few can sustain growth under ambient air (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S2). Using O2 as an electron acceptor can provide a selective advantage, since its positive redox potential [+815 mV at pH 7 (Wood 1988)] provides the largest energy yield per mol of electron donor used; accordingly, many rTCA chemolithoautotrophs are able to use it as an electron acceptor (Supplementary Table S2). Even if it is not used as an electron acceptor, tolerating the presence of O2 is a selective advantage on a planet with an oxidizing atmosphere. The mixed abilities of rTCA autotrophs to tolerate O2 likely stems in part from the mixed sensitivities of the enzymes of the rTCA cycle to this gas.

Many of the OFOR enzymes that catalyze the carboxylations of acetyl-CoA or succinyl-CoA in the rTCA and roTCA cycles (Fig. 1; introduced above) are quite sensitive to O2 damage to their iron-sulfur clusters (Meinecke et al. 1989, Blamey and Adams 1994, Adams and Kletzin 1996), while others are not [e.g. (Pieulle et al. 1995, Wang et al. 2022)]. The O2 sensitivity of many of these oxidoreductases has been invoked as a reason for the requirement for anaerobic or microaerobic conditions for growing most rTCA or roTCA autotrophs (Hugler and Sievert 2011). However, differential expression of OFOR enzymes with different O2 sensitivities can facilitate growth in the presence of O2. Hydrogenobacter thermophilus has genes encoding three of these enzymes, two of which encode OGOR. These two enzymes have different sensitivities to O2, and are differentially expressed in response to the concentration of O2 present during growth (Yamamoto et al. 2006).

The possibility of differential expression of OFOR enzymes in other species is suggested by the distribution of these enzymes in the organisms studied here. As expected, all of the rTCA and roTCA organisms have at least two OFORs encoded in their genomes (Figs. 2 and 4). Presumably, for those with two, one encodes a PFOR, while the other encodes an OGOR. Many have three or more, particularly in families Aquificaceae (phylum Aquificota) and Sulfurovaceae (phylum Campylobacterota). Ca. Endoriftia persephone (phylum Pseudomonadota) is particularly noteworthy, with seven distinct OFOR enzymes encoded in its genome. Given that OFOR enzymes catalyze a variety of reactions beyond acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA carboxylation [e.g. amino acid catabolism; reviewed in (Fukuda and Wakagi 2002, Gibson et al. 2016)[, it is likely that some of these extra OFOR enzymes are involved in other pathways besides the rTCA or roTCA cycles. However, some of them could encode additional copies of PFOR or OGOR to provide their host with the ability to grow under different oxygen tensions. Supporting this notion, many of the organisms with more than two copies of OFOR are microaerophiles, or facultative anaerobes like Hydrogenobacter thermophilus (Fig. 4). Within Chlorobiota, aerotolerant Prosthecochloris spp. have three copies, which could suggest the presence of a more oxygen-robust OFOR among the three copies. However, other strictly anaerobic members of Chlorobiota (e.g. Chlorobium limicola) also have three copies, which suggests that differences among them within Chlorobiota as well as the other phyla extend beyond oxygen sensitivity (e.g. different substrates, as described above, or affinities for substrates, see below). Within phylum Nitrospirota, whose members are mostly microaerophiles or aerobes, most members only have two copies. For organisms that always grow in the presence of O2, it seems likely that both copies are more robust to O2 damage.

Fumarase enzymes, which convert malate to fumarate (Fig. 1), fall into two classes. Class I has an iron-sulfur cluster that is susceptible to oxidative damage, while class II does not; accordingly, E. coli preferentially expresses class II fumarase when O2 concentrations are elevated (Tseng et al. 2001). Distribution of the two classes of fumarase mostly correlates with the organisms’ lifestyles (anaerobe, aerobe, etc.; Fig. 4). Class I fumarase predominates among strictly anaerobic members of Chlorobiota, while class II is present in aerotolerant members of this phylum. The members of Nitrospirota, which are predominantly aerobes, all have class II. Aerobes within phylum Aquificota (Hydrogenobaculum spp.) have class II, while the other members (microaerophiles, facultative and obligate anerobes) have class I. Within Campylobacterota, members of Sulfurovaceae have both class I and II, which suits their facultatively anaerobic lifestyles, but members of Nitratiruptoraceae and Sulfurimonadaceae, which are also largely facultative anaerobes (Supplementary Table S2), mostly have class I or II, respectively. In this phylum, fumarase class correlates with growth temperature, with organisms with higher growth temperatures predominantly using class I, and organisms with cooler temperatures using class II. This also extends to phyla Aquificota (warmer, predominantly class I) and Nitrospirota (cooler, all class II). Given that O2 is less soluble in warm than in cool water, growth in warmer environments may provide less selective pressure for O2-robust fumarase.

Aconitase enzymes, which convert isocitrate to citrate (Fig. 1), also contain iron-sulfur clusters that are sensitive to oxidative damage. Similar to fumarase, two forms of the enzyme exist, and have differential sensitivity to oxidative damage. Aconitase A (AcnA) is more robust, while aconitase B (AcnB) is more sensitive to oxidative damage (Cunningham et al. 1997, Varghese et al. 2003). Accordingly, in E. coli, AcnA is expressed when cells are exposed to oxidative stress, and AcnB is expressed otherwise (Cunningham et al. 1997, Varghese et al. 2003). Interestingly, while AcnB is the more sensitive enzyme, its iron-sulfur cluster is more readily repaired than AcnA once damaged, which could make AcnB helpful to organisms subject to transient oxidative damage (Walden 2002).

The distribution of aconitase enzymes does reflect lifestyle, but to a more muted extent than fumarase. Most aerobes do encode the more robust AcnA (e.g. phylum Nitrospira, and genus Hydrogenobaculum; Fig. 4; but also note AcnB in aerobes Sulfurimonas marina and S. aquatica). Anaerobes and facultative anaerobes predominantly encode AcnB (but note Chloroherpeton thalassium, Desulfurella acetivrans, Hippea maritima). The presence of AcnB in microaerophiles and facultative anaerobes may reflect the advantage of its repairability.

Across the four potentially O2-sensitive enzymes from the rTCA and roTCA cycles, there are interesting patterns within each phylum (Fig. 4). Members of Chlorobiota, particularly Pelodictyon, Chlorobaculum, and some members of Chlorobium, have O2-sensitive fumarase (class I) and aconitase (AcnB), and typically only two OFOR enzymes encoded in their genomes (presumably O2-sensitive, in keeping with the other enzymes), reflecting their anaerobic lifestyles. At the other extreme, members of Nitrospirota have genes encoding O2-robust fumarase (class II) and aconitase (AcnA), and typically only two OFOR enzymes encoded in their genomes (presumably O2-robust, in keeping with the other enzymes). Between the two are the facultative anaerobes, and microaerophiles from Aquificota and Campylobacterota, in which the distribution of O2-sensitive and robust enzymes is mixed, as one might expect, and numbers of OFOR enzymes encoded in the genomes tend to be higher (up to 5), which suggests that, as in Hydrogenobacter thermophilus, they may have different sensitivities to O2 and can be differentially regulated in response to environmental conditions. In Chlorobiota, members of genus Prosthecochloris that are confirmed or likely aerotolerant anaerobes (Beatty et al. 2005, Nie et al. 2023), carry genes encoding O2-robust enzymes (class II fumarase, AcnB), consistent with their tolerance of O2.

Correlations of rTCA and roTCA genes with the environmental availability of CO2 and HCO3−

In most cases, the version of the rTCA or roTCA cycle encoded in the genome of a particular organism includes carboxylases that use CO2 and HCO3− as substrates (Figs. 1 and 5 and Table 1 ). OFOR enzymes use CO2, as do PEP carboxykinase, malic enzyme, and isocitrate dehydrogenase, while pyruvate carboxylase, PEP carboxylase, and sodium pump oxaloacetate decarboxylase use HCO3− [reviewed in (Scott et al. 2024)]. The environmental availability of these compounds varies both as a function of overall DIC concentration, as well as pH. At acidic pH, CO2 is dominant, at high pH, CO32− predominates, and at circumneutral pH, HCO3− is most abundant (Millero and Sohn 1992). Cells can facilitate carbon fixation with a DIC toolkit, consisting of DIC transporters and carbonic anhydrase, to enhance the delivery of CO2 and HCO3− to, and interconversion within the cytoplasm, or by using carboxylases that have substrate specificities that match the form of DIC most readily acquired from the environment.

DIC toolkit (carbonic anhydrase and DIC transporters)

Carbonic anhydrase (CA) and DIC transporters have been demonstrated to facilitate DIC uptake and fixation by organisms using the CBB cycle (Price et al. 2009, Long et al. 2016), and are likely to assist autotrophic growth by autotrophs using other pathways as well (Scott et al. 2024). CA speeds the interconversion of CO2 and HCO3− (Matsuda et al. 2022). At the cell surface, speeding this interconversion can facilitate the diffusion of CO2 across cell membranes (Gutknecht et al. 1977). Within the cell, this interconversion can maintain pools of CO2 and HCO3−, which facilitates carbon fixation by carboxylases using either CO2 or HCO3− as a substrate. CA enzymes are divided into six evolutionarily independent classes: α (including η), β (including carboxysomal carbonic anhydrase CsoSCA and ζ), γ, δ, ι, and θ [reviewed in (Matsuda et al. 2022, Scott et al. 2024)]. Active transport of DIC across cell membranes by DIC transporters facilitates DIC fixation by maintaining intracellular HCO3− concentrations. Currently known DIC transporters fall within five families: SulP (class C and D), Sbt, Chr, ABC, and DAC; reviewed in (Scott et al. 2024). Most of these transport HCO3−, except DAC, which uses CO2 as a substrate and deposits it in the cytoplasm as HCO3− (Mangiapia et al. 2017, Desmarais et al. 2019, Schmid et al. 2021).

Genes encoding either DIC transporters or CA are present in almost every organism examined here (Fig. 5). This ubiquity among autotrophic organisms is consistent with what has been previously noted for organisms using the rTCA and other autotrophic pathways for DIC fixation (Scott et al. 2024), and is probably an underestimate, since new DIC transporters and forms of CA likely remain to be discovered (Scott et al. 2024). Members of all six classes of CA are present among rTCA autotrophs, as are SulP-C, SulP-D, Sbt, and DAC. Since many members of the Chr family transport molecules other than HCO3−, and their substrate specificities cannot yet be determined from their sequences, these transporters are not included here (Nies et al. 1998). Some of the DAC genes found in this genome survey are unusual. It is typical for DAC transporters to include a cytoplasmic domain or subunit homologous to βCA (Desmarais et al. 2019), and a domain or subunit with transmembrane helices (Mangiapia et al. 2017, Desmarais et al. 2019, Schmid et al. 2021). However, in Nitrospira muscoviensis and N. tepida, one of the two genes encoding the cytoplasmic subunits of DAC transporters lacks a domain or companion gene with predicted transmembrane helices (IMG gene object ID numbers 2641367602 and 8084486544, respectively). The amino acid sequences of the proteins predicted from these genes include the residues from other DAC cytoplasmic subunits that bind Zn2+, which suggests catalytic activity as CA (Desmarais et al. 2019). However, other DAC transporter cytoplasmic subunits have not been found to have CA activity when expressed in the absence of their transmembrane subunits. Given that the proteins encoded by these genes from Nitrospira spp. are likely to function in the absence of transmembrane subunits, it would be extremely interesting to screen them for CA activity.

The abundance of DIC toolkit genes varies dramatically by phylum. They are particularly scarce among members of Chlorobi, which entirely lack genes encoding known DIC transporters. Most members of this phylum have a single β or ιCA gene. In contrast, members of phylum Nitrospirota are distinguished by the abundance of DIC toolkit genes carried in their genomes (Fig. 5). Given that these organisms typically grow slowly and many were isolated from habitats anticipated to have elevated DIC concentrations (wastewater, sewage; Supplementary Table S2), this abundance is puzzling. DIC toolkit genes are common in other phyla as well. In Aquificota, organisms typically have either multiple CA (family Hydrogenothermaceae) or DIC transporters (family Aquificaceae). A bimodal pattern is also apparent in Campylobacterota, in which members of order Nautiliales primarily have CA genes, while members of order Campylobacterales most commonly have genes encoding both CA and DIC transporters.

The paucity of DIC toolkit genes in Chlorobi may reflect the relative temporal stability of chemistry in most of the habitats from which these organisms have been isolated (e.g. lakes, sediments; but note also three terrestrial hot springs isolates; Supplementary Table S2). In contrast, many members of Aquificota and Campylobacterota were isolated from terrestrial hot springs or deep-sea hydrothermal vents (Supplementary Table S2), which experience broad sweeps in habitat chemistry over timescales from seconds to hours (Johnson et al. 1988). At deep-sea hydrothermal vents, these sweeps in chemistry include large pH (5.6–7.8) and DIC (2.3–4.7 mM) oscillations (Childress et al. 1993, Goffredi et al. 1997). Variable availability in environmental CO2 and HCO3− may make DIC toolkit components a selective advantage in these habitats.

Among the Aquificota and Campylobacterota, the bimodal distribution of DIC transporters reflects the pH of the habitats from which they were isolated. In Aquificota, isolated primarily from terrestrial hot springs (Supplementary Table S2), DIC transporters are more common among organisms isolated from alkaline hot springs (genera Hydrogenobacter and Thermocrinis, pH optima to 7.8), and less so for the other members, which were primarily isolated from more acidic hydrothermal vents (references in Supplementary Table S2). At the vents, hot, acidic hydrothermal fluid (pH 4–4.7) is diluted with deep sea water (pH 7.8) in a mixing zone [pH 5.6–6.2; (Childress et al. 1993, Goffredi et al. 1997)] populated by many of the members of Campylobacterota discussed here (Supplementary Table S2). Areas in the mixing zone with more input from hydrothermal fluid are warmer and more acidic, while areas with more input from deep sea water are cooler and more alkaline. Among Campylobacterota, organisms with higher optimal growth temperatures [members of orders Desulfurellales (pH optima to 6.9) and Nautiliales (pH optima to 6.6)], DIC transporter genes are scarce, which may reflect the lower pH values present in warmer regions of the mixing zone. In contrast, the members of Sulfurimonadaceae that were isolated from deep-sea hydrothermal vents (pH optima to 7.0) have lower optimal temperatures for growth, suggesting they occupy a niche in mixing zones that is less dominated by hydrothermal fluid, and therefore less acidic (Childress et al. 1993, Goffredi et al. 1997). Many of these organisms, as well as the members of Sulfurimonadaceae that were isolated from more alkaline habitats (e.g. marine sediments, redoxclines; pH optima to 7.3) carry genes for DIC transporters. For members of both Aquificota and Campylobacterota, the presence of DIC transporter genes correlates with habitat pH, with organisms from low pH habitats being less likely to have these genes, presumably because the CO2 abundant at low pH in geothermally-influenced habitats does not require the presence of a membrane-spanning DIC transporter to diffuse into the cytoplasm from the environment.

Members of phylum Nitrospirota all carry an impressive arsenal of DIC transporters and CA enzymes (Fig. 5). The reason for this abundance is mysterious at this point; these organisms do not have rapid generation times (references in Supplementary Table S2) that would require rapid delivery of DIC to the cytoplasm. Furthermore, many of them use electron donors with rather positive redox potentials, which would make the added energetic expense of active DIC transport problematic [NO3−/NO2−, +420 mV; Fe3+/Fe2+, +697 to +770 mV; (Berks et al. 1995, Johnson et al. 2012)]. Some Nitrospira can use other electron donors, and genome sequences suggest this may also be the case for Leptospirillum spp.; perhaps they express DIC transporters when they use electron donors with more negative redox potentials (Fujimura et al. 2012, Daims et al. 2016). Despite this conundrum, all members of this phylum carry genes encoding at least one copy of the DAC transporter, which transports CO2 (Schmid et al. 2021). Those members of this phylum with optimal growth pH values above 7.3 also encode SulP and Sbt-type HCO3− transporters, reflecting the abundance of this form of DIC at higher pH values, as seen in (Schmid et al. 2021, Scott et al. 2024).

Three organisms studied here do not appear to encode any of the currently known DIC transporters or CA enzymes (Desulfurobacterium thermolithotrophum, Desulfurella acetivorans, and Hippea maritima). Hippea maritima and Desulfurella acetivorans, which both use the roTCA cycle, require extremely high CO2 concentrations to grow as autotrophs, which has been attributed to the necessity of forcing citrate synthase to operate in a citrate-cleaving direction [20%–40% CO2 headspace; (Tang and Blankenship 2010, Steffens et al. 2021)]. These high concentrations may also be necessary given the absence of DIC toolkit genes in these genomes (Steffens et al. 2021). It would be interesting to determine whether Desulfurobacterium thermolithotrophum also requires such high CO2 concentrations for growth, given the presence of genes encoding ATP citrate lyase, which removes the necessity to drive citrate synthase in a citrate-cleaving direction. Given that other organisms that use the rTCA cycle have DIC transporters and CA enzymes encoded in their genomes, it may not be necessary to supplement the headspace of their cultures with high concentrations of CO2, as is customary [e.g. 10%–18%; (Wang et al. 2021a,b, Inagaki et al. 2003)[.

Carboxylase substrate specificities and potential affinities

PFOR and OGOR use CO2 as a substrate (see above). Since many of the genomes studied here encode more than two OFOR (Fig. 5), these organisms may express multiple versions of either or both enzymes. As described above, it has been established that these enzymes can be distinguished by different sensitivities to O2, and are differentially expressed in response to O2 availability during growth. Perhaps OFOR enzymes can also have different Km values for CO2, and organisms encoding multiple enzymes that metabolize pyruvate or 2-oxoglutarate could differentially express them in response to CO2 availability during growth.

The remaining carboxylation steps of the rTCA cycle (C3 → C4, C5 → C6) can use either CO2 or HCO3−, depending on the enzyme (see above; Fig. 5). For a few organisms, all potential rTCA or roTCA carboxylases identified in their genomes use CO2 (Fig. 5), and this may be a benefit when the environmental CO2 concentrations are sufficient for reliance on CO2 supply via diffusion of this gas across the cell membrane. For most, though it is possible for them to express an entirely CO2-dependent rTCA cycle, it is also possible to express an rTCA cycle that is dependent on both CO2 and HCO3−. For these organisms, the activity of either a DIC transporter or CA could supplement CO2 supply via diffusion with HCO3− acquired via active transport or CO2 hydration.

The situation is particularly interesting in Aquificota; for most members of this phylum, a completely CO2-reliant rTCA is not possible, as they use pyruvate carboxylase (HCO3−) for the C3 → C4 step. Furthermore, many of them also use HCO3− for the C5 → C6 step. As described above, instead of relying on isocitrate dehydrogenase (CO2) to carboxylate and reduce 2-oxoglutarate, they have a separate oxoglutarate carboxylase that uses HCO3− as a substrate to form oxalosuccinate (Aoshima et al. 2004a), which is then reduced to form isocitrate (Aoshima and Igarashi 2006, Aoshima and Igarashi 2008). It has been suggested that the use of HCO3− by oxoglutarate carboxylase might be helpful under low CO2 conditions. This suggestion is strengthened by the observation that isocitrate dehydrogenase does not have a high affinity for CO2 [1 mM (Kanao et al. 2002)]. However, use of HCO3− requires a means to maintain supply of this anion in the cytoplasm. Indeed, this double reliance of the rTCA cycle (C3 → C4, C5 → C6 steps) on HCO3− supply may explain why all members of Aquificota that have it, also encode DIC transporters that would deliver HCO3− to the cytoplasm (Fig. 5).

Conclusions

As detailed above, there are many correlations of environmental factors with steps of the rTCA and roTCA cycles, consistent with selective pressure by the habitat on this autotrophic pathway. This analysis was limited to microbes with complete genome sequences in the IMG database, and it would be good to extend it particularly to underrepresented lineages (e.g. phylum Nitrospina), and biochemistries (the roTCA cycle).

Given that many rTCA roTCA autotrophs live in habitats where energy sources are low (Chlorobiota) or erratic (vent and hot spring organisms including Aquificota and Campylobacterota), or rely on poor electron donors (Nitrospirota), it is not surprising that genome data from these organisms include steps to diminish energy consumption (e.g. alternatives to PEP carboxylase; DIC toolkit scarcity in some lineages; potential to reverse citrate synthase). It would be good to verify some of these observations experimentally. Can these organisms (particularly members of Campylobacterota) rely on sodium pump oxaloacetate decarboxylase as a carboxylase? Are there conditions under which the many rTCA organisms whose genomes encode citrate synthase, beyond the few known to use the roTCA cycle, use this enzyme to cleave citrate? Energetic considerations would also suggest that expensive options like fumarate reductase (NADH) would be disfavored, but the many unusual fumarate reductase genes described here suggest that it may be widespread. It would be good to verify this activity.

It does appear that rTCA and roTCA cycles reflect the organisms’ lifestyles with respect to O2. Members of Chlorobiota primarily encode genes for an anaerobic rTCA, while members of Nitrospirota encode an aerobic, O2-robust rTCA cycle. For those members of Chlorobiota that appear to encode a more robust rTCA cycle, it would be good to re-examine their sensitivity to O2. Based on the genes gathered here (class II fumarase, AcnA), some may be aerotolerant.

The distribution of the DIC toolkit also follows from habitat as well as carboxylase specificity. It would be good to verify these activities, but also to explore the lineages where they appear to be scarce or absent (e.g. Chlorobiota). It is possible that they are indeed present, but are not homologous to currently characterized DIC toolkit components. The existence of DIC toolkit components remaining to be uncovered is suggested by the relatively recent descriptions of novel components (Mangiapia et al. 2017, Scott et al. 2019, Wieschollek et al. 2024). Given the profound taxonomic, physiologic, and environmental differences among rTCA autotrophs, it is likely that the adaptations necessary to operate this cycle in its many lineages and habitats are at least as numerous and diverse as those observed for the CBB cycle.
