Assembly and comparative analysis of the first complete mitochondrial genome of Camellia sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’, endemic to Hainan Province, China
Authors: Dongliang Li, Yingyi Zhou, Chuanliang Fu, Xiaozhong Pan, Yanpo Liang, Zongzhuang Fang, Chuyan Xiao, Guangyue Wang, Xinchao Wang, Wen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCamellia sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ (C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’), an endemic, valuable tea plant from Hainan Province in China, has been utilized as a precious tea resource by local farmers. Nevertheless, no data on the mitochondrial genome of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ is available, which greatly limits our understanding of its phylogenetic classification and population identification.ResultsIn this study, the Camellia sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ complete mitochondrial genome was successfully assembled using the Nanopore and Illumina sequencing data for the first time. Key findings revealed a complex multi-branched structure comprising 12 contigs, with a total length of 793,531 bp and a GC content of 45.80%. A total of 70 genes were identified, including 38 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 28 tRNA genes, 3 rRNA genes, and one pseudogene (rps19). Out of the 31 PCGs shared by 18 Theaceae species, 28 of the PCGs were undergone purifying selection, and 3 genes (ccmC, rps1, and rps13) showed sign of positive selection. The codon usage analysis demonstrated that Leucine (Leu) and Serine (Ser) were the most preferred amino acids in C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’, and nearly all of the codons with RSCU > 1 showed the A/U bias at the third position. Tetramer repeats made up 40.71% of the simple sequence repeats (SSRs). 74 RNA editing sites were predicted in 17 PCGs, majority (91.89%) of them were C-to-U conversion pattern. 33 MTPTs were identified between the mitochondrial genome and chloroplast genome, with a combined length of 15,346 bp, accounting for 1.93% of the mitogenome. Moreover, the Maximum Likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed of 38 shared PCGs in 30 plant species, the results showed that C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ was phylogenetically independent of the species of sections Camellia, Chrysantha, and Oleifera. However, it didn’t support the independent subclade of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ due to the low support (BS = 55). Meanwhile, the chloroplast PCG-based phylogenetic analysis revealed that C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ clustered together with C. sinensis var. assamica (MH019307.1) into a common subclade with high support (BS = 81).ConclusionsOur work presents the first complete mitogenome of Camellia sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’, which could not only provide a reference genome for the comprehensive analysis of Theaceae family, but also contribute to the valuable information for further genomic breeding and evolutionary research of Camellia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07349-1.Keywords: Camellia sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’, Mitochondrial genome, Comparative genomics, Evolution, Phylogeny

Introduction

Mitochondria, which branch within Alphaproteo bacteria, are considered as semi-autonomous organelle of endosymbiotic origins [1–9]. Plant mitochondria not only provide energy for cellular life-support activities through oxidative phosphorylation, but also participate in biological processes of tricarboxylic acid cycling, phospholipid synthesis, apoptosis and autophagy. As a necessary structure for maintaining cellular life activities, they play an important role in cells with physiological and biochemical functions [9–13].

Mitochondria (along with chloroplasts) occupy a unique position among cellular organelles because of their possession of a separate genetic material that can encode parts of related proteins, but is still regulated by nuclear coding genes [14–16]. The mitochondrial genome of higher terrestrial plants generally contains 50 ~ 60 genes, which are predominantly maternally inherited [17, 18]. Compared to animals, there are many differences in mitogenomes size, structure and sequence mutation. The average size of higher terrestrial plants mitogenomes, which are larger than those of animals, ranges from 66 kb to 12 Mb [19, 20]. The conformation of the higher terrestrial plants mitochondrial DNA exhibits rich diversity, including circular, linear, and multi branched structures [17, 20–23]. These differences are mainly due to redundant mitochondrial protein coding (multiple copies) and frequent recombination and integration of exogenous DNA in the plant mitochondrial genome [8]. The sequence, structure, and size of plant mitochondrial genome are highly variable [24–27], while the encoding sequence mutation rate is very low (much lower than in the nucleus and animal mitochondrial DNA) but the rearrangement rate is high, the frequent recombination and lower mutation are called ‘The Paradox of Evolution’. Plant mitochondrial DNA evolves rapidly in structure, but slowly in sequence [18, 28, 29]. The synonymous substitution rate is several to tens of times lower than that of plant chloroplasts and nuclear genomes, and even 50 ~ 100 times lower than that of mammalian mitochondrial genomes [30]. The structure of the plant mitochondria is highly complex, it seems difficult to sequence, assemble and annotate [19]. Approximately 1,000 plant mitochondrial genomes have been sequenced, assembled and uploaded to NCBI GenBank. Growing research evidence indicates that plant mitochondrial genomes may be useful for development of molecular markers and species taxonomy, which is of significant importance for plant population genetics and evolution research [19].

Camellia sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ is a group cultivar of tea plant known as Huacha No.16 (GSCT16), approved by National Crop Variety Approval Committee as one of the elite tea cultivars in 1985 [31]. The typical plants generally grow in tropical rainforest of the Wuzhishan mountain range (Fig. 1). They are primarily an arbor tree with large thick leaves, whose blade surface is bulging, leaf margin is serrate, and the colour is yellow green [31]. The tea buds are sparse, stout, trichomeless, whereas the nurturing ability is strong, the spring buds germinating in mid-February, and reaching the peak of tea sprout in late February. That's precisely why we named it ‘The earliest spring tea in China’.

Field photo of Camellia sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’

The drinking history of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ can be traced back to the late Eastern Han Dynasty, the early era of Three Kingdoms, which is recorded in the Wu Pu’s Materia Medica (Wu Pu Ben Cao). Since 1950 s, it has been widely cultivated by indigenous peoples of Li and Miao Autonomous Cities and Counties in central mountainous regions of Hainan Province, China. In 1985, Hainan State Farms produced the blended CTC black tea made from C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’, which won the gold medal at the World Black Tea Championships held in the United Kingdom. It became very popular and was exported to more than 50 countries and territories over the world by reputable trade at that time.

Research on C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ has mainly focused on cuttings [32, 33], pest feeding damage characterization [34], key aroma compounds [35–37], and the major metabolic pathways [38]. As the results of self-incompatibility, the genome of Camellia is highly heterozygous and polyploidy [39], it’s regarded as one of the most challenging taxonomic and phylogenetic groups in plants [40–43]. Until now, only a few mitochondrial genomes of tea plant species have been published in the NCBI database [44]. However, the mitochondrial genomes of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ have not been reported currently. It is imperative to ascertain the genomic characteristics and conduct comparative and evolutionary analyses utilizing the complete mitochondrial genomes of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’.

In this study, the first complete mitochondrial genomes of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ were sequenced by combining the Illumina Novaseq 6000 and Nanopore PromethION sequencing platforms with second and third generation sequencing techniques based on the integrated long- and short-read pipelines. Then the assembled and annotated C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ was analyzed for genome structure, codon usage, repeat fragments, RNA editing sites, genome rearrangement, intracellular gene transfer, sequence divergence. Additionally, phylogenetic tree was constructed based on both mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes. Our research on the C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mtDNA will fill up vacancy for the Camellia species and will further promote molecular phylogenetics and evolution studies of this peculiar valuable tea plant.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and genome sequencing

The Camellia sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ specimens collected from their natural habitat in the National Park of Hainan Tropical Rainforest, Hainan Province, China (109°40'E, 18°51'N) (Fig. 1), and identified by researcher Xinchao Wang in Tea Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Voucher specimens were deposited at Hainan Academy of Agricultural Sciences under the voucher number HAAS20240315. The healthy, tender, and fresh leaves were collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately, and then stored at −80℃. High-quality genomic DNA was extracted from the leaves using a CTAB method [45]. After libraries construction using SQK-LSK109(Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Cambridge, the UK), DNA sequencing was performed using Nanopore PromethION sequencing platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Cambridge, the UK).

Raw data were then filtered and re-edited using NanoFilt v2.5.0 (https://github.com/wdecoster/nanofilt) and NanoPlot v1.0.0 (https://github.com/wdecoster/NanoPlot) in Nanopack [46].

Meanwhile, the libraries with average fragment length of 350 bp were constructed with the high-quality DNA using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing was then performed on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). After raw data quality control, using the NGS QC Tool Kit v2.3.3 [47].

RNA extracting and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from fresh plant leaves using the RNApure Fast Plant Kit (CW0598S; CWBIO Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China), after which the concentration, quality and integrity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Three micrograms of RNA were used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated according to the following steps. Firstly, mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Fragmentation was carried out using divalent cations under elevated temperature in an Illumina proprietary fragmentation buffer. First strand cDNA was synthesized using random oligonucleotides and Super Script II. Second strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. Remaining overhangs were converted into blunt ends via exonuclease/polymerase activities and the enzymes were removed. After adenylation of the 3′ends of the DNA fragments, Illumina PE adapter oligonucleotides were ligated to prepare for hybridization. To select cDNA fragments of the preferred 400~ 500 bp in length, the library fragments were purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, CA, USA). DNA fragments with ligated adaptor molecules on both ends were selectively enriched using Illumina PCR Primer Cocktail in a 15 cycle PCR reaction. Products were purified (AMPure XP system) and quantified using the Agilent high sensitivity DNA assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). The sequencing library was then sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Sequencing data contains a number of connectors, low-quality reads, so we use fastp (0.23.4) software to filter the sequencing data to get high quality sequence (Clean Data) for predicting RNA editing sites.

Genome assembly, annotation and visualization

Firstly, the Nanopore sequencing data were aligned with the reference sequence (NCBI Reference Sequence: Camellia tianeensis (PP727208.1), Camellia gigantocarpa (OP270590.1), Camellia sinensis (MH376284.1)) via Minimap2 v2.15-r905 (https://github.com/lh3/minimap2) [48]; then, we use miniasm v0.3-r179 (https://github.com/lh3/miniasm) [49] to assemble the aligned sequences to obtain the initial assembly results; next, we use nextPolish v1.3.1 (https://github.com/Nextomics/NextPolish) [50] to correct the initial results; then, we use bowtie2 software v2.5.4 (https://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/bowtie2/index.shtml) [51] to align the Illumina sequences with the corrected results; after that, we use Unicycler v0.4.8 (https://github.com/rrwick/Unicycler) [52] with default settings to assemble the Illumina data after alignment to obtain contigs, and then use Bandage Software v0.8.1 [53] for visualization. Finally, we use Minimap2 to align the Nanopore data with the contig sequences, combine the connection situation of Bandage, confirm the contig connection relationship, and manually organize and output the final results. We obtained a complete mitochondrial genome of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’. The complete mitogenome of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ was annotated by MITOFY [54] and MFANNOT [55].

Transfer RNA (tRNAs) were annotated using the program tRNAscan-SE (http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE) [56]. The results were checked and corrected manually to obtain the final annotation. The map of the C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitogenome was visualized using the OGDRAW program (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html) [57]. The assembled complete mitogenome sequence of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ has been submitted to the GenBank of NCBI and is openly available under the accession number: PV744423-PV744434.

Analysis of codon usage bias patterns, relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU), effective number of codons (ENC), and repeat sequences

PhyloSuite software v1.2.2 [58] was used to screen the PCGs sequences of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitochondrial genome, the codon usage bias and relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) was calculated based on the PCGs of the mitochondrial genome using MEGA software v7.0 [59]. The RSCU figures were drawn using the PhyloSuite software. The GC content of PCGs was calculated using the online tool CUSP (https://www.bioinformatics.nl/cgi-bin/emboss/cusp) [60].The effective number of codons (ENC) were calculated using CodonW software v1.4.2 [61] with default settings and used to analyze the effect of gene base composition on codon usage preferences. The GC plot was visualized using TBtools software v2.225 [62]. The scatter map of GC1, GC2 (GC12), and GC3 was created using Microsoft Excel 2021.

In this research, a method combined with Vmatch software v2.3.0 (http://www.vmatch.de/) and DupFinder Perl script is proposed to identify dispersed repeat sequences. Forward, palindromic, reverse, and complementary sequences with a length ≥ 30 bp were identified. Online tool MISA (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/) [63] was used to analysis simple repeat sequence. Tandem repeats and dispersed repeats were identified using Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF, v4.07b, https://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf.html) [64] and the REPuter network server [65], respectively.

Transformation of chloroplast-to-mitochondrial DNA and prediction of RNA editing sites

The assembled complete chloroplast genome sequence of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ has been submitted to the GenBank of NCBI Organelle Genome Resource Database (Accession number: PV744435). Homologous sequences in the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes were found out by BLAST software v2.6 (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) [66] with default parameters except E-value was set at le−5. The circular maps of mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes and gene transfer segments were visualized by using Circos v0.69–5 software [67].

Total RNA was sequenced, generating 19.4 Gb of Illumina clean data (Q20 = 98.22, Q30 = 94.60), which was used for RNA editing site predicting. The RNA sequencing data was aligned to coding sequence (CDS) using Bowtie2 software, and sorted using Samtools software v1.9 [68], and then identified the SNP sites between the sequencing data and the genome with bcftools software v1.9–170 [69], which can be used as potential RNA editing sites.

Identification of homologous fragments and collinear analysis

BLASTN results were obtained for pairwise comparison of 18 Theaceae species mitochondrial genomes by BLAST program with an E-values of le−5 and a word_size of 7, and the homologous sequences were used as a conservative collinear block to draw a Multiple Synteny Plot by MCscanX software [70].

Sequence divergence, Ka/Ks, and phylogenetic analysis

Global alignment of 41 homologous gene sequences from 15 Theaceae species was performed using MAFFT software v7.427 (–auto mode) [71]. DNA polymorphism analyses (sliding-window analyses) were calculated using DnaSP v5 [72] based on the alignment results to generate the nucleotide diversity (Pi) of these mitochondrial PCGs, and the results were visualized.

Group the analyzed species into pairs, extract homologous gene pairs, and then align the homologous gene pairs using MAFFT v7.427 software. After alignment, the Ka and Ks values for each gene pair was calculated using KaKs_Calculator v2.0 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/kakscalculator2/) [73] with the calculation method of MLWL. Finally, the Ka/Ks values of each gene pair was counted, and box plots and bar charts were draw by using the R-package (ggplot2) [74].

29 complete mitochondrial genome sequences from six different families (Theaceae, Ebenaceae, Primulaceae, Actinidiaceae, Ericaceae, and Asteraceae) (Supplementary Table S1) were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database, the NCBI accession numbers and abbreviations of all these observed mitogenomes are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The mitochondrial genome of H. annuus (MG770607.2) was used as outgroup.

Thirty-eight conserved mt PCGs (atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, atp9, ccmB, ccmC, ccmFC*, ccmFN, cob, cox1, cox2, cox3, matR, mttB, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6, nad7, nad9, rpl10, rpl16, rpl2, rpl5, rps1, rps12, rps13, rps14, rps19, rps3, rps4, rps7, sdh3, and sdh4) were extracted by the PhyloSuite software, lined up to construct a contiguous sequence and were then individually aligned using MaFFT software among the 30 species analyzed.

The aligned sequences were subsequently concatenated and trimmed by TrimAI v1.4. rev 15 (parameter: -gt 0.7) [75]. After that, the optimal evolutionary model was determined by jModelTest v2.1.10 [76], which identified the GTR model. Finally, a Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using RAxML v8.2.10 (https://cme.h-its.org/exelixis/software.html) [77] with the GTR GAMMA model and 1000 bootstrap replicates.

In the phylogenetic analysis based on chloroplast PCGs, 28 chloroplast genome sequences (Supplementary Table S2) for the same species were downloaded from NCBI database except Camellia sinensis (mitochondrial genome accession number: PP770440) and Rhododendron decorum, due to the data imbalance between mitochondrial genome and chloroplast genome in the NCBI database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/organelle/), the H. annuus (NC_007977.1) was selected as the outgroup.

Totally fifty-seven homologous single copy genes (atpA, atpB, atpE, atpF, atpH, atpI, cemA, matK, ndhC, ndhF, ndhJ, ndhK, petA, petB, petD, petG, petL, petN, psaA, psaB, psaI, psaJ, psbA, psbB, psbC, psbD, psbE, psbF, psbH, psbI, psbJ, psbK, psbL, psbM, psbN, psbT, psbZ, rbcL, rpl14, rpl16, rpl20, rpl22, rpl33, rpl36, rpoA, rpoB, rpoC1, rpoC2, rps11, rps14, rps18, rps2, rps3, rps4, rps8, ycf3, ycf4) were found by using OrthoFinder v2.3.14 [78], the MaFFT version 7.429 [71] was used to align these 57 common protein-coding genes of 28 chloroplast genomes. The gaps in the alignment were trimmed using Gblocks (Version 0.91b, http://molevol.cmima.csic.es). A phylogenetic tree of the 28 chloroplast genomes was then constructed using IQ-TREE-1.6.12 (http://www.iqtree.org/) [79] based on the Maximum-likelihood method with 1,000 bootstrap replications and the best nucleic acid replacement model: TVM + F + R2 model according to the BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion), which was selected using ModelFinder [80].

Results

Multibranched conformation of Camellia sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mtDNA

The total DNA of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ was sequenced, and the clean data were prepared for assembly, resulting in 10.57 Gb Illumina sequencing data (Q20 = 97.1%, Q30 = 92.4%) and 9.88 Gb Nanopore PromethION sequencing data with an average read length of 12.70 kb (Supplementary Table S3). As visualized in Fig. 2A, B, the mtDNA structure of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ was complex and had a multi-branched conformation, which consisted of 12 connected contigs (Fig. 2A). For subsequent analysis, we disassembled them into 12 linear contigs, each contig with the following lengths: 207,699 bp for contig1, 173,916 bp for contig2, 83,864 bp for contig3, 76,638 bp for contig4, 58,727 bp for contig5, 54,235 bp for contig6, 53,082 bp for contig7, 30,187 bp for contig8, 27,826 bp for contig9, 17,726 bp for contig10, 6,596 bp for contig11, 3,035 bp for contig12. For the mitochondrial genome visualization, Bandage software was used, after removing nodes originating from the nuclear and chloroplast genomes, 12 nodes were identified, each labeled with different numbers. These nodes are connected by lines, and the red and yellow nodes represent the predicted repeat regions. The repeated sequences that mediate genome recombination may lead to multiple conformations in the plant mitochondrial genome.

Figure 2B showed that the main structure Master had marked with corresponding length of each contig, custom numbers, and the coverage supported by Nanopore data. Repeat sequences are frequently involved in homologous recombination, Repeat 11 marked with red and Repeat 12 marked with yellow in the figure represent the predicted repeat sequences regions, which can mediate sequences recombination, and can generate 4 secondary structures (Sub1, Sub2, Sub3, and Sub4), with the connection pattern in secondary structure Sub1, Sub2, Sub3, and Sub4 being 3 → 12 → 10, 2 → 12 → 2, 6 → 11 → 4, 8 → 11 → 5; 3 → 12 → 2, 10 → 12 → 2, 6 → 11 → 4, 8 → 11 → 5; 3 → 12 → 2, 10 → 12 → 2, 8 → 11 → 4, 6 → 11 → 5; and 3 → 12 → 10, 2 → 12 → 2, 8 → 11 → 4, 6 → 11 → 5, respectively. The results indicating the complexity of the mitochondrial genome structure of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’. Sequencing depth analysis reveals an average coverage of 104.9 × for second-generation sequencing and 97.1 × for third-generation sequencing (Supplementary Fig. S1). No sequence breakpoints and gaps were observed at any position, indicating high-quality data. The results provide strong evidence for the accuracy of the mitochondrial genome assembly.

The mitochondrial genome was further annotated, revealing a total of 38 distinct protein-coding genes, 28 tRNA genes, 3 rRNA genes and 1 pseudogene rps19 (Table 1). Among the 38 protein-coding genes, 24 were considered to be core genes, and 14 were non-core genes. The 24 core genes included 5 ATP synthase genes (atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, and atp9), 9 NADH dehydrogenase genes (nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6, nad7, and nad9), 4 cytochrome c biogenesis genes (ccmB, ccmC, ccmFC*, and ccmFN), 3 cytochrome c oxidase genes (cox1, cox2, and cox3), a transport membrane protein (mttB), maturases (matR), and a ubiquinol cytochrome c reductase gene (cob). The non-core genes included 4 large subunits of the ribosome (rpl5, rpl10, rpl2, rpl16), 8 small subunits of the ribosome (rps1, rps3, rps4, rps7, rps12, rps13, rps14, and rps19), and 2 succinate dehydrogenase genes (sdh3, sdh4).

We also analyzed the mutation sites in the 26 shared genes among these 9 Theaceae species to construct haplotype networks to investigate gene variation in greater depth (Fig. 3). The results showed that S. sinensis exhibited the most typical haplotype composition except for the rps7 and nad6 gene, while C. chekiangoleosa, C. gigantocarpa, C. granthamiana, C. lanceoleosa, C. nitidissima, C. oleifera, C. sinensis, and C. tianeensis shared the same composition in these 12 genes (atp4, atp9, ccmB, ccmC, matR, nad3, nad4L, nad6, rpl5, rps12, rps19, and sdh3).

Haplotype networks of 26 shared genes based on the 9 Theaceae species

Using the 31 shared protein-coding genes among the 18 Theaceae species, we calculated the non-synonymous/synonymous mutation ration (Ka/Ks) to observe the evolutionary effects of mitochondrial genome under environmental stress (Fig. 4). It showed that the average Ka/Ks values for the 28 PCGs (atp1, atp6, atp8, ccmFC*, ccmFN, cob, cox1, cox2, cox3, matR, mttB, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad7, rpl10, rpl16, rpl2, rpl5, rps19, rps3, rps4, rps7, sdh3, and sdh4) were < 1, especially atp1 (Ka/Ks = 0.18), atp8(Ka/Ks = 0.18), rpl5(Ka/Ks = 0.18), and cox1 (Ka/Ks = 0.1), indicating these 28 PCGs have undergone purification selection during the evolutionary process and have relatively stable protein functions. In contrast, the average Ka/Ks values of ccmC, rps1, and rps13 were > 1, ccmC (Ka/Ks = 1.45) and rps13 (Ka/Ks = 1.95) were strongly positively selected. The Ka/Ks analysis showed that most PCGs of Theaceae mitochondria present negative selection and were highly conserved in the process of evolution.

Analysis of codon usage

In the complete mitochondrial genome of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’, we identified a total of 10,583 codons within the PCGs. All the PCGs used AUG as the initiation codon, while the termination codon UAA, UAG, and UGA were applied with the proportion of 52.63%, 15.79%, and 31.58%, respectively (Supplementary Table S4). The C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitochondrial DNA encodes all 20 amino acids, and we found 61 different types of amino acid codons (Fig. 5, Table 2). The highest frequency codon is UUU. Among the 20 amino acids, Leucine has the highest number of codons, with 1082 codons (representing 10.22% of the total), followed by Serine with 992 codons (accounting for 9.37%), while Tryptophan has the fewest codons, with only 157 (1.48% of the total). Codons with a relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) greater than 1 are considered to be used for amino acid preference. Interestingly, Methionine (AUG) and Tryptophan (UGG) showed no codon preference (RSCU = 1), we observed that 29 codons were used more frequently than expected, indicated by relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values > 1, and 33 codons were used less frequently than expected (RSCU < 1). In these high-frequency codons (RSCU > 1), the position of the third codon is A or U (except for ACC, and UUG). In low-frequency codons (RSCU < 1), the position of the third codon is mostly G or C (except for UGA, GCA, AUA, CUA, and ACA). This is a common feature of codon bias in terrestrial plant organelle genomes. Alanine (Ala) has a preference for using GCU codons, and its RSCU value is the highest among mitochondrial PCGs, reaching 1.5859.

We also calculated the effective number of codons (ENC) for the 38 PCGs (Supplementary Table S5, Fig. 6B), which ranged from 33.62 ~ 61 with an average ENC value of 50.96, 55% of the ENC ratios were distributed in the 0.05 ~ 0.15 group, 24% were distributed in the −0.05 ~ 0.05 group, indicating that the actual ENC values of most genes deviate significantly from the theoretical ENC values, and the codon usage bias is less affected by mutation pressure and more affected by natural selection pressure. Overall, the codon usage bias is influenced by both mutations and natural selection pressure in C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitochondrial DNA, but natural selection pressure is the primary factor influencing the codon usage bias.

Furthermore, in a neutrality plot analysis, we observed a correlation of 0.0558 between GC12 and GC3 in C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitochondrial DNA, indicating a weak correlation between GC3 and GC12, and mutations have different effects on the usability of GC1, GC2, and GC3 bases of codons. It suggests that base mutations play a minor role in the formation of codon preference in C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ genes.

Repeat fragments and prediction of RNA editing events

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) are short tandemly repeated DNA motifs ranging from 1 to 6 bp. SSRs are useful due to their polymorphism, ease of PCR detection, codominant inheritance, and extensive genome coverage. The C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitochondrial genome SSRs were identified using the Tandem Repeats Finder program. There were 226 SSRs discovered, and Fig. 7 displays the proportion of each kind. Tetramer repeats comprised 40.71% of the total, followed by dimer repeats at 27.43%, trimer repeats at 14.16%, monomer repeats at 11.06%, pentamer repeats at 6.19%, and hexamer repeats at 0.44% (Supplementary Table S6). AAAG/CTTT repetitions were the most common form of tetramer SSRs, accounting for 31.52%, whereas AG/CT repeats were the most common form of dimer SSRs, accounting for 59.68%. Moreover, the C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitochondrial genome has only one hexamer SSRs, located on the chromosome 10, which is an empty chromosome, we couldn’t get the annotation information. The positions of the pentamer and hexamer are displayed in Supplementary Table S7. Tandem repeat sequence, also known as satellite DNA, refers to a core repeat unit of about 7 ~ 200 bases that repeats multiple times in a tandem manner. They are widely present in eukaryotic genomes and prokaryotes. According to Supplementary Table S8, the mitochondrial genome of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ has 30 tandem repeats with a percent matches greater than 77%, ranging in length from 14 to 39 bp, and 576 dispersed repeats were observed with lengths extending from 26 to 885 bp, including 276 forward repeats and 300 palindromic repeats (Supplementary Table S9).

In this research, we aimed to predict RNA editing sites in 17 distinct protein-coding genes (PCGs) within the mitochondrial genome of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ to gain a deeper understanding of gene expression within the mitochondrial genome (Fig. 8). We predicted the RNA editing sites based on the RNA sequencing data. A total of 74 RNA editing events were predicted at the editing sites, 68 editing sites involving the conversion of the nucleotide C to T (U), about 91.89% of the total (Supplementary Table S10). In addition, the remaining 6 editing sites involving the conversion of the nucleotide A-T(U), G-A, G-C, G-T(U), (U)T-A, and (U)T-G, respectively. Notably, the rps3 gene exhibited the highest number of RNA editing occurrences among all mitochondrial genes, with 10 RNA editing sites identified, followed by atp4 and rps12 (rpl16) genes, which have undergone 8 and 7 RNA editing events respectively. The cob and matR genes only predicted one RNA editing event, with the least number of edits among all mitochondrial genes.

Intracellular gene transfer from chloroplast to mitochondrial organelles

Using BLAST v2.6, we performed homologous fragment analysis on C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitochondrial and chloroplast genome segments with > 70% similarity, through sequence similarity analysis, 33 gene-containing homologous segments were discovered between the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’, with alignment lengths ranging from 32 to 6,660 bp (Fig. 9). The number of mismatches in these fragments varied from 0 to 174. Collectively, these homologous fragments totaled 15,346 bp, accounting for approximately 1.93% of the C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitochondrial DNA. Mitochondrial plastid DNA (MTPTs) are plastid derived DNA fragments found in the mitochondrial genome. Of these 33 homologous fragments, MTPT 1 and MTPT 2 were the longest with 6,660 bp in length, MTPT 31 was the shortest with 32 bp in length.

Locations of the transferred fragments between mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of Camellia sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’. Gray circle represents mitochondrial genome, and green circle represents chloroplast genome

Based on the nucleotide sequence similarity, a total of 19 ~ 53 mitochondria potentially derived from plastids were identified through C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ and 15 other mitochondrial DNA samples from the Camellia genus (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Table S11). The lengths of these mitochondria range from 32 to 9,573 bp, with total lengths spanning from 10,709 to 30,065 bp, accounting for 1.33% to 2.74% of the respective mitochondrial genomes. Subsequently, we focused on annotating these MTPTs and uncovered the following findings: most MTPTs contain plastid genes or gene fragments. A total of 12 complete PCGs were found, including ndhJ, orf42, petG, petL, psbE, psbF, psbJ, psbL, rpl2, rpl23, ycf15, and ycf68. Majority of them are photosynthesis-related genes, particularly those associated with photosystem II protein complexes, while a few are self-replicating genes or genes of unknown function. Additionally, we identified several gene fragments arising from plastid migration, including atpB, atpE, and psbC genes, which may have undergone sequence loss during migration. Moreover, certain small fragment sequences from chloroplasts appear to be subsets of larger fragments or occur multiple times in mitochondrial DNA, suggesting these fragments may have undergone independent rounds of transfer integration, integration, replication, and recombination within the mitochondrial genome after their integration.

We proceeded to annotate these homologous sequences and identified 17 complete genes, which included 7 protein-coding genes (petG, petL, psbE, psbF, psbJ, psbL, rpl2), 9 tRNA genes (trnA-UGC, trnD-GUC, trnI-CAU, trnI-GAU, trnM-CAU, trnN-GUU, trnP-UGG, trnV-GAC, trnW-CCA), and 1 rRNA (rrn16). Notably, some small fragment sequences from chloroplasts are subsets of larger fragment sequences or appear multiple times in mitochondrial DNA, indicating that these fragments may have undergone multiple independent transfer integration, replication, and recombination within mitochondrial DNA after transfer integration.

Construction of phylogenetic tree based on the PCGs

We constructed a Maximum Likelihood tree based on the mtDNA of 38 conserved PCGs from a total of 30 species belonging to 6 different families (Theaceae, Actinidiaceae, Ebenaceae, Ericaceae, Primulaceae, and Asteraceae) within the angiosperms (Fig. 12). The set of 38 shared PCGs comprised atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, atp9, ccmB, ccmC, ccmFC*, ccmFN, cob, cox1, cox2, cox3, matR, mttB, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6, nad7, nad9, rpl10, rpl16, rpl2, rpl5, rps1, rps12, rps13, rps14, rps19, rps3, rps4, rps7, sdh3, and sdh4. Helianthus annuus is employed as outgroup for phylogenetic relationships studies. Of the 27 nodes on the ML phylogenetic tree, only 13 nodes exhibited bootstrap support values > 80, but only 6 out of 17 nodes well-supported for the Theaceae clade. However, the overall topology of the ML phylogenetic tree was consistent with the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group IV (APG IV) classification system. The phylogeny results demonstrate clear clustering of plants from each of the different families, with the 18 species of Theaceae clustered into one group. Among the 18 Theaceae species, S. sinensis and S. sinensis var. sinensis clustered on the same branch and formed a basal clade (BS = 98), the remaining 16 Camellia species clustered into a single branch with a high support (BS = 98). Within the Camellia lineage, C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ was phylogenetically independent of species of sections Camellia, Chrysantha, and Oleifera. Significantly, C. gigantocarpa (OP270590.1), which was classified into section Oleifera, unexpectly clustered together with C. sinensis var. assamica (MK574876.1), C. sinensis (MH376284.1) and C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ into a common subclade, in which C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ was the basal subclade but with low support (BS = 55).

Discussion

Mitochondrial genome structure and size variations

Many fairly large repetitive sequences frequently recombine intra- or inter-molecular in the mitochondrial genome of angiosperms (flowering plants), a highly active recombination and integration process makes mitochondrial genomes of plants large, complex and dynamic structures [23, 81–86]. With the continuous development and maturity of mitogenome sequencing and assembly techniques, the structure of the mitochondrial genome is evolving to include different forms than the classical single circular structure, such as multiple circular, linear, branching or composite structures [86–93].

In the present study, the integrating Illumina short-read and Nanopore long-read combined sequencing method was employed to complete the mitogenome of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’. The mitogenome is 793,531 bp long and exhibit a multi-branched conformation, composed of 12 connected contigs with the lengths of 207,699 bp, 173,916 bp, 83,864 bp, 76,638 bp, 58,727 bp, 54,235 bp, 53,082 bp, 30,187 bp, 27,826 bp, 17,726 bp, 6,596 bp, and 3,035 bp. Within the mitochondrial genome of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’, 69 functional genes were identified, including 38 distinct protein-coding genes, 28 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, 3 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. Compared to reported species from the Theaceae family in this study, the length of mitogenome is intermediate. The shortest is S. sinensis var. sinensis, spanning 681,203 bp and encoding 40 protein-coding genes, while the longest is C. tianeensis, spanning 1,098,121 bp and encoding 28 protein-coding genes [94]. However, after querying the NCBI database and reviewing the literature, we identified 26 complete Camellia mitochondrion genomes (Supplementary Table S13). The results show that the size of the published Camellia mitogenomes range from 707,441 to 1,098,121 bp, with an average size of 917,108 bp. The shortest is Camellia assamica from India, spanning 707,441 bp, while the longest is still Camellia tianeensis, spanning 1,098,121 bp. The size of Camellia sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitogenome is intermediate, suggesting that the size of the published Camellia mitogenomes is conserved.

Significantly, the guanine-cytosine (GC) content is a key feature to differentiate the genomes of species, a high GC content represents strong stability of double stranded DNA. The multi-branched C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitochondrial genome that was published here is 793,531 bp long, and it has a GC content of 45.80%, which quite close to that of other sequenced Camellia plants mitochondrial genomes, for example, Camellia sinensis var. assamica cv. Duntsa, Camellia tianeensis, Camellia huana, Camellia liberofilamenta, Camellia nitidissima, Indian tea (Camellia assamica) have GC contents of 45.62%, 45.71%, 45.73%, 45.78%, 45.71% and 45.75%, respectively [94–96]. The results provide further evidence that GC content has remained conserved throughout the evolution of higher plants.

Patterns of codon use bias and evolution

Codon usage bias is a complex phenomenon that has been firmly established to be widespread in most living organisms. It plays a significant role on genome evolution [97–99]. Previous studies have demonstrated that codon usage bias is mainly due to the composition of the genome base, tRNA content and selection-mutation-drift. Furthermore, some other possible influencing factors are also involved, including gene expression level, length of genes, GC content bias, evolutionary age of genes, etc. [61, 100–104]. In this study, 38 distinct protein-coding genes were used to evaluate the GC content at various positions and the relative synonymous codon usage in mitochondrial DNA of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’. Overall, there is a strong A/U bases bias at the third codon position in the mitochondrial genome. The finding consistent with similar reports in the previous study on sect. Chrysantha (Camellia L.) [94], Hemerocallis citrina [105], Angelica biserrata [106], Fritillaria ussuriensis Maxim. [107], Mangifera indica [108], Evolvulus alsinoides [109], Blechnopsis orientalis [89], Rubus chingii var. suavissimus [88] mitochondrial genomes (Supplementary Table S14), which is a very common phenomenon observed in studied mitogenomes [110].

Codon preference analysis was conducted on all PCGs of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ and 17 other mitochondrial genomes from the Theaceae family (Supplementary Fig. S3, Supplementary Table S15). When the RSCU was greater than 1, 18 mitochondrial genomes exhibited the use of 29 ~ 31 codons, indicating a preference for these codons in Theaceae family organelle genes. Among the high-frequency codons (RSCU > 1), the third codon position predominantly contained A or U. In contrast, for low-frequency codons (RSCU < 1), the third codon position was mainly G or C. This pattern reflects the common codon bias observed in terrestrial plant organelle genomes. Furthermore, UAA was the preferred termination codon over UAG and UGA across the 18 mitochondrial genomes. Alanine (Ala) showed a strong preference for the GCU codon, with its RSCU value being the highest among mitochondrial PCGs, except in Camellia sinensis var. assamica, Camellia chekiangoleosa, Camellia granthamiana, Camellia lanceoleosa and Camellia oleifera. Among the 5 Camellia mitochondrial PCGs, the highest frequency codon is the termination codon UAA, followed by GCU.

Interestingly, the codon usage patterns at the third codon positions of chloroplast genomes exhibited a trend similar to that of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitochondrial DNA, as observed in Ampelopsis Michaux [111], Angelica biserrate [106], Euphorbiaceae [112], Solanaceae [113]. This observation further underscores the concordant codon usage preferences between mtDNA and cpDNA, highlighting a high degree of similarity in their codon usage bias.

Phylogenetic and evolutionary analyses of Camellia sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’

We performed a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis using shared PCG sequences from the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes. The overall structure of the phylogenetic tree based on mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences aligned with the classification provided by the APG IV system, except the phylogenetic relationships within genus Camellia, which was not entirely consistent. For example, C. gigantocarpa, which is considered as a member of the section Oleifera, grouped with three species from the section Camellia in the mitochondrial-derived tree, the similar clustering results were observed in the mitochondrial genomes study of Camellia drupifera and Camellia tachangensis. However, C. gigantocarpa clustered together with Camellia drupifera or formed an independent basal clade in the phylogenetic trees based on chloroplast sequences [114, 115]. Moreover, the overall structure of phylogenetic tree based on mitochondrial sequences was inconsistent with those built with shared genes of the chloroplast genomes, we can observe that the chloroplast PCG phylogenetic tree exhibits higher resolution compared to the mitochondrial CDS-based phylogenetic tree. Camellia sinensis var. assamica (MH019307.1) and C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ clustered together based on chloroplast sequences, while C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ formed a basal subclade within Theaceae when mitochondrial sequences were used. However, cytonuclear discordance, which refers to significantly different phylogenetic patterns between nuclear markers and cytoplasmic genes (such as mitochondrial and chloroplast genes), has been observed in various plant populations and is often attributed to processes like hybridization and incomplete lineage sorting [116, 117]. Compared to chloroplast genomes, the slower evolutionary rate of mitochondrial genomes and the absence of mutations in certain mitochondrial PCGs may result in smaller genetic distances among related species, leading to unreliable phylogenetic relationships [18, 30]. Furthermore, both mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes may experience paternal leakage during inheritance, which can cause discrepancies in the genetic lineages of these two organellar genomes within the same species [118]. Due to the self-incompatibility, frequent interspecific hybridization, and polyploidization, some species have evolved similar morphological traits. Consequently, the classification of the Theaceae remains challenging, and future studies required to address the incongruences observed in the phylogenetic tree based on mitochondrial sequences.

RNA editing is a post-transcriptional process that involves changes in the nucleotide sequence of the plant mitochondrial genome, including insertion, deletion, and substitution [119–121]. Abundant diversity of genetic information is due to the process, which can modify it on mRNA level, converting cytidine(C) to uridine(U), sometimes resulting in the alteration of initiation and termination codons, but more often creating internal codons with powerful functional correlations, so that contributes to improving protein folding [122–124]. In the mitochondrial genome of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’, 74 RNA editing sites were predicted in 17 PCGs based on the RNA sequencing data, majority (91.89%) of them were C-to-U pattern. rps3 gene underwent 10 times editing events, which is the most-edited gene in of them. By comparison, the cob and matR genes each experienced only one RNA editing event. Moreover, 60.81% of RNA editing sites located at the second codon position. The hydrophobic/hydrophilic property of 54.05% of the amino acids were changed, 41.89% changed from hydrophilic to hydrophobic, 10.81% changed from hydrophobic to hydrophilic, resulting in increased protein hydrophobicity, and remaining 1.35% turn to stop codons (Supplementary Table S10), which terminate the protein synthesis in advance, the same phenomenon also reported in Fritillaria ussuriensis Maxim., kiwifruit(Actinidia), rapeseed (Brassica napus L.), Ventilago leiocarpa, Oenothera, carrots(Daucus carota L.) [107, 125–129]. Early-termination maybe caused by nonsense or functional editing [130, 131]. Overall, identification of RNA editing events is very important for the study of the expression and function of mitochondrial genes in C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’, which could provide possibilities for deciphering gene functions with novel codons.

Nucleotide substitution rate is a key index to familiarize the evolution of plant mt genes when investigating the evolutionary dynamics of PCG sequences within genetically relative species [30, 132]. The Ka/Ks ratio was usually used to estimate the selection pressure on PCGs [73]. Ka/Ks analysis of 31 shared PCGs with mitochondrial genomes from C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ and other 17 Theaceae plants shows that the PCGs of the C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitochondrial genes are generally conserved. However, the Ka/Ks values of ccmC (Ka/Ks = 1.45), rps13(Ka/Ks = 1.95) and rps1(Ka/Ks = 1.002707) were ≥ 1, implying positive selection has worked on these coding genes in C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’. Cytochrome c biogenesis is an important biological process essential for cellular energy metabolism, redox reactions, and apoptosis. Cytochrome c is a protein with significant biological functions, playing a key role in electron transfer within the electron transport chain. The enzyme encoded by the ccmC gene is involved in the folding and modification of cytochrome c protein, ensuring its proper function in respiratory and photosynthetic electron transport [133–135]. The two ribosomal small subunit protein genes, rps1 and rps13, are also involved in ribosome composition and protein synthesis processes, these two genes play essential roles in various biological processes in eukaryotes, including embryogenesis and leaf morphogenesis [136–138]. C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ is an arbor tree with large, thick leaves, primarily found in tropical rainforests characterized by strong light, high temperatures, and high humidity. The three PCGs with high Ka/Ks ratio indicates its vital function in C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ evolution and phylogeny, which maybe relative to habitat environmental stress and adaptation evolution, speculate that they may be possible to regulate the leaf morphogenesis process, thereby driving leaf enlargement and thickening. The Ka/Ks analysis is a widely applied method to estimate the selective constrains acting on the PCGs, which is considered as a classical perspective for delving into area of biological adaptability, heredity and evolution [106].

Gene transfer between mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes

Gene transfer event is a universal phenomenon in mitochondrial genome during the evolution of higher plants [139–141]. Previous studies demonstrated that the transmission of genetic material can be divided into Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT) between species and Intracellular Gene Transfer (IGT) within a species. HGT events often appear between phylogenetically distant species or between cytoplasmic organelles (such as chloroplasts and mitochondria) and the nucleus without the mode of parent-to-offspring inheritance [142–147]. However, IGT events refer to the bidirectional transmission of DNA between the nucleus and mitochondria, as well as from chloroplasts to the nucleus and mitochondria [148–150]. The IGT events from plastids to mitochondria inferred based on the MTPTs originated at least 300 million years ago (Mya), before the divergence of extant gymnosperms and angiosperms [151]. MTPTs have been hypothesized to be “virtually entirely” non-functional genes with only rare instances of replacement of mitochondrial tRNA genes by their plastid equivalents [152, 153]. Reportedly, the total length of MTPTs found varies significantly among different species, which ranging from 0.6 kb in Vigna angularis to 130.5 kb in Amborella trichopoda [110], and the integration of plastome sequences into mitogenomes can range from 0.32 to 12% [154, 155]. Here, C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ presented 15,346 bp MTPTs, accounting for 1.93% of the mtDNA, which is significantly smaller than that of Blechnopsis orientalis (14.89%) [89], Echinacanthus longipes (14.59%) [156], Rubus chingii var. suavissimus (13.16%) [88], Cucurbita pepo (11.6%) [54] and other plants [87, 104, 106, 157, 158], but close to Camellia. sinensis var. sinensis ‘Dahongpao’ (1.91%) [159], C. drupifera (1.73%) [115], Garcinia mangostana (1.7%) [160]. The results indicated there was less transfer events in C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ than that in most reported plants, and the segments transferred from chloroplast into mitochondrial were relatively conserved.

Conclusion

In this study, combined strategy of Illumina and Nanopore sequencing technologies were used to acquire high-quality sequencing data of the C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ mitogenome. Subsequently, we assembled and annotated the mitogenome with a total length of 793,531 bp and overall GC content of 45.80%, which consisted of 12 contigs, its mtDNA structure present a unique complex multi-branched conformation. A total of 70 genes with 38 PCGs, 28 tRNA genes, 3 rRNA genes and one pseudogene (rps19) were predicted in the genome. Codon usage analysis indicated a codons preference of A/U. 226 SSRs and 576 dispersed repeat sequences were identified, the majority of the transferred sequences from the chloroplast genome to the mitogenome of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ were tRNA genes. 74 RNA editing events were predicted in 17 PCGs, 41.89% the amino acids in C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ underwent conversion from hydrophilic amino acids to hydrophobic amino acids, which improved stability of protein structure. Phylogenetic tree was constructed based on C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ and 29 other plants genomes, which strengthen our understanding of C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ status within the Theaceae family despite some inconsistent with the APG IV classification system. However, phylogenetic analysis based on chloroplast genome analysis supported C. sinensis var. assamica ‘Hainan Dayezhong’ close relationship with C. sinensis var. assamica (MH019307.1) (BS = 81). Additionally, Ka/Ks analysis, intracellular gene transfer analysis, and genome duplication and loss analysis were conduct to provide a more comprehensive understanding of mitogenome evolution in these closely related species. In conclusion, our study demonstrated that the complexity of the organelle genome variation in Theaceae family, however, organelle genomic information could help to illustrate the phylogenetic relationships for biological research on taxa in Theaceae family.
