The influence of bilingualism on the assessment and treatment of an Italian–English speaker with the logopenic variant of PPA
Authors: Roberta Tomasoni, Gaia C Santi, Serena Tagliente, Chiara Griseta, Simona Aresta, Allegra Benzini, Paola Santacesaria, Cinzia Palmirotta, Maria Luisa Mandelli, Stephanie Grasso, Petronilla Battista
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe impact of bilingualism on speech‐language assessment and therapy in primary progressive aphasia (PPA) remains underexplored, despite its suggested influence on disease presentation.METHODSA bilingual Italian (L1)—English (L2) individual with logopenic‐variant PPA completed a bilingual assessment as well as dual‐language lexical retrieval therapy (LRT). His bilingual experience was characterized in detail, and therapy outcomes were evaluated within and across languages.RESULTSPre‐morbidly, the patient was a relatively balanced bilingual individual with stronger L1 literacy. Following disease onset, L2 showed faster decline, whereas L1 remained stable. Age at acquisition, dominance, and language use were among the main factors contributing to his language maintenance and decline. Therapy yielded relatively equivalent item‐specific gains in both languages (L1 d 2 = 5.0; L2 d 2 = 5.6), asymmetric crosslinguistic transfer to L2 > L1, and modest functional improvements.DISCUSSIONFindings support the relevance of in‐depth bilingual assessment and therapy to guarantee successful treatment in PPA, unveiling the relevance of bilingual experience in shaping treatment outcomes.Keywords: bilingual experience, bilingual speech‐language assessment, bilingual speech‐language intervention, crosslinguistic transfer, lexical retrieval therapy, multilingualism, primary progressive aphasia

INTRODUCTION

Primary progressive aphasia (PPA) refers to a family of neurodegenerative syndromes in which speech and language impairment is the initial and most prominent feature of the clinical picture. Consensus criteria describe three PPA variants: the semantic variant (svPPA), associated with frontotemporal lobar degeneration pathology and characterized by loss of word meaning; the nonfluent/agrammatic variant (nfvPPA), associated with frontotemporal lobar degeneration pathology and marked by effortful, nonfluent speech with agrammatism and/or apraxia of speech; and the logopenic variant (lvPPA), commonly associated with Alzheimer's disease (AD) and characterized by word‑finding difficulties and impaired sentence repetition due to phonological working‑memory deficits. 1

Tailored speech and language interventions have been developed for people with PPA to enhance communication abilities. 2 A major shortcoming of prior studies is that most have involved monolingual participants only, even though estimates suggest that more than half of the world's population is multilingual. 3 Bilingualism has several advantages for brain health, as it can reshape neurodegenerative trajectories by bolstering cognitive reserve, enhancing executive control and attentional flexibility, and has been linked to delayed dementia onset. 4 Retrospective data suggest an ≈4‐year delay in the onset of lvPPA, with smaller, variable delays in nfvPPA and svPPA. 5

Existing work in bilingual PPA shows parallel or asymmetric First Language (L1) versus Second Language (L2) decline, with L1 consistently better preserved than L2, and that patterns of decline are shaped by age at acquisition, and premorbid and postmorbid proficiency. However, these results may be biased by the lack of systematic language‑sensitive assessments in both languages, which is detrimental to capturing patients’ cognitive–linguistic strengths and impairments, thereby limiting a comprehensive tracking of the evolution of individual abilities. In the same vein, few studies have examined how bilingualism impacts outcomes in speech and language therapy (SLT) for individuals with this syndrome. 6 The effectiveness of SLT can depend on the individual's proficiency in each language, with therapy being potentially more successful in the more dominant language. Evidence suggests that bilingual patients may experience comparable or even better outcomes when therapy is appropriately adapted, as skills acquired in one language can sometimes transfer to the other, facilitating recovery or maintenance of language functions across both languages. 7 , 8 In this case report, we characterized the bilingual profile of an Italian–English lvPPA participant by developing an in‐depth functionally equivalent bilingual assessment and a tailored lexical retrieval therapy (LRT) administered in Italian and English. The relationship between bilingual experience with the language profile before and after treatment was explored by considering the most relevant bilingual features in shaping L1 and L2 performance. We analyzed whether intervention leads to within‐ and cross‐linguistic improvement in (1) naming, (2) formal speech‐language assessment, and (3) connected‐speech production.

Based on prior work, we hypothesized that (1) baseline performance would be modulated by age at acquisition, with more preserved L1 abilities 9 ; (2) LRT would yield gains in both L1 and L2, with greater cross‐linguistic transfer (CLT) to L1 10 , 11 , 12 in the context of more stable performance on language assessments for L1 compared to L2 after therapy; and (3) generalization effects would extend beyond lexico‐semantic tasks to functional communication, although evidence in PPA is inconsistent. 13 , 14

METHODS

Case history

In April 2025, a bilingual speaker of Italian and English (L.R.), a right‐handed 60‐year‐old man with 18 years of formal education, was referred to our laboratory of neuropsychology by his family after a 2‐year history of progressive language deficits. During his first visit to our lab, L.R. was accompanied by his wife, who participated in providing the patient's history. He lived for many years abroad, including in the United Kingdom, France, and Japan, returning to Italy permanently a few years earlier. His symptoms included progressive word‐finding difficulties, as well as frequent latencies and pauses in his oral production, especially in English. Although he was able to carry out his daily activities, he experienced difficulties with writing, including spelling errors, particularly in English and produced occasional phonological errors in both languages. His wife also reported memory deficits for recent events, mild social withdrawal, and anxiety when speaking with unfamiliar people.

Speech and language assessment

To fully characterize L.R.'s speech‐language profile, a structured assessment protocol was administered in Italian and English before and after LRT (Table S1 of Supplementary Material reporting the full list of speech‐language tests). The protocol assessed articulatory, phonetic–phonological, lexico‐semantic, morphosyntactic, and discourse domains. When parallel validated tools were not available across both languages, functionally equivalent assessments were adopted (e.g., Screening for Aphasia in Neurodegeneration [SAND] 16 , 17 was compared to the equivalent Mini‐Linguistic State Examination [MLSE] 18 ). Because one of the patient's main language difficulties related to naming, developing an ad hoc naming task was essential to directly compare lexical retrieval in the two languages. Thus, an experimental 20‐item task was developed in Italian and English. Items consisted of 10 living and 10 non‐living nouns, balanced across frequency tiers (low, medium, high) and matched for word length and semantic category. Italian words were selected from the Corpus of Lexicon of Italian written Frequency (COLFIS) database 19 and English words from Celex. 20 Frequency groups were defined using log10 of frequency, and translation equivalents were carefully matched across languages. All images were selected from the Bank of Standardized Stimuli (BOSS) database 21 and the same images were probed in Italian and English. Stimulus lists and lexical characteristics are provided in Table S2. Connected speech was elicited with the “Cat Rescue” picture, 22 and self‐perceived communication outcomes were assessed with the Communication Outcomes After Stroke (COAST) questionnaire. 23

Bilingual language background

Bilingual experience was assessed using a trans‐adapted Italian version in reference to the Bilingual Language Profile (BLP), 24 Language History Questionnaire (LHQ‐3), 25 and LINGHUA Language Background Survey. The trans‐adapted Italian version of the BLP provided a quantitative index of pre and post‐morbid dominance, proficiency, and usage, while the trans‐adapted version of LHQ‐3 and LINGHUA Language Background Survey detailed age of acquisition, frequency of use, self‐rated proficiency, and sociolinguistic context. These complementary instruments were selected to provide both quantitative pre and post‐morbid dominance indices (BLP) and qualitative developmental context (LHQ‐3 and LINGHUA Language Background Survey) necessary for comprehensive bilingual profiling in progressive aphasia. Consistent with the literature, we define L1 as the first‐acquired language and L2 as the subsequently acquired language. 26

Treatment design

A single‐subject, multiple baseline experimental design was adopted to assess the effect of language intervention on L.R., with a focus on within‐language gains and potential CLT.

The treatment lasted 5 weeks, with three 1 h sessions per week and daily home practice. Therapy was delivered in three stages: 2 weeks in Italian (L1), 2 weeks in English (L2), and a final review week in both languages to mitigate potential effects of temporal delay between the initial Italian baseline assessment and the subsequent post‐therapy evaluations. Sessions were provided remotely using Microsoft Teams by a bilingual speech‐language therapist qualified in both the United Kingdom and Italy (R.T.). Therapy was delivered in L1 first to facilitate comprehension of the therapy task.

Therapy followed an adapted LRT following the Lexical Retrieval Cascade Treatment protocol 27 , 28 including eight steps: (1) naming; (2) semantic self‐cue using semantic prompts; (3) orthographic cue (first syllable); (4) reading; (5) writing and repeating; (6) semantic plausibility judgment; (7) word recall; and (8) generalization tasks (i.e., sentence production using the item in context). Daily homework used a copy‐and‐recall procedure. 29

Items selected for treatment were tailored to the participant, who was asked to identify and take digital photographs of items that were difficult to name but functionally relevant (e.g., related to their home life, work, hobbies). In order to select the therapy items, 155 functional items (selected by the participant and his partner) were probed twice, in each language at baseline, for naming accuracy. Items that were unnamed on at least one of two pre‐treatment probes were eligible for treatment. Stock photos of common objects were used to supplement participant‐provided items, if needed. From this pool, 40 items were selected and randomly divided to form four sets of five words in each language: two treated sets (n = 10 items in Italian; n = 10 items in English) and two untreated sets (n = 10 items in Italian; n = 10 items in English). All sets were matched for several linguistic characteristics (i.e., word frequency, length, syllable count, and semantic category such as food, hygiene, clothing). When items were assigned at each set (i.e., two sets treated in Italian, two sets treated in English, two sets untreated in Italian, two sets untreated in English), we selected different items for the two languages to evaluate potential CLT effects. Each therapy set was treated for 1 week, so items were treated during three sessions. The untreated items were probed one per week at the start of the therapy session, with the clinician not providing any cues or feedback during probing.

Statistical analysis

The bilingual experience was derived from the scoring of trans‐adapted LHQ3/LINGHUA Language Background Survey and BLP questionnaires. To fully characterize the bilingual profile, we also compared baseline speech‐language performance at formal testing in Italian and English using a nonparametric unpaired permutation test, with 10,000 Monte Carlo permutations.

To evaluate whether treatment was effective within and across the two languages, we evaluated the number of correctly named treated and untreated items post‐therapy relative to baseline in both languages. Effects were quantified using Percentage of Non‐Overlapping Data (PND) and d2 effect sizes due to limited data variability. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 Conventionally, PND >90 is highly effective, <50 is ineffective; and d2 values of 2.6, 3.9, and 5.8 indicate small, medium, and large effects, respectively. CLT was defined as any improvement obtained at the naming task for treated items in the untreated language (e.g., training “burro di arachidi” in Italian, leading to the correct naming of “peanut butter” in English). We also investigated pre–post changes in Italian and English across formal speech‐language testing, performing a paired permutation test (10,000 iterations).

To assess generalization effects (i.e., the patient's lexical retrieval ability in non‐naming tasks), two independent raters extracted and qualitatively compared connected‐speech measures of lexical density (ratio of nouns/verbs to total words) and syntactic complexity (subordinate clauses per utterance) 34 , 35 pre‐ and post‐treatment. Finally, COAST scores were compared directly before and after therapy to assess self‐perceived changes in functional communication.

RESULTS

Bilingual language background

L.R. grew up in an Italian–Japanese family, exposed to Italian (L1) from birth, and English (L2) from age 4. Italian predominated in family and informal contexts, whereas English was used primarily in school and work. In adulthood, he acquired oral fluency in French and passive knowledge of Japanese without literacy. At the time that this study was conducted, L.R. did not report any use of French or Japanese. Pre‐morbidly, Italian and English oral proficiency was relatively balanced, with stronger literacy in Italian. At the time of assessment, L.R.’s spoken and written proficiency in L1 and L2 appeared relatively balanced. Specifically, L.R. showed comparable writing proficiency across both languages, although this was lower than his oral proficiency. This pattern should be interpreted in light of his general preference for oral over written production, as he perceived written production as more complex and effortful, regardless of language. After symptom onset, English (L2) use declined, with Italian remaining the dominant and less impaired language (Table 1).

Speech and language characterization

At baseline, L.R. exhibited deficits in line with lvPPA. Naming and sentence repetition were predominantly impaired, with English (L2) naming (p = 0.038) and reading (p < 0.001) performance worse than those in Italian (L1). The patient also showed simplified sentence structure and argument‐structure errors, especially in L2 (p = 0.044). Connected speech showed reduced lexical density in L2 compared to L1, with contextual comprehension relatively preserved across languages.

Treatment

Therapy produced item‐specific gains in both Italian and English. PND values indicated medium‐to‐large effect sizes (Italian d 2 = 5.0; English d 2 = 5.6) (see Figure 3A). Untreated items showed no improvement (PND = 0–50). CLT was asymmetric: items treated in English improved when probed in Italian (PND = 100; d 2 = 3.0), but no transfer occurred from Italian to English (PND = 0) (See Figure 3B). Overall performance was maintained post‐treatment. Within‐language comparisons showed a significant gain in English argument structure production (Northwestern Assessment of Verbs and Sentence [NAVS]; p = 0.008). Despite these improvements, English syntactic abilities showed an overall tendency toward decline (Table 2). Between‐language comparisons revealed significantly lower scores in English than Italian for reading (p = 0.005) and morphosyntactic tasks, including NAVS sentence production priming, Northwestern Anagram Test (NAT), and syntax comprehension (all p’s < 0.05), whereas similar performance was achieved in post‐treatment assessment in naming task from SAND and MLSE batteries (Table 3). Qualitatively, at baseline, connected speech was more preserved in Italian than in English for lexical density (35.17 and 14.18, respectively). After therapy, English showed broader improvements, with lexical density nearly doubling (from 14.18 to 30.21) and verb production increasing in both languages (from 32 to 37 in Italian, from 21 to 48 in English). Syntactic complexity diverged: it increased in Italian (greater use of subordinate clauses, scoring from 0.69 to 1) but decreased in English (from 1.36 to 0.64). Finally, COAST scores improved from 62.5% to 73.3%, indicating a modest but subjectively meaningful functional benefit in the following areas: speaking with a familiar interlocutor, a reduction of the impact of language difficulties on family life, social life, and hobbies.

DISCUSSION

This single‐case study shows that an extensive assessment of bilingual experience was crucial, as it allowed us to interpret age at acquisition, dominance, and language use as factors potentially shaping patterns of language maintenance and decline in a bilingual individual with lvPPA. Moreover, we found that a structured LRT can improve naming of treated items in both languages. Notably, greater gains were observed in L1 because of the cross‐linguistic transfer resulting from treatment of L2. The naming improvement came in the context of a stable speech‐language profile in both languages. With respect to generalization to other linguistic tasks, we found a trend toward improvement in the syntactic abilities measured with NAT in L1, although it did not reach statistical significance and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. A significant gain in English (L2) argument structure production (NAVS) was also observed, suggesting that the inclusion of a sentence generation task in the LRT cascade led to a positive effect on syntactic abilities. With respect to the connected speech task, L.R.'s lexical density improved, with a greater number of nouns produced in L2 and of verbs retrieved in both languages. In functional communication, measured by the COAST scale, L.R. reported a small but personally significant improvement in interaction with familiar speakers and in social participation.

Before treatment, L.R. was more impaired in English (L2) than Italian (L1), which echoes previous reports of faster decline in the less dominant, second‐learned language. 9 This pattern cannot be explained by age at acquisition alone: his premorbid dominance, patterns of use, and stronger literacy in Italian appear to have offered greater protection for L1. The drop in his confidence and use of English after symptom onset may have further accelerated L2 decline. These observations align with the Adaptive Control Hypothesis (ACH), 36 which posits that bilingual individuals recruit domain‐general cognitive control networks, including prefrontal, anterior cingulate, and basal ganglia circuits, to manage processes of language selection, inhibition, and switching. Neurodegeneration may differentially affect these networks, reducing the efficiency of control and thereby increasing the likelihood that the less‐practiced or less‐dominant language becomes more vulnerable to decline. 37 In L.R.'s case, this hypothesis may offer a theoretical interpretation for both the steeper post‐morbid decline in English and the asymmetric transfer, with the weaker language driving maintenance in the stronger one. Indeed, we could not draw a conclusion regarding the observed asymmetries, as no direct measures of executive control, language switching, or neural control networks were collected.

LRT led to substantial item‐specific gains in both languages and CLT from English to Italian. The direction of transfer is noteworthy. Treated and untreated items were matched for baseline naming accuracy within each language, ensuring that the observed CLT effects cannot be attributed to pre‐treatment differences between stimulus sets or to floor/ceiling effects at the item level. This matching allowed us to examine treatment‐related change while holding initial naming performance constant. At the bilingualism profile level, however, the patient showed an asymmetrical bilingual profile at baseline, with greater overall impairment in English than in Italian. Such differences in language dominance and impairment may still modulate the magnitude and directionality of cross‐linguistic transfer, even when item‐level accuracy is controlled, by constraining which lexical representations remain sufficiently accessible to support generalization. The observed pattern of transfer is consistent with the Revised Hierarchical Model (RHM), which predicts asymmetric routes of lexical access in unbalanced bilinguals. In healthy bilinguals, retrieval in the less‐dominant language (L2) relies more strongly on lexical links to the dominant language (L1), making L2→L1 transfer more likely than the reverse. Taken together, we interpret the present CLT findings as reflecting an interaction between treatment effects and the patient's unbalanced bilingual profile. As most bilinguals are not balanced, this case may be broadly representative of bilingual language organization, while also highlighting the importance of accounting for baseline language dominance and impairment when interpreting CLT in treatment studies.

The RHM, 38 often used to explain CLT in bilingual speakers, predicted stronger influences of L1 on L2. However, studies on bilingual healthy populations revealed that lexical retrieval tasks activate the same semantic networks across languages. When bilinguals named items, they presented with a similar degree of semantic interference in L1 and L2, suggesting that semantic control abilities are independent of the language being utilized. 39 Accordingly, evidence from post‐stroke aphasia 40 and PPA literature 7 also highlighted greater CLT from L2 to L1, suggesting that shared semantic representations can facilitate improvement even when the treated language is comparatively less dominant. This is in line with dual‐language intervention studies evidencing that targeting the weaker language seems to benefit both L1 and L2, supporting broader communication gains. 7 , 41 Our results align with the latter findings, suggesting a benefit in training L2 and L1, leading to greater gains in L1 by activating the same shared semantic network. This is further confirmed by a recent meta‐analysis by Lee and Faroqi‐Shah from 2024, 41 which reported medium‐to‐large effect sizes for dual‐language anomia treatment in aphasia. Our d2 values (≈5.0–5.6) fall squarely in that range, which adds weight to these findings despite the single‐case design.

In addition, existing literature suggests that the asymmetry of transfer may be influenced by other factors, including which language is treated and in what order, the relative pre‐ and post‐morbid dominance and proficiency of the two languages, and how frequently and confidently they are used. 12 , 42 , 43 In particular, transfer may depend on the specific neural mechanisms engaged by therapy in L1: therapeutic intervention should recruit networks also shared by L2 and could more likely produce generalized gains across languages. Consistent with the hypothesis that ease of translation may be facilitated from the weaker to stronger language, in L.R.'s case, therapy delivered in English may have elicited language‑processing substrates that substantially overlap with those supporting Italian, yielding asymmetric transfer independent of treatment order. Clarifying the relative contributions of treatment order versus shared neural substrates will require studies that systematically manipulate treatment language and timing and incorporate longitudinal neuroimaging or other mechanistic measures. Moreover, in PPA, differential patterns of language decline can shape confidence and frequency of use in the more impaired language, which may in turn influence cross‐linguistic treatment effects. 7 Accordingly, in the present case, L.R.'s dominance and frequency of use for L2 presented a slight imbalance compared to L1 pre‐morbidly and a significant reduction of frequency, dominance, confidence, and use post‐morbidly. This might have contributed to the lack of transfer from L1 to L2.

Changes in connected speech mirrored the main therapy results: lexical density qualitatively improved in both languages, with particularly striking gains in English. Syntax followed a different trajectory, showing improvement in Italian but declining in English, which likely reflects the combined effects of disease progression and the item‐specific focus of therapy. Improvements on argument‐structure tasks support this interpretation, as they were targeted indirectly during generalization activities. Healthy controls typically produce picture descriptions that are considerably more content rich and syntactically more complex than those of individuals with aphasia. 44 , 45 Against this backdrop, L.R.’s English lexical density improved from ≈14% at baseline to ≈30% post‐therapy, approaching the level typically seen in healthy discourse. Although direct comparisons for Italian should be interpreted with caution, given the lack of Italian‐specific normative data for the “Cat Rescue” connected speech task. Beyond behavior, there may also be a neurobiological explanation. Luk et al. (2011) 46 showed that lifelong bilingualism helps maintain white matter integrity in older adults. This preservation may underlie the resilience we observed in L.R.’s connected speech, particularly when coupled with the modest but meaningful boost in self‐reported communication. Taken together, these results suggest that bilingual experience might support language performance, both through behavioral strategies and through underlying neuroprotective mechanisms. However, this single‐case study cannot establish causal neuroprotective effects; we therefore present this link as a hypothesis‐generating possibility that warrants testing in larger, longitudinal, or mechanistic studies. In addition, these results provide candidate measures derived from connected speech that may prove useful in detecting treatment response in the study of PPA treatment more broadly.

Limitations include small treatment set, and binary scoring limited our analyses' variability and statistical power. A follow‐up assessment was not possible in this case study; in future investigations, inclusion of observations over time would allow us to evaluate the maintenance of treatment‐related improvements. In addition, not all assessments were fully matched across languages. Although this was a pragmatic choice given the lack of equivalent Italian–English tools, international guidelines on bilingual practice 47 stress the importance of matched assessments and cultural competence, pointing to an area for future improvement in the field more generally.

It is also worth situating this report within the broader field. A recent scoping review of bilingual PPA 6 emphasized the limited systematic evidence on therapy outcomes in this population. This is further compounded by several barriers in the Italian context, where accessing SLT services is challenged by long waiting times, reduced awareness, and limited experience of the SLT role in managing people with PPA, and insufficient funding in providing proactive and long‐term services. 48 By providing detailed assessment and intervention data, our study contributes to filling this gap. It underscores the need for larger, multisite studies that can explore therapy responsiveness across different bilingual profiles. Incorporating literacy and neuroimaging measures that correlate with therapy's effects will also be necessary.

To conclude, this case study illustrates how the bilingual experience shapes not only the trajectory of language decline in PPA but also the effectiveness of therapy. In L.R., Italian was preserved longer, likely supported by greater dominance, literacy, and use, whereas English declined steeply yet provided the entry point for cross‐language transfer. These findings extend earlier work showing that lifelong bilingualism contributes to cognitive reserve and shapes cognitive profile in the case of neurodegenerative onset. 49 , 50 They are also consistent with the ACH, 36 which frames neurodegeneration as a disruption of language‐control networks. Integrating these perspectives makes it clear that bilingual profiles must be considered when planning therapy. For aging bilingual populations, cross‐linguistic treatment approaches and bilingual service provision are not optional but essential to ensure equitable and effective care.
