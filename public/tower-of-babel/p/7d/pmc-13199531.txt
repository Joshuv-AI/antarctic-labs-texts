Sex determining region Y box protein 2 (SOX2) expression, alteration, regulation and its diagnostics, prognostics and therapeutics in lung cancer
Authors: Xia Jiang, Meiling Zheng, Lianmei Zhang, Dongmei Xu, Jingliang Cheng, Haoyue Deng, Chunli Wei, Junping Xian, Fangshuo Xu, Mazaher Maghsoudloo, Dabing Li, Wenzhe Ma, Junjiang Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in China and worldwide. Sex determining region Y box protein 2 (SOX2) is involved in pluripotency regulation in embryonic stem cells, morphogenesis, and homeostasis of tracheobronchial epithelia, as well as cancer. Given its oncogenic potential, this study aimed to investigate the potential of SOX2 as a biomarker and evaluate the inhibitory effects of cordycepin (CD), a natural compound, on SOX2-driven oncogenic phenotypes in lung cancer. In this study, by analyzing TCGA and UALCAN datasets together with Chinese patient samples, we found that SOX2 was highly expressed at both the mRNA and protein levels in LUSC. In LUAD, SOX2 mRNA levels were unchanged, whereas SOX2 protein levels were decreased. High SOX2 expression was significantly associated with poorer patient survival (including OS, FPS, DSS, and DFS), regardless of whether it was mutated or not. SOX2 alterations were mainly amplifications in cancers, including that LUSC is the highest (40.04% in all cases), and in LUAD is only the 14th highest (2.58% in all cases) among all cancers, implying that LUSC SOX2 showed high gene amplification that could affect gene expression. Moreover, overexpression of SOX2 promoted lung cancer cell growth, migration, and invasion, while CD, a natural product originally from the traditional Chinese medicine Cordyceps sinensis (BerK.), suppressed SOX2-mediated progression, including the growth, migration, and invasion of lung cancer. Taken together, these results implied that SOX2 is a potential biomarker for diagnostics, prognostics, and therapeutics for lung cancer, including LUAD and LUSC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04928-y.

Introduction

Lung cancer represents the most significant cause of cancer-related mortality on a global scale, with an estimated 2 million new cases diagnosed annually and 1.76 million deaths attributed to the disease each year. Lung cancer is known as the king of cancer and is one of the most commonly diagnosed cancers in the world. In China, it is estimated that approximately 4,824,700 new cancer cases and 2,572,200 new deaths occurred in 2022, while lung cancer ranked first in morbidity and mortality in both men and women [1]. Lung cancer is usually classified into two major subtypes, small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). The latter can be subdivided into lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and large cell carcinoma (LCC) [2, 3]. In the United States, LUAD accounts for 38% of all lung cancers, followed by LUSC (20%) and SCLC (14%) [2], while in China, LUAD accounts for over half of all lung cancers (53.40%), followed by LUSC (30.24%) and SCLC (11.72%) [4]. The incidence of LUAD and LUSC in China is higher than that in the United States. It is important to accurately predict lung cancer prognosis and guide the selection of targeted therapies [5].

Sex determining region Y box protein 2 (SRY-BOX 2, SOX2) (OMIM: 184429) aliases ANOP3 (anophthalmia syndrome 3) or MCOPS3 (microphthalmia syndrome 3). SOX2 is located on chromosome 3q26.33, encodes 317 amino acids, and predicts a molecular weight of 34,310 Da (Protein Accession: P48431) (NM_003106.4 in mRNA and NP_003097.1 in protein). The SOX2 gene lies within an intron of another gene, named SOX2 overlapping transcript (SOX2OT), an RNA gene affiliated with the lncRNA family. Functionally, SOX2 is involved in pluripotency regulation in embryonic stem cells and the morphogenesis and homeostasis of tracheobronchial epithelia [6]. SOX2 protein, as a transcription factor, contains a particular high-mobility group domain that facilitates its capacity to participate in a broad variety of physiological functions by activating genes involved in the processes of pre- and post-embryonic development, adult tissue homeostasis, and lineage specifications. Aberrant SOX2 DNA amplification and protein expression have been noticed in various types of tumors. SOX2 facilitates the oncogenic phenotype via cellular proliferation and enhancement of invasive tumor properties; thus, SOX2 is responsible for cellular proliferation, tumor initiation/invasion/migration/chemoresistance, self-renewal, and maintenance in cancer stem cell populations, such as lung tumorigenesis and esophageal squamous cell carcinomas [7–9]. Although DNA amplification and protein expression of SOX2 in tumor cells are associated with clinicopathological features and prognosis in lung cancers, the results are not always consistent [10, 11]. For example, a meta-analysis in 2013 summarized the associations in NSCLC, but the included studies were relatively rare, and the authors did not distinguish SOX2 amplification, mRNA, and protein expression, and its regulation [11, 12]. Niharika et al. [13] recently identified genes SOX2 and EZH2 as being involved in large cohorts of patients in LUAD by a meta-analysis of mRNA expression from TCGA and GTEx datasets, and they also found that SOX2 is the only gene exhibiting higher expression from a total of 795 differentially expressed genes (DEGs). High SOX2 expression in lung cancer tumors showed more resistance to ferroptosis, and expression of SLC7A11, a key component of the Cystine transporter and ferroptosis inhibitor [14], was positively correlated with SOX2 expression [15].

There is accumulating evidence in favor of three-dimensional (higher-order) folding of chromatin and epigenetic control of the SOX2 gene by chromatin modifications, implying epigenetic regulatory mechanisms, specifically, via DNA methylation and histone H3 modification [16]. Moreover, there is growing evidence that SOX2 mediates resistance towards established cancer therapies and that it is expressed in cancer stem cells, which indicates that studying the SOX2 expression and role in tumor development and cancer progression could lead to the development of new diagnostics, prognostics, or therapeutic options [17]. In view of this, its potential as a marker for diagnostics, prognostics, and therapeutics in lung cancer needs to be studied.

Cordycepin (3′-deoxyadenosine, CD), a bioactive compound derived from the medicinal fungus Cordyceps militaris, has garnered significant attention for its broad-spectrum pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and notably, antitumor effects [18]. In the context of lung cancer, emerging evidence suggests that CD suppresses proliferation, induces apoptosis, and inhibits metastasis through mechanisms involving the regulation of key oncogenic pathways such as PI3K/AKT, MAPK/ERK, and Wnt/β-catenin [19]. However, the precise molecular targets of CD, particularly in relation to SOX2 - a critical transcription factor driving lung cancer stemness and progression - remain underexplored. Investigating cordycepin’s impact on SOX2 expression, alteration, or regulatory networks could unveil novel diagnostic, prognostic, or therapeutic strategies for lung cancer.

In the present study, we performed a comprehensive analysis to investigate the SOX2 expression, alteration, and regulation by small molecules, notably CD, in lung cancer, particularly in NSCLC, mainly in LUAD and LUSC.

Materials and methods

Data analysis

We utilized the TCGAplot R package [20] to assess the pan-cancer expression profile of SOX2 across various cancer types within The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project, accessed via the NCI Genomic Data Commons (GDC) Data Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Furthermore, we generated box plots to visualize the expression levels of SOX2 specifically in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). We also compared SOX2 expression across different pathological stages within these two lung cancer subtypes. In addition, to evaluate the diagnostic potential of SOX2 in distinguishing between tumor and normal tissues in LUAD and LUSC, we conducted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and calculated the area under the curve (AUC). Additional analyses were performed using UALCAN (The University of Alabama at Birmingham Cancer Data Analysis Portal; https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/). Survival, including overall survival (OS, time from randomization to death from any cause), first-progression survival (FPS, time to first disease progression), and post-progression survival (PPS, time from first progression to death) data stratified by SOX2 expression were retrieved from the Kaplan-Meier Plotter(KM-Plotter) database (https://kmplot.com/analysis/). Somatic alteration frequency and survival, including OS, disease-specific survival (DSS, time to death specifically from the disease under study), disease-free survival (DFS, time to recurrence, new primary tumor, or any death following curative therapy), and progression-free survival (PFS, time to disease progression or any death) were obtained via the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (cBioPortal) (https://www.cbioportal.org/).

Sample collection, cellular protein extraction for lung cancer

Sample collection for Chinese patients with lung cancer was performed on carcinoma tissues and their corresponding healthy tissues. Lung cancer in both LUAD and LUSC was diagnosed by two independent pathologists. Tissue samples were lysed with ice-cold 1x EBC buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 125 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, and protease inhibitors) and 2xSDS loading buffer [21, 22]. The proteins were stored at -20 °C until use.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

The aforementioned sample tissues were paraffin-embedded and used for IHC staining analysis to assess the SOX2 protein localization, following the standard immunoperoxidase staining procedure [21]. Tissue sections were incubated with SOX2 antibody (1:100 dilution, Cat No.: 11064-1-AP, Proteintech Group, Inc., Rosemont, IL 60018, USA) at 4 °C overnight and a secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. Once the sections were prepared, the slides were viewed, and photos were taken by light microscopy.

Cell culture and treatments

The lung cancer cells (H460, H1299, NCI-H2170, and NCI-H1703) (cells were purchased from BDBIO, Zhejiang, China) were cultured as previously described in RPMI-1640 or DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and antibiotics at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 [23]. After cell density reached 50 ~ 60% confluency, they were treated with different concentrations of cordycepin (CD) (0, 10, 20, and 40 µM) for 24 h. Then the cells were washed, cellular proteins lysed, and extracted with the aforementioned ice-cold 1 × EBC buffer.

Cell transfection and stable cell lines

Human SOX2 was subcloned into the pcDNA5-2XFlag-AmpR plasmid vector (constructed by MiaoLing Biology, Shanghai, China). The SOX2 plasmid DNA was used to overexpress the SOX2 gene in the SK-MES-1 and NCI-H1703 cells (cells were purchased from BDBIO, Zhejiang, China). For transfection, cells were transfected with 400 ng of SOX2 plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 8 h of transfection at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 cell culture incubator, the fresh medium with 10% FBS was replaced. Cells were harvested 24 h post-transfection for subsequent analysis.

SOX2-targeting shRNA (Target sequences: Sh1-SOX2 CGAGATAAACATGGCAATCAA; Sh2-SOX2 CTGCCGAGAATCCATGTATAT; Sh-GFP ACAACAGCCACAACGTCTATA.)

Plasmids were co-transfected with viral packaging plasmids into HEK293T cells using PEI transfection reagent (Cat No.: HY-K2014, MCE, MedChemExpress, USA). The collected viral supernatant and 8 µg/mL polybrene (Cat No.:C0351-1, Beyotime, China) were added to the culture medium of NCI-H1703 cells. Infected cancer cells with 1 µg/ mL puromycin (P8833, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 2 weeks were selected to establish stable knockdown SOX2 cell lines.

Western blotting

Approximately 50–60 µg of proteins were separated by gel electrophoresis in an 8% SDS-PAGE at 90 ~ 130 V, transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane, and blocked with 5% fat-free milk. Then the PVDF membrane was incubated with the aforementioned SOX2 antibody (1:2500 dilution) (Cat No.: 11064-1-AP, Proteintech Group, Inc., Rosemont, IL 60018, USA) and DYKDDDDK tag Monoclonal antibody (1:5000 dilution) (Cat No.: 66008-4-Ig, Proteintech Group, Inc., Rosemont, IL 60018, USA) at 4 °C overnight. The β-actin (1:5000) (Cat No.: 66009-1-Ig, Proteintech Group, Inc., Rosemont, IL 60018, USA) or Tubulin (Cat No.: 10094-1-AP, Proteintech Group, Inc., Rosemont, IL 60018, USA) (1:5000) was employed as a control for normalization, respectively. After the secondary antibodies were incubated and washed, an imaging scanner was applied to monitor the signals on each membrane strip. The western blotting results were monitored with a substrate using the BIO-RAD chemiluminescence detection system, and protein levels (how intense the stain is) were evaluated through densitometry by normalizing to internal control β-actin or Tubulin.

Semi-quantitative RT-PCR

RNA extraction from drug-treated cells was executed according to our previously reported standard protocols, and the semi-quantitative RT-PCR assays were performed [23, 24]. The RT-PCR primers were used: RT-SOX2-L, 5′-agcatggagaaaacccggta-3′; and RT-SOX2-R, 5′-cctgcaaagctcctaccgta-3′. Primers were designed based on the human SOX2 reference mRNA sequence (NCBI RefSeq; NM_003106.4).The PCR product was 302 bp in length. Or SOX2-ChIP L, 5’-ccatccacactcacgcaaaa-3’, with primer RT-SOX2-R for a 207 bp PCR product. The ACTB gene was employed as a control for normalization. All experiments were repeated three times.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well and cultured for 48 h for the OE-SOX2 group. For the KD-SOX2 group, cell proliferation was assessed at multiple time points (24, 48, 72, and 96 h). Subsequently, 10 µL of the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (Cat NO.: K1018, APE x BIO, USA) was added to each well. After incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, the optical density (OD) was determined at 450 nm to assess cell proliferation.

Cell migration and invasion assay

Cell migration and invasion assays were performed using transwell inserts (REF 3422, Corning Inc., Corning, USA). For the migration assay, 5 × 10⁴ cells in 200 µL of Opti-MEM were seeded into the upper chamber (8 μm pore size), while 600 µL of medium containing 20% FBS was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation, the migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min, and the non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed by scraping. The migrated cells were then counted under an inverted phase-contrast microscope (×10 magnification). Three randomly selected fields were analyzed per sample, and the average number of cells per field was calculated. For the invasion assay, 5 × 10⁴ cells were seeded onto Matrigel-coated transwell inserts (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, USA) in Opti-MEM, followed by the same treatment protocol as in the migration assay.

SOX2 protein stability assay

To assess the half-life of the SOX2 protein, a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay was performed. NCI-H2170 lung cancer cell lines were treated with CHX (20 µg/mL) in the presence or absence of the indicated concentrations of CD. Cells were harvested at the designated time points (0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h) following CHX treatment. Subsequently, whole-cell lysates were subjected to Western blot analysis as described above to monitor the degradation kinetics of the SOX2 protein.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed by SPSS 20 (Statistics v20, https://www.ibm.com/support/pages/downloading-ibm-spss-statistics-20) and GraphPad Prism 9 [Version 9.0.0 (86)] (https://www.graphpad.com/). Pairwise comparisons of normally distributed data were analyzed by Student’s t-test, or for multi-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SOX2 is upregulated in lung cancer

We first compared SOX2 mRNA levels in pan-cancer and found that SOX2 mRNA expression is downregulated in two cancer types (Fig. 1A) and upregulated in five cancer types, including LUAD and LUSC (Fig. 1A&B), when compared with the matched normal tissues. The expression level of SOX2 in tumor tissues was significantly higher than the corresponding control tissues using the TCGA dataset for the types of LUAD and LUSC (P < 0.01). In addition, SOX2 only has one isoform consisting of 317 amino acid residues, which contains domains of HMG_box and SOXp (Fig. 1C).

Further comparison of Chinese lung cancers found that SOX2 protein expression decreased in LUAD and was upregulated in LUSC (Fig. 2B&D) compared with the matched normal tissues. For details, in LUAD, SOX2 protein is upregulated in 3 samples (30%), decreased in 5 samples (50%), and unchanged in 2 samples (20%) out of a total of 10 samples (Fig. 2B). In LUSC, SOX2 protein is upregulated in 8 samples (80%) and unchanged in 2 samples (20%) from a total of 10 samples (Fig. 2D). No tumor tissues were found to be decreased in LUSC.

IHC results showed that SOX2 localized to both the nucleus and cytosol, mainly in the cytosol, in LUAD cancer tissue cells (Fig. 3A), while SOX2 localized to both the nucleus and cytosol, mainly in the nucleus, in LUSC cancer tissue cells (Fig. 3D). In the matched normal tissues, SOX2 staining was markedly weaker or undetectable in LUAD (Fig. 3C) and LUSC (Fig. 3F) compared with the corresponding tumor tissues. Altogether, these results implied that SOX2 is a potential diagnostic marker for lung cancer, including LUAD and LUSC, particularly in LUSC.

SOX2 high expression in lung cancer correlated with short survival

Consistent with the notion that SOX2 exerts oncogenic functions, we further investigated lung cancer survival in relation to SOX2 expression. The results are shown in Fig. 4, which shows that high SOX2 expression in lung cancer is significantly correlated with short survival in OS (Fig. 4A-D) and FPS (Fig. 4E-H) in all 4 available arrays (Affy IDs: 213721_at, 213722_at, 214178_s_at, and 228038_at). In PPS, high SOX2 expression in lung cancer is significantly correlated with short survival in the arrays (Affy ID: 213722_at and 228038_at), but no significant change in the other 2 arrays (Supplementary Fig. 1). Taken together, these results implied that SOX2 may be a potential prognosis marker (unfavored marker), mainly in OS and FPS, for lung cancer, including LUAD and LUSC. Though the heterogeneous SOX2 protein expression observed in our LUAD cohort patient indicates complex regulation, the strong correlation between high SOX2 levels and poor survival in the overall lung cancer analysis underscores its role as a critical oncogenic driver in a significant subset of tumors.

Germline mutations in the SOX2 gene have been associated with optic nerve hypoplasia, syndromic microphthalmia, or anophthalmia (OMIM: 206900) [25, 26]. Aberrant SOX2 DNA amplification and protein expression in tumor cells have been reported in various types of tumors, and SOX2 facilitates the oncogenic phenotype [7, 8]. We first verified the amplifications by alterations from GISTIC (genomic identification of significant targets in cancer, https://broadinstitute.g.ithub.io/gistic2/) in the TCGA database in SOX2 of 32 cancers, and these amplifications were found to potentially affect SOX2 mRNA expression (Fig. 5A). And it was reported that LUSC exhibited the highest alteration rate (40.04%, n = 487), predominantly amplifications (39.43%) across 26 cancer types. In contrast, LUAD ranked 14th (2.58%, n = 566), with amplifications (2.31%) exceeding alterations (0.28%) [27]. We further compared SOX2 expression across different pathological stages within two lung cancer subtypes. And found that there is no significant difference in SOX2 expression across pathological stages in LUAD and LUSC (Fig. 5B). In addition, we evaluated the diagnostic potential of SOX2 in distinguishing between tumor and normal tissues in LUAD and LUSC, and the AUC is 0.928 in LUSC, as shown in the ROC curve (Fig. 5C), suggesting SOX2 as a potential diagnostic biomarker in LUSC.

The survival for SOX2 alterations

Then, the survival for SOX2 alterations in cancer survival data from cBioPortal, and the results are shown in Fig. 5D-F; Table 1. We found that cancer patients with SOX2 alterations had significantly shorter overall survival (Fig. 5D; P = 7.81E-06), disease-specific survival (Fig. 5E; P = 3.69E-03), and disease-free survival (Fig. 5F; P = 0.022). These results implied that SOX2 may be a potential prognostic marker (unfavorable marker) in cancers, including lung cancer, most likely in LUSC. Because SOX2 alterations in LUSC showed the highest or dominant frequency at 40.04%, more than two-fold higher than that of the second-highest, ovarian cancer (OV) (17.98%) (Data not shown).

SOX2 modulates tumor growth, migration, and invasion in lung cancer

To investigate the role of SOX2 in lung cancer progression, we performed both gain-of-function and loss-of-function studies. For the gain-of-function assay, we constructed a SOX2 overexpression plasmid by subcloning the human SOX2 gene into the pcDNA5-2XFlag-AmpR vector and transfected the SOX2 plasmid into the SK-MES-1 and NCI-H1703 lung cancer cell lines. Our results demonstrated that SOX2 overexpression (OE-SOX2) significantly enhanced tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and migration in both cell lines, SK-MES-1 and NCI-H1703 (Fig. 6A-G). Conversely, in the loss-of-function assay, we knocked down SOX2 (KD-SOX2) in the NCI-H1703 cell lines, which was confirmed by Western blot analysis (Fig. 6H). The KD-SOX2 led to a significant suppression of cell proliferation (Fig. 6I), as well as markedly impaired capabilities of migration and invasion (Fig. 6J, K). Collectively, these complementary findings from both OE-SOX2 and KD-SOX2 experiments strongly suggest that SOX2 plays a critical modulating role in promoting lung cancer progression, supporting its potential as a therapeutic target in lung cancer treatment.

Small molecule CD suppresses SOX2-mediated progression in lung cancer cells

Small molecule CD, a natural product from traditional Chinese medicine Ophiocordyceps sinensis or Cordyceps sinensis (BerK.), was used to treat lung cancer cell lines, and the results are shown in Fig. 7. We found that treatment of H460, H1299, NCI-H2170, and NCI-H1703 lung cancer cell lines with CD resulted in a significant downregulation of SOX2 protein levels (Fig. 7A and D).

Intriguingly, while SOX2 protein expression was markedly suppressed, no corresponding reduction in SOX2 mRNA levels was observed (Fig. 7E and H), suggesting that CD regulates SOX2 primarily at the post-transcriptional or post-translational level, rather than through transcriptional repression. Further functional assays revealed that CD treatment effectively inhibited tumor invasion and metastasis (Fig. 7I, J).

CD promotes the degradation of SOX2 protein

To determine whether the reduction in SOX2 protein was due to translational inhibition or enhanced degradation, we performed a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay. NCI-H2170 cells were treated with CHX (20 µg/mL) with or without CD, and the SOX2 protein levels were analyzed by Western blot. As shown in Fig. 8A, the decay of SOX2 was significantly accelerated in CD-treated cells compared to the control. Quantitative analysis revealed that CD treatment shortened the half-life of SOX2 from > 6 h (approximately 6.5) to less than 2 h (approximately 1.5) in CHX-treated cell lines (Fig. 8B), demonstrating that CD facilitates the post-translational degradation of SOX2.

CD antagonizes oncogenic phenotypes driven by ectopic SOX2 expression

To further validate the specificity of CD’s action on SOX2 signaling, we performed rescue experiments by overexpressing SOX2 in two lung cancer cell lines. While SOX2 overexpression promoted tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, concomitant treatment with CD significantly suppressed these SOX2-driven oncogenic phenotypes (Fig. 9). The comparison between the OE-SOX2 and OE-SOX2 + CD groups reveals that CD effectively antagonizes the oncogenic functions of SOX2. These findings highlight CD as a promising therapeutic agent capable of disrupting SOX2-mediated oncogenic signaling in lung cancer. Given the critical role of SOX2 in tumor progression, our study provides a rationale for the development of CD or its derivatives as novel SOX2-targeting therapies for lung cancer treatment.

Discussion

In the present study, we found that SOX2 is highly expressed at both mRNA and protein levels by analyzing datasets of TCGA and UALCAN and Chinese patient samples for LUSC when compared with tumor tissues with the matched normal tissues. Although there is an increase in mRNA, no change in the protein level of SOX2 when analyzing datasets of TCGA and UALCAN in LUAD. In Chinese patient samples for LUAD analyzed by Western blotting, SOX2 protein may be decreased in general, indicating that only 3 samples are upregulated (30%), but 5 samples are decreased (50%), and 2 samples are unchanged (20%) out of a total of 10 samples, which is consistent with Onaitis MW et al. [28] reported that SOX2 is overexpressed in LUSC tumors but is inconsistent with overexpression in some adenocarcinomas by IHC. Yuan et al. [29] also reported that SOX2 protein expression was significantly higher in LUSC compared to LUAD by IHC. These data imply oncogenically important roles for SOX2 in LUSC. But more human samples may need to confirm the results for LUAD, whether SOX2 is truly decreased, since we collected more than twenty pairs of LUAD samples and almost half of them is undetectable by Western blotting (Data not shown). SOX2 high expression correlated with shorter survivals, such as OS and FPS, or OS, disease-specific, and disease-free, regardless of whether SOX2 was mutated in lung cancer. But the survival data is inconsistent with Lu et al. [28] reported that SOX2 is a prognostic marker of better patient survival times. Our results implied that SOX2 is a potential biomarker for diagnostics and prognostics of worse patient survival for lung cancer, mainly in LUSC. However, it should be noted that the survival analysis following SOX2 alterations in this study is based on pan-cancer survival data from the public bioinformatics database cBioPortal. Although such bioinformatic analyses help reveal the potential role of SOX2 in tumorigenesis from a macroscopic perspective, the prognostic value of SOX2 may differ between LUAD and LUSC due to differences in their cellular origins and molecular characteristics, as the data source integrates samples from both subtypes. Therefore, our future research will mainly focus on cohorts with well-defined histology of LUSC, employing integrated analyses of multidimensional public databases, such as TCGA and GEO, complemented by experimental validation, to conduct a more comprehensive and reliable assessment of the prognostic significance of SOX2.

SOX2 is involved in pluripotency regulation in embryonic stem cells, the morphogenesis, and homeostasis of tracheobronchial epithelia [6], as well as in cancer development. It directly interacts with oncogenic signalling pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, which not only promote proliferation but also stabilize key elements of the EMT process [30, 31]. Our findings unequivocally demonstrate that SOX2 overexpression significantly enhances the proliferation, migration, and invasion of lung cancer cells, thus confirming its established role as a primary regulator of stemness and malignancy in carcinogenic processes [32]. Conversely, complementary loss-of-function experiments showed that SOX2 knockdown (KD-SOX2) substantially attenuated the proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities of lung cancer cells, thereby reinforcing the essential role of SOX2 in maintaining the malignant phenotype. SOX2 protein, as a transcription factor, contains a particular high-mobility group domain that enables the capacity to participate in a broad variety of physiological functions by activating genes involved in pre- and post-embryonic development, adult tissue homeostasis, and lineage specifications. Aberrant SOX2 DNA amplification and protein expression have been noticed in various tumors. DNA amplification might be associated with SOX2 protein expression and clinicopathological features in lung cancers. Mechanistically, the proliferative effects of SOX2 may stem from its role as a transcription factor, achieved by driving the expression of key cell cycle regulators. Furthermore, the regulation of SOX2 expression and its synergistic interactions with other mechanisms involve multiple pathways, such as the oxidative stress response/PI3K signalling pathway or ferroptosis. These synergistic interactions may collectively promote the invasive phenotype of tumours [15, 33, 34]. Therefore, the concordant results from both overexpression and knockdown studies provide compelling evidence that SOX2 actively modulates tumor growth and metastatic behaviors in lung cancer. DNA and RNA can adopt various secondary structures. G-quadruplex (G4) structures in DNA or RNA form through self-recognition of guanines, which are transcriptionally or translationally involved in gene expression [35]. Our bioinformatics analysis and preliminary data did find an existing G4 structure with a higher score in SOX2 (data not shown). Although our data suggest its potential regulatory role, the precise mechanisms by which this structure influences SOX2 expression, its interactions with transcription factors, and its functional contribution to the invasive phenotype of lung cancer remain to be elucidated. Despite these limitations, we contend that the discovery of this putative G4 structure opens promising avenues for elucidating the complex regulatory mechanisms of SOX2 in lung cancer development, which are worthy of further investigation.

Small molecule CD, a natural product from traditional Chinese medicine, ophiocordyceps sinensis or cordyceps sinensis [36]. CD has been reported as an anti-cancer component through targeting different biomarkers [37–40] and has been involved in inhibiting several oncogenes’ expression, including SOX2 in this study, in different cancer cell lines [21, 41, 42]. The concentration range of 10–40 µM used in this study was selected based on its potent efficacy against NSCLC cell lines and its highly favorable selective toxicity. This is demonstrated by our previous finding that the IC₅₀ of CD in normal CCD-19 lung cells exceeds 600 µM, which is significantly higher than the IC₅₀ for all tested NSCLC lines (below 100 µM) [37]. Although this range represents the effective in vitro concentration, its pharmacological relevance is further strengthened by literature showing that CD can achieve low micromolar plasma levels in vivo, with potential for enhancement via novel drug delivery formulations [43]. CD suppressed SOX2 expression, implying that SOX2 is a potential biomarker for therapeutics for lung cancer. The effect of CD on nucleic acid metabolism and nucleic acid-synthesizing enzyme systems was reported [44]. Since CD is similar to adenine deoxyribonucleoside (lacking oxygen above position 2 or in the 3’ position with a hydrogen), many enzymes cannot distinguish between the two. Therefore, CD can be involved in specific biochemical reactions, for example, CD incorporated into RNA molecules, leading to the premature termination of its synthesis [45, 46]. Our results demonstrated that CD post-translationally downregulates SOX2 protein without affecting its mRNA, and the cycloheximide chase assay confirmed that CD accelerates SOX2 protein degradation. However, the precise mechanism by which CD achieves this remains unclear, and other mechanisms may be involved in its expression [47]. The translational relevance and efficacy of CD in suppressing SOX2 in vivo warrant further investigation in animal models. Whether the SOX2 G4 structure is implicated or not, the connection between G4 formation and synthetic lethality highlights the potential of targeting G4s for cancer therapy. This suggests that G4 structures could be considered therapeutic targets for human diseases [35].

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that SOX2 plays a critical and distinct role in LUSC, underscoring its potential as a diagnostic biomarker and a therapeutic target specifically for this subtype. In contrast, the regulatory mechanisms and clinical relevance of SOX2 appear to differ in LUAD. The natural product CD, a bioactive compound originally derived from the traditional Chinese medicine Cordyceps sinensis, effectively suppresses SOX2 protein expression, and the precise mechanism by which CD regulates SOX2 is worth our further study.
