Photorespiratory glycine contributes to photosynthetic induction during low to high light transition
Authors: Xinyu Fu, Berkley J Walker
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeaves experience near-constant light fluctuations daily. Past studies have identified many limiting factors of slow photosynthetic induction when leaves transition from low light to high light. However, the contribution of photorespiration in influencing photosynthesis during transient light conditions is largely unknown. This study employs dynamic measurements of gas exchange and metabolic responses to examine the contribution of photorespiration in constraining net rates of carbon assimilation during light induction. This work indicates that photorespiratory glycine accumulation during the early light induction contributes 5–7% to the additional carbon fixed relative to the low light conditions. Mutants with large glycine pools under photorespiratory conditions (5-formyl THF cycloligase and hydroxypyruvate reductase 1) showed a transient spike in net CO2 assimilation during light induction, with glycine buildup accounting for 22–36% of the extra carbon assimilated. Interestingly, levels of many C3 cycle intermediates remained relatively constant in both mutants and wild-type throughout the light induction period where glycine accumulated, indicating that recycling of carbon into the C3 cycle via photorespiration is not needed to maintain C3 cycle activity under transient conditions. Furthermore, our data show that oxygen transient experiments can be used as a proxy to identify the photorespiratory component of light-induced photosynthetic changes.Keywords: Photorespiration, Photosynthesis, Light induction, Metabolism, GlycineSubject terms: Light responses, Plant physiology, C3 photosynthesis

Introduction

In natural environments, photosynthesis changes dynamically in response to fluctuating light intensity, caused by near-constant changes in sun flecks, intermittent cloud cover, and shading of leaves within the canopy1–4. Photosynthetic induction has classically been used to refer only to processes mediating a dark to light transition, but more recently it has also been used to refer to the transient increase in leaf CO2 assimilation (A) to a new steady state following an increase in light5. In this paper, we use it to refer to a period of transition between lower light and higher light when there is a photosynthetic response. The delay in achieving the maximum A from low light to high light decreases overall daily carbon capture through photosynthesis6,7. The slow rate of photosynthetic induction is estimated to cause a 13–21% reduction in net CO2 assimilation under outdoor growing conditions8–10. Understanding the mechanisms behind photosynthetic induction provides insights into how effectively plants utilize transient light energy for carbon capture and identify areas where photosynthesis can be improved under fluctuating light conditions.

It is well-known that the speed of photosynthetic induction is affected by both CO2 diffusion and biochemical processes. Specifically, the movement of CO2 from the atmosphere to the catalytic site of rubisco (the initial carbon-fixing enzyme of photosynthesis) within the chloroplast is one significant limitation of photosynthetic induction11–14. Once CO2 is inside the chloroplast, carbon fixation can be limited by the activation of a sequence of C3 cycle enzymes and the subsequent regeneration of ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP)15,16. For example, the activation of rubisco by its regulatory enzyme, rubisco activase (Rca), is a key factor that controls the rate of both rubisco carboxylation and oxygenation during the transition to higher light levels17. The initiation and relaxation of non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) is another potential limitation of photosynthetic induction during transitions to higher light intensities, where state transitions are involved to balance excitation of the two photosystems in rapid light adaptation18,19.

In addition to these well-known components of photosynthetic induction, photorespiration is being recognized as another factor that might affect the speed of photosynthetic induction20–22. Photorespiration is intrinsically linked with photosynthesis because the enzyme rubisco has both carboxylase and oxygenase activities23. Rubisco oxygenase activity produces an inhibitory compound 2-phosphoglycolate (2-PG) that must be recycled with the trade-off of CO2 release and energy use. It is generally assumed that 75% of the 2-PG carbon is recycled by converting to 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA), which is fed back into the C3 cycle for RuBP regeneration24,25. The remaining 25% of the 2-PG carbon is released as CO2 in the mitochondria via glycine decarboxylase26,27. Since photorespiratory flux is the second largest metabolic flux of carbon in many plants, the rate of CO2 release from photorespiration is a major determinant of net CO2 assimilation under steady state conditions and possibly also under transient conditions.

In addition, carbon-containing metabolite pools in the C3 cycle and possibly photorespiration could function as carbon reservoirs needed for the functioning of photosynthesis during light induction. For example, C3 cycle intermediate pools are maintained at a relatively high level under low light conditions, facilitating a quick transition to higher RuBP regeneration rates when light level increases28,29. Although pool sizes of many photorespiratory intermediates (such as glycine) generally increase during the transition from low to high light30–34 or when the O2 concentration increases or the CO2 concentration decreases26,35,36, many of these studies were conducted under steady-state conditions and the exact impact of photorespiratory metabolism on photosynthetic induction remains unclear. A novel aspect of non-steady-state photorespiratory metabolism is underscored by a finding that the transient accumulation of photorespiratory glycine pools during the shift from low to high oxygen environments leads to carbon being temporarily stored within the metabolite pool rather than released as CO2 through the downstream glycine decarboxylase (GDC). This extra carbon gain maintained in the growing photorespiratory glycine pool accounts for approximately 25% of the increased carbon fixation observed under high oxygen conditions compared to low oxygen conditions21. If similar glycine pool build-ups occur during light induction, a lower stoichiometry of CO2 release from GDC could increase net CO2 assimilation, but it is not clear if glycine pools build up to a large enough degree to impact net CO2 assimilation during light transients.

In this work, we explored the effects of photorespiratory metabolism on net CO2 assimilation during light induction using wild-type and Arabidopsis mutants with enlarged pools of photorespiratory intermediates. Analysis of the wild-type plants determined that 5–7% of the carbon assimilated early during light induction is due to the rapid buildup of glycine pools. Additionally, both 5-formyl THF cycloligase (5-FCL) and Hydroxypyruvate reductase 1 (HPR1) mutants showed greater accumulation of photorespiratory intermediates compared to the wild type, with minor phenotypic alteration under normal photorespiratory conditions37,38. In these mutants, the buildup of photorespiratory glycine correlated with a sharp overshoot in the net CO2 assimilation rate during the early phase of light induction. These findings indicate that the build-up of photorespiratory glycine holds carbon that is not passed through glycine decarboxylation and contributes a greater gain of carbon during light induction. This response can potentially be engineered in systems with increased pool accumulation.

Results

Substantial differences in photosynthetic induction under photorespiratory but not low photorespiratory conditions between mutant and wild-type

The CO2 assimilation rate was characterized in T-DNA insertional mutant and wild-type leaves during a transition from low (50 μmol m−2 s−1) to high (1000 μmol m−2 s−1) light under ambient O2 conditions. Because hpr1 and 5-fcl mutants were generated using different background strains, the ecotype Col-0 and Col-3 were used as wild type for the hpr1 and 5-fcl mutant respectively. While wild-type plants had a gradual and asymptotic increase in CO2 assimilation under the high light, there was a sharp overshoot in the net CO2 assimilation rate in hpr1 and 5-fcl mutants during the first few minutes of light induction followed by a decrease to a steady-state level (Fig. 1). In both wild-type and mutant plants, the CO2 partial pressure in the intercellular airspace of the leaf (Ci) and at the site of Rubisco catalysis (Cc) exhibited a similar decreasing trend following light induction. Concurrently, the stomatal conductance to water vapor (gsw) displayed a comparable increasing trend (Supplementary Figs. 1– 3). Photosynthetic light induction responses were also measured at 2% O2 to minimize photorespiration. Under low photorespiratory conditions, there was no overshoot in CO2 assimilation in hpr1 and 5fcl and their response was restored to a response similar to that of the wild-type (Supplementary Fig. 4), suggesting that the alteration of photosynthetic induction in the two mutants are limited to photorespiratory conditions.

Leaves were initially acclimated to low light (50 μmol m−2 s−1, depicted in the shaded area) for 40 min, followed by exposure to 40 min of high light (1000 μmol m−2 s−1, depicted in the unshaded area). Data points in each subplot denote independent time-course measurements on 10 different plants for each genotype. The blue curve represents the locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) fitted line, providing a visualization of the data trend.

Glycine is the most drastically changed photorespiratory intermediate during light induction

To assess how metabolite pools constrain and respond to photosynthetic induction, metabolic profiles of the hpr1 and 5-fcl mutants were compared to their respective wild-type plants as they transitioned from low light to high light conditions for 0, 5, 10, and 20 min. Among all the photorespiratory intermediates measured, glycine exhibited the largest increase in response to light, and this increase was much more pronounced in the hpr1 and 5-fcl mutants than in the wild-type plants (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 5). Glycine levels in the wild-type increased by 3–4 fold within the first 5–10 min of light induction before gradually stabilizing again. At the beginning of the light induction, glycine levels were 5–7 fold higher in both hpr1 and 5-fcl mutants compared to the wild-type, with the hpr1 mutant increasing by 3.5-fold within the initial 5 min before plateauing, while the 5-fcl mutant exhibited a continuous increase throughout the entire 20-min period, eventually increasing by 6.5-fold (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 1).

Compared to the 5-fcl mutant, the metabolic changes in hpr1 relative to the wild-type were more notable. The hpr1 mutant had a significant accumulation of several photorespiratory intermediates (e.g., 2-PG, glycolate, glycine, serine, hydroxypyruvate, and glycerate) at substantially higher levels than in wild-type plants (Fig. 2). However, not all these perturbed photorespiratory intermediates in the hpr1 mutant showed changes in response to light induction, with only glycine and 2-PG having a significant increase during the light induction (Fig. 2). In contrast, most of the pool sizes of the photorespiratory intermediates in the 5-fcl mutant remained similar to the wild-type levels during the light induction, except for the glycine increase and serine decrease (Fig. 2).

Calculating the extent to which the buildup of photorespiratory glycine limits the rate of net assimilation during light induction

Comparing the glycine level at the beginning of the light induction to the level during different light induction periods lets us determine how much the buildup of glycine increased net assimilation due to a lower stoichiometry of CO2 release from glycine decarboxylase. To determine the extra carbon gained during the 0–5, 0–10, and 0–20 min phases of light induction, we first integrated all the carbon assimilated during this period, then compared this to the amount of carbon accumulated as glycine during the same period. Total carbon assimilation was quantified by determining the total amount of carbon assimilated during each time window by integrating the area under the curve between the dynamic net assimilation rates and a baseline of net assimilation rates at low light conditions (Supplementary Data 2). To compare this integrated carbon assimilated value to the carbon accumulated by glycine buildup, we divided the total glycine accumulation over this same time by two, since only half of the total glycine build-up contributes to the additional fixed carbon. This stoichiometry is because when two molecules of glycine are metabolized by downstream glycine decarboxylase, it results in the release of one molecule of CO2. Therefore, if photorespiratory glycine is not metabolized, it contributes half a molecule of CO2 assimilation for every molecule of glycine that accumulates. During the 0–5 min period, the glycine build-up accounts for 5–7% of the additional carbon assimilated relative to the steady-state assimilation at low light in the wild-type (Fig. 3). However, during the 0–20 min period, glycine buildup accounted for only 1–3% of the extra assimilated carbon (Fig. 3), indicating that the carbon accumulated from glycine buildup lessened as the light induction progressed.

The percentage of additional fixed carbon explained by glycine build-up unit leaf area during various light induction periods of Arabidopsis mutants (hpr1 and 5-fcl) and their respective wild-type (Col-0 and Col-3). The additional fixed carbon during each light induction period (0–5, 0–10, and 0–20 min) was calculated by integrating the area between the assimilation response curve and the baseline assimilation rate at steady state under low light. The glycine buildup was calculated from the differences in area-based glycine levels during each light induction period (0–5, 0–10, and 0–20 min). The standard errors of the percentages were calculated using pooled variance from the fixed carbon measurements and glycine buildup measurements (see Supplemental Data 2).

The larger increase in glycine pools (and lower rates of carbon fixation) in the hpr1 and 5-fcl mutants corresponds to a larger proportion of the extra carbon assimilated during the light induction in the mutant plants compared to wild-type (Fig. 3). During the first 5 min of light induction, the percentage of the fixed carbon explained by the glycine accumulation in hpr1 (36%) was higher than 5-fcl (22%). However, during the first 20 min, the percentage of the fixed carbon explained by the glycine accumulation in hpr1 (12%) was lower than 5-fcl (14%). This shift indicates that the different kinetics of glycine accumulation between the mutants influenced the percentage of fixed carbon that could be attributed to glycine levels over time.

Metabolic changes in the C3 cycle and other central metabolic pathways during the light induction

As photorespiration is tightly linked to other parts of central metabolism, we examined how changes in photorespiratory metabolism during light induction are coordinated with metabolic adjustments in the C3 cycle, TCA cycle, and amino acid metabolism. Due to the accumulation of photorespiratory glycine during the light induction, photorespiratory carbon did not pass completely through the pathway to 3-PGA that feeds back to the C3 cycle. The 3-PGA levels decreased by ~ 2 fold in the hpr1 and 5-fcl mutants but remained unchanged in the wild-type. Other C3 cycle intermediates (RUBP, S7P, and H6P) also remained constant throughout the entire 20-min light induction period in the mutants and the wild-types (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Data 1).

Substantial differences in oxygen transient responses between mutant and wild-type

When transitioning from low to high oxygen conditions, rates of net CO2 assimilation declined at an exponential rate until reaching a lower steady state. There was a more delayed photosynthetic response in the hpr1 and 5-fcl mutants compared to the corresponding wild-type. A two-phase exponential decay model best described the dynamics of the oxygen transient in these mutants. The rate constant that determined the rate of decay for the slower decay component (k_slow) in the mutants was 3–4 times higher than that of the wild-type (Supplementary Fig. 6), quantifying the delayed transient responses of the mutants.

Discussion

Buildup of photorespiratory glycine impacts carbon assimilation during light induction

Combined measurements of gas exchange and the metabolic response under fluctuating light reveals that photorespiration contributes a great deal net rates of carbon assimilation during light induction. During the early phase of light induction, stomatal and mesophyll conductance limit the availability of CO2, increasing the ratio of O2/CO2. This increase leads to a relatively faster rise in the rate of rubisco’s oxygenation activity than its carboxylation activity 13,39. In the wild-type, the photorespiratory glycine build-up accounts for 5–7% of the additional carbon assimilated during the first 5 min of photosynthetic induction (Fig. 3). The unique photosynthetic induction responses observed in the hpr1 and 5-fcl mutants presented an opportunity to investigate if the link between net gas exchange under fluctuating light and glycine pools can be amplified in mutants with high photorespiratory glycine accumulation. This large glycine accumulation in hpr1 and 5-fcl mutants was accompanied by a transient overshoot in net CO2 assimilation, reaching maximum rates of net CO2 assimilation faster than the wild-type under photorespiratory conditions (Fig. 1 and 2). The overshoot in CO2 assimilation under photorespiratory (21% O2) but not non-photorespiratory (2% O2) conditions also indicates that the overshoot was specifically triggered under photorespiratory conditions in the mutants (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Leveraging the contribution of glycine accumulation in facilitating increased CO2 assimilation could enable reengineering plant responses to fluctuating light conditions to optimize net CO2 assimilation. The difference in glycine buildup between the hpr1 and the 5-fcl mutant indicates that carbon assimilation rates can be influenced not only by altering total glycine pool sizes, but also by modifying the kinetics of glycine accumulation. Avoiding a compensatory drop in glycine levels after exposure to lower light could help minimize potential losses of fixed carbon through photorespiration and optimize carbon gain during that transition. Alternatively, processing accumulated glycine through non-decarboxylating pathways would also preserve the carbon gained during the original glycine accumulation. Therefore, engineering plants with rapid glycine accumulation during shade-light transition and gradual glycine relaxation during light-shade transition could optimize overall carbon gain under fluctuating light provided the accumulated glycine was not decarboxylated upon returning to lower total photorespiratory rates. To enrich our understanding of the intricate dynamics between glycine metabolism and photosynthetic efficiency, it is beneficial to also investigate mutants exhibiting reduced levels of glycine accumulation or those with improved glycine turnover.

The larger decline in net assimilation rates after the peak values in the hpr1 than the 5-fcl mutant could be attributed to the inhibitory effects of 2-phosphoglycolate (2-PG) accumulating during the light induction period (Fig. 2). 2-PG inhibits several key enzymes involved in the Calvin cycle and starch metabolism, such as triosephosphate isomerase (TPI), sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphate phosphatase (SBPase), and phosphofructokinase (PFK)40–42. In addition, hpr1 has a higher-than-normal level of glycolate (Fig. 2), which also can inhibit rubisco activity43. This is supported by a previous study which demonstrated that the photosynthetic induction response of dark-adapted leaves is notably delayed in a maize mutant with impaired glycolate oxidase activity, in contrast to the wild-type plant that can uphold low steady-state levels of glycolate44.

Is photorespiratory recycling of intermediates vital for continued function of the C3 cycle?

Photorespiration is often considered vital for maintaining C3 cycle intermediate pool sizes by returning carbon into the cycle that would otherwise be lost42,45, but our findings indicate this may not be the case while glycine pools accumulate. Photorespiratory carbon that accumulates as glycine is not returned to the C3 cycle from photorespiration, so during these periods there is reduced flux back into the C3 cycle in the chloroplast as 3-PGA46,47. If this only partial recycling of carbon from photorespiration starved carbon return to the chloroplast and the C3 cycle, we would expect to see large decreases in the levels of C3 cycle intermediates. Interestingly, these intermediates remained largely unchanged (Fig. 4), suggesting that the accumulation of glycine does not have a substantial effect on the carbon flux through the C3 cycle and the levels of most metabolites within the cycle. These data indicate that photorespiratory recycling of carbon into the C3 cycle is not necessary for continued C3 cycle activity and the carbon fixing capacity of the cycle is enough to maintain pool sizes, at least under transient conditions. Carbon balance may be accomplished during this time by reduced triose phosphate removal for starch and sucrose synthesis during the transient. Additionally, there is growing evidence that carbon for the C3 cycle can be supplied via oxidative pentose phosphate activity in the light (the glucose 6-phosphate shunt)48–50. Such a shunt could be critical to help maintain C3 cycle intermediates during transient times of net carbon removal.

However, this might not be the case when photorespiration is perturbed genetically. In the hpr1 mutant 3-PGA levels decreased at 5 min and in the 5-fcl mutant after 10 min of light induction. This implies that in these mutants with large accumulation of glycine, there is a slight effect on carbon return in the form of 3-PGA to the chloroplast during the initial stages of photosynthetic induction. Notably, this only occurs when glycine pools accumulated to 6–9 fold higher than wild-type, which indicates an even larger decrease in recycling flux as compared to the wild-type metabolism. The decrease in C3 intermediates appears limited to 3-PGA, with no changes to other phosphorylated intermediates indicating that the overall operation of the C3 cycle appears to still maintain its stability. Maintenance of relatively high levels of C3 cycle intermediates in low light and low CO2 has been observed in several species28,29. These findings suggest the robustness of the C3 cycle and its capacity to manage its metabolic pools to buffer transient disturbances in carbon flow.

Using oxygen to probe the photorespiratory induction component of light transients

Our study revealed that the metabolic constraints on the decrease in assimilation during the low-to-high oxygen transient shared similarities to the photosynthetic increase during the low-to-high light transient. This similarity is due to both conditions involving an increase in photorespiration, even if the driver for this increase stems from different sources. The hpr1 and 5fcl mutants showed a significantly slower decline in net CO2 assimilation compared to the wild-type plants (Supplementary Fig. 6). This slower decline rate during the low-to-high oxygen transient in the mutants could be explained by highly accumulated glycine in these mutants compared to the wild-type. Because oxygen transient phenotypes can be observed within 5 min, oxygen transients can serve as an attractive proxy for the photorespiratory induction component of light transients.

Measuring light transients under various oxygen conditions can be useful to assess the contribution of photorespiration in light induction51. Although CO2 assimilation during light induction was induced in Arabidopsis wild-type plants under 2% O2 when compared to 21% O2, it is hard to conclude whether photorespiration is detrimental or not to photosynthetic induction, because the increased transient net CO2 assimilation observed under 2% O2 may be attributed mainly to the higher rates of rubisco carboxylation, which is not specific to photorespiration. The observation of enhanced light induction in the hpr1 and 5-fcl mutants and return to typical light induction patterns under 2% O2 suggests that photorespiration has a considerable impact on adjusting net assimilation to varying photorespiratory conditions. Measuring light transients under high O2 conditions could be used to identify mutants with non-traditional light induction patterns that are relevant to the photorespiratory induction component of light transients.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth

Seeds of A. thaliana ecotypes and T-DNA insertion mutant lines were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC; http://www.arabidopsis.org/)52 and confirmed by PCR-based genotyping. The ecotype Col-0 was used as wild type (WT) for the hpr1 mutant allele (SALK_067724; AT1G68010)49 and ecotype Col-3 was used as WT for the 5-fcl mutant allele (SAIL_28_D07; AT5G13050)16. Seeds were sown in the soil and stratified in the dark at 4 °C for three days. Plants were grown in a controlled growth chamber at 22 °C, 60% relative humidity, and under short-day conditions (8/16 h light/dark at 100 μmol photons m−2 s−1).

Dynamic gas exchange measurements

Gas exchange measurements were taken on fully expanded rosette leaves of 10-week plants using an infrared gas analyzer LI-6800, equipped with a 6800-01A fluorometer chamber with 6 cm2 aperture inserts (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, Nebraska, USA). The chamber conditions were set as follows: sample air flow of 400 μmol s−1, fan mixing speed of 10,000 rpm, leaf temperature of 22 °C, and vapor pressure deficit of 1.5 kPa to avoid condensation and stomatal closure.

For light induction measurements, leaves were initially exposed to a low light condition with a PPFD of 50 μmol m−2 s−1 for 40 min to achieve photosynthetic steady state. Afterward, the light intensity was raised to 1000 μmol m−2 s−1 for 40 min, providing ample time for the leaf to attain a steady state in CO2 assimilation. Leaf gas exchange parameters were logged every 10 s starting from the beginning of low light adaptation period. To analyze photosynthetic carbon gain during the light induction period, the trapezoidal rule was used to integrate the area under the curve of net CO2 assimilation rate during the 0–5, 0–10, and 0–20 min phases of high light exposure. For measuring light induction responses under low oxygen conditions to minimize photorespiration, a pre-mixed 2% O2 and 98% N2 gas mixture was supplied to the filter cover air Inlet on the back of the LI-6800 console, using mass flow controllers (Alicat Scientific, Tucson AZ, USA) connected to a custom Raspberry Pi controller.

For oxygen transient measurements, leaves were first acclimated to high light (1000 μmol m−2 s−1) in 40 Pa CO2 levels (CO2 injector manually controlled) and low O2 conditions (2% O2/98% N2) to reach photosynthetic steady state. Then the cuvette was switched to high O2 conditions (40% O2/60% N2) and leaves were left to reach a new photosynthetic steady state for 5 min. The time course data were recorded with 2 s intervals and fitted using a two-phase exponential function. Cc was determined by Ci- A/gm where A and Ci were determined from gas exchange measurements and gm was determined using assuming a constant gm measured previously for plants grown under identical conditions using gas exchange and online isotope discrimination previously31.

Metabolite analyses

Light induction experiments were conducted on fully expanded rosette leaves of 10-week-old plants, which represented the period of maximum biomass production during their vegetative growth. These experiments were performed around midday, a time when the plants were approaching metabolic steady state53. Leaves were exposed to low light (50 μmol m−2 s−1) for 40 min to reach a steady-state followed by exposure to high light (1000 μmol m−2 s−1) for 0, 5, 10, or 20 min in the LI-COR cuvette. To quench metabolism rapidly under the light source at a given time point, liquid nitrogen was sprayed on the underside of the leaf via an injection port on the LI-COR cuvette using a liquid nitrogen cryospray (Wallach UltraFreeze, Cooper Surgical, USA). The frozen leaf tissues were rapidly removed from the cuvette, weighed, and stored at − 80 °C until metabolite analysis before thawing.

Frozen tissues were homogenized under cryogenic conditions using a FastPrep-24 instrument with a CoolPrep Adapter (MP biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA) and dry ice. Metabolites were extracted using methanol, chloroform, and water as described earlier47. Known concentration of an internal standard (nor-leucine) was spiked onto the tissue before the extraction. The polar fraction was taken from the upper layer after centrifugation, lyophilized to dryness, and derivatized with methoxyamine pyridine and a trimethylsilylating agent (final volume 100 μl) as described previously47. One μl of the trimethylsilyl (TMS) derivatized mixture was injected onto an Agilent 7890A GC equipped with a 30 m VF-5 ms column and interfaced to an Agilent 5975C inert XL Mass Selective Detector (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) as described previously. The oven temperature was set at 40 °C for 1 min, ramped at 40 °C min−1 to 80 °C, then at 10 °C min−1 to 240 °C, then 20 °C min−1 to 320 °C, and hold at 320 °C for 5 min. The mass spectrometer was operated on scan mode (m/z values of 50–600), with electron impact at 70 eV, ion source temperature at 230 °C, and the quadrupole temperature at 150 °C.

Chromatographic peaks were identified based on the spectra and retention times using the NIST 14 Mass Spectral Library and authentic reference standards. Quantification was performed by integrating peak areas from the extracted ion chromatograms by selecting m/z values unique for each identified metabolite. Relative metabolite levels were calculated by dividing the peak areas by the area of the internal standard (norleucine) and the sample fresh weight. Absolute glycine levels were quantified using a response factor. This factor is calculated by comparing the chromatographic peak area from a glycine standard to the peak area of norleucine in a calibration sample analyzed on the same day as the plant samples. One-way ANOVA was performed to evaluate the effect of light induction periods on each metabolite in each genotype using R with p < 0.05 and a Tukey’s post-hoc test.
