Complications of Skin Expansion in the Pediatric Population: A 10-year Retrospective Study
Authors: Zakarya Alami Hassani, Nawfal Fejjal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives: This study aimed to discuss the risks and complications associated with skin expansion in the pediatric population, a common plastic surgery technique. Methods: Fifty-one patients who underwent skin expansion between 2013 and 2022 at the Plastic Surgery and Burns Unit of the Children's Hospital in Rabat were included in this retrospective study. This study aimed to report all results, analyze them, and compare them with existing literature to determine predictive elements of complications in the pediatric population. Results: The study used 96 expanders of three different brands in 70 procedures. Only one expander was often used, but up to seven expanders were used in some cases. Expansion sessions began after an average of 15 days of expander placement, with an average total expansion rate of 105.5%. Complications were encountered in 16 of 96 expanders (16.6%), with infection being the most serious complication, found in four cases. Other complications included skin complications, expander malfunctions, skin necrosis, loose sutures, exposure of the valve, and hair loss with stretch marks. The use of multiple expanders was the only statistically relevant factor toward complications. Conclusions: The study concluded that skin expansion is not without risks and reported complication rate range from 9.6% to 30% in the pediatric population. Multicenter studies specific to each variable will allow better identification of the risk factors and standardization of the indications. The study provides valuable insights into the risks and complications associated with skin expansion in the pediatric population, which could help guide medical professionals in making informed decisions when considering this procedure. Keywords: tissue expansion, pediatric population, treatment of burn sequelae

Introduction

Skin loss is a very frequent problem in plastic surgery, of which skin expansion is currently one of the main therapeutic means. However, this technique is not without risks and complications. In reported series of pediatric populations, the complication rate has ranged from 9.6% to 30%1,2). However, the occurrence of a complication is not synonymous to failure of the procedure, and this allows us to classify complications as major ones that may lead to treatment failure or even a worsening of the previous condition and minor ones that do not compromise the expansion protocol but may modify it.

Methods

We report the results of a retrospective study of all patients who underwent skin expansion during the period between 2013 and 2022 at the Plastic Surgery and Burns Unit of the Children's Hospital of Rabat. Written consent from the guardians of the patients was obtained for the documentation of their children's cases.

All patients were managed by the same senior surgeon from the moment the indication was confirmed to the reconstruction, including the insertion of the expanders and their filling.

The expansion protocol was the same for all patients. Once the indication had been established, and under antibiotic coverage, we proceeded to the insertion of the expander. It was then inflated with 10% of its maximum volume. A second incision allowed the valve to be placed remotely under the skin. A closed suction drain was left in place for 48 h.

After an average of 2 weeks, patients started receiving weekly expansion sessions until the target volume was reached. Reconstruction was then performed under antibiotic coverage.

In our study, we analyzed all factors associated in the literature with tissue expansion complications such as age, etiology, localization, number of expanders, and number of procedures. It is worth noting that long-term complications, such as hypertrophic scarring, were not subject to analysis. Statistical analyses were conducted using Chi-squared tests with the assistance of Jamovi 2.3.18.0 version.

This study aimed to report all our results, analyze them, and compare them with the literature to determine the predictive elements of complications in the pediatric population.

Results

Over a 10-year period, we managed 51 patients who underwent 70 tissue expansion procedures (Table 1), with each patient undergoing between one and five procedures. In these 70 procedures, we placed 96 expanders, all following the same protocol. Patients' age ranged from 4 to 20 years, with a median age of 6. The study group consisted of 30 boys (58.8%) and 21 girls (41.2%). The primary etiology for tissue expansion was burn sequelae in 41 cases (80.4%), road accident sequelae and congenital nevi in 4 (7.8%), and animal bites in 2 (3.9%).

The distribution of expansion locations was diverse, with 22 patients (43.1%) on the scalp and trunk, 4 (7.8%) on the face and neck, and 3 (5.9%) on the lower limb. None of the patients had significant medical conditions that could compromise the tissue expansion procedure.

During the 70 procedures, we used 96 rectangular expanders from three different brands: Eurosilicone [73], MENTOR [16], and Silimed [7]. The expanders' size ranged from 35 cc to 950 cc; the most commonly used sizes were 300 cc (30 cases) and 400 cc (13 cases). A single expander was used in 46 procedures, two expanders in 23 procedures, and three expanders in 2 procedures. The total number of expanders per patient varied from one used in a single procedure to seven expanders used across five procedures for one patient.

Expansion sessions typically started after an average of 15 days of expander placement, with a minimum of 6 days and a maximum of 24 days. In the absence of complications, the total expansion duration ranged from 35 to 156 days, averaging 83 days. On average, 12% of the expander's volume was injected during these sessions, with a range of 4% to 20%. Without complications, the average total filling rate for all expanders was 105.5%, exceeding the manufacturer's recommended rate for 69 expanders (71.87%).

Complications (Table 2) were encountered in 16 out of 96 expanders (16.6%) across all procedures. Among these complications, 3 occurred immediately after prosthesis placement, 11 during the expansion period, and 2 after reconstruction. Infections (Figure 1A) found in four cases were the most serious complications, necessitating early removal of the expander in three cases. Three complications were related to expander malfunctions, including valve malfunction (Figure 1B) and migration (Figure 1C) in 2 cases.

Skin complications occurred in six patients, varying in severity, with exposure of the prosthesis (Figure 1D) being the most significant issue. Other complications included skin necrosis, loose sutures, valve exposure (Figure 1E), and hair loss with stretch marks (Figure 1F). Effusions, such as serous effusion and hematoma, were observed in three cases.

Overall, 7 of the 16 complications required early removal of the expander (Figure 2), resulting in an overall failure rate of 7.2%. The filling rate for these cases ranged from 19% to 88%, with an average of 53%.

Regarding the quantity of expanders employed in a singular procedure, a 100% complication rate was noted when three prostheses were used concurrently, in contrast to a 24.4% rate when employing a single prosthesis (p < 0.01). Notably, there was no discernible correlation between the incidence of complications and the number of procedures undergone by the patient (p = 0.961).

Discussion

Radovan1) was the first to report the use of skin expansion in breast reconstruction. Austad and Rose2) followed with their description of a self-inflating implant. These authors based their work on the cervical and oro-labial expansion rituals practiced in Thailand and Chad and on the work of Illizaov and Matev on bone lengthening and its impact on the soft tissues. The first use of skin expansion in children was in 1981 by Argenta3) in the treatment of burn sequelae on the neck.

Since children are not miniature adults, they have certain characteristics that have a direct impact on the treatment outcome.

Indeed, children's skin is characterized by a good vascularization that allows for faster healing and more hypertrophic scars. It is also thinner, increasing the risk of stretch marks4).

The risk of infection is also greater in children not only because of the many oro-rhino-laryngeal infections that constitute real reservoirs of germs but also because of the many skin diseases specific to children that can lead to scratching lesions.

In children, the indications of skin expansion are diverse and numerous5). They depend not only on the pathology but also on the location. The most common are congenital nevi and burn sequelae. More rarely, skin expansion can be used for congenital alopecia, ear reconstruction, severe hypoplastic breast in adolescents, omphaloceles, myelomeningoceles, loss of substance in the limbs, syndactyly, and clubfoot.

The success of the skin expansion procedure depends on the appropriate indication for its use, individual risk factors, detailed and individualized planning, and, most importantly, factors related to the technique. It is a long and delicate process during which complications of varying degrees of severity can occur.

The average complication rate in the literature is 17.44%6). In series specific to children, the rate is slightly higher (23.4%). The series with the fewest complications is that of Bauer et al., which is 9.6%7). The series by WG Cole8) on lower limb expansion reports the highest rate (40%). This disparity in the complication rates (Table 4) between the different series is explained by the difference in the studied variables. Thus, the populations studied are not always the same and are often targeted on age, pathology, or topography, complicating the comparison between the different studies.

All the authors agree that complications can be classified into two types: major or absolute complications that require a premature termination of the procedure or additional surgeries and the minor or relative ones without any real impact on the expansion process.

Infection is the most serious and most frequent complication in the literature. The germs most frequently involved are Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis. Antibiotic coverage limits the risk of infection and separate approaches allow the conservation of uncontaminated expanders. In our study, the infection rate was 4.16% contrasting to an average rate of 6% in literature9,15,23). It was the second most common complication representing 25% of all of them.

Skin complications are also serious, represented mainly by necrosis and prosthesis exposure, with reported rates in the literature of 3 and 4%, respectively6). A major exposure would require removal of the prosthesis, whereas a late or minimal exposure can be managed by local treatment. In our study, this was the most frequently encountered problem with a rate of 6.25% representing 37.5% of all complications.

Technical issues and equipment malfunctions are considered significant contributors to treatment failures, often resulting in challenges with filling or leaks. Our study identified 3 technical problems, accounting for a rate of 3.125%, compared to a rate of 5.37% in the most recent study22), constituting 18.75% of the overall complications.

Blood and serous collections represented the final category of complications in our study. Smaller collections typically had no discernible impact on the expansion process, while larger ones occasionally necessitated revision surgery. Our study encountered three such collections, comprising 3.125% of all expanders and contributing to 18.75% of the complications observed.

In the case of the youngest children24), literature reports have highlighted the possibility of skull deformities occurring during the expansion process. Therefore, some experts suggest delaying scalp expansion until children reach the age of 12 to 18 months. It is worth noting that in our study, we did not encounter this complication since our youngest patient was 4 years old.

Certain risk factors for complications are consistently acknowledged in the literature. These include sequelae, burns, and scars. In addition, extremities are associated with a higher complication rate due to the thinness of the skin in these areas and their terminal vascularization. Although some authors revealed that young age is a potential risk factor for complications8), there is not unanimous consensus among all authors regarding this issue.

Conclusion

Skin expansion has emerged as a crucial therapeutic approach for various pediatric plastic surgery conditions. However, the diverse target populations studied in different researches make it challenging to establish a consensus regarding the associated risk factors for complications of such procedure. Conducting multicenter studies tailored to specific variables would facilitate risk factor identification and enhance the standardization of indications.
