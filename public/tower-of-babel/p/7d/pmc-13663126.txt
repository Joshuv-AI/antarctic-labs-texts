Association of Aggregate Index of Systemic Inflammation (AISI) with all-cause mortality in critically Ill pediatric patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: De-Chun Tian, Xiao-Lan Zhang, Chun-Jiang Chen, Cheng-Long Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGrowing evidence supports the prognostic utility of AISI in adults, but its association with mortality in the critically ill pediatric population remains poorly characterized.ObjectiveTo investigate the association between AISI and 28-day all-cause mortality in critically ill pediatric patients.MethodsA total of 8,797 patients admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit were included from the PIC Database. The AISI was log₂-transformed and categorized into four groups based on quartiles. The outcomes included 28-day all-cause mortality, both in-hospital and in-ICU. Cox proportional hazards regression, restricted cubic spline, two-piecewise threshold model, and subgroup analyses were used to evaluate the association between AISI and 28-day all-cause mortality.ResultsMales accounted for 4,930 (56.0%) of the patients. The overall 28-day in-hospital and ICU all-cause mortality rates were 4.3% and 4.2%, respectively. The RCS analysis revealed a significant nonlinear association between the log2-transformed AISI and both mortality outcomes. Threshold effect analysis identified that the threshold points of log₂-AISI were 5.410 and 5.407 for 28-day in-hospital and ICU mortality, respectively. Subgroup analyses revealed no notable differences between specific patient subgroups.ConclusionsThis study suggests a nonlinear association between AISI and 28-day mortality in critically ill pediatric patients. AISI levels above the threshold were independently associated with an increased mortality risk.Keywords: AISI, Critically ill children, Nonlinear association, All-cause mortality, Threshold point, PIC Database

Introduction

Systemic inflammatory dysregulation represents a central pathophysiological mechanism underlying critical illness, driving the development of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome and contributing substantially to adverse clinical outcomes[1]. Recently, composite inflammatory biomarkers have garnered increasing attention for their ability to synthesize multidimensional biological data and comprehensively reflect the host’s inflammatory-immune status [2]. The aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI) [3], derived from routinely available peripheral blood indices—neutrophil, lymphocyte, platelet, and monocyte counts—represents a robust yet concise tool for clinical risk stratification. Substantial evidence supports the significant prognostic value of AISI across various adult critical care cohorts [4], including those with sepsis [5], cardiovascular disorders [6], acute kidney injury [7], and malignancies [8]. In these contexts, elevated AISI values independently predict an increased risk of mortality [9], and its discriminative performance often surpasses that of conventional, single inflammatory markers [10, 11].

However, current knowledge of AISI in pediatric critical care remains largely limited because existing evidence stems almost exclusively from adult studies. Pediatric physiology differs substantially from that of adults, featuring an immature immune system with distinct innate and adaptive immune responses and unique inflammatory regulatory pathways [12, 13]. Children admitted to intensive care units present with highly heterogeneous, life-threatening conditions that demand prompt and accurate mortality risk assessment to guide therapeutic decisions, optimize resource allocation, and improve clinical outcomes [14]. Although validated pediatric prognostic tools such as the Pediatric Risk of Mortality (PRISM) and Pediatric Logistic Organ Dysfunction (PELOD) scores are widely used [15, 16], their clinical utility is limited by complex data requirements, laborious calculations, infrequent recalibrations, and reliance on subjective evaluations. These limitations restrict their real-time clinical application. Furthermore, these systems primarily rely on static admission parameters and demonstrate inadequate sensitivity to dynamic clinical progression. Consequently, there is an urgent need for objective, laboratory-derived biomarkers that are easily operationalized, highly reproducible, and amenable to serial measurement—characteristics essential for dynamic risk assessment in rapidly evolving clinical scenarios [17]. This would overcome current limitations and refine the accuracy and responsiveness of early warning systems [18].

Because AISI can be calculated from standard admission complete blood counts without specialized assays, it offers considerable advantages in operational simplicity, cost efficiency, and suitability for serial monitoring. These properties make AISI exceptionally adaptable for integration into routine intensive care workflows. If its prognostic validity is confirmed in pediatric cohorts, AISI could serve as an objective, readily available bedside indicator, enabling clinicians to rapidly identify high-risk children. It could also inform tiered management strategies—including enhanced surveillance, proactive organ support, or timely immunomodulatory interventions—for patients with elevated levels, thereby enabling precision medicine approaches in resource-limited settings. This would optimize resource utilization and potentially reduce mortality. Nevertheless, the clinical relevance of AISI in critically ill children, its precise exposure–response relationship with mortality endpoints, and the optimal prognostic threshold remain undefined—knowledge gaps that significantly impede its translation into clinical practice. In this large-scale, retrospective cohort study, we investigated the association between AISI and 28-day all-cause mortality in critically ill pediatric patients using the Pediatric Intensive Care (PIC) Database.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study used data from the PIC database at the Children’s Hospital of Zhejiang University (ZUCH). The database includes information on 12,881 critically ill children admitted to intensive care units between 2010 and 2018. It captures comprehensive demographic, comorbidity, laboratory, treatment, and outcome data. Detailed documentation is available at http://pic.nbscn.org.

Study population

From the 12,881 admissions, we applied exclusion criteria in three sequential steps. First, we excluded patients with missing key hematologic parameters (platelet, neutrophil, monocyte, or lymphocyte counts) or 28-day mortality status (n = 1,446). Second, we excluded neonates aged less than 28 days (n = 2,572) due to their distinct inflammatory physiology and developmental characteristics. Third, we excluded patients with a zero value for any blood cell index (n = 66), as these values cannot be used for log-transformation. We reviewed the original medical records of these 66 patients, and none had a diagnosis of bone marrow suppression or failure. The zero readings were most likely laboratory reporting errors rather than true biological values. Given that these cases represent only 0.75% of the total cohort, the risk of selection bias is minimal. The final analytical cohort comprised 8,797 patients. Patients with missing exposure or outcome data were excluded a priori. A study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. For covariates with partially missing values, multiple imputation by chained equations was used to generate five imputed datasets [19]. Pooled estimates were obtained using Rubin’s rules.

Flowchart of the patient selection process

Exposure variable

The exposure variable was the AISI, which is calculated as follows: AISI = (Neutrophil count × Monocyte count × Platelet count) / Lymphocyte count, where all cellular counts are expressed in units of 10⁹/L. To normalize its distribution and mitigate the influence of extreme values, the AISI was log₂ transformed prior to analysis. Patients were categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on log₂-transformed AISI values. Q1 served as the reference category in all regression analyses to facilitate interpretation of dose-response relationships.

The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause in-hospital mortality, which was defined as death from any cause within 28 days of intensive care unit (ICU) admission. The secondary outcome was 28-day all-cause in-ICU mortality, which was restricted to deaths occurring in the ICU within the same timeframe. Patients who were discharged alive, transferred, or lost to follow-up before 28 days were treated as censored observations at the time of the last contact.

Covariates

Based on existing literature and clinical relevance, the covariates were grouped into three domains: (1) demographics (age and sex); (2) clinical characteristics (sepsis, shock, pneumonia, congenital heart disease, malignancy, central nervous system infection [CNSI], and vasopressor use); and (3) laboratory parameters obtained within 24 h of ICU admission (pH, glucose, lactate, C-reactive protein [CRP], procalcitonin, albumin, serum creatinine, cystatin C, fibrinogen, potassium, and chloride).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 3.3.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and the Free Statistics software program (version 2.4). A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were summarized as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or the median (interquartile range [IQR]), depending on the distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate; categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test.

We fitted Cox proportional hazards regression models to examine the association between AISI and mortality. We report the following hazard ratios (HRs) and their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs):

We used restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with four knots positioned at the 10th, 50th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of log₂-transformed AISI to characterize dose-response relationships [20]. Where significant nonlinearity was detected, two-piecewise Cox regression was applied to identify threshold points and estimate segment-specific hazard ratios (HRs) [21].

To assess result robustness, subgroup analyses were performed, stratified by age (28 days to 1 year, 1 to 5 years, and ≥ 5 years), sex, pneumonia status, vasopressor use, and CNSI status. Interaction P values were calculated to test for effect modification across strata. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed to compare 28-day survival probabilities across AISI quartiles. Differences were assessed using the log-rank test.

Results

We included 8,797 critically ill non-neonatal children with a median age of 0.7 years (IQR 0.2–2.3); the majority (57.0%) were aged 28 days to 1 year, 33.9% were aged 1–5 years, and 9.1% were aged ≥ 5 years. Males accounted for 56.0% of the cohort. Baseline characteristics differed substantially across AISI quartiles (Table 1). Patients in Q4 were older and less likely to be female than those in Q1. They also exhibited a higher burden of inflammatory and infectious comorbidities, including sepsis (3.8% vs. 3.0%), shock (0.9% vs. 0.5%), CNS infection (5.9% vs. 1.9%), and pneumonia (13.9% vs. 6.8%), but lower rates of vasopressor use (24.0% vs. 30.5%), congenital heart disease (6.0% vs. 27.1%), and malignancy (1.5% vs. 3.0%). Laboratory markers in Q4 indicated a more pronounced inflammatory profile, except for lactate and potassium (both P > 0.05). Specifically, Q4 was associated with higher CRP [11.0 (4.0–35.0) mg/L vs. 5.0 (3.0–28.0) mg/L], procalcitonin [0.4 (0.1–1.7) ng/mL vs. 0.1 (0.1–0.9) ng/mL], and fibrinogen (2.8 ± 1.5 g/L vs. 1.9 ± 0.8 g/L), alongside lower albumin (39.1 ± 7.2 g/L vs. 40.3 ± 6.8 g/L). Although several other parameters (pH, serum creatinine, C-reactive protein, chloride, glucose) differed statistically across quartiles (P < 0.05), their median values were clinically similar and unlikely to be of practical relevance.

Multivariable Cox regression revealed a non-linear association between AISI quartiles and 28-day mortality (Table 2). In crude and minimally adjusted models (Model 1), Q2 was associated with a substantially lower risk than Q1 (in-hospital HR 0.39, 95% CI 0.28–0.55; in-ICU HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.28–0.57; both P < 0.001), whereas Q4 showed an increased risk (in-hospital HR 1.31, 95% CI 1.02–1.69; P = 0.035). After full adjustment for demographics, laboratory parameters, and comorbidities (Model 3), the apparent protective effect of Q2 was attenuated and lost statistical significance, suggesting that the lower mortality observed in this group was largely attributable to confounding factors. By contrast, Q4 remained independently associated with higher mortality (in-hospital HR 1.72, 95% CI 1.03–2.87, P = 0.037; in-ICU HR 1.76, 95% CI 1.06–2.95, P = 0.030). Significant linear trends were observed across quartiles in all models for both outcomes (P for trend ≤ 0.037).

Restricted cubic spline analysis under the fully adjusted model confirmed significant overall associations (P for overall = 0.009 and 0.023) and non-linearity (P for nonlinearity = 0.018 and 0.031) for in-hospital and in-ICU mortality, respectively. With reference points anchored at log₂-AISI 7.406 and 7.424, the curves displayed a U-shaped pattern [22], with hazard ratios declining at lower AISI levels, reaching a nadir around log₂-AISI 5–6, and subsequently rising progressively beyond the reference point, with the upper tail of the 95% confidence interval remaining above 1 (Fig. 2). Two-piecewise regression identified breakpoints at 5.410 and 5.407. Below these thresholds, each 1-unit increment in log₂-AISI was not associated with mortality (in-hospital HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.83–1.25, P = 0.854; in-ICU HR 1.05, 95% CI 0.85–1.29, P = 0.649). Conversely, above the breakpoints, the same increment was associated with significantly elevated risk (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.16–1.51 and HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.15–1.53; both P < 0.001), with likelihood ratio tests confirming non-linearity (P = 0.027 and P = 0.043) (Table 3).

Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated significant survival differences across the four AISI quartiles for both in-hospital and in-ICU mortality (log-rank test, both P < 0.0001). Patients in Q2 exhibited the highest survival probabilities, whereas those in Q4 consistently showed the lowest. The first (Q1) and third (Q3) quartiles displayed intermediate and largely overlapping survival trajectories. This pattern—lower mortality in Q2, intermediate in Q1 and Q3, and highest in Q4—mirrors the U-shaped association observed in the restricted cubic spline analysis and the threshold effect identified in the two-piecewise regression (Fig. 3). The number-at-risk tables indicate that, despite substantial censoring due to discharge, follow-up remained adequate across all quartiles through day 28.

To evaluate the robustness of the AISI–mortality association across clinically distinct populations, we performed stratified Cox regression analyses according to age, sex, vasopressor use, pneumonia status, and central nervous system infection. The adjusted hazard ratios for 28-day mortality per 1-unit increase in log₂-transformed AISI were directionally consistent across all subgroups but did not reach statistical significance in any stratum, with 95% confidence intervals uniformly spanning the null. No significant effect modification was detected for age, vasopressor use, pneumonia, or central nervous system infection (all P for interaction > 0.05). Of note, the interaction between AISI and sex approached nominal significance for in-ICU mortality (P = 0.059) and in-hospital mortality (P = 0.097), though neither met the conventional threshold. Collectively, these findings indicate that the absence of a significant linear association between continuous AISI and 28-day mortality is consistent across major clinical subgroups (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Analysis of 8,797 critically ill pediatric patients from the PIC Database revealed a significant nonlinear relationship between AISI and 28-day all-cause mortality, encompassing both in-hospital and in-ICU deaths. Following a log₂-transformation, AISI exhibited a threshold-dependent effect on mortality, with inflection points identified at log₂-AISI 5.410 for in-hospital settings and 5.407 for in-ICU mortality. Below these cutoffs, AISI showed no significant association with mortality risk; conversely, levels exceeding these thresholds were independently associated with a substantially increased risk. The consistency of these associations across diverse patient subgroups—despite the absence of statistically significant interactions—supports the generalizability of our findings and strengthens the reliability of our observations. Collectively, these findings establish AISI as an independent prognostic biomarker in pediatric critical care, with a clearly defined threshold phenomenon that enables clinically meaningful risk stratification at the bedside.

Our results corroborate and extend the existing literature, confirming the prognostic utility of AISI in critically ill adults. A growing body of evidence demonstrates that elevated AISI levels independently correlate with adverse clinical outcomes across diverse adult populations, including those with sepsis, cardiovascular disease, acute kidney injury, and malignancy [5]. For example, research involving adult patients with sepsis has demonstrated that greater AISI levels are related to heightened disease severity and death risk, outperforming established markers such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) or the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) [11]. Correspondingly, among adults with cardiovascular ailments, AISI strongly predicts major adverse cardiovascular incidents and death from any cause [6]. Investigations of acute kidney injury and oncological contexts further validate AISI’s combined strength in gauging systemic inflammatory load [7, 8]. Our investigation addresses this critical evidence gap by demonstrating that AISI’s prognostic significance extends to pediatric critical care, thereby broadening its potential clinical application across the age spectrum.

Despite largely supportive data, certain studies have documented neutral or conflicting results regarding AISI’s predictive performance [23]. Research focusing on specific adult subgroups (e.g., elective surgical cases or individuals with isolated single-organ failure) occasionally detected no significant independent mortality link following rigorous multivariable adjustments. Such inconsistencies may originate from variations in baseline inflammatory activity, clinical heterogeneity among study populations, or the predominance of non-inflammatory pathological drivers in specific clinical contexts. Furthermore, preliminary evidence suggests the predictive strength of AISI might diminish in subjects undergoing early intensive immunomodulatory treatment or experiencing severe immunosuppression, contexts where inflammatory marker variability could be constrained. Our research goes beyond these inconsistencies by delineating a definitive threshold effect in a uniform pediatric cohort, implying the prognostic capacity of AISI peaks above a specific inflammatory magnitude, a finding that may account for neutral results in cohorts exhibiting mainly low values.

In summary, our study makes several key contributions. First, it confirms an independent association between AISI and 28-day all-cause mortality in a large, general pediatric critical care population, addressing a major evidence gap. Second, pinpointing the exact thresholds (log₂-AISI ≈ 5.41) for dual mortality endpoints provides clinically deployable stratification boundaries. Third, the remarkable consistency of these thresholds across distinct mortality endpoints supports their biological plausibility and suggests a shared pathophysiological mechanism. Fourth, minimal subgroup effect modification suggests broad applicability across pediatric critical presentations, bolstering AISI’s candidacy as a universal bedside assessment tool. These characteristics collectively designate the AISI as a prime contender for embedding within pediatric early warning protocols.

These findings carry important implications for PICU clinical practice. The established risk cutoff (log₂-AISI ≈ 5.41) provides an objective, measurable standard for prompt mortality risk stratification upon ICU entry. Patients presenting below this threshold may be managed with standard observation protocols, whereas those exceeding it warrant intensified monitoring, including frequent hemodynamic and organ function assessments, prompt multidisciplinary team involvement, and proactive planning for advanced support measures. The operational simplicity of AISI, stemming from routine and rapidly obtainable hematological parameters, enhances its practicality, particularly in resource-constrained environments. Embedding AISI within electronic health records may permit automated risk notifications, enabling instantaneous decision aids without adding documentation load for clinicians. Sequential AISI evaluations could additionally function as a dynamic tracking measure during the PICU admission, potentially uncovering clinical worsening sooner than conventional indicators and permitting rapid therapeutic modification. Regarding resource management, stratification guided by the AISI could direct choices about patient triage (e.g., step-down vs. high-dependency units), nurse-to-patient ratios, and precedence for scarce interventions. Validated prospectively and incorporated into clinical algorithms, the AISI might facilitate a paradigm shift from generalized care toward personalized, risk-adjusted therapeutic tactics, maximizing patient outcomes while ensuring judicious resource use.

The identified nonlinear threshold phenomenon likely reflects interconnected biological mechanisms. AISI amalgamates the core elements of innate immunity: neutrophils (mediating acute inflammation and oxidative stress), lymphocytes (reflecting adaptive immune function and immunosuppression), platelets (orchestrating thrombo-inflammatory crosstalk and microvascular compromise), and monocytes (serving as macrophage precursors and cytokine generators). Beneath this threshold, inflammation typically remains within an adaptive, homeostatic range that facilitates microbial clearance and tissue repair without causing substantial collateral damage [24]. However, exceeding this limit may trigger a destructive cascade: excessive neutrophil activation and NETosis is associated with endothelial injury, lymphopenia may increase susceptibility to secondary infections, thrombocytopenia may compromise hemostatic function, and monocyte dysregulation may contribute to hypercytokinemia. This unchecked systemic inflammatory response may be associated with microcirculatory dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, progression to MODS, and increased mortality. this threshold may represent a point beyond which the inflammatory response transitions from a predominantly adaptive to a potentially maladaptive state, aligning with the recognized “cytokine storm” model in severe illness.

The two inflection points (log₂-AISI 5.410 and 5.407) were numerically close, confirming threshold stability. Although pediatric immune reference ranges shift with age [25, 26], this cutoff was derived from a large critically ill pediatric cohort using data-driven methods, and age-stratified analysis detected no significant interaction, supporting its applicability across the PICU population. Independent pediatric studies of pneumonia and bacterial infections have further corroborated AISI’s prognostic value [27]. Nevertheless, this threshold represents a population-level estimate; whether age-specific cutoffs exist requires validation in larger, finely stratified cohorts.

Our study has several inherent limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, the retrospective design precludes definitive causal inference; unmeasured confounders—such as genetic variants affecting inflammatory responses and nuanced illness severity indicators—may persist despite comprehensive statistical adjustments. Second, reliance on a single-center database introduces potential selection bias, necessitating external validation across diverse geographic regions and institutional settings. Third, the AISI derivation used only admission laboratory results, representing a solitary inflammatory snapshot; its longitudinal trajectory during the PICU stay and temporal connection to mortality hazard remain unexamined. Fourth, although robust thresholds were determined, the biological basis for the threshold at log₂-AISI ≈ 5.41 warrants further mechanistic investigation. Furthermore, the unified log₂-AISI cutoff identified in this study may not apply uniformly across all pediatric developmental stages, warranting age-stratified verification in future work. The molecular basis of this threshold also requires further experimental investigation. Fifth, the cohort’s modest mortality incidence (in-hospital: 4.3%; in-ICU: 4.2%), while indicative of modern outcomes, might restrict the statistical strength for identifying minor subgroup variations. Sixth, insufficient granularity concerning specific treatments, immunomodulatory agents, or care withdrawal decisions could impact the observed relationships.

This large retrospective cohort analysis demonstrates a marked nonlinear association between AISI and 28-day all-cause mortality among critically ill children, characterized by a threshold effect at log₂-AISI ≈ 5.41. Values exceeding this threshold independently predict an increased risk of mortality, both in hospital and in ICU. These findings position AISI as a viable, readily accessible prognostic indicator in pediatric critical care medicine that can be incorporated into clinical workflows to facilitate early identification of high-risk patients and guide appropriate interventions. Future prospective validation, mechanistic studies, and randomized controlled trials are needed to facilitate the integration of AISI into clinical practice and ultimately improve outcomes for critically ill pediatric patients.
