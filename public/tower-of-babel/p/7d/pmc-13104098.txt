Diabetes, hyperglycemia, and ATN blood biomarkers in Hispanic/Latino populations: SOL‐INCA study
Authors: Kevin A. González, Wassim Tarraf, Judy Pa, Sarah J. Banks, Katherine J. Bangen, Douglas Galasko, Linda C. Gallo, Ariana M. Stickel, Natasha Z. Anita, Freddie Márquez, Paola Filigrana, Carmen R. Isasi, Martha Daviglus, Fernando D. Testai, Melissa Lamar, Charles DeCarli, Hector M. González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONResearch studying associations between plasma amyloid‐tau‐neurodegeneration (ATN) biomarkers and vascular factors, such as diabetes, is limited. We sought to examine associations between diabetes status and plasma ATN biomarkers in diverse Hispanic/Latino individuals.METHODSWe used data from the Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos and the Study of Latinos‐Investigation of Neurocognitive Aging ancillary studies. Our exposures included diabetes status and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) percentage. Outcomes included amyloid beta (Aβ) 42/40 ratio, phosphorylated tau 181 (p‐tau181), neurofilament light chain (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP).RESULTSThe final analytic sample was N = 6264. Diabetes, but not prediabetes, was associated with higher NfL and p‐tau181 and lower Aβ42/40 ratio. Elevated HbA1c percentage was associated with higher NfL, p‐tau181, and GFAP and lower Aβ42/40.DISCUSSIONDiabetes was associated with plasma biomarkers of Alzheimer's disease pathology and non‐specific neurodegeneration. Reducing diabetes prevalence could be beneficial for lowering dementia risk in this population.

BACKGROUND

By 2060, Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRDs) are expected to double in the United States, 1 and the Hispanic/Latino population is expected to reach 120 million. 2 Hispanic/Latino communities are rapidly aging and are at increased risk for ADRDs compared to non‐Hispanic White communities. 3 This may be partly due to a higher prevalence of some cardiovascular disease risk factors, such as diabetes. 4 Relationships between diabetes and worse cognitive outcomes and brain atrophy have been reported. However, evidence linking diabetes with pathological markers of Alzheimer's disease (AD; i.e., amyloid and tau) remains mixed. 5 One meta‐analysis found that diabetes was associated with increased positron emission tomography (PET) tau and lower PET amyloid. 6 These studies were conducted in small, highly selective clinical samples of White individuals, so it is unclear whether these findings are generalizable to the broader population, including Hispanic/Latino communities. Existing work has also relied on PET or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) modalities, which are considered gold standards for measuring AD pathology. However, these techniques are invasive and cost‐prohibitive, making them impractical for use in large epidemiological studies. Plasma amyloid‐tau‐neurodegeneration (ATN) biomarkers provide a more cost‐effective alternative to assess ADRD pathology using key markers of disease. Since they are less invasive and expensive than PET imaging or lumbar puncture for CSF collection, they could potentially be used to monitor disease progression. Early detection is particularly important in Hispanic/Latino individuals due to potentially earlier dementia onset compared to Black and White individuals. 7

A few studies examined associations between diabetes and ADRD plasma biomarkers, with mixed results. Overall, existing evidence suggests that diabetes may be more strongly connected to plasma biomarkers indicative of non‐AD‐specific pathology (e.g., plasma neurofilament light chain [NfL] and total tau [t‐tau] 8 , 9 ) rather than more AD‐specific pathology (e.g., plasma Aβ42/40 ratio 10 ). The Health and Aging Brain Study: Health Disparities (HABS‐HD) and the Mayo Clinic of Study of aging found an association between diabetes and plasma t‐tau when examining only cognitively healthy participants. 11 , 12 Plasma t‐tau, however, has shown mixed associations with AD, with some studies even showing lower t‐tau concentrations in AD individuals. 13 More work with more reliable AD plasma markers, such as phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) 181 or p‐tau217, is necessary. Lastly, results from the HABS‐HD suggest that, among Mexican American individuals, elevated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) is associated with increased NfL concentrations, but not a lower Aβ42/40 ratio. 9 Little is known about the relationship between diabetes and plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a marker sensitive for both Aβ and vascular pathology. 14 , 15

Examining possible sex differences is important, given these have considerable implications for cardiovascular disease risk, 16 accelerated brain aging, and late‐life cognitive impairments. 17 Uncovering sex‐specific pathways is critical for understanding cognitive and vascular health. Some evidence suggests plasma NfL and t‐tau concentrations may have sex‐specific trajectories, 18 such that women tend to have a higher plasma t‐tau burden compared to men. Another study found that, in amyloid‐positive individuals, men had steeper trajectories (more concentration) in p‐tau181 and NfL 19 ; however the opposite was found in a PET study. 20 Plasma p‐tau181 also seems to be more predictive of AD in women compared to men. 21 Thus, examining possible sex differences in the relationship between diabetes and plasma biomarkers could be important for better understanding sex‐specific risk for ADRD and potentially help explain higher risk for ADRD in women. 17

In this study, we examined the relationship between diabetes and plasma ATN biomarkers (i.e., NfL, p‐tau181, Aβ42/40 ratio, and GFAP) among a large, diverse Hispanic/Latino population. We hypothesized that diabetes and higher HbA1c percentage levels would be more strongly associated with non‐specific plasma biomarkers of neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation (NfL and GFAP) compared to AD‐specific biomarkers (p‐tau181 and Aβ42/40). While evidence is mixed, we hypothesized that the association between diabetes and p‐tau181 would be more pronounced in women than men.

METHODS

Data

We used data from the Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos (HCHS/SOL), which provided rich data on diverse community‐residing Hispanic/Latino populations. HCHS/SOL is a prospective study of persons of Cuban, Central American, Dominican, Mexican, Puerto Rican, and South American backgrounds across four major cities in the United States: San Diego, CA; Miami, FL; Chicago, IL; New York (Bronx), NY. The goal of HCHS/SOL and its numerous ancillary studies is to provide data that allow for deep characterization of risks for disease among an aging Hispanic/Latino cohort. Between 2008 and 2011, the first visit of the study recruited N = 16,415 self‐reported Hispanic/Latino individuals (18 to 74 years). All testing centers received Institutional Review Board approvals, and participants provided informed written consent in their preferred language. Survey design and methodology can be found at the HCHS/SOL website https://sites.cscc.unc.edu/hchs and are also published elsewhere. 22 , 23

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We used PubMed and Google Scholar to identify studies that examined associations between diabetes and plasma amyloid‐tau‐neurodegeneration (ATN) biomarkers. Several recent papers examined associations between diabetes and ATN biomarkers, but this area is vastly understudied. We also could not find papers studying associations between diabetes and plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein. Interpretation: Our findings provide evidence for both AD‐ and non‐AD‐specific pathology in diverse Hispanic/Latino populations. These findings provide support for AD pathology in diabetes, which had been inconsistent in previous diabetes and ATN plasma biomarker studies. Future directions: We believe our findings help establish early work for examining mechanistic pathways between diabetes and brain atrophy. Future work should examine mediative models whereby plasma biomarkers mediate associations between diabetes and cognitive outcomes.

The Study of Latinos/Investigation of Neurocognitive Aging (SOL‐INCA) is an ancillary study of HCHS/SOL. The goal of SOL‐INCA is to study AD, vascular, sociocultural, and genetic risk factors for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and ADRD in a diverse Hispanic/Latino cohort. The SOL‐INCA study recruited N = 6377 individuals who were 50 years old at the time of their second HCHS/SOL visit (on average 7 years from visit 1; response rate = 88.7%). Survey weights were created to adjust for non‐response bias and allow estimates to generalize to the SOL‐INCA target population in the HCHS/SOL sampling area.

Analytic sample

A total of N = 6377 participants were included in the SOL‐INCA study. The current analytic sample excluded N = 117 participants for missing covariates or exposure. Sample sizes differed by biomarker due to the exclusion of extreme values, blood specimen availability, and processing success. The final sample sizes for each of the biomarkers were as follows: N NfL = 6028, N p‐tau181 = 5733, N Aβ42/40 = 5608, and N GFAP = 5848. A full population derivation flowchart is available in Figure S1.

Blood collection

A standardized protocol across sites was used to obtain blood specimens from participants. EDTA plasma biospecimens were processed between 0 and 15 min after blood extraction. Samples were centrifuged and then stored in a −80°C freezer. Samples were then shipped to the central laboratory at the University of Minnesota, where a repository of biospecimens is stored for future use. 24

Plasma biomarker assay

EDTA plasma was used to assess biomarker concentrations. All plasma biomarkers were measured in picograms per milliliter (pg/mL). NfL, Aβ, and GFAP markers were measured with a HD‐X Analyzer (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) using a Multiplex Simoa Assay. P‐tau181 was analyzed with a HD‐X Analyzer (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) using a Singleplex Simoa Assay. A subsample of participants was retested to ensure reliability and replicability of measurements. Values that were above or below the detection threshold were censored to the highest or lowest detectable value, respectively. For this study, we excluded censored values.

Outcomes

We included the following plasma biomarkers measured at visit 2: Aβ42/40, p‐tau181, NfL, and GFAP. All biomarkers were treated continuously, and censored values were excluded. Additionally, p‐tau181, NfL, and GFAP were log transformed due to their skewed distribution.

Exposures

The main exposures of interest were diabetes status and HbA1c percentage measured at visit 1. Diabetes status (no diabetes, prediabetes, or diabetes) was determined using American Diabetes Association guidelines. 25 Diabetes was operationalized as follows: (1) post‐oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL, (2) fasting glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL if fasting time < 8 h, (3) fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL if fasting time > 8 h, (4) HbA1c ≥ 6.5% or 5%), or (5) self‐reported diabetes or medication use. Prediabetes was operationalized as follows: (1) post‐OGTT glucose 140 to 199 mg/dL, (2) fasting glucose 100 to 125 mg/dL if fasting time > 8 h, or (3) HbA1c 5.7% to 6.5%.

Covariates

Covariates included age at visit 2, sex (male, female), Hispanic/Latino background (Dominican, Puerto Rican, South American, Mexican, Central American, Cuban), education (less than high school, high school, more than high school), testing center (San Diego, Chicago, New York [Bronx], Miami), continuous body mass index (BMI; kg/m2), physical activity intensity (inactive, low activity, medium activity, high activity), chronic kidney disease (normal, low, medium, high, end stage), hypertension (no hypertension, hypertension; based on national health and nutrition examination survey definition), and self‐reported stroke or transient ischemic attacks. All covariates, except for age, were assessed at visit 1.

Analytic plan

We used Stata 18.0 software to perform all analyses. We included study survey weights to account for inclusion bias and ensure results were based on target population statistics. First, we generated descriptive statistics by sex across all our covariates of interest (Table 1). We reported means and standard deviations for continuous measures; we reported column‐wise prevalence and standard errors for categorical measures. To test for possible differences by sex, we performed both survey‐adjusted chi‐squared and F‐tests for categorical and continuous measures, respectively. Next, we used survey weighted linear models to examine the relationship between our exposures and outcomes of interest. We ran an unadjusted model (labeled M0); a model that adjusted for age, sex, Hispanic/Latino background, education, and field center (labeled M1); and a model that additionally adjusted for BMI, physical activity, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, and self‐reported stroke or transient ischemic attacks (labeled M2). The non‐standardized adjusted beta estimates and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for both diabetes status and continuous HbA1c percentage, relative to the biomarkers of interest, are presented in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. We also estimated an “age decline” equivalent by dividing the beta coefficient between exposure and outcome with the beta coefficient between age and outcome. To aid in the interpretation of findings, we plotted marginal estimates with 95% CIs (Figure 1 and Figure S2). We tested for possible sex modifications on the associations between diabetes and plasma biomarkers. We ran stratified models by sex for biomarkers with significant interactions in the linear regression models between diabetes status and sex (i.e., diabetes × sex). These results are presented in Table 4. We included supplemental models using non‐log‐transformed exposures, and these findings are included in Table S1. We ran a set of non‐log‐transformed plasma biomarker models with exclusion for plasma values more than three standard deviations from the mean. Results can be found in Table S2. ANOVA F‐test results from sex interaction models can be found in Table S3.

RESULTS

Target population characteristics

Weighted characteristics of the target population are presented in Table 1. Nearly half of the target population (46%) had prediabetes, and almost one in three individuals (28%) had diabetes. On average, women were slightly older than men and had higher BMI, and men were more likely to engage in high physical activity.

Linear models

Results from linear models for the categorical and continuous models can be found in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. Results presented below represent estimates from the fully adjusted model (M2). Compared to no diabetes, meeting criteria for diabetes was associated with increased plasma NfL (b NfL = 0.253, 95% CI: [0.197, 0.309], p < 0.001) and p‐tau181 (b p‐tau181 = 0.091, 95% CI: [0.045, 0.137], p < 0.001) concentrations. The presence of diabetes was also associated with lower Aβ42/40 (b Aβ42/40 = −0.002, 95% CI: [−0.003, −0.001], p < 0.05). Elevated HbA1c percentage was positively associated with NfL (b NfL = 0.138, 95% CI: [0.122, 0.154], p < 0.001), p‐tau181 (b p‐tau181 = 0.063, 95% CI: [0.049; 0.078], p < 0.001), and GFAP (b GFAP = 0.023, 95% CI: [0.009; 0.036], p < 0.01). Elevated HbA1c percentage was negatively associated with Aβ42/40 (b Aβ42/40 = −7.0e−4 [−1.1e−3, ‐3.5e−4], p < 0.001). Results using the non‐log‐transformed measures were consistent with main findings (Table S1) and in the model with only values within three standard deviations (Table S2).

Sex interactions

We found significant modifications by sex for plasma p‐tau181 (F = 4.322, p = 0.014), but not for the other biomarkers (Table S3). In stratified models, diabetes was associated with increased p‐tau181 concentrations in men, but not women (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

In a study of over 6000 mid‐life to later‐life Hispanic/Latino individuals, we found that diabetes status and dysregulated glucose metabolism (higher HbA1c percentage levels) were associated with increased plasma ATN biomarker concentrations. We found that diabetes and higher HbA1c percentage levels were related to plasma biomarkers of AD as well as non‐specific neuropathology among diverse Hispanics/Latino individuals. Specifically, diabetes and higher HbA1c percentage levels were associated with elevated p‐tau181 concentration and lower Aβ42/40 levels, which are suggestive of AD pathology. Additionally, diabetes and higher HbA1c percentage were related to higher NfL concentration, a marker of axonal injury that is non‐specifically elevated with neurodegeneration. We also found that higher glycemic levels (HbA1c percentage levels), but not diabetes status, were associated with increased GFAP concentration. Taken together, these findings suggest that diabetes may be related to increased concentrations of biomarkers often associated with AD and non‐AD‐specific pathology.

We found evidence for elevated plasma NfL among individuals with diabetes, consistent with previous research. 8 , 9 Plasma NfL is a marker of non‐specific neurodegeneration 26 and may predict both worsening of brain integrity (assessed via magnetic resonance imaging) and cognitive decline. 27 Elevated NfL may not only indicate brain neurodegeneration but could also serve as a marker of peripheral nervous system damage. 28 This is important in the context of diabetes given that common sequelae of diabetes are diabetic neuropathies. 29 Future work could account for this by including markers of diabetic neuropathy, such as vascular endothelial growth factor. 30

We also found a relationship between diabetes and higher levels of p‐tau181, an AD‐specific marker of tau and amyloid pathology. 31 , 32 This association is consistent with plasma studies that used t‐tau in cognitively healthy participants, 11 , 12 as well as PET and CSF studies. 6 We also found a relationship between diabetes and lower levels of Aβ42/40, a marker sensitive to brain amyloid burden. 33 Our findings contradict those of previous work, which reported no association between diabetes and Aβ42/40 in a Mexican American population. 9 In our study, we found that diabetes versus normoglycemia was associated with 9‐year equivalent decrease in Aβ42/40, and one percentage point increase in HbA1c percentage was associated with 3.3‐year equivalent decrease in Aβ42/40. Interventions that aim to lower diabetes prevalence could be especially beneficial in Hispanic/Latino individuals, who often have lower access to healthcare and treatment compared to non‐Hispanic Whites. 34 Some evidence suggests that untreated diabetes, but not treated diabetes, is associated with increased p‐tau in CSF. 35 We also found in our population that only those with poorly controlled diabetes, but not those with controlled diabetes, had lower domain‐specific and global cognitive scores compared to those without diabetes. 36 Thus, healthcare interventions that allow diabetes awareness and treatment could be ameliorate ADRD disparities in this population. Lastly, elevated HbA1c percentage, but not diabetes, was associated with elevated GFAP concentrations. Hyperglycemia increases the risk of ischemic stroke, potentially through mechanisms such as inflammation and oxidative stress. 37 Because elevated GFAP concentrations have been found in individuals with acute stroke, 38 diabetes could lead to increased GFAP concentrations through increased risk of stroke. However, given our results were inconsistent across categorical and continuous models, further work should help clarify the relationship between diabetes and GFAP.

Several mechanisms could explain the relationships between diabetes and elevated ATN biomarkers. Impaired brain signaling could directly promote amyloid and tau accumulation. 39 Amylin, a protein co‐secreted with insulin, has also been shown to increase AD risk at high concentrations. 40 , 41 , 42 There may also be indirect pathways: Diabetes could lead to increased amyloid production or increased leakage of amyloid to the blood through brain vascular damage. 43 While evidence is limited, one study provided evidence for a more direct relationship between diabetes and neurodegeneration. 44 The study found that diabetes was associated with neurodegeneration and that neurodegeneration mediated associations between diabetes and AD biomarkers. This model suggests that diabetes may lead to neurodegeneration through direct pathways (e.g., amylin or impair insulin signaling), which may then drive AD pathology and vascular disease. If neurodegeneration does precede AD markers, it could also explain why many studies have found a consistent relationship with NfL and weak or null relationships with amyloid or tau; NfL may be capturing earlier disease progression. Hispanic/Latino individuals have an earlier onset of AD than non‐Hispanic White individuals, 7 so NfL could be a potentially useful marker to track non‐specific neurodegenerative risk in the context of diabetes for this population. Future diabetes interventions and treatments should include ATN plasma biomarkers to assess reduction of ADRD risk, particularly in Hispanic/Latino communities.

We also found that the relationship between diabetes and plasma biomarkers was modified by sex. Diabetes was associated with elevated p‐tau181 in women and men, but associations were stronger in men. Our results are consistent with one study that found higher concentrations in men, although their study was only in those that were amyloid positive. 19 Contradictory to our findings, testosterone seems to be protective toward tau accumulation. 45 Some work suggests that women have lower blood‐brain‐barrier permeability, 46 which would be consistent with work showing that women have higher amyloid and t‐tau deposition at equivalent p‐tau181 concentrations. 21 This means that men and women could have similar levels of tau, but concentration in brain and plasma could be different. These results suggest that sex differences work using only plasma may be limited, and work that combines PET or CSF with plasma may be necessary to fully account for these differences in brain and plasma concentrations.

Our study had several strengths and weaknesses. First, as for the strengths, we used a large dataset representative of diverse Hispanic/Latino communities. Second, we examined relationships between diabetes and AD‐specific and non‐specific plasma biomarkers. This provides us with a more comprehensive understanding of ADRD risk from diabetes and may suggest multiple mechanisms that could be potential treatment targets. We note some weaknesses of our work. First, we did not have PET or CSF data. As such, we could not validate our findings against a gold standard. Second, additional markers like p‐tau217 have been developed that may demonstrate higher sensitivity to tau brain deposition compared to p‐tau181. 47 However, p‐tau181 still has been shown to be a reliable indicator. 47 Further work should also connect ATN plasma biomarkers to brain and cognitive endpoints, particularly in diverse populations. While we had adjusted for important confounders such as BMI, we may have missed other confounders. Notably, despite covariate adjustment, there are important considerations regarding some of our covariates, such as BMI. For example, whole body but not lean mass was associated with increased t‐tau concentrations. 48 We also did not account for baseline cognitive status, which could be an important confounder: plasma concentration trajectories could change based on cognitive status. Non‐inclusion bias is also another confounder; however, SOL‐INCA and HCHS/SOL created robust survey weights to account for this. Lastly, while we used survey weights to generalize our findings to our population of interest, our findings reflect findings within middle/late‐life Hispanic/Latino adults across four cities in the United States. Our findings may not generalize to the overall population or the overall Hispanic/Latino population. Further work should be performed to validate these findings in other populations.

CONCLUSION

Diabetes and elevated HbA1c percentage were associated with plasma biomarkers for AD and neurodegeneration in middle‐aged and older Hispanic/Latino individuals. ATN plasma biomarkers could help clinicians track ADRD risk in the context of diabetes.
