NDC80 promotes epithelial to mesenchymal transition of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma through macrophages polarization and PI3K/AKT pathway activation
Authors: Jiao Shu, Chenbo Yang, Zexin Sun, Yinghao Liang, Na Zhao, Xiaonan Liu, Yiwei Li, Muhammad Saud Tabish, Miaomiao Sun, Kuisheng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEsophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is a lethal malignancy with limited therapeutic options, primarily due to its aggressive metastatic behavior. This study aimed to elucidate the molecular drivers of ESCC metastasis by identifying a critical oncogene and investigate its functional mechanisms.MethodsUsing integrated bioinformatics screening, we identified NDC80 as a potential regulator of metastasis. The role and underlying mechanisms of NDC80 in ESCC progression have remained to be fully elucidated. We conducted in vitro functional assays—including real-time PCR, western blotting, and flow cytometry—to assess the effects of NDC80 on epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and malignant behavior. Mechanistic studies such as co-culture systems and ELISA, were used to evaluate tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) polarization, with a specific focus on the PI3K/AKT pathway. In vivo, we established subcutaneous xenograft models in immunocompromised mice to validate the impact of NDC80 on ESCC progression. Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) is a major global health concern, ranking as the seventh most prevalent malignancy globally with annual new cases exceeding 576,000 [1–4]. Histologically, approximately 90% of EC cases are attributed to ESCC, a subtype characterized by aggressive behavior and a dismal prognosis, accounting for more than 538,000 deaths each year [2]. The high mortality rate primarily stems from late-stage diagnosis due to late-stage diagnosis, a consequence of the absence of early clinical symptoms and the tumor's propensity for rapid metastasis [5, 6]. Despite therapeutic advances, ESCC remains a highly aggressive and often fatal disease with an exceedingly poor prognosis. The molecular drivers of ESCC metastasis are still poorly understood, significantly hampering the development of targeted treatments. Therefore, elucidating the underlying mechanisms is critical for identifying novel therapeutic targets and improving clinical outcomes.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are established core drivers of ESCC metastasis, with emerging evidence highlighting their intimate crosstalk within the tumor microenvironment [7–9]. M2-TAMs facilitate tumor progression by promoting angiogenesis and immune evasion, and critically, through secreting cytokines that directly induce EMT, thereby enhancing invasive and metastatic potential [10, 11]. Mechanistically, factors derived from M2-TAMs activate the PI3K/AKT pathway, which stabilizes key EMT transcription factors and amplifies metastatic behavior. These findings collectively establish the TAM-PI3K/AKT-EMT axis as a compelling therapeutic target in ESCC [12]. However, how tumor cells actively initiate and regulate this pro-metastatic crosstalk, and the key upstream molecular drivers orchestrating this process, remain incompletely understood.

The nuclear division cycle protein 80 (NDC80) gene encodes a vital component of the conserved kinetochore complex, which ensures mitotic fidelity through precise chromosome segregation [13]. Beyond this canonical role in cell division, NDC80 is overexpressed in multiple malignancies, where it correlates with aggressive tumor behavior, metastatic progression, and poor clinical outcomes [14–19]. Mechanistic studies have implicated NDC80 in oncogenesis through diverse pathways. For example, its binding partner NUF2 activated the ERBB3-mediated PI3K-AKT pathway to promote ovarian cancer [20]. While in gastric cancer, NUF2 was shown to drive tumor development by mediating G2/M phase transition and suppressing apoptosis [14]. Furthermore, NDC80/HEC1 has been reported to promote microglial migration by inducing M1 macrophage transformation [17]. Although these findings nominate NDC80 as a promising therapeutic and diagnostic target across multiple cancers, its specific role and mechanistic contributions in ESCC pathogenesis remain incompletely understood.

Through integrated bioinformatics analysis, we identified NDC80 as a key regulator of EMT in ESCC. To elucidate the underlying mechanism, we investigated the role of NDC80 in cellular migration, and invasion, with a specific focus on its functional connection to the PI3K/AKT pathway, macrophages polarization, and EMT. Clinically, elevated NDC80 expression was significantly correlated with adverse prognosis and reduced patient survival. Based on our findings, we hypothesize that NDC80 drives M2 macrophages polarization via M-CSF/CXCL-2 secretion, which subsequently activates the PI3K/AKT pathway to promote EMT and metastasis in ESCC.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

Between January 2019 and December 2020, tumor tissues and paired adjacent normal esophageal tissues were collected from 61 patients diagnosed with ESCC via pathology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. None of the patients had received radiotherapy or chemotherapy prior to surgical resection. Clinical medical records and survival data were obtained for all participants. This study was approved by the Hospital's Ethics Committee (No. 2024-KY-0252–002). Sample and data collection began following ethical approval in August 2024.

Cell culture

Cell lines and culture: The human ESCC cell line EC-1 and the human monocyte THP-1 were obtained from the Henan Province Key Laboratory of Tumor Pathology. The complete culture medium contained 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Vazyme, China) and 10% fetal bovine serum (Vazyme, China). The ESCC cell line was cultivated in DMEM medium, whereas the THP-1 cell line was grown in RPMI 1640 medium. All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a cell culture incubator. The authenticity of the cell lines employed in this research was confirmed through Short Tandem Repeat (STR) analysis.

Macrophages differentiation and polarization: THP-1 cells were treated with Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (Sigma, USA, 100ng/mL, P1585-1MG) and cultured for 24 h to activate into macrophages. For polarization, the resulting macrophages were then induced towards an M1 phenotype by treatment with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Sigma, USA, 100ng/mL, L2630) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) (Peprotech, USA, 20ng/mL, 300–02–20). Alternatively, an M2 phenotype was induced by treatment with interleukin-4 (IL-4) at a concentration of 20ng/mL (Peprotech, USA, catalog #200–04-5UG) and interleukin-13 (IL-13) at the same dosage (Peprotech, USA, catalog #200–13-2UG). All macrophages used in subsequent experiments were derived from this THP-1 differentiation protocol.

Co-culture experiments: non-contact co-culture system was established using Transwell chambers (FALCON, USA, #353,090) to investigate the crosstalk between ESCC cells and macrophages. ESCC cells and macrophages were seeded in the lower and upper compartments, respectively, to assess the impact of tumor cells on macrophage polarization. To examine the role of the PI3K/AKT pathway in the EMT process, a specific PI3K inhibitor (LY294002; MCE, USA, 20 µM) was added to the culture medium.

Cell transfection

The NDC80 encoding sequence was inserted into a vector to construct an NDC80 overexpression plasmid (OE), while an empty vector was served as a vehicle control (VE). NDC80-specific short hairpin RNAs (sh-NDC80)(sh-NDC80-1: 5′-GCCATTCTTGACCAGAAATTA-3′, sh-NDC80-2: 5′-TGCAGACATTGAGCGAATAAA-3′, sh-NDC80-3: 5′-GAAGGACCCGAGACCACTTAA-3′) and a non-targeting sequence (5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3′) as a control group (NC) were used. Plasmids were purchased from GenePharma (China). Utilizing a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50, lentiviral transduction was conducted. The plasmids were packaged into lentiviruses, and after infecting tumor cells, resistant selection was performed to establish stable transfected cell lines.

Real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from cells with TRIzol reagent (Vazyme, China). cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR kit (Vazyme, China); this kit incorporates a thermolabile DNase step to remove genomic DNA contamination. Gene expression was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). The primers, sourced from Primer Bank and validated in silico with Primer-BLAST, were synthesized and purified by Shanghai Biotechnology Corporation (China). Relative quantification of mRNA expression was determined via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, and all primer sequences are provided in Table 1.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted using pre-cooled RIPA lysis buffer (Cwbio, China), and concentrations were quantified with a BCA assay kit (Cwbio, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE on 8%, 10%, or 12.5% gels (selected based on the target protein's molecular weight) and transferred to PVDF membranes. These membranes underwent a blocking step with 5% non-fat dry milk for a duration of 2 h. Incubation with primary antibodies occurred overnight at 4°C: NDC80 antibody (abcam, UK, dilution 1:10,000, ab109496); E-Cadherin antibody (Affinity, USA, dilution 1:1000, AF0131); Vimentin antibody (abcam, UK, dilution 1:3000, ab92547); N-Cadherin antibody (abcam, UK, dilution 1:8000, ab76011); GAPDH antibody (Proteintech, China, dilution 1:10,000, 10,494–1-AP); PI3K antibody (CST, USA, dilution 1:1000, 4257 T); AKT antibody (CST, USA, dilution 1:1000, 4691 T); p-AKT antibody (CST, USA, dilution 1:2000, 4060 T). Following this, the PVDF membranes were exposed to HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, China, dilution 1:10,000; SA00001-2) for 2 h at ambient temperature. Densitometric analysis of each protein band was conducted utilizing Image J software (USA).

Cell migration and invasion assay

Cell migration and invasion assays were performed using Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size; BD Biosciences, USA) in 24-well plates (NEST, China). For the invasion assay, the chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel, whereas for the migration assay, they were left uncoated. After a 24-h incubation at 37 °C, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Non-migratory/invasive cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed with a cotton swab. Cells that had migrated or invaded to the bottom surface were imaged and counted under a microscope.

Flow cytometry analysis

Macrophages were stained for 30 min at 4 °C with the following fluorescently conjugated antibodies: PE anti-human CD163 (BioLegend, #333,605), FITC anti-human CD86 (BioLegend, #374,203), and APC anti-human CD11b (BioLegend, #301,309). The labeled cells were analyzed on a FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and the data were processed using FlowJo software, version 10.6 (BD Bioscience).

ELISA assay

The concentrations of cytokines M-CSF (E-EL-H0097) and CXCL-2 (E-EL-H1904) in the cell supernatants were detected using ELISA kits (Elabscience, China). Utilizing a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA), optical density (OD) values were recorded at a wavelength of 450 nm. Subsequently, standard curves were constructed to facilitate the calculation of cytokine concentrations in every sample group.

Immunohistochemical staining

Tissues were prepared as 4 μm sections and experiments were conducted according to the Universal SAP kit (Zsbio, China, SAP-9100). The sections were incubated with the following primary antibodies: NDC80 (Abcam, 1:500, #ab3613), E-Cadherin (Zsbio, 1:200, #ZA-0565), Vimentin (Zsbio, 1:200, #ZA-0511), and CD11c (Abcam, 1:500, #ab52632). After DAB development, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. All slides were examined under a microscope, and the staining was evaluated by a qualified pathologist.

Animal studies

A subcutaneous xenograft model was established using NOD-SCID nude mice (acquired from Vital River Laboratories, China). Each mouse was injected with a 100 μL suspension containing 5 × 10⁶ ESCC cells mixed with Matrigel (ABW, USA). When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice received an intravenous injection of 1 × 10⁶ M1 macrophages via the tail vein. The tumors were harvested 10 days post-injection.

Subcutaneous xenografts were established as above. After 14 days, different groups of mice received intratumoral injections (20 μL per injection) of concentrated supernatant derived from co-cultures of M1 macrophages with either NDC80-overexpressing or control ESCC cells. The supernatants were concentrated using ultrafiltration tubes (Millipore, UFC910096). Injections were administered once every three days for a total of four injections, after which the tumors were collected. All harvested tumors were subjected to subsequent Western blot analysis and immunohistochemical staining.

All mice were housed under temperature and humidity-controlled conditions. In order to respect the ethical rules for animals, when the experimental endpoint is reached or the nude mice show poor mental state and refusal to eat, the mice are euthanized by professional animal experimenters using cervical dislocation. The Experimental Animal Center at Zhengzhou University served as the venue for conducting the animal experiments, with ethical approval number ZZU-LAC20240322[04].

Bioinformatics analysis

Gene expression datasets for ESCC (GSE100942, GSE89102) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs; GSE59948) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the Limma package in R, with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute log fold change (|logFC|) ≥ 1 defined as significant. Key genes were subsequently identified through protein–protein interaction network analysis using STRING.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9 (provided by GraphPad Software Inc, USA) was utilized for both statistical analysis and the creation of graphical representations. Data are exhibited as mean values accompanied by standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between pairs of groups, the LSD-t test was employed, whereas evaluations involving three or more groups were carried out using a one-way ANOVA succeeded by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. The Kaplan–Meier approach was adopted for survival analysis purposes. A Cox proportional hazards model was established, from which hazard ratios were derived through exponentiating the coefficients of the model. Statistical significance was inferred when p-values fell below 0.05. It is noteworthy that all experimental procedures were replicated independently on three separate occasions.

Results

NDC80 was identified as a potential oncogenic driver in ESCC

TAMs play a pivotal role in ESCC development by modulating the tumor microenvironment. To identify key molecular mediators linking TAMs and ESCC pathogenesis, we performed an integrated bioinformatics analysis of three independent GEO datasets. This revealed 15 consistently differentially expressed genes common to both ESCC and TAM signatures (Fig. 1A). Subsequent protein–protein interaction network analysis using STRING identified NDC80 as a topologically central hub gene, suggesting its potential importance in TAM-mediated ESCC progression (Fig. 1B). As the specific role of NDC80 in ESCC remains unexplored, this study focuses on elucidating its function and underlying mechanisms.

NDC80 facilitated the EMT process in ESCC

The EMT is a pivotal mechanism that drives tumor metastasis by enhancing the migratory and invasive capabilities of cancer cells. Based on our finding that NDC80 acts as an oncogene in ESCC progression, we investigated its potential role in regulating EMT. Immunohistochemical analysis of tumor tissues showed that high NDC80 expression was associated with reduced E-cadherin and elevated Vimentin levels, suggesting EMT activation in these samples (Fig. 2A). This was supported by statistical analyses, which revealed a significant negative correlation between NDC80 and E-cadherin expression (P < 0.001), and a positive correlation with Vimentin (P = 0.007) (Table 2).

Correlation between NDC80 and EMT-related proteins

Functional validation in ESCC cell lines revealed that NDC80 overexpression (OE) significantly reduced the expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin and concurrently elevated the levels of mesenchymal markers, specifically N-cadherin and Vimentin (Fig. 2B). Conversely, shRNA-mediated knockdown of NDC80 (sh-NDC80) (S1 Fig. B) effectively attenuated EMT markers (Fig. 2C). These molecular changes corresponded to functional outcomes: transwell assays confirmed that NDC80 overexpression enhanced cell migration and invasion, whereas its suppression impaired these capabilities (Fig. 2D-E). Collectively, these results establish NDC80 as a critical regulator of EMT in ESCC, mechanistically linking its oncogenic function to the acquisition of metastatic potential.

NDC80 modulated tumor-associated macrophages polarization to promote ESCC progression

As key regulators of the tumor microenvironment, TAMs critically influence cancer development. Our integrated bioinformatics analysis initially suggested a potential association between NDC80 and TAMs in ESCC. To validate this, we first examined the M1 macrophage marker CD11c and the M2 marker CD206 in ESCC tissue samples. Immunohistochemistry revealed that tumors with high NDC80 expression displayed increased infiltration of CD206+ M2 macrophages and a corresponding decrease in CD11c+ M1 macrophages (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, NDC80 expression showed a significant positive correlation with M2 markers and an inverse correlation with M1 markers (Figs. 3B-C).

To further elucidate the role of NDC80 in macrophages polarization, we established an in vitro co-culture system using THP-1-derived macrophages (S1 Fig. C-D, S2 Fig.) and genetically modified ESCC cells. Co-culture with NDC80-overexpressing (OE) ESCC cells significantly promoted M2 polarization, as evidenced by an increased CD11b+CD163+ cell population (Fig. 3D), elevated expression of M2-associated cytokines (IL-10, CCL-22), and a concurrent reduction in M1 markers (IL-1β, i-NOS) (Fig. 3E). Conversely, NDC80 knockdown reversed this effect, restoring M1-associated characteristics (Fig. 3F).

We next sought to identify the signaling factors through which NDC80 influences TAMs polarization. Screening a panel of TAM-related cytokines (CCL-2, M-CSF, CXCL-2, IL-10, TGF-β, TNF-α) revealed that NDC80 overexpression specifically enhanced the secretion of M-CSF and CXCL-2 (Fig. 3G, I), while its depletion markedly reduced these cytokines (Fig. 3H, J). Collectively, these results demonstrate that NDC80 orchestrates macrophages polarization by selectively regulating key cytokines, thereby promoting an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that facilitates ESCC progression.

NDC80-mediated M2 macrophages polarization drove EMT in ESCC

M2 macrophages exert pro-tumorigenic effects that facilitate cancer progression, including metastasis and immune evasion. Having demonstrated that NDC80-overexpressing ESCC cells undergo EMT and induce M2 macrophages polarization, we next investigated whether this NDC80-driven macrophages reprogramming could, in turn, enhance EMT. In co-culture experiments, supernatants from NDC80-induced TAMs promoted EMT in recipient ESCC cells, as shown by increased N-cadherin and decreased E-cadherin expression (Fig. 4A). This molecular shift corresponded with enhanced tumor cell migration and invasion (Fig. 4B).

To validate the role of NDC80 in macrophages polarization-related EMT in vivo, we established subcutaneous xenograft models in NOD-SCID mice using control (VE) and NDC80-overexpressing (OE) ESCC cells (Fig. 4C). NDC80 overexpression significantly accelerated tumor growth (Figs. 4D-E), increased the secretion of M2-polarizing cytokines (M-CSF and CXCL-2) (Fig. 4F), and promoted the infiltration of CD163+ M2 macrophages (Figs. 4G-H). Furthermore, NDC80-expressing tumors exhibited a pronounced EMT phenotype, characterized by elevated Vimentin and reduced E-cadherin levels, as confirmed by both immunohistochemistry and western blot (Figs. 4G, I).

Collectively, these results establish a feedforward loop in which NDC80-expressing tumor cells reprogram the tumor microenvironment via cytokine secretion. This mechanism coordinately drives both M2 macrophages polarization and EMT, two critical hallmarks of aggressive ESCC.

NDC80 drove the EMT process via the activation of PI3K/AKT pathway

Having established that NDC80-induced TAMs promote EMT, we sought to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanism. Provided that the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is widely recognized for its pivotal role in governing essential cellular functions such as growth, survival, and protein production, we investigated its involvement in NDC80-mediated TAMs polarization and EMT induction. NDC80-mediated M2 macrophages polarization upregulated the level of phosphorylated AKT protein in tumor cells (Fig. 5A), while the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 markedly attenuated the NDC80-mediated EMT process (Fig. 5B), suggesting that NDC80 promoted M2 macrophages polarization and the subsequent EMT via PI3K/AKT pathway.

We further validated this mechanism in vivo. Concentrated supernatants from co-cultures of TAMs with ESCC cells were injected into established subcutaneous xenograft tumors to assess their effect on tumor growth (Fig. 5C). While supernatants from M1 macrophages enhanced tumor growth, those from M1 macrophages co-cultured with NDC80-overexpressing (OE) ESCC cells induced the most pronounced effect (Figs. 5D-G). In these tumors, NDC80 overexpression also led to significant EMT marker alterations, with decreased E-cadherin and increased Vimentin at both the transcriptional and protein levels (Figs. 5H-I). Furthermore, NDC80 overexpression activated the PI3K/AKT pathway, as evidenced by an elevated p-AKT/AKT ratio in the xenograft tumors (Fig. 5J).

These findings delineated a mechanistic cascade whereby NDC80-overexpressing ESCC cells polarized macrophages toward an M2 phenotype, which in turn activated the PI3K/AKT pathway to facilitate EMT and drove metastatic progression in ESCC.

NDC80 was overexpressed in ESCC and positively associated with poor clinical prognosis

To characterize the clinical significance of NDC80 in ESCC, we analyzed 61 paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues. Immunohistochemistry revealed pronounced NDC80 overexpression in ESCC specimens compared to matched normal tissues (Fig. 6A). This finding was corroborated at the cellular level, with consistently elevated NDC80 expression observed across multiple ESCC cell lines (KYSE-180, KYSE-270, KYSE-510, KYSE-70, KYSE-30, EC-1, TE-1) relative to normal esophageal epithelial cells (HET-1A) (S1 Fig. A).

Correlation between NDC80 and clinicopathology

Critically, survival analysis revealed that high NDC80 expression was a strong predictor of poor overall survival, identifying NDC80 as a prognostic biomarker in our cohort (Fig. 6B-C). Multivariate Cox regression analysis, adjusted for key demographic and clinicopathological parameters, confirmed that high NDC80 expression (HR = 11.917, 95% CI: 3.170–44.806, p < 0.001) is an independent prognostic factor for ESCC (Fig. 6D). To further validate this, we assessed NDC80 in an external cohort, where Kaplan–Meier analysis confirmed that high NDC80 expression was significantly associated with reduced recurrence-free survival (Fig. 6E) [21, 22].

Collectively, these findings establish NDC80 as a clinically relevant oncoprotein in ESCC, whose overexpression drives aggressive tumor behavior and serves as a powerful predictor of adverse disease outcomes.

Discussion

ESCC remains a predominant gastrointestinal malignancy worldwide, characterized by aggressive metastasis, frequent recurrence, and poor survival, despite ongoing advances from conventional to precision therapies [23]. This persistent challenge underscores the urgent need to define the molecular drivers of ESCC progression. Through integrated bioinformatics, we identified NDC80 as a potential novel risk factor in ESCC. Although NDC80 has been implicated in various other cancers, including gastric cancer [14], lung cancer [24], ovarian cancer [20], breast cancer [25, 26], liver cancer [27], pancreatic cancer [28] and glioma [17], its role in ESCC has been largely uncharacterized. While traditional microtubule-targeting agents (e.g., vinca alkaloids and taxanes) have long been used to disrupt mitotic fidelity [29], their dose-limiting toxicities and lack of tumor specificity have motivated the development of more selective anti-mitotic drugs. Among these, small-molecule inhibitors targeting NDC80 [30, 31] (such as VVT-006 [32]), a key regulator of mitosis and tumor progression, have emerged, advancing early drug discovery and target validation in mitosis research. Nevertheless, the precise role and underlying mechanisms of NDC80 in ESCC has remained unexplored. Although Yang et al. [33] previously proposed NDC80 as a hub gene via bioinformatic analysis, direct experimental evidence and mechanistic insight were lacking. Here, we not only validated NDC80 overexpression in ESCC tissues and its correlation with adverse prognosis, but also demonstrated—through integrated in vitro and in vivo models—that NDC80 critically promotes ESCC progression by driving M2 macrophages polarization, activating the PI3K/AKT pathway, and inducing EMT.

The highly metastatic nature of ESCC is inextricably linked to the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Our study now directly identifies NDC80 overexpression as a key driver of this pivotal process [34]. Clinically, elevated NDC80 expression correlated significantly with deeper tumor invasion, lymph node metastasis, and vascular infiltration in ESCC patients, highlighting its relevance to metastatic progression. Experimentally, modulating NDC80 expression effectively controlled the EMT phenotype: overexpression enhanced ESCC cell migration and invasion by downregulating E-cadherin and upregulating N-cadherin and Vimentin, whereas NDC80 knockdown produced the opposite effects. These findings firmly establish NDC80 as a critical upstream regulator of EMT in ESCC.

Consistent with the established pro-tumorigenic role of M2-polarized TAMs in ESCC [35, 36], we observed significant enrichment of M2 macrophages in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal samples. Expanding on this, we identified NDC80 as a novel upstream regulator of this immunosuppressive microenvironment. Clinically, high NDC80 expression strongly correlated with increased M2 and decreased M1 macrophages infiltration. Through integrated in vitro and in vivo studies, we demonstrated that NDC80-overexpressing tumor cells promote M2 polarization via secretion of M-CSF and CXCL-2. While NDC80 has been implicated in immune modulation in other cancers, including glioma [17], liver [37, 38], and renal cell carcinoma [39], its role in ESCC-associated TAMs education had not been previously reported. Most importantly, our results reveal a pivotal mechanistic connection: NDC80-induced M2 macrophages drive EMT in ESCC cells through activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. We further demonstrate that the PI3K/AKT cascade serves as the critical downstream effector, transducing signals from NDC80-reprogrammed M2 macrophages to activate the EMT program in tumor cells, thereby establishing a self-reinforcing pro-metastatic circuit.

Our study moves beyond the conventional somatic mutation paradigm that has long dominated ESCC research despite limited therapeutic advances [40, 41]. Rather than merely identifying another oncogene, we demonstrate that NDC80 exerts a systemic role by remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment through macrophage polarization. This provides a novel systems-biological perspective, positioning NDC80 as a central hub linking tumor-intrinsic properties to the external ecosystem. Furthermore, NDC80 acts as an ecological niche engineer—not just a driver gene. Overexpressing tumor cells actively shape their microenvironment by secreting M-CSF and CXCL-2 to recruit and polarize M2-type tumor-associated macrophages, effectively turning immune "predators" into "collaborators". This realigned cellular community subsequently activates the PI3K/AKT pathway in tumor cells, promoting EMT and establishing a self-reinforcing pro-metastatic feedback loop. Thus, NDC80 functions as an ecosystem engineer that fosters a malignant niche supporting tumor progression and metastasis.

In conclusion, our study establishes NDC80 as a clinically significant oncogenic driver in ESCC. Mechanistically, we have delineated a novel signaling axis in which NDC80-expressing tumor cells secrete M-CSF and CXCL-2 to polarize macrophages toward a pro-tumorigenic M2 phenotype. These macrophages subsequently activate the PI3K/AKT pathway in tumor cells, promoting EMT and enhancing ESCC aggressiveness. Furthermore, we demonstrated that NDC80 overexpression strongly correlates with metastatic progression and poor survival in both our internal cohort and an external validation dataset. These findings not only position NDC80 as a valuable prognostic biomarker but also reveal its therapeutic potential for ESCC intervention. Targeting this pathway—at the level of NDC80, its secreted cytokines, or the downstream PI3K/AKT activation—represents a promising combinatorial strategy to suppress metastasis in ESCC patients with high NDC80 expression, thereby offering new avenues for targeted therapy against this aggressive malignancy.
