Emergency Free Flap Reconstruction in Traumatic Extremity Injuries: Clinical Indications and Framework
Authors: Omar Moussa, Jatupoom Chansomboon, Floris V. Raasveld, Ian L. Valerio, Krystle R. Tuaño, L. Scott Levin, Kyle R. Eberlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEmergency free flap reconstruction (EFFR) involves flap coverage performed during initial operative intervention or within 24 h after traumatic injury. Although EFFR offers notable advantages, standardized criteria for its application are not well established. This manuscript proposes a treatment framework outlining three primary indications for EFFR, including the use of viable tissue from spare parts.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 14 patients (15 flaps) with traumatic limb‐threatening injuries treated with EFFR across two academic centers from 2016 to 2024. Data collected included patient demographics, injury patterns, operative details, and clinical outcomes, with results compared to existing literature. Functional recovery was defined as independent ambulation for lower extremity injuries and restoration of range of motion for upper extremity or digital injuries.ResultsThe cohort (mean age 35 years; 79% male) presented three key indications for 15 EFFR flaps: spare part utilization (47%, n = 7), immediate coverage of exposed critical structures (20%, n = 3), and distal revascularization using flow‐through flaps (33%, n = 5). Flap success rate was 87%, with an overall complication rate of 47%. In multiple cases, spare part flaps preserved functional levels of amputation, such as the below‐elbow or below‐knee level. Functional recovery averaged 5 months (range, 2–11), and mean hospital stay was 15 days (range, 8–39).ConclusionsEFFR is effective for traumatic limb salvage when tailored to specific indications. Our experience‐based framework offers structured guidance based on spare part use, timely soft‐tissue coverage, and distal revascularization, supporting this acute orthoplastic approach for care of traumatic injuries.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

The management of traumatic limb‐threatening injuries has evolved over the past four decades, with free flap reconstruction becoming a common approach for complex extremity defects. The pioneering work of Byrd, Godina, and Lister et al. established progressively earlier reconstruction timing (within 5 days, 72 h, and 24 h, respectively), with demonstration of favorable clinical outcomes (Byrd et al. 1985, 1981; Godina and Lister 1986; Breidenbach 1989). Early reconstruction allows the potential for single‐stage treatment, offering advantages including prevention of wound contamination, preservation of exposed vital structures, and coverage of hardware, which may lead to improved outcomes (Byrd et al. 1985, 1981; Godina and Lister 1986; Breidenbach 1989; Lister and Scheker 1988; Chen et al. 1992).

Emergency free flap reconstruction (EFFR) within 24 h of complex extremity injuries has demonstrated improved outcomes such as reduced hospital length of hospital stay (LOS), decreased complications, and improved patient outcomes (Godina and Lister 1986; Arnež et al. 2017; Colen et al. 2017). Of the clinical indications for EFFR, spare parts utilization represents the most conceptually distinct, as it transforms anatomical loss into a reconstructive resource while eliminating donor‐site morbidity (Peng and Lahiri 2013). Existing literature focuses predominantly on timing parameters, while indications and decision‐making criteria for emergency free flap selection remain undefined. This knowledge gap limits broader adoption and potentially affects personalized care and outcomes in critically injured patients.

This study aimed to define specific clinical indications, evaluate outcomes, and develop a decision‐making framework for emergency free flap indications and applications within 24 h post‐injury to optimize outcomes in emergency reconstructions.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cohort analyzed 14 patients who underwent EFFR for traumatic limb‐threatening injuries by dedicated orthoplastic teams at two academic level I trauma centers between January 2016 and December 2024. Institutional review board approval was obtained at both institutions. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Table S1) (Cuschieri 2019). All cases followed formalized orthoplastic protocols including thorough debridement, skeletal stabilization, anatomical reconstruction, and definitive soft‐tissue coverage.

Patients were identified using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes for microvascular free‐tissue transfer (15,756, 15,757, 15,758, 20,969, 20,970, 20,972, and 20,973) (Table S2). Inclusion criteria included any EFFR, defined as microsurgical tissue transfer performed during the initial operative intervention or within 24 h of injury. All patients in this series underwent reconstruction during their initial operative intervention. Exclusion criteria included vascularized bone flaps and incomplete records.

Data Collection and Acquisition

Patient data were identified through chart review using standardized data collection forms. Data collection included patient demographics, injury characteristics, and surgical variables such as time from injury to flap coverage, flap type and size, donor site, anastomotic technique, ischemia time, and operative duration. Injury characteristics included trauma mechanism and anatomic location. Ischemia time was defined as the interval between flap harvest completion and completion of initial vascular anastomosis, and operative time was defined as skin incision to closure.

Flaps were categorized by tissue composition (fasciocutaneous, myocutaneous, or composite) and by primary indication for EFFR. These indications included: (1) spare parts reconstruction, which involved harvesting viable tissue from amputated or non‐salvageable segments with preserved axial perfusion to reconstruct traumatic defects and preserve residual limb length and function without incurring additional donor‐site morbidity; (2) immediate coverage of exposed critical structures, including major vessels, nerves, joints, or denuded bone, to mitigate further tissue loss and facilitate early recovery; and (3) combined revascularization and soft tissue coverage using flow‐through flaps. Flow‐through flaps were defined as free tissue transfers that restore vascular continuity while providing soft tissue coverage. These include arterial flow‐through flaps (incorporating arterial inflow and outflow anastomoses) and venous flow‐through flaps (utilizing various vascular anastomotic patterns) (Bullocks et al. 2006; Fujioka 2018).

Outcomes

Primary outcomes included flap survival, defined as complete flap viability without necrosis requiring surgical intervention. Secondary outcomes included partial flap necrosis, infection, vascular complications, requirement for reoperation, hospital LOS, time to functional recovery, and duration of follow‐up. Reoperations were classified by indication, including amputation, debridement, drainage, skin grafting, or flap revision. Time to functional recovery was defined as achievement of independent ambulation for lower extremity injuries or restoration of purposeful hand activities (including activities of daily living, occupational therapy completion, or functional range of motion) for upper extremity injuries (Gracia‐Ibáñez et al. 2017).

Statistical Analysis

Due to the descriptive nature of this case series and limited sample size, statistical analysis focused on descriptive measures. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and as median (interquartile range, IQR) for non‐normally distributed data, based on Shapiro–Wilk normality testing. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages (%). Outcomes were analyzed both on a patient basis (n = 14) and flap basis (15 flaps) as clinically appropriate.

Results

Fourteen patients underwent a total of 15 EFFRs for limb‐threatening injuries. Emergency free flap indications included: seven spare parts utilization from amputated tissue as the most common indication (47%, n = 6), three immediate soft tissue coverage of exposed vital structures (20%, n = 3), and five flow‐through flaps for revascularization of the distal limb in the setting of a soft tissue defect (33%, n = 5).

The mean age was 35 ± 21 years (range, 6–76), with a predominance of male patients (79%, n = 11 patients). Pediatric patients (< 18 years) comprised 29% of the cohort. Motor vehicle accidents were the leading cause of injury (43%, n = 6), followed by suicide attempts and crush injuries (14% each, n = 2). Upper extremity injuries were more common than lower extremity involvement (64% vs. 36%). Traumatic amputations and Gustilo‐Anderson IIIC fractures were each observed in 43% of cases (n = 6). Two patients (14%) with isolated single‐digit trauma sustained soft tissue degloving without associated fractures (Table 1).

Surgical Management and Flap Characteristics

Of 15 free flaps performed, 7 were used for reconstruction of primary amputations, while 8 addressed soft tissue defects ranging from 4 to 200 cm2 (median 77 cm2; IQR, 5.3–157.5). The mean time from injury to flap coverage was 12 ± 6 h (range, 5–23). Median ischemia time was 60 min (IQR, 50–90), and the mean operative duration was 7 ± 2 (range, 4–11) hours. The average length of hospital stay (LOS) was 15 days (range, 8–39).

Of 15 free flaps, six of seven spare parts comprised fillet flaps (47%), used to cover and/or preserve the functional level of amputation (n = 6), while one thumb was harvested from a contralateral non‐salvageable limb (Table 2). Five flow‐through flaps (33%) were employed for distal upper extremity defects. Two anterolateral thigh (ALT) flaps (13%) were utilized for medium to large extremity wounds, including one designed as an arterial flow‐through flap. Two latissimus dorsi flaps (13%) were selected for pediatric lower limb reconstruction, with one also applied to an amputation defect. End‐to‐end microvascular anastomosis was performed in 14/15 free tissue transfers (93%).

Outcomes and Complications

Outcomes varied substantially by flap indication (Table 3). Overall flap survival was 87% (13/15), with limb salvage achieved in 88% (7/8) of attempted flaps and 86% (6/7) successful residual limb coverage. The total complication rate was 47% (7/15), including: one case of total flap loss requiring delayed amputation, one partial necrosis, and one isolated infection. A total of 11 reoperations were performed, with 36% delayed wound healing requiring skin grafting (5/15) and 13% vascular complications (2/15).

Stratified by indication, flap success was 87% (6/7) for spare part utilization, 100% (3/3) for vital structures coverage, and 80% (4/5) for flow‐through revascularization. Functional recovery was favorable across all groups, with a mean return to function of 5 ± 3 months overall (range, 2–11). Digital reconstructions demonstrated the most rapid recovery, averaging 3 ± 1 months (range, 2–3). Median follow‐up was 8 months (IQR, 5–19).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated 14 patients undergoing 15 EFFR within 24 h of traumatic injury to define clinical indications and evaluate outcomes. Our findings identified three primary indications in a clinical framework (Figure 4) that may inform surgical decision‐making for EFFR: (1) salvage of viable tissue (spare parts) from unreplantable segments, (2) immediate coverage of exposed critical structures, and (3) combined soft tissue coverage and revascularization (Table S3). Across these indications, 87% flap survival was achieved, consistent with outcomes reported in prior emergency free flap series (range, 80%–100%), and the mean hospital LOS (15 days) was notably shorter than that reported in early reconstruction cohorts (24–42 days). Observed complication rates, 13% flap necrosis (2/15) and 7% infection (1/15), were consistent with published ranges (necrosis: 5%–15%; infection: 0%–35%) (Klifto et al. 2021). These findings suggest that a preliminary, experience‐based decision‐making framework may enhance emergency reconstruction outcomes when implemented by experienced orthoplastic teams with institutionalized collaborative treatment.

Stratified analysis of complications revealed that spare parts utilization carried the highest complication burden (4/7 flaps, 57%), largely attributable to wound‐healing challenges inherent to traumatic amputation zones. The sole total flap loss occurred in a polytrauma patient (Case 4) with 300 min of warm ischemia, substantially exceeding the established 180‐min threshold for irreversible muscle ischemia, underscoring ischemia time as a critical factor. Flow‐through revascularization accounted for 2/5 complications (40%), including one late loss in a distal digital case. Pediatric patients demonstrated no flap failures and no wound complications, though the sample size precludes definitive conclusions.

Previous emergency reconstruction literature emphasizes the prompt coverage of exposed vital structures and arterial injuries (Chen et al. 1992). However, standardized frameworks for clinical decision‐making have remained limited, and optimal outcomes require dedicated orthoplastic and microsurgical capability with early multidisciplinary coordination (Moussa et al. 2026).

The concept of “spare parts” involves harvesting viable tissue from non‐replantable or amputated parts to enable autologous reconstruction (Figure 1) (Tosti et al. 2020). This approach eliminates additional donor‐site morbidity while optimizing outcomes such as limb length preservation and functional recovery, and optimal prosthetic fitting (Peng and Lahiri 2013). In this series, spare part utilization represented the most common indication for EFFR with an 86% success rate. Fillet flaps were frequently employed to provide durable residual limb coverage and/or preserve a functional level of amputation (Mendenhall et al. 2019). The feasibility of spare‐part reconstruction depends on tissue viability, patient condition, and multidisciplinary surgical coordination. Although within the established ideal ischemic timeframe of 6–8 h, flap failure may still occur under complex conditions. In Case 4, total flap loss occurred despite successful anastomosis in a polytrauma patient with 300 min of warm ischemia; the extended ischemic interval likely caused irreversible myonecrosis and ischemia–reperfusion injury, precipitating flap thrombosis in the presence of compromised recipient vessels. This case reinforces that warm ischemia exceeding 180 min represents a relative contraindication to muscle‐containing spare‐part flaps. Previous studies suggest a 10% decrease in limb salvage likelihood for every hour of delay, with irreversible muscle ischemia occurring within 3 h under normothermic conditions (Perkins et al. 2022). Hypothermic preservation may extend viability to 6–8 h (He et al. 2022), while skin and subcutaneous tissues may tolerate up to 24 h (He et al. 2022; Hadzimustafic et al. 2024). These findings suggest that muscle or myocutaneous fillet flaps should be avoided when warm ischemia exceeds 3 h (Mendenhall et al. 2019). Given the heterogeneous nature of EFFR that precludes standardized guidelines, careful assessment of tissue viability remains essential for successful outcomes. In addition to ischemia time, patient factors such as dysvascular morbidities, vascular injury, and mechanism of injury should be considered when selecting both recipient site and spare parts (Küntscher et al. 2001). Contraindications include severely crushed or contaminated tissue intended for reconstruction. While spare part use is limited by the availability of healthy tissue in the non‐salvageable segments, the orthoplastic surgical team's expertise and coordinative abilities for the primary reconstruction planning remain crucial for success (Peng and Lahiri 2013).

Two spare parts of free flaps were utilized: A heterotopic thumb‐to‐thumb transfer to reconstruct the left thumb (center) and a free ulnar artery forearm flap for coverage of the right stump. Timely soft tissue coverage has been associated with improved limb salvage and patient outcomes (Chummun et al. 2015; Khan et al. 2011; Lacey et al. 2024). In this series, free flaps designed for immediate soft tissue coverage achieved 100% flap success. Successful EFFR for immediate coverage of critical structures requires systematic evaluation of patient and defect characteristics by experienced orthoplastic teams (D'Cunha et al. 2023; Azoury et al. 2021; Naique et al. 2006). Key considerations include patient hemodynamic stability, bacterial load, fracture type, location and extent of the defect, vital structure exposure, and wound healing capacity (Breidenbach 1989). While defects smaller than 30 cm2 may be amenable to local coverage, various free flaps are commonly used for larger defects or when critical structures are exposed.

For upper extremity trauma, fasciocutaneous flaps are often chosen for their gliding surface and potential for sensory restoration, which facilitate early rehabilitation and reduce tendon adhesions (Byrd et al. 1981). For example, the 25‐year‐old woman shown in Figure 2 had both limb preservation and recovery of musculotendinous function achieved with ALT fasciocutaneous flap reconstruction. However, realizing the full potential of fasciocutaneous flaps often necessitates secondary procedures to meet the varied reconstructive demands of the upper limb (Byrd et al. 1985; Godina and Lister 1986; Breidenbach 1989; Lister and Scheker 1988).

Case 7: A 25‐year‐old woman sustained a mangled left upper extremity injury (Gustilo‐Anderson grade IIIC) following a motorcycle collision. Preoperative images showed a severely comminuted wrist fracture with associated extensive soft tissue destruction (left). Definitive management included skeletal stabilization with multiple plates and screws, combined with soft tissue repair and reconstruction (middle). A 150 cm2 anterolateral thigh (ALT) fasciocutaneous flap was applied for wrist coverage (top right). Postoperative follow‐up at 8 months demonstrated restoration of wrist and hand motion and fracture union (bottom right).

In concordance with earlier literature, latissimus dorsi flaps in our series served as reliable workhorses for lower extremity EFFR (Chen et al. 1992), achieving 100% survival rates. Latissimus dorsi flaps may be favored to address more extensive defects with more voluminous needs (Arnež et al. 2017; Georgescu and Ivan 2003; Ninković et al. 1996). In pediatric patients, they are especially valuable due to their robust vascularity and relative preservation of donor‐site function, besides the ability to cover larger surface areas (Ninković et al. 1996).

Based on our experience, fasciocutaneous flaps may be considered for moderate‐to‐large superficial defects requiring preservation of a gliding surface (Breidenbach 1989; Georgescu and Ivan 2003). Muscle flaps can be considered for extensive lower limb trauma with bone exposure or substantial soft tissue loss, particularly in pediatric patients, where donor‐site morbidity and functionality are important for optimizing the substantial long‐term impact.

In the setting of limb‐threatening injuries with concomitant arterial disruption and soft tissue loss, flow‐through flaps can provide the advantage of simultaneous revascularization and defect coverage. Arterial flow‐through flaps are particularly suited for large defects involving segmental arterial loss, whereas venous flow‐through flaps are more applicable to small‐caliber vessel injuries, such as digital artery disruptions. Common donor sites for these flaps include the proximal or distal forearm (Figure 3).

Case 11: A 46‐year‐old man sustained a grinder injury to the left small finger, resulting in a 6 cm2 soft tissue defect along with loss of the ulnar digital artery and nerve (top row, left), while preserving flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) and flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) function (top row). A free fasciocutaneous venous flap was harvested from the volar forearm (middle row, right) and inset with proximal anastomosis to the digital artery and distal anastomosis to the ulnar digital artery. The ulnar digital nerve was reconstructed using a 5‐cm nerve allograft. Postoperative images at 1 week (bottom row, left) and 6 months (bottom row, center + right) demonstrate flap viability and satisfactory recovery.

Venous flow‐through flaps were successful in 3 out of 4 patients in this series. Anatomic considerations remain critical in flap design and recipient vessel selection; for proximal defects, traditional free flaps can be considered, whereas venous flaps are generally favored for more distal defects (Garlick et al. 2015). Published data report an overall flap loss rate of 5.8%, with 70% of flaps experiencing transient congestion resolving within 24 h to 3 weeks; predictors of success include younger patient age, flap sizes of 10–25 cm2, and the presence of arterial inflow (Roberts et al. 2020). Venous flow‐through flaps remain a versatile option for digital trauma, with optimal outcomes contingent on meticulous recipient vessel selection and vigilant postoperative monitoring. In Case 13, late flap loss occurred in a small finger crush injury, where progressive vascular compromise likely precipitated failure, illustrating that distal crush injuries represent the highest‐risk subset for venous flow‐through flap reconstruction (Roberts et al. 2020).

Based on this series, we propose an experience‐based clinical decision‐making framework to inform the application of EFFR for traumatic limb‐threatening injuries. Unlike prior reconstructive frameworks that address timing parameters or flap selection in isolation, the proposed EFFR framework integrates three distinct indication‐based pathways: spare parts reconstruction, immediate vital structure coverage, and flow‐through revascularization–within a unified decision structure applicable within 24 h of injury (Figure 4). The framework's primary novelty lies in the systematic categorization of clinical indications, not the individual microsurgical techniques, which are established. Decision‐making for each pathway requires confirmation of: (1) intent for limb salvage or residual limb optimization; (2) hemodynamic stability; (3) appropriate ischemia time thresholds (< 180 min for muscle‐containing spare parts; < 24 h for fasciocutaneous flaps); and (4) institutional microsurgical availability. In cases where limb salvage versus amputation is being considered, previously published criteria may aid in decision‐making (Higgins et al. 2010). However, these frameworks often fall short in clearly differentiating between salvageable and non‐salvageable limbs (Mendenhall et al. 2019). In the absence of absolute contraindications, reconstruction should proceed in accordance with established reconstructive principles (Tintle and Levin 2013; De Francesco et al. 2023; Jeyaratnam et al. 2024; Soh et al. 2024). When EFFR is considered and no relative contraindications exist (Amendola et al. 2023), the availability of institutional orthoplastic expertise and microsurgical capability is critical. For facilities lacking such resources, early referral to a specialized orthoplastic trauma center is advised. Referral delays and other systemic barriers can be mitigated using formalized referral pathways and centralized institutional networks (Arnež et al. 2017; Hsu et al. 2009; Steinberger et al. 2021).

Emergency free flap clinical framework for traumatic limb‐threatening injuries: This experience‐based decision‐making framework provides a systematic approach to EFFR in traumatic limb‐threatening injuries. Key decision points include initial assessment of limb salvage potential, evaluation of EFFR feasibility (hemodynamic stability, ischemic thresholds, wound contamination, institutional microsurgical availability), and indication‐specific pathway selection. For each of the three primary pathways, specific decision criteria are provided: (1) spare parts reconstruction–tissue viability, ischemia time < 180 min for muscle‐containing flaps, < 24 h for fasciocutaneous flaps; (2) immediate vital structure coverage–defect ≥ 30 cm2 or critical structure exposure, hemodynamic stability; (3) flow‐through revascularization–segmental arterial loss (flow‐through free flap) or digital/small vessel injury (venous flow‐through). The framework is presented as an experience‐based clinical guide and warrants prospective multicenter validation before broader adoption.

In unsalvageable amputation scenarios, the feasibility of utilizing a fillet flap from the amputated segment should be considered if there is a need for soft tissue coverage to preserve acceptable length of the residual limb for prosthetic fitting or functional reconstruction, with or without innervation. Flap selection should be tailored to the defect's size, location, and reconstructive goals. When segmental vascular discontinuity exists, flow‐through flaps should be considered: arterial flow‐through flaps for medium‐to‐large defects and venous flow‐through flaps for smaller lesions, especially in digital reconstructions.

Effective intraoperative coordination can enhance outcomes. A two‐team approach when possible—simultaneous flap harvest and recipient site preparation—has been shown to reduce operative time and minimize complications (Mendenhall et al. 2019; Amendola et al. 2023; Hsu et al. 2009; Steinberger et al. 2021). In practice, this approach allows orthopedic stabilization to be performed while the reconstructive team works concurrently to avoid unnecessary delays (Colen et al. 2017). Postoperative monitoring is critical, particularly during the first 24 h, with early recognition of vascular compromise being paramount (Breidenbach 1989; Pu 2017).

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design and small sample size. The heterogeneity in flap types and clinical scenarios limits the ability to conduct meaningful statistical comparisons or draw definitive conclusions regarding optimal reconstructive techniques. The absence of a systematically captured comparison group of patients evaluated but not selected for EFFR introduces selection bias and limits definitive conclusions regarding comparative effectiveness. Both institutions maintain dedicated orthoplastic programs with 24/7 microsurgical availability and formalized emergency operative protocols; generalizability to centers without equivalent infrastructure may be limited. Additionally, the relatively short follow‐up period may not capture long‐term outcomes. Standardized patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs), including the DASH score, Michigan Hand Outcomes Questionnaire, or Limb‐Q, were not systematically collected, limiting assessment of patient‐perceived functional recovery and satisfaction; future prospective studies should incorporate validated PROMs as primary outcome measures (Sayyed et al. 2024). Despite these limitations, the results support the feasibility of EFFR across a broad spectrum of patients–including pediatric cases–when performed under appropriate indications and with institutional microsurgical capability. The demonstrated reduction in hospital LOS compared to delayed reconstruction approaches suggests potential overall cost saving; however, larger cohort and cost‐effectiveness analyses are warranted (Byrd et al. 1981; Mendenhall et al. 2019; Ninković et al. 1996). While prospective validation is needed, the framework offers a preliminary, experience‐based structure intended to inform institutional protocols and training initiatives, and warrants prospective multicenter validation before broader adoption.

Conclusion

Emergency free flaps represent a feasible approach in traumatic limb‐threatening injury management with specific indications extending beyond conventional timing parameters. This study proposes an experience‐based decision‐making framework centered on three indications: spare parts utilization, immediate vital structure coverage, and simultaneous revascularization with soft tissue coverage using flow‐through techniques. The favorable outcomes observed—including reduced hospital stay, time to functional recovery, and high limb salvage rates—suggest the feasibility of emergency free flaps in orthoplastic centers with multidisciplinary coordinated microsurgical capabilities—although prospective multicenter validation is warranted prior to broader adoption.

Table S1: STROBE Checklist for Observational Cohort Studies. Completed checklist confirming adherence to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for this retrospective cohort study, with corresponding manuscript line references for each of the 22 required items.

Table S2: Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) Codes Used for Patient Identification. The seven microvascular free‐tissue transfer CPT codes (15,756, 15,757, 15,758, 20,969, 20,970, 20,972, and 20,973) used to identify eligible patients at both institutions. Identified cases were subsequently screened by chart review against prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria; the final cohort was identified using codes 15,756, 15,757, and 15,758 for soft‐tissue free flap transfers.

Table S3: Stratified Clinical Outcomes by Primary EFFR Indication. Detailed outcomes data stratified by the three primary EFFR indications; spare parts utilization (n = 7 flaps), immediate vital structure coverage (n = 3), and flow‐through revascularization (n = 5); including flap success, complication profile, reoperation frequency, hospital length of hospital stay, time to functional recovery, follow‐up duration, and pediatric subgroup outcomes. Data derive from the 14 patients (15 flaps) described in the primary manuscript.
