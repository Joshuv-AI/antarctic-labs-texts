Life-course trajectories of cardiovascular disease risk factors in rural India: Andhra Pradesh Children and Parents Study (APCAPS) 2003–2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of disability-adjusted life years in South Asia, with adverse trends in CVD risk seen in rural residents in recent decades. Assessment of life-course trajectories of CVD risk factors is important to understand CVD aetiology.MethodsThe Andhra Pradesh Children and Parents cohort Study included all children (n = 2601) born during 1987–90 and their mothers (n = 1963) residing in 29 villages of South India. Participants were followed up in 2003–05, 2009–10, 2010–12, and 2021–23. Children (n = 4777 observations, 35% female) were assessed for body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, waist–hip ratio, waist–height ratio, fasting glucose, and central and peripheral skinfolds. For mothers (n = 3830 observations), repeated measures of BMI were available. This highlights the need for interventions to mitigate the potential CVD burden.

Introduction

Rural South Asians experience a higher incidence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and associated mortality compared with urban populations [1]. National surveys show that overweight and obesity prevalence [body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2] for ages 15–49 years tripled from 6.9% in 2005 to 19.4% in 2021 in rural India [2]. Abdominal obesity was seen in more than one-third of rural women and one-tenth of rural men in 2021 [3]. Furthermore, over a short period from 2015 to 2021, the prevalence of diabetes increased from 5.6% to 8% [2].

South Asians compared with other ethnicities experience acute myocardial infarction incidence half a decade earlier, at an average age of 53 years compared with 58.8 years [4]. Furthermore, South Asians develop insulin resistance at younger ages and at lower body-composition thresholds, with a quicker progression through subsequent stages of diabetes [5, 6]. Potential explanations lie in a transgenerational risk transmission model in which a lower capacity for homeostasis during fuel metabolism develops largely due to compromised growth during early life [7].

Assessing the life-course trajectories of CVD risk factors in subgroups of populations is key to identifying risk divergence and helps to target potential interventions [8]. There is limited life-course evidence on the progression and clustering of CVD risk factors in South Asia. The 1969–72 urban New Delhi Birth Cohort observed no sex differences in adverse shifts in abdominal obesity indicators, with both sexes reaching the CVD risk cut-off for adult South Asians during early adulthood [9]. In the 1969–73 Vellore Birth Cohort study, participants at a mean age of 28 years showed no sex differences in BMI, waist–hip ratio (WHR), and fasting glucose levels in both urban and rural settings, with only early shifts in CVD risk [10]. These birth cohorts in India, in urban–rural settings or with multigenerational follow-ups such as the Pune Maternal Nutrition Study, can fully realize their potential by using appropriate methods based in life-course epidemiology [11].

This study aimed to describe the life-course trajectories of BMI for children and mothers of the Andhra Pradesh Children and Parents cohort Study (APCAPS) based in rural Telangana that included life stages ranging from adolescence to the third decade, and early to late adulthood, respectively. Additionally, trajectories of markers of abdominal, central, and peripheral obesity and fasting glucose were assessed in children.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study uses data from APCAPS, originally the Hyderabad Nutrition Trial (HNT, 1987–90), which is based in two adjacent administrative clusters of 29 villages of Telangana, South India (see Supplementary Figure 1, ‘Study description’ in the Supplementary file) [12]. The first follow-up of the APCAPS study (2003–5) invited only those children (n = 1492) and their mothers (n = 1454) of the HNT trial who were alive in 2003 and were successfully retraced and linked to trial records [12]. The second (2009–10), third (2010–12), and fourth (2021–23) follow-ups were expanded to include all children (n = 2601) born during the HNT trial period, irrespective of linkage to trial records, and their mothers (n = 1963) [12]. Mothers were not assessed in the second follow-up.

This study included risk factors important to CVD aetiology in South Asians [7], namely, body composition and glucose metabolism that were comparably assessed at three or more life stages in the APCAPS cohorts. This study included participants with data on CVD risk factors at one or more assessments (Supplementary Figure 1 and Table 1). Data from female participants who reported being pregnant (n = 80 observations) were excluded for that time point. Children of both sexes (n = 2086 children, 4777 observations, 35% female) were followed up at adolescence, twice in their early twenties, and in their thirties. Measures of BMI, waist circumference (WC), WHR, waist–height ratio (WHtR), and fasting glucose were assessed in all follow-ups. Skinfolds, namely biceps, triceps, subscapular, supra-iliac, and the sum of these four sites, were not available in the last follow-up. For mothers, only BMI (n = 1729 mothers, 3830 observations) was available, from early to middle adulthood at baseline (2003) and middle to late adulthood in the recent follow-up (2021). The mother cohort was divided into three age groups at baseline, namely <35, ≥35 to <45, and ≥45 years, corresponding to life-stage transitions at early adulthood and the average age of menopause in this region, respectively.

Procedures and outcomes

The life-course trajectories of BMI in children and mothers were the main outcome and trajectories of markers of abdominal obesity, fasting glucose, and multiple skinfolds in children were the secondary outcome. Sex was selected by investigators through observation (male and female) and age was self-reported by using event calendars.

All anthropometric and body-composition measures were taken in light clothing, barefoot, and appropriate posture. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kilogram by using digital Seca 899 (UK) in the first three follow-ups and in a subsample in the fourth, and largely by using TANITA BC 418 M57NA (Japan) in the fourth follow-up. Height was measured to the nearest 1 mm by using a Leicester stadiometer (Chasmors, UK). Waist and hip circumference were measured to the nearest 1 mm by using ADE non-stretch metallic tape, with the former measured at the narrowest part of a relaxed abdomen at the end of expiration and the latter at the widest part of the buttocks. Skinfolds were measured by using a Holtain calliper (UK) in the first three follow-ups only. Anthropometric measurements were taken twice (three times for skinfolds), with an additional reading taken if a large difference between readings was found (0.5 kg for weight, 5 mm for height, 1 cm for waist or hip circumference, and 2 mm for skinfolds), and the mean scores of the two readings closest to each other were reported.

Venous blood samples after overnight fasting of 8 hours were collected. Fasting glucose was assessed on the same day by using an auto-analyser (ACE Clinical System, USA) and recommended kits (Alfa Wasserman, USA) in the first follow-up and the oxidase–peroxidase enzymatic method (GOD-PAP) using Randox reagents (UK) in the other follow-ups. A subsample of participants was reassessed for repeatability and the standard procedures for calibration were employed. Internal and external quality assurances for biochemical assays were followed.

Statistical analysis

Mixed-effects linear models with a random slope for age were used to model BMI and the other CVD risk factors by age. Linear or quadratic models were used in the fixed effect. An interaction between age and sex or mother age groups was included in fixed effects to assess potential sex and age differences, respectively.

Models accounted for clustering at the village, family, and individual levels, as appropriate, including a random effect for all the three when assessing children and a random effect for village and individual when assessing mothers. Restricted maximum-likelihood estimation and the Kenward–Roger degrees-of-freedom method accounted for low superclusters (29 villages). Age was mean-centred by using the baseline mean age. Improvement in model fit was assessed through the predicted trajectory against the observed values, the likelihood ratio test, residual variance, difference in observed and predicted values by each occasion and subgroup, and the Bayesian Information Criterion. South Asian specific cut-offs, where available, were applied for interpretation (Supplementary Table 2).

Participants were included under the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption and therefore participants with data on at least one measurement of the outcome were included. To assess attrition bias, we compared the baseline characteristics (2003–05) between participants retained and those lost to follow-up by 2021–23 via t-tests, proportion, or chi-squared tests, as appropriate. In sensitivity analyses, the models were restricted to participants with data in three or more follow-ups (see ‘Sensitivity analyses’ in the Supplementary file). Stata SE 17 (StataCorp LLC: College Station, TX, USA) was used for all analyses.

Results

Data from 2086 children (80% of 2601 of the APCAPS child cohort) and 1729 mothers (88% of 1963 of the APCAPS mother cohort) were available for analysis (Tables 1 and 2). The mean age varied in the children cohort from 15 years (SD 1.1) in the first follow-up to 34 years (SD 1.1) in the fourth follow-up. In the mother cohort, the mean age ranged from 39 years (SD 5.8) in the first follow-up to 57 years (SD 6.0) in the last follow-up. The mean follow-up time was 12.7 years (SD 6.3) for male children, 8.3 years (SD 7.0) for female children, and 13.3 years (SD 5.7) for mothers. The maximum follow-up time was 20 years for all cohorts. The mean number of repeated measures available for BMI, WC, WHR, WHtR, and fasting glucose for male children was 2.6 (SD 1.0) and was 1.8 (SD 0.9) for female children. The mean number of repeated measures available for skinfolds for male children was 2.1 (SD 0.8) and was 1.6 (SD 0.7) for female children. Similarly, the mean number of repeated measures available for BMI for mothers was 2.2 (SD 0.7) and it was similar between age groups.

In mothers, all age groups had a similar mean BMI at baseline and the BMI increased linearly, with a higher rate of increase observed in young mothers (<35 years old at baseline) (Figure 1ii and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). In young mothers, the mean BMI at age 32 years was 20.3 kg/m2 (95% CI 19.9, 20.7), with an increase of 0.25 kg/m2 per year (95% CI 0.24, 0.27). For mothers aged ≥35 to <45 years at baseline, the mean BMI at age 39 years was 20.6 kg/m2 (95% CI 20.2, 20.9), with an increase of 0.21 kg/m2 per year (95% CI 0.20, 0.23). Finally, for the oldest mothers (≥45 years old at baseline), the mean BMI at age 49 years was 20.2 kg/m2 (95% CI 19.7, 20.8), with an increase of 0.17 kg/m2 per year (95% CI 0.15, 0.20). The mean BMI for young mothers reached both the WHO’s adult BMI risk cut-off points earlier than the other mother age groups, 23 kg/m2 at age 42.9 years and 25 kg/m2 at age 50.8 years. The mean BMI for mothers aged ≥35 to <45 years at baseline reached the cut-off points at ages 50.3 and 59.7 years, respectively. Finally, the mean BMI for the oldest mothers reached the cut-off points at ages 64.8 and 76.4 years, respectively.

At 15 years old, females had a similar mean WC, lower mean WHR, and higher mean WHtR compared with males (Figure 1iii-v, Table 3, and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). The WC increased non-linearly in both sexes, with a higher increase observed in males than in females, and the difference between sexes widened with age (Figure 1iii). The WHR increased non-linearly in males, with a faster rate of increase after 25 years of age, while it remained stable in females over the years (Figure 1iv). The WHtR increased linearly in both sexes, with a slower rate of change in females, and, by age 35 years, the WHtR was higher in males than in females (Figure 1v). In males during early adulthood, the mean WC reached the adult risk cut-off point for central obesity of 85 cm at age 33.7 years, the mean WHR reached the adult risk cut-off point of 0.90 at age 33.5 years, and the mean WHtR reached the adult risk cut-off point of 0.50 at 32.4 years. In females, the mean WC, WHR, and WHtR stayed below the adult risk cut-off points for central obesity of 80 cm, 0.80, and 0.50, respectively. In adolescents, the mean WC remained below the adolescent cut-off points of increased CVD risk in both sexes.

At 15 years old, females and males had similar mean fasting glucose levels and they increased linearly in both sexes, with a higher rate of change observed in males (0.76 mg/dL/year, 95% CI 0.63, 0.89) compared with females (0.49 mg/dL/year, 95% CI 0.31, 0.67) (Figure 1vi, Table 3, and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). In males, the mean fasting glucose reached the prediabetes cut-off point of 100 mg/dL at age 36 years, while, in females, the mean stayed below the cut-off point.

At baseline, females had a higher average skinfold for each skinfold site and sum of four skinfolds, which changed linearly over time for both sexes (Figure 1vii-xi, Table 3, and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Whilst the rate of change was similar in males and females for biceps (0.2 mm/year, 95% CI 0.2, 0.2) and triceps skinfolds (0.5 mm/year, 95% CI 0.5, 0.6), the increase in subscapular skinfold and sum of four skinfolds was much faster in males than in females. The supra-iliac skinfold decreased over time and this decrease was at a higher rate in females than in males. In summary, by age 25 years, females had a higher average sum of skinfolds and skinfold thickness at biceps, triceps, and supra-iliac sites, while males had a higher average at the subscapular site.

Discussion

An adverse shift was seen in the BMI trajectory of all age groups that was not restricted to recent birth cohorts or attributable solely to ageing [13]. Results indicate differential fat deposition by sex with increase in weight (BMI trajectories), resulting in faster progression to prediabetes in males. The increasing WHR in males, compared with stable WHR in females, is indicative of higher abdominal fat deposition (harmful) over the pelvic region (protective) [14]. The higher abdominal fat deposition in males is additionally confirmed by higher rates of increase in the WC and WHtR. Further, decreasing trajectories of supra-iliac skinfolds (superficial subcutaneous fat in the abdomen) indicates that this abdominal fat deposition may be visceral or deep-subcutaneous in both sexes. The lack of assessment of skinfolds in the most recent follow-up limits such evaluation up to the mid-twenties.

The relative trajectories of central and peripheral skinfolds, especially the faster rate of increase in subscapular skinfolds in males, might additionally explain the early progression of males to prediabetes by the third decade. Through ages of 15–25 years, females had higher peripheral subcutaneous fat (biceps and triceps skinfolds), while males had higher chest subcutaneous fat (subscapular skinfolds) deposition. Hyperinsulinaemia in South Asians has been associated with the preservation of central skinfolds (subscapular) over peripheral skinfolds in newborns and adolescents [15–18]. Further, children of Indian ethnicity, even though at lower weight (or BMI levels) compared with Caucasians, had only small differences in subscapular skinfolds, indicating a preservation of central subcutaneous fat across childhood, including newborn, infant, toddler, 6-year-old, and adolescence [15, 17, 19].

The accelerated shift in BMI trajectories seen in this study is strikingly similar to those found in upper-middle [20] and high-income countries [13, 21], where the average BMI of all birth cohorts entered the WHO overweight cut-off (≥25 kg/m2) by the early to mid-thirties. These changes occurred a few decades earlier in these cohorts compared with the APCAPS (1987–90) birth cohort. For instance, a combined analysis of multiple UK birth cohorts of 1946, 1958, and 1970 showed that the median BMI reached the overweight category at progressively younger ages with each subsequent birth cohort, to as early as 30 years in males and 41 years in females for the 1970 British Cohort Study [22].

It is useful to draw parallels with the quicker CVD risk progression in migrant populations to urban areas or to developed nations that provides a causal framework for development as a determinant [23–25]. Rural development includes the physical activity and nutrition transition, occupational shifts, elevated levels of air pollution, and the rapid changes in the rural built environment [6, 23, 24]. Similar mechanisms have been referenced for the obesity transition in the USA [13].

All birth cohorts were resident of a single study site at baseline and the study period of 2003–23 is concurrent with the CVD risk transition seen in other studies [2] and larger lifestyle and environmental transition observed in India [23, 24]. CVD risk factors were objectively measured by using a standardized protocol with quality monitoring and any misclassification is likely to be non-differential. Further, multiple anthropometric and body-composition measures, features of the South Asian thin–fat phenotype, were used to assess CVD risk [14, 26].

Participants had minimal missing data for a particular follow-up, except for a few who refused blood samples due to discomfort. The follow-up rates of 39% for children and 72% for mothers in 2021–3 are higher than those of other cohort studies in India with participants in their third decade of life [9, 10]. Missing data due to attrition were accounted for by the use of linear mixed-effects models under the MAR assumption. There was higher attrition in female children compared with males (Supplementary Tables 7 and 8, ‘Assessing bias due to attrition’ in the Supplementary file), which could have led to selection bias, and might be consequently associated with the sex differences in the trajectories of CVD risk factors. Differential attrition was not observed by other demographic characteristics in children or mothers. The lack of life-course data on time-varying covariates such as lifestyle behaviours, socio-economic mobility, and pubertal status may have resulted in residual confounding.

The absence of baseline records limits assessment of whether the APCAPS cohort is representative of the village population. Future research should include all life stages and a broad range of population subgroups based in diverse settings, and assess features of the South Asian thin–fat phenotype, including insulin-resistance and body-fat and lean-mass indices [23]. Ensuring adequate nutrition and physical activity along with hygiene and the control of infectious diseases will support development during early life and beyond, and consequently normal CVD risk-factor trajectories that gradually improves the parental capital over generations [27, 28]. Targeting structural determinants that drive adverse changes in rural lifestyle and environments is needed [23].
