Introduction

Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) have been a cornerstone therapy for decades to prevent sudden cardiac death in patients at risk for life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias.1 Conventional transvenous ICD (TV-ICD) systems provide reliable defibrillation but carry lead-related risks, including vascular injury at implantation, systemic infection, and tricuspid valve dysfunction related to endovascular hardware.2,3 The extravascular ICD (EV-ICD) has recently emerged as a non-intravascular, lead-based system that delivers defibrillation via a substernal lead, thereby avoiding the bloodstream while expanding device options for selected patients.4,5

The EV-ICD system has not been evaluated in patients with prior sternotomy and has been contraindicated in this setting, making previous sternotomy a practical barrier to standard EV-ICD implantation.6 Although recent case reports have described EV-ICD implantation performed at the time of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG),7 evidence remains limited to inform perioperative management for patients with a preexisting EV-ICD who subsequently require cardiac surgery via median sternotomy.

Here, we report a case of a patient with a pre-existing EV-ICD undergoing CABG, detailing intraoperative and postoperative care of the EV-ICD system, including practical lead-preservation strategies.

Case report

A 75-year-old man with extensive coronary artery disease (CAD), including prior percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and ischemic cardiomyopathy with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 32%, experienced ventricular fibrillation (VF) arrest during ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) requiring veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. He subsequently underwent secondary prevention implantation of a Medtronic Aurora EV-ICD in October 2024. An EV-ICD was selected over a transvenous system to minimize intravascular hardware, given a history of infections, including a partially treated testicular abscess. Before EV-ICD implantation, the patient had a complex CAD history, including multiple prior drug-eluting stents in the right coronary artery (RCA) and a STEMI involving the RCA complicated by VF arrest. A surveillance coronary angiogram performed approximately 8 months before EV-ICD implantation showed no significant left main disease and moderate RCA disease without intervention. EV-ICD implantation was later pursued for primary prevention.

Approximately 10 months later, after developing progressive heart failure symptoms, repeat coronary angiography showed worsening multivessel CAD involving the left main and right coronary arteries, prompting CABG. Preoperative planning involved an electrophysiology consultation to prepare for EV-ICD lead management, and the device therapies were deactivated before surgery. Review of a pre-EV-ICD axial chest computed tomography demonstrated proximity of the left internal mammary artery (LIMA) to the posterior sternum, with an approximate sternum-to-LIMA distance of 20 mm (Figure 1). At surgery, a standard median sternotomy was performed, including resection of a prominent xiphoid process. During sternotomy, the EV-ICD lead was noted to lie approximately 3–15 mm medial to the LIMA along its distal half. Because the lead was curved, some portions were in very close proximity to the LIMA, whereas other portions of the coil were farther away. The previously placed left parasternal substernal lead was carefully dissected free from adhesions and retracted gently to the side. The EV-ICD lead was not disconnected from the generator, and the EV-ICD pocket did not need to be accessed. Approximately 10 cm of the lead was dissected from the distal tip, back to the flexible portion proximal to ring 2, to allow gentle retraction of the active portion away from the operative field. The LIMA was densely adherent to the chest wall in the region of the substernal EV-ICD lead, which limited safe LIMA harvest. Consequently, LIMA harvest was abandoned to preserve lead integrity and maintain hemostasis. Instead, the right internal mammary artery (RIMA) was harvested and grafted to the left anterior descending artery, alongside saphenous vein grafts to the obtuse marginal and posterior descending arteries. Left atrial appendage exclusion was also performed using a 40 mm clip.

Pre-extravascular implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (EV-ICD) axial chest computed tomography (CT). Axial chest CT obtained before EV-ICD implantation showing close proximity of the left internal mammary artery to the posterior sternum, with an approximate distance of 20 mm.

After anastomosing the grafts, the EV-ICD lead was repositioned in the left parasternal region over the right ventricular (RV) outflow tract, avoiding the right atrium, and secured in place with 2 looped absorbable Vicryl sutures. Sternal closure used rigid fixation with 4 anterior plates (including multiple “boomerang” plates) to reduce the number of wires required. Sternal wires were placed under direct visualization more than 1 cm away from the device to prevent contact with the lead electrodes. The sternum was closed in layers over the intact lead (Figure 2A).

Intraoperative and radiographic imaging. A: Intraoperative photograph demonstrating sternal closure using rigid fixation with “butterfly” (boomerang) plates. B: Preoperative posterpanterior (PA) chest X-ray. C: Postoperative PA chest X-ray following coronary artery bypass grafting.

The patient’s immediate postoperative course was uneventful. Postoperative chest X-ray confirmed that the EV-ICD lead remained in a stable position without displacement (Figure 2C). Intraoperative device interrogation revealed a borderline R-wave amplitude of 0.7 mV with stable impedance. Consequently, defibrillation threshold (DFT) testing was deferred to the outpatient setting, ICD therapies were turned off, sensing stayed on to detect any oversensing, and the patient was discharged with a wearable cardioverter defibrillator. After 6 weeks, the patient was brought to the electrophysiology laboratory for DFT testing. VF was induced via direct current defibrillation, and VF was sensed correctly using the EV-ICD at 0.45 mV. A single 30 J shock successfully terminated VF to sinus rhythm without any sensing dropout (Figure 3). Sensitivity was subsequently programmed to 0.075 mV. On follow-up at 6 months, the patient remained asymptomatic, and device interrogation showed stable sensing with no oversensing or electrical noise.

Discussion

The Medtronic Aurora™ EV-ICD expands defibrillator options by delivering therapy through a substernal electrode positioned in the anterior mediastinum, thereby avoiding chronic intravascular hardware while preserving the ability to sense ventricular signals and provide defibrillation and limited pacing therapies.8 This entirely substernal design avoids transvenous complications; however, its use poses unique challenges in patients requiring subsequent cardiac surgery. Currently, de novo implantation is generally contraindicated in patients with prior sternotomy because the obliteration of the retrosternal space by adhesions prevents safe passage of the tunneling tool and increases the risk of RV perforation.6 However, for patients with an existing system, the device’s anatomic position creates a unique surgical vulnerability: the lead traverses the retrosternal plane that is routinely entered, dissected, or reconstructed during median sternotomy and internal mammary artery harvest. Consequently, patients who undergo sternotomy after EV-ICD implantation present distinct intraoperative and postoperative risks. First, there is a direct risk of mechanical injury—including insulation breach, conductor damage, or lead displacement—during sternal entry, xiphoid resection, mediastinal dissection, and sternal closure. Second, disruption of the substernal tissue planes and postoperative edema may alter sensing vectors, reduce R-wave amplitude, and increase susceptibility to oversensing or noise. Third, any metal-on-lead contact (eg, sternal wires or plates abutting the electrode segment) could theoretically create intermittent artifact, abnormal impedance trends, or changes in shock vector geometry.6,8

Nevertheless, some recent case reports describe innovative EV-ICD use in surgical settings. Although this report was in preparation, Collings et al9 described preservation of a pre-existing EV-ICD during median sternotomy for surgical myectomy. Their case differed from ours in that the substernal lead remained leftward and outside the direct operative field, allowing the procedure to proceed without extensive adhesiolysis or intentional lead repositioning. In contrast, our case involved CABG in the setting of adhesions in the region of the substernal lead and the left internal mammary bed, requiring direct lead dissection, abandonment of LIMA harvest in favor of RIMA use, active lead repositioning and fixation, and a modified sternal closure strategy designed to maintain separation from the lead electrodes.9 Liu et al7 reported a novel hybrid procedure in which an EV-ICD was implanted at the time of CABG by creating a tunnel in the anterior mediastinum protected by a fat pad before sternal closure. Moltrasio et al10 described the first worldwide case of intraoperative EV-ICD placement during open-heart surgery for endocarditis, with the lead sutured directly to the cardiac epicardium to ensure stability in the absence of a pre-formed tunnel. Markman et al11 also reported EV-ICD implantation in a patient with challenging anatomy (prior abdominal surgery and ascites). These reports highlight that EV-ICD therapy can be extended to unconventional scenarios. However, aside from the recent report by Collings et al9, the published surgical experience has largely involved de novo EV-ICD implantation rather than preservation of a pre-existing system.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of preserving a functioning EV-ICD lead during CABG surgery. This was accomplished through careful multidisciplinary planning. The primary surgical challenge was safely removing the insulated lead from dense adhesions involving the LIMA and chest wall. In our case, the LIMA was unable to be safely harvested, so a RIMA graft was used to bypass the left anterior descending. Unlike transvenous leads, which are intravascular, the EV-ICD lead relies on a specific spatial relationship with the RV and the coil to generate an effective shock vector. The cardiac surgeons carefully dissected the substernal lead from scar tissue without damaging the insulation or conductor. The lead was re-routed superficially to avoid the atrium and secured in a position over the RV outflow tract, which likely minimized atrial oversensing by maximizing the distance from the atria while maintaining the critical shock vector across the ventricular mass.

A critical technical modification in this case was the sternal closure strategy. For sternal closure, rigid plating was chosen instead of standard wires. Standard sternal wires can create electromagnetic interference or short-circuit the high-voltage therapy if they come into direct contact with the substernal coil. Rigid plates mitigated this risk by providing a fixed, non-mobile closure that could be placed under direct visualization, helping maintain a physical separation of >1 cm from the lead electrodes.

Another consideration was defibrillation efficacy and the timing of testing. We deferred DFT testing to allow postoperative healing. Immediate post-bypass DFT testing is often confounded by transient changes in impedance due to mediastinal air, tissue edema, and myocardial stunning, which can elevate DFTs and impair sensitivity. At 6 weeks post-CABG, the patient underwent elective DFT testing, which revealed a successful defibrillation at 30 J with appropriate sensing at 0.45 mV. His R-wave amplitude was 0.7 mV, and there was no evidence of P-wave oversensing. For comparison, at initial implant, his R-wave was 2.0 mV. This suggests that despite the disruption of the original tissue plane and subsequent scar formation, defibrillation performance remained intact. Follow-up device checks showed appropriate sensitivity and no electrical noise. Although these findings support the feasibility of EV-ICD lead preservation during CABG, the available follow-up remains limited. Longer-term follow-up is needed to confirm continued lead stability, stable sensing and impedance measurements, and absence of delayed lead-related complications after surgical healing.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that an existing substernal EV-ICD lead can be safely preserved during open-heart surgery and median sternotomy with careful surgical technique and planning. The EV-ICD remained fully functional by freeing the lead from adhesions, repositioning it to avoid atrial contact, and using rigid sternal plating to minimize electromagnetic interference. DFT testing after 6 weeks confirmed effective VF sensing and termination, and subsequent follow-up showed normal device function without noise.
