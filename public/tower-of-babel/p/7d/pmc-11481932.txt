Invited Perspective: Drilling Down into the Mechanisms Linking Oil and Gas Development to Adverse Perinatal Health Outcomes
Authors: Joan A Casey, Mary D Willis
Published: 2024

Almost 5% of the US population resides within 1.6km (1 mi) of active oil and gas development.1 A growing body of literature demonstrates associations between such residential proximity and adverse health outcomes on a national scale.2–4 State-specific studies have used residential address data to assign exposure to oil and gas wells based on proximity and well activity (e.g., production volumes), documenting associations between perinatal exposure and adverse birth outcomes.5–11 However, the specific exposure pathways linking oil and gas development and population health remain understudied.12 In a study published in this issue of Environmental Health Perspectives,13 Poole and Boland homed in on a subset of oil and gas development (hydraulically fractured wells) and one potential pathway (endocrine-disrupting chemicals) related to county-level birth outcomes.

Operators break open rock formations during hydraulic fracturing (“fracking”) to release oil and gas reserves trapped inside. To do this, they inject millions of gallons of fluid containing proppants (materials designed to keep fractures open, e.g., sand) and other chemicals (e.g., benzene, formaldehyde).14 Many of these chemicals have endocrine-disrupting properties.15–17 Population health researchers know less than they would like about frac fluid chemicals because disclosure laws often permit companies to protect trade secrets and not release the list of chemical additives used.18 However, some states require oil and gas companies to deposit chemical additive data into FracFocus (https://fracfocus.org/), a publicly available centralized database.19 Using data from FracFocus, Poole and Boland connected specific chemicals from frac fluid to their health-harming properties via ontological linkages.17,20 To date, we are unaware of any other study that has leveraged publicly available frac fluid data to estimate potential population-level exposure, and this step marks a major achievement for exposure science.

Exposure assessment considers not just the chemicals involved but also the frequency, duration, and intensity of exposure. Human exposures most likely involve process issues such as fluid spills, casing failures, or leakage from wastewater pits;21,22 thus, aside from occupational exposures, frequency of exposure may be low. This is supported by the environmental sampling of water quality near oil and gas development.23 Because the fracturing process typically lasts 3–5 d of the multiple years a well may operate, the duration of exposure to frac fluid chemicals may be limited.24 In the case of groundwater contamination resulting from well failure, however, exposure intensity is likely high and potentially persistent.

Despite advances in exposure assessment, Poole and Boland used county-level exposure measures, which do not necessarily align well with population locations. We illustrate this potential mismatch, which can result in exposure misclassification, using publicly available oil and gas development data25 in two US counties with substantial counts of active oil and gas wells as examples (Figure 1). A county-level exposure would be more accurate in Bradford County given its more even distribution of wells compared with Denton County, where most wells are in the west and the population-weighted centroid is in the east. In addition, fracking exposures, including water contamination, likely do not adhere to administrative county boundaries, and counties adjacent to those containing many wells may also experience health-relevant exposure levels (Figure 1). Therefore, many prior studies of oil and gas development and adverse birth outcomes have used full residential addresses for exposure assessment.5–11 In contrast, Poole and Boland assigned exposure based on wells located only within a specific county, not considering exposures from neighboring counties. The presence of chemicals in the county does not necessarily result in human exposure from the environment. Interpretation is also muddied by the cumulative measure of the fracking sites from 2001 to 2018 in each county for the primary analyses, further contributing to exposure misclassification. Thus, the results of the authors’ study should be interpreted with these important limitations in mind.

Poole and Boland linked their chemical exposure assessment to county-level birth outcome measures, broadly concluding that a higher density of hydraulic fracturing sites using estrogen-targeting chemicals was associated with increased rates of preterm birth. We caution readers to interpret these results in the context of several of the study’s epidemiologic limitations. The results provide a snapshot of the prevalence of preterm birth and low birth weight between 2015 and 2018. With the justification of studying outcomes in rural locations, where most fracking takes place,28,29 the authors relied on aggregated prevalence between 2015 and 2018 owing to low counts. This challenged their ability to make meaningful statements about temporality between exposure and outcome, and the lack of individual data prevented them from conducting a more conventional survival analysis for birth outcomes, evaluating critical windows of exposure, or isolating etiologies of preterm birth.

Further, the authors lacked the underlying counts of adverse events and ran linear models with prevalence as the outcome, thus treating counties containing 10,000 individuals the same as those with 100,000 individuals. This can cause problems when counts are very low, and an absolute change in preterm birth (e.g., a few additional) can result in a large change in prevalence (e.g., a 20% increase). The authors estimated that a unit increase in density of wells using testosterone-targeting chemicals was associated with more than a standard deviation increase in preterm birth prevalence (an absolute 3.2% increase). In general, a statistical model for counts with a population offset or using spatial fixed effects could avoid treating units with very different populations as the same. Spatial autocorrelation is likely a problem, potentially resulting in artificially narrow 95% confidence intervals and increased risk of type I error.30 With these limitations in mind, we caution against a causal interpretation of this analysis.

Although future health studies on fracking should continue to consider chemical exposures, this work should also consider the host of other potential hazards the industry produces. Existing literature has demonstrated that air pollution emitted by oil and gas development can travel broadly.31,32 Previous work has demonstrated the potential influence of exposure pathways—such as flaring, which can occur for days on end—for perinatal health.33 Persistent nighttime light and noise from oil and gas industrial activity can influence community stress, anxiety, and sleep quality.34–38 Economic development often coincides with environmental changes wrought by the oil and gas industry, creating a cycle of booms and busts in the community and resulting in changes in population health.29,39 Little work to date has sought to disentangle this complex mixture of co-occurring exposures.

Beyond this specific paper, the growing body of epidemiologic evidence on oil and gas development demonstrates consistent health harms to local communities.40,41 This industry is disproportionately sited near persistently marginalized populations, exacerbating a wide array of environmental injustices.42–44 Poole and Boland’s article offers preliminary insights into understanding mechanisms by which the oil and gas industry may cause harm, which is important to advancing health-protective policy.
