Comparative Diagnostic Accuracy of the Braden and Cubbin/Jackson Scales for Predicting Pressure Injury Development in an Adult Intensive Care Unit: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Gizem Yağmur Yalçın, Meryem Koçaslan Toran, İlayda Okyanuz, Sude Dilan Antep, Hakan Parlak, İsmail Toygar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study evaluated and compared the diagnostic accuracy of the Braden and Cubbin/Jackson Pressure Injury Risk Scales for predicting pressure injury development in adult ICU patients. In this prospective cohort study, 153 patients admitted to an adult ICU between September and November 2025 were assessed within 24 h using both scales and followed during their ICU stay. Diagnostic accuracy indices were calculated across cut‐off points and optimal cut‐offs were selected using the Youden J index. ROC curves were compared using the DeLong test. Precision–recall analysis and adjusted logistic regression analyses were also performed. Pressure injury developed in 37 patients (24.2%). The optimal Braden cut‐off was ≤ 12, with sensitivity 43.2%, specificity 67.2%, PPV 29.6%, NPV 78.8%, LR+ 1.32, LR− 0.84 and Youden J 0.105. The optimal Cubbin/Jackson cut‐off was ≤ 35, with sensitivity 73.0%, specificity 50.9%, PPV 32.1%, NPV 85.5%, LR+ 1.49, LR− 0.53 and Youden J 0.238. AUCs were 0.546 (95% CI: 0.439–0.653) and 0.605 (95% CI: 0.503–0.707), respectively; the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.187). Precision‐recall analysis showed limited predictive performance and adjusted logistic regression analyses indicated that neither cut‐off was a statistically significant independent predictor of pressure injury development (p > 0.05). Both scales showed limited discriminatory accuracy. Cubbin/Jackson performed numerically better, but not significantly so; therefore, both scales should support, rather than replace, comprehensive clinical judgement in intensive care nursing.Keywords: Braden Scale, Cubbin/Jackson Scale, pressure injury, risk assessment

Introduction

Pressure injury (PI) is typically characterised as localised damage to the skin or underlying soft tissue in proximity to a bony prominence, or as a result of excessive pressure from medical or other devices [1]. Globally, the incidence of hospital‐acquired pressure injuries is reported to be approximately 5.4 per 10 000 patients per day. The prevalence of pressure injuries in hospitalised adults ranges from 10%–18%, with 6%–9% of patients developing a newly occurring pressure injury during their hospital stay [2, 3].

Despite considerable endeavours to establish quality healthcare standards and international guidelines, hospital‐acquired pressure injuries persist in their clinical significance. PIs, while potentially preventable, continue to be among the most prevalent adverse events in healthcare settings [4]. Furthermore, PIs have been shown to prolong hospital stays, complicate care processes and increase the financial and organisational burden on healthcare institutions [5, 6].

Intensive care units are a clinical setting in which the incidence of pressure injury is particularly high. Research has indicated that pressure injuries may develop in 9%–29% of patients during monitoring in these units, where critically ill patients are admitted [5, 7, 8]. This high incidence can be attributed to patients being exposed to multiple risk factors, including haemodynamic instability, immobility, incontinence and nutritional deficiencies [9].

Current guidelines for the assessment of the risk of pressure injury development recommend the utilisation of a valid and reliable risk assessment tool in conjunction with clinical judgement [10]. The Braden and Cubbin/Jackson Pressure Injury Risk Scales are among the most frequently utilised instruments in contemporary clinical practice. The strengths and limitations of these scales, particularly in the context of critically ill patients, are subjects of increasing discourse in the extant literature. The Braden Scale is the preferred option due to its comprehensive assessment of six components: moisture, activity, mobility, sensory perception, nutrition and friction/sliding. The Scale's ease of application in clinical practice is a significant advantage [11]. However, systematic reviews published in recent years have demonstrated that the Braden Scale can exhibit low sensitivity in intensive care patients, potentially complicating clinical decision‐making processes by frequently overestimating the risk level [12, 13]. Conversely, the Cubbin/Jackson Pressure Injury Risk Scale is emphasised as a tool that more comprehensively reflects the clinical reality of the critically ill population. This is due to the fact that it considers intensive care‐specific physiological and treatment‐related variables such as haemodynamic status, respiratory support, metabolic requirements, level of consciousness, mobility and medical treatments administered [14, 15]. In this context, a comparison of the sensitivity and specificity values of both scales will contribute to supporting clearer and evidence‐based clinical decisions regarding pressure injury risk assessment in critical care settings.

The variability observed in the performance of risk assessment scales across different countries and healthcare institutions highlights the importance of comparative studies. Sensitivity and specificity are fundamental indicators of the clinical reliability of a risk assessment tool [14]. It is vital to note that high sensitivity is essential for accurately identifying patients at risk of pressure injuries. In addition, high specificity is crucial to avoid overly broad initiation of preventive interventions at lower cut‐off thresholds, which may increase nursing workload and reduce cost‐effectiveness. Consequently, a systematic comparison of the diagnostic accuracy of the Braden and Cubbin/Jackson scales in intensive care patients is imperative for the development of nursing practices based on empirical evidence.

This study aimed to compare the diagnostic accuracy of the Braden and Cubbin/Jackson Pressure Injury Risk Scales for predicting pressure injury development in adult intensive care unit patients.

We hypothesised that the Cubbin/Jackson Scale would demonstrate superior diagnostic performance compared with the Braden Scale in predicting pressure injury development among critically ill patients due to its inclusion of intensive care–specific clinical variables.

Methods

Study Design

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted in the adult intensive care unit of a private hospital in Istanbul, Türkiye, between September and November 2025. Patients were assessed at ICU admission and prospectively followed for the development of pressure injuries during their ICU stay.

Participants and Setting

The study was conducted in a third‐level (tertiary) adult general intensive care unit of a private hospital in Istanbul, Türkiye. The unit has a total capacity of 10 beds and is staffed by five nurses providing continuous patient care. It is a multidisciplinary ICU that admits critically ill adult patients requiring advanced monitoring and life‐support interventions, including mechanical ventilation and vasoactive therapy.

Inclusion Criteria

Patients aged 18 years or older; an intensive care unit stay of at least 48 h; and documented risk assessments using both the Braden and Cubbin/Jackson Pressure Injury Risk Scales during hospitalisation.

Exclusion Criteria

Presence of a pressure injury at the time of intensive care unit admission; an intensive care unit stay of less than 48 h; incomplete records for either risk assessment scale; presence of widespread skin conditions complicating pressure injury assessment; and age under 18 years.

Sample Size Calculation

The required sample size was initially calculated as 128 patients, based on previously reported pressure injury prevalence rates of 20%–30% in intensive care units [5, 6, 7], with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. The sample size calculation was planned to provide sufficient precision for estimating the sensitivity and specificity of the Braden and Cubbin/Jackson scales. To account for potential attrition due to mortality, transfer from the intensive care unit, or incomplete data, recruitment was continued beyond the minimum required sample size. As a result, 153 patients were included in the final analysis. Although the final sample size exceeded the initially calculated requirement, the limited number of patients who developed pressure injuries should be considered when interpreting the precision and generalizability of the findings.

Data Collection

Data were collected using a sociodemographic and clinical information form, the Braden Pressure Injury Risk Scale and the Cubbin/Jackson Pressure Injury Risk Scale. The sociodemographic and clinical information form was developed based on the relevant literature on pressure injury risk factors in intensive care patients and included variables such as age, gender, body mass index, reason for intensive care unit admission, comorbidities, mechanical ventilation status, sedation status and use of inotropic agents.

Data were obtained from two sources: electronic patient records and direct clinical observation. Age, gender, body mass index, reason for admission, comorbidities, mechanical ventilation status, sedation status and inotropic therapy were extracted from the hospital's electronic medical record system. Body mass index was recorded as kg/m2. Direct observational data included pressure injury risk assessments using the Braden and Cubbin/Jackson scales and daily skin assessments for pressure injury development. Risk assessment scores were recorded once within the first 24 h of intensive care unit admission as part of routine clinical evaluation. Pressure injury occurrence was assessed through standardised daily skin inspections performed by trained nurses in accordance with institutional clinical protocols. Prior to data collection, the nurses responsible for pressure injury assessment received standardised training regarding the application of the Braden and Cubbin/Jackson scales and pressure injury classification procedures.

The Braden Pressure Injury Risk Scale assesses pressure injury risk across six domains (sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition and friction/shear), with total scores ranging from 6 to 23, where lower scores indicate higher risk [16]. The Cubbin/Jackson Pressure Injury Risk Scale, developed specifically for critically ill patients, includes intensive care–related clinical parameters and yields total scores ranging from 10 to 40, with lower scores indicating higher pressure injury risk [17].

During the data collection period, 178 patients admitted to the intensive care unit were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 25 patients were excluded based on the predefined criteria, including an intensive care unit stay of less than 48 h, development of pressure injury at admission, incomplete risk assessment records, or widespread skin conditions complicating pressure injury assessment. Consequently, 153 patients were included in the final analysis.

Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0, with additional statistical procedures applied where required for ROC curve comparison and confidence interval estimation. Analyses were performed on the full dataset of 153 patients. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed before analysis. Normally distributed continuous variables were summarised as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non‐normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were reported as number (n) and percentage (%).

Between‐group comparisons were performed according to pressure injury development status. For continuous variables, the independent samples t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test was used depending on the distribution of the data. For categorical variables, the χ 2 test was used and Fisher's exact test was applied where expected cell counts were small. All tests were two‐sided and a p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The screening performance of the Braden and Cubbin/Jackson total scores for predicting pressure injury development was evaluated across a range of cut‐off points. Patients with a total score of less than or equal to the relevant cut‐off value were classified as ‘at risk’ and pressure injury development during the intensive care unit stay was considered the positive outcome. For each cut‐off point, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio (LR+), negative likelihood ratio (LR−) and Youden J index were calculated using 2 × 2 contingency tables. Where applicable, 95% confidence intervals were calculated for diagnostic performance measures. The optimal cut‐off value for each scale was defined as the cut‐off point with the highest Youden J index.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminatory performance of the Braden and Cubbin/Jackson scales. The area under the curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each scale. The AUC values of the two scales were formally compared using the DeLong test. In addition, precision–recall analysis was performed as a supplementary analysis because of the relatively limited number of pressure injury events.

Logistic regression analyses were conducted to further examine the association between the Youden J index‐derived optimal cut‐off values and pressure injury development. Adjusted logistic regression models were performed by including age, sex, length of intensive care unit stay, inotrope treatment, infection status and sedation as covariates. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals. The reporting of the study was guided by the STARD 2015 recommendations [18].

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Istinye University (approval number: 2025‐216; meeting date: 03 September 2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study. For patients who were unable to provide informed consent due to impaired consciousness, consent was obtained from their legally authorised representatives.

Results

Pressure injury developed in 24.2% of the study population (37/153). The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the 153 intensive care patients included in the study were compared according to the development of pressure injuries (Table 1). No statistically significant differences were found between the groups with and without pressure injuries in terms of age, length of hospital stay, Braden total score and Cubbin/Jackson total score (p > 0.05 for all). There were also no significant differences between the groups in terms of gender, primary diagnosis distribution, inotropic therapy and sedation status (p > 0.05). The presence of infection was significantly higher in the group with pressure injuries (p < 0.001).

ROC analysis demonstrated poor discriminatory ability for both scales. The AUC was 0.546 (95% CI: 0.439–0.653) for the Braden Scale and 0.605 (95% CI: 0.503–0.707) for the Cubbin/Jackson Scale. Although the Cubbin/Jackson Scale had a numerically higher AUC than the Braden Scale, the difference between the ROC curves was not statistically significant according to the DeLong test (p = 0.187). These findings indicate that neither scale demonstrated strong discriminatory performance for predicting pressure injury development in this cohort. The Youden J index‐derived Braden cut‐off value of ≤ 12 was not significantly associated with pressure injury development (adjusted OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 0.40–3.02, p = 0.849). Discussion

The prediction of pressure injury risk in intensive care units is closely linked to how sensitivity and specificity are balanced within the risk assessment tools used to support clinical decision‐making. The findings of the present study demonstrate that increasing cut‐off points on the Braden Scale leads to higher sensitivity, accompanied by a marked reduction in specificity. The Cubbin/Jackson Scale showed a relatively more favourable sensitivity and NPV at the Youden J index‐derived cut‐off; however, its overall discriminatory performance remained limited. These findings suggest that reliance on sensitivity alone may be insufficient in settings where patients are exposed to multiple, concurrent risk factors. This observation is consistent with previous studies reporting limited specificity of the Braden Scale and a more balanced diagnostic performance of the Cubbin/Jackson Scale in critically ill populations [15]. Although Cubbin/Jackson total scores showed a trend towards lower values among patients who developed pressure injuries, neither scale demonstrated a clear ability to distinguish between patients with and without pressure injury based on raw total scores alone. This observation indicates that, in this sample, mean score comparisons may offer limited discriminative insight and that additional evaluation using cut‐off–based approaches may be informative in intensive care settings.

Analysis of the Braden Scale across different cut‐off values in this study highlights a clear trade‐off between sensitivity and specificity. While higher cut‐off scores improved sensitivity, this improvement was accompanied by a substantial reduction in specificity, resulting in an increased likelihood of false‐positive risk classifications. Conversely, lower cut‐off values achieved greater specificity but failed to identify a considerable proportion of patients who subsequently developed pressure injuries. The cut‐off value of ≤ 12, identified using the Youden J index, was therefore selected to balance sensitivity and specificity rather than to maximise sensitivity alone. Clinically, focusing on sensitivity without sufficient attention to specificity may result in unnecessary classification of patients as being at risk for pressure injury. This has been reported in previous studies describing the limited specificity of the Braden Scale in intensive care settings [11, 13].

In contrast to the Braden Scale, the Cubbin/Jackson Scale demonstrated a more stable sensitivity–specificity relationship across the range of cut‐off values examined. However, it is important to interpret these findings with caution. Although the Cubbin/Jackson Scale demonstrated slightly better performance than the Braden Scale, the overall discriminatory ability of both instruments remained limited. The AUC values obtained in this study (0.546 for the Braden Scale and 0.605 for the Cubbin/Jackson Scale) indicate poor to modest discrimination. Therefore, neither scale demonstrated sufficient accuracy to serve as a stand‐alone predictor of pressure injury development in critically ill patients. In the present study, the cut‐off value with the highest Youden J index for the Cubbin/Jackson Scale was ≤ 35, providing higher sensitivity and NPV than the Braden Scale, although specificity remained modest. The relatively better performance of the Cubbin/Jackson Scale may be related to the inclusion of intensive care–specific clinical variables, such as haemodynamic status, respiratory support and level of consciousness, which are not captured by more general risk assessment tools. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies demonstrating improved risk classification with the Cubbin/Jackson Scale in intensive care settings [14, 19]. Recent systematic reviews and validation studies have reported variable diagnostic performance for both scales in intensive care populations. Reported AUC values for the Braden Scale in critically ill patients have ranged from poor to moderate discrimination, while the Cubbin/Jackson Scale has generally demonstrated slightly higher predictive performance in ICU‐specific settings. However, substantial variability between studies and clinical settings remains, highlighting the need for further comparative validation studies [20]. The relatively low diagnostic performance observed in the present study may differ from findings reported in some previous validation studies. Several factors may explain these differences, including the relatively small sample size, variability in patient case mix, differences in pressure injury prevention protocols and characteristics specific to the study setting. In addition, intensive care populations are highly heterogeneous and local clinical practices may substantially influence the predictive performance of risk assessment tools. Variation in sensitivity and specificity across cut‐off values indicates that thresholds derived from ROC analysis need to be interpreted within the clinical context, rather than applied as fixed values across different patient groups [15, 21].

Although the Cubbin/Jackson Scale had a numerically higher AUC than the Braden Scale, the DeLong test showed that this difference was not statistically significant. Therefore, the present findings do not support a clear superiority of the Cubbin/Jackson Scale over the Braden Scale, but rather suggest that both instruments had limited discriminatory performance in this cohort.

The precision–recall analysis provided a complementary perspective, particularly given the relatively limited number of pressure injury events. The AUPRC values were only modestly above the observed pressure injury incidence, indicating that neither scale achieved strong positive predictive performance. This finding is consistent with the low PPV values observed at the Youden J index‐derived cut‐off points.

The likelihood ratios also support this cautious interpretation. For both scales, LR+ values were close to 1, indicating limited ability to rule in pressure injury risk. Although the Cubbin/Jackson Scale had a lower LR− than the Braden Scale, the value was still not sufficiently low to confidently rule out pressure injury development.

In the exploratory adjusted logistic regression analyses, the Youden J index‐derived cut‐off values of the Braden and Cubbin/Jackson scales were not statistically significant independent predictors of pressure injury development after adjustment for selected clinical covariates. This finding further suggests that pressure injury development in critically ill patients is likely influenced by multiple interacting clinical factors that may not be fully captured by risk assessment scale scores alone.

These findings are consistent with previous systematic reviews. Wei et al. and Zhang et al. also reported limited independent predictive validity for both scales in ICU populations, with neither scale achieving excellent discrimination [15, 20]. Clinically, this suggests that risk scales should inform, rather than replace, nursing judgement, a position also advocated by Kottner and Coleman [21].

An additional finding of this study was the presence of infection among patients with pressure injuries. As the source and timing of infection were not assessed, it is not possible to determine whether infection contributed to the development of pressure injuries or occurred as a consequence of them. Large‐scale intensive care studies have shown that clinical conditions commonly associated with infection and immune compromise frequently coexist with pressure injury development, suggesting an overlap of underlying risk profiles in critically ill patients [5]. In parallel, evidence from overviews of reviews indicates that infection‐related and inflammatory conditions are repeatedly identified as factors associated with hospital‐acquired pressure injuries, while pressure injuries themselves may predispose patients to secondary infection [4]. Therefore, this association should be interpreted with caution and further studies incorporating detailed assessment of infection source and timing are required to clarify the direction of this relationship. In the adjusted models, infection status showed the most consistent association with pressure injury development; however, because the timing and source of infection were not assessed, this association should not be interpreted as causal.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the study was conducted in a single adult intensive care unit, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other intensive care settings with different patient profiles, staffing patterns and pressure injury prevention protocols. Second, although the final sample size exceeded the initially calculated minimum requirement, the number of patients who developed pressure injuries was limited. This may have affected the precision of the estimates, particularly for cut‐off‐specific performance measures, likelihood ratios and adjusted regression analyses. Third, external validation was not performed. Therefore, the Youden J index‐derived cut‐off values identified in this study, particularly Braden ≤ 12 and Cubbin/Jackson ≤ 35, should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger, multicentre cohorts before being adopted as fixed thresholds in clinical practice. Fourth, although adjusted logistic regression analyses were performed, the limited number of pressure injury events restricted the number of covariates that could be included in the models. Therefore, residual confounding by unmeasured clinical factors such as nutritional status, severity of illness, vasopressor dose, mechanical ventilation parameters, detailed mobility status and positioning practices cannot be excluded. Fifth, formal inter‐rater reliability assessment was not performed for pressure injury assessment or scale administration during the study period. Although the assessments were conducted in routine clinical practice, the absence of formal reliability testing may have introduced measurement variability. Sixth, potential classification or documentation bias should be considered, as pressure injury detection and staging in critically ill patients may be influenced by rapid changes in skin condition, haemodynamic instability, sedation level and the complexity of care delivery. Finally, infection status was included as a clinical covariate; however, the timing, source and severity of infection were not assessed in detail. Therefore, the observed association between infection and pressure injury development should not be interpreted as causal. Future studies with larger samples, multicentre designs, external validation, standardised assessor training and formal inter‐rater reliability assessment are needed to confirm the predictive performance and clinical applicability of these scales in critically ill patients.

Implications for Clinical Practice

The findings of this study suggest that cut‐off values should be interpreted according to clinical priorities and the characteristics of the intensive care population. For the Braden Scale, higher cut‐off values increased sensitivity but substantially reduced specificity, whereas lower cut‐off values improved specificity at the expense of missing a greater proportion of patients who developed pressure injuries. The Youden J index‐derived optimal cut‐off value for the Braden Scale was ≤ 12; however, its sensitivity and overall discriminatory performance were limited.

For the Cubbin/Jackson Scale, the Youden J index‐derived optimal cut‐off value was ≤ 35, which provided higher sensitivity and NPV than the Braden Scale, although specificity remained modest. This suggests that the Cubbin/Jackson Scale may be more useful when the clinical priority is early identification of patients who may be at risk. However, the modest AUC values, low PPV values and likelihood ratios close to 1 indicate that neither scale should be used as a stand‐alone predictor of pressure injury development.

In clinical practice, higher cut‐off values may be considered when the aim is to maximise early risk identification, while lower cut‐off values may reduce false‐positive classifications. Nevertheless, the cut‐off values identified in this study should be applied cautiously and used alongside comprehensive clinical assessment, professional judgement and patient‐specific risk factors. Further validation in larger and multicentre intensive care cohorts is needed before these thresholds can be recommended as fixed clinical decision points.

Conclusion

Both the Braden and Cubbin/Jackson scales demonstrated limited discriminatory performance in predicting pressure injury development among critically ill patients. Although the Cubbin/Jackson Scale showed numerically higher sensitivity, NPV and AUC values than the Braden Scale at the Youden J index‐derived cut‐off value, the difference between the ROC curves was not statistically significant. Therefore, the findings do not support the use of either scale as a stand‐alone predictor of pressure injury development. These tools should instead be used alongside comprehensive clinical assessment, professional judgement and patient‐specific clinical risk factors. Further multicentre studies with larger samples and external validation are needed to confirm the applicability of the identified cut‐off values and to improve risk prediction in intensive care settings.
