Feasibility and safety of cortical access point biopsy during hydrocephalus shunt surgery
Authors: Ramy Amirah, Da Bin Kim, Maide Pinar, Laura Krismer, Daniel Erny, Marius Schwabenland, Marco Prinz, Vidhya Madapusi Ravi, Kevin Joseph, Jürgen Beck, Jakob Straehle, Mukesch Johannes Shah
Published: 2025

Introduction

Hydrocephalus is a condition where cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) accumulates in the brain's ventricles, leading to increased intracranial pressure and potential neurological issues. It is classified into obstructive (non-communicating) and communicating types. Obstructive hydrocephalus is due to a blockage in CSF pathways, while communicating hydrocephalus occurs despite open pathways due to impaired CSF absorption (Nguyen and Cohen, 2015; McAllister et al., 2015). Recently, in 2023, the ASPECT classification for hydrocephalus was introduced to provide a more precise and practical description of the condition. It enhances diagnosis and treatment by linking different types of hydrocephalus to their physiological mechanisms and clinical manifestations (Juhler et al., 2021). Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH), or Hakim-Adams syndrome, is a unique form of hydrocephalus and treatable dementia (Hakim and Adams, 1965). It presents with gait disturbance, urinary incontinence, and cognitive impairment, despite normal cerebrospinal fluid pressure (Nakajima et al., 2019). NPH, which primarily affects older adults, shows an increased comorbidity rate (40–75 %) with other neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease, or subcortical arteriosclerotic encephalopathy, significantly influencing the progression prognosis (Dersch et al., 2022). Moreover, there is growing evidence suggesting that a mixed presentation of these diseases may exist, or that disrupted CSF dynamics negatively impact all these conditions, potentially contributing to their causation (Bech-Azeddine et al., 2006). These patients are likely not to respond to CSF shunting and may therefore be referred to as shunt non-responders (Hasselbalch et al., 2023). Diagnosing idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (iNPH) is crucial due to its potential reversibility through surgery, distinguishing it from AD or other neurodegenerative diseases. iNPH presents with a set of symptoms that can overlap with those seen in AD (Nagata et al., 2018). Accurate differentiation between iNPH and other forms of dementia is essential, as misdiagnosis may lead to inappropriate treatment strategies that do not address the underlying hydrocephalus or may result in possible surgical overtreatment (Nagata et al., 2018; Hiraldo-González et al., 2021).

Cortical brain biopsy can be a valuable tool in the diagnosis of neurodegenerative diseases, especially in cases of suspected iNPH (Venneti et al., 2011). Studies have shown that the presence of AD pathology, such as β-amyloid (Aβ) and hyperphosphorylated tau, in frontal lobe biopsies of NPH patients strongly correlates with clinical AD in a significant proportion of cases (Greenberg et al., 2024). This suggests that cortical biopsy can help identify an underlying neurodegenerative pathology in NPH patients, which is crucial for guiding treatment and predicting outcomes (Venneti et al., 2011; Elobeid et al., 2015).

Cortical biopsy in hydrocephalus patients can be performed using endoscopic, stereotactic, or open surgical methods. Open biopsy carries risks such as infection, hemorrhage, and worsening of hydrocephalus, potentially requiring further intervention. Careful risk assessment is essential, as postoperative complications may include neurological deficits and cerebrospinal fluid leakage (Cofano et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2017).

Our access point biopsy utilizes a small punch knife with a diameter of 3 mm. Since the access site is already punctured as part of the procedure, we propose that an access point punch biopsy is the most viable option, ensuring that no significant additional brain tissue is damaged or extracted. With the access point biopsy, there is no need to alter or enlarge the amount of bone removed; any burr hole made for shunt insertion is sufficient.

The aim of the study was to evaluate the feasibility and safety of small cortical access point punch biopsies during a ventriculoperitoneal (VP), ventriculoatrial (VA) shunt operation, or endoscopic third ventriculocisternostomy (ETV).

The study was conducted at the Department of Neurosurgery, Medical Center - University of Freiburg. Ethical approval for a retrospective data analysis was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Medical Center Freiburg (24-1471-S1-retro).

Study variables and outcomes

The primary endpoint was the frequency of brain biopsy-related complications and whether the biopsy could be analyzed neuropathologically. Secondary endpoints included the frequency of obtaining a final diagnosis and the frequency of obtaining a specific histological diagnosis. Histological results of brain biopsies were categorized into three groups based on the presence of pathological markers: findings indicating β-amyloid deposits and hyperphosphorylated tau protein suggested diseases such as Alzheimer's that may accompany hydrocephalus; changes limited to unspecific gliosis within the cortex without evidence of specific neurodegenerative pathology; and samples showing no pathological alterations were classified as normal brain tissue.. A specific lesion was defined as a biopsy finding indicative of a disease accompanying hydrocephalus, such as AD. Nonspecific lesions were defined as nonspecific gliotic changes in the cortex without evidence of specific neurodegenerative neuronal disease. Patient data (diagnosis, age, biopsy results) were collected for all patients. To evaluate the safety of the method, the following parameters were collected: evidence of postoperative biopsy-associated complications in the first postoperative imaging (inpatient or outpatient within 3 months postoperatively), and documentation of biopsy-associated complications.

Statistics

We performed statistical analyses using SPSS version 25. The analysis included descriptive statistics, calculating frequencies, percentages, and standard deviations of selected parameters. No inferential statistical tests were applied.

Access point biopsy technique

All patients undergoing CSF shunt implantation or ventriculocisternostomy received at least one preoperative cranial CT (cCT) or cranial MRI (cMRI) to confirm their diagnosis. The indication for CSF shunt (VP or VA) or ventriculocisternostomy was determined by a board-certified neurosurgeon which is confirmed in an interdisciplinary board. The preferred site for a standard VP or VA shunt is the Kocher's point above the frontal cortex, typically on the right, unless there are indications for the left. Similarly, a ventriculocisternostomy is typically performed near the Kocher's point on the right. After clipping, disinfecting, and draping, a burr hole trepanation is performed using an AESCULAP® CRANIAL drill (ELAN 4) with a medium drill (Ø 9/12 mm). In the case of an endoscopic ventriculocisternostomy, a larger burr hole (12/15 mm) was used. After trepanation at Kocher's point, the dura mater is superficially coagulated. After a cross-shaped dura opening, a 3mm punch (e.g., PFM KAI Dermal Punch) is inserted into the superior frontal gyrus of the cortex, positioned perpendicularly and away from the vessels, without prior coagulation. With a slight twisting motion, a cylindrical piece is separated from the cortex. The sample can be retrieved after cutting using a dissector. Neuropathological examination

Tissue samples were fixed in formaldehyde 4 % and embedded into paraffin blocks (FFPE). Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described (Schwabenland et al., 2021, 2024). In short, 3 μm thick sections were prepared and deparaffinized in xylene. For Beta-A4 immunohistochemistry, objective slides were treated with formic acid for 5 minutes (Carl Roth, cat. # 4724.1). Immunohistochemistry for both Beta-A4 and TAU was performed using the EnVision Flex Kit (DAKO, Agilent, cat. #K8000) and a DAKO Autostainer Link 48 system. Beta-A4 sections were cooked in EnVision High pH pre-treatment buffer for 40 minutes. No pre-treatment was performed for TAU immunohistochemistry. After blocking, slides were incubated with primary antibodies for 30 minutes at room temperature (Beta-A4: DAKO, cat. #M0872, dilution, 1:100. TAU: Invitrogen, cat. # MN1020, 1:100). Incubated with FLEX HRP Polymer lasted for 20 minutes. Slides were then incubated in DAB substrate for 10 minutes. Specimens were counterstained with Gill's Haematoxylin solution (Morphisto, cat. 11769.02500). Vitro-Clud (R. Langenbrinck GmbH, cat. # 04-0001) served as mounting medium.

Results

In 15 patients (22.4 %), the operative diagnosis was hydrocephalus malresorptivus, resulting from various pathologies such as subarachnoid hemorrhage or intracerebral hemorrhage with bleeding into the brain's ventricular system. In a cohort of 67 patients, postoperative follow-up revealed no complications in 55 cases (82.1 %). Early complications were observed in 12 patients (17.9 %). Discussion

Our study demonstrates that cortical access point biopsies performed during cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) shunt implantation or endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) are both feasible and safe. Histological examination of these 3 mm punch biopsies, taken at the precise site of ventricular catheter insertion, revealed normal brain tissue in 22.3 % of cases, AD neuropathologic change were found in 6 % of cases, β-amyloid deposits along and nonspecific gliotic changes were observed in 64.2 %, cerebral amyloid angiopathy in 1.5 % and metastatic neoplasia in 1.5 %.

These biopsies, measuring 3 mm in diameter at the precise site of shunt insertion and almost the same size as the shunt catheter itself (2.5 mm), while also being suitable for histopathological examination. Importantly, the procedure required only 2–3 additional minutes of surgical time. The use of a 3 mm punch allowed for precise tissue sampling within the burr hole, ensuring selection of a gyral area free of larger vessels, which helped minimize intraoperative challenges. Hemostasis was achieved effortlessly, both at the biopsy site and during ventricular catheter placement.

Postoperative clinical evaluations, conducted in both inpatient and outpatient settings within the three-month follow-up period, revealed no biopsy-associated complications, such as acute hemorrhages or new focal neurological deficits unrelated to shunt placement. Among the 60 patients who underwent postoperative imaging within this timeframe (49 cranial CTs and 11 cranial MRIs), no cortical contusions, acute epidural or subdural hemorrhages, or cortical/subcortical ischemic changes at the biopsy site were observed.

These findings are consistent with the study by Leinone et al., who also published research in 2010 describing cortical biopsies taken during shunt placement. Their study reported biopsy-associated complications in only 3 out of 468 cases (0.6 %), with one to three cylindrical cortical brain biopsies obtained using biopsy forceps, measuring 2–5 mm in diameter and 3–7 mm in length (Leinonen et al., 2010). Given our results, the access point biopsy technique appears to be a safe approach, as no biopsy-related complications were identified in our cohort.

Early shunt-related complications occurred in 12 patients (17.9 %), including ventricular catheter malposition or disconnection in 4 cases (6 %), all of which required revision surgery.

7 patients experienced mild overdrainage, 5 of whom had accompanying subdural hygroma and additional signs of overdrainage, such as slit ventricles. The presence of a subdural hygroma on postoperative CT or MRI scans in 5 cases may be considered a potential biopsy-related complication. However, in our cases, subdural hygromas were either associated with slit ventricles or appeared bilaterally, suggesting that they were more likely a consequence of overdrainage rather than a direct complication of the biopsy procedure. These cases were successfully managed through valve pressure adjustments without the need for secondary surgical interventions. No severe overdrainage necessitating hematoma evacuation or further surgery was observed, and all affected patients showed clinical and imaging improvements following valve regulation.

Compared to reported revision rates in previous studies, shunt malfunctions remain a common challenge, occurring in 26 % of cases with valveless VP shunts and 29 % in valve-regulated systems (Tervonen et al., 2021). Furthermore, catheter disconnection and intraperitoneal migration have been documented in up to 47.1 % of cases (Ishiguro et al., 2021). Similarly, postoperative VP shunt infection rates typically range between 3 % and 12 % (Lee et al., 2012). In our cohort, one case of purulent VP shunt infection caused by Staphylococcus aureus was identified a known complication of shunt placement. However, none of these complications were associated with the cortical biopsy procedure.

After evaluating all cases and short-term postoperative outcomes up to three months postoperatively, an uncomplicated course regarding the shunt was documented in 55 patients (82.1 %), allowing discharge to home care or rehabilitative treatment. Additionally, 60 patients underwent at least one postoperative outpatient follow-up, with no biopsy-related complications reported.

Based on the analysis of these findings, the safety and accessibility of the method were confirmed.

Of the 67 biopsies, 3 (4.5 %) were not analyzed due to technical reasons, such as the sample size being too small or containing only blood from a subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) or bone fragments, making adequate histopathological examination impossible. This number is significantly lower compared to the work of Greenberg et al., where 10 out of 49 biopsies (20.4 %) were uninvestigable due to technical reasons, and slightly higher than in the study by Leinone et al., where 6 out of 468 biopsies (1.3 %) were uninvestigable (Greenberg et al., 2024; Leinonen et al., 2010).

These results demonstrate that our technique is highly effective and reliably yields tissue samples suitable for detailed histological analysis.

One of the main findings from the biopsies in our study was the presence of β-amyloid and tau protein in the biopsy samples, particularly among iNPH patients, alongside normal brain tissue.

β-amyloid and tau proteins serve as key biomarkers in AD research. β-amyloid plays a central role in the development of amyloid plaques, while tau is associated with neurofibrillary tangles, characteristic of tauopathies (Jack et al., 2018; Budd et al., 2022). The amyloid cascade hypothesis centers on the accumulation of β-amyloid and the aggregation of tau, suggesting that these processes play a crucial role in driving neuronal toxicity and the progression of cognitive decline (Alzheimer's disease facts and figures, 2023).

The AD neuropathological changes follow the revised National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association guidelines, which introduce the ABC scoring system to assess the severity of Alzheimer's disease (AD). This system evaluates A (Amyloid-β deposits), B (Neurofibrillary tangles), and C (Neuritic plaques) separately, with each component graded from minimal (A0, B0, C0) to severe (A3, B3, C3). The classification helps establish the likelihood that AD pathology contributes to cognitive impairment, recognizing that some individuals may exhibit significant neuropathological changes without clinical symptoms (Montine et al., 2012; The Consortium to Establish a).

Out of all 67 biopsies, 15 (22.3 %) One case of cerebral amyloid angiopathy was identified in a patient with hydrocephalus malresorptivus associated with a subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), which was also previously undiagnosed. A known metastatic case indicated possible neoplastic cells.

Among the 44 iNPH patients, β-amyloid deposits and tauopathy were identified in four cases (9.1 %) as signs of AD neuropathological change, requiring further clinical correlation. Another 12 patients (27.2 %) exhibited β-amyloid deposits, suggesting an underlying non-specific neurodegenerative process, albeit without definitive tauopathy.

Overall, in at least five cases, the biopsy revealed a suspected secondary diagnosis that had not been clinically identified—Alzheimer's disease or cerebral amyloid angiopathy—necessitating further specific evaluations and treatment strategies in addition to the implanted VP shunt.

While β-amyloid deposition in 14 cases warrants further investigation in the context of other neurodegenerative diseases, cortical β-amyloid deposits are characteristic features of AD and other neurodegenerative disorders (Jung et al., 2014). Early identification of β-amyloid deposits could aid in identifying patients at higher risk for cognitive decline, as well as provide a better prognosis assessment (Kim et al., 2020).

The importance of cortical biopsy during shunt surgery for hydrocephalus patients lies in its ability to provide critical pathological information that can guide subsequent treatment decisions, particularly in cases where concomitant neurodegenerative disease or other intracranial pathologies are suspected (Shao et al., 2024). This approach not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but may also contribute to better long-term prognoses by addressing both hydrocephalus and any coexisting cerebral abnormalities (Jang et al., 2018). Furthermore, research has shown that patients with comorbid AD experience a less favorable long-term prognosis following shunt surgery compared to those without AD (Hamilton et al., 2010; Kanemoto et al., 2023). Specifically, the presence of AD-related pathological features, such as β-amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, correlates with diminished postoperative improvements in cognitive and functional measures (Hamilton et al., 2010). This association suggests that Alzheimer's pathology hampers shunt responsiveness and potentially indicates a more complex underlying cognitive impairment that shunting alone may not adequately address. By integrating access point biopsy with shunt placement in a single procedure, it is possible to obtain crucial pathological information without requiring a separate, invasive surgery.

Considering that 29.9 % of all biopsies showed pathological findings, particularly in iNPH cases where biopsy confirmed AD neuropathologic changes or indicated an ongoing neurodegenerative process, we emphasize the necessity of performing cortical punch biopsy during every CSF shunt procedure. Given the proven safety and very low complication nature of this method, integrating biopsy can enhance prognosis assessment and shunt responsiveness. For instance, in cases where histopathological signs of AD neuropathological changes are present along with a clinical diagnosis of Alzheimer's dementia, the need for shunt revision surgery should be critically reconsidered if cognitive improvement remains absent following CSF shunting in iNPH patients.

Limitation

Our study has some limitations. Firstly, it employs a retrospective design, with all inherent bias. Additionally, the follow-up period is relatively short, lasting only up to 3 months postoperatively. This short timeframe does not allow for the assessment of long-term outcomes and the identification of potential late complications. Furthermore, the sample size is small, with only 67 patients recruited over a short period of 18 months.

Although biopsy results may indicate a normal finding, neurodegenerative conditions such as AD or cerebral amyloid angiopathy cannot be entirely ruled out. This is because cortical biopsy, routinely performed during CSF shunt implantation, is taken from the frontal lobe, which may appear normal, while the pathology could predominantly affect other regions, such as the temporal or parietal lobes, or even remain confined to subcortical structures (Tan et al., 2019; Rostamzadeh and Jessen, 2020). A normal finding in the cortical biopsy from the frontal lobe during CSF shunt implantation does not rule out a coexisting dementia disorder. Therefore, in cases of clinical abnormalities, further investigations—such as PET-CT scans, including 18F-florbetaben PET or [11C]PIB PET —are advisable before surgery (Rinne et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2019).

Conclusion

Obtaining small cortical access point biopsies—i.e., similar in size to the catheter—at the access site of the catheter or endoscope during shunt surgery is a safe and feasible technique that provides crucial histological information about underlying pathologies, including Alzheimer's disease in patients with hydrocephalus, with minimal biopsy-associated complications.
