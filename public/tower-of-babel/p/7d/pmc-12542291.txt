The effect of hip-hop dance training on motor competence, physical activity, and enjoyment in early adolescent girls
Authors: Yasin Akinci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study addresses the declining physical activity participation among adolescent girls by evaluating the effects of a 14-week structured hip-hop dance intervention on motor competence, physical activity levels, and enjoyment of physical activity. Given that the competitive nature of traditional sports can alienate some students, especially girls, thereby decreasing overall participation and enjoyment, there is a need for culturally relevant and inclusive alternatives like hip-hop dance.MethodsThis quasi-experimental, pre-test-post-test control group study involved 192 seventh-grade girls (intervention group n = 96, control group n = 96). The intervention group participated in a 14-week hip-hop dance training program with three 90-minute sessions per week, while the control group continued with their regular school curriculum. Motor competence was measured using the Bruininks-Oseretsky Motor Proficiency Test, Second Edition (BOT-2), physical activity levels as daily step counts with pedometers, and enjoyment of physical activity with the Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale (PAES). Baseline equivalence between groups was tested using independent samples t-tests. Repeated measures MANOVA and ANOVA evaluated within- and between-group changes.ResultsAt baseline, groups were largely equivalent, with small, non-practically significant differences in manual dexterity and running speed & agility. Post-intervention, the dance group showed significant improvements in bilateral coordination (η² = 0.216), balance (η² = 0.147), running speed & agility (η² = 0.048), upper-limb coordination (η² = 0.126), and fine motor integration (η² = 0.032). No notable changes were observed for fine motor precision, manual dexterity, or body strength. Enjoyment increased significantly (η² = 0.027), and daily steps rose markedly (η² = 0.656) in the dance group compared to controls.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that a structured hip-hop dance intervention can significantly enhance motor competence, physical activity levels, and enjoyment of physical activity in adolescent girls. The findings highlight the potential of dance as an inclusive and motivating alternative to traditional physical education approaches. Programs should emphasize cultural relevance, program quality, and teacher training aligned with Self-Determination Theory to sustain benefits.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24875-7.

Introduction

Physical activity (PA) is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) [1] as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure, encompassing daily actions such as working, playing, household chores, transportation, and recreation. Adequate PA is essential for the physical, mental, and social health of individuals of all ages. In children and adolescents, regular PA supports motor skill development, healthy weight maintenance, cardiovascular health, bone density, and cognitive function [2, 3]. To promote these outcomes, WHO [4] recommends at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity per day for individuals aged 5–17 years, along with vigorous or strength-building exercises at least three times a week. However, post-2020 data highlights a global shortfall in meeting these recommendations. Over 80% of adolescents do not achieve the suggested PA levels [5]. National surveillance data indicate that only about 13–15% of adolescent girls in Turkey meet WHO PA guidelines, with participation dropping sharply during the transition from primary to secondary school [6]. This issue worsened during the COVID-19 pandemic due to reduced opportunities for structured movement [7–9]. Early inactivity patterns often continue into adulthood, increasing the risk of obesity, cardiovascular disease, and psychosocial problems [10, 11].

School-based physical education (PE) remains a central strategy for promoting physical activity among youth, given its wide reach and structured setting. Research consistently supports the idea that quality PE programs can enhance physical fitness, motor competence, and enjoyment of physical activity [12, 13]. Moreover, PE classes can instil lifelong movement skills and attitudes necessary for ongoing participation in physical activity. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of PE in achieving these goals is limited by several structural and pedagogical barriers. Time allocated to PE in many educational systems is insufficient to meet daily MVPA recommendations [14, 15]. Curricula often emphasize competitive sports, which may alienate less athletic students, especially girls, thereby decreasing overall participation and enjoyment [16, 17]. Teachers frequently face resource constraints, overcrowded classrooms, and a lack of training in delivering engaging, inclusive activities [18, 19]. Traditional pedagogies focused on repetitive skill acquisition may fail to motivate students or foster creativity and autonomy [20–22]. In Turkey, PE is typically limited to two 40–45-minute sessions per week, often dominated by team sports such as football and volleyball. This structure may not provide sufficient opportunities for girls who prefer non-competitive or creative movement activities, underscoring the need for alternative, culturally relevant programs [23, 24]. Indeed, research shows that adolescent girls are more likely to be motivated to participate in dance forms such as hip-hop because of its high-energy music and performance appeal [25, 26]. The current intervention was deliberately designed to align with the interests and cultural context of early adolescent girls, ensuring age-appropriate content and accessible movement patterns [27, 28].

Puberty marks a critical transition period during which girls are particularly vulnerable to disengaging from physical activity. Hormonal changes, body image concerns, and social pressures often result in decreased self-esteem and avoidance of structured or public physical activities [29, 30]. Furthermore, a lack of culturally relevant or socially supportive environments exacerbates this withdrawal [31]. One alternative strategy for engaging adolescent girls in physical activity is through hip-hop dance, a culturally resonant and expressive movement form. Hip-hop dance offers an accessible, non-competitive, and socially engaging alternative to traditional sports. It blends creativity, rhythm, and social connection, which can help reduce the psychological barriers that often deter girls from participating in PA [32, 33]. In the Turkish context, exposure to hip-hop dance through televised talent competitions and the influence of popular female singers’ dance crews can further enhance its motivational appeal among adolescent girls, making it both familiar and aspirational [34]. From a theoretical standpoint, the use of Self-Determination Theory (SDT) provides a valuable lens through which to understand and promote sustained engagement in dance activities. Self-Determination Theory (SDT) suggests that individuals are more likely to adopt and sustain behaviors when their fundamental psychological needs, autonomy (the experience of self-direction), competence (a sense of effectiveness), and relatedness (a feeling of social connection), are adequately fulfilled [35, 36]. The satisfaction of these needs is considered essential for promoting long-term adherence to physical activity. Hip-hop dance allows for personal expression and self-directed learning, thereby enhancing autonomy. As dancers learn and master new choreography, they build a sense of competence [28]. Group performances and peer interactions foster relatedness, creating a psychologically supportive environment that can bolster motivation and enjoyment [25, 37]. In this study, SDT principles were not only used to interpret motivational processes but also to guide instructional strategies, with choreographies and teaching methods intentionally structured to foster autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

Studies demonstrate that dance can contribute to improvements in cardiovascular endurance, muscular strength, flexibility, and coordination [38, 39]. Hip-hop dance often involves high-intensity intervals and complex movement patterns, which can meet or exceed MVPA thresholds [40]. In addition to aerobic benefits, dance, particularly hip-hop, enhances agility, balance, and reaction time, which are critical for overall motor competence. Research has demonstrated that dance education significantly improves a wide range of motor skills, including dynamic balance, flexibility, coordination, and agility, in preadolescent and adolescent children [41]. Moreover, even short-term hip-hop dance workshops have been shown to improve reaction time in participants [42, 43]. Dance also promotes proprioception and spatial awareness, contributing to reduced injury risk and improved movement efficiency [44]. Psychological outcomes such as increased self-esteem, improved mood, and reduced stress are also well documented [39, 45]. In culturally relevant formats such as hip-hop, these physical and psychological benefits may be amplified by heightened motivation, peer engagement, and alignment with adolescents’ personal interests.

Despite the popularity of hip-hop dance and its potential as a tool for physical and emotional development, there is a scarcity of rigorous, evidence-based studies examining its impact on adolescent girls’ motor competence, physical activity levels, and enjoyment. Specifically, there is a notable absence of school-based, quantitative interventions incorporating hip-hop dance for adolescent girls, despite its cultural relevance, high motivational potential, and accessibility as a non-competitive, socially engaging activity. This gap is particularly pressing in the Turkish context, where PA levels among adolescent girls are low, and accessible, socially supportive activities are limited within school curricula. Hip-hop’s rhythmic, expressive, and culturally resonant nature aligns closely with the preferences of early adolescent girls, offering an alternative to traditional, competitive PE activities that may alienate some students. To our knowledge, this is the first school-based PE intervention study in Turkey to evaluate hip-hop dance in this age group. Given the logistical constraints of school-based research (e.g., fixed class groupings, timetable limitations), a quasi-experimental design was adopted to maintain ecological validity while accommodating real-world implementation.

Based on the dynamic and rhythmic demands of hip-hop choreography which involves frequent weight shifts, rapid directional changes, and complex arm patterns, we hypothesize the following: (i) The BOT-2 scores of students participating in the 14-week hip-hop dance training will show significantly greater improvement in motor skills, particularly in bilateral coordination, balance, running speed and agility, and upper-limb coordination, compared to the control group. (ii) The intervention group will exhibit significantly higher levels of daily physical activity (step count) than the control group post-intervention. (iii) The intervention group will report significantly higher scores on the Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale (PAES) compared to the control group.

Method

Research design

This study employed a quasi-experimental pretest–posttest control group design to examine the effects of a 14-week hip-hop dance training program on motor competence, physical activity levels, and enjoyment of physical activity among 7th grade female students [46]. Participants were recruited from two public schools located in the same central district and serving populations with similar socioeconomic backgrounds, as verified by district-level demographic data including household income and parental education rates. Both schools followed the same national curriculum and employed PE and dance teachers with similar qualifications and at least five years’ experience. Comparability between the two schools was further assessed based on location, school size, student demographics, and available physical education resources, minimizing potential school-level variation. Due to the school-based nature of the intervention and logistical constraints, random assignment of individual students was not feasible. Therefore, one school was designated as the intervention group and the other as the control group [47].

The control group continued with their regular physical education (PE) curriculum, which consisted of two 45-minute sessions per week, typically involving team sports and general fitness exercises. The intervention group, in addition to the standard PE curriculum of two 45-minute weekly classes delivered by the same teachers following the national curriculum, attended three structured hip-hop dance sessions per week, thereby increasing their total weekly training volume. To ensure equivalent baseline PE exposure and minimise potential school-level effects inherent to the clustered design, comparable schools were carefully selected. Sensitivity analyses controlling for school as a covariate were deemed unnecessary, as such adjustments were not expected to influence the primary outcomes.

All procedures received ethical approval from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Uşak University (725-725-29) and were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size and participants

An a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1 indicated that a minimum of 164 participants (82 per group) was required to detect a medium effect size (f = 0.25) for repeated measures ANOVA with α = 0.05 and 80% power [48]. To account for potential attrition, we aimed to recruit at least 192 participants, ultimately exceeding the requirement and increasing statistical power.

The study involved 192 seventh-grade female students, divided equally into intervention (n = 96, mean age = 12.45 ± 0.72 years) and control (n = 96, mean age = 12.63 ± 0.83 years) groups. Eligibility criteria included being physically able to participate in dance activities, regular school attendance, and parental consent. Students with medical conditions contraindicating moderate-to-vigorous activity were excluded.

Anthropometric measurements included body height (intervention: 156.8 ± 6.4 cm; control: 157.2 ± 6.1 cm), body weight (intervention: 49.3 ± 7.8 kg; control: 50.1 ± 8.2 kg), and BMI (intervention: 19.9 ± 2.6 kg/m²; control: 20.1 ± 2.8 kg/m²). BMI was classified as underweight (≤ 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9), overweight (25.0–29.9), and class I obesity (30.0–34.9). In the intervention group, 14% were underweight, 74% normal weight, 10% overweight, and 2% class I obese. In the control group, 12% were underweight, 73% normal weight, 11% overweight, and 4% class I obese.

Socioeconomic background was assessed through parental education level. In the intervention group, mothers’ education levels were as follows: primary school 12 (12.5%), middle school 18 (18.8%), high school 42 (43.8%), and university 24 (25.0%); in the control group: primary school 10 (10.4%), middle school 20 (20.8%), high school 44 (45.8%), and university 22 (22.9%). Fathers’ education levels in the intervention group were primary school 10 (10.4%), middle school 16 (16.7%), high school 40 (41.7%), and university 30 (31.2%); in the control group: primary school 8 (8.3%), middle school 18 (18.8%), high school 42 (43.8%), and university 28 (29.2%).

Instruments

Motor competence was measured using the Bruininks–Oseretsky Test of Motor Proficiency, Second Edition (BOT-2), which is widely validated for use in school-aged children and assesses fine and gross motor skills through standardized subtests [49]. The BOT-2 Short Form consists of 14 test items from 8 subtests: (1) Fine motor precision: drawing line through crooked paths + folding paper; (2) Fine motor integration: copying a square + copying a star; (3) Manual dexterity: transferring pennies; (4) Bilateral coordination: jumping in place, same side synchronized + tapping feet and fingers, same side synchronized; (5) Balance: walking forward on a line + standing on one leg on a balance beam, eyes open; (6) Upper limb coordination: dropping and catching a ball with both hands + dribbling a ball with alternating hands; (7) Strength: knee push-ups and sit-ups; (8) Speed and Agility: jumping on one leg [49]. To specifically determine the effect of hip-hop dance practice on each subsection of the BOT-2 Short Form, the scores obtained by the participants were included in the analysis without grouping them.

Physical activity levels were objectively measured using Silva Ex3plus pedometers which have demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability for step count measurement in adolescent populations [50, 51]. The devices were clipped to the waistband of each participant and recorded total daily steps over a seven-day period both before and after the intervention. Participants were instructed to wear the pedometers during all waking hours, except during water-based activities. Actual daily wear time was self-reported in personal logbooks, and only days with ≥ 10 h of wear were included in the analysis to minimize underestimation bias. At the end of each day, participants recorded their total step count in logbooks collected weekly by the researchers. Average daily steps were calculated over the valid days for both pre- and post-intervention measurements.

Enjoyment of physical activity was assessed with the Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale (PAES), developed by Mullen et al. [52] and adapted to Turkish by Özkurt et al. [53]. The scale consists of 8 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), measuring emotional and cognitive responses to physical activity. In this study, confirmatory factor analysis supported the original one-factor structure (χ²/df < 3, CFI > 0.95, RMSEA < 0.06), and internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.86). Total scores were computed by summing item responses, yielding a range from 8 to 56, with higher scores indicating greater enjoyment.

Procedure

The dance program was grounded in Self-Determination Theory [35], with the explicit aim of fostering autonomy (offering choices in movements and music), competence (progressively increasing choreographic complexity), and relatedness (promoting group collaboration and peer feedback). Activities included both individual and group-based routines to enhance self-expression and social engagement. The control group received only their regular physical education curriculum (two 45-minute sessions per week, primarily team sports and general fitness), whereas the intervention group received the same PE curriculum plus the hip-hop dance intervention.

The intervention lasted 14 weeks, with three 90-minute sessions per week. To ensure effective instruction, the 96 participants in the intervention group were divided into 4 subgroups of approximately 25 students, each practicing in rotation under the supervision of the same certified hip-hop dance instructor. This ensured consistency in instructional style, content delivery, and feedback across all subgroups. The instructor followed a pre-developed curriculum and session plan, and an assistant researcher used a standardized observational checklist once per week to monitor adherence to the planned activities and to record any deviations.

Each session followed a structured format:

Choreographic content was age-appropriate and culturally relevant, incorporating movements from popular music videos, elements seen in televised talent competitions, and locally popular hip-hop tracks adapted for school use. Age-appropriate modifications were applied to ensure accessibility for all skill levels, and music selections were screened for school suitability. This approach not only aligned with students’ cultural references but also enhanced motivation through familiarity and aspirational role models. Collaborative learning elements, such as peer-led segments and student-generated movement ideas, were integrated to reinforce SDT principles and promote social relatedness. Creativity was encouraged by allowing students to contribute movement ideas during certain sections of choreography, thereby reinforcing SDT principles.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26). Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) were calculated for all variables to summarize the data, and baseline equivalence between the intervention and control groups was tested using independent samples t-tests. Prior to conducting the main analyses, assumptions for multivariate testing were examined. Box’s M test for equality of covariance matrices was statistically significant (p < 0.05), indicating a potential violation of the homogeneity of covariance matrices assumption; however, equal group sizes reduce the impact of this violation. Levene’s Test of Equality of Error Variances showed that homogeneity of variances was met for 4 out of the 10 dependent variables, suggesting some degree of heteroscedasticity in certain univariate outcomes. Inspection of skewness and kurtosis values revealed that all dependent variables fell within ± 2, supporting acceptable univariate normality [54].

A repeated measures MANOVA was conducted to evaluate the within-subjects effect of time (pretest vs. posttest), the between-subjects effect of group (intervention vs. control), and their interaction for the BOT-2 motor competence subscales. Separate repeated measures ANOVAs were used to analyse changes in physical activity enjoyment and physical activity level (daily step count). Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (η²). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Missing data were minimal (< 2% across all variables) and were handled using multiple imputation under the assumption of data missing completely at random (MCAR), confirmed via Little’s MCAR test (p > 0.05) [54].

Results

Baseline equivalence between groups

At baseline, independent samples t-test results indicated that the intervention and control groups were largely comparable across the majority of outcome measures (Table 1). No statistically significant differences were found in fine motor precision, fine motor integration, bilateral coordination, balance, upper-limb coordination, strength, physical activity enjoyment, or physical activity level (all p > 0.05). These findings suggest that, prior to the intervention, the two groups exhibited similar profiles in terms of motor competence, daily physical activity, and enjoyment of physical activity.

Two measures demonstrated statistically significant baseline differences: manual dexterity (t = 2.11, p = 0.04, Cohen’s d = 0.30) and running speed & agility (t = − 3.48, p = 0.001, Cohen’s d = − 0.50). In both cases, the effect sizes were small to moderate, indicating that although these differences reached statistical significance, their practical or clinical relevance is likely limited [55]. This baseline context was considered when interpreting post-intervention outcomes.

Discussion

This study investigated the impact of a 14-week school-based hip-hop dance program on motor competence, enjoyment of physical activity, and daily physical activity levels among early adolescent girls, a group known to exhibit declining physical activity engagement during puberty. Consistent with our hypotheses, the findings demonstrate that participation in structured, culturally relevant dance sessions can produce significant benefits in selected motor skills, foster greater enjoyment of movement, and encourage more active daily habits compared to standard physical education alone.

In terms of motor competence, results from the BOT-2 subscales revealed clear patterns aligned with the nature of hip-hop dance. Notably, bilateral coordination (η² = 0.216), balance (η² = 0.147), running speed and agility (η² = 0.048), and upper-limb coordination (η² = 0.126) improved significantly in the dance group. These are considered large to medium effect sizes, indicating a substantial practical impact of the intervention on these motor skills. Fine motor integration (η² = 0.032) also showed a significant but smaller effect. Conversely, fine motor precision (η² = 0.001), manual dexterity (η² = 0.01), and body strength (η² = 0.005) did not show notable changes, with very small effect sizes. While baseline comparisons revealed statistically significant differences between groups in manual dexterity and running speed & agility, these differences were associated with small effect sizes (Cohen’s d < 0.20) [55] and are unlikely to have practical or clinical relevance, suggesting that post-intervention changes in these domains are primarily attributable to the dance program. These outcomes reflect the physical demands and limits of hip-hop choreography. Beaulac et al. [32], who implemented a 13-week community hip-hop program with Canadian adolescents, similarly found that while general body confidence improved, fine motor tasks requiring detailed finger control did not benefit directly. Rodrigues-Krause et al. [56] confirmed this pattern in a review of dance effects on children, concluding that dance strongly supports gross motor domains such as balance and coordination but has limited impact on fine manipulative skills and body strength due to insufficient resistive loading. The significant improvements in bilateral coordination and balance observed in our study are consistent with existing literature. Aldemir [41] demonstrated notable balance and coordination gains in primary school children following a 12-week folk dance program, reasoning that the frequent weight shifts and symmetrical limb movements inherent to dance refine neuromotor control. Additionally, Cain et al. [33] showed that structured hip-hop sessions contribute substantial moderate-to-vigorous physical activity levels, fostering agility and upper-limb coordination through rapid directional changes and expressive arm patterns. The non-significant results for body strength (knee push-ups and sit-ups) align with previous research showing that while dance develops endurance and motor control, it does not provide the progressive overload necessary for meaningful strength gains [56]. Therefore, future interventions might benefit from incorporating complementary resistance exercises to address this gap.

Beyond motor competence, the intervention significantly enhanced participants’ enjoyment of physical activity (η² = 0.027). This aligns with Self-Determination Theory, which posits that activities fulfilling basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness promote intrinsic motivation and sustained engagement [36]. Jago et al. [25] found similar results in their school-based dance project with UK adolescent girls, where culturally relevant, socially supportive dance sessions boosted enjoyment and participation. Likewise, Connolly et al. [57] highlighted how hip-hop dance provided adolescent girls a non-competitive, expressive outlet that strengthened self-esteem and made movement intrinsically rewarding. In our study, the energetic, socially engaging nature of hip-hop, coupled with its cultural resonance among adolescent girls, likely created a motivational climate that contrasted favourably with more rigid, skills-focused PE activities. The freedom of expression and the collaborative learning environment inherent in hip-hop dance fostered a sense of autonomy and relatedness, which are crucial for sustained engagement in physical activity. Consistent with the baseline analysis, no significant pre-intervention differences existed between groups for enjoyment or activity level, supporting that these post-intervention changes can be attributed to the program rather than pre-existing disparities. Importantly, the dance group also exhibited a marked increase in daily physical activity levels, as evidenced by higher average daily step counts post-intervention. This finding extends previous research by O’Neill et al. [58], who demonstrated that dance classes can contribute significantly to girls’ weekly step totals by motivating practice outside formal sessions. However, Fong Yan et al. [59] cautioned that poorly designed dance programs may fail to generate sufficient intensity or frequency to raise overall daily activity levels. In our case, the carefully structured, high-energy choreography likely ensured that dance practice translated into increased habitual movement, underscoring the importance of program quality.

Collectively, these findings emphasize that integrating structured dance programs into school curricula and extracurricular offerings can serve as a powerful alternative to traditional sports, particularly during early adolescence, a critical period when girls often withdraw from organized physical activity [31]. Consistent with Self-Determination Theory, dance provides a unique means to foster autonomy, skill mastery, and social connection, thereby sustaining motivation and active participation. By leveraging culturally relevant and socially engaging formats like hip-hop, such programs may be especially effective in re-engaging girls who are at risk of declining activity levels, while also promoting inclusivity for those less interested in competitive sport. Moreover, teacher training in SDT-based pedagogical strategies could enhance the transfer of motivation and enjoyment gained from hip-hop to other forms of physical activity, supporting broader lifelong engagement.

To maximize the benefits observed, physical education teachers and youth coaches should receive training in creative dance pedagogy and motivational strategies that prioritize student-centred, culturally relevant movement experiences. Policymakers should recognize that dance is not merely an arts enrichment activity but a viable tool for promoting physical literacy, psychosocial well-being, and lifelong active lifestyles in girls who may feel alienated by competitive or traditional sport settings. Integrating such approaches into both curricular and extracurricular contexts could ensure that diverse groups of students, particularly those disengaged from traditional sports have access to enjoyable, skill-building physical activities that foster long-term adherence.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration. The quasi-experimental design, necessitated by the school-based context, may have introduced selection bias, and the absence of true randomization leaves room for unmeasured confounding variables. Additionally, the intervention group’s higher total weekly training volume, due to the inclusion of hip-hop dance sessions alongside the standard PE curriculum, could have contributed to some of the observed improvements. Furthermore, although a single certified hip-hop instructor delivered all sessions to ensure consistency, subtle variations in instructional delivery, group dynamics, or song selection could have influenced participants’ engagement and performance, representing a contextual factor to consider when interpreting results.

While efforts were made to recruit from schools in similar socioeconomic areas, the lack of true randomization means that unmeasured confounding variables could have influenced the results. Future research should ideally employ randomized controlled trials to establish stronger causal inferences. Second, the reliance on pedometers for physical activity measurement, while objective, does not capture intensity or type of activity. Future studies could incorporate accelerometers or heart rate monitors for a more comprehensive assessment of physical activity levels and energy expenditure during dance sessions and daily life. Third, the 14-week intervention period, while sufficient to observe short-term changes, does not provide insights into the long-term maintenance of physical activity and motor competence. Longitudinal studies with extended follow-up periods are needed to determine the sustainability of these behavioural changes. Fourth, while the study focused on hip-hop dance, future research could explore the effects of other dance genres or culturally specific movement forms on similar outcomes, potentially identifying interventions tailored to diverse populations. Finally, the non-significant results for strength suggest that dance alone may not provide sufficient overload for meaningful strength gains. In future interventions, combining dance with structured resistance training could yield more comprehensive motor development outcomes. Additionally, exploring the underlying mechanisms through which dance influences motor competence and enjoyment, perhaps using qualitative methods to capture participants’ experiences and perceptions, could provide deeper insights.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a structured, culturally relevant hip-hop dance program can significantly enhance key aspects of motor competence, increase enjoyment of physical activity, and promote more active daily routines among early adolescent girls. Improvements were especially notable in coordination, balance, agility, and upper-limb control, reflecting the dynamic, rhythmic demands of hip-hop choreography. Although no gains were found for body strength and fine manipulative skills, these results highlight the specific pathways through which dance supports gross motor development. The observed boost in enjoyment and daily physical activity levels underscores dance’s potential to re-engage girls who often disengage from traditional sports during puberty.

Together, these findings suggest that integrating dance modules into school curricula and extracurricular programming offers a promising, motivating alternative to conventional physical education. Future interventions should consider combining dance with targeted body strength and fine motor activities to maximize comprehensive motor development. In the Turkish context, where school-based PE is often limited in duration and focused on competitive sports, integrating culturally resonant, non-competitive activities like hip-hop could provide a viable avenue for increasing participation among early adolescent girls. These findings may also be relevant for other regions with similar cultural dynamics and resource constraints, suggesting that locally adapted dance-based interventions could be an effective and scalable strategy for promoting physical activity and motor competence in populations at risk of disengagement.
