2026 Donald Trunkey Lecture: strengthening care for the injured globally: successes, challenges, opportunities
Authors: Charles Mock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrauma is a major health problem, killing 4.5 million people each year. Most (84%) of these deaths are in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where resources for trauma care are constrained, often severely. These resource constraints lead to discrepancies in outcomes, with higher case-fatality rates for similar injuries in LMICs compared with high-income countries. Over one million trauma deaths could be averted each year if case-fatality rates in LMICs could be brought down to high-income country levels. There have been many local examples of affordable and sustainable improvements in trauma care and trauma outcomes in LMICs. The WHO and professional societies have worked to make more sustained progress globally. These efforts have resulted in moderate improvements in the availability of resources for trauma care, as assessed by consensus panels over the past 20 years. During this time, mortality for trauma has declined by 24% in the richer half of the world (high-income and upper middle-income countries) and by 15% in the poorer half (low-income and lower middle-income countries). To make further progress, there are several priorities (long-term efforts that probably require significant investment and new technology) and opportunities (which are immediately actionable). Priorities include increased availability of blood; improved care for traumatic brain injury; and improved prehospital care. Several opportunities are calling out for immediate action: increased use of trauma short courses and quality improvement programs; development of an international trauma registry for benchmarking; and use of the newly updated edition of the International Association for Trauma Surgery and Intensive Care’s Guidelines for Essential Trauma Care for needs assessments of trauma care resources, with advocacy to fill identified gaps.

I thank the International Association for Trauma Surgery and Intensive Care (IATSIC) for inviting me to give the Trunkey lecture. I cannot adequately indicate just how honored I am by this. I have been proud to be an IATSIC member since 1998. I am especially honored, given Dr. Trunkey’s profound legacy in trauma. In this lecture, I would like to give a few details about the burden caused by trauma, the challenges that confront trauma care, especially in low and middle-income countries (LMICs), and some successes that we have had in improving trauma care. I would then like to address what the net effect of these successes has been on trauma outcomes globally. Finally, I would like to give what I see as some priorities for future trauma care development and opportunities for some easy wins. I will be drawing from the literature and some of my own personal experiences, including my work in the USA; 4 years as a surgeon in Ghana (2 years in a rural area and 2 years in an urban teaching hospital); 4 years working at the WHO in Geneva; and shorter collaborative work with colleagues in several other countries.

Health burden of injury by country economic level

Each year, 4.5 million people die from trauma. Tens of millions more are injured.1 How are these deaths distributed globally? To answer that question, let’s look at the distribution of the world’s people by the economic status of their countries. The World Bank categorizes countries into four categories (high, upper middle, lower middle and low) based on their gross national income per capita per year (table 1).2–6 Most people live in middle-income countries, as typified by the two biggest countries: India and China. Although these categories are somewhat artificial, they are closely related to many measures of human well-being, such as literacy, malnutrition, life expectancy, mortality rates, and to the availability of resources for trauma care. It is also important to note that there has been economic progress in many countries over the past few decades, with decreases in the number of low-income countries and increases in the number of upper middle-income and high-income countries.3

Looking at the numbers of trauma deaths (figure 1), most (84%) occur in LMICs, which makes sense. That is where most people live. We often talk about resource-limited settings. However, that is the majority of the world, and the majority of patients with trauma. The largest single group of trauma deaths (1 736 000) are in lower middle-income countries and half of all trauma deaths are in the low-income and lower middle-income countries combined. So, care for most patients with trauma faces considerable resource restrictions and these restrictions are especially notable for half of all patients with trauma (ie, those in lower middle and low-income countries). To give a few examples of the barriers and resource restrictions, we are talking about: there are logarithmic differences in the availability of healthcare personnel, such as surgeons, with the following numbers of surgeons per 100 000 people: 70 (high-income), 39 (upper-middle), 8 (lower middle), 1 (low-income).7 There are also frequent shortages of physical resources (equipment, supplies). This applies not only to high-cost items (eg, CT scans, ventilators), but to many lower cost essential items (eg, airway equipment, chest tubes, intravenous fluids), which are often absent, not due to their cost, but due to problems with organization and planning. There are also frequent delays in care due to barriers in access, both geographic and financial.

How do these resource restrictions translate into differences in case-fatality rates, between countries at different economic levels? There are few studies on this question. First, at least one study showed no difference, for pediatric patients in two tertiary centers, with mortality rates of 1.9% in South Africa (upper middle-income) versus 1.3% in the USA (high-income) (difference not significant).8 However, the weight of evidence is that more often there are differences. The best study that I found on this was by Nobhojit Roy and colleagues, comparing four tertiary centers in India to the National Trauma Data Bank (NTDB) in the USA, with stark findings. Case-fatality rates in India were 14 times higher than in the USA.9 Moreover, this difference varied among severity groups, with higher odds of death in India versus the USA, as you went down in injury severity score (ISS). Odds ratios (ORs) for death in India versus the USA were: 5 for ISS >25; 17 for ISS 15–24; 23 for ISS 9–14; and 59 for ISS <8 (ie, patients in this ISS category were 59 times more likely to die in India than in the USA). This suggests that a great many lives could be saved among the less severely injured by simple, lower-cost measures such as improved resuscitation and early management.

These are data from one country. It would be good to know similar data more widely, but we do not. From what we do know, if we could bring down case-fatality rates in LMICs to those in high-income countries, two studies estimated that we could avert between 1 and 1.7 million deaths globally each year.10 11 I have focused on deaths. There is also a large burden of disability, including disability from extremity injuries, much of which could be improved by affordable improvements in orthopedic care and rehabilitation.12

The differences in outcomes are a result of the differences in resource availability that I showed earlier. These in turn arise from the logarithmic differences in economic resources, both overall and in health sectors, with some of the poorest countries having only single digits of dollars per capita per year to spend on all of health (table 1). One might think, how can a country whose government has only these small amounts to spend on all of health make ANY improvements in trauma care?

But, every day, in every country, dedicated doctors and nurses take care of the injured, often under very difficult circumstances and often they come up with very innovative solutions. I would like to give a few examples of these.

Successes: local and global

At two tertiary centers in India, a quality improvement (QI) program was started using audit filters derived from a trauma registry. This was accompanied by a decrease in mortality over a 4-year period, from 32% to 24%.13

At a hospital in Colombia, introduction of standardized trauma protocols in the emergency unit improved the process of care (eg, large bore IVs started). Overall mortality declined marginally (3.9% to 2.9%). Mortality among patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI) decreased substantially (38% to 18%).14

In Ecuador, one hospital started a new trauma and acute care surgery service. Some new resources were introduced (eg, hiring of several additional surgeons and anesthesiologists), but mostly the new service relied on better organization and planning. Mortality for all surgical emergencies (including trauma) decreased from 1.10% to 0.86%.15

At eight non-tertiary hospitals in Ghana, one of our IATSIC Councilors, Adam Gyedu, instituted the use of a trauma intake form with built-in memory prompts, replacing what had been the norm before, the use of blank paper to record patient data. This increased achievement of multiple key performance indicators (eg, chest auscultated, abdomen evaluated) and decreased mortality among seriously injured patients (19% to 8%).16 17

There are many similar success stories, many by members of this audience. How do we build on such individual successes and make more progress globally? I would like to discuss activities carried out by two groups: the WHO and professional societies.

Surgeons are usually unfamiliar with the WHO. However, it has a high potential to catalyze improvements in trauma care globally. It is the main public health agency in the world and has offices in almost all countries. It was created by treaty relations among country governments. Until recently, this included almost all countries. In a move that has far-reaching detrimental consequences, the US government decided to be one of the very few countries in the world to not belong. This decision has had major ramifications. When WHO was created in 1948, countries agreed to contribute to its finances in proportion to the size of their economies. As the USA has the biggest economy (25% of the world’s GDP), it was the biggest contributor. This loss has led to WHO downsizing, laying off over a fourth of its staff,18 with cessation of many programs, including most on trauma care. Nonetheless, its existing products and ongoing activities are still contributing to trauma care.

WHO has been instrumental in increasing injury prevention and road safety globally, such as through several sentinel publications, and through periodic updates on the road safety situation in every country.19–23 It has very active efforts in emergency and trauma care, with publications on assessment tools and training materials. It runs a consultation program for ministries of health on how to improve emergency care nationwide.24 It created two well-known checklists: Surgical Safety Checklist and Trauma Care Checklist.25 26 IATSIC and WHO copublished several trauma care guidelines, which I will get into more shortly.

The other main actors are professional societies. To mention just a few: global professional societies (IATSIC, International Federation of Emergency Medicine, World Trauma Coalition and International Society of Orthopedic Surgery and Traumatology) and regional societies (eg, PanAmerican Trauma Society, African Federation of Emergency Medicine, and the West African College of Surgeons). There are others doing notable work.

In terms of IATSIC, our biennial program is one of the main global forums on trauma care. We run two courses, both with wide global reach: National Trauma Management Course (NTMC) and Definitive Surgical Trauma Care (DSTC). Getting back to our publications with WHO: these guidance documents define basic essential services (in the prehospital and hospital-based settings) that WHO and IATSIC feel could be provided to all injured people and lay out the human and physical resources needed to assure these, even in the poorest countries.27–29

Guidelines for Essential Trauma Care (EsTC) lays out over 200 items of human and physical resources that should be available at different levels of the healthcare system. It is similar in concept to the American College of Surgeons’ Resources for Optimal Care of the Injured Patient, but oriented for the realities of countries that have US$10s-100s per capita per year to spend on health versus thousands in high-income countries.27 30 The Guidelines for EsTC have now been revised and published as of a few days ago.31

Prehospital Trauma Care Systems gives guidance to governments as to how to improve prehospital care, in terms of both formal EMS and better utilizing first responders. Trauma QI is under-utilized globally. To increase its use, IATSIC and WHO copublished Guidelines for Trauma Quality Improvement Programmes, emphasizing simple, broadly applicable techniques and especially corrective action.

These publications have been widely cited, but more importantly, they have been extensively utilized. A few years ago, Lacey Lagrone did a systematic review and found 140 published instances of implementation of these guidelines, in 51 countries, with a wide geographic spread, including several types of implementation.32 Most were needs assessments conducted using the Guidelines for EsTC as a template. Some of these used findings of deficiencies to successfully advocate for greater investment in trauma care, especially in Vietnam.33 34 The smallest category, but potentially the most impactful, was use in drafting nationwide policy, such as the first national EMS legislation in Colombia.32

All of IATSIC’s activities rely on our members. In the past, we have been heavily weighted towards people from high-income countries (such as me). This has been changing. In 2015, 19% of IATSIC members were from LMICs. This is now up to 36%. I would like to acknowledge the difficulty our members from LMICs face in attending our biennial congresses, with tedious visa application processes and with high travel costs compared with local salaries.

So, with a lot of great activities on the ground, supported by global efforts. What has been the net effect on trauma care worldwide, and how do we know or measure that?

Net effect: trauma care and outcomes globally

As with any health problem, we can look at structure, process and outcome. For structure, there have been many studies assessing resources available, both for trauma and for surgery, some are country-wide, but few have addressed changes over time. One of the few was from Ghana, reporting changes in several resources over 10 years (figure 2). Most had improved. It would be great to have some metric like this for the world, but we do not. The best I can do for now is to show the consensus we arrived at while developing the Guidelines for EsTC. For both the 2004 original and the newly released update, IATSIC and WHO convened consensus panels of 40 experts, from different specialties and continents.27 31 They gave their opinion on what resources should be core or extended at different levels of the healthcare system (clinic, first-level hospital, second-level hospital, tertiary hospital). Core was defined as a given resource item should be at that level and it is feasible and affordable to have it there in any country. Extended items are useful, but not as universally applicable. Thus, the essential trauma care criteria provide a snapshot of what global experts consider feasible, even in the poorest areas. So, the changes in what is deemed core likely represent improvements in availability. In 2004, many of the items in table 2 were considered too expensive to promote globally. That has changed, with many of these items now being considered core at one or more levels of the healthcare system. I think that these changes are a metric of real improvements globally. In terms of process, earlier I discussed several examples of local programs that improved process of trauma care. I do not know of any indicator of the quality or process of care that is monitored globally. The 2015 Lancet Commission on Global Surgery recommended several indicators, some of which are trauma-related (eg, surgical volume per population per year; perioperative mortality; 2-hour access to care for open fractures). Many studies have assessed these for specific locations and times.35 36 But, as far as I know, no nation regularly monitors and reports these.

So, what about outcome? Here, I think we have some good news. Trauma deaths per population have been decreasing in all income categories. For ease of discussion, I have collapsed the data into two categories: richer half of the world, poorer half of the world (figure 3). In the richer half, there have been moderate, but steady decreases over the past 20 years. In the poorer half, progress has been uneven. Rates remain higher than in wealthier countries, but still there has been a decrease.

Changes in injury deaths per 100 000 by country economic level (grouped) over the past 20 years. Source of data: WHO Global Health Estimates.

It is hard to know how much of these decreases are due to better trauma care versus better injury prevention, especially road safety, for which most countries have finally been making some progress. It would be good to know, specifically, if case-fatality rates among treated patients with trauma have been decreasing. Even in high-income countries, such data are not published routinely, but some studies have looked at this, mostly showing improvements over time. A study from the NTDB in the USA showed that case-fatality rates for hospitalized motor vehicle crash victims decreased by around half over a 10-year period (5.6% to 2.6%).37 Several locations (eg, Pennsylvania state, Quebec province) have documented decreases in area-wide case-fatality rates (including prehospital deaths).38 39

Similar longitudinal case-fatality rates are hard to find from LMICs. But, there are a few. Despite Malawi being one of the world’s poorest countries, one tertiary center has a good trauma registry, which has led to multiple publications, including one that showed steady decreases over time in risk-adjusted mortality. In that study, crude mortality decreased from 12.6% in 2009 to 4.9% in 2014, with an adjusted risk ratio of 1.00 down to 0.35 in 2014.40

So, some evidence of improved structure of care globally; local data on improved process; and moderate decrease in trauma mortality globally. Is it feasible to make more progress? I think that is very realistic and as an example of how it should be possible, we can look at what has been happening in a similar field: global efforts to lower maternal mortality. There has been considerable progress over the past 20 years, with 40% decreases, in both total numbers and rates of maternal deaths. These decreases especially pertain to Africa and South Asia.41 These 40% decreases are much more substantial than the 15% decreases in trauma deaths in the same areas (figure 3).

Our two fields (trauma care and lowering maternal mortality/safe motherhood) are very similar in what needs to be done on the ground, including: (1) the need for healthcare workers outside of hospitals; (2) the need for rapid prehospital transport and (3) rapid access to surgical services and blood, much of which can be provided at first-level hospitals. However, safe motherhood has been more successful at the global level. Earlier, I mentioned that there are no indicators followed globally for trauma care. Several indicators of maternal care are indeed monitored and well known in most countries (eg, percent of women with antenatal care; percent of births with skilled attendant; percent of births at an institution; and more).42 Use of one particular tool is notable: maternal death audit. This is a simple, standardized, case review technique has been used widely in many countries to identify and target preventable factors in maternal deaths. Accomplishing similar work in trauma care is eminently feasible.

To work towards such future improvement, I want to say a few words about three particular priorities (which are longer term efforts that probably require significant investment and new technology) and opportunities (which are immediately actionable). Both address some of the major causes of preventable trauma deaths including airway, shock (including recognition, early resuscitation and early intervention), TBI and inadequate (or absent) prehospital care.

Priorities

Priority 1: blood

Limitations of blood availability in LMICs are well known. A recent review by the Blood DESERT (Blood Delivery via Emerging Strategies for Emergency Remote Transfusion) coalition discussed three ways this could be improved.43

Walking blood banks, which are lists of donors who can be contacted at the time of need. This is in use around the world. When I worked in rural Ghana, this was our only source of blood. It was always fresh, warm, whole blood. But you were limited to 2–3 units. Above that would have been considered irrationally heroic care. Much can be done to promote this source of blood, and to make it safer (eg, infection screening).

On the program this week, we have had interesting talks on two of these topics: walking blood banks by Jennifer Gurney and autotransfusion by Timothy Hardcastle.

Priority 2: traumatic brain injury

TBI is a leading cause of death in trauma, with death rates 8–9 times higher in LMICs versus high-income countries.9 44 For TBI treatment, there is a lot of focus on the higher cost resources, such as neurosurgeons and CT scans. We should work toward greater availability of these. But, most people with severe TBI (Glasgow Coma Scale <8) do not have mass lesions needing decompression, whereas everyone with TBI is susceptible to secondary brain injury. Improved initial resuscitation can have notable effects on TBI mortality, even without improvements in availability of CT scans and neurosurgery, as multiple studies have documented.14 45 46

There have been some interesting global developments. Spearheaded by one of our members, Andres Rubiano, the BOOTSTRAP (Beyond One Option for Treatment of Traumatic Brain Injury: A Stratified Protocol) project has been promoting locally relevant protocols for TBI care.47 There have also been some interesting technological developments as regards non-invasive monitoring for intracranial pressure and use of handheld near-infrared spectroscopy to assess for intracranial hematoma.48 49

Priority 3: prehospital care

I have focused mostly on hospital care. But, improving prehospital care is equally, if not more, important. There are no good global data on this, but probably most trauma deaths occur in the prehospital setting in areas of LMICs with no (or limited) formal EMS. Likewise, probably the percent of all trauma deaths in the prehospital setting is higher in LMICs. Older data from three cities across the economic spectrum suggest this: Seattle, USA (59% of all trauma deaths occurred in the prehospital setting) versus Monterrey, Mexico (72%) vs Kumasi, Ghana (81%).44 In order to meaningfully lower overall trauma mortality in locations where 80% of trauma deaths are in the field, prehospital care needs to be improved. How to proceed? Is it better to expand formal EMS or to build on existing, sometimes informal, networks of first responders (eg, commercial drivers, good Samaritans, etc)? Or how might the interaction of these two levels be optimized? These are huge issues, which you scarcely see addressed in the trauma literature.

Opportunities

Let’s wrap up by looking at some potential easy wins (low-hanging fruit).

Opportunity 1: greater use of trauma continuing medical education (CME)/continuing professional development courses

Many trauma deaths could be prevented by improved training for early care (especially airway and resuscitation). This includes both training in medical schools and subsequently. First looking at medical schools, a lot of trauma care in LMICs is delivered by general doctors with only the training they receive in medical school (and rotating internships/house jobs). A few years ago, our president (David Zonies) surveyed 700 medical students from 70 countries, finding great variability in what is taught on trauma (eg, elective vs required, topics taught, rotation length).50 At many schools, very little was taught on trauma. Understanding how to make improvements in this would be a good avenue of work to pursue, but would take a while. I encourage some interested young trauma surgeons to look further at this globally.

In the meantime, we can make considerable progress by addressing short courses: 2–3-day refresher courses, of which there are several great options (Advanced Trauma Life Support, National Trauma Management Course, Primary Trauma Care and local equivalents). These courses provide doctors and other providers with a uniform, minimum foundation of knowledge and skills. They are well documented to improve care, but are considerably underutilized, especially in LMICs.51 52

Increasing use of trauma CME needs to address both supply and demand. Supply side issues include number, distribution and cost of courses (and I acknowledge the hard work of many in this room in making such courses more available). We also need to address demand-side issues: willingness and ability of doctors to take the courses. There is more we need to do on this. Looking specifically at ATLS, Barclay Stewart and several people from the ATLS subcommittee surveyed 30 ATLS country directors to see what countries had in place for policies to promote use of ATLS.53 Interestingly, most (20/30) countries did have something in place, such as ATLS being:

Few countries had multiple such policies and use of these policies was more prevalent in high-income countries.

To promote demand side policies, IATSIC put out a policy statement promoting greater use of the above policies.54 One of the biggest pieces of low hanging fruit that I will mention today is that we should work towards every surgical college and every college of emergency medicine requiring such a course for graduation. Many countries do this, but not most. I propose that IATSIC create a working group to promote and monitor such policies.

Opportunity 2: quality improvement

Trauma QI programs are underdeveloped globally. There is a strong evidence base for their effectiveness.29 55 Some options are more in-depth, such as audit filters. But, much can be gained from simple measures. As one example, Dominic Yeboah, an orthopedic surgeon from the 1000-bed Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital in Kumasi, Ghana has been running interdepartmental preventable death panel reviews over the past 10 years.56 57 These have helped to decrease preventable trauma deaths (from 25% of all trauma deaths to 13%) and especially to decrease deaths from inadequate resuscitation (17% to 8%). Even simple single department morbidity and mortality (M&M) conferences or case reviews can be effective. These are done in hospitals around the world, but often with minimal effect. Some simple things can make these more effective, such as better documentation and more purposeful case selection.29 One of the biggest is just making a plan for corrective action. In a survey of 200 trauma care providers in 21 Latin American/Western Hemisphere countries, it was shown that M&M conferences that developed a plan for follow-up of problems were seven times more likely to result in improvements.58

A big piece of low-hanging fruit is to promote greater use of corrective actions. A great project for some young trauma surgeons would be to document examples of successful corrective actions from QI programs in their region.

Opportunity 3

IATSIC’s Guidelines for Essential Trauma Care have just been revised and published.31 This would be a good time for surveys to evaluate how hospitals at any level are meeting these very feasible resource guidelines, with any deficiencies being fertile ground for advocacy efforts.

Opportunity 4

As I have mentioned several times, there are a lot of gaps in our understanding, such as what are the gains to be made in lowering trauma mortality within different ISS brackets. Having data across different countries for benchmarking would be very helpful and should not cost much. There have been several efforts at this. The Panamerican Trauma Society runs a trauma registry here in Latin America.59 A few years ago, several IATSIC members put together comparative data from different countries and called for the creation of an international trauma registry.60 It would be great to rejuvenate and expand these efforts.

Conclusions

There is a large burden of death and disability from trauma globally. There have been a lot of successes in confronting the resource restrictions that hinder trauma care. These have had moderate success in decreasing overall trauma mortality globally. We have several feasible opportunities to make more progress that are calling out for attention (table 3).
