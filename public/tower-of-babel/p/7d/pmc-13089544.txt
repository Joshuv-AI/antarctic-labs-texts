Detecting Positive Selection by Modeling Structure Within Images of Genetic Variation
Authors: Md Ruhul Amin, Sandipan Paul Arnab, Mohammad Khan, Michael DeGiorgio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA major challenge in population genomics is accurately identifying and characterizing natural selection from genomic data. The wide availability of dense whole-genome datasets has enabled researchers to analyze and localize genetic variation within populations. Powerful supervised machine learning methods allow researchers to extract spatial information about genetic variation across the genome and identify traces of natural selection. While convolutional neural networks capture correlations among neighboring features, design choices such as heavy-pooling or limited receptive fields can lead to loss of fine-grained spatial resolution. Extensions like dilated convolutions or attention mechanisms mitigate this issue of loss of spatial resolution but at the cost of increased architectural complexity and parameter count when capturing correlations at different scales. In contrast, trend filtering directly models the autocovariation of neighboring features, ensuring that spatial relationships remain intact without any architectural extensions. When integrated into a classical machine learning model, such as a support vector machine, trend filtering offers a natural framework to create powerful predictive models while retaining the spatial integrity of the input. Here, we introduce SKINET, which employs a novel trend filter kernel within a support vector machine framework and apply it to the task of detecting and characterizing regions affected by positive natural selection. Specifically, SKINET not only distinguishes regions under positive natural selection from neutrally evolving regions but also functions in a regression framework to estimate associated adaptive parameters. Moreover, applying SKINET to empirical human genome variation identifies adaptive candidate genes consistent with previous findings while also uncovering novel adaptation targets, such as FAM177A1, that are linked to cancer.

Detection of natural selection remains a key task in the field of population genomics, despite the adoption of cutting edge statistical and machine learning tools. Existing approaches like convolutional neural networks do not preserve locations of pixels in image data, leading to a loss of global structure, which may be critical for classification. We present the method SKINET, which is a support vector machine that employs a new kernel for modeling covariation within images while preserving both local and global structure based on trend filtering. The integration of trend filtering within a support vector machine framework not only extends power when inputs have important two-dimensional structure, such as images, but also marks a theoretical advancement in working with such models applied to image data. In empirical analyses, SKINET recapitulated well-evidenced and novel genes as candidates evolving through positive selection, and found notable connections of some of these genes with cancer.

Introduction

A driving force behind evolutionary change in populations is natural selection, as beneficial or deleterious traits influence adaptation to specific environments by altering trait frequencies (Gillespie 2004; Brommer et al. 2005; Godfrey-Smith 2007; Gregory 2009; Linnen and Hoekstra 2010). Different environmental pressures, such as limited sunlight or pathogen exposure (Cuadros-Espinoza et al. 2022), shape organismal traits within populations through the action of natural selection, with its indirect effects reflected in genetic variation. Natural selection operates through several forms, including balancing, positive, and negative selection, each leaving distinct genomic patterns within populations. These different forms influence genetic variation in unique ways (Hermisson and Pennings 2005; Pennings and Hermisson 2006a, 2006b; Brandt et al. 2018; Rapaport et al. 2021). Balancing selection maintains genetic variation, whereas negative and positive selection respectively remove harmful alleles and increase the frequencies of beneficial ones, both often leading to a reduction in genetic diversity (Gillespie 2004).

In particular, when positive selection acts, a beneficial allele can rapidly increase in frequency within a population. Consequently, neighboring neutral alleles located near the selected locus may also rise in frequency due to genetic hitchhiking (Smith and Haigh 1974; Flaxman et al. 2013). This effect reduces genetic variation at nearby loci, creating a pattern known as a selective sweep (Hermisson and Pennings 2017; Stephan 2019). Similarly, the action of negative selection has similar influences on nearby neutral variation through a process termed background selection (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Charlesworth et al. 1997; Charlesworth 2012). In contrast, because balancing selection often maintains polymorphism at selected loci for long periods of time, genomic variation at linked neutral sites is also inflated (Barton and Navarro 2002; Charlesworth 2006; Isildak et al. 2021). Moreover, more complicated processes exist, such as adaptive introgression, that can lead to mixtures of patterns, with inflation or deflation of genomic variation at neutral sites as a function of genomic proximity to the adaptive locus (Setter et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2023b). Thus, the resulting footprint of altered genetic variation provides insights into the adaptive forces shaping populations. With the abundance of whole-genome data and advanced computational tools, it is possible to analyze these distinct signatures of natural selection with greater precision (Kent et al. 2002; Yamaguchi-Kabata et al. 2015; Torada et al. 2019).

Numerous techniques have been introduced over the last several decades to detect natural selection from genomic variation. Traditionally, summary statistics have been used to uncover the signature of selection by assessing changes in the distribution of allele frequencies across single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (Slatkin 1978). Methods based on summary statistics often search for patterns in nucleotide variation within and among species that deviate from expectations under simplified models of neutrality, which may suggest the presence of selection (Stern and Nielsen 2019). Likelihood methods, which use information on allele or haplotype frequencies in a model-based framework, have also been applied to detect selection (Kim and Stephan 2002; Nielsen et al. 2005; Chen et al. 2010; DeGiorgio et al. 2014; Huber et al. 2015; Vy and Kim 2015; DeGiorgio et al. 2016; Racimo 2016; Lee and Coop 2017; Cheng and DeGiorgio 2019; Harris and DeGiorgio 2020b; Setter et al. 2020; Cheng and DeGiorgio 2022; DeGiorgio and Szpiech 2022). These approaches are based on probabilistic models, which can be computationally demanding (Stamatakis et al. 2005; Stern and Nielsen 2019), and their performance is often contingent upon the accuracy of the underlying model assumptions.

The emergence of machine learning and deep learning has paved the way for classifying and characterizing adaptive events from genomic data (Korfmann et al. 2023). In particular, such modern tools for image classification and recognition tasks and have found applications in detecting and distinguishing among evolutionary processes (Chan et al. 2018; Torada et al. 2019; Isildak et al. 2021; Hejase et al. 2022; Arnab et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023b; Zhao et al. 2023; Amin et al. 2024). With the advent of modern computational tools coupled with machine learning, we can now represent the underlying haplotypic structure in image form and deploy models for classification tasks. Classification inherently is a supervised machine learning task in which labeled data are used as input to train models that seek to estimate the input-output relationship (Hastie et al. 2009). With enough training data, the example input-output pairs provide sufficient information to uncover their relationship, so that when new input data are provided, the model can accurately predict the output class from which the input derives. Beyond summary-statistic and likelihood frameworks, traditional supervised learning algorithms such as support vector machines (SVMs) have also been explored for detecting adaptive events in genomic data (Schrider and Kern 2015).

A popular supervised machine learning framework for detecting adaptive processes is the deep neural network (LeCun et al. 2015), in which multiple layers are employed to extract patterns from data for use in downstream prediction tasks. A convolutional neural network (CNN; LeCun et al. 1998) is a type of deep neural network that is especially suited for image recognition and classification tasks. CNNs typically consist of an input layer, convolutional and pooling layers for feature extraction and downsampling, fully connected layers that combine features, and an output layer for generating predictions (Keiron and Ryan 2015). Specialized forms of CNNs, such as DenseNets (Huang et al. 2017), are also available that connect each layer with all the preceding layers, but this dense connectivity incurs a computational and memory cost greater than traditional CNNs (Zhou et al. 2025). In the case of detecting evolutionary processes using CNNs, image representations of haplotype alignments or summary statistics serve as input (Chan et al. 2018; Kern and Schrider 2018; Flagel et al. 2019; Torada et al. 2019; Gower et al. 2021; Isildak et al. 2021; Arnab et al. 2023, 2025; Cecil and Sugden 2023; Lauterbur et al. 2023; van den Belt et al. 2024). Beyond CNNs, neural-network-based approaches have been extended for inferring adaptive parameters by incorporating regression heads or auxiliary loss functions, enabling simultaneous classification and parameter estimation (Sheehan and Song 2016). In genomic images, the location of altered genomic diversity plays an important role in detecting natural selection (Hermisson and Pennings 2005; Charlesworth 2006; Pennings and Hermisson 2006a; Charlesworth and Eyre-Walker 2008; Racimo et al. 2017). However, CNNs are suboptimal in this context because they overlook the explicit spatial arrangement of features within images. While CNNs can model local dependencies effectively, they often rely on architectural extensions such as dilated convolutions, attention mechanisms, or positional encodings to capture spatial information (Fisher and Koltun 2015; Vaswani et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2023a). Also, CNNs can be used as preprocessing for sequential models like transformers or long short-term memory (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997; Vaswani et al. 2017), but such hybrid designs still come with an increased model complexity and burden of tuning numerous parameters (Melis et al. 2017; Pham et al. 2018; Yu and Huang 2019). To circumvent this issue without employing parameter-intensive architectures, classical supervised machine learning algorithms (Hastie et al. 2009) can be employed while incorporating spatially explicit modeling.

SVMs are classical supervised learning models that construct an optimal separating hyperplane to differentiate between classes in a feature space (Cortes and Vapnik 1995). Through the use of kernel functions, SVMs can project input features into higher-dimensional space, where complex or nonlinearly separable data become linearly separable by an optimal hyperplane. This projection enables the model to capture intricate class boundaries in the original feature space while maintaining a convex optimization framework (Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004). The flexibility of substituting or designing custom kernels makes SVMs particularly powerful, as the kernel function defines how relationships among features are measured and thus directly shapes the decision boundary of the trained model. A kernel based on trend filtering would allow the model to incorporate spatial smoothness and continuity within the robust theoretical foundation of SVMs.

Trend filtering is the process of filtering noise and outliers from spatial or temporal sequences by encouraging smoothness in the estimated signal (Kim et al. 2009; Wang et al. 2016). The filtering is achieved by adding penalty or constraint terms to the objective function that is being optimized, with this penalty encouraging predicted neighboring features (or their estimated model parameters) to be similar to each other. This regularization effectively smooths the features or their model parameters while preserving local structure. As a result, trend filtering can serve as a precursor for capturing gradual or piecewise changes in genomic signals. When integrated with SVMs, it could yield a framework that retains the theoretical rigor and interpretability of classical machine learning while explicitly modeling spatial continuity within input signals of genomic variation.

In this article, we derive a kernel that applies trend filtering to neighboring input features (image pixel intensities), which we use to implement a support vector machine (SVM) model termed SKINET (Support vector Kernel for Inferring Natural selection and Evolutionary Target detection). When this kernel is applied within a standard SVM framework, it enables the SVM to perform classification and regression tasks while accounting for the autocovariation of image pixel intensities, all within a linear model framework. We examine the behavior of this kernel in comparison to a standard linear kernel and to a mixture of kernels with different proportions of nonlinear radial basis and trend-filtered kernels. Additionally, we compare SKINET to two published deep learning models designed for the same tasks of identifying selective sweep patterns (classification) and inferring underlying adaptive parameters (regression). We show that SKINET provides comparable or superior performance within these benchmarks, while also making the fitted model interpretable by enforcing smoothness in the importance of the estimated model parameters for neighboring image pixels. The open-source implementation of SKINET is available at https://github.com/RuhAm/SKINET.

Results

Simulation Protocols and Kernel Description of SKINET

Using the coalescent simulator discoal (Kern and Schrider 2016), we generated our dataset under a nonequilibrium fluctuating population size demographic history containing a strong population bottleneck that was inferred (Terhorst et al. 2017) from central European (CEU) human individuals in the 1000 Genomes Project dataset (The 1000 Genomes Project Consortium 2015). Each simulated replicate was derived from a 1.1 megabase sequence using a uniform mutation rate of 1.25×10−8 per site per generation (Scally and Durbin 2012), and a recombination rate drawn from an exponential distribution with a mean of 10−8 per site per generation (Payseur and Nachman 2000), truncated at three times the mean (Schrider and Kern 2016). We sampled 200 haplotypes from each replicate.

For simulating positive selection scenarios, we introduced a beneficial mutation at the center of the simulated sequences (550 kilobases). We drew the per-generation selection coefficient s∈[0.005,0.5] and the initial frequency of the beneficial allele at the time of selection f∈[0.001,0.1], both of which were sampled uniformly at random on a logarithmic scale. The time t at which the beneficial mutation reaches fixation was drawn uniformly at random as t∈[0,1200], such that mutation and recombination can erode signals of past adaptive events if this time is long enough in the past. Details of the demographic and simulation parameters are provided in Table S1 and in the Simulation protocol for generating population genetic variation subsection of the Methods.

To generate final datasets, we simulated replicates with and without an introduced beneficial allele, denoted as the sweep and neutral classes, respectively. Given the large range of values from which s, f, and t were sampled, we expect significant class overlap between sweep and neutral settings, which may present a challenge for classifiers. For training and testing purposes, we generated datasets consisting of 10,000 and 1,000 replicates per class, respectively. These datasets were then processed through the SKINET pipeline (Fig. 1) with the classification goal of distinguishing sweeps from neutrality.

Illustration of the SKINET classification and regression frameworks for distinguishing sweeps from neutrality and predicting evolutionary parameters, respectively. The feature matrix is prepared from the original image set that has total N observations and each observation is a matrix that is of dimension 64×64. Each observation is flattened into a vector of dimension p=4,096, and then the final training set is comprised of a matrix of dimension N×4096, where each of the N observations are stacked along the rows that are subsequently standardized column-wise. The standardized feature matrix is then fed to a support vector machine with a linear kernel as well as the four SKINET models (SKINET[d1], SKINET[d2], SKINET[d1-RBF], and SKINET[d2-RBF]) for training purposes. The best models were chosen through five-fold cross-validation (CV) that involved hyperparameter tuning of the model and kernel hyperparameters. After the training phase is completed, regression or classification tasks are performed where in the case of classification, SKINET distinguishes selective sweeps from neutrality and for regression (see Discussion section), SKINET predicts evolutionary parameters f, s, and t that represent the frequency of a mutation when it becomes beneficial, the selection coefficient of this mutation, and the time at which the mutation reaches fixation, respectively. We have explored two different training set sizes involving 10,000 and 1,000 observations per class for classification tasks. The test set remained 1,000 observations per class for all cases of training set sizes.

The parameter C2 determines the bias-variance trade-off, where larger values constrain the model parameters of neighboring features to be more similar to each other than do smaller values. Regardless of trend degree d or training set size, SKINET minimizes its CV loss for an intermediate value of C2 between the two extremes considered. This result suggests that moderate levels of constraint on the difference of neighboring model parameters is ideal for distinguishing sweeps from neutral observations.

We also adapted the SKINET framework to accommodate a hybrid model that combines a mixture of the trend-filtered kernel, with mixture proportion α, and a nonlinear radial-basis kernel (RBF) with mixture proportion 1−α. Thus, the hybrid kernel reduces to the RBF kernel when α=0 and to the trend-filtered kernel when α=1. We denote this hybrid kernel within the SKINET[d1] and SKINET[d2] classifiers as SKINET[d1-RBF] and SKINET[d2-RBF], respectively.

Power and Accuracy for Identifying Sweeps

To assess the power and accuracy of SKINET models, we compared all SKINET models (including hybrid ones) with a linear SVM under different training sizes of 1,000 or 10,000 observations per class, while keeping the test dataset fixed at 1,000 observations per class. The linear SVM was implemented using the SVC module from scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al. 2011) with a linear kernel. The regularization parameter C was tuned over a grid of values C∈{10−4,10−3,…,1010} and the final model was selected based on the model that provided smallest five-fold cross-validation (CV) hinge loss (Rosasco et al. 2004). Our experiments reveal that in terms of accuracy, SKINET outperforms the linear SVM model by a maximum of 4.5% when trained on 1,000 observations per class, whereas there is a larger gap of 5.95% with 10,000 observation per class (Fig. 2). All SKINET models are able to correctly classify neutrally evolving regions with an accuracy in the range of 96.0 to 97.5% for 1,000 and 10,000 training observations per class while maintaining an overall accuracy in the range of 93.05 to 95.90%. Similar to the upward trend in accuracy, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves also exhibit greater power for SKINET models, with high area under the curves (AUCs) and quick ascent to the upper left hand corner of the ROC curves (Fig. 2). Powers yielded by SKINET models are proportionate with the accuracies displayed by the varying training sample sizes, in which the linear kernel model shows the slowest ascent to the upper left hand corner of the ROC curve (Fig. 2), signifying the lowest power among all the models compared. This gap in power between the linear SVM and SKINET models is further echoed by DeLong’s test (DeLong et al. 1988), showing that SKINET variants achieve significantly higher AUCs than the linear SVM model (Tables S2 and S3).

We also evaluated the performance of SKINET[d1] across a systematic grid of per-site per-generation mutation rate μ and recombination rate r (Fig. S1). The central cell of this grid (μ×1.00, r×1.00) corresponds to the parameter combination used for training and achieves the highest classification accuracy. Moving away from this point, overall accuracy exhibits a clear gradient, with performance declining most strongly as the mutation rate deviates from the training value (Fig. S1). This pattern likely reflects covariate shift in the feature distribution induced by changes in SNP density and haplotypic structure as μ increases.

Within the highest mutation-rate column, accuracy increases modestly with increasing recombination rate. Rather than indicating that high recombination intrinsically enhances sweep detectability, this pattern likely reflects partial restoration of the joint (μ,r) structure relative to that seen during training, particularly when the ratio r/μ approaches its training value. Thus, the gradients observed in Fig. S1 reflect both biological effects on sweep signal structure and sensitivity of the trained model to parameter misspecification.

Furthermore, through the feature maps generated by the SKINET models, we can identify the regions of the images that the model emphasizes for distinguishing sweeps from neutrality (Fig. 3 and Fig. S2). The SKINET feature maps show a pronounced red region at the center of the SNPs that signifies where the models focus for identifying sweep patterns against neutrality (Fig. 3 and Fig. S2). These feature maps also display strong emphasis at the center of the bottom rows (dark blue pixels), which likely accounts for variation from the most recent, strongest, and hardest sweeps in the training set (Fig. 3 and Fig. S2). In contrast, we observe that the linear SVM feature map does not exhibit as distinct of a red region at the center of the heatmap (Figs. 3 and Fig. S2), suggesting that the lack of emphasis on the central region of the image may have contributed to its lower classification performance compared to SKINET under varying training sample sizes (Fig. 2). Overall, SKINET outperforms the linear SVM with enhanced model interpretability, power, and accuracy that underscores the value of our trend-filtering implementation within a linear or hybrid SVM framework that improves upon the standard linear SVM.

Effect of Recombination Rate Variation on SKINET Models

Recombination rates vary extensively within and among genomes across species, shaping haplotype diversity in ways that can confound the identification of selective sweeps (Smukowski and Noor 2011; Cutter and Payseur 2013; Winbush and Singh 2020). In regions of low recombination, the reduction in haplotypic diversity may resemble the footprint of a selective sweep, potentially leading to false positive signals (Hernandez et al. 2011). Conversely, regions with high recombination can erode sweep signatures more rapidly, masking true footprints of selection (Harris and DeGiorgio 2020a). Given the potential for recombination rate variation to influence sweep identification, it is essential to assess the robustness of our SKINET models across a range of recombination scenarios, particularly under extreme recombination rate heterogeneity.

To ascertain the effect of recombination rate variation on SKINET models, we simulated an additional 1,000 neutral test replicates in which the simulation parameters were kept the same as the training dataset with the exception of an altered recombination rate distribution. Specifically, recombination rates were drawn from an exponential distribution with mean of either 10−10 or 10−9 per site per generation and truncated at three times the mean. The SKINET models were identical to those previously trained, using replicates simulated with a recombination rate drawn from an exponential distribution with a mean of 10−8 per site per generation and truncated at three times the mean.

Our results indicate that when the mean recombination rate is reduced by one to two orders of magnitude relative to the training conditions, the SKINET models experience a modest rise in neutral misclassification rates up to about 10% (Fig. 4). This misclassification rate increase reaches its maximum (close to 10%) when the recombination rate was two orders of magnitude lower than the recombination rate employed during training and achieves its minimum (close to 5%) when the recombination rate was one order of magnitude lower than the recombination rate employed during training (Fig. 4). In the two orders of magnitude lower setting, SKINET[d1] shows the least inflated misclassification of neutral regions whereas in the one order of magnitude lower setting, the hybrid models exhibit the lowest inflation of misclassification rates. Overall, SKINET models display robustness when faced with variable recombination rate as the change in neutral misclassification remains within a margin of 5-10% even under extreme conditions (Fig. 4).

Performance of SKINET under Mutation Rate Variation

Mutation rates vary within and among species due to multiple factors, including replication errors, and physicochemical damage (Pray 2008; Bromham et al. 2015; Bergeron et al. 2023; Danovi 2023). Such mutation rate variation affects patterns of genomic variation and can obscure sweep signals, making the identification of sweep footprints challenging. We set out to explore whether SKINET models are robust against this setting.

To evaluate the efficacy of SKINET models under variable mutation rates, we employed the previously-trained SKINET models that were based on a fixed mutation rate of μ=1.25×10−8 per site per generation, and applied these models to 1,000 test replicates for each class simulated under variable mutation rates drawn uniformly at random within the interval [μ/2,2μ] while keeping other parameters identical to the training set. Our results indicate that SKINET models achieve classification accuracy ranging from 94.5 to 95.0%—though these values represent a slight reduction of 0.9 to 1.2% compared to the results obtained without mutation rate variation (compare Figs. 5 to 2b). This small drop in accuracy is unsurprising given that mutation rate variation confounds the identification of sweeps and this setting leads to subtle domain shift between the training and test set distributions. Also, all SKINET models demonstrate high power evidenced by their ROC curves, which show high true positive rates at low false positive rates (Fig. 5). Overall, SKINET models remain robust against mutation rate heterogeneity while displaying only around a 1% drop in overall accuracy compared to settings without variation in mutation rate.

Performance of SKINET Against Additional Demographic Scenarios

So far, our evaluation of SKINET models has focused on a European human (CEU) demographic model of Terhorst et al. (2017). To assess the robustness of SKINET under additional evolutionary constraints, we next examined its performance across three alternative demographic scenarios representing Japanese (JPT), Yoruban (YRI), and a constant population size (details in the Simulation protocol for generating population genetic variation subsection of the Methods). Across all three demographic settings, SKINET[d1] consistently outperformed the standard linear SVM classifier in both classification accuracy and power. For the JPT model, SKINET[d1] achieved an accuracy of 89.2%, representing a 4.3% improvement over the linear SVM (Fig. S3). Similarly, under the YRI demographic history, SKINET[d1] attained an accuracy of 85.65%, exceeding the linear SVM by 4.1% (Fig. S3). The largest performance gain was observed under the constant population size model, where SKINET[d1] reached an accuracy of 95.05%, again outperforming the linear SVM by 11.65% (Fig. S3). These trends of SKINET[d1] outperforming the linear SVM are further supported by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, where SKINET[d1] exhibits consistently higher true positive rates across a wide range of false positive rates for all three demographic histories (Fig. S3), indicating superior sweep detection power relative to the linear SVM. Overall, SKINET[d1] has an edge over standard linear SVM model across the three demographic scenarios we experimented on, both in terms of power and classification accuracy.

Benchmarking with a CNN-based Sweep Detector

So far, we have utilized linear models within our SKINET framework, in addition to the hybrid models that incorporate both linear and nonlinear kernels. However, it is also important to evaluate how SKINET performs against contemporary nonlinear approaches, particularly complex CNN-based methods that have shown considerable accuracy in identifying signatures of selection. To this end, we selected ImaGene, which is a CNN-based classifier for predicting selective sweeps that also operates on images of haplotype alignments (Torada et al. 2019). However, there exists a key difference in the processing of the haplotype alignments between SKINET and ImaGene, in which SKINET utilizes window-based local sorting and averaging of the alignments whereas ImaGene utilizes a global sorting approach. ImaGene converts these haplotype alignments to grayscale images and resizes them to 128×128, which contrasts with SKINET that resizes the processed alignments to 64×64. ImaGene processes the image inputs through three 3×3 convolutional layers (each with 32 filters and ReLU activations followed by 2×2 max-pooling) and a 64-unit fully connected layer (Torada et al. 2019).

We employed the same 10,000 observations per class for training and 1,000 observations per class for testing as we did for SKINET (Fig. 2b). We trained ImaGene for a large maximum number of epochs (200 epochs) using 9,000 observations per class for training and 1,000 observations per class for validation, with early stopping applied by selecting the epoch that yielded the lowest validation loss. We then retrained ImaGene for this optimal number of epochs with all 10,000 training observations per class. Our results indicate that all SKINET models outperform ImaGene in terms of classification accuracy by 8.8 to 8.9% (compare Fig. 2b with Fig. 6). A striking difference in performance is that SKINET models have an edge over ImaGene in the range of 15.1 to 15.3% in terms of sweep classification accuracy (compare Fig. 2B with Fig. 6). This diminished sweep classification accuracy of ImaGene likely stems from both the image preprocessing steps and the location-agnostic nature of CNNs, which do not preserve the global structure of the image, and therefore ignore information that may be critical for identifying signature of sweeps. The superior accuracy of SKINET models is further accentuated by the ROC curves, which show a steeper ascent toward the upper left corner compared to ImaGene, indicating higher classification power (compare Fig. 2b with Fig. 6).

Powers (true positive rates) and classification accuracies for the convolutional neural network-based ImaGene classifier using both its original configuration and a modified version that applies the haplotype alignment processing and haplotype alignment image representations employed by SKINET. We used 10,000 observations per class for training and 1,000 observations per class for testing. Power to classify sweeps is showcased using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (right panel) and classification accuracy and rates are depicted using confusion matrices (left and middle panels).

To account for the differences in preprocessing between ImaGene and SKINET and to place these two methods on the same scale in terms of comparison, we modified ImaGene to adopt the same window-based local sorting and averaging strategy used by SKINET, and then resized the resulting haplotype alignments to 64×64 instead of 128×128. This modified version of ImaGene exhibited a notable elevation in terms of overall classification accuracy (6.25% improvement) and sweep identification accuracy (7.6% improvement) relative to the original setting employed by ImaGene (Fig. 6). However, even with the improvement in accuracy, the modified ImaGene model was still outperformed by SKINET with a 2.55% to 2.75% edge in overall accuracy (compare Fig. 2b with Fig. 6). This performance edge of SKINET over ImaGene underscores the finding that while ImaGene can benefit from using a local sorting and averaging approach in its image preprocessing, the overall superiority of SKINET prevails due to its autocovariation-based kernel that explicitly accounts for the location within the image of altered genetic variation as well as the local structure of pixel similarities. Overall, both in terms of power and accuracy, SKINET outperforms the CNN-based sweep detector ImaGene whether using the native preprocessing method or the preprocessing method used by SKINET.

Benchmarking with a Summary Statistic-Based Deep Learning Classifier

Though we have benchmarked the SKINET models against the nonlinear classifier ImaGene, which also uses image-based representations of haplotype alignments, it is equally important to compare SKINET with classifiers that operate on different inputs, such as summary statistics. To this end, we benchmarked SKINET against the summary statistic-based deep learning classifier SISSSCO (Arnab et al. 2023), which is also designed to detect signatures of selective sweeps from population genomic variation. SISSSCO utilizes nine summary statistics, each calculated across 128 overlapping windows, and transforms these sequences of statistics into spectral images using three distinct techniques—wavelet transform, multitaper spectral analysis, and the S-transform. These transformations capture both spatial- and frequency-domain patterns by segmenting summary statistic arrays into windows, calculating the contributions of a range of frequency components for each window, and using images of these windowed frequency components as input to CNNs for classification. To ensure a fair comparison, we incorporated the same nine summary statistics into the SKINET pipeline and evaluated the relative performance of the two models. Both SISSSCO and SKINET were trained on 10,000 observations per class and tested on 1,000 observations per class.

We set out to evaluate how SKINET[d1], which is a linear model, performs in terms of power and accuracy when compared with the nonlinear SISSSCO (Arnab et al. 2023). Our experiments reveal that SKINET[d1] slightly outperforms SISSSCO in all three image generation modes, with accuracy gains ranging from 0.1% to 0.6% (Fig. 7). When it comes to power, as demonstrated by the ROC curves, both SKINET[d1] and SISSSCO exhibit comparable power with nearly identical trajectories toward the upper left hand corner of the curve as well as similar area under the curve (Fig. 7). Overall, SKINET[d1] demonstrates a slight edge over a powerful summary statistic-based nonlinear classifier, highlighting its ability to operate effectively on summary statistic-based data within a linear framework.

Benchmarking with an Allele Frequency-Based CNN Classifier

Moving beyond benchmarking against a summary statistics-based deep learning method (e.g. SISSSCO) (Arnab et al. 2023), we compared SKINET with FAST-NN (van den Belt et al. 2024), which is a CNN that detects selective sweeps using the spatial distribution of derived allele frequencies across a genomic region. This comparison allows us to assess how SKINET, which operates on image-based representations, fares against a modern CNN model that directly leverages allele frequency and positional information. During training, FAST-NN was optimized using mini-batches of size eight over 20 epochs with a learning rate of 5×10−4. Optimization was performed using the Adam optimizer (Kingma and Ba 2017), and the model corresponding to the highest validation accuracy (observed at epoch 14) was chosen as the best model for comparison against SKINET[d1]. These parameters, including the mini-batch size, learning rate, choice of optimizer, and the use of validation accuracy as the criterion for selecting the best model, were adopted from the FAST-NN software (van den Belt et al. 2024). However, we deviated from the default training protocol used by FAST-NN when it comes to the number of epochs used, as FAST-NN trains models for 10 epochs by default and we increased the number of training epochs to 20 to ensure sufficient training. For FAST-NN, we used 10,000 observations per class for training and 1,000 observations per class for testing, which are identical to the training and test set sizes used for SKINET.

Our results indicate that SKINET[d1] has higher classification accuracy and power compared to FAST-NN as evidenced by the confusion matrices and ROC curves, respectively (compare bottom row of Fig. 2 with Fig. S4. Specifically, SKINET[d1] has 3.45% higher overall accuracy compared to FAST-NN. When it comes to accuracy of classifying neutral observations, FAST-NN has a 2.6% edge over SKINET[d1]. However, the difference in performance is more stark when evaluating only the sweep classification accuracy, in which SKINET[d1] achieves 9.5% higher sweep classification accuracy compared to FAST-NN. The ROC curves also depict the higher power of SKINET[d1] by showing a quicker ascent to the upper left hand corner of the plot than FAST-NN, alluding to higher true positive rate at low false positive rates. Overall, both in terms of classification accuracy and power, SKINET[d1] outperforms the allele frequency-based CNN classifier FAST-NN by a moderate margin, whereas FAST-NN shows stronger neutral classification performance when compared to SKINET[d1].

Scan of European Human Haplotype Variation

We next investigated whether SKINET could recapitulate previously-identified sweep signals as well as uncover novel candidates from human haplotype variation data. Specifically, we applied SKINET in a genome-wide scan of the 99 individual from the 1000 Genomes Project dataset (The 1000 Genomes Project Consortium 2015). The CEU population is a European cohort that has been extensively studied for signatures of positive selection, making it an ideal empirical test case. For this analysis, we trained SKINET on 10,000 simulated replicates per class using the same model fitting and hyperparameter tuning protocol that we have used so far. We chose to apply the SKINET[d1] model in this setting, as it consistently exhibited robustness under varying mutation and recombination rate conditions (Figs. 4 and 5) that arise in natural populations.

We found that approximately 3.78% of genomic windows exceed a sweep probability threshold of 0.9 (see Table S4 for per chromosome percentages), consistent with observations that selective sweeps may be relatively rare in human populations (Hernandez et al. 2011). To delineate regions of interest (Figs. S5–S7), we define sweep peaks as genomic intervals where the predicted sweep probabilities rise from below 0.15 to above 0.80 and subsequently decline back below 0.15.

One of the strongest signals detected by SKINET is located on chromosome 2, in the region containing the genes LCT and MCM6 (Fig. 8), with a peak probability of 0.93. The LCT gene encodes lactase, the enzyme responsible for lactose digestion. The continued expression of the LCT gene into adulthood is known as lactase persistence and is a well-documented example of adaptation among populations with a history of dairy consumption, particularly in Europe (Scrimshaw and Murray 1988; Tishkoff et al. 2007; Ingram et al. 2009; Itan et al. 2010). Adjacent to LCT, we also find strong sweep support at MCM6, which contains regulatory elements that enhance LCT expression. These elements have been identified as targets of positive selection in multiple previous studies (Bersaglieri et al. 2004; Sabeti et al. 2006; Tishkoff et al. 2007; Itan et al. 2010; Amin et al. 2024). The detection of this region highlights the ability of SKINET to recapitulate one of the classical targets of selective sweeps in humans.

Sweep probabilities predicted by SKINET[d1] as a function of chromosomal position surrounding the LCT and MCM6 genes on chromosome 2 within the CEU population, where SKINET was trained with spectral images from the multitaper transform applied to a sequence of H12 values computed in overlapping windows.

Among other genes identified with elevated sweep support is RASSF8 with a peak probability of 0.89 (Fig. S6), located on chromosome 12 , which has also been previously reported as a candidate for positive selection in the Khomani San population using a machine learning framework (Sugden et al. 2018). Though the Khomani San population is diverged from the CEU, the recurrence of putative selection signals at this locus suggests a potentially important functional role of RASSF8 in adaptive evolution. Functionally, RASSF8 encodes a tumor suppressor protein involved in maintaining adherens junctions in epithelial cells and regulating cell migration (Lock et al. 2010). It has been implicated in various cancers, including melanoma and gastric cancer, where its expression levels have been associated with tumor progression and metastasis (Falvella et al. 2006; Lock et al. 2010; Wang et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2015). Apart from RASSF8, SKINET also found the gene SYNJ2BP with a peak probability of 0.94 (Fig. S7), which has been detected in a diverged population (Tanzanian) based on extended haplotype homozygosity metrics (Ravenhall et al. 2018). SYNJ2BP encodes a protein localized to the outer mitochondrial membrane, where it plays a key role in sustaining contact sites between mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum, and in regulating endocytic pathways (Pourshafie et al. 2022). It has also been reported to be associated with hepatocellular carcinoma (Liu et al. 2016) adding to the list of genes identified by SKINET with cancer associations.

PTPRJ, located on chromosome 11, is another gene flagged by SKINET having high sweep support (peak probability of 0.97; Fig. S6) that was also reported by a haplotype-based hidden Markov model (Chen et al. 2015). Functionally, PTPRJ encodes a receptor-type protein tyrosine phosphatase involved in the regulation of cell adhesion, proliferation, and migration (Li et al. 2023). It is recognized as a tumor suppressor gene, with reduced expression or function linked to the development and progression of several cancers, including endometrium, gastric, colorectal, thyroid, and colon cancer (Zhao et al. 2015; Li et al. 2023). In our exploration with novel genes, we found FAM177A1 on chromosome 14 with a peak probability of 0.93 (Fig. S6), which is known to be a critical immune associated gene with elevated expression levels linked to breast cancer recurrence (Kohler et al. 2024).

Our scan also identified several novel sweep candidates with high support probability. Among these genes is DAPK1 that is located on chromosome 9 and exhibiting a peak probability of 0.86 (Fig. S5), which encodes death-associated protein kinase 1, a calcium/calmodulin-regulated serine/threonine kinase involved in multiple cellular signaling pathways that trigger cell survival, apoptosis, and autophagy (Elbadawy et al. 2018). Functionally, DAPK1 acts as a tumor suppressor and has been involved in colorectal cancer, where the lack of expression of DAPK1 promotes tumor invasion and metastasis (Steinmann et al. 2019). DAPK1 deficiency in colorectal tumor cells also enhances their invasive capacity, highlighting its role in suppressing tumor progression (Gozuacik and Kimchi 2004). Another candidate is CCSER2, located on chromosome 10 with a peak probability of 0.86 (Fig. S6), which encodes a coiled-coil serine-rich protein implicated in microtubule dynamics (Shirai et al. 2023). CCSER2 has been found to function as a microtubule plus-end-tracking protein in human breast cancer MCF-7 cells, localizing to the extending tips of microtubules and potentially influencing cell projection formation and cytoskeletal remodeling (Shirai et al. 2023). This cancer cell study underscores the significance of CCSER2 as a cancer biomarker.

Adding to this novel candidate list is ERCC1, located on chromosome 19 with a peak probability of 0.85 (Fig. S7), which encodes a critical component of the ERCC1-XPF endonuclease complex involved in nucleotide excision repair, interstrand cross-link repair, and DNA double-strand break repair (Apelt et al. 2020). ERCC1 plays a pivotal role in maintaining genomic stability by facilitating the repair of various DNA lesions (Manandhar et al. 2015). Mutations in ERCC1 have been associated with severe clinical manifestations, including progressive liver and kidney dysfunction, as well as features of premature aging. Apelt et al. (2020) described two siblings with biallelic ERCC1 mutations leading to impaired DNA repair capacity and subsequent organ dysfunction. An interesting insight from these novel genes is that their association with deleterious phenotypes and specifically cancer, which is echoed by other studies that find positive selection signals at cancer-associated genes (Lou et al. 2014; Ostrow et al. 2014; Fortunato et al. 2017; Schrider and Kern 2017; Mughal et al. 2020; Amin et al. 2023; Arnab et al. 2023; Amin et al. 2024).

We also conducted a targeted regression experiment using the window within the LCT region that exhibited the highest probability of undergoing positive selection. We used the same multitaper-transformed sweep images applied to a sequence of H12 values that were employed in our empirical analyses for this experiment. The goal of this regression experiment was to assess whether the frequency of a mutation when it became beneficial (f), and the selection coefficient of the mutation (s) align with previously reported estimates for this locus. We chose not to estimate the time at which the sweep was completed (t), as SKINET employed training data where the beneficial mutation is fixed, whereas the frequency of the potentially advantageous allele at LCT is estimated to be about 0.77 in present-day Europeans (Bersaglieri et al. 2004). Our estimates indicate that selection acted on a variant at a frequency of f≈0.013, and with a selection coefficient of s≈0.058. Studies employing temporal sampling from ancient DNA have estimates consistent with ours, as Segurel et al. (2020) report three lactase-persistence individuals among 259 Europeans, corresponding to a T-allele frequency of 3/259≈0.012, which closely matches our inferred value (f≈0.013). Furthermore, our inferred selection coefficient (s≈0.058) falls within the range of 0.014 to 0.19 as reported by Bersaglieri et al. (2004). More recent time-series–based inference methods yield more tightly constrained estimates that closely match our result, including diplo-locus (Cheng and Steinrücken 2025), which reports s∈[0.056,0.072], and an expectation–maximization framework by Fine and Steinrücken (2025), which infers s∈[0.057,0.099]. Overall, the estimates provided by the SKINET regression framework is in concordance with both old and modern studies predicting evolutionary parameters acting on the LCT locus.

Finally, our Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of genes with high sweep probabilities (greater than 0.9) using GOrilla (Eden et al. 2007) uncovered a notable connection to categories dominated by various cell adhesion related processes, such as “biological cell-adhesion”, “homophilic cell adhesion via plasma membrane adhesion molecules”, and “cell adhesion through plasma-membrane adhesion molecules” (see Table S5). This convergence of cell adhesion related processes on highly supported sweep candidates alludes to cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions as a potential axis of positive natural selection. Furthermore, these cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions are reported to be precursors to tissue invasion by cancer cells (Friedl and Alexander 2011), which strengthens the connection of our identified top candidates with cancer phenotypes.

Discussion

In this study, we introduced a powerful trend-filtered SVM framework termed SKINET, designed to operate on two-dimensional images of genomic variation and to distinguish selective sweeps from neutrality. Our results demonstrate that SKINET models achieve higher classification accuracy in distinguishing sweeps from neutrality than two contemporary deep CNN models as well as a standard linear SVM model. Notably, the linear SKINET[d1] model exhibits resilience to confounding factors like variable mutation and recombination rates, making it an ideal candidate for empirical application. Going beyond classification, SKINET also demonstrates its utility as a regression tool by providing good estimates of selection strength, the frequency of the allele at the time it became beneficial, and the time of sweep completion, as indicated by violin plots summarizing the distributions of prediction errors that are centered on zero and that are considerably narrower than that of the standard linear SVM model (Figs. S8–S10). Though these violin plots showed that errors are centered at zero on average, we identified a monotonic bias in the regression estimates, particularly evident when residuals were examined as a function of the true parameter values (Fig. S11). To address this issue, we applied a linear calibration procedure, which removed these trends and produced well-centered residual distributions across all three parameters (Fig. S11). The calibrated results for SKINET[d1], including residual diagnostics for the frequency parameter f, selection coefficient s, and sweep completion time t, are shown in Fig. S11, with full methodological details provided in the Regression framework and calibration subsection of the Methods. These estimates complement the already strong classification performance of SKINET in distinguishing sweeps from neutrality, underscoring the efficacy of our kernel in both classification and regression tasks. Moreover, SKINET offers model interpretability through feature maps that highlight the regions of images that it places greatest emphasis for classification (Figs. 3 and Fig. S2) and regression (Fig. S12). These SKINET feature maps show concentrated emphasis in key regions of the image, whereas those of the standard linear SVM show more diffused pattern of emphasis on random pixels.

We also compared the mean memory usage (Fig. S13) of SKINET models (including hybrid kernels) and standard linear SVM. We find that the SKINET models (including hybrid kernels) exhibit slightly higher memory utilization compared to the standard linear SVM. Specifically, the SKINET[d1-RBF] and SKINET[d2-RBF] models both consume approximately 6.40 gigabytes (GB) of memory on average, whereas the SKINET[d1] and SKINET[d2] variants each use around 5.61 GB and 6.18 GB (Fig. S13), respectively. In contrast, the standard linear SVM model requires about 5.60 GB of memory on average (Fig. S13), making it marginally more memory-efficient than all SKINET variants. Within the SKINET models, including the hybrid ones, SKINET[d1] and SKINET[d2] are the least memory-intensive. We attribute the increased memory usage of the SKINET architectures (particularly the hybrid kernels) to their more complex kernel formulations due to the inclusion of trend-filtering, which imposes additional computational complexity. The SKINET[d1-RBF] and SKINET[d2-RBF] models further elevate memory consumption as expected compared to standalone SKINET models due to additional overhead incurred upon incorporation of the radial basis kernel, whereas the simpler standard linear SVM avoids such overhead due to its less complex structure.

In addition to measuring the memory usage, we evaluated the runtime of each method (Fig. S14) by measuring total wall-clock time that constitutes cumulative time required for five-fold CV as well as the final model training time. All runtime experiments were conducted on a system equipped with 10 CPU cores and 100 GB of available memory. Among the evaluated methods, the linear SVM exhibited the lowest runtime, taking approximately 0.25 hours (Fig. S14), as the linear SVM kernel has only one hyperparameter that needs to be optimized. Also, SKINET[d1] and SKINET[d2] models, which have an additional hyperparameter (C2) incurred a runtime of approximately 3.45 and 4.14 hours, respectively (Fig. S14). In contrast, the hybrid-kernel variants incurred substantially higher runtime of 41.9 and 42.1 hours for SKINET[d1-RBF] and SKINET[d2-RBF], respectively (Fig. S14). The increased runtime observed for the hybrid models arises from the additional cross-validation introduced by the hyperparameter α, which controls the mixture proportion of the particular SKINET variant and the RBF kernel, as well as the hyperparameter γ of the RBF kernel.

Furthermore, we benchmarked genome-wide scan time by measuring the total time required to process each autosome in the CEU dataset. Scan time includes time required for VCF parsing, computation of the H12 summary statistic, and multitaper image generation. On average, the per-window scan time is approximately 0.26 seconds using a system equipped with 10 CPU cores and 100 GB of available memory. In our genome scans across chromosomes 1 to 22, the total scan time was found to be approximately 94.5 hours, with chromosome-level scan times ranging from the highest of about 7.54 hours for chromosome 1 to the lowest of about 1.56 hours for chromosome 21 (Fig. S15). This roughly 95-hour runtime can be reduced considerably through parallelization across chromosomes or genomic windows, as these units are largely independent and can be distributed across CPU cores or computing nodes. Additionally, while classical SVM implementations are traditionally CPU-based, modern libraries enable meaningful acceleration. For example, ThunderSVM (Wen et al. 2018) leverages GPUs and multi-core CPUs to accelerate both training and inference of kernel SVMs, and GPU-enabled frameworks such as RAPIDS cuML (Raschka et al. 2020) provide scalable SVM solvers with APIs compatible with scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al. 2011).

Classical SVMs usually rely on off-the-shelf kernels like linear, polynomial, or Gaussian RBF, which are powerful but do not account for the spatial autocovariation expected from image representations of genome variation. Though SKINET permits only binary classification, it can be extended for multiclass problems where we may train it to distinguish among other evolutionary processes like balancing selection or adaptive introgression, allowing nuanced evolutionary inference. Such a multiclass extension is not trivial, as SVMs are designed for binary problems. However, we can decompose a multiclass task into a collection of binary subproblems. One approach is a one-versus-all scheme (Hsu and Lin 2002), where multiple SKINET models are trained, each treating one evolutionary scenario (e.g. neutrality, sweep, balancing selection, or adaptive introgression) as the positive class and all remaining scenarios as the negative class. Another binarization technique is a one-versus-one scheme (Hsu and Lin 2002), where a separate SKINET model is trained for each distinct pair of classes, and the final predicted class label is achieved through a majority vote process. In both cases, no changes are needed to the trend-filtered kernel.

When comparing to the contemporary deep CNN sweep detector ImaGene (Torada et al. 2019), we observed that all SKINET models consistently outperformed it in terms of overall accuracy. This edge of SKINET models against ImaGene demonstrates a common drawback of CNN-based methods, which is its location-agnostic nature that ignores the spatial location of a particular feature within input images. However, the original implementation of ImaGene employed 50,000 observations per class for training (Torada et al. 2019), whereas we provided it 10,000 per class here for direct comparison with SKINET. Therefore, increasing the number of training observations may lead to ImaGene displaying more competitive performance. Moreover, SKINET utilizes 64×64 matrices in contrast to the 128×128 matrices utilized by ImaGene, which may also be a factor behind their performance gap as the larger dimensions (128×128) may retain more noise than the more compact representation of 64×64, which averages out the potential noise.

Another aspect of SKINET that adds to its flexibility is that it works effectively with summary statistic data. For a fair comparison with SISSSCO (Arnab et al. 2023), which is a deep CNN sweep detector designed to operate on spectral images computed from summary statistics, we trained SKINET on the same input. Despite both models receiving identical input, SKINET consistently achieved slightly better performance across all three image generation strategies used by SISSSCO. SKINET has a key advantage over SISSSCO when it comes to visualizing model saliency. Specifically, feature maps displaying the emphasis placed by SKINET on specific regions of input images are readily obtained from the fitted model parameter vector, yet more complicated approaches, such as saliency or class activation maps (Lee et al. 2021), are needed for SISSSCO or in general any CNN.

SKINET presents a viable alternative to CNNs and other methods that operate on haplotype alignment images for detecting adaptive events. Methods such as α-DAWG (Amin et al. 2024) and T-REx (Amin et al. 2023) use haplotype alignment images as input and rely on feature extraction schemes (e.g. wavelets, curvelets, and tensor decomposition) followed by classification with linear or nonlinear models. Because our experiments used the same input images as Amin et al. (2023, 2024), we can directly compare our findings with the T-REx and α-DAWG results from those studies. We find that linear SKINET[d1] model consistently outperformed linear counterparts of both these approaches, achieving a classification accuracy up to 95.70% (Fig. 2), compared to 93.80% for best performing α-DAWG model (Fig. 4 of Amin et al. 2024) and 92.85% for T-REx (Fig. 3 of Amin et al. 2023). These results suggest that the ability of SKINET to learn structured representations directly from image data confers an advantage over other methods that are also using image data.

Our derivation of the trend filtered kernel presents a theoretical advancement in working with SVM kernels apart from the power and accuracy gains demonstrated using simulated data. In our benchmarking with other frameworks, SKINET consistently matched or exceeded the power and accuracy of contemporary CNN approaches, a gain we attribute to its autocovariation-based kernel that accentuates spatial patterns key to sweep discrimination. It also reiterated well-known sweep candidates in a scan of human genomic variation and flagged novel candidates that had notable connections with cancer. Furthermore, SKINET enables the exploration of other hybrid SVM kernels that can go beyond the traditional kernels in terms of performance. Given that machine learning has taken a center-stage in detecting adaptive events, SKINET offers researchers a toolkit that utilizes the power of data-driven frameworks with solid theoretical footing to distinguish these adaptive events.

Simulation Protocol for Generating Population Genetic Variation

We employed the coalescent-based simulator discoal (Kern and Schrider 2016) to generate haplotype variation under a nonequilibrium demographic history characterized by a pronounced bottleneck. This demographic model was previously inferred (Terhorst et al. 2017) from central European (CEU) individuals from the 1000 Genomes Project (The 1000 Genomes Project Consortium 2015). Each simulation replicate comprised a 1.1 Mb genomic region, modeled using a constant mutation rate of 1.25×10−8 per site per generation (Scally and Durbin 2012). Recombination rates were drawn from an exponential distribution with mean 10−8 per site per generation (Payseur and Nachman 2000) and capped at three times this mean as in Schrider and Kern (2016). From each simulated replicate, we sampled 200 haplotypes. To simulate selective sweeps, we positioned a beneficial mutation at the center of the simulated segment (550 kb). We drew the selection coefficient from the interval s∈[0.005,0.5] and the initial allele frequency at the onset of selection from f∈[0.001,0.1], sampling both parameters uniformly at random on a logarithmic scale. The fixation time of the beneficial allele was sampled uniformly at random from t∈[0,1200], permitting past adaptive events to display attenuated sweep signatures due to mutation and recombination. To assemble the final labeled datasets, we generated simulations both with an introduced advantageous mutation (the sweep class) and without one (the neutral class). Using the same selection parameters that we employed for the nonequilibrium demographic history from the central European (CEU) individuals, we simulated a dataset based on a constant population size demographic history with discoal (Kern and Schrider 2016). Similar to the CEU dataset, the number of haplotypes remained at 200 and two classes were considered, in which one contains an advantageous mutation (sweep) and another is without it (neutrality).

We further employed discoal (Kern and Schrider 2016) to simulate East Asian (Japanese or JPT) and sub-Saharan African (Yoruban or YRI) human demographic histories. For these two demographic models, we followed the protocol employed by Schrider and Kern (2017) where the per-site per-generation mutation rate was drawn uniformly at random within the range of 2.72×10−9 to 2.72×10−8. The recombination rates for these two demographic histories were drawn from an exponential distribution with a mean of 1.25×10−8 and truncated at three times the mean. To simulate selective sweeps, the selection coefficient was drawn uniformly at random on a logarithmic scale from the interval [0.00625,0.125], the initial frequency of the beneficial mutation was sampled uniformly at random on a logarithmic scale from [1/(2Ne),0.2] for JPT and YRI. For both demographic settings, the time at which the beneficial mutation reached fixation was sampled uniformly at random from the interval [0,1600]. These parameter ranges introduce substantial overlap between sweep and neutral classes, creating a challenging classification task. The length of the simulated sequences was 1.1 Mb for both datasets and the beneficial mutation was introduced at the center of the simulated sequences for the sweep replicates. A full summary of the demographic assumptions and simulation inputs for these four datasets (CEU, constant, JPT, and YRI) is provided in Table S1.

Regression Framework and Calibration

The SKINET regression framework infers key evolutionary parameters associated with selective sweeps, including frequency of the adaptive mutation when it became beneficial (f), selection coefficient (s), and time (in generations) at which the sweep completed (t). For each parameter, prediction errors were quantified by computing residuals defined as the difference between the true and predicted values. For f and s, residuals were computed on a logarithmic scale, and for t, residuals were computed on the original scale, keeping it consistent with our simulation protocol (Simulation protocol for generating population genetic variation subsection above).

To assess and correct for systematic bias in the regression estimates, we performed a linear calibration of the predicted values. Data Processing for Empirical Analysis

We employed SISSSCO (Arnab et al. 2023) for processing the simulated data used to train SKINET for our empirical analysis, which computes nine population-genetic summary statistics and converts them into images via three signal transforms: wavelet decomposition, S-transform, and multitaper spectral analysis. For simulated training data generated with discoal (1.1 Mb haplotype sequences) (Kern and Schrider 2016), we retained the central 400 SNPs (200 to the left and 200 to the right of the center), calculated summary statistics in sliding windows of 10 SNPs with a stride of three, and obtained 128 windows per simulation. When working with empirical genomic data, we first filtered out singleton and doubleton SNPs by removing those with minor allele counts less than three (Mughal et al. 2020) and masked 100 kb genomic segments whose Centre for Genomic Regulation (CRG) mappability and alignability score fell below 0.9 (Talkowski et al. 2011) to minimize technical artifacts. After filtering, we traversed each of the 22 autosomes of the 99 individuals from the CEU population of the 1000 Genomes Project (The 1000 Genomes Project Consortium 2015) in 400 SNP windows with a stride of 10 SNPs. For each window, we generated the multitaper spectral decomposition image upon calculating the summary statistic H12 in each of 128 sub-windows with a stride of three SNPs between sub-windows, which is identical to the process employed for simulated data. We selected the lienar SKINET[d1] model for our empirical analysis, as it shows a notable accuracy of 96.75% with the lightweight multitaper-transformed images applied to a sequence of H12 values (Fig. S16). Furthermore, under variable mutation rate in the test samples, SKINET[d1] achieves an overall accuracy of 94.2% (Fig. S17) and when tested on neutral samples drawn from a variable recombination rate, the deviation in neutral classification rate compared to the baseline setting is limited to only 2.3% (Fig. S18). This demonstration of robustness under challenging scenarios with variable mutation (Fig. S17) and recombination (Fig. S18) rates further reinforces the suitability of SKINET[d1] as our chosen model for empirical analysis.

Gene Ontology (GO) Enrichment Analysis of High-probability Genes

The enrichment analyses were performed for the three Gene Ontology (GO) categories available within GOrilla (Eden et al. 2007): “Biological Process”, “Cellular Component”, and “Molecular Function”. Two unranked gene lists were supplied where the target set was comprised of genes whose peak probability exceeded 0.9 and a background set comprising all genes. For each ontology, we compared the two lists using a hypergeometric test followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction (Benjamini et al. 2001) to ascertain the statistical significance of the associated GO terms. We considered terms with a false discovery rate adjusted q-value≤0.05 to be significant and found that significant enrichment emerged only in the Biological Process ontology.
