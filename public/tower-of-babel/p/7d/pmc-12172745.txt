Quantitative 3D reconstruction of viral vector distribution in rodent and ovine brain following local delivery
Authors: Roberta Poceviciute, Kenneth Mitchell, Angeliki Maria Nikolakopoulou, Suehyun K. Cho, Xiaobo Ma, Phillip Chen, Samantha Figueroa, Ethan J. Sarmiento, Aryan Singh, Oren Hartstein, William G. Loudon, Florent Cros, Alexander S. Kiselyov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Viral vectors are an active area of research and development to treat diseases of the central nervous system (CNS). However, systemic delivery of large-molecular weight biologics is complicated by limited crossing of the blood-brain barrier, immunological clearance from the circulation, off-target effects, and systemic or organ toxicity. Local drug delivery can mitigate these obstacles, however, the drug must still be distributed over sufficiently large tissue volume to achieve the desired efficacy. In the field of drug delivery, quantitative, high resolution spatial analysis of drug distribution in the brain and other organs poses a challenge. To address this issue, we introduce a computational pipeline to reconstruct and quantify 3D distribution of locally delivered viral vectors from 2D microscopy images of subsampled brain sections. This pipeline, which combined existing and newly developed machine-learning and other computational tools, effectively removed false positive artifacts abundant in large-scale images of uncleared tissue sections, and subsampling adequately predicted the dispersion of model viral vectors from the point of local drug delivery. Furthermore, the pipeline successfully captured differences in the distribution of adeno virus (AdV) and adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors exhibiting varying sizes and transport properties. Notably, the proposed method is directly applicable to the distribution studies of therapeutics in large animal models. Thus, our developed pipeline is an accessible tool that can aid the research and development of local drug delivery strategies for the treatment of CNS diseases with viral vectors and potentially other therapeutics.

Introduction

The challenges associated with the treatment of central nervous system (CNS) diseases motivated the shift towards genetic therapies (Sun and Roy, 2021) and other biologics (Dyck, 2018; Lindvall et al., 2004) to treat Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and other neurodegenerative diseases. Viruses represent an attractive gene delivery platform due to their naturally evolved ability to deliver genetic material to cell cytoplasm with high efficiency (Robbins and Ghivizzani, 1998; Lentz et al., 2012). Adeno-associated virus (AAV) is the most common platform due to its safety and capacity for cell and tissue specificity (Hudry and Vandenberghe, 2019). Many AAV-based therapies are in clinical trials for the treatment neurodegenerative diseases, some of which are already approved (Hudry and Vandenberghe, 2019).

Systemic administration of AAV therapies is the least invasive and most attractive route. However, multiple challenges impede its broad utility in clinical practice. Most importantly, blood-brain barrier (BBB) limits the uptake of systemically administered drugs by the brain tissue (Terstappen et al., 2021). AAV serotypes capable of crossing the BBB have been discovered and further engineered; however, the ability to cross BBB alone does not resolve drug loss to the peripheral organs, the accompanying toxicity, clearance from the circulation by the liver or the immune system, and the resulting need for a high viral vector dose (Hudry and Vandenberghe, 2019). AAV capsid engineering for enhanced cell specificity and selection of cell type-specific promoters could mitigate side effects to peripheral organs as well as off-target brain regions and cell types (Li and Samulski, 2020). However, the degree of specificity that can be achieved is limited (Goertsen et al., 2022; Chuapoco et al., 2023), and a high dose of therapy is still required to achieve and maintain therapeutic concentration at the target sites.

Local delivery of AAV and other viral therapies directly to the brain parenchyma can overcome the challenges associated with systemic drug administration (Hudry and Vandenberghe, 2019). Various local drug delivery platforms are available or are being actively developed, including needle or catheter injections (Hudry and Vandenberghe, 2019), convection-enhanced delivery (Lonser et al., 2015), and implantation of controlled drug release systems (Han et al., 2019). Naturally, local drug delivery has its unique challenges that are intimately linked to its advantages. First, delivering the drug where it is needed is generally more invasive than systemic drug administration (Hudry and Vandenberghe, 2019). Second, while direct drug delivery to the brain parenchyma reduces circulation-related drug losses, it also limits drug transport to processes that are much slower than circulation, such as diffusion and interstitial fluid flow (Hudry and Vandenberghe, 2019). Therefore, an important parameter of local drug delivery performance is the impacted tissue volume, which is poorly understood.

Unlike other analytical techniques that require tissue homogenization (Marsic et al., 2015), optical microscopy can capture and quantify spatial drug distribution in fine detail. However, tissue light scattering and absorption interfere with optical imaging of thick tissue samples (Ueda et al., 2020). To minimize these effects, tissue samples have been traditionally sectioned into thin sections for two-dimensional (2D) imaging. 2D imaging of representative thin sections is common but can bias the results, whereas 3D reconstruction from 2D images is laborious. To automate laborious reconstruction, serial two-photon (STP) tomography has been developed that alternates between two-photon microscopy of whole brain samples and sectioning so that thin sections do not have to be collected and mounted for imaging (Ragan et al., 2012). However, so far, STP tomography has been limited to visualizing endogenous fluorescence of a single probe per sample (Ragan et al., 2012; Vousden et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2015, 2017) – its compatibility with staining techniques and multicolor imaging has yet to be demonstrated (Ricard et al., 2018). Several three-dimensional (3D) techniques, such as CLARITY, CUBIC, DISCO, and others, have been developed that reduce tissue light scattering and absorption in whole tissue samples so that they can be imaged in 3D by confocal or light sheet microscopy (Ueda et al., 2020); these 3D imaging techniques have already been applied to visualize AAV distribution in the brain (Yang et al., 2014; Pan et al., 2016). However, the instrumentation and expertise required to perform 3D imaging is not ubiquitous.

At Bionaut Labs, we are developing remote-control millimeter-scale robots for site-specific drug delivery applications to the CNS (Brown et al., 2023). To study the relative performance of various local drug delivery systems, we developed and validated a computational pipeline built upon open-source tools to reconstruct and quantify 3D distribution of viral vectors from 2D images of subsampled brain sections. We applied the pipeline to study local drug delivery by standard needle injection as well as liquid-filled capsule implantation; the latter represents the simplest form of controlled local drug release from the Bionaut bot. We show that our developed pipeline can effectively remove false positive artifacts, accurately estimate virus distribution from subsampled brain tissue sections, and capture differences in the distribution of different viral vectors. Furthermore, the developed methodology can be used to evaluate 3D tissue distribution in both small and large animal models as demonstrated in rodent and ovine animal models.

Methods

Local drug delivery modalities

Bionaut Labs is developing tetherless, remote-controlled, millimeter-scale robots that are propelled to the target site using external magnets. The robots are designed to deliver the drug by either ejecting drug solution or dropping off a formulated drug cargo, such as a liquid-filled capsule. However, for the purposes of developing and validating image analysis pipeline, solutions of model viral vectors were administered by either needle injection or liquid-filled capsule implantation (Fig. 1a); the latter represents the simplest form of controlled drug release from a Bionaut bot. Needle injections were performed using 5 μL Hamilton Neuros syringes fitted with 33G needles (65460-02; Hamilton, Reno, NV, USA) and controlled by a microinjector (78–8130; KD Scientific, Holliston, MA, USA). Liquid-filled capsule implantations were performed using custom capsules designed by Bionaut Labs. Briefly, cylindrical capsules were fabricated from biocompatible polymers; rat capsules measured 1.0 mm in outer diameter (OD), 0.8 mm in inner diameter (ID), and 5.0 mm in length, and sheep capsules measured 3.0 mm in OD, 2.6 mm in ID, and 10 mm in length. The capsules were sealed at both ends. The capsules were equipped with side microports for drug loading and release. In all but one experiment, the drug diffused passively through the side microports. In these cases, the capsules were implanted in the brains for up to several weeks to ensure full release (Fig. 2, Fig. 4, Fig. 6). However, in one of the experiments, the drug solution was actively pushed out through the side microports to mimic active drug ejection from the Bionaut bot; in this case, the capsule was retracted after drug release (Fig. 3, Fig. 5). Detailed capsule designs will be presented elsewhere. Animals

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Lundquist Institute with National Institutes of Health guidelines (IACUC protocol numbers 22758 and 22860). Wild-type, male Sprague Dawley rats were acquired from Charles Rivers Laboratories (Wilmington, MA, USA) at 2–3 months of age. Upon arrival, they were housed in plastic cages on a 12 h light cycle with ad libitum access to water and a standard laboratory diet. The rats were allowed to acclimate for at least 3 days before initiating experiments. Male, Rambouillet sheep were acquired at 40 kg of weight. They were tested for Q-fever and quarantined for 1 week before initiating experiments. Because gender was not expected to affect the performance of the image analysis pipeline and female sheep have a greater chance of carrying infectious disease, only males were considered across all experiments.

In consideration of animal welfare, experimental design aimed to reduce the number of research animals. When available, rodent data were re-used from several internal experiments to develop and validate the image analysis pipeline. This led to varying virus serotypes across different figures. Notably, controlling virus serotype was not essential to developing and validating the pipeline. Considering the larger size of sheep brain, several different treatments were administered to a single animal.

Viruses

All viruses were acquired from Charles Rivers Laboratories (CRL) and stored at −80 °C upon arrival. Virus titers were determined by the vendor and expressed as genome copies per mL (GC/mL) or viral particles per mL (VP/mL) in the case of AAV and AdV, respectively. Virus stock solutions were administered to animals without further dilutions. For the administration of AAV5-CAG-eGFP (CV17169-AV5 at 2.5E13 GC/mL; CRL) to rats, 1 μL of virus stock was administered by either needle injection or capsule implantation to two independent rats, one rat per delivery method (Fig. 2, Fig. 4). For the combined administration of AAV1-CMV-mCherry (CV10020 at 1E13 GC/mL; CRL) and AdV-CMV-eGFP (CV10001 at 2.3E12 VP/mL; CRL) to rats, 2.5 μL of each virus stock was mixed and a total of 5 μL were administered to two independent rats, one rat per delivery method (Fig. 3, Fig. 5); only in this experiment, a retractable, active release capsule was used. The same AAV1-CMV-mCherry and AdV-CMV-eGFP mixture was administered to three addtional rats by needle injection only (Fig. 6). Finally, for the administration of AAV9-CAG-eGFP (CV17169-AV9 at 5.6E13 GC/mL; CRL) to sheep, 5 μL of the virus stock was administered by each delivery method to two brain locations of the same sheep (Fig. 6). Of note, 5 μL of AAV5-CAG-eGFP stock (CV17169-AV5 at 2.5E13 GC/mL; CRL) was delivered by each method to another two brain locations of the same sheep. However, at least at the dose administered, this serotype did not show any expression of the fluorescence reporter protein, so AAV5 sheep data were not analyzed.

Intracerebral needle injections and capsule implantations into rats

The rat brain is relatively small (up to 2 g by weight (Herculano-Houzel et al., 2006) and ∼1 cm wide), so each rat received a single treatment to the right hemisphere. Although bilateral treatments were possible, they would have required different fluorescent reporter proteins to account for potential viral vector transport from one hemisphere to the other. As a result, the differences in reporter protein properties as well as in infectivity of independent viral vector preparations may have complicated the comparison between different treatments. Thus, unilateral treatments were chosen. Rats were initially anesthetized with 5% isoflurane/1.5% oxygen followed by subcutaneous administration of analgesic (Buprenophrine, 0.03 mg/kg). The head was shaved and 200 μl of 0.25% lidocaine was administered below the dermis. Isofluorane was decreased to 3% and the animal was moved to a stereotactic frame (942; Kopf, Tujunga, CA, USA) and placed on a heating pad (37 °C). The animal was secured with a nose cone and rat ear bars until its head was stable. Skin was incised with a scalpel blade, and a burr hole in the skull was drilled with a drill (1474; Kopf) mounted onto the stereotactic frame. To deliver the selected virus into the brain parenchyma, needle injection or capsule implantation were performed stereotactically 0.75 mm rostral and 2.75 mm lateral of midline from Bregma and 6 mm below the surface of the brain corresponding to striatum. Striatum was targeted because it was large enough to accommodate capsules. In the case of needle injection, the needle was inserted into the brain tissue at the rate of 3 mm/min and the virus solution was ejected at a rate of 0.2–0.5 μL/min (depending on the total injected solution volume). The needle was then left in the brain for another 5 min before retraction at a rate of 3 mm/min. The burr hole was closed with bone wax, and the skin incision was sutured. Rats were observed under blanket warmers until they recovered from sedation and monitored for ill effects.

After virus administration, the rats that received AAV5 or a combination of AAV1 and AdV survived for 3 weeks or 10 days, respectively. Briefly, animals were anesthetized, perfused with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS, and brains were removed and postfixed with 4% PFA as previously described (Nikolakopoulou et al., 2019). Brain sections were cut at 30 μm thickness using a vibratome (VT1000-S; Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany). During sectioning, free-floating sections were pre-screened for the fluorescent reporter protein expression to ensure that all brain tissue transduced with the virus had been sectioned. Sections were then mounted onto microscope slides with Fluoromount-G mounting medium containing DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) (00-4959-52; Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA USA).

To stain rat brain sections with antibodies (Fig. S2), rat brain sections were first blocked with 5% normal donkey serum (100487-948; VWR, Radnor, PA, USA) and 0.1% Triton X-100 (28314; ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in PBS containing 0.05% sodium azide (50-238-7786; Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. For staining with primary antibodies, the sections were incubated with either 5 μg/mL of rabbit anti-AAV antibody (PA5-22815; ThermoFisher Scientific) or 5 μg/mL of goat anti-AdV antibody (PA1-7201; ThernoFisher Scientific) in the blocking solution overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the sections were washed 3 times with PBS and blocked again. For staining with secondary antibodies, the corresponding sections were incubated with either 5 μg/mL of donkey anti-rabbit IgG (A31573; ThermoFisher Scientific) or 5 μg/mL of the donkey anti-goat IgG antibody (A21447; ThermoFisher Scientific) in the blocking solution overnight at 4 °C. Both secondary antibodies were conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647. The next day, the sections were washed 3 times with PBS and mounted with Fluoromount-G mounting medium containing DAPI (00-4959-52; Life Technologies).

Intracerebral needle injections and capsule implantations into sheep

The sheep brain is larger (130–140 g (Murray and Mitchell, 2022), so multiple treatments can be administered without the need for different fluorescence reporter proteins. In total, four treatments (AAV5-CAG-eGFP and AAV9-CAG-eGFP, each by needle injection and capsule implantation) were administered to a single sheep brain spaced ∼1.5 cm apart. Before the surgery, the sheep was fasted for 24 h with ad libitum access to water. Anesthesia was induced with ketamine at 22–44 mg/kg intramuscularly (IM) and maintained with 1–5% isofluorane. The vital signs (breathing and heart rate) were monitored during the entire procedure. The skin was incised with a scalpel blade, and a separate burr hole was drilled for each treatment ∼2 cm apart. The insertion of the needle/capsule was performed in the center of the burr hole ∼1.5 cm deep below the dura, reaching the white matter of cortex. Needle injection parameters in rats and sheep were identical. After virus administration, the burr holes were closed with bone wax and the skin incision was sutured. After the surgery, the animal was monitored for 24 h.

The sheep was survived for 6 weeks and euthanized without perfusion (PFA is volatile and toxic and must be handled in the fume hood, so only small animal models can be safely perfused with PFA). The brain was extracted and dissected to isolate ∼3x3x3 cm3 tissue blocks around the sites of local drug administration, one block per treatment. Brain tissue blocks were then fixed in formalin for 5 days. Fixed brain tissue blocks were sliced into 100 μm thick sections using a vibratome and mounted for imaging as described above.

Microscopy

Free-floating and mounted sections were imaged on BZ-X810 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan) operated by BZ-X800 Viewer software (version 1.2.3; Keyence). Mounted sections were tile-scanned with 10× magnification objective (BZ-PA10; Keyence) and at 2x2 pixel binning, yielding pixel size of 1.5x1.5 μm. The sections were imaged in bright field (BF) channel for tissue morphology, DAPI fluorescence channel for DAPI staining of the nuclei, and GFP and/or mCherry fluorescence channels for fluorescence reporter protein expression (which signified productive viral transduction of cells). Imaging settings varied across the experiments and are provided in Supplementary Data 1. Notably, for each experiment, imaging settings were kept constant across all brain sections. After imaging, tile scans were stitched in BZ-X800 Analyzer software (version 1.1.21; Keyence) without compression.

Image analysis

Similar to Quint workflow (Yates et al., 2019), we combined various open-source machine learning tools such as Ilastik (Berg et al., 2019), QuickNII (Puchades et al., 2019), and Nutil (Groeneboom et al., 2020), with custom algorithms and tools developed in Python (version 3.9.2). Our implementation leveraged popular Python libraries such as NumPy, SciPy, scikit-image, and OpenCV.

To detect cells productively transduced with the virus (hereafter, virus+ cells), multi-channel (reporter protein fluorescence, DAPI fluorescence, and BF) microscopy images were first processed with a Random Forest (RF) pixel classification model in Ilastik (Berg et al., 2019). For each experiment, representative sections of each brain were selected for training, then image regions were labeled as virus+ cells, autofluorescent cells, or background, until pixel classification performance was sufficient across all training sections. To compare multi- and single-channel pixel classification (Fig. 2), these labels were subsequently applied to single-channel images of reporter protein fluorescence to obtain single-channel RF classifier built from identical labels. With the obtained RF pixel classifier(s), all imaged brain sections were processed in batch mode to produce virus+ masks, which were further cleaned up with scikit-image to remove small- and large-size outliers and to close holes. Brain sections were also processed on their single-channel DAPI images with a separate RF pixel classifier built to isolate brain tissue from its surroundings in the image. The resulting tissue masks were applied to the virus+ masks, thus removing any false positives lying outside of the tissue boundary. In the case of capsule implantation, these tissue masks were also used to extract the boundary of capsule cavity created during capsule implantation, aiding in visualization and data analysis. In the case of needle injection, the needle track was drawn manually in ImageJ in those sections where the track was visible based on visual inspection.

In practice, the obtained virus+ masks represented candidate virus+ cells because, upon examination of these masks, false positive objects were still obscuring accurate detection of virus+ cells (Fig. 3a). To further remove false positives, we employed unsupervised learning through the DBSCAN density clustering algorithm (Ester et al., 1996). DBSCAN was selected for its strength at identifying spatial, density-based relationships between objects—we suspected true virus+ cells to be spatially related because they clustered near the delivery site. However, clustering based on position alone could be insufficient, as true virus+ cells could exhibit varying densities or appear separated from a larger cluster on the tissue section. Moreover, the varying densities of false positive objects can further complicate clustering. To address this challenge, we introduced a new feature inspired by image moments (Chaumette, 2004): the spatial moment of an object (Equation (1)). We defined the spatial moment of an object to represent its relative position to all other objects in the section, weighed by the intensity of those objects in the reporter protein fluorescence channel. By incorporating this spatial moment, we captured not only the object's position but also its relationship with the surrounding objects. This created a descriptor that was similar across true virus+ cells, allowing us to better differentiate true virus+ cells from false positives.(1)SpatialMomentofObject=∑i|(xi−xj)(yi−yj)|pM00(i)Here x and y denote normalized object spatial coordinates within an image, with j corresponding to the object of interest and i – to all other objects within the same image. The spatial moment incorporated the 0th order moment of objects, M00, which was defined to be the sum of the pixel intensity values in the object. The order of the spatial moment, denoted by p, can be selected based on the spatial distribution of the dataset. For instance, if the densities of true and false positives differ substantially, the spatial component is particularly informative, so it may be advantageous to select a large p. Conversely, in instances where those densities are similar, the spatial component is less informative, and a smaller p value may be selected. In the present work, the order of 0.5 was selected most commonly to facilitate the clustering of sparse virus+ cells.

While DBSCAN traditionally works on spatial data alone, we innovated on the DBSCAN algorithm to introduce the spatial moment in addition to the spatial coordinates. To achieve this, we calculated the distance between two objects as the geometric mean of their Euclidean distance and the spatial moment (Equation (2)). The DBSCAN hyperparameters were chosen manually for each experiment apart from the maximum distance between an object and its nearest neighbor in the cluster (commonly referred to as EPS), which was selected automatically for each brain section. This automatic selection process used information from the previous, already clustered section to optimize EPS value iteratively. As a result, a few representative sections were clustered manually to provide seed points, and then, the neighboring sections were clustered, and so on. If clustering produced multiple clusters, information from the previous, already clustered section was again used to select true positive clusters. In this way, we ensured that spatial information was retained across sections, leading to an optimal continuous 3D reconstruction of the entire virus+ cell cloud.

To reconstruct 3D point clouds of virus+ cell distribution, 2D images of whole rat brain sections were aligned to a 3D rat brain atlas using QuickNII (Puchades et al., 2019), and the alignments were then applied to the final masks of virus+ cells and needle track/capsule cavity using Nutil (Groeneboom et al., 2020). 2D images of sheep brain sections could not be aligned to an atlas because, due to the larger brain size, only a small portion of the sheep brain could be sliced and imaged. Instead, 2D images of consecutive sheep brain sections were aligned by similarity in ImageJ, and 3D distribution was reconstructed in Python. The 3D volumes of the virus+ cells as well as the needle track/capsule cavity were reconstructed and calculated by using the Alphashape algorithm on the respective 3D point clouds. We then combined the 3D point cloud of virus+ cells and the 3D volume of the needle track/capsule cavity to quantify the distance between virus+ cells and the boundary of drug delivery. To estimate the total number of virus+ cells in each brain, we first extrapolated the number of virus+ cells in the missing sections. Briefly, we first smoothed virus+ cell counts in the analyzed sections with a Gaussian kernel to reduce noise and then extrapolated virus+ cell counts in the missing sections by performing linear regression between each two consecutive analyzed sections. The validation of subsampled data collection method is provided in Fig. 4.To compare the performance of Ilastik models to an existing method (Fig. 2c), image regions of interest (ROIs) were also segmented in CellProfiler (Carpenter et al., 2006) following a previously described workflow (Vousden et al., 2015). Parameter values were optimized as needed for the present dataset. Briefly, the images were rescaled and then smoothed with a median filter (diameter = 3.0) as well as a Gaussian filter (diameter = 3.0), and bright features were enhanced (feature size = 30). Primary objects were selected based on size (size range = 4–80), shape (threshold correction factor = 2.1), and intensity using the global robust background thresholding method. Mean and standard deviation were used as the averaging and variance methods, respectively, with the number of deviations being set to 2.1.

Results

Pipeline overview

To study local delivery of viral therapies to the rodent brain, we built the quantitative 3D reconstruction pipeline by adopting and further developing various open-source image processing tools (Fig. 1). The pipeline was designed to process stitched 2D microscopy tile scans of subsampled brain sections where viral payload was visualized by the expression of a fluorescent reporter protein (GFP or mCherry), cell nuclei – staining with DAPI fluorescent nuclear stain, and tissue morphology – bright field (BF) microscopy (Fig. 1b and c). Candidate virus transduced (virus+) cells were first obtained by supervised machine learning (ML) based pixel classification in Ilastik (Fig. 1d) (Berg et al., 2019). Candidate virus+ cells were further classified using a density clustering algorithm to remove distant, low-density false positives (Fig. 1e) (Ester et al., 1996). Ilastik was also used to detect brain tissue in 2D images (Fig. 1f); in the case of capsule implantation, the inversion of the tissue mask allowed to detect the boundaries of the cavity left behind by the implant (Fig. 1g). In parallel, stitched 2D tile scans were aligned to a rat brain atlas with QuickNII (Fig. 1h) (Papp et al., 2014; Puchades et al., 2019). In Nutil, segmentation and alignment outputs were combined (Groeneboom et al., 2020), and 3D distribution of virus+ cell distribution within the brain with respect to the capsule hole/needle track was rendered in Meshview (Fig. 1i). Volumes of the brain tissue transduced with the virus and of the cavity left behind by the implant were then estimated by drawing an alphashape around the 3D object clouds (Fig. 1j). Finally, various quantitative metrics were extracted, such as affected brain tissue volume, total number of virus+ cells in that volume, and the distance of virus+ cells from the drug delivery boundary (Fig. 1k).

Multi-channel pixel classification improves the removal of false positives

As the first step, pixels in 2D microscopy tile scans were classified in Ilastik to identify candidate virus+ cells. When dataset size is limited, pixel followed by object classification in Ilastik is a suitable ML tool to segment fluorescence microscopy images and separate true positive cells from background and false positive objects (Berg et al., 2019). However, in the present dataset, a significant number of false positives remained after image segmentation in Ilastik. High background signal in uncleared brain tissue sections and small size of true positive cells (which resulted in insufficient features) may have interfered with Ilastik performance. Based on visual inspection, a significant fraction of false positives was a population of autofluorescent cells near the site of local drug delivery (Fig. 2a, yellow arrows). These cells autofluorescent in the reporter channel appeared dark in BF channel (Fig. 2a, red arrows); possibly, they were related to hemosiderin formation in response to tissue damage and hemorrhage at the site of drug delivery (Epstein et al., 2001; Zhang et al., 2018). Furthermore, these autofluorescent cells were more prevalent in the capsule implantation condition (Fig. 2a, arrows), which was consistent with the capsule being larger and causing more damage. Even if some true positive cells may have been present in this population of autofluorescent cells, they may have been injured due to their vicinity to the delivery site, so it was sensible to exclude them from total virus+ cell counts. In contrast, true positive cells away from the drug delivery site consistently did not show changes in the intensity of BF channel (Fig. 2a). Therefore, we predicted that, compared to single-channel pixel classification, multi-channel pixel classification would improve the removal of false positives.

To compare the performance of single- and multi-channel pixel classification, we trained Ilastik using either one-channel fluorescence images of reporter protein expression (1ch model) or three-channel images that additionally included fluorescence images of nuclear staining and BF images of tissue morphology (3ch model). Both models were trained consistently using the same training dataset and the same set of pixel classes (virus+, autofluorescence (pixels dark in BF channel and autofluorescent in reporter channel), and background). First, the 3ch model was trained so that cells autofluorescent in the reporter channel and dark in BF channel could be labeled as autofluorescence. Then, the same labels were imported into the 1ch model. After pixel classification, the two models were compared for pixel intensities in BF channel across pixels classified as virus+. As anticipated, the 3ch model removed pixels dark in BF channel, whereas the 1ch model was minimally selective with respect to pixel intensity in BF channel (Fig. 2b). To further quantify the performance of the two models, representative ROIs were labeled by an expert annotator, and recall, precision, and F1 score metrics (Hicks et al., 2022) were calculated (Fig. 2c). Notably, the ROIs were selected to encompass the boundary of drug delivery with the challenging-to-classify autofluorescent cells. In both needle and capsule ROIs, 3ch model outperformed the 1ch model with respect to all performance metrics (Fig. 2c). In part, the better performance of the 3ch model could be attributed to the fact that the training labels were generated in the 3ch model and then imported to the 1ch model. Nonetheless, while the precision of the 3ch model was comparable between the needle and capsule ROIs, the precision of the 1ch model was nearly 2-fold lower in the capsule ROIs (Fig. 2c). Likely, the 1ch model struggled to accurately classify false positive autofluorescent cells that were more prevalent near the capsule boundary (Fig. 2a, arrows). For comparison, the same ROIs were also analyzed using CellProfiler, an existing open-source, automated cell segmentation tool (Carpenter et al., 2006). CellProfiler can only analyze single-channel data, so, like 1ch Ilastik model, it was expected to segment autofluorescent cells near the boundary of drug delivery as virus+ cells. As predicted, the precision of CellProfiler was also lower in the more challenging capsule ROIs (Fig. 2c). Thus, both Ilastik and CellProfiler results supported that single-channel segmentation did not adequately remove false positives, and that multi-channel segmentation performed better in this regard.

Density clustering in consideration of object spatial moment further removes false positives

In our initial studies, false positive objects were still present even after 3ch pixel classification in Ilastik. Therefore, as the second step, candidate virus+ cells had to be classified to further remove noise. We recognized that, in the context of local drug delivery, true positive cells clustered at high density near the site of drug delivery, whereas false negative objects were scattered further away at low density (Fig. 1, Fig. 3a). Thus, we reasoned that object density would be an important parameter in object classification. Although Ilastik can further classify objects obtained after pixel classification, it does not consider object density. Therefore, to further improve the precision of virus+ cell detection, we leveraged DBSCAN, a density clustering algorithm (Ester et al., 1996), to remove low-density false positive objects.

DBSCAN algorithm had to be tuned to account for challenges associated with the present dataset. First, the optimal value of one of DBSCAN parameters – specifically, the maximum distance between an object and its nearest neighbor within a cluster, or EPS – varied greatly across sections depending on the densities of true and false positives objects. Second, DBSCAN could yield multiple clusters, requiring further evaluation to identify true positive clusters. Often, true positives were captured by the single largest cluster. However, depending on the distribution and density of signal and noise, it was also possible for the true positives to cluster into several clusters, or for the false negatives to be the dominant cluster. Therefore, we implemented an iterative DBSCAN algorithm where information from the previous, already clustered section was used to inform not only the clustering but also the selection of clusters. This required manual selection of clusters in a few representative sections to provide seed points to the algorithm and to capture possible locations of the virus+ cells in the brain.

Third, two-dimensional (2D) DBSCAN based on object x and y coordinates did not perform consistently in complex cases where the density of the true positive cloud was not uniform, or the density of nearby false positive noise was relatively high. Possibly, density clustering based on x and y object coordinates alone was not sufficient. Additional information could be extracted from various object intensity metrics. However, we predicted that their value would be limited because a large fraction of false positives was relatively bright and, vice versa, a large fraction of true positives was relatively dim. Visual inspection of the fluorescence tile scans suggested that object environment within a section could be a better predictor of positivity, specifically, object distance to other objects and the intensity of those objects. Therefore, we built upon the framework of image moments where various moments are calculated by summing over pixels in consideration of pixel position within the image and/or pixel intensity (Chaumette, 2004). Similarly, we defined object spatial moment to be the sum of Euclidean distances to all other objects in the section weighted by the intensity of those objects (Eq. (1)). In this new framework, object spatial moment decreased with the number and intensity of close neighbors. We predicted that, if two clusters were close together in the x and y plane but were characterized by different object distributions or intensities, they could be separated along the third spatial moment axis.

Considering object spatial moment allowed to identify complex virus+ cell clouds and to separate them even from high-density nearby noise (Fig. 3a and b). For example, some artifacts could be challenging to remove during pixel classification in Ilastik, especially if they were not present in the training dataset (Fig. 3a, arrows). When high-density artifacts were present close to the site of local drug delivery, they could not be separated from the virus+ cell cloud considering spatial coordinates alone (Fig. 3a, arrows). In contrast, consideration of the 3rd spatial moment dimension allowed to separate and remove high-density artifacts from the nearby virus+ cell cloud (Fig. 3a and b).

Precision, recall, and f1 metrics were again calculated to compare the performance of DBSCAN algorithm that relied on spatial coordinates alone and a fixed EPS value (2D DBSCAN) to the performance of the modified DBSCAN algorithm that additionally considered the 3rd spatial moment dimension and optimized EPS value iteratively (3D iDBSCAN) (Fig. 3c). As the ground truth, true positive virus+ cell clouds were selected manually by an expert annotator in several representative images including the image shown in Fig. 3a. For 2D DBSCAN, EPS value was selected to ensure that the true virus+ cell clouds were fully clustered across representative images, and all resulting clusters were assumed to be true positive. Both algorithms performed similarly with respect to recall (Fig. 3c), suggesting that they missed true positives at a similar rate. However, 3D iDBSCAN outperformed 2D DBSCAN with respect to precision (Fig. 3c), indicating that 2D DBSCAN selected more false positives. The improved precision of 3D iDBSCAN led to overall better performance as demonstrated by the higher F1 score (Fig. 3c). Thus, based on visual inspection and quantitative analysis, 3D iDBSCAN clustering algorithm could further improve the removal of false positives in complex cases where false positives were abundant to form cluster or even cluster densely near the true positive cloud.

3D reconstruction from subsampled 2D sections adequately predicts virus distribution

To validate quantitative 3D reconstruction pipeline, we sought to understand how the estimates of quantitative metrics depended on sampling rate. For this, a third of collected brain sections were analyzed and further subsampled at various rates in silico (Fig. 4). At each subsampling rate, the total number of virus+ cells in the brain were estimated (Fig. 4a). For this purpose, we assumed that virus+ cell counts were smooth and continuous across sections but contained error. To mitigate the effect that this error may have on the estimates, virus+ cell counts were smoothed across sections with a one-dimensional Gaussian filter. Linear regression was then performed between consecutive sections, and the obtained section estimates were summed to estimate the total count of virus+ cells in the brain. The estimates calculated at various subsampling rates were then compared to the estimate obtained when all data (or every 3rd section) was considered, which was assumed to give an accurate prediction. As expected, relative error increased with subsampling rate as the amount of data used to inform the estimate decreased (Fig. 4a). Median relative error remained below 10% for sampling rates up to every 15th section (Fig. 4a). However, at subsampling rates of every 12th or 15th section, relative error could increase significantly above 10% depending on which sections were subsampled (Fig. 4a). Therefore, we concluded that subsampling every 9th section, or approximately 10% of all sections, can give an accurate prediction for the total count of virus+ cells in the brain.

To further validate the estimates for virus+ cell counts in each section, we split the entire imaged dataset into the training and validation datasets. The training dataset was comprised of every 9th section (which we concluded to accurately predict the total virus+ cell count in the brain), whereas the validation dataset included all remaining imaged sections. Predicted virus+ cell counts in the missing sections were obtained by performing Gaussian smoothing on the training dataset followed by linear regression between each pair of consecutive sections (Fig. 4b). Thus, even at the same subsampling rate, less data were used to inform the predictions of virus+ cell counts in each missing section than in the whole brain, so the estimates shown in Fig. 4b were expected to have higher error than the estimates shown in Fig. 4a at the subsampling rate of every 9th section. Prediction uncertainty was estimated as the standard deviation of the differences between the actual and predicted virus+ cell counts in the training dataset (Fig. 4b). The obtained predictions were compared against the actual virus+ cell counts in the sections of the validation dataset. We expected that, if the prediction was accurate, 68% and 95% of the actual virus+ cell counts in the sections of the test dataset would fall within one and two standard deviations of the prediction, respectively. We observed that ∼10% fewer actual counts – 56% and 83% – fell within one and two standard deviations, respectively, which was within 12% of the expected values.

The subsampling validation was repeated on additional datasets (Fig. S1). In Fig. 4a, AAV5 was administered by capsule implantation, so we repeated the analysis on a dataset where the same viral vector was administered by needle injection (Fig. S1, a and b). This time, the predictions were less accurate (Fig. S1, a and b). For example, at the subsampling rate of every 9th section, the relative error in total virus+ cell count varied from 10 to nearly 40% (Fig. S1a). We speculated that the smaller dataset size was responsible for less accurate predictions. Specifically, while AAV5+ cell cloud spammed over 150 consecutive sections in the case of capsule implantation (Fig. 4b), it spammed only ∼50 sections in the case of needle injection (Fig. S1b). So, we repeated subsampling validation on yet another dataset where AAV1 was administered by needle injection (Fig. S1, c and d). AAV1+ cell cloud spammed ∼120 sections, so accurate predictions (within 10% of the ground truth) were again generated at the subsampling rates as high as every 18th section (Fig. S1, c). Furthermore, at the subsampling rate of every 9th section, 65% and 88% of the actual virus+ cell counts fell within one and two standard deviations, respectively (Fig. S1d). Therefore, quantitative 3D reconstruction pipeline could adequately (although not perfectly) predict the number of virus-transduced cells in the whole brain and even in each section if ∼10% of sections were sampled. However, more sections may have to be sampled to analyze narrower virus+ cell clouds.

Quantitative 3D reconstruction pipeline captures differences in the distribution of different viruses

Next, we tested whether the quantitative 3D reconstruction pipeline could capture differences in the distribution of two different viral vectors, AAV and adeno virus (AdV) (Fig. 5). AdV is several times larger than AAV (80–100 nm vs ∼24 nm in diameter, respectively (Atchinson et al., 1965)), so we predicted that the smaller AAV vector would disperse further from the point of local drug delivery. In this experiment, AAV serotype 1 (AAV1) was used. Consistent with the starting hypothesis, visual inspection of the 3D renderings of viral vector distribution suggested that the smaller AAV1 vector dispersed further in the rat brain than the larger AdV vector (Fig. 5a). Quantification of various parameters of viral vector distribution further supported the starting hypothesis (Fig. 5b and c). By either needle injection or capsule implantation, the smaller AAV1 vector transduced on the order of 10,000 cells over on the order of 100 mm3 of brain tissue volume (Fig. 5b). In contrast, the larger AdV virus did not disperse as well, transducing 30-40x fewer cells than AAV1. Compared to AAV1, AdV also distributed through a considerably smaller brain tissue volume (Fig. 5b). In the needle case, AdV was confined to the needle track (Fig. 5a), and it affected >100x smaller brain tissue volume that AAV1 (Fig. 5b). In the capsule case, AdV affected a relatively larger volume (Fig. 5b); however, more replicates would be needed to support this finding. Quantification of the distance between virus+ cells and the boundary of drug delivery further supported the superior dispersion of AAV1 (Fig. 5b and c). Most AAV1+ cells could be detected within 3 mm from the drug delivery boundary by either delivery method. In contrast, most AdV+ cells could be detected only within 1 mm from that boundary. Thus, overall, the quantitative 3D reconstruction pipeline successfully captured the wider dispersion of the smaller viral vector.

To test whether wider spread of AAV1 vector was reproducible, we injected another three rats with the same virus mixture (Fig. 6). Consistently with the first experiment (Fig. 5), AAV1 vector transduced more cells, impacted a larger tissue volume, and travelled further from the drug delivery boundary than the AdV vector (Fig. 6). Furthermore, various parameters of virus spread were consistent across biological replicates (Fig. 6). However, the parameters differed tangibly between the two experiments, with the AdV vector now performing better (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Across the two experiments, the lots of the viral vectors were identical. However, different researchers performed the experiments. We speculate that different researchers may have handled the virus differently, which may have affected virus infectivity at the time of injection. Furthermore, they may have zeroed the needle at the reference point on the skull differently, which may have impacted the exact location of where the viral vectors were delivered. In turn, this may have impacted viral vector spread in the brain.

Differences in transduction profiles or efficiencies may have contributed to the apparent wider spread of the smaller AAV1 vector. To account for such a possibility, representative sections from the second experiment (Fig. 5) were stained with either anti-AAV or anti-AdV antibodies to confirm that the particles of the larger AdV vector were not present further from the drug delivery boundary (Fig. S2). However, in the case of both antibodies, no signal characteristic of true positive antibody staining could be detected (Fig. S2), possibly because antibody binding targets of non-replicating viral vectors were depleted by day 10 post administration. Thus, it remained inconclusive whether differences in transduction profiles or efficiencies contributed to the apparent wider spread of AAV1. Nonetheless, near the needle track, the signal of both fluorescent reporter proteins was strong (Fig. S2, cyan arrows), suggestive of high transduction efficiency by both AAV1 and AdV. In contrast, away from the needle track, only AAV1+ clusters could be detected (Fig. S2, white arrows). Therefore, because both viral vectors showed good transduction efficiency near the needle track but only the smaller AAV1 was observed to transduce cells away from the needle track, the role of size in the viral vector spread was supported.

Quantitative 3D reconstruction pipeline can be extended to large animal models

Finally, we tested whether the quantitative 3D reconstruction pipeline could be translated to analyze the distribution of locally delivered viral vectors in large animal models. For this purpose, we administered AAV9 serotype to ovine brain. A sheep brain is much larger than a rat brain (see Methods), so the scanning of whole sheep brain sections by fluorescence microscopy is not practical. Only a small region around the site of local delivery could be sectioned and imaged (Fig. 7a), which prohibited 2D image alignment to a 3D brain atlas. Therefore, to reconstruct 3D virus distribution in the sheep brain (Fig. 7b), the tile scans of neighboring sections were instead aligned by similarity in ImageJ. Furthermore, virus+ cells appeared larger in the images of sheep brain sections, so they exhibited more features and could be classified with Ilastik object classification to further remove false positive objects. Otherwise, the detection of virus+ cells in sheep and rat brain sections was identical. Quantitative analysis of AAV9 distribution in the sheep brain revealed that the AAV9 delivery by either needle injection or capsule implantation resulted in similar distribution (Fig. 7c). Based on the order of magnitude assessment, both delivery methods led to a similar number of AAV9+ cells over a similar tissue volume (Fig. 7c). However, the virus appeared to spread further from the boundary of drug delivery in the case of needle injection (Fig. 7c and d). However, we speculated that the needle tip appeared to be further away from the virus+ cell cloud in the reconstruction (Fig. 7b) than in the actual brain because we could not reconstruct the entire needle track from the subsampled sheep brain sections. We acknowledge that a more rigorous comparison of the two delivery methods would require more biological replicates, which is outside the scope of this work. Discussion

Unlike systemic drug administration, local drug delivery can circumvent the BBB and deliver the drug to the locus of therapeutic interest, minimizing drug losses and toxicity to peripheral organs and off-target brain regions. To be effective, local drug delivery must distribute the drug over a sufficiently large target tissue volume. However, the distribution of locally delivered viral therapies cannot be predicted or monitored accurately. To close this knowledge gap, we developed a 3D reconstruction pipeline to visualize and quantify the distribution of locally administered viral vectors from 2D images of subsampled rat brain tissue sections. The pipeline was designed to be accessible to the research community. It analyzes 2D microscopy tile scans of brain sections, which are commonly prepared in biology labs or outsourced to histopathology labs and can be imaged on the less expensive epifluorescence microscopes. In contrast, the expertise required to prepare samples for 3D imaging is less common, and confocal and light sheet microscopes are more expensive and often inaccessible to small drug discovery and delivery venues. Furthermore, the pipeline was built upon open-source computational tools. Similar to Quint workflow (Yates et al., 2019), Ilastik (Berg et al., 2019) was chosen for ML-based pixel classification given the limited size of the datasets, and QuickNII (Puchades et al., 2019) and Nutil (Groeneboom et al., 2020) were used to align 2D images of rat brain sections to an atlas and reconstruct 3D distribution. Therefore, although our pipeline requires a foundational understanding of computer science and bioinformatics, it can be implemented with minimal upfront cost.

However, we recognized that large-scale tile-scanning of whole uncleared rat brain sections complicated accurate detection of virus transduced cells during supervised ML-based pixel classification in Ilastik. First, uncleared brain tissue sections had high background. Second, to image whole rat brain tissue at a reasonable speed, high magnification objective could not be used (in this work, 10× objective was selected). As result, virus transduced cells did not have enough features to be effectively separated from false positive objects. In situations where spatial information is limited, additional information can be extracted from images acquired in other channels. We further noted that autofluorescent false positive objects that clustered near the site of local drug delivery were dark in the bright field channel. We speculated that these false positives objects may have been related to hemosiderin formation in response to tissue damage (Epstein et al., 2001; Zhang et al., 2018). Including bright field images in the pixel classification model and creating a separate label class for this group of autofluorescent false positive pixels significantly improved model accuracy. More broadly, our approach emphasized the importance that the annotation strategy has on the final ML model performance when data is limited.

In the context of local drug delivery, density clustering is well suited to further remove low-density false positives from the high-density true positive cloud. However, when true and false positive object densities vary significantly across or within sections, density clustering requires further tuning. To account for different densities across sections, we implemented an iterative density clustering algorithm where optimal clustering parameters for each section were determined by comparing the object cloud in the present section to the corresponding true positive cloud in the previous, already clustered section. Furthermore, to account for variable densities within sections, we defined object spatial moment to mathematically describe object position within an image with respect to other objects (namely, their positions and intensities). We observed that, in complex cases where a high-density false positive cluster was located close to the true positive cloud, consideration of the object spatial moment as an additional dimension during density clustering allowed to separate the two clusters. Similarly, object moments can be used in other object classification algorithms to consider object context during classification.

Alignment of 2D images of whole rat brain sections to a 3D rat brain atlas (Papp et al., 2014) (or of consecutive sections of a sheep brain fragment by similarity) then allowed to estimate various metrics of virus dispersal in the brain, such as affected tissue volume, total number of virus transduced cells, and virus distribution from the boundary of drug delivery. Notably, the pipeline could account for the omitted tissue sections to arrive at the overall accurate distribution. We validated that, generally, as few as 10% of the total sections can be analyzed to obtain accurate estimates; in practice, sections can be sliced at different thicknesses (e.g. 1x50 and 9 × 50 = 450 μm) to speed up the slicing process. However, it is recognized that the optimal subsampling rate depends on virus spread in the brain and other parameters, such as section thickness, and should be determined for each dataset. With the quantitative 3D reconstruction pipeline, we were able to capture that the smaller AAV vector dispersed better in the rat brain than the larger AdV vector. Furthermore, we determined that, in both rat and sheep brains, AAV distribution was comparable by either needle injection or liquid-filled capsule implantation. Overall, our quantitative 3D reconstruction pipeline provides an accessible, accurate, and fast means to study the performance of various local gene therapy platforms in both small and large animal models towards the treatment of CNS diseases.
