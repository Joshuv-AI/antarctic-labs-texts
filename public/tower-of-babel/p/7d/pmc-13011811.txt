Corticothalamic layer 6 controls cortical activity and thalamic firing mode in a bidirectional manner
Authors: Ross Folkard, Emilio Ulises Isaías-Camacho, Alexander Groh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCorticothalamic layer 6 modulates information flow between cortical and thalamic circuits. Previous research reported contrasting inhibitory or excitatory effects of corticothalamic layer 6 on cortical dynamics, potentially reflecting technological discrepancies or physiological differences in corticothalamic layer 6 function. To resolve these discrepancies, we combined translaminar, multi-channel in vivo electrophysiology in the primary somatosensory cortex of anesthetized mice with optogenetic stimulation across a range of stimulation regimes to manipulate firing rate and spiking statistics of corticothalamic layer 6. Increasing corticothalamic layer 6 firing rates exerted a transition from inhibition to excitation across cortical layers. Furthermore, corticothalamic layer 6 activity imparted population synchrony onto distinct cortical subpopulations, independent of changes in overall corticothalamic layer 6 activity. In the thalamus, corticothalamic layer 6 modulated thalamic bursting in a bidirectional manner, dependent on optogenetic stimulation frequency. These results demonstrate that corticothalamic layer 6 in primary somatosensory cortex can bidirectionally modulate both cortical firing and thalamic firing mode, elucidating a more nuanced function of somatosensory corticothalamic layer 6 in thalamic and cortical signaling than previously recognized.Keywords: corticothalamic feedback, firing-mode switching (burst → tonic), frequency-dependent modulation, population synchrony, sensory gain control

Introduction

Corticothalamic layer 6 (L6CT) neurons play key roles in regulating activity in both the cortex and thalamus, exerting monosynaptic excitation and disynaptic inhibition onto these targets (Lam and Sherman 2010; Jurgens et al. 2012; Olsen et al. 2012; Bortone et al. 2014; Kim et al. 2014; Mease et al. 2014; Frandolig et al. 2019; Whilden et al. 2021). L6CT is known to bidirectionally modulate thalamic excitability depending on stimulation frequency and intensity (Crandall et al. 2015; Dimwamwa et al. 2024). However, whether L6CT similarly exerts activity-dependent control over cortical dynamics remains an open question.

In visual and somatosensory cortex in vitro experiments, L6CT has been shown to strongly excite layer 5 pyramidal neurons whilst weakly exciting layer 4 excitatory neurons (Kim et al. 2014). Additionally, L6CT provides feedforward inhibition to layers 4 and 5 via distinct GABAergic subtypes (Kim et al. 2014; Frandolig et al. 2019; Olsen et al. 2012; Bortone et al. 2014; Adesnik, 2018). However L6CT contains neurons with different intrinsic properties and connectivity, and in vivo studies using optogenetic activation of L6CT have reported conflicting findings, with some observing net excitatory effects and others reporting net inhibition, depending on sensory modalities and experimental conditions (Olsen et al. 2012; Guo et al. 2017; Whilden et al. 2021; Ziegler et al. 2023).

This variability raises the question of whether these differences reflect genuine physiological distinctions between sensory cortices or are instead due to differences in experimental methodology. The few direct comparisons between modalities suggest that L6CT circuitry is largely conserved (Kim et al. 2014), implying that methodological factors may account for much of the observed heterogeneity. If this is the case, systematically varying L6CT stimulation parameters within a single cortical area should reproduce a range of effects seen across prior studies (Olsen et al. 2012; Guo et al. 2017; Ziegler et al. 2023).

In this present study, we examined how different L6CT stimulation regimes influence neuronal spiking in the primary somatosensory cortex (S1) and in ventral posterolateral thalamus (VPL), a first-order thalamic recipient of S1 L6CT feedback (Rouiller and Welker 2000; Guo et al. 2020), of anesthetized mice, with the central question of whether L6CT stimulation has a net excitatory, inhibitory, or bidirectional effect on ongoing spiking activity. We previously observed both suppressive and excitatory effects of continuous L6CT stimulation on single units in the S1-VPL hind-limb system (Ziegler et al. 2023) which gave rise to the current hypothesis that L6CT firing rate and firing statistics determine L6CT’s effect on the thalamus and cortex. Here, by varying L6CT stimulation parameters (intensity and rate), we observed that L6CT activity bidirectionally modulates layers 4 and 5, with low L6CT activity inducing inhibition and high L6CT activity driving excitation. Additionally, L6CT stimulation modulated thalamic firing mode in a frequency-dependent manner that diverged from L6CT-induced changes in thalamic rate. These findings provide new insights into how L6CT dynamically regulates cortical and thalamic activity, refining our understanding of its role in sensory processing.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The local governing body approved all following experimental procedures (Regierungspräsidium Karlsruhe, Germany, approval numbers: 35–9185.81/G-29/16, 35–9185.81/G-70/21, T-39-20, 35–9185.82/A-8/20) and procedures were performed to ethical guidelines.

Animals

Mice (male and female, 7–16 weeks of age) were housed with food and water ad libitum on 12 h light/dark cycles (housing conditions 20°C–22°C, 45%–65% humidity). Data presented is from a combined 9 mice: 5 for S1, 4 for VPL.

Mouse line

“Ntsr1-Cre-ChR2-EYFP”; crossbreed between “Ntsr1-cre” (B6.FVB(Cg)-Tg(Ntsr1-cre)GN220Gsat/Mmucd) x “Ai32” (B6;129S-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm-32(CAG-COP4*H134R/EYFP)Hze/J).

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Mice were humanely euthanized (i.p. Ketamin 120 mg/kg; Xylazin 20 mg/kg) followed by transcardial perfusion using a solution of 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline. Brain tissue was sliced into 80 μm sections using a vibratome (Thermo Scientific Microm HM 650 V) and visualized using an epifluorescent microscope (Leica DM6000).

In vivo electrophysiology

Anesthetized in vivo electrophysiology

Mice were anesthetized using urethane (1.4 g/kg, i.p.) and regulated using an oxygen-isoflurane mixture (0.2%). Mice were securely positioned with ear bars, and the skull was leveled. A craniotomy was performed above the well site, and a well, filled with isotonic Ringer’s solution, was cemented in place using Paladur. 64-channel silicone probes (impedance ~ 50 kOhm, Cambridge Neurotech) were inserted into the S1-Hind Limb (S1HL) cortex (Medial-Lateral (ML) = +1.67 mm, Anterior–Posterior (AP) = −1.0 mm, Dorsal-Ventral (DV) = −1.4 mm) and/or VPL (ML = +1.8, AP = −1.3, DV = −4.5) using a micromanipulator (Luigs Neumann 3-axis Motor) at 2 μm/s. Probes were connected to a RHD2164 headstage amplifier chip (Intan technologies) via a connector (ASSY-77) and an adaptor (A64-Om32x2 Samtec). Signals were amplified and digitized at a sampling rate of 30,030 Hz using an RHD2000 Intan evaluation board through a USB 2.0 interface. Data was acquired using an Intan Talker module (Cambridge Electronic Devices, Cambridge, UK) with Spike2 (v9.06) software. An optical fiber (Thorlabs GmbH, NA = 0.22; radius = 52.5 μm) was positioned ~ 0.5 mm perpendicular above the craniotomy. Laser intensity ranged between 58 and 1201 mW/mm2 from the fiber tip. VPL recordings employed laser intensities of 802 mW/mm2 in one experiment and 1201 mW/mm2 from the fiber tip in the other 3. Light pulses were initiated automatically by an Omicron Light-Hub2 (Wavelength = 488 nm) with protocols programmed in Spike2 through interface hardware (Power1401, Cambridge Electronic Design, Cambridge, UK). Laser stimulus conditions were cycled in a 5 s on, 5 s off paradigm such that every condition was administered at least once per minute.

Spike sorting

Voltage data was band-pass filtered upon initial acquisition (0.1–10,000 Hz or 500–5000 Hz). Spikes were sorted with Kilosort 2.5, followed by curation in Phy2 (available at https://github.com/cortex-lab/phy). Single units with less than 0.5% refractory period violations (within 1 ms) and a baseline spike rate greater than 0.1 Hz were selected for further analysis. Putative cell-type classification

Thalamic units

VPL units were identified based on probe location within VPL through histological examination plus significant responses (Wilcoxon sign rank) to continuous laser condition. All units within this determined range were classified as VPL units.

Cortical units

Recording channels from silicon probes were aligned with histological layer demarcations to attribute each unit a specific cortical depth and layer. Putative L6CT units were identified from their low latency and low-jitter responses to high intensity (802 mW/mm2) laser stimulation at 10 Hz frequency (Ziegler et al. 2023) (Fig. 1D). The discrepancy between the proportion of tagged L6 units (52%) Ntsr1+ L6CT cells previously reported (Kim et al. 2014) may be due to underrepresentation in spike-sorted data due to their comparatively low firing rate (Vélez-Fort et al. 2014). Waveform separation was not implemented (Barthó et al. 2004; Cardin et al. 2009; Madisen et al. 2012; Halassa et al. 2014; Anastassiou et al. 2015; Schmitt et al. 2017; Yu et al. 2019). Electrophysiological analyses therefore encompassed putative FS and RS units.

Population synchrony quantification

Triggered Peri-Stimulus-Time-Histograms (PSTHs) were calculated for each condition across each trial. This resulted in unit-by-bin-by-trial matrices for each condition, with each time bin containing a value of spike counts n. Transforming the matrix such that n > 0 = 1, bins in this “Coactivity PSTH” denoted whether a unit fired per time bin per trial. Summing the bins across units constitutes a measure of coactivity for the population per time period per trial. The coactivity measure for a given PSTH was calculated as the mean coactivity across the bins where at least one unit during that time period was active. The rate of change in coactivity measures as bin sizes increased from 0.1 ms to 2.048 s differed between conditions. The maximum value of the rate of change represented the increases in bin size necessary to capture the greatest increase in average coactivity. The higher the score, the closer a population’s neurons spike together.

Thalamic burst identification

Thalamic bursts were classified as 2 or more spikes that each do not exceed 6 ms inter-spike-intervals, which are preceded by at least 50 ms prior silence, and do not exceed 100 ms total duration. Parameters were based on the ones used to study nociceptive signaling in VPL by Hains et al. but with a stricter inter-spike interval (ISI) threshold and a pre-burst silence to reflect prior hyperpolarization necessary for T-Type Ca2+ channel availability (Zhan et al. 1999; Hains et al. 2006).

Heterogeneity analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated on mean evoked rates of the single units during the first 4.5 sof laser-evoked activity in each subpopulation for each pair of conditions (15 pairs total) (Fig. S1). Differences in median population rates between each pair of conditions were also calculated for a given subpopulation. Thus for each pair of conditions we calculated the degree of heterogeneity in the populations and the difference in median firing rate per population.

Results

To assess L6CT modulation of cortical population activity, we recorded multilayer activity across the S1HL cortex in anesthetized Ntsr1-Cre-Chr2-EYFP mice whilst optogenetically stimulating L6CT neurons at increasing light intensities (Fig. 1A). Increasing stimulation intensity in steps led to progressive decreases in post-pulse first-spike response latencies across cortical populations, with the deepest units exhibiting the shortest first-spike latencies (Fig. 1C).

Putative L6CT units were identified through “optotagging” based on their low first-spike response latencies and standard deviations (Fig. 1D). Tagged units coincided with the deepest cortical electrodes, corresponding to layer 6, where approximately 52% of layer 6 units were tagged. Separating L6CT units from the remaining S1 units enabled examination of how L6CT population activity influenced S1 activity. At the lowest laser intensity, insufficient to sustain elevated L6CT activity, overall S1 firing rate remained unchanged (Fig. 1E). However, at sustained yet low L6CT firing rates (fr), overall S1 fr was suppressed throughout the period of optogenetic stimulation (Fig. 1E). Surprisingly, increasing L6CT activity through increased laser intensity caused a reversal of this effect, with overall S1 fr rising above baseline levels in a ramp-like fashion, peaking earlier as a function of the sustained L6CT population fr.

To assess whether this population-level activity switch reflected heterogeneous responses across S1 subpopulations, we calculated MI to track proportional changes in single unit fr during this L6CT-induced switch of S1 activity. Whilst low L6CT fr (0.83 Hz) globally reduced MIs across the S1 population, high L6CT fr (3.77 Hz) increased MIs (Fig. 1F). Notably, 65% (517/791) of S1 units exhibited increased MIs in high vs low L6CT activity. Of the 71% of single units that were suppressed under low L6CT activity (559/791), 57% (316/559) of these displayed activity elevated from baseline during high L6CT activity, with ~ 40% (316/791) of total single units switching from suppression to excitation. Only ~ 10% (84/791) displayed reduced MIs during high vs low L6CT activity. These results demonstrate that sustained L6CT activity can bidirectionally modulate cortical excitability of single units depending on population L6CT fr, transitioning from S1 suppression at low L6CT population activity to S1 excitation at higher L6CT activity.

The laser response profiles observed in Fig. 1C & D suggest three subpopulations that are separable by their respective first-spike latencies and depths. Analysis of first-spike latencies and unit depths revealed three distinct subpopulations within S1, corresponding to putative layer 4 (L4), putative layer 5 (L5) and L6CT neurons. L6CT units had low mean first-spike latencies and low standard deviations of these latencies. L4 units had longer mean first-spike latencies with higher standard deviations compared to L6CT units and resided on the upper recording channels, whereas L5 units were situated between L6CT and L4, exhibiting the longest mean first-spike latency with the highest standard deviations (Fig. 2A). Basal fr was highest in L5 units (0.55 Hz) followed by L4 (0.11 Hz), and L6CT populations (0.03 Hz).

Both L4 and L5 units exhibited suppression during low L6CT activity (Fig. 2B). However, the magnitude of suppression differed between populations, with L5 units displaying lower MIs (−0.96) than L4 units (−0.39) (Fig. 2C). Under high L6CT activity, both L4 and L5 exhibited elevated MIs that differed significantly from each other, with L4 (0.98) exhibiting greater enhancement than L5 (0.58). Of the L4 units that were suppressed under low L6CT activity, 75% (68/91) displayed elevated MIs under high L6CT activity, with 76% of all single units (120/157) displaying increased MIs under high vs low L6CT activity. Similarly, 71% of L5 units that were suppressed under low L6CT activity had elevated responses under high L6CT activity (143/202), with 84% (183/219) exhibiting increased MIs under high vs low L6CT activity. These findings indicate that L6CT-mediated bidirectional modulation affects cortical subpopulations differently, with the extent of suppression and excitation at a given L6CT activity level dependent on the cortical layer, suggesting different switch points between layers (Fig. 2D).

The bidirectional activity switch of L4 and L5 populations to increasing continuous L6CT stimulation raised the question of how L4 and L5 populations would respond to varying temporal profiles of L6CT stimulation. To do so, 10 ms laser pulses were delivered at 1 Hz and 10 Hz frequencies at both low and high laser intensities to drive L6CT spiking activity with these temporal profiles. Stimulations elicited reliable L6CT spiking across the duration of stimulation, particularly in the high intensity stimulation conditions (Fig. 3A), though L6CT single units did not respond to every laser pulse (Fig. 3B). L6CT fr progressively increased from 1 to 10 Hz, to continuous low intensity L6CT stimulation. L4 exhibited a bidirectional modulation to low intensity stimulation of L6CT; Low 1 Hz L6CT stimulation did not induce significantly different L4 fr compared with baseline fr, whereas 10 Hz L6CT stimulation increased L4 fr and continuous L6CT stimulation suppressed L4 fr (Fig. 3C). Compared to baseline, L5 displayed incremental fr decreases to 1 Hz, 10 Hz, and continuous low L6CT stimulation. The bidirectional modulation from low L6CT intensity stimulation conditions were reflected in L4 MIs (Fig. 3D), with 10 Hz L6CT stimulation increasing L4 MIs compared with 1 Hz, and continuous stimulation decreasing them. Continuous low L6CT stimulation decreased L5 MIs compared to 1 Hz and 10 Hz low L6CT stimulation. In summary, the direction of change in L4 fr depended on the pattern of low intensity L6CT stimulation, whereas all 3 low L6CT stimulation patterns decreased L5 fr, with the continuous stimulation causing the biggest decrease.

Similarly to low L6CT stimulation, L6CT population fr under high L6CT stimulation was increased from 1 Hz to 10 Hz, to continuous stimulation. In contrast to low intensity L6CT stimulation, high intensity L6CT stimulation increased L4 fr from baseline during 1 Hz, 10 Hz, and continuous L6CT stimulation (Fig. 3E). L5 fr was similarly elevated from baseline during 10 Hz and continuous high L6CT stimulation, though differed significantly in both these conditions from 1 Hz L6CT stimulation. L4 MIs differed under 1 Hz L6CT stimulation compared with 10 Hz and continuous L6CT stimulation, whereas the different stimulation patterns of high L6CT excitation did not induce significantly different MIs in L5 (Fig. 3F). In summary, high L6CT stimulation increased L4 fr by different magnitudes in the 1 Hz, 10 Hz, and continuous stimulation patterns, whereas L5 fr was increased by all stimulation patterns, though the magnitudes of increase did not differ between every stimulation pattern.

Given that L4 and L5 population responses differed greatly between L6CT stimulation patterns that altered the magnitude and temporal profile of L6CT population activity, L6CT population fr alone may not be sufficient to account for the varied responses. Alongside total activity, L6CT single-unit responses could differ across stimulation patterns in heterogeneity and synchrony. We investigated heterogeneity by quantifying differences in single unit response rankings between each pair of conditions using a Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Correlation coefficients (cfs) denoted the degree of heterogeneity of single unit responses between a given pair of conditions (cf. closer to 1 = less heterogeneous) (Fig. S1A). We observed no significant Pearson correlation between Spearman cfs nor differences in L4 or L5 median fr between conditions, suggesting that L6CT single unit response heterogeneity had little influence on differences in population fr in L4 and L5 (Fig. S1B). In contrast, changes in L6CT median fr were strongly and linearly correlated with changes in L4 fr (R2 = 0.78; P < 0.0001). We therefore asked whether L6CT synchrony, independent of overall L6CT activity, might be a key determinant of L4 and L5 responses. To quantify synchrony independently of fr, we binned spiking activity during the response windows into PSTHs of varying bin sizes. We then calculated population synchrony as a measure of maximum increase in the proportion of coactive neurons relative to changes in bin size (Fig. 4A; also Methods). L6CT synchrony and overall activity were uncorrelated across conditions (slope = 0.08; Fig. 4B), indicating that parameters varied independently. At high laser intensities, low frequency L6CT stimulation conditions produced highly synchronous but low L6CT activity, whereas continuous stimulation had the opposite effect that indicated more stochastic population firing (Fig. S2A). L6CT activity correlated with L4 activity but not L5 activity. Additionally, neither L4 activity nor L5 activity correlated significantly with L6CT synchrony (Fig. 4C). In contrast, L6CT synchrony correlated significantly with both L4 and L5 synchrony, and L4 synchrony further correlated significantly with L6CT activity (Fig. 4D). This analysis suggests that L6CT spiking exerts a strong influence on L4 spiking, with its activity shaping both L4 activity and synchrony. Additionally, L6CT entrains both L4 and L5 spiking through its influence on population spiking synchrony.

After establishing L6CT’s effect on cortical dynamics, we next tested how different L6CT spiking patterns modulate thalamic activity. Previous studies in vivo have demonstrated that continuous L6CT excitation induces a switch in neurons from burst to tonic mode (Mease et al. 2014; Ziegler et al. 2023). However, the effects of different L6CT stimulation frequencies on thalamic firing modes remain less explored. We recorded from VPL single units in Ntsr1-Cre-Ch2-EYFP mice during 1 Hz, 10 Hz, and continuous L6CT stimulation. (Fig. 5A). All L6CT stimulation patterns elevated overall VPL fr relative to baseline (0.7 Hz), with 1 Hz (0.89 Hz) eliciting the smallest increase in fr followed by 10 Hz (1.47 Hz), and continuous stimulation inducing the highest fr (2.59 Hz) (Fig. 5B). Burst events were classified as ≥2 spikes with ≤ 6 ms inter-spike intervals, preceded by ≥50 ms silence, and lasting ≤ 100 ms (Fig. 5C). As measured by the proportion of burst events to all firing events (burst event ratio, br), we observed that VPL br decreased during continuous stimulation (0.02) of L6CT compared to baseline (0.03), indicating a shift towards tonic-firing mode (Fig. 5D). Interestingly, both 1 Hz (0.06) and 10 Hz (0.07) L6CT stimulation incrementally increased bursting activity. These findings suggest that L6CT excitation enhances thalamic throughput, with the temporal pattern of L6CT spiking mediating whether VPL neurons engage burst or tonic firing modes.

Discussion

Whether L6CT activity suppresses or enhances cortical firing has remained contentious, with studies across sensory cortices reporting either an inhibitory or excitatory effect of optogenetic L6CT excitation, but not both within the same sensory cortex (Olsen et al. 2012; Guo et al. 2017; Ziegler et al. 2023). Using an established optotagging approach based on first-spike latency and its variability (Kvitsiani et al. 2013; Hangya et al. 2015; Ziegler et al. 2023), we identified S1 L6CT single units and examined their impact on cortical and thalamic populations across stimulation regimes. We observed a clear activity-dependent switch: low L6CT activity suppressed firing in S1 layers 4 and 5, whereas high L6CT activity excited these same subpopulations. Rather than this activity switch resulting from heterogeneously behaving populations within each cortical layer, where i.e. one subpopulation is suppressed under low L6CT stimulation and a different subpopulation activated under high L6CT stimulation, the activity switch occurred within individual single units, with the majority of single units suppressed under low L6CT activity displaying elevated firing under high L6CT activity (Fig. 1F). This switch occurred from L6CT activity between approximately 1 and 4 Hz, a range reported in behavioral contexts (O’Connor et al. 2010; Clayton et al. 2021; Dash et al. 2022; Dimwamwa et al. 2024). Whilst previous studies have suggested a mainly inhibitory role of L6CT in S1 (Ziegler et al. 2023; Dimwamwa et al. 2024), we demonstrate that S1 L6CT population activity can exert either an inhibitory or excitatory effect on cortical activity dependent on the magnitude and dynamics of L6CT activity (Fig. 6). L6CT modulated two distinct cortical subpopulations in both directions, with L4 more excited than L5 at both low and high L6CT activity (Fig. 2C). Together with similar reports of activity switches in other circuits (Crandall et al. 2015; Dimwamwa et al. 2024), this suggests that bidirectional switches may be common functional motifs.

Cortical responses to optogenetic L6CT stimulation not only depended on stimulation intensity but also on L6CT stimulation frequency (Fig. 3C-F). Because optogenetic stimulation not only increases neuronal fr but also population synchrony (Boyden et al. 2005; Han et al. 2009), we introduced an analysis to disentangle changes in firing rate from changes in spike synchrony across stimulation conditions. Whilst L6CT activity correlated with L4 activity, that in turn preceded L5 activity, L6CT activity did not linearly correlate with L5 activity (Fig. 4C). However, L6CT synchrony correlated with both L4 and L5 synchrony (Fig. 4D). Thus, differences in the patterns of L6CT activity appear to influence the timing of L5 spiking across L5 units, but not necessarily the magnitude. Thus, L6CT synchrony and activity may constitute partially independent channels during information transfer (Buzsáki 2010; Buzsáki and Vöröslakos 2023).

L6CT-evoked spikes in downstream neurons had longer latencies and variability in L5 than L4, suggesting that L6CT engages L5 through multiple synapses (Fig. 2A; Fig. S3C). One possibility would be a disynaptic pathway from L6CT to L5 via L4, and indeed L4 activity correlated with L5 activity (Fig. S2B). However, although L6CT synchrony correlated with both L4 and L5 synchrony, L4 synchrony did not correlate with L5 synchrony (Fig. S2B), suggesting L6CT modulates L5 synchrony distinctly from its effects on L4 synchrony.

We observed that L4 and L5 exhibited facilitation at 10 Hz L6CT stimulation (Fig. S3B). This 10 Hz stimulation is close to spindle frequency, which is known to facilitate thalamic neurons (Borbély et al. 2025). Therefore, the observed facilitation in L4 and L5 could be caused by a thalamo-cortical resonant mechanism in which L6CT engages VPL into facilitation (Fig. S3E), which in turn facilitates L4 and L5. Whilst both L4 and L5 facilitated at 10 Hz L6CT stimulation, only L4 facilitated at 1 Hz, indicating that L4 integrates L6CT input over longer time windows than L5. This frequency-dependent facilitation of L4 could aid in amplifying sensory inputs from thalamic regions, of which a substantial proportion terminates in L4 (Gilbert and Wiesel 1979; Sherman and Guillery 2002; Douglas and Martin 2004; Clascá et al. 2012; Constantinople and Bruno 2013; Adesnik and Naka 2018).

Furthermore, we observed that L6CT exerts frequency-dependent influence over VPL firing mode (Fig. 5C & D). Rhythmic L6CT input (1 or 10 Hz) increased bursting, whereas continuous L6CT activity shifted thalamic activity towards tonic firing (Fig. 5D). However, VPL fr was higher under conditions where L6CT was stimulated for larger proportions of the stimulation period (stimulation duty cycle) (Fig. 5B). This suggests that VPL throughput may correlate more with changes in total L6CT activity, but that VPL burst probability depends on the rhythmicity or synchrony of L6CT spiking, potentially representing a distinct channel of neural information transfer (Payeur et al. 2021; Mease et al. 2017).

Our observation of a predominantly spike-driving effect of L6CT on VPL activity diverges with those of Dimwamwa and colleagues, who demonstrated in awake mice that S1 L6CT activation induces bidirectional modulation of fr in the ventral posteromedial nucleus (VPm), with the direction (suppression vs enhancement) determined by L6CT firing rate and synchrony (Dimwamwa et al. 2024). In contrast we did not observe significant thalamic suppression with our L6CT manipulations (Fig. 5B). This apparent discrepancy could possibly be explained by differences in the laser intensities and light scattering between studies, and therefore differences in L6CT recruitment (Wang et al. 2018), and/or differences in thalamic state between anesthetized and awake mice (Huh and Cho 2013; Lewis et al. 2015).

Cortical layer 6 is composed of several distinct neuronal subpopulations, with distinct dendritic morphologies, axonal projection patterns and diverse functional impacts on postsynaptic circuits (Thomson 2010; Crandall et al. 2017; Hoerder-Suabedissen et al. 2018; Frandolig et al. 2019; Whilden et al. 2021; Breuer and Krieger 2022; Kim et al. 2025; Messore et al. 2025). Although the Ntsr1-cre line labels a genetically-defined subpopulation within L6, a potential point of complexity is that this L6CT population still contains cells with different cortical and subcortical circuitry that are still under active investigation, and will likely exert differential effects on their postsynaptic targets (Briggs 2010; Olsen et al. 2012; Kim et al. 2014, 2025; Hoerder-Suabedissen et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2018; Frandolig et al. 2019; Whilden et al. 2021; Martinetti et al. 2024). It is therefore conceivable that some of the effects we describe here are the results of differential recruitment of Ntsr1 subclasses during different stimulation conditions. Indeed, the different stimulation conditions elicited heterogenous recruitment of L6CT units. However, we observed no relationship between the degree of heterogeneity in L6CT and the differences in median activity in either L4 and L5 populations, suggesting that L4 and L5 population activity across the stimulation conditions was more heavily influenced by differences in L6CT population synchrony and population activity rather than by differences in the heterogeneity of L6CT unit recruitment (Fig. S1B).

The direction of L6CT feedback on VPL modulation has previously been demonstrated to vary with respect to connectivity motifs such as the balance between excitation and inhibition between individual L6CT-relay-neuron pairs (Wang et al. 2006, 2018). The range of modulation exerted by individual L6CT connectivity motifs may be obscured under the broad optogenetic stimulation of L6CT. For example, L6CT motifs that have antagonistic effects on individual VPL neurons would be more likely to be simultaneously excited by optogenetic L6CT stimulation, whereas under natural conditions these circuits may exhibit more independence. As such, the observations described here might reflect a net modulation of VPL resulting from mass activation of various types of L6CT-VPL connectivity motif.

Moreover, urethane is reported to suppress spiking activity in cortical pyramidal neurons (Sceniak and Maciver 2006; Clement et al. 2008; Potez and Larkum 2008), suggesting that the frequency range of L6CT to transition from a suppressor to a driver of L4 and L5 may be higher during anesthesia compared to the awake state. To safeguard against changes in oscillatory behavior from urethane anesthesia that are reported to occur over ~ 5 minute timescales (Clement et al. 2008), we cycled through all L6CT stimulation conditions within each minute. Nevertheless, both cortical and thalamic activity may be more homogeneous under anesthesia compared to heterogeneous awake states. Thus, the range of modulation naturally arising through L6CT in awake mice in different contexts may vary more than observed here.

Taken together, this research suggests that L6CT can dynamically “tune up” or “tune down” signaling in S1 and can independently shape cortical synchrony, whilst altering incoming information from the thalamus via bidirectional control of thalamic firing mode.
