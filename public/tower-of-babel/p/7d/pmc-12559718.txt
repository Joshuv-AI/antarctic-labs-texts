Stage-specific roles of clonally expanded CD8+ T cells in regulating amyloid pathology in Alzheimer’s disease models
Authors: Masaki Ohyagi, Minako Ito, Mana Iizuka-Koga, Setsuko Mise-Omata, Akihiko Yoshimura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Clonally expanded CD8+ T cells may contribute to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology through interactions with brain-resident cells. However, the functional impact of AD-specific T cell receptor (TCR) clonotypes remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that CD8+ T cells undergo clonal expansion in early-stage AD mouse models, AppNL-G-F and 5xFAD, and that their depletion reduces amyloid plaque accumulation. Expanded TCR-expressing CD8+ T cells preferentially infiltrate the brain, exacerbating plaque deposition. Moreover, brain-infiltrating CD8+ T cells impair microglial transition into disease-associated states, suppressing amyloid clearance via CCL5-CCR5 signaling. Pharmacological blockade of CCL5 attenuates amyloid deposition, whereas CCL5 administration aggravates pathology. Notably, T cell depletion at later disease stages exacerbates amyloid pathology, suggesting a temporal shift in their function. Early-stage CD8+ T cells exhibit cytotoxic and effector profiles, whereas late-stage cells acquire tissue-resident and exhausted phenotypes. This temporal switch—from pathogenic to protective roles—highlights the stage-specific contribution of CD8+ T cells to AD and their potential as therapeutic targets.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most common form of dementia, is pathologically characterized by extracellular deposition of 40–42 amino acid amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques, intracellular neurofibrillary tau tangles, neuroinflammation, and neuronal loss. The deposition of Aβ is the crucial early momentum for AD progression, as evidenced by the disease-modifying effects of anti-amyloid antibodies in patients during the early stages of the disease1,2. Neuroinflammation and immune dysfunction are increasingly recognized as pivotal factors in AD3,4. Microglia, the innate immune cells in the brain, engage in multifaceted interactions with Aβ plaques and exhibit diverse influences on the AD progression4,5. Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technologies have identified microglia transition from a homeostatic phenotype to a reactive state, termed disease-associated microglia (DAM)6 or microglial neurodegenerative phenotype (MGnD)7, with conserved transcriptional signatures across mouse models of AD. DAM was localized near Aβ plaques and participated in the Aβ plaques clearance6,8–10, suggesting a protective role against amyloid pathology.

Adaptive immune cells also face phenotypic alterations in AD patients and AD mouse models11–16. CD8+ T cells display amplified clonal expansion and heightened expression of activation and cytotoxicity genes in the brains of AD patients and mouse models12,15,16, supporting their deleterious effects on AD pathology. However, some studies have described brain-infiltrating CD8+ T cells as protectors of AD progression17,18. Although the role of clonally expanded CD8+ T cells in AD pathology remains unclear, predictions based on curated databases of epitopes recognized by T cell receptor (TCR) sequences suggest that TCR clones associated with AD pathology may recognize antigens derived from Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)12, lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus, or herpes viruses17. Similar to the established strong involvement of EBV in the pathogenesis of multiple sclerosis (MS)19, it has been hypothesized that virus-specific CD8+ T cells may directly respond to EBV or cross-react with brain self-antigens, thereby promoting neuroinflammation and accelerating AD progression. However, whether AD-specific TCRs directly influence AD pathology remains unexplored.

Using AppNL-G-F mice, a model carrying Swedish, Beyreuther/Iberian, and Arctic mutation in the App gene20, we demonstrated that CD8+ T-cell depletion ameliorates Aβ plaque accumulation during the early phase of AD pathology. Unbiased scRNA-seq analysis of AppNL-G-F mouse brain at an early stage revealed that CD8+ T cells exhibit clonal expansion and functional alterations. We reconstituted clonally expanded AppNL-G-F-associated TCRs into primary CD8+ T cells. Peripheral administration of these TCR-reconstituted T cells resulted in their preferential infiltration into the brain, where they exacerbated amyloid pathology. Mechanistically, CD8+ T cells suppressed DAM signature gene expression, thereby impairing amyloid plaque clearance. Ligand-receptor communication analysis reveals the CCL5-CCR5 axis as a key mediator of interactions between CD8+ T cells and microglia. Blocking CCL5 in AppNL-G-F mouse brains reduced amyloid deposition, whereas recombinant CCL5 administration into the brains of T cell-deficient AppNL-G-F mice exacerbated amyloid pathology. By directly targeting the chemotactic axes and CD8+ T cell infiltration based on disease progression, our results provide a potential therapeutic approach for early-stage AD and describe the involvement of adaptive immunity in AD pathogenesis and progression.

Results

CD8+ T cell infiltration aggravates early but not late Aβ pathology

To investigate the impact of T cells on Aβ plaque accumulation, AppNL-G-F mice were crossed with T cell-deficient Cd3e knock-out mice (AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/–). AppNL-G-F mice exhibit progressive Aβ pathology, with sparse amyloid plaques appearing at 2–3 months of age20. We assessed Aβ plaque deposition by immunohistochemistry in the cortex and hippocampus, two brain regions critically affected in AppNL-G-F mice20. Immunostaining with the monoclonal anti-Aβ antibody 82E1, which recognized the N-terminus of Aβ but not full-length APP, revealed age-dependent amyloid plaque accumulation in AppNL-G-F mice. At 9 and 12 months of age, T cell-deficient AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice showed significantly higher amyloid plaque burden (percentage area covered by Aβ immunoreactivity) than T cell-competent AppNL-G-F mice (Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Fig. 1a), consistent with the recent study17. However, at 3 months of age, an early stage of Aβ plaque deposition, amyloid plaque load was approximately 50% lower in AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice (mean values: 3.22% in AppNL-G-F vs. 1.52% in AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/–) (Fig. 1a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 1a). Similar results were obtained using the monoclonal anti-Aβ antibody, 6E10, which reacts to residues 1–16 of Aβ (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed significant infiltration of CD3+ T cells into the brains of AppNL-G-F mice as early as 3 months of age compared to wild-type (WT) controls (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1d). Brain CD3+ T cell increased with age in both AppNL-G-F and WT mice, with pronounced accumulation in the brains exhibiting amyloid pathology (Fig. 1e). Flow cytometry analysis showed an age-dependent increase in the frequency and number of brain-infiltrating CD8+ T cells in AppNL-G-F mice, correlating with amyloid plaque deposition (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 1f). Conversely, the frequency of CD4+ T cells among CD45+CD11b⁻ cells decreased with age, while CD8+ T cells increased. This shift in relative proportions may reflect the preferential accumulation of CD8+ T cells in the brain. (Fig. 1f). At 3 months of age, the frequency and absolute number of CD8+ T cells, but not CD4+ T cells, were significantly higher in AppNL-G-F brains compared to WT controls, coinciding with the observed reduction in amyloid plaque load in AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 1g).

To determine which T cell population influences early amyloid pathology, we administered antibodies against CD4 or CD8α intraperitoneally to deplete these cell populations in AppNL-G-F mice. While both antibody treatments effectively reduced their respective target cell populations in the brain and cervical lymph nodes (cLN) (Supplementary Fig. 2a), only CD8α-neutralizing antibody attenuated Aβ plaque deposition (Fig. 1h), consistent with the observations in T-cell-deficient AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice. To validate these findings, we used AppNL-G-F; Cd8acre/Rosa26DTA mice, in which Cd8a-expressing cells are ablated by diphtheria toxin fragment A (DTA) (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Compared to control mice (AppNL-G-F; Cd8awt/Rosa26DTA), CD8+ T cell ablation significantly reduced Aβ plaque deposition at 3 months of age (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

The progressive role of CD8+ T cells was further examined using the 5xFAD mouse model, which expresses five familial AD-associated mutations and exhibits progressive Aβ pathology beginning around 4 months of age. CD8+ T cells, but not CD4+ T cells, accumulated in 4-month-old 5xFAD mouse brains (Supplementary Fig. 2d). T-cell depletion in 5xFAD mice (5xFAD; Cd3e–/–) significantly reduced amyloid plaque burden during the early phase of pathology but led to increased plaque accumulation at later stages (Supplementary Fig. 2e), consistent with pathological findings observed in the AppNL-G-F mouse model. These results suggest that CD8+ T cells exert pathogenic effects during the early phase of amyloid pathology, but acquire a regulatory role as the disease progresses, a pattern observed across multiple AD mouse models.

To validate the role of CD8+ T cells ex vivo, we utilized organotypic brain slice cultures (BSCs). Replenishing BSCs from AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice with CD8+ T cells isolated from AppNL-G-F mouse brains, but not brain CD4+ T cells or splenic CD8+ T cells from AppNL-G-F mice, worsen Aβ deposition (Supplementary Fig. 2f). These findings collectively demonstrate that CD8+ T cell infiltration into the brains of AppNL-G-F mice correlates with the progression of amyloid pathology and exacerbates early-stage disease progression.

The observed effects on Aβ pathology were not linked to altered APP expression or amyloidogenic processing, as revealed by western blot and quantitative RT-PCR analyses (Supplementary Fig. 2g, h). These suggest that reduced Aβ load and levels was unlikely to be mediated by altered Aβ production.

Finally, we examined the spatial distribution of CD8+ T cells in the brain. CD8+ T cells in AppNL-G-F brains were predominantly located along the meninges and choroid plexus, which serve as immunological niches harboring various peripheral immune cells. Notably, these cells were absent from the vasculature (Fig. 1i), as brain tissues were analyzed after transcardial perfusion with PBS to remove intravascular cells. Intravenous injection of phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated anti-CD45 antibody confirmed the efficient elimination of peripheral CD8+ T cells from the brain (Supplementary Fig. 2i). Interestingly, CD8+ T cells in AppNL-G-F mice were often observed in close proximity to Aβ plaques (Fig. 1i).

CD8+ T cell heterogeneity in early versus late Aβ pathology

We characterized brain immune cells at single-cell resolution using scRNA-seq analysis on CD45intCD11b+ cells (primarily microglia) and CD45+CD11b– cells (non-microglial immune cells) isolated from the brain parenchyma of AppNL-G-F, AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/–, and WT mice at 3 months of age. Cells from three mice per group were pooled for analysis. Unsupervised Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and clustering identified 10 distinct immune cell clusters (Fig. 2a). CD8+ T cells were defined by Cd3e and Cd8a expression, as unsupervised clustering did not clearly separate the CD8+ T cell population between WT and AppNL-G-F mice. Differential gene expression analysis revealed upregulation of Pdcd1 (PD-1), Itga4 (CD49d), and Cx3cr1, with significant downregulation of Ifng (IFN-γ) and Cd69 in CD8+ T cells from AppNL-G-F mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 2b). Protein-level validation by flow cytometry corroborated these findings, showing increased PD-1 and reduced CD69 expression in brain CD8+ T cells from 3-month-old AppNL-G-F mice relative to WT mice (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 3a). Brain CD8+ T cells from AppNL-G-F mice also exhibited higher PD-1 expression compared to cLN CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 3a). To further distinguish brain-resident from circulating CD8+ T cells, we intravenously injected PE-conjugated anti-CD45 antibodies and harvested the brains after 3 min with PBS perfusion. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that brain-resident (PE−) CD8+ T cells from AppNL-G-F mice showed higher PD-1 expression than vascular-associated (PE+) CD8+ T cells, as well as brain-resident CD8+ T cells from control mice. These findings suggest that elevated PD-1 expression is a distinguishing feature of brain-infiltrating CD8+ T cells in AppNL-G-F mice (Supplementary Fig. 3b). PD-1 expression in brain CD8+ T cells increased with age and amyloid plaque deposition (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3c), consistent with previous reports showing that PD-1 upregulation in clonally expanded brain CD8+ T cells and PD-1+ CD8+ T cells with regulatory CD8+ T cell signatures in the aged mouse brain with amyloid pathology17. In addition to elevated PD-1 expression, CD8+ T cells from AppNL-G-F brains at 3 months of age demonstrated a strong skewing toward an effector phenotype with elevated frequencies of CX3CR1, CD44, and KLRG1 (Fig. 2e), markers for antigen-experienced effector T cells21–24. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed TCR signaling- and T cell differentiation-associated signatures enriched in CD8+ T cells from AppNL-G-F mice, while type II interferon- and cytotoxicity-associated signatures were diminished (Supplementary Fig. 3d). Consistent with these findings, IFNγ and Granzyme A, but not TNFα and Granzyme B, were reduced in ex vivo-stimulated brain CD8+ T cells from AppNL-G-F mice compared to WT controls (Supplementary Fig. 3e). To further characterize brain CD8+ T cells in AppNL-G-F mice, we reclustered CD8+ T cells from AppNL-G-F and WT mice, identifying two transcriptionally distinct subclusters: CD8+ T cell subcluster 1 (CD8+T1) and subcluster 2 (CD8+T2) (Fig. 2f). The CD8+T1 exhibited high expression of genes associated with translation activity, cytoskeleton dynamics (e.g. Actb, Pfn1, Myl6, Ubb, Arpc1b, and Tubb5), and effector functions (e.g. Tmsb4x, Nkg7, and Ccl5), supporting the possibility that these cells undergo proliferation and develop effector functions25 (Supplementary Fig. 4a). CD8+T2 expressed elevated levels of cell adhesion genes, such as Itga4, Itga1 (CD49a), Itgal, and Itgb2 (LFA-1), as well as Cd69, which are indicative of tissue-resident T cells26,27 (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Pseudotime analysis using Monocle 3 suggested a differentiation trajectory from CD8+T1 to CD8+T2, characterized by downregulation of effector function markers and upregulation of tissue-residence markers (Fig. 2g). Dynamic expression analysis confirmed the pseudotime-dependent regulation of genes such as Ifng, Cd69, Itga1, Itgal, and Itga4 (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Effector-related genes, particularly Ccl5, were predominantly expressed in AppNL-G-F CD8+ T cells, whereas tissue-residence markers were more prominent in WT-derived CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2h). Quantitative analysis also revealed a trend toward an increased proportion of CD8+T1 in AppNL-G-F mice and CD8+T2 in WT mice, although these differences did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2i). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that CD8+ T cells from AppNL-G-F mice exhibited higher expression of Ki-67, a proliferation marker, and lower co-expression of both CD69 and CD103, tissue-resident T cell markers, compared to WT controls (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). The altered proportions of CD8+T1 and CD8+T2 in the brains of AppNL-G-F mice reflect a shift favoring proliferation and effector functions over tissue residency. This shift appears to be driven by cumulative TCR stimulation and inflammatory signals, even during the early stages of amyloidosis. Notably, while some CD8+ T cells exhibited signs of exhaustion, including PD-1 expression and reduced IFNγ production, a distinct subset remained chronically activated, retaining effector functions that could potentially modulate the progression of amyloid pathology.

CXCR6+ PD-1+ CD8+ T cells, which skew toward tissue-residency, have been reported to limit Aβ aggregation through interactions with CXCL16 produced by microglia in 5xFAD mouse models aged 8–10 months17,28. We observed upregulated expression of Cxcr6 in CD8+T2 along with other tissue-resident T cell marker genes (Fig. 2h). Flow cytometry analysis revealed reduced CXCR6 expression levels on brain CD8+ T cells in AppNL-G-F mice compared to WT controls, despite no significant differences in the proportion of CXCR6+ CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 4e). These results suggest that brain CD8+ T cells at the early stages of amyloid pathology differ from those in aged mice with AD pathology, where most brain CD8+ T cells exhibit tissue-resident memory T cell gene signatures. To further explore the involvement of the CXCR6-CXCL16 axis in early-stage amyloid pathology, we analyzed amyloid plaque deposition in AppNL-G-F mouse brains following intracerebroventricular administration of anti-CXCL16 antibody. However, there were no significant changes in amyloid plaque accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 4f), suggesting that the CXCR6-CXCL16 axis plays a limited role in early AD pathology.

T cell deficiency in AppNL-G-F mice led to reduced amyloid pathology at 3 months of age but resulted in exacerbated pathology at 9 months and beyond (Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Fig. 1a–c). These findings suggest that the impact of CD8+ T cells on amyloid pathology changes over the course of disease progression, potentially reflecting a dynamic shift in their functional state. To investigate this further, we analyzed the transcriptional profiles of brain CD8+ T cells at different disease stages. In addition to our previously generated scRNA-seq datasets of brain immune cells from 3-month-old WT and AppNL-G-F mice, we incorporated two datasets representing the progression phase of amyloid pathology: a publicly available dataset from 8-month-old 5xFAD mice (GSE207702)17 and a newly generated dataset from 8-month-old AppNL-G-F mice (GSE303309) (Fig. 2j). Differential gene expression analysis of brain CD8+ T cells revealed that cells from 3-month-old AppNL-G-F mice exhibited increased expression of genes such as Notch2, Cx3cr1, Itga4, S100a8, and S100a9, suggesting that CD8+ T cells are activated and increasingly recruited into the brain during early-stage pathology (Fig. 2k). GO enrichment analysis further identified pathways enriched in these early-stage CD8+ T cells, including “serine/threonine kinase activity” and “cell adhesion molecule binding”, both of which are associated with TCR signaling and immune cell activation (Supplementary Fig. 4g). In contrast, CD8+ T cells from 8-month-old 5xFAD mice showed elevated expression of exhaustion-related markers such as Icos, Nr4a1/2/3, and Socs3. Similarly, CD8+ T cells from 8-month-old AppNL-G-F mice exhibited increased expression of Tox and Id2, along with reduced expression of activation-related genes including Ifng, Cx3cr1, and S100a8/9 (Fig. 2k). These findings indicate a shift toward a functionally exhausted phenotype in late-stage CD8+ T cells. Notably, the transcriptional profiles differed between AppNL-G-F and 5xFAD mice at 8 months, which may reflect differences in sample processing or model-specific immune responses. Flow cytometric analysis comparing 3-month-old WT and AppNL-G-F and 8-month-old AppNL-G-F mice corroborated these findings. PD-1 expression progressively increased with age (Fig. 2d), and tissue-resident markers, such as CD69 and CXCR6 were downregulated at 3 months but upregulated at 8 months in AppNL-G-F mice. These results indicate that, with disease progression, CD8+ T cells undergo a phenotypic shift from an antigen-experienced, cytotoxic state toward a tissue-resident, functionally exhausted state. Previous studies have suggested that tissue-resident CD8+ T cells in the late stage may exert suppressive effects on amyloid pathology17. Taken together with our current findings, we propose that the stage-dependent functional reprogramming of CD8+ T cells—from pro-inflammatory and pathogenic in the early phase to immunoregulatory and potentially protective in the late phase—underlies their contrasting impact on amyloid pathology across disease progression.

Clonal CD8+ T cell responses worsen early Aβ pathology

T cells express a TCR on their surface, which detects specific antigens and stimulates the T cell to initiate an immune response. Upon exposure to a specific antigen recognized by the TCR, T cells become activated and undergo clonal expansion. To investigate antigen-specific T-cell clonality, we performed single-cell TCR sequencing (scTCR-seq) analysis to identify clonally expanded T-cell populations in the brains of three-month-old AppNL-G-F mice. This analysis demonstrated a reduced clonal diversity of brain CD8+ T cells in the brain of AppNL-G-F mice compared to that observed in WT controls (Fig. 3a). Consistently, rarefaction analysis based on the Shannon index revealed a lower diversity in AppNL-G-F mice (5.04) than in WT controls (5.18), further supporting the reduction of TCR repertoire diversity in the model. TCR clones can typically be classified into five types: hyperexpanded, large, medium, small, and rare clone types. While hyperexpanded- and rare-type clones were absent in the brains of both AppNL-G-F and WT mice, CD8+ T cells in AppNL-G-F mouse brain represented large-type clone types (Supplementary Fig. 5a). From these, we selected two TCRs—AppTCR#1 and AppTCR#2—which corresponded to the most highly expanded clonotypes among brain-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells in AppNL-G-F mice (Fig. 3a). These TCRs were then transduced into a TCR-negative mouse T cell hybridoma, TG40 cell line29 (Supplementary Fig. 5b). As a control, we randomly cloned TCR (Vβ5.1) from WT mice. We generated bone marrow dendritic cells (BMDCs) from WT mice and then pulsed them with brain tissue homogenates from AppNL-G-F or WT mice overnight. BMDCs loaded with each brain homogenate were exposed to TG40 cells expressing AppTCR#1, AppTCR#2, and control TCR. After 2 days co-culture, IL-2 levels produced by TCR-transfected TG40 cells expressing AppTCR#1 and AppTCR#2 were increased when exposed to AppNL-G-F brain homogenate, compared to when exposed to WT brain homogenate (Fig. 3b). To further investigate antigen-specific response and pathogenicity of brain CD8+ T cells in the development of Aβ pathology in vivo, we transduced green fluorescent protein (GFP)-encoded AppTCR#1 and control TCR into primary CD8+ T cells isolated from WT mice by retroviral transduction, and adoptively transferred these cells into AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice. Intriguingly, adoptively transferred GFP+ CD8+ T cells exhibit a significantly higher recruitment frequency into the brain compared to cLN, with a notable subset of these cells infiltrating in close proximity to amyloid plaques (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, immunohistochemistry analysis demonstrated that adoptive transfer of AppTCR#1-expressing CD8+ T cells resulted in greater Aβ plaque accumulation than control TCR-expressing CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3d). To validate that CD8+ T cell impact on amyloid pathology dependent on antigen specificity, we utilized AppNL-G-F; OT-I mice generated from a cross between AppNL-G-F mouse and OT-I mouse which contains transgenic TCR designed to recognize ovalbumin peptide residues 257–264 (OVA257–264) in the context of MHC I molecule. Intracerebroventricular injection of OVA257–264 peptide, intended to activate CD8+ T cells specifically recognizing OVA257–264 peptide in the brain, increased CD8+ T cells in the brain and cLN, but not in the spleen (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 5c). OVA257–264-specific TCR stimulation of brain CD8+ T cells resulted in a significant increase in Aβ plaque burden and a trend towards an increased number of Aβ plaque (Fig. 3f). These data strongly supported that TCRs of CD8+ cells infiltrating the brain of AppNL-G-F mice recognize a disease-specific brain antigen, thereby facilitating Aβ plaque deposition.

It has been reported that clonally expanded CD8+ T cells in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of the AD patients share the TCR sequence for Herpesviridae Epstein-Barr nuclear antigens (EBNA3)12, and clonally expanded CD8+ T cells from 5xFAD at later stage of amyloid pathology were predicted to recognize insulin-2, nucleoprotein of lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus, and human herpes virus17. We found that M38 protein, encoded by mouse cytomegalovirus 1, as a potential epitope that the most clonally expanded TCR clone may recognize based on a prediction of TCR specificity by TCRmatch analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5d). However, BMDCs pulsed with M38 protein peptide, Aβ42 peptide, and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) 35–55 peptide failed to specifically stimulate hybridomas expressing AppTCR#1 or AppTCR#2 (Supplementary Fig. 5e), suggesting that M38 epitope is not recognized by AppTCR#1 and AppTCR#2. Our findings that clonally expanded TCRs from brain CD8+ T cells in AppNL-G-F mice respond to disease-specific brain antigens and contribute to the enhancement of amyloid pathology, although the specific target antigen remains unidentified. The absence of a clearly defined antigen raises the possibility that these TCRs may recognize post-translationally modified self-antigens or other endogenous antigens present in the AppNL-G-F knock-in mouse brain that are difficult to detect using conventional screening approaches.

Thus, we examined microglial phenotypes in T cell-depleted AppNL-G-F mice, which display reduced amyloid plaque deposition. Flow cytometry analysis showed an increased population of CD45int CD11b+ microglia in amyloid pathology, with a significant increase in AppNL-G-F mice compared to WT controls. To assess T cell impact on microglial phenotypes in the amyloid pathology, we analyzed transcriptomic changes in microglia from AppNL-G-F, AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/–, and WT controls using scRNA-seq data. Microglia with amyloid pathology demonstrated distinct microglia phenotypes relative to WT controls (Supplementary Fig. 6c). After further subclustering of microglia from AppNL-G-F and AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice, we identified eight transcriptionally distinct subclusters of microglia, categorizing microglia into five states, homeostatic microglia (hMG), transitional state microglia (transitional MG), DAM1, DAM2, and interferon-responsive microglia (IFN-MG)8, based on their cluster-specific gene expression profiles6,8,9 (Supplementary Fig. 6d–f). Among these states, hMG and DAM cluster composition differed between AppNL-G-F and AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 6g, h). Interestingly, we observed a loss of homeostatic genes, such as Cx3cr1, P2ry12, and Cst3, and significant induction of DAM marker genes, such as Apoe, Tyrobp, Cd9, Lpl, Cst7, Axl, and Itgax (CD11c), in DAM of AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice compared to those of AppNL-G-F microglia at 3 months of age (Fig. 4d). Immunohistochemistry analysis confirmed upregulated expression of the DAM markers, CLEC7A and CD11c, in microglia from AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice compared with those from AppNL-G-F mice (Fig. 4e). Upregulated chemokines (Ccl3 and Ccl4) and lysosomal genes (Cst7 and Ctsb) were also observed in DAM from AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice (Fig. 4d), aligning with markers Aβ-phagocytic microglia of AD mouse models30,31.

To verify the impact of T cells on microglial Aβ-phagocytosis in vivo, we treated AppNL-G-F and AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice with methoxy-XO4, a fluorescent probe that crosses the blood-brain barrier and binds to fibrillar β-sheets of amyloid30. Methoxy-labeled microglia in AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice, detected by flow cytometry, were slightly but significantly increased compared to those in AppNL-G-F mice at three months of age (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 6i). Flow cytometry analysis also confirmed elevated AXL expression, a DAM marker gene and regulator of microglial phagocytosis process5, in microglia of AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice compared with those of AppNL-G-F mice (Fig. 4g), with similar results in the 5xFAD model (Supplementary Fig. 6j). To further investigate the impact of T cell deficiency on microglia, we conducted a morphological analysis of microglia. Compared to AppNL-G-F mice, microglia from AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice at 3 months of age exhibited a reduced ramification index, indicative of an ameboid-like morphology characteristic of activated microglia (Supplementary Fig. 6k). In contrast, 9 months of age, no significant differences in microglial morphology were observed between the two genotypes, despite the increased amyloid plaque burden in AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice (Supplementary Fig. 6l). These results suggest that T cell deficiency induces morphological activation of microglia in the early stage of amyloid pathology, whereas at the later stage, microglial morphology becomes comparable regardless of T cell presence. Collectively, these data imply that the presence of T cells in the brain may modulate microglial phenotypes in a stage-dependent manner—attenuating early microglial activation and DAM transition, while supporting microglial homeostasis and function in advanced disease.

Ligand-receptor pairs from CD8+ T cells to microglia identified upregulated communications between Ccl5 (ligand in CD8+ T cells) and Ccr5 (receptor in microglia) in AppNL-G-F mice, while interactions between Ifng (ligand in CD8+ T cells) and Ifngr1 and Ifngr2 (receptors in microglia) were downregulated (Fig. 5c). CD8+T1 showed upregulated Ccl5 expression compared to CD8+T2, and Ccr5 expression was elevated in hMG compared to DAM (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 7c). CCL5 protein levels in the brain cortex of AppNL-G-F mice were upregulated compared to those in T cell-deficient AppNL-G-F mice and tended to be elevated compared to those in WT mice (Fig. 5d). Intracellular cytokine staining following ex vivo stimulation revealed a significantly higher number and frequency of CCL5-producing CD8+ T cells in the brains of AppNL-G-F mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 7d). Notably, there was a trend toward a higher frequency of CCL5-producing CD8+ T cells in 3-month-old AppNL-G-F mice relative to their 8-month-old counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 7d) Consistently, integrated analysis of scRNA-seq datasets showed that CD8+ T cells from 3-month-old AppNL-G-F mice exhibited a broader range and higher expression of Ccl5, including a distinct population of high-expressing cells, compared with those from 8-month-old AppNL-G-F and 5xFAD mice (Supplementary Fig. 7e). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed upregulation of microglial CCR5 expression in response to amyloid pathology (Supplementary Fig. 7f), consistent with the previous findings33. Immunohistochemistry analysis revealed that CCL5+ CD8+ T cells were located in close proximity to IBA1+ microglia in the brains of AppNL-G-F mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 5f). To investigate the impact of CCL5 on early-stage amyloid pathology in vivo, we intracerebroventricularly injected CCL5 into AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice at 6–7 weeks of age, resulting in increased Aβ plaque deposition after 6–7 weeks (Fig. 5g). Conversely, anti-CCL5 antibody into AppNL-G-F mouse brains at 6 and 10 weeks of age reduced Aβ plaque deposition, with a trend towards increased CD11c+ plaque-associated microglia 4 weeks after the final injection (Fig. 5h, i). These in vivo results indicate that microglia express high levels of CCR5 in response to amyloid pathology, and its ligand CCL5, produced by brain CD8+ T cells, suppresses microglial transition to disease-associated states. This suppression leads to increased Aβ plaque deposition during the early stages of amyloid pathology.

We treated freshly isolated ex vivo microglia from WT mouse brains with CCL5 after lipopolysaccharide stimulation, which slightly but significantly reduced fluorescence-labeled Aβ uptake (Fig. 5j). We also investigated whether CD8+ T cells produce CCL5 in a TCR-repertoire-dependent manner. CD8+ T cells expressing AppTCR#1 or a control TCR were co-cultured with BMDCs pulsed with brain lysates from AppNL-G-F mice or WT mice. CD8+ T cells expressing AppTCR#1 upregulated Ccl5 expression when co-cultured with AppNL-G-F brain lysates-pulsed dendritic cells, but not with WT brain lysates-pulsed dendritic cells, compared to those expressing the control TCR (Fig. 5k). Furthermore, TCR stimulation of brain CD8+ T cells in AppNL-G-F; OT-I mice through intracerebroventricular OVA257–264 injection significantly reduced CD11c+ plaque-associated microglia (Fig. 5l and Supplementary Fig. 7g). These findings suggest that brain CD8+ T cells from AppNL-G-F mice produce CCL5 in a TCR-specific manner associated with amyloid pathology and modulate microglial phagocytosis of Aβ via the CCL5-CCR5 axis.

Discussion

Recent studies have highlighted a key role for adaptive immune cells in the pathogenesis of various neurodegenerative diseases, including AD, Parkinson’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis34,35. In this study, we demonstrate that CD8+ T cells exhibit disease stage-dependent transcriptional and functional changes that modulate amyloid pathology. Notably, even during the early stages of amyloid pathology, brain CD8+ T cells in AD mouse models undergo clonal expansion and facilitate Aβ aggregation. This occurs through the suppression of DAM signatures and the attenuation of microglial phagocytosis of Aβ plaques. Using scTCR-seq of brain-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, we identified a restricted TCR repertoire associated with early amyloid pathology. Engineering CD8+ T cells to express TCRs characteristic of early-stage amyloid pathology revealed a selective response to brain homogenates from mice with amyloid pathology. Upon transfer into AppNL-G-F mice lacking T cells, these engineered T cells preferentially infiltrated the brain and exacerbated Aβ plaque deposition. These findings suggest that, even at the early stages of amyloid pathology, brain-infiltrating CD8+ T cells recognize specific antigens within the brain and contribute to amyloid plaque accumulation through antigen-specific activation.

A curated database of TCR sequences with known antigen specificities predicted that the TCR sequence associated with early amyloid pathology might target the M38 protein, expressed by cytomegalovirus (CMV), as a candidate antigen. Similarly, EBV-specific TCR clones have been identified in CD8+ T cells within the CSF of patients with AD12. These findings are consistent with the growing recognition of pathogens as risk factors for age-related neurodegenerative diseases36,37. For instance, cumulative exposure to CMV has been linked to an increased risk of AD and an accelerated rate of cognitive decline38. Although the precise mechanisms by which viral infections contribute to dementia initiation remain unclear, viral infections themselves can induce systemic and brain inflammatory cascades, potentially disrupting immune cell functions and impairing cognitive performance. While we do not directly demonstrate a response of clonally expanded TCRs from brain CD8+ T cells in early amyloid pathology to CMV antigens, our findings, together with prior evidence, suggest that brain CD8+ T cells may recognize or cross-react with viral antigens, potentially infiltrating into the brain, triggering neuroinflammation and exacerbating amyloid plaque accumulation. In this study, we utilized the AppNL-G-F knock-in mouse model, which maintains physiological levels of APP expression and processing, thereby closely mimicking endogenous conditions. This contrasts with transgenic models such as 5xFAD, which are associated with ectopic overexpression and artificial splicing events that may lead to the generation of non-physiological or neo-antigenic peptide fragments. While it remains possible that the clonally expanded TCRs from brain CD8⁺ T cells in AppNL-G-F mice recognize endogenous brain antigens, the use of a physiologically relevant model in our study makes it unlikely that such responses are directed against artificial or ectopic peptides. The inability to identify a specific antigen despite robust T cell activation suggests that these TCRs may recognize post-translationally modified self-peptides or other disease-associated antigens that are challenging to detect using conventional screening approaches. These findings highlight the need for improved antigen discovery strategies that account for post-translational modifications and other context-dependent peptide alterations in the diseased brain.

The presence of CD8+ T cells in AD brains and mouse models has been documented, yet their role—protective or pathogenic—remains unclear. Recent studies largely support the hypothesis that brain-infiltrating CD8+ T cells with upregulated cytotoxic effector genes contribute to disease progression12,15. Alternatively, some findings propose that CD8+ T cells may limit AD pathogenesis, highlighting CXCL16-CXCR6 interactions between microglia and CD8+ T cells as drivers of CD8+ T cell clonal expansion and enhanced tissue residency in 5xFAD mice17. PD-1 upregulation has also been linked to clonally expanded CD8+ T cells in the brain17. In tauopathy models, another pathological feature of AD, clonally expanded granzyme K+ PD-1+ CD8+ T cells have been shown to modulate microglial function and exert protective effects18. While these insights are based on aged AD mouse models from 8 months of age onward, our study focuses on early amyloid pathology. In this context, we found no evidence of elevated CXCR6 expression on CD8+ T cells, nor did blocking CXCL16 in the brain affect amyloid plaque accumulation at this early stage. In the brains of 3-month-old mice, we identified two CD8+ T cell clusters: one with upregulated effector function genes, predominantly observed in AppNL-G-F mice, and another marked by elevated tissue-residency genes, more abundant in WT mice. CXCR6 expression was enriched in the tissue-resident cluster, consistent with its reported role in regulating the localization of tissue-resident CD8+ T cells across peripheral tissues39. However, most brain CD8+ T cells during early amyloid pathology exhibited elevated effector function gene expression rather than tissue residency markers, suggesting a dynamic phenotypic shift in CD8+ T cells over the course of amyloid pathology. In the early stages, CD8+ T cells predominantly acquire cytotoxic effector functions as amyloid plaques begin to emerge. As pathology advances, tissue-residency features, marked by CXCR6 expression, become dominant and may exert regulatory functions. Additionally, we observed age-associated PD-1 upregulation correlating with amyloid plaque accumulation. PD-1 is initially upregulated on CD8+ T cells during activation in response to their cognate antigens40,41. Under chronic stimulation, such as in the brain during progressive amyloid pathology, sustained high PD-1 expression on CD8+ T cells leads to impaired proliferation and cytokine production despite continued antigen stimulation41. These indicate that PD-1, a key molecule associated with clonally expanded CD8+ T cells in amyloid pathology17, may reflect a phenotypic transition in brain CD8+ T cells—from cytotoxic effector activity in early pathology to tissue-resident regulatory functions in later stages.

Furthermore, our findings indicate that the interaction between CD8+ T cells and microglia in Alzheimer’s disease is also dynamic and stage-dependent. In the early phase of amyloid pathology at 3 months, genetic ablation of T cells in AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice led to a significant reduction in amyloid plaque burden, accompanied by an increase in ameboid-like morphology, as well as upregulation of DAM-associated genes. These results indicate that, in the presence of CD8+ T cells, microglial activation and DAM transition may be actively constrained during early disease stages. In contrast, at 8–9 months, AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice exhibited increased amyloid plaque accumulation despite no overt differences in microglial morphology compared to controls, suggesting that morphological activation is uncoupled from functional impairment at this stage. This temporal dissociation supports the hypothesis that CD8+ T cells play distinct roles over the course of disease progression—suppressing early microglial activation, but later supporting homeostatic or protective microglial functions, such as sustained Aβ phagocytosis. The loss of CD8+ T cells in the later stage may compromise these regulatory functions, resulting in impaired microglial clearance and increased plaque burden. These findings are consistent with recent reports showing temporal shifts in brain-resident T cell subsets—from effector to tissue-resident memory phenotypes—and underscore the importance of considering both disease stage and T cell heterogeneity when evaluating neuroimmune interactions in Alzheimer’s disease.

In early amyloid pathology, an unbiased cell-cell interaction analysis based on the scRNA-seq data identified upregulation of the CCL5-CCR5 axis, mediated by CCL5 from CD8+ T cells and CCR5 on homeostatic microglia. CCL5 is elevated in CD8+ T cells within the CSF of AD patients12 and the plasma of relatively early-stage AD patients42. Its cognate receptor CCR5 is significantly upregulated in microglia in AD patients43, and protein-level network analysis identified CCR5 as a major hub gene in AD44, suggesting a crucial involvement of this chemokine pathway in AD pathogenesis. In AppNL-G-F mice with early-stage amyloid pathology, we observed increased CCL5 expression, and CD8+ T cells expressing TCRs specific to early amyloid pathology selectively responded to amyloid pathological brain lysates, resulting in enhanced CCL5 expression. We also demonstrated that CCL5 significantly, though moderately, reduces microglial capacity to phagocytose amyloid plaques. These findings suggest that antigen-specific CD8+ T cells drive early disease progression through the CCL5-CCR5 signaling pathway.

The findings of this study may have important implications for developing therapeutic strategies targeting CD8⁺ T cells in AD. With the recent clinical approval of anti-amyloid therapies and the increased availability of early diagnostic tools such as amyloid PET imaging and fluid biomarkers, the identification of patients at the early stage of AD—when CD8⁺ T cells may exert pathogenic effects—has become more feasible. This raises the possibility of stratifying patients based on immune activation status for precision immunomodulatory interventions. Moreover, advances in T cell–based therapeutic platforms, such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapies, suggest the feasibility of genetically modifying patient-derived CD8⁺ T cells to reduce proinflammatory cytokine production, such as CCL5, or to redirect their function. Alternatively, targeted delivery of nucleic acid–based drugs to modulate CCL5 expression in CD8⁺ T cells could be considered a strategy to mitigate their contribution to early amyloid pathology. These approaches highlight the potential for translating our mechanistic findings into immune-targeted interventions for early-stage AD.

While our findings describe aspects of the role of brain-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells and the CCL5-CCR5 axis in early amyloid pathology, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although this study focused on CD8⁺ T cell–microglia interactions, we did not investigate potential interactions between CD8⁺ T cells and other Alzheimer’s disease–relevant cell types, such as astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, vascular endothelial cells, or neurons. Future studies are warranted to determine whether CD8⁺ T cells directly or indirectly influence these additional cellular compartments. Second, the temporal dynamics and functional impact of CD8⁺ T cells across different stages of Alzheimer’s disease remain unclear. While we focused on the early disease phase, the contribution of CD8⁺ T cells to pathology during later stages requires further investigation. Third, our single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing analyses were conducted on pooled samples, each consisting of brain immune cells isolated from three mice per group. While this approach enabled comprehensive profiling, the lack of biological replicates limits the ability to perform robust statistical validation at the individual level. Finally, although our results implicate the CCL5–CCR5 axis as a mediator of CD8⁺ T cell–microglia communication, further mechanistic studies using genetic or pharmacological manipulation of the CCR5 pathway will be essential to establish a causal link and assess the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway.

In summary, our study provides evidence of antigen-specific clonal expansion of brain CD8+ T cells even at the early stage of amyloid pathology and elucidates their dynamic functional transition during AD progression through interactions with other brain immune cells. CD8+ T cells infiltrating the brain respond to the local environment, undergo clonal expansion, and express TCRs associated with amyloid pathology, promoting Aβ aggregation in an antigen-specific manner. This process involves effector CD8+ T cells modulating microglial reactivity via the CCL5-CCR5 signaling pathway. The dynamic and complex roles of adaptive immune cells in neurodegenerative disease pathogenesis underscore the need for further investigation to fully elucidate their contributions to disease progression. As innovative immunotherapeutic approaches like CAR T-cell therapies gain prominence, our findings highlight the potential for developing disease-modifying strategies by tailoring CD8+ T cell modulation to specific stages of AD. This precision-based approach may inform therapeutic interventions targeting the adaptive immune system in neurodegenerative diseases.

Methods

Mice

The research conducted in this study complies with all relevant ethical guidelines. Animal protocols were approved by and were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Research Committee and Ethics Committee of Keio University School of Medicine and the Tokyo University of Science Animal Care and Use Committee. C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Tokyo Laboratory Animals Science. AppNL-G-F transgenic mice with knock-in of human App mutated transgenes20 were obtained from T.C. Saido (RIKEN Center for Brain Science) and were backcrossed in the C57BL/6 J background for more than ten generations. Cd3e–/–45, 5xFAD46, Cd8a-Cre47, Rosa-DTA48, and OT-I-TCR49 transgenic mice (all under the C57BL/6 J background) have been described previously and obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. All mice were housed and bred under specific pathogen-free animal facilities (temperature: 18–24 °C; humidity: 40–70%) in a day-night controlled light cycle, provided with food and water ad libitum at Keio University School of Medicine and Research Institute for Biomedical Sciences, Tokyo University of Science. Both male and female mice were used under age- and sex-matched conditions. Mice with predetermined genotypes (not blinded to investigators) were randomly assigned to control and experimental groups. Comparable results were obtained in both sexes. For intravenous labeling experiments, anti-CD45-PE was administered to mice by tail vein injection for 3 min before euthanasia and collection of the brain. Transient T-cell depletion was performed by injection intraperitoneally twice weekly from 4 to 12 weeks of age with anti-CD4 (clone GK1.5, 100 μg per mouse, BioXCell) and anti-CD8α (clone 2.43, 400 μg per mouse, BioXCell). For intracerebroventricular injection, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane before being stereotaxically injected with anti-CXCL16 (MAB503, 5 μg per mouse, R&D Systems), anti-CCL5 (clone R6G9, 5 μg per mouse, BioXCell), or recombinant mouse CCL5/RANTES (1 μg per mouse, 478-MR-025, R&D Systems) into the lateral ventricle bilaterally at a dose of 100 μg per mouse. Control mice underwent the same procedure but received vehicle injections.

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical staining

Mouse brains were collected after transcardial perfusion with PBS, and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde before processing and embedding in paraffin blocks. A total of 5-μm thick sections mounted on slides were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval in a citrate solution. Sections were then blocked with Blocking One Histo (06346-64, Nacalai Tesque) and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies and dilutions were as follows: anti-amyloid β (1:200, 82E1, 10323, Immuno-Biological Laboratories), anti-amyloid β (1:200, 6E10, BioLegend, 803004), anti-IBA1 (1:400, 019-01974, Wako), anti-CD3 (1:100, MCA1477, Bio-rad), anti-CD8α (1:400, 98,941, Cell Signaling Technologies), anti-Dectin-1 (CLEC7A, 1:50, mabg-mdect-2, InvivoGen), anti-CD11c (1:400, 97585, Cell Signaling Technologies), and anti-CCL5 (1:200, MAB478, R&D Systems). Sections were then incubated with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies from Invitrogen (1:400, Goat anti-mouse IgG H + L-Alexa Fluor 488; Goat anti-rabbit IgG H + L-Alexa Fluor 546; Donkey anti-mouse IgG H + L-Alexa Fluor 488; Donkey anti-rabbit IgG H + L-Alexa Fluor 546; Donkey anti-goat IgG H + L-Alexa Fluor 647, all from invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature. After three washes with PBS, slides were coverslipped in VECTASHIELD mounting medium with DAPI (H-1200, Vector Laboratories). For Thioflavin-S staining, the sections after staining of fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated with Thioflavin-S (T1892, Sigma-Aldrich) for 8 min at room temperature. The sections were then sequentially washed in 80% ethanol and distilled water twice. All images were acquired with a BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope and accompanying software (Keyence). The mean area and total counts of amyloid burden in the cortex or hippocampus stained by 82E1 or 6E10 were measured using a BZ-X Analyzer software (Keyence). The mean plaque size or circularity of each 82E1+ Aβ plaque was quantified using Fiji software. Spatial analysis was performed using QuPath (version 0.5.1)50 on fluorescence-immunostained brain sections labeled with CCL5, CD8α, and IBA1. Cells were classified as CCL5+ CD8+ T cells or IBA1+ microglia based on mean fluorescence intensity in the respective channels. The shortest distance between each CCL5+ CD8+ T cell and its nearest IBA1+ microglial neighbor was calculated. Microglial morphology was quantitatively analyzed using MotiQ51, an ImageJ-based plugin optimized for high-throughput assessment of IBA1-labeled microglia. Morphological parameters were then extracted using MotiQ, including Ramification Index, which is calculated as the ratio of the squared perimeter to the area of each cell and reflects the complexity of cell processes. A lower Ramification Index corresponds to a more ameboid (activated) morphology.

Single-cell suspensions

Mice were perfused with PBS transcardially, and the forebrain was removed. For isolation of brain T cells, brains were minced and enzymatically dissociated with 2 mg ml−1 collagenase D (11088858001, Roche), and 2 mg ml−1 DNase I (10104159001, Roche) in RPMI-1640 (Nacalai Tesque) for 30 min at 37 °C. The digested tissue was centrifuged and resuspended in 30% Percoll (GE Healthcare), layered on 70% Percoll, and centrifuged at 800 × g for 20 min. Cells were collected from 70 to 30% interface. For isolation of microglia, brains were roughly minced and homogenized with a potter tissue grinder in RPMI-1640. Whole brain homogenates were separated by 37% Percoll gradient centrifugation at 80 × g for 20 min (no brake). The pellets were washed once with RPMI-1640 containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) before staining. Isolated cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies and analyzed as below in the Flow cytometry analysis methods section. To perform scRNA-seq analysis of brain immune cells, we employed the aforementioned non-enzymatic dissociation protocol, which inhibits ex vivo transcription to avoid isolation artifacts. For single-cell isolation from the spleen and cervical lymph nodes (cLN), the spleen and cLN were removed and crushed through a 70-μm cell strainer, washed with PBS, and then centrifuged at 350 × g for 5 min. Red blood cells were lysed with ammonium-chloride potassium (ACK) lysis buffer for 1 min at room temperature and neutralized with the same volume of RPMI-1640 containing 2% FBS. Cells were then centrifuged and resuspended with PBS containing 2% FBS before staining.

Flow cytometry analysis

For analysis of surface markers, cells were stained in PBS containing 0.5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin and 2 mM EDTA for 30 min on ice. Before surface staining, cells were incubated with anti-CD16/CD32 (for Fc receptor blocking; 1:200, clone 93, 14-0161-86, eBioscience) and fixable viability dye eFluor 780 (FVD, 1:2,000, 65-0865-18, eBioscience) for 10 min on ice. For intracellular staining of transcription factors and cytokines, cells were fixed with Foxp3/Transcription staining buffer set (00-5523-00, eBioscience) and intracellular fixation and permeabilization buffer set (88-8824-00, eBioscience), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For cytokine staining, cells were stimulated for 4 h with a cell stimulation cocktail (00-4970-93, eBioscience) in the presence of brefeldin A solution (00-4506-51, eBioscience). The following antibodies were used at 1:200 dilution: anti-CD45 (30F-11), anti-CD11b (M1/70), anti-CD4 (RM4-5), anti-CD8α (53-6.7), anti-CD44 (IM7), anti-KLRG1 (2F1), anti-IFN gamma (XMG1.2), anti-TNF alpha (MP6-XT22), anti-Granzyme A (GzA-3G8.5), anti-Ki-67 (SolA15), anti-CD69 (H1.2F3), anti-CD103 (2E7), anti-Axl (MAXL8DS), anti-CD195 (CCR5, HM-CCR5(7A4)), and anti-GFP polyclonal antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (all from eBioscience); anti-PD-1 (29 F.1A12), anti-mouse CX3CR1 (SA011F11), anti-Granzyme B (GB11), anti-CXCR6 (SA051D1), anti-TCRβ chain (H57-597), and anti-CCL5 (RANTES, 2E9/CCL5) (all from BioLegend). Flow cytometry data were acquired using FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences) or CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed with FlowJo software v10 (TreeStar).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and immunoblotting

Mouse cortex and hippocampus were separated from PBS-perfused mice and stored at −80 °C. For TBS-soluble fraction protein sample extraction, samples were homogenized in 150 mg ml−1 (tissue/buffer) TBS (120 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris, pH 8.0), including protease inhibitor cocktail (4693116001, Roche) in a Dounce homogenizer and centrifuged for 20 min (200,000 × g at 4 °C). TBS-soluble fraction sample supernatants were adjusted to be 0.5 M GuHCl and stored at −80 °C until use. For guanidine-hydrochloride (GuHCl)-soluble fraction protein sample extraction, the remaining sample pellets were resuspended in 6 M GuHCl supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail and loosened the pellet with a pestle, followed by sonication for 30 sec. After incubation for 1 h, samples were centrifuged for 20 min (200,000 × g at 25 °C). Gu-HCl-soluble fraction sample supernatants diluted 12-fold with GuHCl diluent were stored at −80 °C. For formic acid (FA)-soluble fraction, leftover sample pellets were resuspended in 90% FA (Fujifilm Wako) and loosen the pellet with a pestle, followed by incubation for 30 min. After centrifugation for 20 min (200,000 × g at 4 °C), FA-soluble supernatant was separated and neutralized using 0.5 M GuHCl and stored at −80 °C. Quantitative analyses were conducted by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for Aβ1–42 protein (292-64501, Fujifilm Wako) and for CCL5 (DY478, R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For immunoblot analysis, TBS-soluble fraction samples were separated on a 15% SDS-polyacrylamide gel and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with the blocking buffer for 1 h and incubated overnight with anti-APP (1:5,000, 22C1, Merck), anti-BACE1 (1:1,000, D10E5, Cell Signaling Technologies), or anti-Presenilin 1 (1:1,000, D39D1, Cell Signaling Technologies). Membranes were washed with TBS plus 1% Triton X-100 and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse or rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:10,000, SA00001-1 or −2, respectively, Proteintech) or horseradish peroxidase-conjugated mouse anti-GAPDH Monoclonal antibody (1:10,000, 1E6D9, Proteintech) for 1 h. After washing with TBS plus 1% Triton X-100, the membrane was imaged using an ImageQuant LAS 4000 mini. Protein band intensities were quantified with the Fiji image processing software.

Organotypic brain slice cultures (BSCs)

Brains from 3-month-old AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice were used to prepare BSCs as previously described52. In brief, brains were dissected and sectioned sagittally into 250-μm-thick slices using a vibratome (VT1200S, Leica). The hippocampi and adjacent cortices were cultured on Millicell culture inserts (Merck) in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Nacalai Tesque). The culture medium was changed 1 day after preparation and subsequently every 3 days. After 6 days in culture, the slices were treated with CD8+ T cells isolated from the brains and spleens of AppNL-G-F mice, and CD4+ T cells isolated from the brains of AppNL-G-F mice were added at 1 × 104 cells per brain slice. Following a 3-day incubation, each brain slice was analyzed by immunohistochemistry as described above.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from mouse cortex or brain cells using RNeasy Mini or Micro kit (74104 or 74004, QIAGEN), respectively. To detect mRNA, 1 μg of RNA was reverse transcribed with PrimeScript RT Master Mix (RR036A, TaKaRa). Quantitative RT-PCR analysis was performed on cDNA samples using THUNDERBIRD Next SYBR qPCR Mix (cat # QPX-201, TOYOBO) and CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Relative target mRNA levels were normalized to Gapdh mRNA levels.

Mouse brain CD45+ immune cell scTCR/RNA-seq

Single-cell suspensions were generated from the brains of 3-month-old sex-matched WT, AppNL-G-F, and AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice, or 8-month-old AppNL-G-F mice as described in ‘Single-cell suspensions’, and cell-surface staining and sorting were performed as described in ‘Flow cytometry analysis’. Viable immune cells (FVD–CD45intCD11b+ microglia FVD–CD45+ immune cells) were sorted into a 15-ml centrifugation tube with RPMI-1640 containing 2% FBS. CD45+ immune cells were enriched by mixing them with CD45intCD11b+ microglia in PBS supplemented with 0.5% BSA, resulting in a cell suspension containing a total of 12,000 cells to ensure sufficient cell numbers for subsequent analyses. Single-cell suspensions were loaded onto a 10x Genomics Chromium Controller to generate 10,000 single-cell gel beads-in emulsions per sample, and the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Kit v2 (dual index) and Chromium Single Cell Mouse TCR Amplification Kit were used separately to generate a cDNA library for transcriptome expression and targeting TCR. The cDNA content of each sample and libraries were quality-checked using a high-sensitivity DNA chip with a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 at the Laboratory for Research Support of the Institute of Bioregulatory Medicine, Kyushu University.

Gene counts were obtained by aligning reads to the mm10 mouse reference genome with the Cell Ranger software pipeline provided by 10x Genomics. For gene expression sequencing, the filtered count matrices were read into the R package Seurat (v4) and then merged into a single Seurat object with the use of FingIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData. Genes present in no more than three cells, cells with unique gene counts of more than 5000 or less than 200, cells with a unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts of more than 20,000 and cells with a high fraction of mitochondrial reads (>5%) were removed from the dataset. Potential doublet cells were predicted using DoubletFinder for each sample separately, with high-confidence doublets removed. Principal components analysis was conducted, and an elbow plot was used to select components for UMAP analysis and clustering. Cell clusters were identified with the Seurat functions FindNeighbors (using the top 15 principal components) and FindClusters, before manual annotation of clusters based on the expression of canonical gene markers. Cells were classified as CD8+ T cells if they expressed greater than 1.5 transcripts of Cd3e and 1 transcript of Cd8a and exactly 0 of Cd4. Differential gene expression analysis was done using the FindMarkers function. Statistical analysis of cluster proportion was done using scProportionTest.

For TCR sequencing, filtered contig annotation matrices from the Cell Ranger output were loaded into R. Annotation and quantification of TCR clonotypes were processed with the scRepertoire package (v1.3.5). Clonal frequencies were categorized as follows: single (1), small (2–5), medium (6–20) and large (>20). Cells were considered clonally expanded if the clonotype size was greater than 1 and non-expanded if the clonotype size was equal to 1. Cells without clonal information were excluded from clonally expanded versus non-expanded analyses. To quantify clonal diversity, Shannon entropy was calculated using the clonalRarefaction function. The top three groups of CDR3b patterns in clonally expanded CD8+ T cells from the brains of AppNL-G-F mice were then input into the TCRMatch tool (http://tools.iedb.org/tcrmatch/) to query annotated sequences in the Immune Epitope Database. For pathway analysis, differentially expressed genes between genotypes were entered into the Gene Ontology (GO) database (http://geneontology.org/) and enriched for terms of GO biological processes using clusterProfiler (v.4.12.20) with a threshold of log2foldchange (FC) <−0.1 or >0.1 and adjusted P values < 0.05. Volcano plots were generated using the EnhancedVolcano package (v1.12.0). For trajectory analysis, the Monocle3 package (v1.2.7) was used to infer developmental trajectory in the scRNA-seq data of CD8+ T cell subclusters, and pseudotime graphs were created. The CellChat package was used to identify cell-cell interaction pathways corresponding to scRNA-seq data generated by the Seurat package. Briefly, the Seurat object containing cells annotated as microglia and CD8+ T cells was converted into a CellChat object. Circle plots and bubble chart was calculated based on significant ligand-receptor interactions between microglia and CD8+ T cell subclusters. Chord diagrams of pathway activity by cell type were also generated.

Retroviral transduction of T-cell receptors on CD8+ T cells

TCR sequences were obtained from scTCR-seq data from the brains of WT and AppNL-G-F mice. The DNA was synthesized by eBlocks Gene Fragments (Integrated DNA Technologies). The α and β chains were linked by the P2A sequence and inserted into the pMX-IRES-GFP vector53,54. For plasmid transfection, Plate-E cells were seeded in a 10 cm dish in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (both from Nacalai Tesque) the day before transfection. Retroviral plasmids with the desired TCR genes and pCL-Eco retrovirus packaging vector (NBP2-29540, Novus Biologicals) were transfected into Plate-E cells using polyethylenimine MAX (PEI MAX, 24765, Polysciences). At 12 h after transfection, the medium was replaced with DMEM with 10% FBS. The supernatant containing retroviral particles was collected 48 h after transfection and centrifuged at 10,000 × g at 4 °C for 16 h to concentrate the viral vector.

Primary CD8+ T cells were isolated from the spleen and lymph nodes of C57BL/6J mice using a mouse CD8a+ T cell isolation kit (130-104-705, Miltenyi Biotec). Isolated CD8+ T cells were resuspended in culture medium containing 2 μg ml−1 of anti-CD3e (clone 17A2, 16-0032-86, eBioscience), 1 μg ml−1 of anti-CD28 (clone 37.51, 16-0281-86, eBioscience), 10 ng ml−1 of recombinant murine IL-2 (212-12, PeproTech), and then seeded in a 12-well plate. After incubation for 36–48 h at 37 °C, 1 mL of 0.45-μm filtered medium containing retroviral particles and 8 μg ml−1 of polybrene (12996-81, Nacalai Tesque) were added, followed by centrifugation for 2 h (2000 × g at 32 °C). Immediately after the transduction, cells were washed twice and resuspended in RPMI-1640 containing 20 ng ml−1 IL-2. On the day of adoptive transfer (24 h after transduction), the infection efficiency was analyzed by quantifying GFP-positive cells by flow cytometry. Cells were then collected, washed once with PBS, filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer, and resuspended at a concentration equivalent to 1 million cells per 100 μL of PBS, followed by intravenously injected into AppNL-G-F; Cd3e–/– mice.

TCR reconstitution on TG40 cell line

TG40 cell line lacking the expression of surface TCRα/β chains transduced with mouse CD8, kindly provided by Prof. Yokosuka (Tokyo Medical University), was resuspended in 500 μl of culture medium, mixed with 1 ml of medium containing retroviral particles, and added to a single well of a 12-well plate. The transfection reagent polybrene was added to the well at a final concentration of 5 μg ml−1. The cells and virus mixture was centrifuged for 1 h (2000 × g at 32 °C) and then incubated at 37 °C overnight. The cells were further cultured in the presence of 2 μg ml−1 puromycin for selection. After infection, the cells were washed and cultured in fresh medium for an additional 48 h. CD8α+TCRβ+ double-positive cells were subsequently isolated using FACSAria III (BD Biosciences).

Antigen presentation assay

Bone marrow derived-dendritic cells (BMDCs) were generated as previously described55. Briefly, bone marrow cells from C57BL/6 J mice were cultured for 7 days in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, L-glutamine, non-essential amino acids, and 2-mercaptoethanol, in the presence of 10 ng ml−1 recombinant murine GM-CSF (315-03, PeproTech). BMDCs were then seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and exposed brain lysates or peptides, including M38 protein peptide (EP11845_1, peptides&elephants), Aβ42 (4349-v, Peptide institute), and MOG35–55 (4530-v, Peptide institute), at a final concentration of 1 μM. Co-culture was performed with 1 × 105 TCR-transduced TG40 cells. Brain lysates were prepared by homogenizing the brains from WT and AppNL-G-F mice in TBS buffer (150 mg ml−1) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail, using a Dounce homogenizer. After 72 h of co-culturing, supernatants were collected for IL-2 quantification using an IL-2 ELISA kit (88-7024-88, Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Microglial phagocytosis of methoxy-XO4-labeled Aβ in vivo

Microglial phagocytosis of Aβ was determined using mice intraperitoneally injected with 5 mg/kg methoxy-XO4 (2 mg ml−1 in 50% DMSO/50% saline) 3 h before killing. Mice were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS prior to brain extraction, and single-cell suspensions were prepared by mechanical dissociation described above. After blocking by adding 1 μg of anti-CD16/CD32, the cells were stained with antibodies to microglial cell surface markers (CD11b-PE/Cy7, 1:200, eBioscience; CD45-PE, 1:200, eBioscience; Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 780, 1:2,000, cat # 65-0865-14, eBioscience). Cells were measured on a FACSCanto II (BD Bioscience) or CytoFLEX (Beckman), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software v10 (TreeStar). For analysis, the live/FVD–, CD11b+, and CD45int single cells were gated (Gating strategy in Fig. S1e). WT and 10-month-old aged AppNL-G-F mice injected with methoxy-XO4 were used to determine the methoxy-XO4 threshold for non-phagocytosing cells and phagocytosing cells, respectively.

Aβ42 peptide phagocytosis in ex vivo microglial culture

Fluorescein amidite (FAM)-conjugated amyloid-β1–42 peptide (AS-23526-01, Anaspec) was prepared and fibrillized prior to use. Briefly, Aβ42 was initially dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Fujifilm Wako) at a concentration of 25 μM and subsequently diluted in DMEM (Fujifilm Wako) to a final concentration of 0.5 μM. The peptide solution was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to promote aggregation. Fibrillized Aβ42 was then added to fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-isolated WT microglia cultures that had been pre-stimulated overnight with lipopolysaccharide and subsequently treated with or without 0.2 μg ml−1 recombinant CCL5. Aβ42 uptake by microglia was quantified by measuring FAM fluorescence using flow cytometry.

Statistical information

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10 and R. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests, two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc tests, simple linear regression with ANCOVA principles, Pearson’s correlation coefficient (two-sided), and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Bonferroni or Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment were used as indicated in the figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size; the numbers used are consistent with previous reports17,56,57. Age- and sex-matched mice with predetermined genotypes were randomly assigned to control and experimental groups. No data were excluded. The experiments were not randomized, and investigators were not blinded. All findings were reproduced in at least three biological replicates across at least two independent experiments.
