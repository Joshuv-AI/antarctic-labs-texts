Effect of Alkali Fusion Methods on the Preparation of One-Part Geopolymer From Coal Gasification Slag
Authors: Changshuai Chen, Keiko Sasaki, Quanzhi Tian, Haijun Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the one-part geopolymer synthesis by coal gasification slag through a calcination treatment. Two preparation methods, the traditional alkali fusion method (M-I) and the alkali fusion and modification method (M-II), were compared to illustrate the more suitable way for coal gasification slag-based one-part geopolymer (CGS-based geopolymer) synthesis. The reaction products and structure of the geopolymer were tested by XRD, FTIR, TG-DTG, and SEM. The results showed that the compressive strength of geopolymers prepared by M-I was raised as the NaOH amount increased, and the maximum compressive strength of geopolymers was achieved at 8.44 MPa. The low compressive strength suggested that M-I failed to be used for CGS-based geopolymer synthesis. When solid NaOH was selected for one-part geopolymer synthesis by M-II, the geopolymer possessed a compressive strength of 25.52 MPa and exhibited a well-combined and dense matrix. More Si and Al species in F-SH could be released for geopolymerization, and the coal gasification slag additive could be partially dissolved and act as the filler in the geopolymer matrix, which enhanced the geopolymerization degree. The M-II has been proven to be more suitable for preparing CGS-based geopolymers than the M-I.

Introduction

Coal gasification is a process that uses oxygen or water vapor to react with coal at high temperatures to produce valuable gas while producing a large amount of coal gasification slag.1,2 With the development of the coal gasification industry, large-scale coal gasification slag has brought a series of prominent environmental problems, which will occupy many land resources and risk groundwater pollution.3−6 Approximately 33 million tons of coal gasification slag are produced annually in China.7 With the development of coal chemical enterprises, it can be predicted that more coal gasification slag will be produced in the near future. Some researchers have proposed that coal gasification slag can be utilized as geopolymer concrete or cement additive.6,8−11

In recent years, geopolymers have garnered a lot of interest as a potential ordinary Portland cement replacement.12,13 Geopolymers are inorganic polymers with a three-dimensional network structure of AlO4 and SiO4 tetrahedral structural units.14,15 According to the different preparation methods, geopolymers can be divided into two- and one-part geopolymers. The former mixes the raw material with a liquid activator to form a uniform slurry. The latter premixes solid raw material and solid alkali as the precursor by calcination or hydrothermal activation, which avoids the utilization of liquid alkali solution.16 The alkali fusion method is a promising treatment for one-part geopolymer synthesis. The raw material could be converted to a highly reactive material, and the activity of the aluminosilicate material was enhanced through calcination with solid alkali.17 The solubility of the fusion product was improved, and Si and Al species can be released from the precursor for geopolymerization.18 It was found that the reactivity of the fusion product depended on the Na2O amount incorporated during the calcination treatment.17 The increase in the alkali amount would lead to a higher activity of the fusion product, in turn increasing the compressive strength of the geopolymers. However, an excess of alkali amount would cause an efflorescence phenomenon on the surface of the geopolymer and reduce the mechanical properties of the geopolymers. Mao et al.19 found that alkali fusion treatment increased the reactivity of basalt, and the reaction degree of geopolymer was raised when 50% NaOH amount was added to the synthesis system. Moreover, Bao et al.20 employed the alkali fusion and modification method to prepare the one-part geopolymer. The homogeneous blend of vanadium tailing and NaOH was calcined to obtain the fusion product, and then, metakaolin was added to the synthesis system to produce the geopolymeric precursor. The vanadium tailing was combined with a geopolymer gel and yielded a geopolymer with high mechanical strength. However, the alkali fusion and modification method has not been applied for the synthesis of a coal gasification slag-based one-part geopolymer (CGS-based geopolymer).

Geopolymers can be prepared by aluminosilicate, whereas other solid wastes, such as fly ash, coal gangue, tailings, blast furnace slag, and perlite, can also be used as silicon and aluminum sources for geopolymer synthesis.21−24 However, there is only a small part of the research serving coal gasification slag as building materials. Fu et al.25 used coal gasification slag to replace Portland cement and proved that a low dosage of coal gasification slag (10%) could play a role in nucleation and pozzolanic effect in Portland cement, which was conducive to the hydration reactions of Portland cement. Luo et al.26 studied the feasibility of using decarbonized coal gasification fine slag and coarse slag as the admixture of cement-based materials. According to the results, the synthetic use of coal gasification fine slag and coarse slag had a good effect on the fluidity and strength of the cement-based materials. Meanwhile, coal gasification slag modified by sodium sulfate could be used as cemented backfill materials, and the results showed that about 4% sodium sulfate had a good activating effect on modified coal gasification slag.27 There is little study on one-part geopolymer synthesis by alkali fusion from coal gasification slag.

In this study, the main objective is to use alkali fusion treatment for the coal gasification slag toward the synthesis of one-part geopolymers, which has been less explored in the scientific community. As a comparison, alkali fusion and modification treatment of coal gasification slag was conducted to illustrate the synthesis process of one-part geopolymers. The reaction products were evaluated by X-ray diffraction, Fourier transform infrared, and thermogravimetric analysis, and the microstructure of the geopolymer specimen was observed by scanning electron microscopy. The uniaxial compressive strength was employed to study the mechanical properties of the geopolymer. This research has laid the foundation for further preparation of CGS-based geopolymers by the alkali fusion method.

Experimental Section

Materials

The coal gasification slag was collected from Ningxia Coal, China. To prepare the materials, it was ground to <75 μm. Flotation technology was employed to remove the carbon component in the coal gasification slag and obtain a low-carbon coal gasification slag. Then, coal gasification slag was dried at 100 °C for 12 h. The dried coal gasification slag was employed as the raw material for the alkali fusion treatment. The main components of coal gasification slag are SiO2, Al2O3, Fe2O3, and CaO.

Alkali Fusion Procedure and One-Part Geopolymer Synthesis

Traditional Alkali Fusion Method (M-I)

The procedure for one-part geopolymer synthesis by M-I is shown in Figure 2. Dried coal gasification slag was blended with solid NaOH at a given proportion, and then the mixed powder was calcined at 550 °C for 3 h in a muffle furnace. Finally, the fused material was cooled down to room temperature, and the fusion product was produced. According to different NaOH amounts, the fusion products were named FP-10%, FP-15%, FP-20%, FP-25%, FP-30%, and FP-35%. The liquid-to-solid ratio of 0.3 was employed, and deionized water was added to the fusion product. After fully stirring, the slurry was transferred into a cubic mold with a size of 20 × 20 × 20 mm. The 28 day compressive strength was tested by an electronic universal testing machine (CSS-100 KN). The detailed experimental design of M-I is shown in Table 2.

Alkali Fusion and Modification Method (M-II)

The procedure for one-part geopolymer synthesis by M-II is shown in Figure 2. Potassium hydroxide (KOH), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium metasilicate (Na2SiO3), and sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) were selected to be calcined with coal gasification slag at a mass ratio of 1.2:1 to obtain the fusion product. The corresponding fusion product was named FP-PH, FP-SH, FP-SM, and FP-SC. Then, the 20 wt % fusion product was mixed with 80 wt % coal gasification slag additives to obtain the precursor for the one-part geopolymer synthesis. The total amount of alkali consumed in every geopolymer sample was 1.53 g. A liquid-to-solid ratio of 0.3 was employed, and the mixing was continued for 3 min to fully contact the solid and liquid. The remaining steps for one-part geopolymer synthesis are the same as those for the M-1. The synthesized geopolymers were named G-PH, G-SH, G-SM, and G-SC. As shown in Table 3, four different agents were selected, and different calcination temperatures were employed. Because the objective of the alkali fusion treatment is to fuse solid alkali and coal gasification slag, the calcination temperature must be higher than the fusion point of the solid alkali.

Leaching Test

Leaching tests were performed to analyze the dissolving properties of the fusion product.20 One gram of the fusion product was blended with 50 mL of deionized water and placed in the shaker for 30 min at room temperature. Then, the filtrate and residue were collected by centrifugation and filtration. The filtrate was used for the ICP-OES test, and the residue was dried and subjected to an XRD test.

Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were measured with an Ultima IV diffractometer (RIGAKU, Cu Kα radiation, Japan). The instrument was set to scan at a speed of 2°/min with a step size of 0.02°. Typical KBr pellets were used in a spectrometer to get the Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR, Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS20, America) of the geopolymers. The microstructures of geopolymers were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, VE-9800, KEYENCE, 20 kV accelerating voltage, Japan). TG-DTG was measured by a thermogravimetric instrument (STA449F3, Netzsch, Germany). The element concentrations in the leaching solution of the fusion product were analyzed by ICP-OES (PerkinElmer Optima8300, America). The typical detection limits of ICP-OES for Si and K were 0.1–1.0 μg/L, while those for Al and Na were 0.01–0.1 μg/L.

Results and Discussion

Compressive Strength

The 28 day compressive strength of geopolymers prepared by M-I is shown in Figure 3a. The compressive strengths of geopolymers prepared using FP-10% and FP-15% were 1.50 and 1.41 MPa, respectively. The compressive strength of geopolymers was raised with the increase in the NaOH amount. When the NaOH amounts were 20%, 25%, and 30%, the compressive strengths of G-20%, G-25%, and G-30% were 7.34, 6.8, and 8.44 MPa, respectively, which indicated that a higher amount of NaOH improved the compressive strength of geopolymers. Although the high NaOH amount provided more Al and Si species for geopolymerization, the compressive strength of G-30% was still relatively low. When the NaOH amount was 35%, geopolymer (G-35%) failed to build adequate strength. Meanwhile, the surface of G-35% underwent wrinkling and was accompanied by white spots. The high NaOH amount in the fusion product would release a large amount of Na+ and OH– when it encountered water. Finally, it eventually led to corrosion inside the geopolymer, reducing the compressive strength of the geopolymer block.28

Compressive strength of geopolymers synthesized by different methods: (a) M-I; (b) M-II.

The compressive strength of geopolymers synthesized by M-II is shown in Figure 3b. There were significant differences in the compressive strength of geopolymers prepared by different alkali agents. The compressive strength of G-PH was 10.08 MPa, and the compressive strengths of G-SM and G-SC were 2.32 and 6.91 MPa, respectively. The maximum compressive strength was achieved at a value of 25.52 MPa in G-SH. Compared to the M-I, the compressive strength of the one-part geopolymer could be improved by the M-II when KOH and NaOH were used. Meanwhile, 1.53 g of NaOH was used and achieved a compressive strength of 25.52 MPa (NaOH usage equivalent was 10.9% compared to the M-I), while the compressive strengths of G-10% and G-15% were 1.5 and 1.41 MPa, corresponding to the NaOH consumption of 1.4 and 2.1 g, respectively. Compared to the compressive strength of geopolymers synthesized by the M-I, the results showed that the M-II could improve the compressive strength of the one-part geopolymer.

Solid Analysis

Figure 4a shows the XRD patterns of geopolymers prepared via the M-I. The diffraction peak of quartz (SiO2) was detected at 2θ = 26.7°, which suggested that quartz existed in the geopolymers.29 The diffraction peak intensity of thermonatrite (Na2CO3.H2O) became stronger in G-30%. This phenomenon illustrated that a high NaOH amount promoted the generation of carbonate. Moreover, sodium aluminum silicate (NaAlSiO4) was detected in geopolymers. With the increase in the NaOH amount, the peak intensity belonging to sodium aluminum silicate (NaAlSiO4) reduced. Moreover, sodalite (Na8Al6Si6O24CO3) and sodium silicate (Na6Si2O7) were detected in geopolymers. Furthermore, geopolymers with a high NaOH amount had high crystallinity, which suggested a harmful activating effect and led to a low compressive strength of the geopolymer.30

XRD patterns of the geopolymers: (a) M-I; (b) M-II.

The XRD patterns of the geopolymers prepared by M-II are shown in Figure 4b. The geopolymers were composed mainly of an amorphous substance. The quartz crystals were detected in geopolymers originating from the coal gasification slag additive. The sodium silicate (Na6Si2O7) existed in G-SC. The hump at 2θ = 20–40° was partially derived from the unreacted coal gasification slag, while the newly formed N/K-A–S-H gel was also present in the geopolymer.31

Figure 5a shows the FTIR spectra of geopolymers synthesized by the M-I. The bands around 400–500 cm–1 were caused by the bending vibration of the Si–O–Si or O–Si–O bond.32,33 The bands in the range of 690–700 cm–1 were attributed to the Si–O group, representing quartz in the geopolymer.34 The bands in the range of 950–1050 cm–1 were caused by the asymmetric stretching vibration of tetrahedral TO4 (T is Si or Al) in Si–O–T in the geopolymer structure. With the increase in the NaOH amount, the bands shifted to a lower wavenumber, which might be because that part of the Si–O tetrahedron was replaced by the Al–O tetrahedron, resulting in the local environment change of the Si–O bond.35 Moreover, the inverted peak area of the Si–O–T in the range of 800–1200 cm–1 was used to quantitatively assess the geopolymer reaction.36 The inverted peak area of G-10% was the lowest, indicating a low geopolymerization. The inverted peak areas of G-25% and G-30% showed a reasonable amount of geopolymerization. An increase in the NaOH amount from 10 to 30% resulted in an increase in the inverted peak area of geopolymers, indicating a higher degree of geopolymerization within this range. The bands located at 1400–1500 cm–1 were caused by efflorescence since the Na+ ions reacted with CO2 in the air to generate carbonate. With the increase of NaOH amount in the system, the intensity of the CO32– bands became stronger, which had a negative effect on the compressive strength of the geopolymer.37 The bands located at 1600–1700 and 3400–3500 cm–1 were attributed to bending and stretching vibrations of the O–H group, respectively.38 As the NaOH amount was increased in geopolymers, more water molecules were generated.

FTIR spectra of the geopolymers: (a) M-I; (b) M-II.

The FTIR spectra of geopolymers synthesized by M-II are shown in Figure 5b. The bands at 440–470 cm–1 were caused by the bending vibration of the Si–O–Si or O–Si–O bond. The bands at 690–720 cm–1 were an internal extension of the Si–O group and represented quartz in the coal gasification slag. The absorption bands at 990–1040 cm–1 were attributed to stretching vibrations of the Si–O–T bond. The bands at 1410–1480 cm–1 were representative of the CO32– group. The bending vibration of the H–O–H bond in free water caused the bands at 1630–1660 cm–1. The bands at 3400–3450 cm–1 were assigned to the stretching vibration of the O–H bond in structured water and in free water. The free water adsorbed on the surface of the reaction products or was embedded in the pores of the reaction products, while the structural water was present as the hydration product in the geopolymer.39

TG-DTG Analysis

The TG-DTG curves of the geopolymers synthesized by M-I are shown in Figure 6a. The weight loss of geopolymers can be divided into three stages. The weight loss at <300 °C was caused by the elimination of physically and chemically bound water and the dehydration of N-A–S-H gels,40 where the evaporation of free water in the geopolymer pore structure was concentrated at <100 °C. The weight loss of geopolymers represented the content of the losing substances. As shown in Table 4, the weight loss of geopolymers in the first stage was raised with the increase in the NaOH amount. A high NaOH amount was beneficial for the generation of free water and chemically bound water, and more hydration products were generated in geopolymers. This could improve the compressive strength of the geopolymers and was consistent with the FTIR results.40 The second stage, from 300 to 500 °C was regarded as the elimination of chemical structural water from Si–OH and Al–OH.41 The thermonatrite (Na2CO3.H2O) in geopolymers lost its crystal water. As the NaOH amount was increased, the weight loss of geopolymers increased, which indicated that more thermonatrite was produced in geopolymers. In the temperature range of 500–800 °C (stage 3), the weight loss was associated with the decarbonization of sodium carbonate, and G-30% showed the highest weight loss.42 The high amount of NaOH promoted the compressive strength of the geopolymers and led to strong efflorescence. From room temperature to 800 °C, geopolymers with a high NaOH amount exhibited high weight loss due to the presence of more Na-rich phases.

Microstructure Observation

Figure 7 exhibits the microstructure of geopolymers prepared by the M-I and M-II. Since G-10% and G-15% had lower compressive strengths and their specimens were loose, G-20%, G-25%, and G-30% were used to describe the internal structure of geopolymers. SEM images of G-20% showed that many spheres adhered to the surface of the specimen and exhibited lower connectivity. The connectivity of G-25% and G-30% was enhanced due to the high amount of NaOH. The particles were combined by flocculent products in specimens and produced a geopolymer gel in G-30%. However, there were still many holes in G-25%, and it failed to form a compact and dense matrix. It can be seen in G-25% and G-30% that there was more crystalline phase generation, which is in good agreement with the XRD results (Figure 4a). One of the reasons is that a part of the hydration products was consumed under a high NaOH amount and left more crystalline phases in the geopolymer matrix.

SEM images of the geopolymers.

Due to the low compressive strength of G-SM, only G-PH, G-SH, and G-SC were selected for SEM observation. Since a large amount of coal gasification slag was used as the additive, it is inevitable that some particles were not dissolved in the geopolymer specimens. As shown in G-PH, an obvious ball particle was embedded in the geopolymer specimen, and there was a crack shown in G-PH, which might be due to the traces that remained by the coal gasification slag.45 G-SH exhibited a denser structure, and small particles were combined to form a uniform and complete matrix. Moreover, the geopolymer gel was formed in the geopolymer matrix, which was a signal of a well-activated effect. On the contrary, many particles adhered to the surface of the specimen under the SEM view in G-SC, showing a loose and unfixed microstructure. Although no large particles were detected, the less dense structure showed a bad microstructure, suggesting that Na2CO3 was not suitable for activating coal gasification slag.

Discussion

The difference between the M-I and M-II can be concluded as two aspects. The first one was the difference in mass ratios between alkali and coal gasification slag during the alkali fusion treatment. The largest mass ratio between alkali and coal gasification slag in M-I was 0.3, whereas in M-II was 1.2. The second one was the coal gasification slag additive used in M-II. To clarify the effects of the fusion product, the XRD patterns of the fusion product obtained by alkali fusion treatment by the M-I and M-II were conducted. Figure 8 shows the XRD patterns of the fusion product obtained after alkali fusion by the M-I. Since NaOH was introduced as the alkali flux, Na-rich phases were observed in the fusion product.46 The initial aluminosilicate minerals presented in the coal gasification slag were reacted with fused NaOH, and the structure of the coal gasification slag was destroyed. It was found that during the reaction of NaOH with coal gasification slag, Na+ and Si4+ were combined in different forms. Sodium silicate (Na6Si2O7), sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), and quartz (SiO2) were detected in FP-10%. With the increase in NaOH addition, the characteristic peak intensity of quartz became weaker. Meanwhile, as the NaOH amount was increased, the diffraction peak strength and crystallinity of the fusion product increased and the destructive effect of the high NaOH amount on coal gasification slag became more obvious. Na2CO3 was detected in all fusion products due to the reaction between NaOH and CO2 in the air during the alkali fusion process.47 As the NaOH amount increased, the diffraction peak of the Na2CO3 crystal became stronger, indicating that the Na2CO3 content in the fusion products was raised. A leaching test of the fusion product was performed to study the leaching amounts of Si and Al elements. As shown in Figure 9, Al and Si were presented at concentrations of 0.0113 and 0.0352 mmol/g in the FP-10% leaching solution, respectively. Al and Si concentrations in the leaching solution increased along with the NaOH content, and the concentrations of Al and Si presented in the FP-30% leaching solution were 0.0354 and 0.3532 mmol/g, respectively. The increase in the NaOH mass ratio in the calcination system promoted more coal gasification slag to be fused. Thus, more Al and Si components were dissolved during the leaching process, which provided more active species for geopolymerization. Moreover, the concentration of Al in the fusion product leaching solution was very low. The Si/Al molar ratio in the FP-10% leaching solution was 3.12. The Si/Al molar ratio in the fusion product leaching solution increased with the addition of the NaOH amount, and the Si/Al molar ratio of FP-30% was 9.98. As the NaOH amount increased, the leaching rate of Si in fusion products significantly increased, ultimately resulting in a larger Si/Al molar ratio in the leaching solution. This indicated that the amount of Al was insufficient for geopolymerization.

Leaching properties of fusion product synthesized by the M-I.

The XRD patterns of the fusion product obtained by M-II are shown in Figure 10. In Figure 10a, Potassium aluminum oxide (K2Al2O4) and potassium carbonate (K2CO3) were detected in the XRD patterns of FP-PH. This was because the active component in coal gasification slag reacted with KOH and formed K-rich phases. After the leaching test, the crystals of potassium aluminum oxide and potassium carbonate were dissolved, and potassium aluminum oxide hydrate (K2Al2O4.3H2O) was present in the FP-PH-leaching residue. As seen in Figure 10b, different forms of the Na-rich phases were generated after alkali fusion. After the leaching test, only sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) crystals were detected, which suggested that part of the aluminosilicate was dissolved and released Si and Al components into the leaching solution. As seen in Figure 10c, there was no crystal generation in FP-SM, and the peak of the quartz disappeared. During the calcination process, the coal gasification slag and sodium metasilicate fused and turned into a glassy phase at 1100 °C. Moreover, after the leaching test, the XRD pattern of FP-SM leaching residue showed little difference compared to FP-SM, which suggested that FP-SM had a relatively low solubility. In Figure 10d, FP-SC consisted mainly of sodium silicate (Na2SiO3), sodium aluminum silicate (Na1.75Al1.75Si0.25O4), and sodium silicate (Na2Si2O5). After the leaching test, only sodium aluminum oxide (NaAlO2) was found in the FP-SC leaching residue. The solubility of the fusion products played a significant role in geopolymer synthesis, and it was also the primary component in providing active Si and Al species for geopolymerization.

XRD patterns of fusion products and leaching residues activated by (a) KOH, (b) NaOH, (c) Na2SiO3, and (d) Na2CO3.

The Si and Al components in the fusion product were transferred to the filtrate. Figure 11 shows the concentrations of Si and Al in the filtrate of the fusion product. The concentrations of Si and Al in FP-SH were higher than those in the other three fusion products, which suggested that more Si and Al species in FP-SH could be released and used for geopolymerization. Meanwhile, the FP-SM had very low concentrations of Si and Al, which illustrated that the FP-SM could hardly release Si and Al, which were useless for geopolymerization. Moreover, it was found that the Al concentration of the fusion product was lower than the Si concentration, and a large Si/Al molar ratio had a negative effect on the geopolymer.

The M-I is often used to prepare one-part geopolymers.18,38,48−50Table 6 summarizes some literature about one-part geopolymer preparation via the M-I. Regardless of whether the alkali content used in the alkali fusion process was higher than that in this study, the compressive strength of the geopolymers was far higher than that in this study. The M-I was not suitable for preparing CGS-based geopolymers. When the NaOH amount was less, geopolymers could not be activated efficiently during alkali fusion, and lower reactive fusion products could not release sufficient Si and Al species for geopolymer synthesis. As the NaOH amount was raised, the enhancement of the compressive strength of geopolymer could be predicted, but the compressive strength of G-30% was only 8.44 MPa, which was lower than the limit of usage in the field of construction material. Meanwhile, it is worth noting from FTIR data (Figure 5) that geopolymers showed a strong efflorescence phenomenon as the NaOH amount was raised. The Na-rich phases formed in the fusion product were also detected in the geopolymers, which showed weak dissolution and proved its poor reactivity. Therefore, it cannot continue to increase the NaOH amount to achieve high compressive strength.

When the M-II was used to explore the one-part geopolymer synthesis, and results found that the M-II promoted the compressive strength of geopolymers at a value of 25.52 MPa, which proved that the M-II was beneficial to synthesize CGS-based geopolymer with a low NaOH dosage. A high alkali-to-slag ratio promoted the dissolution of the Si and Al components from the fusion product. Compared to the M-I, the fusion product with high reactivity could provide more active species for geopolymerization in the M-II. Moreover, the fusion product dissolved in the water and then released active Si and Al species for geopolymerization and increased the pH of the reaction system, which caused the particle of coal gasification slag to be partially dissolved to provide a reactive substance. It was undeniable that there was still a large amount of coal gasification slag additive in the geopolymer that was not or partially dissolved. The unreacted coal gasification slag existed in the geopolymer matrix as a filler, which reduced the porosity of the geopolymer matrix.25

Conclusions

This study investigated the feasibility of using coal gasification slag for one-part geopolymer synthesis by calcination treatment. The M-I produced geopolymers with a high compressive strength of 8.44 MPa in a 30% NaOH amount. The lower NaOH amount in the synthesis system was not beneficial to strength development, and the coal gasification slag could not be activated. Moreover, a high NaOH amount of 35% in the synthesis system caused strong efflorescence, which destroyed the geopolymer structure. Therefore, M-I failed to prepare the one-part geopolymer to achieve high compressive strength. The maximum compressive strength of the geopolymer prepared by M-II was 25.52 MPa, which showed a well-combined matrix in G-SH. However, the compressive strength of G-SM and G-SC was still low, especially in G-SM. 20 wt % of coal gasification slag was calcined with NaOH, and the reactivity of the fusion product was guaranteed. The existing 80 wt % coal gasification slag was regarded as the additive, promoting the compressive strength of geopolymers. This work compared different alkali fusion methods for one-part geopolymer synthesis and promoted a new way to utilize coal gasification slag.
