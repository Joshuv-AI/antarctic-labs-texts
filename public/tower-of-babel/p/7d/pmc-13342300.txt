Pharmacokinetics of asciminib in a patient with chronic myeloid leukemia undergoing maintenance hemodialysis
Authors: Maiko Abumiya, Ko Abe, Atsushi Watanabe, Naoto Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Asciminib (ASC) is a novel BCR::ABL1 tyrosine kinase inhibitor that binds to the ABL myristoyl pocket. Although dose adjustments for renal impairment are generally considered unnecessary, pharmacokinetic data for patients with end-stage renal disease or those on dialysis are limited. A 72-year-old man with chronic phase chronic myeloid leukemia undergoing maintenance hemodialysis for chronic renal failure was treated with ASC. Owing to cardiovascular risk, treatment was initiated at a reduced dose of 40 mg once daily. A major molecular response was achieved within 3 months without adverse events. Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed a delayed apparent Tmax (> 4 h), suggesting prolonged absorption. ASC concentrations did not decrease during dialysis sessions compared with non-dialysis days. The median trough concentration was 89.0 ng/mL, with a coefficient of variation of 30.9%. ASC demonstrated negligible dialyzability, likely because of its high plasma protein binding (97.3%). However, the study revealed a significantly delayed apparent Tmax and high intra-individual variability in plasma concentrations. These findings suggest that clinicians should prioritize monitoring for delayed gastrointestinal absorption and potentially reduced bioavailability in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis, rather than focusing on drug removal by dialysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00280-026-04919-4.

Introduction

Asciminib (ASC) is a specifically targeted ABL myristoyl pocket inhibitor. Although it is effective for newly diagnosed chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), as well as for patients with CML resistant or intolerant to prior tyrosine kinase inhibitors, clinical trials have excluded patients with end-stage renal disease [1, 2]. Consequently, the pharmacokinetic (PK) profile of ASC in patients on hemodialysis remains poorly understood. Here, we report the PKs of ASC in a patient on maintenance hemodialysis.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old man with a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia presented with leukocytosis. He was undergoing maintenance hemodialysis thrice weekly for chronic renal failure. Hemodialysis was performed for 4.0 h per session using an NVF-21 H dialyzer (Toray Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), which consists of a polysulfone membrane with a surface area of 2.1 m². The blood flow rate (Qb) was maintained at 230 mL/min, and the dialysate flow rate (Qd) was set at 500 mL/min. Bone marrow aspiration and cytogenetic analysis confirmed the diagnosis of BCR::ABL1-positive CML in the chronic phase, categorized as intermediate risk by the Eutos long-term survival score, with no additional cytogenetic abnormalities.

Considering the patient’s high risk of cardiovascular events associated with comorbidities and end-stage renal disease, ASC treatment was initiated at a reduced dose of 40 mg once daily rather than the standard 80 mg once daily or 40 mg twice daily. ASC was administered orally at 10:00 AM, exactly 3 h after his usual breakfast at home (7:00 AM), which coincided with the commencement of the 4-hour hemodialysis sessions on dialysis days, and the patient remained fasted during the 4-hour hemodialysis session to prevent intradialytic fluid shifts and hypotension, consuming lunch after 2:00 PM. In contrast, on non-dialysis days, the patient consumed lunch at approximately 12:00 PM (2 h post-dose). Concomitant medications included elobixibat, precipitated calcium carbonate, and other supportive therapies tailored for end-stage renal disease, which are comprehensively detailed in Table 1. Notably, none of these concomitant medications were known inhibitors or inducers of CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein (P-gp), ruling out potential confounding drug-drug interactions.

The treatment was well tolerated without significant adverse events. Although grade 1 QT prolongation was observed, it was consistent with the baseline QT interval, and the therapy was continued. The molecular response was remarkably rapid; the BCR::ABL1 (IS) transcript levels decreased from 70.5126% at baseline to 0.0665% within 3 months, achieving a remarkably rapid major molecular response. The halving time of the BCR::ABL1 transcripts was calculated to be 10 days, indicating high sensitivity to ASC despite the reduced dosage.

Pharmacokinetic results

To assess the impact of hemodialysis on ASC exposure, PK evaluations were performed more than 3 months after the initiation of treatment, when steady-state plasma concentrations had been achieved. Plasma concentrations of ASC were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography [3]. Contrary to the typical Tmax of 2 to 3 h reported in healthy participants [4], the plasma concentration of ASC in this patient continued to rise even at 4 h post-dose, indicating a significantly delayed apparent Tmax (> 4 h; Fig. 1).

A comparison between dialysis and non-dialysis days revealed that ASC concentrations did not decrease during hemodialysis sessions. Notably, at 4 h post-administration, the plasma concentration was higher on dialysis days than on non-dialysis days, with a concentration difference of approximately 270 ng/mL.

The median trough concentration (Ctrough) across five separate measurements (including both dialysis and non-dialysis days) was 89.0 ng/mL (range: 54.6–127.0 ng/mL). The intra-individual coefficient of variation for these trough levels was high (30.9%), reflecting significant fluctuations in daily drug exposure.

Discussion

The observed delay in apparent Tmax and high intra-individual variability in trough concentrations suggest that the absorption phase of ASC was uniquely altered in this patient. The median steady-state Ctrough observed in this patient (89.0 ng/mL) was lower than the mean steady-state Ctrough of approximately 250–300 ng/mL typically expected with the standard 80 mg/day dose in patients with normal renal function. Crucially, a definitive therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) reference range or an established efficacy threshold for ASC has not yet been formalized in peer-reviewed literature. Despite the lower dose and lower relative exposure, the achievement of a rapid MMR within 3 months is clinically noteworthy, demonstrating that a reduced dose of 40 mg once daily can still exert potent and prompt therapeutic efficacy in this specific population.

Patients on maintenance hemodialysis frequently experience autonomic neuropathy and reduced gastrointestinal motility [5, 6]. In the present case, the patient’s chronic constipation, which was managed with the ileal bile acid transporter inhibitor elobixibat, further indicated impaired intestinal peristalsis. Such reduced motility likely extends gastric emptying time and slows the transit of ASC through the small intestine, leading to attenuation of the absorption peak.

Furthermore, the bioavailability of ASC is significantly reduced when taken with food [7, 8]. Although the drug was administered 3 h after breakfast, the patient’s decreased gastrointestinal motility may have resulted in the prolonged presence of food in the upper digestive tract. This would effectively maintain a “fed state” over a longer duration, potentially lowering overall bioavailability and contributing to the erratic absorption profile. The high intra-individual coefficient of variation of 30.9% likely reflects daily variations in gastric emptying and intestinal transit times.

The second significant finding of this study is that hemodialysis did not clinically affect the plasma concentration of ASC. Our PK data clearly demonstrated that ASC levels did not decrease during the dialysis session compared with non-dialysis days. In fact, ASC concentrations continued to rise during the procedure owing to the timing of administration. This observation is consistent with the physicochemical and pharmacological properties of the drug.

ASC is a small-molecule compound with a low molecular weight (449.8 g/mol), which theoretically allows it to pass through the pores of high-flux dialysis membranes. However, its plasma protein binding rate is exceptionally high, at approximately 97.3% [9]. In renal replacement therapy, the dialyzability of a drug is primarily determined by its unbound (free) fraction rather than its molecular weight alone. Drugs with a protein binding rate exceeding 90%, such as propranolol, are known to be poorly cleared by hemodialysis [10, 11]. Although a case report indicated that dose adjustments were necessary owing to significant ASC clearance observed during hemodialysis based on PK analysis [12], the negligible impact of dialysis on ASC concentrations in our patient highlights distinct differences (Table 2). In our patient, hemodialysis was conducted using an NVF-21 H polysulfone membrane. Although the NVF-21 H is structurally categorized as a high-flux membrane due to its superior uremic toxin clearance capabilities, it is specifically engineered to exhibit high albumin retention properties, behaving similarly to a low-flux membrane exclusively in terms of preventing albumin leakage. Given that ASC possesses an exceptionally high plasma protein-binding rate of 97.3% (predominantly to albumin), the structural retention of albumin by the NVF-21 H membrane directly hinders the dialytic removal of the drug. Therefore, the divergent conclusions between our report and Naka et al. may be heavily influenced by these subtle yet clinically critical differences in dialyzer membrane characteristics (such as specific albumin-sieving coefficients), alongside the differing sampling schedules relative to oral intake. The negligible impact of dialysis on ASC concentrations in our patient confirms that the drug remains largely bound to albumin or other plasma proteins, rendering it inaccessible for removal via the dialyzer. Although the prescribing information for ASC indicates that the drug is primarily eliminated via the fecal route (approximately 80%) and that renal impairment does not significantly alter its AUC in non-dialysis patients, our data provide the first clinical evidence that even the physical process of hemodialysis does not require dose supplementation or timing adjustments post-dialysis to maintain therapeutic levels.

Interestingly, a concentration difference of approximately 270 ng/mL was observed at 4 h post-dose, with higher levels recorded on the dialysis day than on the non-dialysis day. While this could partially reflect dialysis-induced fluid shifts, the difference in lunchtime schedules between the two days offers a more compelling pharmacological explanation. On dialysis days, the patient remained fasted until the session concluded at 2:00 PM (4 h post-dose). Conversely, on non-dialysis days, he had lunch at 12:00 PM, which was 2 h after ASC administration. Given that patients on maintenance hemodialysis frequently exhibit significant autonomic neuropathy and reduced gastrointestinal motility, the introduction of food at 12:00 PM—right during the active drug absorption phase—likely exacerbated gastric emptying delays and prolonged the “fed state” within the upper gastrointestinal tract. Because the bioavailability of ASC is known to be substantially decreased when taken with food, this mid-phase meal on the non-dialysis day may have further attenuated and delayed drug absorption, resulting in the lower 4-h concentration compared to the intra-dialytic fasting state. This highlights that dietary timing, interacting with uremic gastroparesis, can serve as a primary driver of the high intra-individual pharmacokinetic variability observed in this population.

This study has several important limitations. First, due to practical and clinical constraints in a routine outpatient setting, pharmacokinetic blood sampling was restricted to 4 h post-dose, which prevented the capture of the complete concentration-time profile. Consequently, the true Cmax and definitive area under the curve (AUC) could not be calculated, and the reported Tmax must be interpreted strictly as an apparent or estimated value. Furthermore, we did not collect the dialysate fluid or measure the drug concentration in the dialysate, which precluded the direct calculation of dialysate clearance (CLD). Second, because the absorption phase was incompletely sampled, the hypothesis regarding altered bioavailability remains speculative, as a prolonged absorption with a delayed, broad peak could still result in a total drug exposure (AUC) similar to that of individuals with normal renal function. Lastly, without extended post-4-hour data, it remains unclear whether the plasma concentration profiles observed on dialysis and non-dialysis days eventually converge, which limits a definitive interpretation of the concentration differences at the 4-hour mark. Future studies with extended sampling windows (e.g., up to 12–24 h) are required to fully elucidate the steady-state pharmacokinetics of ASC in this patient population.

Conclusion

In patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis, ASC exhibits negligible dialyzability because of its high plasma protein binding, requiring no dose supplementation after dialysis sessions. However, clinicians should be vigilant regarding delayed gastrointestinal absorption and potentially reduced bioavailability. These phenomena, likely caused by impaired gastric motility and prolonged dietary influence in patients undergoing dialysis, result in a significantly delayed apparent Tmax and high intra-individual variability. Further case studies are required to establish optimal dosing for this specific population.

New findings

This case report provides key pharmacokinetic insights into asciminib use during maintenance hemodialysis. First, asciminib demonstrated negligible dialyzability, as the plasma concentrations did not decrease during dialysis sessions. This was attributed to its high plasma protein binding (97.3%). Second, we observed a significantly delayed apparent Tmax (> 4 h) and high intra-individual variability (coefficient of variation, 30.9%). These findings suggest that impaired gastrointestinal motility and prolonged dietary influence in patients undergoing dialysis can attenuate absorption and affect bioavailability, necessitating careful consideration of pharmacokinetic, regardless of the specific dose.
