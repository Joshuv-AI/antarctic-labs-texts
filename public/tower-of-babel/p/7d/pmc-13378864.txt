Parental communication on sexual and reproductive health and contraceptive knowledge among adolescents in indigenous contexts, Mexico
Authors: Estibeyesbo Said Plascencia-Nieto, José Á. Hernández-Mariano, Lourdes Campero, Celia Hubert, Fátima Estrada, Raffaela Schiavon, Mónica A. Cureño-Díaz, Edna E. G. Vences, Aremis Villalobos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Adolescent pregnancy has a profound impact on young mothers, their families, and their communities. In rural and indigenous areas, this effect is exacerbated by structural factors such as poverty, educational gaps, gender inequality, and discrimination against indigenous peoples. The aim is to analyze the association between parental communication about sexual and reproductive health and adolescents’ knowledge of contraceptive methods in some rural communities of Mexico.METHODS AND MATERIALS:This study used a cross-sectional design based on the secondary analysis of data from a school-based survey of adolescents (12 to 15 years) in Mexico. Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire to assess adolescents’ sexual and reproductive health knowledge and communication with parents. The final analytic sample included 911 adolescents with complete information. The primary outcome was contraceptive knowledge, and the main exposure was parent–adolescent communication. We used frequencies and percentages to describe the study variables and logistic regression models to evaluate the association between communication with parents and knowledge of contraceptive methods.RESULTS:Fifty percent of adolescents knew two contraceptive methods. While 86.3% were familiar with oral contraceptive pills, only one-third knew where to obtain them. Adolescents who discussed sex and pregnancy prevention with parents were more likely to know contraceptive methods than those who never discussed these topics.CONCLUSION:Communication with parents is positively associated with contraceptive knowledge, but gaps remain in functional understanding. Gender and cultural differences influence these dynamics, with stronger associations among females and nonspeakers of indigenous languages.

Introduction

Adolescence is a transitional period between childhood and adulthood (ages 10–19), commonly categorized into early (10–14 years), middle (15–18 years), and late adolescence (>19 years), and characterized by profound biological, psychological, and social changes that shape personal identity and behavior.[1] Early adolescence marks the onset of pubertal maturation, driven by activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis and accompanied by increased sexual motivation. At the same time, cognitive and psychosocial capacities related to impulse control, decision-making, and anticipation of consequences (functions largely mediated by the prefrontal cortex) are still developing.[23] This neurodevelopmental imbalance increases adolescents’ vulnerability to risk behaviors, including early sexual initiation, and increases their susceptibility to sexually transmitted infections and unintended pregnancy. During this stage, adolescents also begin to acquire knowledge, attitudes, and expectations regarding sexuality and reproduction, often prior to sexual debut, making early and middle adolescence a particularly relevant stage for examining parental communication and contraceptive knowledge.[23]

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), adolescent pregnancy is defined as a pregnancy occurring in women between the ages of 10 and 19.[4] Although it is a global public health problem, the highest prevalence is observed in low- and middle-income countries, where an estimated 21 million pregnancies occur each year among women aged 15 to 19. Latin America remains one of the most affected regions, with a fertility rate of 51.4 births per 1000 adolescents.[5] Within this regional context, Mexico continues to rank among the countries with the highest adolescent fertility rates, with Chiapas among the states with the highest incidence.[6]

In communities of México, this problem is exacerbated by structural factors such as poverty, educational gaps, gender inequality, and discrimination against indigenous peoples. As one of the most culturally diverse states in the country, Chiapas is home to indigenous peoples who represent more than 25% of the state’s population.[78] This ethnic, linguistic, and worldview diversity poses significant challenges for the design and implementation of public health and sex education policies, which must incorporate intercultural approaches to ensure equitable access to reproductive health information and services. However, despite institutional efforts to implement comprehensive sexuality education programs, many adolescents continue to encounter obstacles to obtaining reliable information about sexuality and contraception.[910] In rural and indigenous communities, these obstacles are compounded by the limited availability of culturally appropriate health services, the geographical distance to care centers, the persistence of myths and taboos surrounding sexuality, and entrenched gender inequalities that restrict adolescents’ autonomy in making decisions about their bodies.[11]

Adolescent pregnancy has profound consequences for young mothers, their families, and their communities. On a personal level, it often interrupts schooling, limits future job opportunities, and increases economic and emotional dependence. From a health perspective, teenage mothers face higher risks of complications during pregnancy and childbirth, as well as a greater likelihood of having children with low birth weight or other health problems. At the social level, early pregnancy perpetuates cycles of poverty and exclusion, disproportionately affecting young indigenous women who already experience multiple forms of inequality.[12]

Adolescent pregnancy is now recognized as a complex phenomenon arising from the interplay of social, structural, and psychosocial determinants, with family dynamics constituting a key domain influencing adolescents’ reproductive health trajectories [Figure 1].[13] Consistent with this perspective, emerging evidence indicates that family functioning and self-esteem are relevant correlates of adolescent pregnancy, highlighting the central role of the familial environment in adolescents’ decision-making processes.[14] Within this framework, parental involvement may represent a key mechanism influencing adolescents’ decision-making and vulnerability to early pregnancy. Parent–child communication about sexual and reproductive health has been identified as a protective factor against risky behaviors as it encourages informed decision-making, strengthens self-esteem, and supports the development of healthy relationships.[1516] Yet, in many families, and more frequently in settings where sexuality is viewed as a forbidden or shameful topic, these opportunities for dialog are limited, vague, or shaped by fear, judgment, and misinformation.[1718] In rural and indigenous contexts, intergenerational communication is further influenced by cultural norms, traditional gender roles, respect for family and community hierarchies, and the oral transmission of knowledge. While these dynamics can offer an opportunity to strengthen sexual education from a community-based perspective, they may also foster silence and omissions when sexuality is perceived as inappropriate or incompatible with traditional values.[1920]

Interacting structural and interpersonal factors in adolescent pregnancy

Against this backdrop, it is essential to examine the extent to which parents convey information about sexual and reproductive health to their adolescent children, and how this communication affects their knowledge and use of contraceptive methods. Understanding this relationship not only helps to identify information gaps but also highlights communication practices that, even in culturally complex contexts, can support health promotion and the prevention of adolescent pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections. Therefore, the present study aimed to analyze the association between parental communication about sexual and reproductive health and adolescents’ knowledge of contraceptive methods in rural communities of Chiapas, Mexico.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study used a cross-sectional design based on the secondary analysis of data from a school-based survey conducted among adolescents in the state of Chiapas, Mexico. The original survey aimed to assess sexual and reproductive health needs, knowledge, and rights among adolescents living in vulnerable rural and semi-rural contexts and has been described in detail elsewhere.[11]

Chiapas is one of the states with the highest adolescent pregnancy rates in Mexico. It is characterized by marked socioeconomic inequalities, high levels of poverty, and a large proportion of indigenous populations. The study was conducted in two municipalities with predominantly rural characteristics, where access to sexual and reproductive health services is limited and cultural norms may influence family communication about sexuality.

Data collection took place in public lower secondary schools, which serve as a key institutional setting for reaching early adolescents in these communities. Information was collected through structured, self-administered questionnaires completed by students during school hours under standardized conditions, with trained field staff present to provide clarification as needed.

Study participants and sampling

The study population consisted of adolescents aged 12 to 15 years enrolled in public lower secondary schools in selected municipalities of Chiapas. For the present secondary analysis, we included data from 911 adolescents with complete information on parent–child communication variables and knowledge of contraceptive methods.

Participants were recruited through a school-based census, in which all eligible students present on the day of data collection were invited to participate. Eligibility criteria included enrolment in participating schools, age within the target range, and assent and written informed consent from a parent or legal guardian, as required by ethical regulations.

No probabilistic sampling was conducted because the original study aimed to capture the full range of adolescent experiences among public school students in these rural contexts. As a result, the sample reflects adolescents who were actively enrolled in school and may not be representative of out-of-school adolescents or those living in other regions of Mexico. Nevertheless, this approach enabled the inclusion of a substantial, diverse sample of adolescents living in settings characterized by cultural diversity and structural vulnerability.

Data collection tool and technique

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire designed to assess adolescents’ sexual and reproductive health knowledge, experiences, and communication with parents. The instrument was derived from previously validated tools used in large-scale national surveys in Mexico and was adapted for use in school-based adolescent populations.

Items assessing knowledge of contraceptive methods were adapted from the National Health and Nutrition Surveys (ENSANUT 2012, 2018–2019, and 2021–2024), which are widely used to monitor sexual and reproductive health indicators among adolescents in Mexico. These questions evaluated recognition of specific contraceptive methods (oral contraceptive pills, intrauterine device, contraceptive implant, and male condom), knowledge of where to obtain them, and functional knowledge related to male condom use, including purpose, frequency of use, and correct timing of placement. A detailed description of the conceptual and operational definitions of the variables is provided in Supplementary Material S1.

Conceptual and operational definitions of study variables

Parent–child communication about sexual and reproductive health was measured using items from the Knowledge and Attitudes Questionnaire on Sexuality, developed by the National Institute of Public Health (INSP) in collaboration with the National Center for the Prevention and Control of HIV and AIDS (CENSIDA). Adolescents were asked whether they had discussed sex, pregnancy prevention, and male condom use with their parents, as well as the frequency of these conversations. Response options for frequency included never, once, rarely, sometimes, and often. For analytical purposes, and to ensure sufficient statistical power across categories, the communication variables were dichotomized into two groups: adolescents who had engaged in these discussions at least once and those who had never discussed these topics with their parents.

All questionnaire items were reviewed by experts in adolescent sexual and reproductive health to ensure clarity, cultural appropriateness, and content validity. The instrument was pilot-tested in a similar adolescent population before implementation, and minor wording adjustments were made to improve comprehension.

Given the sensitive nature of the topic, specific procedures were implemented to ensure confidentiality and privacy. Data were collected using anonymous questionnaires without personal identifiers. The instruments were administered individually on tablet devices with headphones, allowing participants to listen to each question privately and preventing others from overhearing their responses. The resulting database was stored in a password-protected file accessible only to authorized members of the research team, and all analyses were conducted using deidentified data.

Ethical consideration

This secondary analysis was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Research Committee of Universidad Anáhuac, which registered the project under folio 202345.

The original study was previously approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the National Institute of Public Health (approval number 1692). During the primary data collection, written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, and informed assent was obtained from all participating adolescents. All data used in the present analysis were anonymized prior to use to ensure participant confidentiality.

Statistical analysis method

We used frequencies and percentages to describe the quantitative variables (age and number of contraceptive methods known to adolescents). Since these variables did not meet the assumption of normality, as determined by the Shapiro–Wilk test, we summarized them using medians and interquartile ranges.

To evaluate the association between communication with parents and knowledge of contraceptive methods, we fitted separate logistic regression models for each type of parent–child discussion: sex, pregnancy prevention, and male condom use. We estimated odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals for each contraceptive method. The original variables measuring communication frequency had six categories; therefore, we simplified them into two groups: “never talked about it” and “have talked about it at least once.” This dichotomization was necessary to ensure sufficient statistical power to detect associations. In addition, discussion about male condom use was specifically analyzed regarding adolescents’ functional knowledge of this method, including correct usage and its effectiveness in preventing both pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections.

Given that the relationship between parent–child communication and knowledge of contraceptive methods may vary by gender and ethnicity, we assessed potential effect modification (interaction) by these variables. We used speaking an indigenous language as a proxy for ethnicity. Therefore, a multiplicative interaction term, representing knowledge of birth control pills and indigenous-language-speaking status, was independently incorporated into each of the adjusted logistic models.

We adjusted all models for potential confounding variables selected using Directed Acyclic Diagrams (DAGs; Supplementary Material S2).[21] The minimum set of adjustments included age, gender, education, parental education, residence, and household structure. Statistical significance for all models was assessed using a P value threshold of < 0.05. We conducted all analyses using Stata, version 19.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Knowledge of contraceptive methods among adolescents

The median number of contraceptive methods known by adolescents was 3 (IQR = 2). Overall, 86.3% were familiar with oral contraceptive pills; however, only about one-third knew where to obtain them. Similar patterns were observed for other methods, such as the implant and the male condom. An exception was the intrauterine device (IUD), known by fewer than half of adolescents (42.7%), and IUD was the least commonly known method for obtaining it (17.7%). In terms of functional knowledge about male condoms, 51.9% of adolescents understood that condoms are single-use only. Additionally, 64.6% were aware that condoms not only serve as a contraceptive method but also help prevent sexually transmitted infections. However, only 28.1% correctly identified the appropriate timing for condom placement [Table 3]. Overall, contraceptive knowledge showed limited breadth and overlap, with most adolescents recognizing only a small subset of commonly known methods rather than demonstrating comprehensive knowledge across multiple contraceptive options.

Discussion

Our findings suggest that parent–child communication about sexuality, pregnancy prevention, and male condom use is associated with adolescents’ contraceptive knowledge in rural and indigenous contexts of Mexico. Although previous studies in the country have examined parental communication about sexual and reproductive health and, separately, adolescents’ contraceptive knowledge, empirical evidence explicitly linking these two dimensions remains limited. In this regard, the present study contributes to existing literature by analyzing their association within socially and culturally vulnerable settings, where structural inequalities and prevailing cultural norms may influence communication practices as well as access to accurate information.

Among the main findings of this study, between 42% and 95% of adolescents reported knowledge of at least one contraceptive method, with the male condom being the most widely recognized and the intrauterine device the least known. Nevertheless, fewer than 60% knew where to obtain these methods. These results align with data from the National Health and Nutrition Surveys (ENSANUT, its Spanish acronym), which have documented a downward trend in contraceptive knowledge among adolescents between 2018 and 2022.[22] Given that limited knowledge of family planning is associated with a higher risk of unintended pregnancies,[2324] our findings emphasize the need to enhance strategies for disseminating information on sexual and reproductive health to adolescents.

Our findings regarding adolescents’ understanding of male condoms revealed some concerning gaps in functional knowledge. Only half of adolescents knew that condoms can be used only once. Additionally, one-third were unaware that condoms not only serve as a contraceptive method but also prevent sexually transmitted infections, and more than 70% did not know the correct timing for their placement. These results align with national trends, which have shown a decline in functional knowledge of male condoms among adolescents since 2018.[22] The male condom is one of the most widely used family planning methods and directly involves men. Its effectiveness at preventing STIs and unintended pregnancies depends largely on correct use. Therefore, these findings highlight the urgent need to strengthen functional knowledge of the male condom among adolescents.

In this study, we found that fewer than 50% of participants had discussed condom use with their parents at least once, and fewer than 70% had talked to them about sex and pregnancy prevention. These figures are significantly lower than those previously reported among school-going adolescents in Morelos, where 83.1% had discussed sexual relations with their parents at least once.[25] Regional differences in educational attainment may help explain this contrast. For example, in Chiapas, 12% of the population has no formal education, and the average years of schooling is 7.9.[26] In contrast, in Morelos, the average is 9.9 years, and only 4.3% of the population lacks formal education.[27] Parental education has been linked to greater willingness to communicate with children and to the type of knowledge transmitted regarding sexual health, which could partly account for the disparities observed.[2829] Furthermore, the high proportion of adolescents who have never spoken with their parents about sexual and reproductive health may reflect embarrassment, cultural taboos, or parental reluctance to address these topics.[3031] These findings underscore the need to strengthen family-based communication strategies and develop culturally sensitive interventions that reduce barriers to dialog. Promoting open conversations within families could play a crucial role in improving adolescents’ knowledge, fostering safer sexual behaviors, and reducing the risk of unintended pregnancy. However, to break this communication barrier and encourage parental involvement in sexual and reproductive health, it is crucial to implement public policy and educational programs under a rights-based approach. This approach should foster community alliances that involve both sectoral and intersectoral actions among parents, schools, health services, and civil society.

The findings of this study indicate that adolescents who had talked about sex, pregnancy prevention, and condom use with their parents at least once were more likely to know about each of the contraceptive methods evaluated compared with those who had never had such discussions. These results are consistent with previous studies reporting similar associations.[323334] Open communication between parents and children about sexuality can help correct misconceptions, provide accurate information, and foster a supportive environment where adolescents feel comfortable seeking guidance on sensitive issues. This dialog may help reduce risk behaviors, including early sexual initiation, unintended pregnancies, and other reproductive health problems.[35]

No significant association was observed between parental communication and adolescents’ knowledge of where to obtain contraceptive methods, except in the case of male condoms. Similarly, discussions with parents about condom use were not significantly associated with adolescents’ functional understanding of this method. Taken together, these patterns suggest that parent–child communication, when it occurs, may be limited in both scope and depth, often emphasizing general messages rather than practical guidance or access-related information. This constrained communication may reflect a broader context of silence, discomfort, or intergenerational anxiety surrounding sexual and reproductive health. Parents may fear that discussing contraception could be interpreted as encouraging sexual activity, or they may feel inadequately prepared to address these topics due to limited knowledge or confidence. In such contexts, adolescents may rely on peers, social networks, or media as alternative sources of information–channels that often provide fragmented content and may perpetuate misconceptions or misinformation.[3637] Overall, these findings underscore the need for interventions that move beyond simply promoting dialog. Effective strategies must equip parents with accurate, comprehensive, and practical information about a wide range of contraceptive methods, thereby strengthening both the content and potential impact of parent–child communication.

From a policy perspective, these findings highlight an important paradox. Although parent–child communication was consistently associated with greater contraceptive knowledge, overall levels of functional understanding and awareness of where to obtain contraceptive methods remained low. This indicates that communication within the family, while valuable, operates within broader structural constraints that limit adolescents’ ability to translate information into practical, actionable knowledge. In this context, strengthening parental communication alone is unlikely to be sufficient if adolescents continue to face barriers such as limited availability of adolescent-friendly health services, lack of culturally competent care, and inconsistent quality of school-based sexuality education. Addressing these systemic limitations is essential to ensure that adolescents not only receive accurate information at home but also have access to supportive environments and reliable resources that can reinforce and operationalize that information.

These findings underscore the need for coordinated multilevel responses. Although family-based communication plays a meaningful role, it must be complemented by systemic improvements that ensure access to adolescent-friendly sexual and reproductive health services and the provision of high-quality, skills-oriented sexuality education within schools. The observed gaps in functional knowledge and awareness of access-related issues clearly illustrate these limitations. The fact that fewer than one-third of adolescents knew the correct timing for condom placement represents not only a knowledge gap but also a direct risk factor for unintended pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections. This pattern suggests that existing educational efforts may prioritize the identification or naming of contraceptive methods over the delivery of practical, step-by-step guidance needed for their effective use. Likewise, the low proportion of adolescents who knew where to obtain certain contraceptive methods (particularly long-acting reversible contraceptives) highlights structural barriers that render contraceptive knowledge largely theoretical rather than actionable.

Stratified analyses showed that the association between parental communication and knowledge of contraceptive methods, such as pills, the intrauterine device, and the implant, was significantly stronger among females. Adolescent girls’ greater openness may explain this through their discussion of sensitive issues with their parents and by spending more time at home, which facilitates such conversations. Furthermore, by reflecting gender social norms, parents may perceive a greater responsibility, and even social authorization, to regulate their daughters’ sexual and reproductive behavior more closely. Consequently, they may communicate more with them and provide more information, although prohibitions and controls often frame this.

These findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in Mexico[22] and in other countries,[383940] which have reported that adolescent boys tend to have lower levels of knowledge about family planning methods. Prior evidence suggests that these differences may be linked to prevailing social constructions of gender and sexuality, whereby reproductive risk is often perceived as lower for men. In Latin America, the persistence of machismo (a set of patriarchal norms that assign contraceptive responsibility to women) reinforces the belief that preventing pregnancy is primarily a woman’s responsibility, as illustrated by qualitative studies with statements such as: “I thought she was taking care of it.”[41] Masculine identities have also been associated with justifications for not using contraceptives, particularly among adolescents and young men. These justifications include the notion of an uncontrollable sexual drive, the display of virility, and a willingness to take risks in unplanned sexual encounters.[42] Furthermore, the gestational process directly affects women, who often face public scrutiny when becoming pregnant.[43] This societal pressure may explain their heightened awareness of family planning and the risks of sexually transmitted infections. Nevertheless, even when adolescent girls know contraceptive methods, other gender-related factors, such as shame, social sanctions, or feelings of romantic love toward their partners, may influence their decisions to use contraception or request condom use.[44454647]

Compared with females, the magnitude of the association between parental communication and knowledge about the male condom and its sources was significantly greater among males. Since the condom is one of the most widely used methods and is directly used by men, parents may have placed greater emphasis on this topic when talking with their sons than with their daughters.[48] This gender-based pattern of communication reflects socially constructed expectations around sexuality. It highlights the need to promote more comprehensive discussions that address a broader range of contraceptive methods with both sons and daughters.

In this setting, gendered and cultural patterns suggest that generic messages encouraging parents to “talk to their children” about sexuality are unlikely to be sufficient. Parent–child communication does not occur in a social vacuum; rather, it is shaped by deeply embedded gender norms and cultural scripts that assign differential responsibility for contraception to girls and boys. Without explicitly addressing these scripts, communication-based interventions may inadvertently reinforce existing inequalities, framing pregnancy prevention as a female responsibility while limiting boys’ involvement to condom use alone.

Similarly, the weaker associations observed among adolescents who speak an indigenous language suggest that cultural norms, worldviews, and historical experiences may shape both the content and the feasibility of intrafamily dialog. In these contexts, standardized communication campaigns that fail to account for linguistic, cultural, and relational dynamics risk being ineffective. Interventions must therefore move beyond universal messaging and adopt culturally responsive approaches that acknowledge local meanings of sexuality, authority, and trust.

Stratified analyses by indigenous language status revealed that the association between parental communication and knowledge of contraceptive methods was stronger among adolescents who did not speak an indigenous language. Those who speak an indigenous language are likely from indigenous families, where cultural taboos and worldviews regarding sexuality and pregnancy may influence both their own and their parents’ willingness to engage in such conversations.[3031] These findings underscore the importance of developing intercultural approaches that acknowledge and respect indigenous cosmovision while at the same time promoting dialog on sexual and reproductive health, ensuring that adolescents in these communities have equitable access to information and preventive sexual and contraceptive resources.

These stratified findings have clear implications for the design of sexual and reproductive health programs. Gender-sensitive strategies are needed that address the distinct ways in which girls and boys receive and process information within families. For girls, interventions may benefit from supporting parents to move beyond protective or restrictive messaging and toward open, autonomy-supportive communication. For boys, programs should actively challenge norms that position contraception as a female responsibility and promote shared accountability, particularly with respect to the correct and consistent condom use.

Moreover, the differential patterns observed by indigenous language status highlight the importance of tailoring interventions to specific cultural and linguistic contexts. Programs implemented in indigenous communities should prioritize culturally and linguistically adapted materials, engage community leaders and families in co-design processes, and incorporate local concepts and narratives related to sexuality, health, and responsibility. Strengthening parental communication in these settings requires approaches that honor indigenous worldviews while facilitating the transmission of accurate and practical knowledge, ensuring that adolescents are not excluded from effective sexual and reproductive health education due to cultural or linguistic barriers.

Taken together, these findings reflect everyday realities in which many adolescents navigate sexual and reproductive health with partial and uneven information. Conversations with parents, when they occur, tend to be infrequent or limited in scope, and adolescents’ knowledge often appears concentrated on a small number of familiar methods rather than demonstrating a comprehensive understanding of available options. This fragmented informational landscape helps contextualize the observed associations and underscores the complexity of adolescents’ sexual and reproductive health decision-making in rural and indigenous settings.

Strengths and limitations

The cross-sectional design does not allow for establishing a temporal sequence between communication with parents and adolescents’ knowledge of contraceptive methods. Due to this temporal ambiguity, the estimated associations cannot be interpreted as causal and should therefore be considered with caution.

Although the logistic models accounted for several relevant confounding variables, an important limitation of the study is the absence of detailed parental-level information. Specifically, data on parents’ educational attainment, sexual and reproductive health knowledge, and attitudes toward adolescent sexuality were not available. These factors may influence both the likelihood and quality of parent–child communication as well as the accuracy and depth of the information transmitted. Additionally, the study did not distinguish between communication with mothers and fathers, a relevant omission given documented differences in communication patterns, openness, and content by parent gender. The absence of this information limits a more nuanced interpretation of the observed associations and increases the risk of residual confounding.

Another limitation is that the data were obtained through self-report, which may lead to misclassification of both the independent and dependent variables. Social desirability bias, in particular, could result in overreporting of communication or contraceptive knowledge. However, the distribution of knowledge across methods (concentrated mainly on the most familiar options rather than uniformly high across all methods) does not suggest a generalized inflation of responses. In addition, data collection procedures were standardized across all participants, and, because this was a secondary analysis, adolescents were unaware of the specific study hypothesis, reducing the likelihood of differential misclassification. Although some degree of measurement error cannot be fully excluded, these factors lessen the probability that the observed associations are attributable solely to reporting bias.

An important limitation related to generalizability is that the study sample was drawn exclusively from adolescents enrolled in public lower secondary schools. Consequently, out-of-school adolescents who often experience higher levels of structural vulnerability, reduced access to sexual and reproductive health information, and increased risk of early pregnancy were not included. This exclusion may lead to an underestimation of knowledge gaps and communication barriers present in the broader adolescent population and limit the extrapolation of the findings to all adolescents living in rural and indigenous contexts.

Moreover, the social and demographic characteristics of adolescents in Chiapas may differ from those in other regions of the country, particularly in the center and north, which limits the extrapolation of the results. However, the findings may be generalizable to other settings with similar sociocultural characteristics.

In this context, future research would benefit from longitudinal designs that allow for temporal ordering between parental communication and adolescents’ contraceptive knowledge, as well as from the inclusion of parental-level characteristics, such as educational attainment, sexual and reproductive health knowledge, and attitudes toward adolescent sexuality. Additionally, studies that include out-of-school adolescents and employ qualitative or mixed-methods approaches could provide a more comprehensive understanding of communication processes and the contextual barriers that shape adolescents’ sexual and reproductive health in rural and indigenous settings.

Despite these limitations, the study has several important strengths. It draws on a large sample of early adolescents from culturally diverse and structurally vulnerable settings, a population that remains underrepresented in the literature. The use of standardized instruments derived from national surveys allowed for a detailed assessment of both recognition and functional knowledge of contraceptive methods. Moreover, the analytic approach adjusted for multiple confounders and examined effect modification by gender and indigenous language status, providing insight into how social and cultural factors shape the relationship between family communication and contraceptive knowledge. Taken together, these strengths enhance the relevance of the findings for informing culturally sensitive, multilevel interventions to improve adolescent sexual and reproductive health.

Conclusion

This study highlights the relevance of parent–child communication in shaping adolescents’ knowledge of contraceptive methods in rural and indigenous communities of Mexico. Our findings show that adolescents who reported having conversations with their parents about sexuality, pregnancy prevention, and condom use tended to report higher levels of contraceptive knowledge. Nevertheless, substantial gaps persist, particularly in relation to functional understanding of the male condom use and awareness of where contraceptive methods can be obtained. Additionally, differences by gender and cultural context were also observed, suggesting that broader social and cultural factors shape these dynamics.

Rather than indicating a causal relationship, these results point to parent–child communication as a meaningful context in which information about sexual and reproductive health is shared and negotiated. In this sense, strengthening family-based communication should be understood not merely as encouraging parents to “talk more” but as supporting them to communicate in ways that are informed, respectful, and sensitive to their own concerns, beliefs, and uncertainties. Providing parents with accessible information and basic communication tools may help improve the content and clarity of these conversations, even in settings where sexuality remains a difficult topic to address.

The findings also underscore the importance of intercultural perspectives, particularly in indigenous communities, where cultural norms, language, and worldviews influence how sexuality is discussed within families. Approaches that actively engage indigenous communities in the design of educational strategies, draw on culturally meaningful narratives, and rely on trusted local actors may facilitate dialog while respecting cultural values.

Finally, the observed associations suggest that efforts to improve adolescents’ sexual and reproductive health knowledge would benefit from multilevel strategies. Family communication can be complemented by adolescent-friendly, culturally competent health services and by comprehensive sexuality education in schools that emphasizes practical knowledge and skills. Together, these aligned efforts may help address persistent knowledge gaps and support adolescents as they navigate sexual and reproductive health decisions in contexts of social and cultural vulnerability.
