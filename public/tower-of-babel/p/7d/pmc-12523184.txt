Epigenetic regulation by WTAPP1 promotes trophoblast dysfunction in gestational diabetes via CNR1/NF-κB activation
Authors: Na Yang, Yuan Li, Jiefang Zhang, Xianghong Gao, Peng Zhang, Weiling Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) poses significant risks to maternal and fetal health, necessitating exploration of novel molecular mechanisms. Clinical data from placental tissues from 33 GDM patients and 30 controls revealed Wilms tumor 1 associated protein (WTAP) pseudogene 1 (WTAPP1) was significantly upregulated in GDM, correlating with fasting glucose levels. In vitro, high glucose (25 mM) triggered oxidative stress (manifested by increased MDA, reduced GSH/GSSG ratio, and decreased CAT and SOD activity), inflammation (evidenced by elevated secretion of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-1), and apoptosis (along with upregulated cleaved caspase-3 or Bax, and downregulated Bcl-2) in trophoblast cell HTR-8/Svneo, while WTAPP1 knockdown partially restored viability, reduced apoptosis, and mitigated inflammatory cytokines and oxidative markers. Mechanistically, WTAPP1 promoted cannabinoid receptor 1 (CNR1) expression via WTAP-mediated m6A methylation, stabilizing CNR1 mRNA and activating the NF-κB signaling, as evidenced by increased phosphorylated IkBα and nuclear p65. Rescue experiments confirmed that CNR1 overexpression reversed the protective effects of WTAPP1 knockdown on high-glucose-induced damage in HTR-8/Svneo cells, while NF-κB inhibition by BAY-11-7085 attenuated high glucose-induced damage. This study unveils a novel WTAPP1/WTAP–m6A–CNR1–NF-κB axis driving trophoblast cell injury in GDM, highlighting WTAPP1 as a potential therapeutic target for hyperglycemia-induced placental injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03217-8.Keywords: WTAPP1, Gestational diabetes mellitus, CNR1, Trophoblast cells, M6A

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) refers to glucose metabolism abnormalities occurring during pregnancy, characterized by elevated blood glucose levels that do not meet the diagnostic criteria for overt diabetes. As a pregnancy-specific disorder, GDM significantly increases the risk of adverse perinatal outcomes for both mother and fetus, as well as long-term metabolic diseases in both the mother and offspring [19, 23, 49]. Its incidence has been rising annually. Current clinical management of GDM primarily includes lifestyle interventions on diet and exercise and, when necessary, glucose-lowering medications [36]. However, these approaches have limitations: lifestyle interventions are challenging to maintain, with variable effectiveness across individuals [35]. Insulin therapy involves complex administration and carries risks of hypoglycemia, leading to poor patient compliance [16]. Oral hypoglycemic agents like metformin and glyburide face safety controversies and are not universally approved for GDM treatment [12, 39]. Traditional Chinese medicine has shown potential in alleviating symptoms and controlling blood glucose, but lacks support from large-scale clinical trials and exhibits significant individual variability [17]. The pathogenesis of GDM remains incompletely understood, and exploring its molecular mechanisms could facilitate earlier diagnosis and personalized therapies.

Cannabinoid receptor 1 (CNR1), a core component of the endocannabinoid system, regulates diverse physiological functions [2, 7]. Cellular stress, such as oxidative damage and inflammation, can induce changes in CNR1 expression [29, 30]. Studies suggest its involvement in diabetes, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory modulation [1, 33, 46]. CNR1 knockout has been reported to suppress oxidative stress [47], enhance insulin sensitivity, and reduce blood glucose [5]. Notably, CNR1 gene variants are associated with preeclampsia in Central European populations [3]. Further studies demonstrated that mice with hyperactive endocannabinoid signaling are prone to LPS-induced preterm birth and decidual senescence, while CNR1 antagonism ameliorates these effects [43]. Cannabinoid signaling also inhibits decidualization of endometrial stromal cells and impairs trophoblast–endometrium interactions [31]. However, the role and mechanisms of CNR1 in GDM and high glucose-conditioned trophoblast cells remain unexplored.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A), a key RNA epigenetic modification, has recently been implicated in GDM pathogenesis and complications [8, 9, 45]. Dysregulated m6A-related genes correlate closely with glucose levels, while aberrant m6A modifications exacerbate inflammation, insulin resistance, placental dysfunction, and metabolic disorders, promoting GDM development [8, 9, 11, 26]. The dynamic reversibility of m6A highlights its potential as a diagnostic tool and therapeutic target [27]. Wilms tumor 1 associated protein (WTAP), a core subunit of the m6A methyltransferase complex, is essential for maintaining the catalytic activity of METTL3–METTL14 heterodimers [34]. WTAP dysfunction disrupts global m6A patterns, leading to aberrant gene expression in various diseases, including cancers and metabolic disorders [4, 13]. Pseudogenes, once considered "non-coding junk DNA", have emerged as key regulators of gene expression by sequestering miRNAs, interacting with proteins, or modulating parental gene transcription [42]. WTAPP1, a pseudogene of WTAP, was initially identified in cancer studies [41, 50], where it enhances WTAP protein translation by stabilizing WTAP mRNA or competing with inhibitory factors [6]. Our preliminary studies analyzing transcriptomic datasets from placental samples (GEO database) revealed significantly elevated expression of WTAPP1 and CNR1 in GDM patients versus non-GDM controls. Given that WTAPP1 promotes WTAP translation, and bioinformatics predictions identified abundant m6A modification sites in CNR1 mRNA, we hypothesize that WTAPP1 may regulate CNR1 expression through m6A modification, thereby participating in GDM progression. This study aims to validate this mechanism, with the potential to highlight WTAPP1/m6A/CNR1 as diagnostic biomarkers for early GDM detection and therapeutic targets to address current management limitations, ultimately providing novel molecular bases for personalized GDM therapies.

Materials and methods

Clinic data

Placental tissues from 30 healthy volunteers (NC) and 33 GDM patients admitted to the obstetrics department from January 2022 to May 2023 were collected for research. The sampling site was the maternal side of the placenta (villous tissues adjacent to the decidual contact area), avoiding the marginal and calcified regions. Inclusion criteria were singleton pregnancy, age 20–40 years, no insulin use in the past month, and no other gestational complications (e.g., preeclampsia or placental abnormalities), severe organic diseases, infections, tumors, and pre-existing diabetes or hypertension. GDM was diagnosed in women without prior diabetes who, at 24–28 weeks of gestation, had a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) with any of the following: fasting blood glucose between 5.1 and 7.0 mmol/L, 1-h OGTT glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-h OGTT glucose between 8.5 and 11.1 mmol/L. The clinical characteristics of these women included in this study are listed in Table 1, and there were no significant differences in age, BMI, gestational age, and parity between the two groups.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cells using Trizol reagent. The PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara, Dalian, China) was used for reverse transcription. Quantitative PCR was then performed using the SYBR reagent (Qiagen, Dusseldorf, Germany). The reaction program was: 95 ℃ for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 ℃ for 15 s and 60 ℃ for 30 s. β-actin was used as the internal reference gene, and the 2−ΔΔCt method was applied to calculate the relative expression levels of the target genes (WTAPP1, CNR1 and WTAP). Primers for WTAPP1 were 5’-CAAATCTGTGACAATGCCCC-3’(forward) and 5’-GTTATGACTCAAGCGAAAATGG-3’(reverse), WTAP were 5’-TTCCCAAGAAGGTTCGATTG-3’(forward) and 5’-TGCAGACTCCTGCTGTTGTT-3’(reverse), CNR1 were 5’-CAAGGAGAATGAGGAGAACA-3’(forward) and 5’-CCAGCGTGAGGGACAGGACT-3’(reverse), and β-actin were 5’-TGACGTGGACATCCGCAAAG-3’(forward) and 5’-CTGGAAGGTGGACAGCGAGG-3’(reverse). All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Company (Shanghai, China). Primer efficiencies (95–109%) were determined from standard curves generated with fivefold serial dilutions of pooled cDNA (slope = − 3.28 ± 0.15, R2 > 0.998). Specificity was confirmed by single-peak melt curves (1.5 °C/s ramp from 65 to 95 °C). No-template controls and no-reverse transcription controls were included in each run. No-template controls showed no amplification (Ct > 35 cycles). No-reverse transcription showed no amplification, indicating negligible genomic DNA contamination.

Cells

Human embryonic trophoblast cells HTR-8/SVneo (CellCook, Guangzhou, China) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, USA, 11,879,020) supplemented with indicated concentrations of glucose, 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Zeye, Shanghai, China). The cell culture conditions were 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Cell treatment

The WTAPP1 knockdown vectors, CNR1 overexpression vectors, and WTAP knockdown vectors were synthesized by GeneCopoeia Company. They were transfected into HTR-8/Svneo cells (pre-cultured to 70–90% confluence) using the EndoFectin™ Max Transfection Reagent (GeneCopoeia, Guangzhou, China). Cells were harvested for testing 48 h post-transfection.

To create an in vitro GDM model, HTR-8/Svneo cells were stimulated with 12.5 mM, 25 mM, and 50 mM glucose for 24 h, with 5.5 mM as the normal control. The base medium used was Gibco’s Glucose-Free Basal Medium, to which additional glucose was supplemented. In later experiments, 25 mM glucose was used as the high-glucose condition. To inactivate the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling, cells were treated with 10 μM BAY-11–7085 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at the same time as high glucose inducement.

m6A-RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

The m6A modification level of CNR1 was analyzed using the m6A RIP Kit (CST, Boston, USA). Total RNA from tissues or cells was treated with RNA fragmentation buffer, then incubated at 70℃ for 5 min and cooled on ice. It was incubated with m6A antibody or IgG antibody (1:50 diluted) on a rotating mixer at 4 ℃ for 2 h, followed by the addition of 50 μL of Protein A/G magnetic beads and further incubation for 1 h. The magnetic beads were collected on a magnetic rack, washed, and the RNA on the beads was eluted with 100 μL of elution buffer. The purified RNA was analyzed by qPCR to determine the content of CNR1 mRNA (n = 3 independent experiments).

Cell viability analysis

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK8 kit (Beyotime). HTR-8/Svneo cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells per well. Then, 10μL CCK8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, with the cell survival rate of the normal glucose-cultured group set as 100% for normalization (n = 3 independent experiments).

Cell apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis of HTR-8/Svneo cells was analyzed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Cells were resuspended in 1 × binding buffer at 1 × 106 cells/ml. Then, 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC Reagent and 5 μL of PI Reagent were added to 100 μL cell suspension, and the mixture was incubated for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were analyzed on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer with ≥ 10,000 events acquired per replicate. Cells were gated based on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) to exclude debris, followed by doublet discrimination using FSC-Width and FSC-Height. Apoptotic populations were identified as Annexin V⁺/PI⁻ (early apoptosis) and Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ (late apoptosis) populations. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo Software v10.8 (n = 3 independent experiments).

Western blot analysis

Total proteins from tissues and cells were isolated using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China), separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ℃ and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated immunoglobulin G (IgG) secondary antibodies for 2 h at 37 ℃. After washing, the membranes were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Thermo, Shanghai, China), and the band intensities of target proteins were analyzed by imaging systems. Protein expression was normalized to the internal reference protein β-actin or Lamin B (n = 3 independent experiments). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) detection

After 24 h of culture, cell supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 2500 rpm and 4 ℃ for 10 min. ELISA kits (Elabscience) were used to analyze the levels of inflammatory cytokines interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β, E-HSEL-H0001), interleukin-6 (IL-6, E-HSEL-H0003), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α, E-EL-H0109), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1, E-EL-H6005) in the supernatants. Samples and biotinylated detection antibodies were added to microplates according to the kit instructions, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader after color development (n = 3 independent experiments).

Assessment of oxidative stress

Oxidative stress levels in HTR-8/Svneo cells were analyzed using detection kits for malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH)/glutathione disulfide (GSSG), catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), all purchased from Elabscience (MDA: E-BC-K814-M; GSH/GSSG: E-BC-K097-S; CAT: E-BC-K031-M; SOD: E-BC-K020-M) (n = 3 independent experiments).

RNA stability detection

Cells were treated with 5 μg/mL actinomycin D (Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China) and harvested before treatment (0 h) and at 3, 6, 12 h post-actinomycin D exposure for RNA extraction. All RNA samples met the criterion of RNA integrity number ≥ 8.0 as determined by the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100, with 28S/18S ratios maintained between 1.8 and 2.1. The RNA was subsequently used for RT-qPCR analysis, with data normalized to 18S rRNA and HPRT1, using the 0-h time point as the reference (n = 3 independent experiments).

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD and analyzed using Prism 9.0 software. Homogeneity of variances was tested by Levene’s test, and normality was assessed by Shapiro–Wilk test combined with Q–Q plots. For normally distributed and homogeneous variance data: one-way analysis of variance was used for multi-group comparisons, followed by Tukey HSD test for post hoc comparisons if significant; independent-sample t-test (for homogeneous variance) or Welch’s t-test (for heterogeneous variance) was used for two-group comparisons. For non-normal or heterogeneous variance data: Kruskal–Wallis test for multi-group comparisons and Mann–Whitney U test for two-group comparisons. Differences with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

WTAPP1 was up-regulated in GDM patients and high glucose-induced trophoblast cells

In our preliminary study, we screened the GDM dataset GSE263483 and found that the pseudogene WTAPP1 was significantly upregulated in GDM women’s placental samples, compared with healthy non-GDM women’s placental samples (Fig. 1A). This result was then validated by RT-qPCR in placental samples from clinical GDM patients (Fig. 1B), and the results also show that WTAPP1 expression was proportional to fasting blood glucose levels (Fig. 1C). Subsequently, we stimulated the trophoblast cell line HTR-8/Svneo in vitro with different glucose concentrations. As the glucose concentration increased from 12.5 to 50 mM, cell viability dropped significantly while WTAPP1 expression rose markedly (Fig. 1D, E), indicating WTAPP1’s potential involvement in high-glucose-induced trophoblast cell damage.

WTAPP1 knockdown alleviated oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis in high glucose-induced trophoblast cells

To verify WTAPP1’s role in high-glucose-induced trophoblast cell damage, we transfected HTR-8/Svneo cells with WTAPP1 knockdown vectors #1, #2, and #3. RT-qPCR confirmed vector #2 had the best knockdown effect (Fig. 2A), so we used it in subsequent experiments. HTR-8/Svneo cells transfected with WTAPP1 knockdown and negative control vectors were cultured under normal and high-glucose conditions. Results showed WTAPP1 knockdown partially restored cell viability and reduced apoptosis under high-glucose stimulation, as evidenced by decreased cleaved caspase 3 and Bax protein expression and increased Bcl-2 expression (Fig. 2B–D). Also, WTAPP1 knockdown significantly increased the secretion of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-1) in high-glucose-stimulated cells (Fig. 2E). Moreover, WTAPP1 knockdown reduced MDA production, but increased the GSH/GSSG ratio and the activity of antioxidant enzymes CAT and SOD in high-glucose conditions (Fig. 2F). Rescue experiments were performed by re-expressing WTAPP1 in knockdown vector #2-transfected trophoblasts. As shown in new Figure S1A-B, WTAPP1 levels were fully restored (rescue efficiency: 119.83 ± 1.04%), and the restored cell viability induced by knockdown vector was significantly rescued (118.47 ± 9.78%). Collectively, these experiments indicate that WTAPP1 promotes inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in trophoblast cells under high-glucose environments.

WTAPP1 silence downregulated the expression of CNR1 and the NF-κB signaling pathway

In our previous differential gene analysis of the GEO dataset, besides WTAPP1, we found CNR1 expression was also significantly upregulated in GDM samples, compared to normal samples (Fig. 3A). CNR1 is reported to be associated with diabetes, insulin sensitivity, and inflammation regulation [14, 15]. RT-qPCR of clinical placental samples further confirmed that CNR1 mRNA was upregulated in GDM patients and positively correlated with WTAPP1 expression (Fig. 3B). Western blot revealed that in GDM patients, CNR1 protein expression, IKBα and p65 phosphorylation, and nuclear p65 content were increased compared to controls (Fig. 3C). Similarly, high glucose upregulated CNR1 mRNA and protein expression in HTR-8/Svneo cells, promoting the phosphorylation of IKBα and p65 and the nuclear translocation of p65 (Fig. 3C). However, WTAPP1 knockdown significantly inhibited CNR1 expression and NF-κB pathway activation in high-glucose conditions.

WTAPP1 promoted CNR1 expression in an m6A-dependent manner

To explore how WTAPP1 regulates CNR1 expression, we conducted bioinformatics analysis using an online SRAMP tool. This predicted abundant m6A modification sites in CNR1 (Fig. 4A). RIP assays confirmed upregulated CNR1 m6A modification in placental tissues of GDM patients and in high-glucose-stimulated HTR-8/Svneo cells, compared with normal controls (Fig. 4B). Interestingly, WTAPP1 knockdown reduced CNR1 m6A modification in cells. We then measured the expression of WTAP, an m6A-related gene linked to WTAPP1 [6, 44]. Results showed WTAP protein was upregulated in GDM placental tissues and high-glucose-induced HTR-8/Svneo cells, with no mRNA change (Fig. 4C, D). Then, the CNR1 3'UTR containing either the wild-type m6A motif or a mutant m6A motif was cloned downstream of the luciferase reporter gene in the pmirGLO vector to construct wild-type and mutant reporter plasmids, respectively. These reporter plasmids were then co-transfected with either a negative control or a WTAPP1 knockdown vector into HTR-8/SVneo cells for 48 h. Dual-luciferase reporter gene system showed that only in the reporter vector containing the CNR1 wild-type m6A sites did knockdown of WTAPP1 significantly inhibit luciferase activity (Fig S1C). In contrast, when the m6A sites were mutated, the regulatory effect of WTAPP1 was completely abolished. Moreover, compared to the wild-type construct, the mutant reporter plasmid with disrupted m6A sites exhibited significantly reduced luciferase activity. These findings directly demonstrate that WTAPP1-mediated post-transcriptional regulation of CNR1 strictly depends on m6A modification within its 3'UTR. Similarly, WTAPP1 knockdown decreased WTAP protein but not mRNA in normal-glucose or high-glucose-induced HTR-8/Svneo cells (Fig. 4C, D). To verify if WTAPP1 regulates CNR1 via WTAP-mediated m6A modification, we overexpressed WTAPP1 and knocked down WTAP in HTR-8/Svneo cells. Overexpressed WTAPP1 increased CNR1 expression and m6A modification, which was reversed by WTAP knockdown (Fig. 4E, F). After transcriptional inhibition, CNR1 mRNA degradation slowed in WTAPP1-overexpressed cells, while WTAP knockdown counteracted the stabilization effect of WTAPP1 overexpression on CNR1 mRNA (Fig. 4G). Western blot confirmed that WTAPP1 overexpression upregulated the protein expression of WTAP and CNR1, and activated the NF-κB signaling, which was reversed by WTAP knockdown (Fig. 4H–J). These results indicate that WTAPP1 stabilizes CNR1 in a WTAP/m6A-dependent manner and activates the downstream NF-κB signaling.

Blocking the WTAPP1/CNR1/NF-κB signaling reduced high glucose-induced cellular damage in trophoblast cells

To confirm whether WTAPP1 influences high-glucose-induced trophoblast cell damage through the CNR1–NF-κB axis, we performed in vitro rescue experiments. Specifically, HTR-8/SVneo cells were co-transfected with a WTAPP1 knockdown vector and a CNR1 overexpression vector. These stably transfected cells were then treated with the NF-κB inhibitor BAY-11-7085 and exposed to high glucose for 24 h. Under high-glucose conditions, compared with HTR-8/SVneo cells with WTAPP1 knockdown alone, cells subjected to both WTAPP1 knockdown and CNR1 overexpression exhibited significantly increased CNR1 protein levels and reactivation of the NF-κB pathway. Notably, treatment with the NF-κB inhibitor BAY-11-7085 effectively suppressed NF-κB pathway activation in the high glucose-treated cells with both WTAPP1 silencing and CNR1 overexpression (Fig. 5A, B). Phenotypically, WTAPP1 knockdown alone rescued 72.8–92.2% of high glucose-induced damage across core phenotypes (Fig. 6A–E and Table S1), confirming its protective role. CNR1 co-expression abolished the protective effects of WTAPP1 knockdown on high glucose-exposed cells, as evidenced by a reduction in cell viability to 143.6 ± 11.7%, an elevation in apoptosis to 102.4 ± 4.6%, and deregulation of apoptotic mediators (with increased cleaved caspase-3 and Bax expression, and decreased Bcl-2) within the range of 74.0–139.6% (Fig. 6A–C). Concurrently, pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-1; 93.0 ~ 116.0%) and oxidative stress markers (MDA; 118.8 ± 18.1%) were elevated, with concomitant reduction in antioxidant capacity (GSH/GSSG ratio, CAT and SOD; 87.8 ~ 119.5%) (Fig. 6D–E and Table S1). Critically, all injury phenotypes induced by CNR1 rescue were reversed by BAY-11-7085 treatment, with recovery levels ranging from 71.2–115.3%. Most decisively, NF-κB inhibition by BAY-11-7085 achieved near-complete reversal (71.2 ~ 115.3%) of all CNR1-induced injuries, with core phenotypes showing more than 100% rescue (viability: 102.4 ± 4.6%, apoptosis: 103.5 ± 5.9%). This quantitative hierarchy establishes CNR1-NF-κB as an obligatory downstream axis in WTAPP1 signal transduction. In summary, these results confirm that WTAPP1 promotes high-glucose-induced inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in trophoblast cells through activating the CNR1/NF-κB pathway.

Discussion

GDM is highly heterogeneous, involving interactions among genetic, epigenetic, metabolic, and other factors [18, 37]. In GDM, high glucose-induced apoptosis of trophoblast cells reduces cell numbers, directly impairing placental function; inflammatory responses trigger local immune imbalance in the placenta, exacerbating disorders at the maternal–placental interface; and oxidative stress damages cellular structure and function, further aggravating the injury [22]. Our study identified that WTAPP1 is highly expressed in placental tissues of GDM patients, and its knockdown promotes oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis in trophoblast cells under high-glucose conditions by suppressing the CNR1–NF-κB axis. This regulatory molecular axis, reported for the first time, may provide a novel biomarker for GDM diagnosis and treatment. While WTAPP1 may regulate additional targets, its axis with CNR1–NF-κB contributes to > 70% of GDM-related trophoblast impairment in our models.

As a non-coding pseudogene, WTAPP1 participates in the progression of various diseases, particularly cancers, through RNA-level regulatory mechanisms, with most studies focusing on its role in tumors [6, 25, 44]. The role of WTAPP1 in other pregnancy disorders is yet to be clarified, but its induction dependent on hyperglycemia points to a unique link with diabetic pathophysiology. Since it promotes the translation of WTAP, a critical component of the m6A methyltransferase complex [6, 44], WTAPP1 indirectly regulates m6A methylation-dependent gene expression via WTAP [13]. Our findings demonstrate that WTAPP1 enhances m6A modification of CNR1 by WTAP, promoting CNR1 transcript stability. This methylation-mediated mRNA stabilization mechanism aligns with previous studies. For example, Martina Rossi et al. report that the METTL3/METTL14 complex enhances m6A modification on PTCHD4 mRNA to stabilize it and promote expression during cellular senescence [38]. Intriguingly, WTAP is also reported to be upregulated in diabetic nephropathy where it promotes NLRP3 inflammasome activation in an m6A-dependent manner [24]. A separate study reports that high glucose elevates WTAP expression in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, exerting oncogenic effects through m6A-mediated stabilization of HK2 mRNA [32]. This stabilization mechanism likely involves recruitment of RNA-stabilizing proteins (e.g., HuR) that enhance transcript stability of target genes [4].

CNR1 was also found to be significantly upregulated in GDM transcriptome datasets and in our RT-qPCR analysis of clinical placental samples. CNR1 overactivation is implicated in the pathogenesis of diabetes and its complications [21]. Animal studies demonstrate that CNR1 inhibition alleviates diabetes-induced renal dysfunction and retinopathy [10, 20, 33]. Mechanistically, CNR1 knockdown exerts dual protective effects: (1) preserving pancreatic β-cell viability by shielding against metabolic stress-induced damage and attenuating islet inflammation, thereby reversing β-cell dysfunction and diabetic progression [1]; (2) directly enhancing target cell survival under hyperglycemic conditions through downregulation of oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling pathways [10]. Our study reveals that CNR1 promotes NF-κB expression, a key signaling pathway involved in inflammation and oxidative stress in GDM [48]. Activation of NF-κB triggers excessive release of inflammatory cytokines, which exacerbate cellular damage by interfering with insulin receptor signaling and upregulating oxidative stress-related molecules, thereby impairing glucose metabolism [28, 40, 51]. These researches support that the CNR1–NF-κB axis drives inflammation, oxidative stress, and trophoblast dysfunction under hyperglycemic conditions. There are several limitations warrant consideration: (1) although HTR-8/SVneo enables rigorous mechanistic dissection, its immortalized nature may not capture full in vivo complexity. Future studies will prioritize primary trophoblast validation pending ethical access to gestational samples. (2) While our findings from human trophoblasts provide mechanistic insights, the systemic developmental implications require future validation in ethically constrained models such as primate pregnancies or advanced in vitro bionic systems. (3) To explore whether the abnormal regulation of WTAPP1 is related to pregnancy, future studies should compare its expression patterns in pregnancy complications such as preeclampsia. (4) While we established CNR1 as a key regulatory node, transcriptomic or RIP-seq analyses will be essential to map WTAPP1's complete interactome and identify auxiliary partners. (5) Our bulk tissue analysis could not distinguish cell-type-specific contributions. Future spatial transcriptomics or single-cell studies are needed to resolve the exact cellular sources of dysregulated genes.

In summary, our study discovers that WTAPP1 is highly expressed in GDM patients and highlights a novel regulatory mechanism in GDM pathogenesis, where WTAPP1, through its interaction with WTAP-mediated m6A methylation, enhances the stability and expression of CNR1 mRNA and activates the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby alleviating high glucose-induced oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis in trophoblast cells (Fig. S2). These findings identify a novel WTAPP1–CNR1–NF-κB regulatory axis involved in trophoblast damage in GDM, advancing our understanding of its pathogenesis. Crucially, this axis represents a druggable target for clinical intervention. Targeting key nodes (e.g., WTAPP1 or CNR1) with specific modalities, such as placenta-tropic antisense oligonucleotides or small-molecule inhibitors (e.g., CNR1 selective antagonist Rimonabant), could alleviate GDM-related placental impairment.
