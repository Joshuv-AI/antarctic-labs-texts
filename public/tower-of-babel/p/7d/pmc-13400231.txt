Artificial intelligence beyond perceptions: Trust as the missing link in primary care
Authors: Marilou D Alngag, Marlita E Quesada, Pinky Larcelle L Gas-ib
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the systematic review by Prat-Carreño et al. examining the social effects and controversies surrounding artificial intelligence (AI) in primary care (PC).1 Through the identification of ten themes – including ethics, physician–patient relationships, governance, bias and equity, professional and patient perceptions, and future imaginaries – the authors provide a valuable overview of the social dimensions of AI in primary care. Most notably, they reveal a considerable gap between studies focused on expectations and perceived potentialities and those examining the real-world social effects of AI implementation.

We would like to expand this discussion by proposing that trust may represent the missing link between AI potential and AI adoption in primary care. The themes identified by Prat-Carreño et al. suggest that the success of AI is unlikely to depend solely on technical performance. Rather, it may depend on whether patients and healthcare professionals trust the systems that increasingly inform clinical decisions, influence healthcare delivery, and shape patient experiences. Trust is not merely an ethical consideration; it is a practical determinant of whether AI-supported recommendations are accepted, questioned, or integrated into routine clinical care.

This perspective is particularly relevant because many current discussions surrounding AI continue to emphasize innovation, efficiency, and future possibilities. However, technological promises do not automatically translate into acceptance or meaningful use. Trust is built when AI systems are perceived as transparent, explainable, fair, accountable, and aligned with patient-centered values.2, 3, 4 Conversely, concerns regarding algorithmic bias, privacy, opacity, and accountability may weaken confidence and limit adoption, even when technological performance is high.

The findings reported by Prat-Carreño et al. further suggest that much of the existing literature remains concentrated on perceptions and prototype evaluations rather than routine implementation.1 This observation raises an important question: how can trust be established when many AI systems have yet to demonstrate their social consequences in everyday primary care practice? Without evidence demonstrating positive effects on patient relationships, professional judgment, and health equity, AI may struggle to achieve the legitimacy required for sustainable integration into healthcare systems.

In this context, we propose the concept of human-centered readiness, defined as the capacity of healthcare institutions and professionals to integrate AI while preserving trust, transparency, ethical accountability, and human-centered care. Human-centered readiness extends beyond governance or technical competence alone. It encompasses organizational preparedness, AI literacy, ethical competence, explainability, patient engagement, and the preservation of meaningful clinician–patient relationships during technological adoption.

For primary care professionals, readiness should include the ability to explain AI-assisted recommendations, critically evaluate algorithmic outputs, protect patient privacy, and maintain shared decision-making processes. Human judgment, empathy, contextual understanding, and continuity of care remain essential features of primary care that cannot be fully delegated to algorithmic systems.

We congratulate Prat-Carreño et al. for drawing attention to the social dimensions of AI in primary care. Future research should move beyond perceptions and prototype evaluations toward empirical investigations of how AI influences trust, professional practice, patient experiences, and health equity in real-world settings. Ultimately, the successful integration of AI in primary care may depend less on technological sophistication than on the ability of healthcare systems to cultivate and sustain trust. Human-centered readiness should therefore be regarded as a prerequisite – not a consequence – of responsible AI adoption.
