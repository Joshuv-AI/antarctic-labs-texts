Late Phenotypic Expression of Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Aakash Bavishi, Robyn Bryde, Matthew W. Martinez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Guidelines recommend clinical assessment, an electrocardiogram, and imaging every 3 to 5 years in adults who are genotype positive, phenotype negative for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) or who have a first-degree relative with genotype-negative HCM. The age to stop screening is unclear. We report 4 cases of individuals transitioning from phenotype-negative to phenotype-positive HCM at various ages.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is an autosomal dominant cardiac myocyte disease caused by sequence variations in sarcomere genes encoding for contractile function. HCM demonstrates significant clinical and genetic heterogeneity, incomplete penetrance, and variable expressivity.1 Given the genetic nature of the disease, appropriate screening of first-degree family members with both genetic testing and cardiac imaging is essential. The 2024 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) HCM guidelines recommend clinical assessment, electrocardiogram (ECG), and cardiac imaging every 3 to 5 years in adults who are genotype positive, phenotype negative for HCM or who have a first-degree relative with genotype-negative HCM.2 The age to stop screening is not well defined and is controversial.Take-Home Messages•Although most diagnoses of HCM are made in adolescence and in the second and third decades of life, phenotypic expression of HCM can occur at older ages.•The ideal age at which to stop screening individuals with genotype-positive, phenotype-negative HCM or first-degree family members of individuals with HCM is unclear and requires further study.

Historically, it has been thought that most individuals who will phenotypically express HCM will do so at a younger age, namely during adolescence or their second or third decade of life.3, 4, 5, 6 Studies evaluating family screening of individuals with HCM have shown the highest yield at the initial screening, with subsequently lower yields with further testing.7 Although contemporary HCM guidelines do not explicitly state an age to stop screening for HCM, many physicians in practice stop once a patient reaches a certain age in the belief that it is low yield and “safe to stop screening.” The popular physician reference website UpToDate,8 in fact, states: “Adults (aged >18 years) with a normal echocardiogram should have screening approximately every three to five years. If there are no signs of abnormal heart function by the early 40s, screening can stop.”8

In this case series, we present 4 cases of adults of varying age, sex, genetic sequence variations, and comorbidities who transitioned from phenotype negative to phenotype positive for HCM. This series highlights the variable onset of phenotypic expression in HCM and raises questions on when and whether screening for HCM should be stopped.

Case 1

A 51-year-old man presented to his cardiologist after an abnormal ECG at his primary care physician’s office. He had no cardiac symptoms, and an ECG was ordered for routine surveillance (Figure 1C). His ECG showed sinus rhythm with left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and anterolateral T-wave inversions. His previous ECGs performed for each of the previous years showed normal sinus rhythm with no abnormalities (Figure 1A) and normal sinus rhythm with nonspecific T-wave flattening (Figure 1B). Case 2

A 69-year-old woman with a long history of hypertension (HTN) presented to her cardiologist because of an abnormal ECG and for evaluation of possible HCM. She stated that she had no current cardiac symptoms. Her current ECG prompting the cardiology referral demonstrated sinus rhythm with left atrial enlargement and LVH with repolarization abnormalities (Figure 3C). This represented a change from previous ECGs 5 years and 28 months earlier, respectively (Figures 3A and 3B). She also underwent TTE and CMR approximately 36 months before her current presentation. The TTE showed mild concentric hypertrophy with a maximum wall thickness of 11 mm (Figure 4). There was no evidence of systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve or left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction. Case 4

A 37-year-old professional male athlete presented for evaluation after an abnormal ECG as part of preparticipation screening. His family history was notable for HCM in his biological mother. He was an active athlete with no current symptoms. His ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm with T-wave inversion in the anterolateral leads (Figure 10B). This was a change from an ECG done 2 years earlier that showed sinus rhythm with no ST-T wave abnormalities (Figure 10A). After his abnormal ECG, he underwent TTE, which demonstrated a foreshortened but thickened apex measuring 14 mm (Video 10). CMR was performed, which confirmed apical hypertrophy and revealed significant LGE, occupying 10% of left ventricular myocardial mass (Supplemental Figure 3). Discussion

In this case series, we present 4 adults of varying ages, sex, comorbidities, and genotypes who transitioned from phenotype negative to phenotype positive for HCM. These cases demonstrated dramatic ECG and cardiac imaging changes in individuals over a short time span, predominantly without accompanying symptoms. Although historically HCM has been thought to express phenotypically predominantly in adolescence and earlier decades of life, our case series and other studies have shown that the transition to phenotypic HCM can occur at much older ages. In fact, temporal trends in age of diagnosis of HCM have shown that an increasing proportion of overall HCM diagnoses are being made in patients at older ages. When evaluating patients with a diagnosis of HCM inside and outside the United States before 2000, between 2000 and 2010, and after 2010, the age of diagnosis increased significantly over time (40 ± 14 years vs 47 ± 15 years vs 51 ± 16 years; P < 0.001).9 These older patients overall were more likely to be genotype negative and to present with milder phenotypes.9 A large component of making the diagnosis at an older age in this study may reflect increased cardiac testing at older ages, and if cardiac testing had been performed earlier, perhaps the diagnosis would have been made earlier.

As presented in this case series, the opportunity to convert to phenotype-positive HCM occurs at later ages, even in patients with frequent and previously normal cardiac testing results, and this finding has important screening implications. The evidence for conversion to midlife or beyond phenotype-positive HCM has largely been confined to case series. However, it raises the question of when and whether screening should be stopped in genotype-positive individuals and first-degree family members of those with HCM. Contemporary HCM guidelines do not give an age at which to stop screening for HCM.2 One could argue that there is unclear clinical significance of making the diagnosis of a mild phenotype of HCM in an older person in their sixth decade or older who is asymptomatic. although making this diagnosis of HCM may not have a meaningful impact on the affected individual, it would trigger downstream cascade screening in family members. The natural history and potential complications of HCM (heart failure, arrythmias, sudden cardiac arrest) in the children of those individuals who had a diagnosis of a mild asymptomatic course of HCM at an older age are not known. One potential benefit of making a definitive HCM diagnosis is that if a pathogenic gene is identified in the proband and the same gene is identified in the children, it would provide valuable information for family planning and offer opportunities for gene therapy to prevent HCM transmission in future generations. Most family members who have not converted to phenotype-positive status by the end of puberty never do so, and the cost-benefit ratio of screening all family members to midlife is likely not favorable.

Finally, our case series and other studies have demonstrated the stark heterogeneity in penetrance and expressivity in HCM. Several studies have attempted to analyze factors that affect penetrance of HCM in sarcomere protein sequence variation carriers. Obesity and male sex in multiple studies have been found to be key factors in the penetrance of HCM.6 Poor control of HTN has been shown in other studies to be a key risk factor in increasing both penetrance and severity of expressivity in HCM.10 The impact of several other environmental modifiers of HCM expressivity and penetrance, including sex, ethnicity, diet, and fitness, should not be underestimated.10 While acknowledging that some risk factors are immutable, these findings suggest that modifiable risk factors, such as HTN and obesity, could minimize chances of expression of an aggressive HCM phenotype. Our study had multiple limitations. One key limitation is that in patients without CMR imaging, mild phenotypic expression of HCM cannot be excluded. In conclusion, late phenotypic expression of HCM can occur and raises important questions about screening in HCM patients.
