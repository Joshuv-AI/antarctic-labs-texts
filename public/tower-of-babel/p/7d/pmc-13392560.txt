Regular intake of caffeinated but not decaffeinated coffee attenuates behavioral modifications in mice subject to chronic unpredictable stress
Authors: Ângelo R Tomé, Nuno J Machado, Ana Paula Ardais, Ana Nunes, Henrique B Silva, Manuella P Kaster, Paula Agostinho, Rodrigo A Cunha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe regular intake of moderate doses of coffee is increasingly recognized as a lifestyle factor associated with a lower incidence of depressive-like conditions; however, there is still a debate on the relative effects of caffeinated and decaffeinated coffee. We now resorted to a model of chronic unpredictable stress to compare how the regular drinking of caffeinated and decaffeinated coffee impacted on stress-induced deterioration of mood and memory in adult mice. Stressed mice displayed lower weight gain, increased locomotion and greater anxiety, lower motivation in the splash test, less struggling and greater immobility in the forced swimming test, increased anhedonia in the sucrose preference test and decreased spatial reference memory in the object displacement and modified Y-maze tests. The continuous intake of caffeinated, but not decaffeinated, coffee attenuated all these stress-induced mood and memory deficits. Moreover, the intake of caffeinated, but not decaffeinated coffee, also attenuated the stress-induced decrease of long-term potentiation in hippocampal synapses and of brain-derived neurotrophic factor levels in frontocortical synapses, a neurophysiological and neurochemical signature of mood deterioration. These results prompt caffeine as a prominent coffee constituent responsible for the benefits of the regular intake of coffee on stress-induced behavioral deficits.Keywords: Chronic stress, Coffee, Decaffeinated, Caffeine, Anxiety, Depression, Anhedonia, Memory, Hippocampus, LTP, BDNF

Introduction

Depression is a major burden of disease in Europe (Fan et al., 2025), probably because of stressful patterns of living, since repeated stress is a major trigger of depressive-like mood conditions (McEwen and Akil, 2020). The neurobiology of mood dysfunction is still incompletely understood, therefore antidepressive strategies have a poor biological rationale and are not always effective (Akil et al., 2018). An alternative is to exploit life-style factors associated with better mood patterns, amongst which stem the consumption of coffee; indeed, several epidemiological studies have associated the regular intake of moderate doses of coffee with a lower incidence of depression (reviewed in Grosso et al., 2016; Torabynasab et al., 2023). Coffee is a mixture of over 2000 potentially bioactive substances with particularly high levels of chlorogenic acids and caffeine (de Melo Pereira et al., 2020). However, the decreased incidence of depression associated with the regular intake of coffee has mostly been associated with the effects of caffeine (Grosso et al., 2016; Torabynasab et al., 2023). Accordingly, in animal models of repeated stress, mood dysfunction is attenuated by caffeine (Kaster et al., 2015; Yin et al., 2015; Machado et al., 2020) through a defined biological mechanism: at non-toxic doses caffeine primarily acts as an antagonist of adenosine receptors (Fredholm et al., 1999; Lopes et al., 2019) to increase allostasis and normalize brain function (IJzerman et al., 2022), thus dampening the incidence of neuropsychiatric diseases (Lopes and Cunha, 2024). This involves an epigenetic, transcriptomic and metabonomic preconditioning (Paiva et al., 2022; Lopes et al., 2023a) coupled to a reorganization of brain responsiveness (Magalhães et al., 2021; Picó-Pérez et al., 2023). Accordingly, antagonists of adenosine A2A receptors mimic the effects of caffeine to attenuate behavioral and neurophysiological alterations resulting from chronic stress in rodents (Kaster et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2023a) and polymorphisms of adenosine A2A receptors are associated with mood in humans (e.g. Hohoff et al., 2010; Geiger et al., 2016; Oliveira et al., 2019).

Strikingly, other prominent constituents of coffee, namely chlorogenic acids, have also been shown to control mood in humans (Cropley et al., 2012; Camfield et al., 2013; Nieman et al., 2018) and in animals (Lenzi et al., 2015; Murlanova et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022a; Holvoet et al., 2023), although a putative underlying mechanism remains to be unveiled. Since chlorogenic acids are abundant in decaffeinated coffee (de Melo Pereira et al., 2020), we now directly compared the impact of caffeinated and decaffeinated coffee extracts on behavioral, electrophysiological and neurochemical alterations caused by chronic unpredictable stress.

Methods

Animals

C57bl\6j mice (8-10 weeks old) were acquired to Charles River (Barcelona, Spain). Both males and females were used (15 males and 9 females, balance distribution between groups) and this pooling of mice of both sexes precluded using sex as a variable. Until starting the stress protocol, mice were housed in groups of three per cage in a temperature-controlled room (22 ± 2 ◦C) with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle (lights on at 8:00 a.m.) and with free access to food and water or to the tested beverages. Animals were handled twice daily during 7 days prior to behavioral testing. All procedures were conducted following the "3Rs" guidelines of the European Union (2010/63/EU), as well as FELASA and ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Research of the Center for Neuroscience and Cell Biology of the University of Coimbra (ORBEA-78-2013). All efforts were made to minimize animal use and reduce their stress and discomfort.

Experimental design

Mild behavioral alteration of mice drinking caffeinated and decaffeinated coffee extracts have been previously reported (Machado et al., 2024). The present study aims to compare the impact of these coffee beverages on alterations induced by repeated stress. To this end, mice were divided into 4 groups (n = 6 per group): 1) control mice drinking water and not subject to stress; 2) mice drinking water and exposed to chronic unpredictable stress (CUS); 3) mice drinking caffeinated coffee and exposed to CUS; 4) mice drinking decaffeinated coffee and exposed to CUS. Our previous studies have shown that CUS-induced alterations of mood and memory, persist for at least 3 weeks after cessation of the stress protocol (Kaster et al., 2015). Therefore, behavioral assessments were performed in all four groups during the week following the end of CUS (see Fig. 1). Mice were subsequently euthanized for electrophysiological characterization to assess alterations in hippocampal neuronal network function and to quantify frontocortical levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a major regulator of synaptic plasticity (Leal et al., 2015) and mood (Castrén and Monteggia, 2021). These two readouts associated with mood dysfunction were quantified in different brain structures due to limited availability of tissue, although both the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex are affected by chronic stress and their alterations contribute to mood dysfunction (e.g. Tripathi et al., 2016).

Beverages

In this naturalistic study, we used aqueous extracts of commercially available coffee blends widely consumed in Portugal, representative of caffeinated coffee (cof, Café Moído, Moagem Máquina, Delta Cafés®) and of decaffeinated coffee (decaf, Café Moído Torrado Descafeinado Universal, Delta Cafés®). Beverages were prepared daily by dissolving 1 g of powder into 1 L of hot water, followed by a centrifugation at 50 x g and the supernatants were placed in the drinking bottles supplied to the mouse cages (3 mice per cage). Mice had free access to these beverages during 12 h/day (8 PM - 8 a.m., dark phase) until euthanasia, beginning 7 days prior to the start of other experimental protocols. As we previously informed (see Machado et al., 2024), this roughly corresponds to a daily intake of 0.25-0.35 mg of caffeine and 1.2-1.6 mg of chlorogenic acids in the circa 14.5 mL of coffee estimated to be freely consumed daily by mice (14.6 ± 0.3 mL, n = 4), similar to the consumption of decaffeinated coffee (14.6 ± 0.4 mL, n = 4) or of water (14.3 ± 0.6 mL, n = 4). This amount of coffee translates into circa 350 mg of caffeine in adult humans (Watanabe et al., 2017). These values are approximate as mice were group-housed during the pre-stress consumption of the different coffee extracts to avoid stress associated with isolation; moreover, caffeine and chlorogenic acid levels were not quantified in the beverages nor in mouse fluids or tissues.

Chronic unpredictable stress

Mice consuming the different tested beverages were subjected to the previously validated CUS protocol (Kaster et al., 2015) for 21 days, as described in Fig. 1. On day 1, mice were transferred to individual cages and exposed daily to one of several stressors applied in a random order, consisting of damp bedding, paired housing for 1 h with a different unfamiliar stressed mouse in a neutral, unfamiliar cage, restraint for 2 h, cold bath (15 °C, 20 min), inescapable shock (0.7 mA) in a UGO BASILE Fear Conditioning System (Ugo Basile, Gemonio, Italy), exposure 24 h after to the same apparatus where they were previously shocked with no foot shock, light/dark cycle inversion, cage tilt (45°), food + water deprivation (24 h), exposure to an empty bottle for 1 h (food was returned to the animals, whereas the drinking bottle remained empty for an additional 1 h, during the light cycle). Control (non-stressed) mice were handled daily but were not subjected to the CUS protocol.

Behavioral testing

Mice were first weighed and then subject to a structured schedule of behavioral testing, with minimal time intervals between tests required to prevent cross-interference (Kaster et al., 2015; Lopes et al., 2023a; Machado et al., 2024). Behavior assessments were conducted at 8 am or 2 p.m. between days 22 and 25 after the onset of caffeinated or decaffeinated coffee administration, following the schedule shown in Fig. 1. Testing was performed by experimenters blinded to the treatment conditions in a sound-attenuated room with a 10 lux red illumination, with animals previously habituated to the environment and visual cues. Most sessions were video-recorded and analyzed using ANY-maze Video Tracking Software (Stoelting Europe, Dublin, Ireland). Apparatuses were cleaned with 10% ethyl alcohol between animals or groups to eliminate odor cues.

Open field: Locomotion and exploratory behavior were monitored in the morning of day 22 (see Fig. 1), using an open-field arena. Each mouse was placed in the center of the arena, and activity was recorded for 10 min, including total locomotion (peripheral and central locomotion), with central locomotion used as an index of anxiety (Kaster et al., 2015).

Object displacement: Hippocampal-dependent memory was evaluated using the object displacement test, carried out as previously done (Lopes et al., 2023aa; Lopes et al., 2023b) in the afternoon of day 22. Mice were first exposed to two identical objects placed in opposite corners of the same open field arena used for habituation (20 cm away from walls and 50 cm apart) and were allowed to explore for 5 min. In the test trial, conducted 2 h later, mice were reintroduced into the same arena for 5 min, with one object relocated to a novel position. Memory performance was quantified using an object displacement index, defined as the ratio of time spent exploring the displaced object to the total exploration time of both objects. Exploration of an object is defined as directing the nose to the object at a distance equal to or less than 2 cm from the object and/or making nose contact.

Elevated plus-maze: Anxiety was further assessed in the morning of day 23, using the elevated plus-maze, where each animal was allowed to explore during 8 min the open and closed arms of the crossed maze. The number of entries in both open and closed arms were recorded, considering an entry only when the whole body and four paws were inside an arm (Kaster et al., 2015; Machado et al., 2024).

Forced swimming test: Depressive-like behavior was assessed using the forced swimming test on the afternoon of day 23, as previously described (Kaster et al., 2015; Machado et al., 2024). Mice were individually placed in glass cylinders (40 cm height, 17 cm diameter) filled with water (30 cm depth, 25 ± 1 °C) and immobility and climbing behaviors were recorded during a 10-min session. Immobility was defined as floating motionless or making only the movements necessary to keep the head above the water. Climbing (or struggling) behavior was defined as upward-directed movements of the forepaws, typically along the cylinder walls. Diving and face-shaking behaviors were not included in the analysis.

Splash test: The splash test was performed on the morning of day 24, as previously described (Kaster et al., 2015; Machado et al., 2024). A 10% sucrose solution was applied to the dorsal coat of each animal and the latency to initiate grooming bouts (head washing, nose/face, and body grooming) was recorded as an index of self-care, along with total grooming time over 5-min as a measure of motivation.

Modified Y-maze: Spatial memory was further assessed using a two-trial Y-maze paradigm on the afternoon of day 24, as previously described (Lopes et al., 2003b, 2023a). The test consists of two sessions of 8-min sessions separated by a 2-h inter-trial interval. In the first session, mice were placed at the end of the start arm and allowed to explore two arms, with the third (novel) arm blocked by a guillotine door. In the second session, the ‘novel’ arm was open, and mice were allowed to explore the three arms for 8-min. Memory performance was evaluated as the time spent exploring the ‘novel’ arm relative to the other arms. An arm entry was defined as the placement of all four paws within an arm.

Sucrose preference test: Anhedonic-like behavior was evaluated during day 25 using the sucrose preference test. Mice were individually housed in cages equipped with two bottles. After 4-h habituation period starting at 8 a.m., one bottle was randomly replaced with a 1.2% sucrose solution, and consumption of water and sucrose was measured over a 16-h period (8 h light phase and 8 h dark phase). Sucrose preference was calculated as the ratio of sucrose intake to total fluid intake, as previously described (Kaster et al., 2015).

Euthanasia and tissue collection

Mice were euthanized in mixed pairs from different experimental groups (one pair in the morning and one in the afternoon) over 6 consecutive days, starting the day after completion of behavioral testing. Animals were euthanized by decapitation under deep halothane anesthesia (absence of response to tail pinch). Brains were rapidly removed and placed in ice-cold, oxygenated (95% O2, 5% CO2), artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF; in mM: 124.0 NaCl, 4.4 KCl, 1.0 Na2HPO4, 25.0 NaHCO3, 2.0 CaCl2, 1.0 MgCl2, 10.0 glucose). The hippocampi were dissected for slice electrophysiology and the frontal cortex was collected for quantification of BDNF levels in purified synapses (i.e. synaptosomes).

Hippocampal slice electrophysiology

Electrophysiological recordings were performed in 400 μm-thick coronal slices of the dorsal hippocampus, as previously described (e.g. Lopes et al., 2019). After dissection, slices were cut transverse to the hippocampal long axis using a McIlwain tissue chopper (Brinkmann Instruments, NY, USA) and allowed to recover for 1h at 32 °C in oxygenated (95% O2, 5% CO2) ACSF. Individual slices were then transferred to a submerged recording chamber and superfused with oxygenated ACSF for 3 mL/min at 30.6 °C. A bipolar concentric stimulating electrode was positioned on Schaffer fibers to deliver 0.1 ms pulses every 20 s using a Digitimer DS3 stimulator (Digitimer Ltd, Letch-worth, UK). The evoked field excitatory postsynaptic potentials (fEPSP) were recorded in the CA1 stratum radiatum using 2-4 mΩ glass electrodes. Signals were amplified (ISO-80, World Precision Instruments Ltd, Hertfordshire, UK), digitized (BNC-2110, National Instruments, Newbury, UK), averaged in groups of three, and analyzed using WinLTP software (Anderson and Collingridge, 2007). Stimulation intensity was set at 40–50% of the maximal fEPSP response, determined from input/output curves plotting fEPSP slope versus stimulus intensity. Long-term potentiation (LTP) was induced by high-frequency stimulation (HFS, 100 Hz for 1s) (e.g. Lopes et al., 2019) and quantified as the percentage change in fEPSP slope between baseline, 10 min before HFS and 50-60 min post-HFS.

BDNF quantification in frontocortical synaptosomes

Synaptosomes (i.e., enriched nerve terminals) were prepared as previously described (Rodrigues et al., 2008). Briefly, the frontal cortex was mechanically homogenized in 10 mL of sucrose solution (0.32 M) containing 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (pH 7.4) at 4 °C. The homogenate was centrifuged at 3000× g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was subsequently centrifuged at 14,000× g for 12 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet (P2 fraction) was resuspended in 1 mL of a 45% (v/v) Percoll in Krebs-HEPES buffer (140 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 25 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM glucose; pH 7.4). Following centrifugation at 14,000x g for 2 min at 4 °C, the upper white layer corresponding to the synaptosomal fraction was collected, resuspended in 1 mL Krebs-HEPES buffer and centrifuged again at 14,000x g for 2 min at 4 °C. The purity of this synaptic fraction has been previously reported to exceed 95% (Rodrigues et al., 2008). The final pellet was then resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, with 50 mM Tris-HCl, 137 mM NaCl, 10 mM NaH2PO4, pH 7.4) supplemented with 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 0.001% CLAP protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma, USA) and PhosSTOP™ phosphate inhibitors (Roche, Switzerland) and homogenized by sonication on ice. After centrifugation at 14,000× g for 12 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and stored at −20 °C until use. Protein concentration was determined with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce™, Thermo Scientific™, USA).

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels were quantified by an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), as previously described (Wei et al., 2014), using a commercial kit (Total BDNF Quantikine ELISA Kit, DBNT00, from R&D systems, Oxon, England). All samples were analyzed in duplicate and results are expressed as ng/mg of protein.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM (n = 6 mice per group), their normal distribution was assessed with a Shapiro-Wilk test and homogeneity of variance was evaluated with a Brown-Forsythe test. Our experimental design has an unbalanced factorial design, where two groups are missing since we previously described discrete effects of both beverages in control mice (see Machado et al., 2024), precisely the two groups that are now missing. Thus, instead of a three-way ANOVA with 3 variables (stress, caffeinated coffee, decaffeinated coffee), we opted to ignore the factorial nature of the design and analyze the data using a one-way ANOVA followed by a Tukey's post hoc multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (Boston, USA).

Results

Behavioral alterations

The multi-dimensional analysis of behavioral performance of adult mice confirmed that the 3 weeks protocol of chronic unpredictable stress (CUS) deteriorated mood and memory performance and these stress-induced modifications were nearly abolished in mice consuming caffeinated coffee (CUS + cof) but not decaffeinated coffee (CUS + decaf).

Compared to control mice (n = 6), mice subject to CUS (n = 6) exhibited a lower body weight (CUS: 25.3 ± 0.42 g vs. control: 27.9 ± 0.20 g; p < 0.001; Fig. 2A), increased spontaneous locomotion (p = 0.031; Fig. 2B) and heightened anxiety, as indicated by the decreased time spent in the more aversive central region of the open field (p = 0.001; Fig. 2C), and the reduced time spent in the open arms of the elevated plus-maze (p < 0.001; Fig. 2D). CUS-exposed mice also had an increased despair behavior in the forced swimming test, as indicated by the increased time spent immobile (p = 0.002 vs. control; Fig. 2E), and a decreased resilience to adversity (decreased struggling time, p = 0.002 vs. control; Fig. 2F); CUS also increased anhedonia (lower sucrose preference, p = 0.016 vs. control; Fig. 2G) and decreased motivation for selfcare in the splash test (p < 0.001 vs. control; Fig. 2H). Finally, CUS-exposed mice also displayed a deficit in spatial reference memory, as concluded by their lower time spent exploring the novel arm (previously hidden) in the modified Y-maze test (p < 0.001 vs. control; Fig. 3A) and by the decreased exploration of the displaced object in the object displacement test (p < 0.001 vs. control; Fig. 3B).

The above-described behavioral alterations displayed by mice drinking water and exposed to CUS, were essentially eliminated in mice regularly consuming (regular or caffeinated) coffee extracts (CUS + cof, n = 6), which displayed a behavioral pattern nearly superimposable to control mice (i.e. not subject to CUS and drinking water). Thus, CUS + cof mice recovered their weight (27.3 ± 0.23 g, p = 0.002 vs. CUS, p = 0.588 vs. control; Fig. 2A), their spontaneous locomotion (p = 0.007 vs. CUS, p = 0.905 vs. control; Fig. 2B), their anxiety in the open field (p = 0.043 vs. CUS, p = 0.379 vs. control; Fig. 2C) and in the elevated plus-maze (p < 0.001 vs. CUS, p = 0.919 vs. control; Fig. 2D), their immobility time (p = 0.041 vs. CUS, p = 0.507 vs. control; Fig. 2E) and increased struggling time in the forced swimming test (p = 0.010 vs. CUS, p = 0.847 vs. control; Fig. 2F), their sucrose preference (p = 0.038 vs. CUS, p = 0.976 vs. control; Fig. 2G) and their motivation for selfcare (p = 0.007 vs. CUS, p = 0.574 vs. control; Fig. 2H). CUS + cof mice also recovered their spatial reference memory performance in the modified Y-maze test (p = 0.011 vs. CUS, p = 0.178 vs. control; Fig. 3A) and in the object displacement test (p < 0.001 vs. CUS, p = 0.888 vs. control; Fig. 3B).

The behavioral pattern displayed by CUS-exposed mice regularly drinking decaffeinated coffee extracts (CUS + decaf, n = 6) was substantially different from CUS + cof mice since most of the differences identified between CUS and CUS + cof mice were not observed between CUS and CUS + decaf mice (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Thus, CUS + decaf mice failed to recover body weight (25.9 ± 0.41 g, p = 0.674 vs. CUS, p = 0.002 vs. control; Fig. 2A), spontaneous locomotion (p = 0.835 vs. CUS, p = 0.160 vs. control; Fig. 2B) and anxiety levels in the open field (p = 0.999 vs. CUS, p = 0.001 vs. control; Fig. 2C) and elevated plus-maze (p = 0.099 vs. CUS, p = 0.042 vs. control; Fig. 2D). Likewise, immobility time (p = 0.957 vs. CUS, p = 0.006 vs. control; Fig. 2E) and struggling behavior in the forced swimming test (p = 0.165 vs. CUS, p = 0.165 vs. control; Fig. 2F) were not restored. CUS + decaf mice also failed to normalize sucrose preference (p = 0.946 vs. CUS, p = 0.051 vs. control; Fig. 2G) and grooming behavior in the splash test (p = 0.205 vs. CUS, p = 0.036 vs. control; Fig. 2H). Furthermore, CUS + decaf mice also failed to recover spatial reference memory performance in both the modified Y-maze test (p = 0.842 vs. CUS, p = 0.001 vs. control; Fig. 3A) and the object displacement test (p = 0.768 vs. CUS, p = 0.004 vs. control; Fig. 3B).

In summary, only caffeinated but not decaffeinated coffee modified the behavior of mice exposed to CUS. Although this indicates a primordial role of caffeine in preserving behavior in stressed mice, it is important to note that there were some apparent minor alterations of behavior in CUS + decaf mice, which indicates that components besides caffeine might also have a discrete impact in the deterioration of mood and memory performance in stressed mice.

Alterations of long-term potentiation in hippocampal slices

There is ample evidence that alterations of hippocampal synaptic plasticity underlie modifications of learning and memory, as best heralded by the contention that alterations of long-term potentiation (LTP) might be a neurophysiological basis of spatial reference memory processing (Martin et al., 2000; Lynch, 2004). Since CUS-exposed mice displayed deficits of spatial reference memory recoverable by the intake of caffeinated coffee, we tested the impact of CUS on LTP in hippocampal slices and the effects of coffee and decaffeinated coffee thereupon.

As shown in Fig. 4A, there were no apparent effects on basal hippocampal synaptic transmission of CUS and of the intake of the different tested beverages, as gauged by the near superimposable input-output curves of the field excitatory post-synaptic responses (fEPSP) recorded near distal dendrites of CA1 neurons upon stimulation of afferent Schaffer fibers (n = 6 from each group, 1 slice from each animal). High frequency stimulation (100 Hz for 1 s) elicited LTP, which magnitude was decreased by CUS (60.99 ± 6.19% over baseline in control vs. 25.52 ± 3.18% for CUS, p < 0.001; Fig. 4B–D). As shown in Fig. 4B–D, LTP magnitude was recovered in CUS + cof (p = 0.001 vs. CUS, p = 0.724 vs. control; Fig. 4D), but not in CUS + decaf mice (p = 0.851 vs. CUS, p = 0.001 vs. control; Fig. 4D).

Alterations of BDNF levels in synaptosomes from the frontal cortex

A prominent signal of synaptic plasticity (Leal et al., 2015), which is deficient in depressive-like condition (Castrén and Monteggia, 2021), is brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). Since apart from synaptic signaling, BDNF also fulfils other roles such as the control microglia-derived neuroinflammation (Gomes et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2014), we decided to quantify BDNF in frontocortical synaptosomes (i.e. purified synaptic compartments; see Rodrigues et al., 2008), where cortical BDNF is enriched (Fawcett et al., 1997). In control mice, the synaptic levels of BDNF in the frontal cortex were 9.52 ± 0.46 ng/mg protein (n = 6). These synaptic levels of BDNF were decreased in CUS-exposed mice (p < 0.001 vs. control) and were recovered to near control values in CUS + cof (p = 0.001 vs. CUS, p = 0.801 vs. control), but not in CUS + decaf mice (p = 0.738 vs. CUS, p = 0.002 vs. control) (n = 6 for all groups; Fig. 5).

Compared to control (black bars and symbols), CUS decreased the levels of BDNF in frontocortical synapses (red bars and symbols), which was prevented by the intake of caffeinated coffee (+cof, dark blue bars and symbols), but not by the intake of decaffeinated coffee (+decaf, light blue bars and symbols). Discussion

The present study provides a direct comparison of the impact of caffeinated and decaffeinated coffee extracts on behavioral, electrophysiological and neurochemical alterations caused by chronic unpredictable stress. We report an ability of caffeinated but not of decaffeinated coffee to prevent alterations caused by chronic unpredictable stress in adult mice, as previously also observed in relation to depression-like behaviors in sleep-deprived rats (Gu et al., 2022). Although the chemical composition of both beverages as well as the levels and pharmacokinetics of different coffee components were not determined in this naturalistic-like study, the striking behavioral difference between caffeinated and decaffeinated coffee is strongly suggestive of ascribing a major mood-related behavioral effect to caffeine.

The ability of chronic intake of moderate doses of caffeine to counteract mood-related behavioral deterioration upon repeated stress has been repeatedly reported in different rodent models (Pechlivanova et al., 2012; Kaster et al., 2015; Yin et al., 2015; Hall et al., 2016; Kasimay Cakir et al., 2017; Ibrahim et al., 2020; Machado et al., 2020; Dias et al., 2021; Fang et al., 2021; Basu et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2025; but see Henry and Stephens, 1980; Machado et al., 2017). Likewise, different meta-analyses of epidemiological studies in humans showed that the regular consumption of coffee has a protective effect against the development of depression (Grosso et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2021; Torabynasab et al., 2023), a benefit most evident with caffeinated rather than decaffeinated coffee (Lucas et al., 2011; Hall et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023b), with dietary caffeine mimicking the mood-related benefits afforded by coffee (Grosso et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2016; Kim and Kim, 2018; Iranpour and Sabour, 2019; Bao et al., 2022; but see Ruusunen et al., 2010; Kimura et al., 2020). Thus, the bulk of evidence tentatively converges to support the contention that caffeine plays a prominent role in the ability of regular intake of coffee to normalize mood changes and to prevent mood dysfunction.

Importantly, the benefits afforded by caffeine to normalize mood are now rooted on defined molecular and neurophysiological mechanisms. Thus, imaging studies have documented the ability of caffeinated coffee and of moderate doses of caffeine to alters brain functional connectivity in humans (Magalhães et al., 2021; Picó-Pérez et al., 2023) and to optimize synaptic and metabolic processes (Paiva et al., 2022) particularly in synapses (Lopes et al., 2023a) of the mouse hippocampus. Moreover, in keeping with the identification of adenosine receptors as the main molecular targets of moderate doses of caffeine to impact on information processing in neuronal networks (Lopes et al., 2019), previous studies showed that the impact of the regular intake of caffeine to prevent emotional dysfunction associated with repeated stress in mice is due to the antagonism of adenosine A2A receptors (A2AR) (Kaster et al., 2015; Bevilacqua et al., 2026; Lee and Jung, 2026), which controls mood-related behavioral alterations (El Yacoubi et al., 2001; Wang et al., 2023a; Zhao et al., 2022b). The involvement of A2AR in the effects of caffeinated coffee in stress-induced mood dysfunction is further supported by the presently reported ability of caffeinated coffee to prevent aberrant hippocampal long-term potentiation (LTP) and frontocortical BDNF levels. LTP has been associated with spatial reference memory (Martin et al., 2000; Lynch, 2004) and A2AR are prominent controllers of hippocampal LTP (Rebola et al., 2008; Costenla et al., 2011), namely of stress-induced dysfunction of hippocampal LTP (Kaster et al., 2015). In parallel, caffeine intake affects brain BDNF levels (Wu et al., 2025) and A2AR are major controllers of BDNF levels in cortical neurons (Jeon et al., 2012) and in the prefrontal cortex (Wei et al., 2014), namely in synapses (Fontinha et al., 2008; Vaz et al., 2015), where BDNF sustains synaptic plasticity processes (Leal et al., 2015) to influence mood processing (Castrén and Monteggia, 2021). This remarkable superimposition of the effects of caffeinated coffee and of A2AR at the molecular to neurophysiological and behavioral levels, heralds the proposal that the benefits of coffee in the control of mood may be mostly due to the antagonism of A2AR by caffeine in the brain.

The impact of decaffeinated coffee on stress-induced behavioral alterations is less clear. It seems obvious that it failed to prevent the effects of chronic unpredictable stress; however, it brought several behavior readouts (spontaneous locomotion in the open field test, resilience as struggling in the forced swim test, anhedonia in the sucrose preference test) to levels non-significantly different from control. This leaves open the possibility that decaffeinated coffee might have caused discrete behavioral effects on stress responses, as was also noted by others (Song et al., 2026). Given the evidence that the process of decaffeination does not significantly affect all major non-caffeine components of the beverage (Hall et al., 2018), the present finding implies a limited effect on stress-induced behavioral alterations of several components abundant in coffee, namely phenolic acids (chlorogenic acids, caffeic acid, ferulic acid), quinic acid, trigonelline and quercetin. This contrasts with the proposed role of decaffeinated coffee in neurodegenerative diseases (Colombo and Papetti, 2020) and with the evidence supporting that dietary polyphenol intake is inversely associated with depressive symptoms (Chang et al., 2016; Godos et al., 2018). More studies focused on the different non-caffeine components of coffee should be carried out in order to understand the limited benefits afforded by chlorogenic acid-rich beverages on mood and emotional-related domains of behavioral performance (Cropley et al., 2012; Camfield et al., 2013; Nieman et al., 2018). In fact, a limitation of the present study is that it did not assess the levels of coffee-containing bioactive substances in the beverages and in the mice and did not monitor the individual intake of coffees. Additionally, the testing of the beverages was performed in a mixed population of males and female mice where the sex variable was not isolated, and some discrete differences in the impact of coffee on mood were previously reported (Machado et al., 2024). Importantly, the design of the experiments has an unbalanced factorial design, where two groups corresponding to the impact of caffeinated and decaffeinated coffee in control (non-stressed) mice are missing because we previously described discrete effects of both beverages in control mice (see Machado et al., 2024), which argues but does not demonstrate a particular effect of caffeinated coffee to normalize behavior in stressed mice. Finally, the extrapolation of animal behavior to humans always needs to be considered with care.

Despite the need of caution in the translation to humans, the conclusion prompted by the present study may be of considerable societal interest, by providing a rationale supporting to consider the regular intake of moderate doses of caffeinated coffee as a prophylactic lifestyle to attenuate stress-induced depression - a major global health burden.
