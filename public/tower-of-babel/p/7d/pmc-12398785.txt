Racial and Ethnic Disparities in Catheter Ablation Utilization for Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Waseem Nosair, Liza Shaban, Marc El Khoury, Rama Al Ashqar, Layla Heimlich, Paul Kolm, Jamal Smith, Sarahfaye Dolman, Apostolos Tsimploulis, Athanasios Thomaides, David Strouse, Sung Lee, William S Weintraub, Morgana Mongraw-Chaffin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRacial and ethnic disparities in catheter ablation (CA) utilization for atrial fibrillation have been reported, but inconsistent estimates complicate comparisons and understanding of root causes of disparity.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to quantify CA disparities by race/ethnicity, explore sources of heterogeneity, and offer recommendations for standardized definitions and methods to improve research.MethodsWe systematically searched MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane’s CENTRAL from inception to January 15, 2024, for U.S.-based studies. Data were extracted on equity standards, cohort characteristics, methods, and risk of bias by 2 reviewers. We metaanalyzed cross-sectional and survival data separately using random effects models. We evaluated heterogeneity through qualitative synthesis, meta-regression, and sensitivity analyses.ResultsEighteen studies were included. None explicitly defined disparity or the source of race/ethnicity data. The most common analytic approach estimated disparity as the residual direct effect of race/ethnicity after adjusting for confounders, but key confounders were missing. Only one study evaluated mediators of disparity through sensitivity analysis. Compared to non-Hispanic White patients, non-Hispanic Black (OR: 0.65; 95% CI: 0.58-0.74), Hispanic/LatinX (OR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.73-0.83), and Asian (OR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.54-1.0) patients were less likely to receive CA. There was a high degree of between-study heterogeneity, likely from differences in source population, methods, and risk adjustment.ConclusionsWhile evidence confirms racial and ethnic disparities in CA utilization for atrial fibrillation, significant heterogeneity exists across studies. Standardized disparity definitions and consistent covariate adjustment may help confirm the scale of disparities and identify underlying mechanisms to inform interventions.Key words: access to health care, atrial fibrillation, catheter ablation, health care disparities, health equity

Atrial fibrillation (AF), the most common arrhythmia, is associated with an increased risk of stroke, heart failure, and death.1 These risks may be reduced with anticoagulation for stroke prevention and rhythm control to lower morbidity and mortality. Race and ethnic disparities in AF care have been documented, with underrepresented patient groups experiencing worse health outcomes.2 However, large cohort studies have reported a lower clinically detected prevalence of AF among minority groups compared to non-Hispanic White (NHW) patients despite having a higher burden of risk factors.3,4 The observation that minority patients have a higher burden of risk factors, symptoms, and worse outcomes despite a lower prevalence of AF has there been termed the “AF paradox.”5 While termed a paradox, this finding raises concerns about ascertainment bias from delayed or underdiagnosis of AF in minority patients, nontraditional risk factors, and disparities in access to care as reasons for these observed differences.6 The “AF paradox” may exacerbate structural biases and lead to worse outcomes by delaying AF care.

Catheter ablation (CA) has emerged as an important rhythm control therapy that may prevent disease progression and lower risk of stroke, heart failure, and mortality. Previously reserved for symptomatic patients who were refractory or intolerant of antiarrhythmic drugs, CA is now recommended as a first-line therapy in select symptomatic patients and those with heart failure.7 While racial and ethnic disparities in anticoagulant utilization have been systematically reviewed, no systematic review to our knowledge has comprehensively examined disparities in the use of CA for AF.8 Studying race or ethnic disparities in CA utilization is a challenging task due to the complex factors that influence CA utilization like health status, clinical appropriateness, and social determinants of health (SDOH). Accordingly, varying and conflicting estimates of disparities have been reported yet these differences have not been critically reviewed. Exploring these differences may highlight underlying mechanisms of inequities in CA utilization that can be targeted for interventions to improve access to this important therapy.

We aimed to systematically review and synthesize the literature on racial and ethnic disparities in CA utilization. Specifically, we sought to evaluate how disparities were defined, the methods used to quantify them, and the methodological quality of existing studies. While meta-analysis is commonly used to estimate a single, generalizable pooled treatment effect—often from intervention studies—it also plays a critical role in assessing heterogeneity. In this study, we used meta-analysis to estimate disparities in CA utilization and explore sources of heterogeneity in reported estimates, providing insights into methodological differences and to guide future equity research in this field.

Methods

Definitions of health care disparity, race, and ethnicity

Different definitions of what counts as disparity have been proposed and may influence the scale of resulting estimates (Figure 1).10 The definition by the Agency for Healthcare Quality and Research—what we refer to as “total difference”—equates disparity with the total unadjusted difference between groups. While this approach is straightforward to measure, it does not distinguish between differences that may be clinically appropriate and those that reflect unjust inequities, such as discrimination or the influence of SDOH. The “residual direct effect” (RDE) approach defines disparity as the residual race or ethnicity effect after adjusting for covariates in a multivariate model. While RDE provides an easily interpretable coefficient representing the independent effect of race or ethnicity, it is sensitive to which covariates are included in the model which may alter estimates. The RDE approach may underestimate disparities by adjusting for mediators of disparity such as socioeconomic status (SES) or other SDOH which are shaped by structural inequities. The Institute of Medicine's (IOM) definition addresses the shortcomings of prior definitions by only adjusting for differences explained by health needs, clinical appropriateness, and patient preference, with remaining difference in outcomes considered to be true disparity.11

Different Definitions of Disparity Influence Scale of Disparity Estimates

Institute of Medicine (IOM) definition of disparity equates disparity with any unjust difference between 2 groups that is not explained by group differences in health needs, clinical appropriateness, and patient preference. Mechanisms of disparity may include implicit or structural bias and inequities stemming from the health access and care. The “residual direct effect” equates disparity with a race/ethnicity coefficient after adjusting for all available covariates but may underestimate disparity by adjusting for mediators that are linked to race/ethnicity like social determinants of health (SDOH). The definition by the Agency for Healthcare Quality and Research (AHRQ) counts the total difference in outcome as disparity but does not differentiate between acceptable or clinically appropriate reasons and unjust causes of disparity. Since definitions of disparity and their corresponding statistical methods heavily influence disparity estimates, we documented definitions and methods used by studies. If no formal definition is mentioned, we inferred one of the above definitions based on methods used. We also evaluated whether studies framed race and ethnicity as social constructs and avoided grouping minorities under broad categories like “other” which overlooks intergroup and intragroup differences. Although race and ethnicity are sometimes used interchangeably, we distinguish between race, a social construct based on perceived physical traits, and ethnicity, based on shared culture and ancestry. We used Hispanic/LatinX (HLx) as a gender-neutral term for Latin American identity. We evaluated whether studies specified if race and ethnicity data were self-reported, the gold standard for capturing such data.

Search strategy

The literature search focused on publications reporting associations between race and ethnicity and the utilization of CA for AF in the United States. A medical librarian (S.L.H.) developed the search strategy in Medline (Ovid platform) in consultation with clinicians. The search included a combination of MeSH terms, keywords, and adjacency phrases for concepts around health equity, bias, vulnerable populations, AF, and CA. The search was peer reviewed by a librarian at another institution and then adapted for Embase (Ovid platform), Web of Science, and Cochrane’s CENTRAL database (including the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and the Cochrane Clinical Trials Registry) (Supplemental Methods 1). No language restrictions were applied. Medline results date back to 1946, Embase to 1974, and Cochrane and Web of Science had no date limits. Searches were conducted on October 17, 2024, and updated on January 15, 2025, with articles imported into Covidence software for deduplication and screening.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Papers were included if they reported empirical data on the association between race or ethnicity and CA utilization in AF, with the following criteria: adult AF patients in the United States, English language, race or ethnicity as the primary exposure (with NHW as the comparator), and CA utilization as the outcome (excluding surgical and atrioventricular node ablation). Review articles, commentaries, case reports, case series, and letters were excluded.

Screening and data extraction

The search yielded 2,453 results after removal of duplicates. Four reviewers screened titles and abstracts and abstracted data (L.S., R.A., M.E., W.N.). The full text of each reference was then screened for its inclusion eligibility by 2 independent reviewers. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus. Extracted data domains included: 1) equity standards (definitions of race, ethnicity, disparity, and race/ethnicity data collection method [self-report or other]); 2) study-level variables (study year, cross-sectional vs cohort study design, years of enrollment, sample size, setting [impatient, outpatient, or both], race or ethnic groups included in the study); 3) data source [electronic health record [EHR], administrative/claims, registry]; 4) patient-level variables (demographics, income, insurance type, comorbidities, AF type, AF burden, echocardiographic parameters, treatment history, follow-up, recurrence, mortality); and 5) systems-level variables (practice rurality, neighborhood-level social determinant indices). We extracted unadjusted and adjusted ORs or HRs for utilization of CA by race/ethnic group compared to NHW as the reference group. A weighted average of ORs was calculated for non-Hispanic Black (NHB) group in one study that stratified the effect measure by sex.12 For 2 studies without measures of association, raw counts were used to calculate unadjusted ORs and CIs using the Wald method or Fisher exact test for rare events.13,14

Synthesis and meta-analysis

We synthesized the studies using qualitative and quantitative descriptive analysis of study characteristics. We assessed risk of bias using the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute quality assessment tool for cross-sectional and cohort studies.15 Reviewers independently applied the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute Observational Cohort & Cross-Sectional Studies tool, judged each bias domain (14 items) as yes/no/other and qualitatively assigned an overall rating of good (minimal concerns), fair (some concerns, no fatal flaws), or poor (≥1 fatal flaw or multiple high-risk domains). For studies using the National Inpatient Sample, we also assessed whether authors used appropriate weighting and analytic approaches accounting for the database’s stratified and clustered sampling design as recommended by guidelines offered by the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project. We pooled studies reporting cross-sectional (OR) and survival data (HR) stratified by race or ethnic group separately using precalculated log-transformed ORs using the metagen package.16 Standard errors were estimated from reported 95% CIs and intervals were re-estimated by normal approximation for both cross-sectional (OR) and cohort (HR) studies. ORs for Golwala and Alhuneafat et al were calculated using raw event counts for each race/ethnicity group. For Golwala et al, we used Fisher exact test to estimate ORs with 95% CIs due to small cell counts. For Alhuneafat et al, Wald-based methods were used given large sample sizes. For Naderi et al, sex-specific-adjusted ORs for Black and Latinx patients were pooled using inverse-variance weighting, with standard errors calculated from reported 95% CIs using normal approximation. To account for study heterogeneity, we utilized the random effects models.17 We examined between-study heterogeneity using the I2 statistic, and prediction intervals (PI) which estimate the range in which the effect size of a new study would likely fall, accounting for between-study variability. There was not enough data on American Indian/Native Alaskan or Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander groups to be included in the meta-analysis. We assessed for publication bias using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test.18 We performed sensitivity (“leave-one-out”) and subgroup analyses to assess impact of individual or groups of studies on heterogeneity. Univariate meta-regression was used to assess whether heterogeneity could be explained by study variables (year, midpoint year for enrollment, overall proportion of NHW patients, overall proportion of females, whether each study adjusted for specific covariate categories [demographics, SES, health status, health care access, echocardiographic data, demographics, geographic], data source [National Inpatient Sample, Explorys, Optum Clinformatics, EHRs, ORBIT-AF, state database], study quality). Analyses were performed using R (R Core Team, 2024) and RStudio (RStudio, 2024). The PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) reporting guidelines checklist can be found in Supplemental Methods 2.

Results

The search yielded 2490 citations after removal of 212 duplicates. After title and abstract screening, 39 were assessed for eligibility. The most common reasons for exclusion were incompatible study design or outcomes (Supplemental Figure 1). The 18 studies included were published between 2014 and 2024, with data collection spanning from 2000 to 2022 with study details included in Table 1. The studies represented a total AF population of 49,907,766 patients, including 42,737,527 (85.6%) NHW patients, 4,011,133 (8%) NHB patients, 2,405,285 (4.5%) HLx patients, and 753,821 (1.5%) Asian patients. NHB patients were the most represented group in studies (n = 17), followed by HLX (n = 13) and Asian (n = 8). American Indian or Alaskan Native and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander were underrepresented (n = 2). Most studies reported demographic (Supplemental Table 1) and comorbidity data (Supplemental Table 2), with minority patients being younger and having a higher comorbidity burden in most cohorts. Few studies provided data on AF history, echocardiographic data, or mortality outcomes (Supplemental Table 3); however, where available, NHB and HLx had higher CHA2DS2-VASc scores and mortality. Similarly, reporting of treatment for AF by group was also often lacking (Supplemental Table 4). Regarding study quality and risk of bias, most studies were rated as fair (n = 12), with 5 rated as poor and only one rated as good (Supplemental Table 5).

No studies provided explicit definitions of disparity. Few studies contextualized race or ethnicity as social constructs. Almost half of studies combined race/ethnic groups into “other” (n = 8), a practice that has fallen out of favor. Studies rarely mention whether race or ethnicity data come from a self-reported source. The RDE was the most common statistical method for estimating race or ethnic disparity (n = 14), followed by the IOM definition (n = 2) and total difference definition (n = 2). Studies most commonly adjusted for health status/comorbidities and demographics (n = 15), followed by insurance type (n = 11), geographic location (n = 9), and SES (n = 8) in the form of a zip-code linked median household income (Figure 2). Only one study adjusted for AF type, echocardiographic data, and symptoms. None adjusted for AF burden or patient preference. One study adjusted for community level/environment covariate using a zip-code linked social deprivation index.

Differences in Study Covariate Adjustment for Disparities in Catheter Ablation

Heat map displaying which covariates were adjusted for by individual studies of catheter ablation (CA) utilization for atrial fibrillation (AF). Most studies adjusted for demographics and health status. Studies variably adjusted for socioeconomic status (SES), and health access (insurance type), and geographic location. One study adjusted for clinical variables important for catheter ablation selection including atrial fibrillation (AF) type, symptom burden, and echocardiographic data. None adjusted for AF burden or patient preference, 2 key determinants in the decision to pursue CA.

Based on the random effect models, cross-sectional studies showed a lower odds of CA in NHB patients (OR: 0.65; 95% CI: 0.59-0.73; PI: 0.43, 0.98), HLx (OR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.72-0.84; PI: 0.61, 1.00), and a trend to a lower OR for Asian patients (OR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.54-1.01; PI: 0.26, 2.12) (Figure 3). There was a high level of between-study heterogeneity for the NHB (I2 = 96.9%), HLx (I2 = 84.1%), and Asian (I2 = 93.8%) patients. Survival data showed similar trends, with a lower HR for HLx (HR: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.57-0.88; PI: 0.29, 1.76) and Asian (HR: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.48-0.94; PI: 0.02, 22.41) patients, with an attenuated difference for NHB patients (HR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.65-1.01; PI: 0.39, 1.66). There was also a high level of heterogeneity in survival data for all groups. Only the cross-sectional data for NHB and HLx groups had enough studies to generate funnel plots, which did not show significant asymmetry around the overall effect size indicating a low likelihood of publication bias (Supplemental Figure 2). However, there were several studies lying outside the bounds for the expected distribution of studies confirming heterogeneity (Supplemental Figure 3). In addition to visual inspection of the funnel plot, Egger's regression test for publication bias was not significant for NHB group. Among HLx group, there was a trend toward significance (P = 0.079) and indication of potential small-study effects suggesting possible bias in cross-sectional studies.

Differences in Catheter Ablation Utilization by Race and Ethnicity

Forest plots of studies comparing catheter ablation utilization for atrial fibrillation between (A) non-Hispanic Black (NHB), (B) Hispanic/LatinX (HLx), and (C) Asian patients compared to non-Hispanic White (NHW) patients as reference group. For each race or ethnic group, cross-sectional studies reporting ORs were pooled separately from studies reporting time-to-event data as HRs. For each plot, the squares represent the ORs for individual studies, with the size of the square proportional to the study's weight. The horizontal lines represent the 95% CIs. The diamond represents the pooled OR, and its width represents the 95% CI. The vertical dashed line represents an OR or HR of 1 (no difference). Prediction intervals (PIs), which represent the range in which a future study will fall taking into account intra- and between-study heterogeneity is also reported with the pooled effect size. Between-study heterogeneity is reported as the I2 value.

In mixed-effects univariate meta-regression models for cross-sectional NHB studies, overall age of study population significantly moderates the treatment effect (β = 0.03; SE = 0.01; P = 0.02), indicating studies with older populations had a weaker disparity estimate. The model accounted for 45.2% of between-study heterogeneity (R2 = 0.452), but with a high degree of residual heterogeneity (I2 = 83.7%). Other moderators including study year, midpoint of data year range, data source, overall study proportion of NHW patients, overall study proportion of female patients, overall mean age, adjustment for various covariates (health insurance access, demographics, SES), and study quality (risk of bias assessment) did not explain between-study heterogeneity (Supplemental Tables 6 and 7). For the HLx group, the overall proportion of White patients (R2 = 38.7%, residual I2 = 80.04%), overall proportion of female patients (R2 = 7.2%, I2 = 83.1%), midpoint of data enrollment (R2 = 29.6%, I2 = 80.5%), and adjustment for SES (R2 = 35.9%, I2 = 74.6%) contributed to between-study heterogeneity; however, none of these moderators reached statistical significance. Subgroup analyses of cross-sectional data for both NHB and HLx groups showed that disparity estimates differed significantly by data source (P < 0.0001) (Supplemental Figures 4 and 5). Adjustment for SES or geographic location attenuates the disparity estimate modestly (Supplemental Figures 6 to 9).

Sensitivity analyses including “leave-one-out” analysis did not reveal that the exclusion of any one study had an outsized impact on between-study heterogeneity in the NHB cross-sectional data (OR) (Supplemental Figures 10 and 11). In survival data (HR), exclusion of Bhave et al strengthened the disparity estimate (OR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.61-0.88; from OR: 0.81 95% CI: 0.65-1.01) and lowered between-study heterogeneity (I2 61.8% from 92.6%).15 Exclusion of Kummer et al also lowered heterogeneity (I2 65.3% from 92.6%).19,21 In the HLx cross-sectional studies, exclusion of Alhuneafat et al lowered between-study heterogeneity (I2 68.6% from 84.1%) (Supplemental Figures 12 and 13).31 In survival data for HLx patients, exclusion of Kipp et al and Kummer et al only marginally reduced heterogeneity.21,22

Discussion

The primary focus of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to quantify the scale of disparities in CA utilization for AF by race and ethnicity, synthesize and assess the methodological quality of studies, and to investigate sources of heterogeneity for these findings. Our review shows evidence of race and ethnic differences in CA utilization across time and health care settings, but with a high level of between-study heterogeneity in the scale of disparities likely owing to systematic differences in definitions of disparities, methodologies, and data sources. In addition, studies lacked data on key clinical confounders—such as symptom severity and AF burden—limiting interpretation on whether differences reflected inequitable access to care or appropriate clinical variation. Despite these limitations, the findings are consistent with broader racial and ethnic differences in care utilization and quality in cardiovascular care.34,35

While high levels of qualitative heterogeneity indicate that a single point estimate of disparity at the population level is not currently feasible, this meta-analysis offers insights into how differences in data sources and methods influence disparity estimation. Our review suggests that the high degree of between-study heterogeneity stems from variability in source populations and systematic differences in study methods and covariate adjustment strategies. Subgroup analysis revealed significant differences in disparity estimates based on the database used. The only finding of no disparity for NHB patients comes from Bhave et al. This study differs from the others in a few ways, including its use of MediCare data, definition of race and ethnicity, and extensive variable adjustment. Exclusion of this study substantially reduced heterogeneity, further highlighting that data source may be an important contributor to between-study heterogeneity. Interestingly, Bhave et al found a significant disparity for HLx patients despite MediCare access, suggesting that other unmeasured mediators, such as language or sociocultural barriers, may explain disparities for this patient population. The high level of qualitative heterogeneity for Asian patients may reflect that this categorization often combines a large number of diverse groups with differing patterns of SES and access to care. Age was found to be a significant moderator in meta-regression, further supporting that demographics of the source population can influence disparity estimates.

More broadly, the high variability in modeling and covariate choice stems from differences in defining health care disparities and the statistical approaches used to quantify them. Notably, none of the studies explicitly defined health care disparity a priori. The most common approach was the RDE method. However, this approach defines disparity too narrowly by adjusting for important mediators such as SDOH which can underestimate the scale of disparity. Therefore, operationalizing definitions of disparity requires careful consideration of which variables to treat as confounders vs mediators when constructing models of disparity.

Among studies included in our metanalysis, there was variability in which covariates studies adjusted for in regression models, as well as how studies defined covariates like comorbidities. Few studies offered sensitivity analyses to assess the influence of mediators on disparity estimates. Kipp et al is an exception. They performed a sensitivity analysis removing education and SES from their primary model which produced a stronger disparity estimate for NHB and HLx patients highlighting their role as mediators rather than confounders.22 They also show how adjustment for AF type, an important confounder that influences clinical appropriateness, diminished the disparity estimate for NHB patients suggesting that differences in CA utilization may occur because NHB have more advanced disease at the time of diagnosis and management as previously reported.22 While adjustment for SES did not explain differences in disparity estimates, this illustrative example highlights how impactful covariate choices may be on estimates of disparity. Using a single clear definition of health care disparities and accompanying framework for analysis would assist in reducing between study variability.

Ultimately, the current literature on inequity on CA utilization is limited by missing or incomplete data on important factors that influence CA utilization. Few studies included granular data on AF type, burden, symptoms, echocardiographic data, and patient preference. The decision to perform CA is complex, with specific indications based on type of AF, symptom burden, and specific comorbidities like heart failure. Lastly, patient preference ultimately plays a large role in whether service is utilized. Unfortunately, data on shared decision-making and patient preferences can be the most challenging to abstract from unstructured fields and notes in the EHR and lacking entirely from claims data sets. Importantly, data source is often linked to the availability of key variables like SDOH, particularly in administrative and claims databases. This overlap makes it difficult to determine whether observed differences are due to true population differences or simply reflect limitations in data availability and adjustment.24 For example, studies in this review relied on zip-code linked median household income, and only one reported a zip-code linked social deprivation index, which are crude representations of social determinants that may not capture individual-level needs. Lastly misclassification is common with regard to race and ethnicity data, and several categories, like NHB, Asian and HLx, Asian and HLx, are likely too broad to capture the unique experiences of specific groups within the health care system.36, 37, 38

We suspect that the differences in reported race or ethnic disparities in the literature likely underestimate true effect sizes due to overadjustment of mediators by studies and ascertainment bias from delayed or underdetection of AF in underserved populations. We therefore expect that the pooled estimates from the meta-analysis are also an underestimate. The examples above highlight how treating mediators as confounders by controlling for them leads to overadjustment, which can underestimate or mask their role in disparity estimates. These differences may reflect a deeper disconnect between studies aiming to estimate the RDE and those aiming to understand the full range of causal contributors (both direct and indirect) to disparities in CA utilization. Furthermore, given that NHB and HLx patients are less likely to be diagnosed with AF, we expect their presentation for care with more symptomatic and severe cases to result in a higher likelihood of receiving CA.39 Similarly, these groups may also be less well represented in claims databases in general. While the underdetection of AF in these groups speaks to the compounding of disparities throughout the health care process, it also suggests that the scale of disparities in CA utilization is an underestimate. Despite this, the general concordance between cross-sectional and survival data suggests that NHB, HLx, and possibly Asian patients not only have lower odds of receiving CA in the short term but may also face delays in access, leading to higher risk profiles and worse health outcomes in the long term. Such findings should motivate interventions for earlier detection of AF and referral to subspecialty care.

Limitations and recommendations for improving af equity research

Our meta-analysis confirms racial and ethnic disparities in CA use, but data and methodological constraints prevent quantification of the scale or the identification of underlying mechanisms of disparity. Our meta-analysis has several limitations that mirror the gaps in the literature. First, while data suggest disparities in CA utilization by both sex and race, we were unable to abstract sex- and race-specific estimates from enough studies to investigate these subgroups. Second, most studies exhibited inconsistent or incomplete reporting of data, covariates, and models, limiting our ability to fully account for these moderators. Finally, we restricted our review to English-language studies; however, given our focus on the U.S. health care system and its unique sociocultural context, we believe this limitation had minimal impact on the generalizability of our findings. Despite these limitations, our study provides a synthesis of the current literature on inequities in CA utilization by race and ethnicity, identifies key methodological gaps, and offers insights that may lead to improvements in equitable AF care.

Standardized definitions, interoperable SDOH data, and improved methods are key to understanding heterogeneity in estimates of AF disparities and ultimately advancing equitable care for AF (Central Illustration). A standardized definition of disparity such as the IOM definition may improve comparisons of disparity estimates and guide analytic methods by providing a theory-driven framework for model specification.10,40 In order to implement the IOM framework, improved data collection on SDOH is needed at the individual, interpersonal, and community level as highlighted in the recent report by the American College of Cardiology and the American Heart Association which advocates for the implementation of a universal library of data elements in EHRs and national registries.41 More accurate self-reported race and ethnicity can be captured by the U.S. Office of Management and Budget’s new and expanded race and ethnicity categories.42 Improved data collection must be paired with best practices in statistical approaches. Methodological innovation has been an ongoing priority for the National Institute of Minority Health and Health Disparities which published an important report with recommendations on advancing the science of disparities research.43 This report advocates for analytic methods that maximize the ability to draw causal inferences from observational data to better clarify root causes and mediators of disparity. Lastly, there was an under-representation of certain minority groups in the disparities literature on CA for AF including Asian, Native American, Alaskan Native, Pacific Islander, and Native Hawaiian groups emphasizing the need for their inclusion in future research.

Heterogeneity Prevents Reduction of Disparities in Catheter Ablation Utilization

The current evidence demonstrates racial and ethnic disparities in catheter ablation utilization for atrial fibrillation, however high between-study heterogeneity exists due to differences in data sources and a lack of a standardized definition of disparity and covariate adjustment methods. This heterogeneity limits understanding of underlying mechanisms and ultimately ability to reduce disparities in care for atrial fibrillation. Several recommendations and tools are already available to improve consistency of future research to address this important problem. AF = atrial fibrillation; SDOH = social determinants of health.

Conclusions

Racial and ethnic disparities in the utilization of CA for AF remain qualitatively consistent and persist after adjustment for SDOH, access to care, and comorbidity burden, with few exceptions. However, assessing the scale of these disparities with certainty is challenging due to the high level of quantitative heterogeneity between studies arising from differences in source population, disparity definitions, methodological approaches, and data gaps. The current literature most likely underestimates the true size of disparities given global ascertainment bias of AF in minority populations, further emphasizing the need for early detection and diagnosis of AF. To advance equity in AF care, future research must adopt a standardized framework that utilizes the IOM definition of disparity and corresponding methods to generate comparable estimates and clarify the pathways of disparity.10 Furthermore, implementing this framework will require granular data on patient preferences, shared decision-making, clinical appropriateness, and key mediators such as SDOH to fully clarify the causal pathways of disparity. A unified approach will also be crucial to reducing disparities by understanding their multifactorial causes, evaluating interventions to improve AF care, and guiding policy.

COMPETENCY IN PRACTICE-BASED LEARNING: This systematic review and meta-analysis found that racial and ethnic minorities are less likely to undergo CA for AF. However, substantial heterogeneity across studies limits clear interpretation of the magnitude of disparities and obscures their underlying drivers.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Findings underscore how disparity estimates are shaped by the definitions and analytic methods implemented. Advancing equity research in AF and broader cardiovascular care will require standardized definitions of disparity, theory-informed analytic frameworks, and improved data infrastructure—including interoperable and granular data on self-reported race and ethnicity, and SDOH.
