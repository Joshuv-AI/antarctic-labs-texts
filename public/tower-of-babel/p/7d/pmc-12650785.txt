Epstein-Barr virus LMP1 promotes tumorigenesis in a murine epithelial tumor model
Authors: Jing Tan, Yalin Li, Luting Hu, Yuxiang Zhao, Weicheng Yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), an oncogenic herpesvirus, is associated with diverse epithelial and lymphatic malignancies. The latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1) is well-established as a key oncogenic driver in B-cell malignancies, while its functional role in epithelial tumors remains poorly understood. This study aimed to establish an epithelial tumor model stably expressing LMP1 and investigate its oncogenic functions and mechanisms. We generated mouse lung epithelial cells TC-1 stably expressing EBV LMP1 using the PiggyBac transposon system (TC-1-LMP1). The TC-1-LMP1 model demonstrated that LMP1 enhanced proliferative and invasive capacities while reducing cell adhesion in vitro, and promotes aggressive tumor growth in vivo. RNA-seq analysis revealed that LMP1 increased the expression of Myc, leading to enhanced ribosome biogenesis and mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming. Our findings demonstrate that LMP1 act as a potent oncogenic driver in epithelial cells by regulating the expression and function of Myc, thereby advancing the understanding of the mechanism underlying EBV-associated epithelial tumorigenesis. The TC-1-LMP1 model could also serve as a valuable platform for investigating LMP1-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), an oncogenic gamma-herpesvirus, the first human tumor virus discovered in Burkitt lymphoma (BL) in 1964 [1], establishes lifelong infection in ∼95 % of adults and contributes to ∼1.9 % of global cancer cases (up to 357,900) [2]. EBV infection exhibits tropism for both epithelial and B lymphocytes cells [3], leading to a variety of epithelial and lymphatic malignancies, including BL, Hodgkin's lymphoma (HL), EBV-positive diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DL-BCL), posttransplant lymphoproliferative disease (PTLD), as well as gastric carcinoma (GC) and nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). In addition to its association with malignancies, EBV is also linked with non-malignant disease including infectious mononucleosis (IM), oral hairy leukoplakia, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and multiple sclerosis (MS) [4].

Latency is a hallmark of EBV, and the virus establishes lifelong latency with only sporadic episodes of reactivation and lytic replication. Of the viral proteins expressed during latency, the latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1) stands out as the principal oncogenic effector, capable of transforming rodent fibroblasts and being essential for B-cell immortalization into lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) [5,6]. LMP1 functions as a structural and functional mimic of tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor CD40 [7,8]. The C-terminal flexible cytoplasmic tail of LMP1 harbors three C-terminal activation regions (CTAR1-3) serve as scaffolds for recruiting tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR)-associated factors (TRAFs) and TNFR-associated death domain (TRADD) proteins, thereby activating multiple signaling pathways, such as the nuclear factor-kB (NF-kB), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) and Janus kinase (JAK) [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. LMP1 manipulated fundamental cellular processes including survival, proliferation and immune evasion [[13], [14], [15]].

LMP1's oncogenic roles in B-cell malignancies have been extensively characterized with various experimental models [5,[16], [17], [18]], but its functions in epithelial cancers, particularly in NPC and GC, remain less understood despite clear clinical associations [19,20]. There are limited reports of LMP1 associated epithelial tumors model to explore the molecular interplay between LMP1-driven intrinsic signaling in epithelial tumors. The TC-1 mouse lung epithelial cell line has proven valuable for studying other viral oncoproteins, including EBV LMP2 and HPV E6/E7, as well as for vaccine development and host-pathogen interaction studies [[21], [22], [23]].

The Myc transcription factor also represents crucial player in EBV-associated oncogenesis. It belongs to a family of “super-transcription factors” that regulates approximately 15 % of the genome and coordinates diverse cellular processes including ribosome biogenesis, metabolism, and cell cycle progression [[24], [25], [26]]. Myc is frequently dysregulated due to chromosomal translocations in aggressive EBV1 lymphomas, including endemic BL and some cases of DL-BCL [27]. LMP1 enhances Myc-mediated degradation of the p27kip1 tumor suppressor and accelerates Myc-induced lymphomagenesis [28]. Latent EBV infection collaborates with Myc over-expression in normal human B cells to induce Burkitt-like Lymphomas in mice [29]. What's more, LMP1 oncogenic interact with Myc via increasing Pim kinase 1 cytoplasmic retention in B cell lymphoma model [30]. However, whether this regulatory relationship extends to epithelial malignancies remains unknown.

In this study, we established an epithelial tumor model by stably expressing EBV LMP1 in TC-1 cells using the PiggyBac transposon system. Through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo characterization combined with transcriptomic analysis, we demonstrate that LMP1 promotes tumorigenesis by enhancing cell proliferation and invasion capacities while reducing cell adhesion, mechanistically through over-expressing Myc to activate of the ribosome biogenesis and mitochondrial metabolism. Furthermore, this TC-1-LMP1 model provides a valuable platform for evaluating LMP1-targeted therapeutic strategies, including vaccine development.

Materials and methods

Cells and mice

The TC-1 cell line was derived from C57BL/6 mouse lung epithelial cells and was obtained from American tissue culture collection (ATCC, Catalogue number: CRL-2493). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. And the cell line was authenticated within three years of use and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, specific pathogen-free) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Zhejiang Province and housed under pathogen-free condition. All animal-related experiments in this study were approved by the Animal Experimental Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Institute of Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Approval number: AP2024-07-0057).

Construction of TC-1-LMP1 cell line

The LMP1 stably expressing cell line was established using the PB transposon system (containing an EGFP reporter gene) available in our laboratory. The full-length EBV LMP1 gene (GenBank accession: KY118083.1) was codon-optimized and synthesized commercially (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The LMP1 gene was amplified by the specific primers LMP1-F (5′- GATTCTAGAGCTAGCGAATTCGAAGCCACCATGGACTATAAGGATCATGATGGGGATTA-3′) and LMP1-R (5′- TTCGCGGCCGCGGATCCGATTTAAATCAGTCATAGTAGGACAGTTGCACAGGG-3′). The plasmid PB was linearized using the specific primers PB-F (5′- TTTAAATCGGATCCGCGGCC-3′) and PB-R (5′- TTCGAATTCGCTAGCTCTAGAATCTTC-3′). Following purification, the PCR products were assembled into the linearized PB plasmid using a homologous recombination kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The resulting recombinant plasmid, named as pPB-LMP1, was verified by sequencing. A control plasmid containing only EGFP (pPB-EGFP) was similarly constructed.

For stable cell line generation, TC-1 cells were co-transfected with pPB-LMP1 and PB transposase helper plasmid (pPBase) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The pPBase plasmid expresses hyperactive PB transposase to facilitate genomic integration. At 72 h post-transfection, stable TC-1-LMP1 clones were selected with 7.5 μg/mL puromycin (YEASEN, Shanghai, China) and subsequently isolated by flow sorting using the co-expressed EGFP fluorescence (Beckman MoFlo Astrios EQs, Brea, CA, USA). Parallel control TC-1-EGFP cell line was generated following the same protocol.

TC-1-LMP1 cell line stability and identification

To assess the stability of TC-1-LMP1 cell line, the 1st (P1) and 20th (P20) passages were harvested and analyzed by reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction amplifications (RT-PCR) and Western blot. For RT-PCR, the total RNA from cells were extracted using the RNApure Kit (Aidlab, Beijing, China) and reverse transcribed into cDNA with the TRUEscript 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (Aidlab). The LMP1 gene was amplified via PCR using the previously described primers (LMP1-F/R), and the products were resolved on a 1 % agarose gel. The identity of the inserted LMP1 sequence was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Sangon).

For Western blot, the harvested samples were subjected to 12 % SDS-PAGE and subsequently characterized by Western blot as described previously [31]. In briefly, the protein samples transferred onto PVDF membranes and incubated with LMP1 mouse monoclonal antibody (1:1000 dilution; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or GAPDH rabbit monoclonal antibody (1:2000 dilution; Cell signaling technology, Massachusetts, USA). The horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse/anti-rabbit IgG (ABclonal, Wuhan, China) diluted 1:5000 was used as the secondary antibody.

Confocal immunofluorescence and flow cytometry analysis

The immunofluorescence was performed as previously described [32]. Briefly, Cells were collected, washed with PBS and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (Sangon) for 20 min at room temperature (RT). After permeabilization with 0.3 % (v/v) Triton X-100, cells were blocked with 3 % (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Biosharp, Hefei, China) at 37 °C for 45 min. For immunostaining, cells were incubated with mouse anti-LMP1 monoclonal antibody (1:500 dilution, Abcam) at 37 °C for 90 min, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200 dilution, ABclonal) at 37 °C for 60 min. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 15 min at RT. Confocal imaging was performed using a Nikon A1 (RHD25) confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with 405 nm, 488 nm and 561 nm laser lines. Each fluorescence channel was acquired sequentially under optimized conditions to prevent spectral overlap.

To verify the purity of TC-1-LMP1 cells, flow cytometry was conducted. Harvested cells were washed with cell staining buffer (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), and analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman CytoFlex S, USA). Purity was assessed by detecting both the intrinsic EGFP fluorescence and immunostaining with an anti-LMP1 primary antibody (1:200, Abcam), followed by an AF594-conjugated secondary antibody (1:100, ABclonal). Data were processed with FlowJo software.

Growth curve, adhesion assays, wound healing assay and transwell invasion assay

To assess cellular proliferation, 5 × 103 TC-1-LMP1 cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates. Cell viability was measured at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h using CCK-8 reagent (YEASEN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. And the absorbance was quantified at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan Spark, Switzerland).

To evaluate the TC-1-LMP1 cells adhesion ability, Matrigel (2 μg/50 μl per well) was added in 96-well plates, and allowed to air-dried overnight on an ultra-clean bench. 5 × 104 cells/well were plated and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to allow adhesion. Non-adherent cells were removed by PBS washing, then adherent cells were assessed by CCK-8 reagent (YEASEN). And the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (‌Tecan Spark).

To analyze the migration capacity of TC-1-LMP1 cells, 2 × 104 cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured to 90–100 % confluence. Then the cells were scratched and the debris was removed using medium. Plates were maintained at 37 °C and imaged every 4 h for 72 h, using a 10 × objective lens and analyzed by Incucyte SX5 (Sartorius, Gottingen, Germany).

To evaluate the invasion potential of TC-1-LMP1 cells, Matrigel-coated chambers (Corning, New York, USA) were used. 5 × 104 cells in serum-free medium were added to the upper chamber, while 20 % FBS medium was placed in the lower chamber. After incubated at 37 °C for 48 h, non-invading cells were removed from the upper surface with cotton swabs and invaded cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (Sangon), and stained with 0.1 % crystal violet (Sangon). Photographs were obtained using a microscope and counted using ImageJ software.

Animal experiments

Female C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 105 TC-1-EGFP or TC-1-LMP1 cells (n = 6). Tumor volume was measured every 3 days using calipers and calculated as V = 0.5 × length × width2. Animals were euthanized when mice either showed signs of suffering (loss of >20 % of initial body weight, severe lethargy or unresponsiveness to gentle stimulation and inability to access food or water) [33], or the tumor volume exceeded 2000 mm3. And tumor tissues were processed for histology.

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (Sangon), paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. For HE staining, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Solarbio, Beijing, China). For IHC, antigen retrieval was first performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Tissue sections were then incubated with the respective primary antibodies against LMP1 (Abcam), Ki67 (Abcam), and Myc (Cell Signaling Technology), followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (ABclonal). Staining was visualized with DAB (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China).

RNA-seq and bioinformatics

Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and sequencing-ready cDNA libraries were prepared following manufacturer's protocol. The resulting cDNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq6000 platform (Novogene Co., Ltd. Beijing, China). Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using FastQC (v.0.12.1) and subsequently processed with fastp (v.0.23.4) to remove low-quality bases and adapter sequences. The high-quality reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using STAR (v. 2.7.11b). Gene expression levels were quantified using StringTie (v.2.2.3). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of |log2(FoldChange)|≥1 and adjusted P-value ≤ 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the R package cluster Profiler and GSEABase. Volcano plots were created using ggplot2 (v3.4.2) to visualize DEGs. Heatmaps of significant DEGs were generated using pheatmap (v1.0.12) with hierarchical clustering. Interactive visualization and additional analyses were performed using the online platform https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/.

RNA synthesis rate assay and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) assay

The RNA synthesis rate assay was measured using BeyoClick™ EU RNA Synthesis Kit with AF594 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions [34]. Briefly, TC-1-LMP1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured overnight. The cells were then pulsed with 1 mM EU solution for 2 h at 37 °C. After labeling, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (Sangon) and permeabilized with 0.3 % Triton X-100 (Sangon). The click reaction was performed by adding 50 μl of click additive solution and incubating for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Fluorescence intensity (excitation: 590 nm, emission: 615 nm) was quantified using a microplate reader. (‌Tecan Spark).

Mitochondrial respiration was assessed by measuring the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) using a commercial assay kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol [35]. TC-1-LMP1 cells were seeded in a 96-well microplate and cultured overnight. Then, 100 μl of Reagent I (probe) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After adding 50 μl of Reagent II (sealing solution), the microplate was immediately transferred to a Tecan Spark multifunctional microplate reader preheated to 37 °C. Fluorescence readings (excitation: 405 nm, emission: 675 nm) were taken every 2 min for 90 min in dynamic mode. The OCR values were determined from the slope of the fluorescence intensity versus time curve.

RNA interference (RNAi)

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting Myc (siMyc, 5′-AAGGUAGUGAUCCUCAAAAdTdT-3′) and non-targeting control siRNA (siCtrl) were designed and synthesized by GenScript (Nangjing, China). For siRNA transfection, TC-1-LMP1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and transfected with 100 pmol siMyc or siCtrl using 10 μl of Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer's protocol. Knockdown efficiency was evaluated at 48 h post-transfection by Western blot and RT-qPCR.

Subsequently, functional assays, including cell growth curve, Transwell invasion, adhesion, RNA synthesis rate, and OCR, were performed on siMyc-transfected TC-1-LMP1 cells 48 h after transfection, using the methods described above. Cells transfected with siCtrl served as the negative control.

Real-time reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was performed as previously described [36]. Total RNA was extracted from the cells using RNApure Kit (Aidlab) and 1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA with the TRUEscript 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (Aidlab). The relative expression levels of selected differentially expressed genes were quantified with SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a BioRad CFX Connect Real-time PCR Detection System (California, USA), using GAPDH as the endogenous control. Gene expression fold changes were calculated using the comparative threshold cycle (2−ΔΔCT) method.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed by unpaired Student's t-test, and multiple groups by one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test using GraphPad Prism 10. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

LMP1 enhances cell proliferation and invasion capacity while reducing cellular adhesion in vitro

To investigate the functional role of LMP1 in vitro, we performed CCK-8 assay to assess the cell viability. The results demonstrated that LMP1 overexpression significantly accelerated cell proliferation. At 48 h post incubation, TC-1-LMP1 cells exhibited 2.6-fold higher viability compared to TC-1-EGFP cells (Fig. 4A). Transwell invasion assays showed that TC-1-LMP1 cells had significantly enhanced invasiveness (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, Adhesion assays revealed a marked reduction in the adhesive capacity of TC-1-LMP1 cells relative to TC-1-EGFP cells (Fig. 4C; P < 0.01). In contrast, wound healing assays detected no significant difference in migration between TC-1-EGFP and TC-1-LMP1 cells (Fig. 4D), suggesting that LMP1 specifically promotes invasion rather than motility. LMP1 promotes aggressive tumor growth in vivo

To assess the tumorigenic potential of LMP1 in vivo. TC-1-EGFP and TC-1-LMP1 cells were implanted in C57BL/6 mice. Subcutaneous xenograft models demonstrated that TC-1-LMP1 tumors grew significantly faster than LMP-1-EGFP controls. At 15 days post-implantation, the average tumor volume in the TC-1-LMP1 group (1973.53 ± 360.19 mm3) was approximately 4.2-fold larger than those in the TC-1-EGFP group (467.02 ± 170.81 mm3; Fig. 5A). Consequently, mice bearing TC-1-LMP1 tumors exhibited a significantly shorter overall survival period compared to those in the control group (Fig. 5B). H&E staining showed that the TC-1-LMP1 tumor cell nuclei were larger while the cytoplasm shrank, which were typical of malignant cells (Fig. 5C). The IHC analysis revealed strong LMP1 expression in TC-1-LMP1 tumors, in contrast to the absence of signal in TC-1-EGFP control. Consistent with this finding, the expression of the key proliferation and invasion genes Ki67 and Myc was also markedly upregulated in TC-1-LMP1 tumors (Fig. 5D). LMP1 promotes tumorigenesis through Myc-mediated enhancement of ribosome function and mitochondrial metabolism

To elucidate the molecular mechanism of the oncogenic effect of LMP1 in vitro and in vivo, we adopted transcriptome analysis between TC-1-LMP1 and TC-1-EGFP cells. Principal component analysis (PCA) of normalized data revealed distinct transcriptional profiles between the two groups (Fig. 6A). We identified 744 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in TC-1-LMP1 cells, comprising 372 up-regulated and 372 down-regulated genes (Fig. 6B), with expression patterns visualized by heatmap (Fig. 6C). GO enrichment analysis of biological processes showed significant alterations in pathways related to ribosome biogenesis, RNA modification, and mitochondrial metabolism (Fig. 6D). GO analysis of Myc target genes revealed predominant involvement in ribosome and mitochondrial functions (Fig. 6F), consistent with the overall DEGs enrichment pattern. These results demonstrate that LMP1 enhances ribosome function and mitochondrial metabolism may through Myc-mediated regulation of target genes.

To validate the RNA-seq data, some key DEGs were selected for confirmation by RT-qPCR, and the results were consistent with the transcriptomic data (Fig. 6G). Subsequently, functional assays revealed that both the RNA synthesis rate and the OCR were significantly increased in TC-1-LMP1 cells compared to TC-1-EGFP control (Fig. 6H and I). Collectively, these findings align with the transcriptome analysis, indicating that LMP1 enhances ribosome biogenesis and mitochondrial metabolism in TC-1-LMP1 cells.

To further determine whether LMP1 mediates the enhancement of ribosome biogenesis and mitochondrial metabolism by Myc, we first confirmed that LMP1 upregulated Myc expression via transcriptome analysis and RT-qPCR (Fig. 6E and G). Then the expression of Myc in TC-1-LMP1 was knocked down by transfected with siRNA (siMyc, Fig. 7A). Myc knockdown led to decreased cell proliferation (Fig. 7B) and invasion (Fig. 7C), alongside increased cellular adhesion (Fig. 7D), compared to the siCtrl group. Furthermore, both the RNA synthesis rate and OCR were reduced upon Myc knockdown (Fig. 7E and F). These results demonstrate that Myc is a key downstream effector through which LMP1 enhances ribosome activity and mitochondrial metabolism to promote oncogenicity. Discussion

EBV is a highly successful pathogen, infecting more than 95 % of the global population [4,37]. The oncoprotein LMP1 plays a critical role in the early development of EBV-mediated pathogenesis and disease phenotypes [13,37]. Extensive studies have demonstrated that LMP1 is essential for transformation and proliferation in multiple cell backgrounds in vitro as well as in transgenic mice [5,16]. Additionally, LMP1 contributes to cytokine and chemokine induction, immune modulation, apoptotic resistance, cell migration, cell-cell contact, and invasive growth of cancer cells [[13], [14], [15]]. Functionally, LMP1 mimics CD40 costimulatory receptor, recruiting TRAFs and TRADD proteins via its CTAR1-3 domains to activate multiple signaling pathways, including NF-κB, PI3K, and JAK, thereby exerting its oncogenic effects [[9], [10], [11]].

While the roles and mechanisms of LMP1 in B cells have been extensively studied, its contribution to epithelial malignancies (NPC and GC) remains less understood, particularly regarding the intrinsic signaling pathways driving tumor invasion and metastasis [19,20]. Thus, we established a murine epithelial cell model for LMP1. The TC-1 cell line, previously utilized for studying EBV LMP2 and HPV E6/E7 in preclinical vaccine evaluations, as well as H9N2 influenza virus-host immune interactions, served as an ideal platform [[21], [22], [23],38,39]. Although EBV cannot infect mice, the murine model serves as the closest available approximation of human biology. The use of a murine model is a deliberate reductionist approach, aimed not at recapitulating the natural history of EBV infection, but at dissecting the fundamental, conserved functions of its oncoproteins.

In this study, we generated TC-1 cells stably expressing LMP1 (Figss. 1–3) and demonstrated that EBV LMP1 acts as a master oncogene, coordinately enhancing proliferation and invasion while reducing cell adhesion in vitro (Fig. 4), and driving aggressive tumor growth in vivo (Fig. 5). Notably, these tumorigenic effects were consistently observed across both in vitro and in vivo models.

Our results showed that TC-1-LMP1 cells exhibited enhanced invasiveness in Transwell assays (Fig. 4B) but did not show a significant migratory difference in wound healing assays (Fig. 4D). This apparent discrepancy may be explained by the fundamental distinction between invasion and migration: invasion requires the active degradation and penetration of the extracellular matrix (ECM) barrier [40], a component absent in the two-dimensional wound healing assay. Critically, our transcriptome and RT-qPCR analyses revealed a significant upregulation of Mmp9 in TC-1-LMP1 cells (Fig. 6E and G). Since matrix metalloproteinases (Mmps) like Mmp9 are essential for degrading the ECM during invasion [41,42]. We propose that the increased invasiveness of TC-1-LMP1 cells is primarily driven by this enhanced proteolytic capacity, which is not a requisite for migration in a simple wound closure model.

Transcriptomic analysis of TC-1-LMP1 versus TC-1-EGFP cells revealed that LMP1 likely promotes tumorigenesis by augmenting ribosome function and mitochondrial metabolism through over-expressing Myc (Fig. 6). To validate the transcriptome findings, we knocked down Myc in TC-1-LMP1 cells by siRNA. This intervention reduced both RNA synthesis rate and OCR, ultimately diminishing cell proliferation and oncogenic potential (Fig. 7). The reciprocal regulatory interplay between Myc and LMP1 has been reported in EBV-associated B-cell lymphomas [28,29]. Here, we observed up-regulated Myc expression in TC-1-LMP1 cells, correlating with enhanced ribosome activity and mitochondrial metabolism, which collectively promote cell proliferation and oncogenicity. These findings suggest that Myc may serve as a potential therapeutic target for EBV-associated epithelial tumors [43,44].

Furthermore, tumor vaccines represent a promising immunotherapeutic strategy for EBV-associated malignancies like NPC [45]. While AAV-LMP2 and MVA-EL vaccines have shown efficacy in eliciting cellular immune responses, and mRNA-based LMP2 vaccines are currently in clinical trials (NCT05714748) [22,46,47], the evaluation of LMP1-specific vaccines remains limited, particularly in assessing their tumor-challenge efficacy. The stable expression of LMP1 was generated in mouse epithelial cells rather than in human cells, which was beneficial for evaluating the efficacy of the vaccine based on LMP1 using the complete immune system of mice. Our TC-1-LMP1 model provides a valuable tool for testing the anti-tumor potential of LMP1-targeted vaccines, thereby advancing the development of EBV immunotherapy.
