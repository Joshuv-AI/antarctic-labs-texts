RNF144A as a potential risk gene for endometrial carcinoma: Insights from Mendelian randomization, bulk RNA sequencing, single-cell RNA, and experimental analysis
Authors: Xiqin Ruan, Ni Wang, Qingwen Xie, Yi Du
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is a prevalent gynecological malignancy that poses a significant threat to women’s health worldwide. However, its pathogenesis and underlying mechanisms remains unclear. In this study, expression quantitative trait loci data, Mendelian randomization analysis, and differential expression analysis were performed to identify potential targets. A prognostic risk signature was subsequently constructed for EC patients based on the expression of these genes. Four bioinformatics algorithms, including generalized linear model, extreme gradient boosting, support vector machine, and random forest, were used to identify hub genes in EC. The expression of ring finger protein 144A (RNF144A) was validated using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction. Cellular proliferation and migration ability were evaluated using CCK-8 and Transwell assays, respectively. The genes RNF144A, ketohexokinase, and Rab interacting lysosomal protein like 2 were identified as potential targets for EC. Their differential expression was observed in EC patients, and Mendelian randomization analysis revealed a negative correlation between these genes and the development of EC. Mechanistic analyses suggested a strong association between these genes and the tumor immune microenvironment. The constructed risk signature was significantly associated with the prognosis, age, cancer stage, and grade of EC patients. Furthermore, based on interacted model algorithms, RNF144A was identified as a hub gene in EC. It was found to be significantly downregulated in EC samples, and its expression was positively correlated with the stage and grade of EC patients. In vitro experiments showed that overexpression of RNF144A significantly promoted cell growth and migration in EC cells. In conclusion, this study provides insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying EC progression and identifies preliminary candidate biomarkers for the development of EC treatment strategies.

1. Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is the most common gynecological cancer in developed countries, with a rise of 55% in incidence and 23% in mortality over the past 30 years.[1] EC is clinically categorized into 2 main subtypes: type I, including endometrioid adenocarcinomas and mucinous adenocarcinomas, and type II, characterized by less common pathological variants like clear cell carcinoma, serous carcinoma, and carcinosarcoma.[2] The endometrioid adenocarcinoma subtype is the most prevalent form of EC. Thanks to its generally slow growth and early symptom detection, including abnormal vaginal bleeding, the majority of EC cases are discovered at an early stage.[3,4] Early detection allows for treatment with surgery, potentially combined with radiation or chemotherapy, which can lead to a 5-year survival rate ranging from 74% to 91%.[5] Conversely, patients with poorly differentiated tumors or those with metastasis face a markedly lower 5-year survival rate.[6] Despite these treatment modalities, effective biomarkers for the prognostic evaluation and routine diagnosis of EC remain elusive. In the last 10 years, the advent of immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors, has marked a significant shift in the treatment of various solid tumors, including advanced or recurrent EC, melanoma, and several others.[7–9] Nonetheless, the effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitors is often constrained by factors such as the status of DNA mismatch repair.[10] Consequently, there is a necessity to uncover more effective biomarkers, providing a theoretical basis for the construction of accurate prognostic feature models to predict patient outcomes.

Bioinformatics analysis has become an increasingly important tool for identifying driver genes and exploring underlying mechanisms in various diseases. It encompasses a range of dimensions, including transcriptome alterations and copy number variations, to identify key factors associated with cancer progression and clinical outcomes. Recently, the Mendelian randomization (MR) method, using single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), has been widely applied in epidemiological studies.[11] This method effectively reduces biases in traditional observational studies.[12,13] An increasing number of SNPs have been reported to be linked to the progression of various cancers, including EC.[14] However, the identification of prognosis-related SNPs in cancer tissues is still limited, and the mechanisms by which they influence cancer outcomes and clinical behaviors are not fully understood. The expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) mapping, which correlates gene expression with SNPs, may serve as an effective bridge between genotypes and human phenotypes.[15,16] In this study, we utilized eQTL and bulk RNA sequencing technologies to identify hub genes in EC. Additionally, single-cell transcriptomics was used to provide high-resolution insights into the functional characteristics and cellular types associated with EC. Experimental analyses were conducted to validate the gene expression and progression-promoting findings obtained from public databases. Our aim is to provide a more accurate assessment of the correlation between these hub genes and EC and to offer valuable insights for EC diagnosis.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Source of genome-wide association study (GWAS) data

The GWAS statistical data for EC were obtained from the GWAS Catalog (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk) with the ID ebi-a-GCST90018838. This cohort consisted of 237,839 controls and 2188 individuals with EC of European ancestry. The following criteria were applied to filter out valid instrumental variables for bidirectional MR: (1) each IV had to meet a significance threshold of 5 × 10−8. (2) Linkage disequilibrium between SNPs was calculated using the 1000 Genomes European reference panel, with a linkage disequilibrium threshold set at r2 < 0.001 and a clumping window size of 10,000 kb. (4) Weak instrumental variables, as indicated by an F-statistic < 10, were eliminated to prevent bias. (5) Palindromic SNPs were excluded.

2.2. MR analysis

For the MR analysis, several methods were employed, including the inverse variance-weighted (IVW), weighted median, MR Egger, simple mode, and weighted mode methods. The IVW model was regarded as the primary approach for determining the causal association between exposure and outcome. Heterogeneity among SNPs was examined using Cochrane’s Q statistic test, with a significance threshold set at P < .05. Moreover, sensitivity analysis methods, such as the MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier test, MR-Egger intercept test, and leave-one-out analysis, were primarily conducted in this study. The degree of horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier global test and MR-Egger intercept analysis. Leave-one-out analysis was performed to assess whether significant results were caused by specific SNPs. Scatter plots and funnel plots were used for sensitivity assessment. All MR analyses were conducted using the “TwoSampleMR” R package (version 4.1.3).

2.3. Mapping SNPs to genes

The online web-based variant annotation tool SNPnexus (https://www. snp-nexus.org/v4/) was applied to map each queried variant to its closest gene, as described in a previous study.[17] Subsequently, MR analysis was performed on the identified exposure genes and SNPs. The comprehensive statistical information on eQTLs was obtained from a CAGE study, which investigated transcript gene expression levels in peripheral blood from 2765 individuals of European descent.[18] We selected independent eQTLs for genes with a significance level of P < .05 for further analysis.

2.4. Source of bulk RNA data

The transcriptional gene expression of EC was downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database on February 1, 2024 (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). This dataset consisted of 35 normal endometrial tissues and 554 EC samples, along with corresponding clinical information, including overall survival (OS) status, OS time, gender, age, TNM stage, and cancer grades. Meanwhile, datasets GSE39099 (10 normal endometrium and 40 EC samples), GSE17025 (12 normal endometrium and 91 EC samples), GSE63678 (5 normal endometrium and 7 EC samples), and GSE120490 (145 EC samples) were used as external validation cohorts from GEO repository (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The “SVA” R package was used to eliminate batch effects among 4 GEO cohorts. The “PCA” R package was used to conduct dimensionality reduction analysis and obtain the reduced dimensionality matrix. All public data resources were obtained following relevant guidelines, and no ethical approval or individual consent was required for this study.

2.5. Identification of hub genes for EC

Differential gene expression analysis between TCGA-EC patients and endometrial tissues was performed using the “limma” R package with the criteria of |log fold change| > 1 and P < .05. The overlapping genes from the differential expression analysis and eQTL analysis were selected and visualized using the “VennDiagram” R package. Subsequently, these selected genes underwent univariate Cox regression analysis using the “survival” R package with a criterion of P < .01.

2.6. Construction of risk signature

Three identified genes from the univariate Cox regression analysis were used to construct the risk signature through a multivariate Cox proportional-hazards model. The median value of the calculated risk score was used as a threshold to classify EC patients into high- and low-risk subgroups. The distribution of risk subgroups was visualized using principal component analysis (PCA) and t-SNE analyses conducted with the “Rtsne” R package. Kaplan–Meier survival plots were generated to assess the prognostic value using the “survival” R package, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to calculate the predictive accuracy of the risk signature using the “timeROC” and “cliROC” R packages. Additionally, correlations between risk scores and clinical features including age, TNM stage, and tumor grade were explored. A nomogram integrating risk valuations and clinical variables was generated to predict the outcomes of EC patients at 1, 3, and 5 years using the “rms” R package, and its predictive accuracy was assessed using a calibration plot.

2.7. Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

The scRNA-seq transcriptomic data used in this study was obtained from the GSE173682 database, which included data from 5 individuals with EC. All scRNA-seq data were analyzed using the “Seurat” R package according to previous study.[19] To ensure data quality, cells with fewer than 1000 expressed genes or with mitochondrial gene content exceeding 20% were filtered out. The remaining data were transformed into a Seurat object, and the “NormalizeData” function was applied for data normalization. Next, the top 1500 highly variable genes were identified using the “FindVariableFeatures” function. PCA was then performed to select the top 20 principal components using the “RunPCA” function. To visualize the data, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimension reduction was applied to the identified principal components using the “RunUMAP” function. Cell clusters were identified using the “FindClusters” function. The identified clusters were annotated with known cell types using the “SingleR” R package. The expression levels of hub genes in each cell type were determined using the “FindMarkers” function and visualized based on violin, UMAP, and bubble plots. MUC16 (also known as CA125) was used to identify malignant EC cells, as described in a previous study.[20]

2.8. Immune cell infiltration analysis

The CIBERSORT algorithm was applied to assess the proportions of 22 distinct immune cells infiltration in EC samples. The “vioplot” R package was used to visualize the abundance of these immune cells. Moreover, the correlation between the expression of hub genes and immune cell infiltration was also calculated.

2.9. Model algorithms construction

Thehub genes identified from eQTL analysis, DEGs, and Cox regression analysis were applied to construct model algorithms, including support vector machine, extreme gradient boosting, generalized linear models, and random forest. R packages “dalex,” “xgboost,” “caret,” and “randomForest” were used to construct these algorithms, respectively. Residual analysis was used to evaluate the accuracy of algorithms, followed by plotting the reverse cumulative distribution of residuals. ROC curves were also plotted by using the “pROC” R package. Each hub gene in the algorithms was assigned an importance score, and ROC curves were plotted for each hub gene in EC. Based on the results of the ROC analysis and model algorithms, ring finger protein 144A (RNF144A) was selected for further analyses. Differential expression analysis was used to compare the expression level of RNF144A between EC and normal control samples. Furthermore, relationship between RNF144A expression and clinical features was also explored.

2.10. Functional enrichment analysis of RNF144A

Functional enrichment analysis, including gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and gene ontology (GO) pathways, was conducted using the “clusterProfiler” R package. GSEA analysis was performed to detect Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways based on the “c2.cp.kegg.symbols.gmt” sets. GO analysis included biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. The significance threshold was set at P < .05.

2.11. Cell culture and transfection

Human endometrial cells, hEM15A, and EC cells, HEC1A, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. The cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 100 units/mL penicillin/streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Recombinant adenoviral vectors carrying RNF144A were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China) and transfected following the manufacturer’s instructions. After confirming the transfection efficiency of RNF144A, EC cells with upregulated RNF144A expression were used for subsequent experiments.

2.12. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Differential gene expression level of RNF144A was verified by qRT-PCR analysis. Total RNA was extracted through Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). The Prime Script RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Japan) was used for reverse transcription. Finally, the SYBR green master mix (TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Japan) was performed for qRT-PCR reactions. The primer sequences of RNF144A were as follows: forward: 5′-CCACCTACAGGAGAACGAG-3′, reverse: 5′-TCCGACAGGGATCAAACA-3′.

2.13. Cell proliferation assay

The transfected cells were seeded at 24, 48, and 72 hours in 96-well plates. CCK-8 solution (Dojindo Laboratories, Shanghai, China) was added to each well and incubated for 2 hours. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

2.14. Transwell assay

Cells in Transwell chambers were cultured in 24-well plates (Corning, Lowell, MA). After incubation for 6 hours, the migrated cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. The migrated cells were counted under a microscope.

3. Results

3.1. Identification of potential hub genes in EC

A total of 7492 DEGs were successfully identified from the TCGA cohort by comparing EC samples with normal endometrial tissues (Fig. 1A, Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N672). The heatmap presented the distribution of the top 50 significantly regulated genes in EC (Fig. 1B). By conducting MR analysis of eQTL, 265 eQTL target genes showed a significant causal relationship with EC based on the IVW method (P < .05; Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N672). After excluding genes with inconsistent OR directions among the 5 MR methods and genes exhibiting pleiotropy (P > .05; Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N672), we selected 215 EC risk-associated genes (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N672). The Venn diagram illustrated the intersection of DEGs and selected eQTL target genes, resulting in 88 overlapping genes (Fig. 1C). Among them, 3 genes, namely ring finger protein 144A (RNF144A), ketohexokinase (KHK), and Rab interacting lysosomal protein like 2 (RILPL2), exhibited a significant correlation with the OS of EC patients (P < .05; Fig. 1D). Therefore, these 3 genes were chosen as the focus of our study and subjected to further analysis.

Identification of hub genes in EC. Volcano plot (A) and heatmap (B) of DEGs in the TCGA database. (C) The Venn diagram. (D) Univariate Cox regression analysis selected prognosis associated genes.

3.2. Causal effect of hub genes on EC

According to the IVW MR analysis, all 3 identified genes demonstrated a causal impact on the risk of EC (Fig. 2A). As shown in Figure 2B–D, the expression of KHK (OR = 0.858, 95% CI = 0.747–0.985, P-value = .030), RNF144A (OR = 0.742, 95% CI = 0.551–0.999, P-value = .049; Fig. 2E–G), and RILPL2 (OR = 0.879, 95% CI = 0.774–0.998, P-value = .047; Fig. 2H–J) was significantly negatively associated with the risk of EC. The chromosomal distribution of RILPL2, RNF144A, and KHK was depicted in Figure 3A. Regarding the expression levels of the identified genes, it was observed that KHK (Fig. 3B), RILPL2 (Fig. 3C), and RNF144A (Fig. 3D) were all significantly downregulated in EC samples (P < .05). When exploring the relationship between the identified genes and the OS of EC patients, the results indicated that the expression of KHK (Fig. 3E) and RNF144A (Fig. 3G) were negatively associated with EC prognosis, whereas RILPL2 (Fig. 3F) was positively associated with EC prognosis.

3.3. Analysis of scRNA-seq data

The high-dimensional scRNA-seq data were reduced to low-dimensional data using PCA analysis (Fig. 4A). After strict quality control preprocessing, the reliability of the cells in EC samples was ensured by limiting the percentage of erythrocyte and mitochondrial genes (Fig. 4B). The filtered cells were classified into 20 clusters (Fig. 4C) and assigned to 9 known cell types, including T cells, fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, tissue stem cells, endothelial cells, macrophages, neurons, and NK cells (Fig. 4D). The expression of the primary EC marker MUC16/CA125 indicated that EC cells may originate from epithelial cells (Fig. 4H and M). Among the 3 identified hub genes, RNF144A showed the highest similarity to MUC16/CA125, being enriched in epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells (Fig. 4E, I, and J). The other genes, KHK (Fig. 4F and K) and RILPL2 (Fig. 4G and L), were relatively more abundant in immune cells, specifically macrophages.

3.4. Immune correlation analysis

CIBERSORT analysis revealed differences in immune cell infiltration between EC samples and the normal control group. EC samples exhibited significantly elevated infiltration of activated CD4 memory T cells, follicular helper T cells, M0 and M1 macrophages, activated dendritic cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils, while the normal control group showed significantly higher infiltration of naïve B cells, memory B cells, CD8 T cells, resting CD4 memory T cells, activated NK cells, M2 macrophages, and resting mast cells (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, the correlation between the 3 hub genes and 22 types of immune cells is shown in Fig. 5B and C. It was discovered that RNF144A was significantly positively correlated with monocytes, KHK was significantly positively correlated with resting mast cells but negatively correlated with Tregs, and RILPL2 was significantly positively correlated with Tregs but negatively correlated with eosinophils and resting CD4 memory T cells.

Associations between genes and immune characteristics. (A) The proportion of immune cells between normal control and EC samples. (B and C) Correlations between infiltrated immune cells and identified genes in EC. EC = endometrial carcinoma.

3.5. Role of risk signature for EC patients

Based on the expression levels of hub genes RNF144A, KHK, and RILPL2, TCGA-EC patients were classified into high- or low-risk subgroups (Fig. 6A). The clinical value of the risk signature in EC patients was investigated, revealing that patients in the high-risk subgroup had significantly poorer OS compared to those in the low-risk subgroup (Fig. 6B). Meanwhile, the constructed risk signature showed moderate predictive accuracy in EC patients with AUC values of 0.817, 0.678, and 0.698 at 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS follow-up, respectively (Fig. 6C). While compared with other clinical features (including age, tumor stage, and grade), our risk signature showed the highest predictive accuracy (Fig. 6D). Notably, the risk scores were significantly higher in EC patients aged over 60, with advanced tumor stages, or higher tumor grades, compared to those aged 60 or below (Fig. 6E), with lower tumor stages (Fig. 6F), or lower tumor grades (Fig. 6G). A column chart depicting clinical characteristics and risk scores was generated to assess the OS of EC patients at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals (Fig. 6H). The calibration curve demonstrated the consistency of our constructed nomogram (Fig. 6I).

3.6. Identification the core gene RNF144A in EC

The reverse cumulative distribution (Fig. 7A) and boxplots (Fig. 7B) indicated small residuals ranging from 0 to 0.25 for all 4 employed algorithms. The ROC curve analysis revealed that the generalized linear models algorithm had the highest accuracy in predicting EC samples compared to the other 3 algorithms (Fig. 7C). Moreover, when evaluating the importance scores of the hub genes, RNF144A consistently exhibited the highest score across all 4 algorithms (Fig. 7D). The ROC curves of the hub genes confirmed that RNF144A was the most accurate predictor for the occurrence of EC (Fig. 7E). In conclusion, RNF144A was identified as the core gene in EC samples and was subsequently employed for further analysis.

Based on the aforementioned investigation, RNF144A exhibited significantly lower expression in TCGA-EC samples compared to normal controls. To further validate the expression level of RNF144A in EC, this study utilized qRT-PCR and merged additional independent databases (GSE39099, GSE17025, GSE63678, and GSE120490 cohorts). Following batch correction (Fig. S1A and B, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N671), GEO-EC samples demonstrated a significant downregulation of RNF144A gene expression when compared to normal endometrium (Fig. 7F). Consistent findings were observed in the qRT-PCR analysis, which showed a significant downregulation of RNF144A expression in HEC1A cells (Fig. 7G). Interestingly, Figure 7H and I revealed significantly higher expression levels of RNF144A in EC patients with stage III to IV tumors or higher tumor grades, suggesting a potential association between RNF144A and EC progression. Furthermore, GSEA enrichment analyses indicated a strong correlation between RNF144A and several Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways, including arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, dilated cardiomyopathy, focal adhesion, regulation of the actin cytoskeleton, and tight junction (Fig. 8A). GO analysis revealed that higher RNF144A expression levels were associated with adverse outcomes in EC patients, potentially involving activities such as positive regulation of the proteasomal ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic process, ubiquitin ligase complex, ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme binding, and others (Fig. 8B and C).

3.7. The role of RNF144A in EC cells

The qRT-PCR analysis confirmed the upregulation of RNF144A in HEC1A cells (Fig. 9A). Moreover, the overexpression of RNF144A significantly improved the proliferative ability of EC cells compared to control cells (P < .05; Fig. 9B). The cell migration assay showed that EC cells overexpressing RNF144A exhibited a significantly higher number of migrated cells compared to the control subgroup (P < .05; Fig. 9C).

4. Discussion

EC represents a significant threat to the mental and physical health of women, with approximately 66,570 new cases of EC and 12,940 EC-related deaths worldwide annually.[21] Prior study has established that reduced expression of PR and ER correlates with increased EC mortality; however, these markers are insufficient to assess individual mortality risk.[22] While abnormal expression of p53 and p16 are indicative of a poor prognosis in high-risk EC patients, their clinical application has been limited.[23] Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify additional potential biomarkers to enhance the predictive accuracy of prognosis and diagnosis in EC. This study utilized differential expression analysis, univariate Cox regression, and bioinformatics to identify key genes in EC and explore their underlying mechanisms. A MR analysis was also performed, aiming to systematically identify the core genes associated with EC. The incorporation of MR analysis represents an innovative aspect of this study, as it can control for potential confounding factors, thereby ensuring reliable causal inferences.

We identified 3 genes (RNF144A, KHK, and RILPL2), which significantly impact the prognosis of EC patients. Subsequent MR analysis confirmed that these genes are risk factors for EC development. A risk signature, developed using these 3 genes, was strongly correlated with poorer OS and higher tumor TNM stages and grades in EC patients. This risk signature showed greater predictive accuracy for EC stages and OS than the standard clinicopathological parameter, the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM staging system. Given the significant role of RNF144A, as indicated by 4 machine learning algorithms and ROC analysis, this study focused on exploring the function of RNF144A in EC.

The RBR (RING-Between-RING)-type E3 ligase is an emerging subgroup of ring finger proteins,[24–26] and plays important roles in cancer modulation, inflammation, and neurodegenerative diseases.[27] RNF144A, a less characterized member of the RBR family,[25] is a highly conserved transmembrane (TM) E3 ligase featuring a single TM domain at its C-terminus. This domain localizes it to subcellular membranes and is crucial for trafficking between intracellular vesicles and the cell surface.[28] The TM domain of RNF144A is implicated in signaling through interactions with DNA-dependent protein kinase, catalytic subunit, and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR).[29,30] RNF144A can promote apoptosis in response to cancer progression by inducing the ubiquitination and degradation of DNA-dependent protein kinase, catalytic subunit. In cancers such as breast, glioma, and epithelial ovarian, RNF144A overexpression has been shown to inhibit tumor growth and aggressiveness, functioning as a tumor suppressor.[31–33] Conversely, RNF144A can also stimulate cell proliferation via the EGF/EGFR signaling pathway,[34] forming a positive feedback loop that amplifies the response to growth factor stimulation. EGFR, a receptor tyrosine kinase, is known to drive cancer proliferation and migration,[35] and its overexpression is linked to chemotherapy resistance.[34] In EC, increased EGFR expression correlates with a poor prognosis,[36] hinting at a possible role for RNF144A in EC progression. However, the precise functional and mechanistic role of RNF144A in EC is yet to be defined. This study aims to bridge this knowledge gap by employing various analytical approaches to investigate the significant contribution of RNF144A to EC prognosis. Contrary to expectations, RNF144A was found to be downregulated in EC samples compared to normal endometrium, yet its reduced expression was significantly associated with poorer OS in EC patients. Additionally, increased RNF144A expression was shown to promote EC growth and migration. Notably, robust associations between RNF144A and key clinical parameters of EC, such as tumor grade and TNM stage, were identified.

This study has 2 principal limitations. Firstly, its retrospective design restricts the capacity to predict long-term outcomes in EC. Consequently, prospective studies are warranted to substantiate our findings. Secondly, the absence of in vivo experiments is a notable limitation; further mechanistic studies are essential to clarify the precise functions and underlying mechanisms of RNF144A in EC.

5. Conclusions

In summary, RNF144A emerges as a potential biomarker for the prognosis and progression assessment of EC patients, as well as an indicator of their immune status. This study firstly investigated the role of RNF144A in EC, providing a novel perspective that may enhance therapeutic strategies for EC patients.
