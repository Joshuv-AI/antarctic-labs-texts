Real-world implementation of Collaborative Care in the United States: a systematic review
Authors: Erin LePoire, Jessica Whitfield, Amy Bauer, Anna Ratzliff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe demand for behavioral health services continues to outpace the supply of the behavioral health workforce, resulting in many people seeking behavioral health services within their primary care practice. Collaborative Care is an effective, evidence-based model of behavioral health service integration into primary care. An extensive literature of randomized controlled trials exists, with much less published about the outcomes of Collaborative Care in real-world settings. This systematic review aims to describe and summarize results of the published literature regarding real-world implementation efforts of CoCM and suggests a reporting framework for future implementation efforts to better understand, compare, and evaluate CoCM programs.MethodsWe searched articles in PubMed and Web of Science published between Jan 1st 2000-September 30th 2024. Eligibility criteria included the following: (1) conducted in the United States, (2) real-world implementation studies, (3) adult patient population, (4) demonstrated the main elements that define CoCM as outlined by Medicare, (5) information above was publicly available in the article or supplementary material. The definition of CoCM was selected based on major professional organizations and Center for Medicaid/Medicare Services (CMS) criteria for Collaborative Care billing codes.ResultsA total of 21 articles were included in this review. Overall, Collaborative Care programs were found to be clinically effective across a wide range of settings and populations. The most common clinical outcomes reported were depression response (a 50% reduction in PHQ-9 score or a PHQ-9 score less than 10) and remission (PHQ-9 score less than 5). Depression response ranged from 30% to 58%. Remission ranged from 19% to 54%. Process measures were reported inconsistently. This suggests the potential utility of a reporting checklist for integrated care implementation.ConclusionsThis review adds to a growing literature capturing Collaborative Care effectiveness and feasibility in real-world settings. More consistent reporting of Collaborative Care model components and process measures in the Collaborative Care literature is needed.

Introduction

The demand for behavioral health services continues to grow, with over 50 million Americans experiencing a behavioral health issue over the course of a year [1]. Existing behavioral health workforce shortages mean the supply is unable to keep up with demand, resulting in less than half of people in need of behavioral health services receiving them in a timely manner [1–5]. Additionally, quality of behavioral health services varies widely, and people with comorbid medical issues (such as diabetes and cardiovascular conditions) experience worse medical outcomes when mental health conditions are undertreated [6–8]. To address this gap in care needs, behavioral health services are now offered in many primary care clinics via behavioral health integration. Over the past twenty years, research on integrated behavioral health care has demonstrated its effectiveness in treating depression and anxiety in the primary care setting [6–9].

The Collaborative Care model (CoCM), in particular, has a large evidence base with over 90 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that demonstrate its effectiveness in treating behavioral health disorders, expanding beyond depression and anxiety to include posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), bipolar disorder, and substance use disorders [10–13]. The key care team members in CoCM include the primary care provider (PCP), Psychiatric Consultant, Behavioral Health Care Manager (BHCM), and the patient. The BHCM meets with the patient regularly, tracks their symptoms using validated measures (such as the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) or Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7(GAD-7), and records symptom scores and clinical progress in a registry. To achieve population health management goals, the BHCM and the Psychiatric Consultant meet weekly to conduct systematic caseload review. They review the registry and prioritize discussion of CoCM patients that are not improving and/or may need treatment adjustments. As a result of these discussions, the Psychiatric Consultant provides written recommendations for medication modifications and/or brief behavioral interventions that are communicated back to the PCP and BHCM, who both discuss these recommendations with the patient and act on any suggested adjustments to the treatment plan.

Over the past decade, research has shown CoCM to be effective outside of primary care clinics, with evidence supporting effective depression reduction in specialty care settings such as oncology, infectious disease, cardiology, obstetrics/gynecology, and pediatrics [11, 14–22]. CoCM has also expanded beyond high-income countries, with successful results demonstrated in low- and middle-income countries [23–26].

With a robust evidence base from clinical trials, data from real-world implementations is growing. Evidence from the last fifteen years suggests that the implementation process itself is critical to program success, and variations in implementation can ultimately impact clinical outcomes [27]. Though CoCM is a well-described model with clearly defined elements (e.g., a registry to track patient outcomes, BHCM who coordinates and delivers care), how programs implement and operationalize those elements varies due to specific organizational circumstances and patient population needs [28]. Reporting data on processes of care, or the procedures and workflows involved in healthcare delivery, can help inform these comparisons. Critically, some process measures can be directly correlated with population-level clinical outcomes, yet the lack of consistency in reporting process of care outcomes in the literature makes it difficult to compare results across implementation efforts [29–31]. This review aims to describe and summarize results of the published literature regarding real-world implementation efforts of CoCM.

Methods.

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with guidelines set by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta Analyses (PRISMA) to identify existing studies evaluating real-world implementation of Collaborative Care programs in primary care settings [32]. Search terms and eligibility criteria

The search query “Collaborative Care” OR “Integrated Behavioral Health Care” AND “Primary Care” were used in both PubMed and Web of Science databases for articles published between January 1st, 2000, and September 30th, 2024.

Study selection

Articles were eligible if they met al.l the following criteria: (1) conducted in the United States, (2) real-world implementation studies (randomized control trials of CoCM were excluded), (3) adult patient population (4) demonstrated the main elements that define CoCM as outlined by Medicare (use of a behavioral health registry and description of a regularly scheduled systematic caseload review between a psychiatric consultant and behavioral health care manager), (5) information above was publicly available in the article or supplementary material [33]. The definition of CoCM was selected based on the accepted definition of CoCM according to major professional organizations and Center for Medicaid/Medicare Services (CMS) criteria for CoCM billing codes [33, 34]. We limited the review to published information publicly available to reflect the average utilizer of the medical literature. Three investigators (EL, AR, DB) reviewed titles for initial review. Five investigators (EL, AR, AB, JW, SR) completed abstract and full article review. Investigators reviewed disputes and finalized articles for inclusion.

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction Excel sheet was used across all investigators to collect the following information: first author name, publication year, clinic and patient population demographics, CoCM program structure, CoCM caseload statistics, and process of care measures. Articles that included a secondary analysis of data reported in a previous paper were included only if they reported outcomes that were not in the original analysis.

Reporting domains

Following review of results as published in the Tables and appendix, four authors (EL, JW, AB, AR) observed variability in reporting of process measures that prompted the development of a reporting checklist to improve on consistency of model description and data reported in the literature. The authors first reviewed the literature for similar checklists and responded to gaps in this literature by focusing a reporting checklist on operationalized elements of integrated care models across 5–7 main domains based on above literature and results review as well as author practice knowledge. Initial drafts were reviewed by local subject matter experts, and feedback incorporated into a final proposal.

Results

Search results returned 4, 713 articles (2,019 from PubMed and 2694 from Web of Science). After duplicates were removed (n = 1675), 3,038 articles were screened via title review. 2,762 articles did not meet our criteria based on title review, leaving 276 articles to be screened via a title and abstract review. 222 were further excluded due to not meeting our criteria leaving 54 screened using the full article. 33 were further excluded, with 21 articles undergoing data extraction and inclusion in this review. Results are reported by extraction variable category.

Collaborative care program structure

All articles reported on the roles of care team members, however only six [35–40] reported the actual number of people to staff a care team role (Table 3). Among the 11 articles that reported BHCM qualifications [36, 38–46], the majority were social workers (n = 6) followed by registered nurses (n = 5), licensed practical nurses (n = 2), and nurse practitioners (n = 1). Psychiatric consultant licensure as reported in six articles consisted mostly of medical or osteopathic doctors (n = 5) and psychiatric mental health nurse practitioners (n = 4), with one article [38] reporting the use of a physician assistant. Six articles [31, 39, 40, 44–46] mentioned whether the care team members worked on-site or remotely, five reported on-site only [39, 40, 44–46]. One article reported that the psychiatric consultant worked both on- and off-site (via telephone) [31]. A patient registry is an important component of Collaborative Care as it enables the BHCM to track patient measures such as the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), for depression and the General Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7) [47, 48]. All articles used some sort of registry, as it was part of our inclusion criteria. Eight articles reported the exact registry used, [27, 29, 36, 37, 40, 44–46] with four using the Care Management Tracking System (CMTS), an online registry from the Advancing Integrated Mental Health Solutions (AIMS) Center, which provides training and technical assistance for implementing CoCM [27, 29, 36, 37]. The frequency of systematic caseload reviews (SCR) between the BHCM and psychiatric consultant was reported in five articles, all of them reporting weekly meetings [31, 35, 40, 41, 46].

A key component of CoCM is using validated screening and monitoring tools. Illustrated in Table 4, all articles reported using the PHQ-9 to screen and measure depression symptoms. Six articles also reported using the GAD-7 for anxiety [12, 37, 41, 44, 45, 49]. Other measures used were the Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) Checklist (PCL) [40, 44–46] and Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test- Consumption (AUDIT-C) [37, 41, 46]. Twelve articles reported on how patients were identified as eligible for CoCM services, with most (n = 10) using an elevated PHQ-9 or GAD-7 screening score (29,37,41-,43,45,49,50). Three articles reported using PCP discretion/referral [44, 45, 49] as well as diagnosis of depression [35, 41, 46]. Nine articles reported on the modality of patient care, with six offering both in-person and telephone-based services [31, 35, 36, 40, 42, 43], two offering in person only [37, 50], and one offering phone-based services only [46]. Two articles reported on the type of therapy that was offered to CoCM patients, one reporting cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) [31], and the other problem-solving therapy (PST) [35]. No articles reported the percentage of patients that received therapy.

Clinical outcomes and process of care measures

There are many clinical and process of care measures to consider when discussing CoCM (Table 5). The most common clinical outcomes reported were depression response (a 50% reduction in PHQ-9 score or a PHQ-9 score less than 10) and remission (PHQ-9 score less than 5). Depression response rate among the 12 articles, ranged from 30% to 58% [12, 27, 29, 31, 36, 37, 42, 43, 49, 51–53]. Nine articles reported remission rates which ranged from 19% to 54% [27, 31, 36, 39, 41, 43, 45, 49, 53]. Six articles reported the average number of contacts CoCM patients had with a BHCM, ranging from 2.0 to 7.7 visits. [12, 31, 36, 37, 42, 45]. Average time in treatment ranged from 2.4 to 4.6 months, among the five articles that reported this variable, three of which did not report the average number of contacts [12, 43, 45, 50, 53]. Early engagement, defined as having a first follow visit within one month from a patient’s initial assessment was reported in three articles [36, 41, 53]. Two articles [28, 36] reported from the same dataset, only Powers et al. article was included in analysis to avoid duplication) reported the proportion of patients who received early engagement (range 60%-72%) [41, 53] and one article reported the average number of days between initial assessment and first follow up (12.6 days) [50]. Two articles reported on the proportion of CoCM patients that received antidepressant medications, with Coleman et al. reporting 60% and Rubenstein et al. reporting 93% [43, 46].

Collaborative care caseload statistics

None of the articles reported on caseload size of unique patients per month. Unützer et al. did report an average of 49 CoCM patients seen per year per clinic, and Rubenstein et al. reported a caseload size of around 100 patients per BHCM [27, 46]. Almost all articles (n = 19) reported the total number of patients enrolled in CoCM, with ranges from 19 patients to 17,052 patients [12, 27, 29, 31, 35–37, 39–46, 49–51, 53]. The population reach of CoCM can be considered as the proportion of patients eligible to receive CoCM that do receive it. Across eleven articles, the reach ranged from 8% to 56% [29, 35–37, 41, 43, 45, 49, 53].

CoCM financing

Appendix 1 provides an overview of CoCM insurance and billing information. Billing codes for CoCM were introduced for Medicare in 2017 and have subsequently been adopted by a number of state Medicaid programs and private insurers. Four articles discuss billing for CoCM codes but did not describe exactly which codes were billed [12, 44, 45, 49]. Five articles described their participants’ payer mix [12, 27, 42, 44, 49], and one article described the percentage of payers at the clinic level (rather than participant level) [38]. Among those reporting participant-level data, the proportion of Medicaid as the primary insurance ranged from 33 to 75% [12, 42, 44, 49], private/commercial insurance 10–84% [12, 27, 42, 44, 49], Medicare 3–20% [12, 42, 44, 49], and uninsured or unknown 7–12% [44, 49].

Discussion

This systematic review fills a gap in the literature by summarizing real-world CoCM implementation efforts from 21 articles. As with other systematic reviews regarding CoCM, similarities and differences existed in the reporting of clinical and process of care outcomes across studies. Overall, CoCM programs were found to be clinically effective across a wide range of settings and populations.

Several structural and process-of-care measures were reported across the programs, with some noted heterogeneity. The most frequently reported program elements were the validated measures used, the eligibility criteria for CoCM, and specific team roles. However, less than half of articles reported on licensure of each role or location of work, two common questions that arise in the process of integrated care implementation. Workflows and processes central to CoCM were similarly under-reported, with less than a third reporting on specifics of the Systematic Caseload Review, registry, or modes of team communication and collaboration. None of the articles reported the average monthly BHCM caseload size, which is associated with program success over time [30], and is also crucial to understanding the financial feasibility and clinic capacity to offer CoCM. Only two articles reported on the percentage of patients receiving antidepressant medications, and none of the articles reported number of therapy sessions. The type of therapy offered was also minimally reported, a potential missed opportunity, as understanding which specific behavioral health interventions are most effective in different settings can help clinics determine which training their clinicians may want or need to provide effective care. In fact, manualized psychotherapy was recently identified as a critical component of CoCM treatment effectiveness [54].

The inconsistency in reporting of process measures has made it challenging to compare program implementations directly and suggests the potential utility of a reporting checklist for integrated care implementation and research trials. Consistent reporting of integrated care program structure (e.g., CoCM team staffing and training) and process of care measures (e.g., caseload size, use of screening tools, early engagement, and similar indicators) is important to both implementation research and front-line healthcare teams seeking to implement CoCM in their organizations. Currently, research trials to compare effectiveness of CoCM programs with variations in model components remains out of reach, as the number of clinics required for such comparisons is rarely feasible. Having consistently reported CoCM model information allows researchers to more accurately compare integrated care models or drive aggregation of data critical for larger meta-analyses of associations between program elements and clinical effectiveness, such as a recent meta-analysis of CoCM program elements [54]. Likewise, clinical organizations benefit from comparing data across programs by being able to evaluate in more detail program elements that characterize successful implementation, or pitfalls to avoid. Transparency around the number, licensure, and full-time equivalent employment of the three main care team roles could help organizations understand what is most feasible and effective for their clinic size and setting.

Published frameworks could support standardization in study reporting. Calciolari et al. published guidelines to facilitate consistent descriptions of or reflection on integrated care implementations that includes 40 items across seven dimensions: Person-centered care, Clinical integration, Professional integration, Organizational integration, Systemic integration, Functional integration, and Normative integration [55, 56]. In Washington State in 2017, the Bree Collaborative published a checklist of integrated care elements, including a suggested list of quality of care metrics for any integrated care model. Similarly, the Quintuple Aim proposes domains that have been shown to be critical to improving the quality of health care delivery and outcomes across a wide variety of medical care models [57]. However, while important for supporting integrated care efforts at local, institutional, or national scales, none of these frameworks provide feasible guidelines for describing in publications the concrete, operationalized components of integrated care models.

Building on this prior work, we propose a list of operationalized model elements and processes for integrated care implementers and researchers to report on when publishing program results (Appendix 2). This proposal includes eight reporting domains, each divided into priority subdomains with recommended quantitative and qualitative approaches to capturing each recommended program component, as described in Appendix 2. Of these eight reporting domains (Program structure: team roles and training; Program structure: workflows; Screening; Evidence-based practice; Program monitoring; Caseload statistics; Demographics; Funding), elements in Screening and Demographics were the only domain elements reported in more than half of studies. Initial feedback on this checklist gathered by the authors from subject matter experts (including clinicians, implementation coaches, and researchers) indicated further review and development from a broader audience of experts and potential checklist utilizers would be necessary before disseminating a final draft, largely to obtain consensus regarding a more limited list of high priority checklist items. As article word counts often limit detail in publication, such a reporting checklist might serve best as supplementary or appendix material.

Limitations of this study include restricting our criteria to studies within the United States given the significant variation in health care system structure and financing across countries. However, there is a robust literature of implementation trials in both low-and-middle-income countries (LMICs) and high-income countries (HICs) around the world. Additionally, limiting our search to peer-reviewed articles excludes reports outside the literature of real-world CoCM implementations, likely those in non-academic organizations or communities.

Conclusion

This review adds to a growing literature capturing Collaborative Care effectiveness and feasibility in real-world settings. Our results build upon recent work emphasizing the identification of components that are associated with clinical effectiveness. Ongoing research is needed to highlight how CoCM is implemented in pragmatic settings. Future studies should focus on elucidating the elements of Collaborative Care most associated with positive clinical outcomes to support effective implementation and include consistent reporting of model components and process measures in the CoCM literature.
