Adaptive Responses of Accommodation and Vergence Following Exposure to Augmented Reality in a Head-Mounted Display
Authors: Walter K. Yego, Stuart J. Gilson, Rigmor C. Baraas, Ellen Svarverud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe vergence–accommodation conflict in augmented reality head-mounted displays (AR-HMDs) can alter the oculomotor system, leading to visual discomfort and fatigue. The purpose of this work was to evaluate changes of accommodation and vergence and their interaction after completing a visually and cognitively demanding 3D task using an AR-HMD.MethodsOculomotor parameters, including tonic accommodation (TA), CA/C ratio, tonic vergence (TV), and AC/A ratio were assessed with a photorefractor in 16 participants (10 females; mean age, 23.0 ± 2.6 years). Measurements were taken before and after performing physical and AR versions of an executive function 3D task (Tower of London). Participants moved spheres between pegs at 0.4 m to match the configuration presented on a 2D monitor at 4 m, creating vergence and accommodation demands of 2.5 and 0.25 MA/D. Accommodation demand of virtual content in the AR-HMD, however, remained fixed at 0.5 D.ResultsOculomotor parameters differed across tasks for TA, F(2, 81) = 9.640, P < 0.001, and CA/C ratio, F(2, 84) = 9.720, P < 0.001, but not for TV, F(1, 81) = 0.485, P = 0.488, and AC/A ratio, F(2, 90) = 0.001, P = 0.99. TA and CA/C ratio reduced significantly post-AR task (P < 0.01) but not post-physical task (P > 0.05).ConclusionsThe AR task induced changes in the CA/C ratio and TA, but no such changes were observed after the physical task, suggesting that exposure to visually and cognitively demanding 3D tasks in an AR-HMD can, at least temporarily, alter the oculomotor system.

Augmented reality (AR) head-mounted displays (HMDs) overlay virtual visual elements onto the physical world in such a way that the virtual elements appear to be part of the physical world.1 They are widely used in fields such as manufacturing, education and training, and health care.2,3 In medical training, for instance, AR can simulate real-world scenarios, aiding in practicing complex medical procedures to improve surgical efficacy. However, AR-HMDs may alter the oculomotor system and contribute to visual discomfort and fatigue.4,5 In these displays, there is a mismatch between accommodation and vergence demands, leading to a vergence–accommodation conflict (VAC),6,7 which occurs when the fixed accommodation demand at the display's focal plane does not match the varying vergence demands of the virtual scene.

Under natural viewing conditions, accommodation and vergence are coupled and interact through the accommodation–vergence and vergence–accommodation neural cross-links,8–11 influenced by the fast (phasic) and the slow (tonic) controllers12,13 (Fig. 1). The tonic and phasic controllers influence oculomotor adaptation and can be differentiated by long and short time constants, respectively.12,14 Tonic accommodation (TA) can be measured when an observer binocularly views a low-spatial-frequency target (open-loop accommodation), such as a 2D Gaussian blur, for at least 5 seconds.9 Similarly, tonic vergence (TV) can be measured when an observer monocularly views a high-contrast target (open-loop vergence) for at least 5 seconds.15 The vergence–accommodation cross-link can be quantified as vergence-driven accommodation per unit vergence (CA/C ratio, units: D/MA), obtained by stimulating vergence in open-loop accommodation conditions.8 Similarly, the accommodation-driven vergence cross-link can be quantified as accommodation–vergence per unit accommodation (AC/A ratio, units: MA/D), obtained by stimulating accommodation in an open-loop vergence condition.12

A simplified diagram of the accommodation and vergence control systems (after Schor12). The input signals of blur and disparity drive the accommodation and vergence systems. The phasic and tonic controllers represent the initial fast and slow adaptive components of the responses, respectively, while the neural cross-link of the accommodation and vergence responses are represented by AC/A and CA/C ratios. The plants represent the physiological reactive components for accommodation (lens) and vergence (extraocular muscles) systems and provide negative feedback signals that moderate the respective signals into the phasic controller.

It is possible that long-term exposure to the inherent VAC present in AR may alter the tonic components and cross-links between the accommodation and vergence systems. Quantifying these changes may, therefore, provide insight into the potential impact of AR on oculomotor adaptation. Previous studies have reported changes in AC/A and CA/C ratios after exposure to various stereoscopic displays, such as telescopic spectacles,16 haploscopes,17 and simulated virtual reality displays.18 These studies observed a decrease in the CA/C ratio and either no change17 or an increase18 in the AC/A ratio, along with a tendency of accommodation and vergence adaptation to decrease and increase, respectively.17 These changes suggest oculomotor adaptation to VACs in stereoscopic displays, and similar adaptations are anticipated in AR displays. The mixture of fixed (virtual) and variable (physical world) focal distances in AR environments has been reported to result in a more variable judgment of size compared with VR,19,20 indicating differential adaptations in accommodation and vergence in AR. It is, therefore, reasonable to hypothesize that performing a task in AR would lead to an adaptative response in the oculomotor system, involving changes in tonic accommodation and vergence, as well as alteration in the cross-links between accommodation and vergence. This study aimed to objectively measure the adaptive changes of accommodation and vergence and their interactions (AC/A and CA/C ratios) after a visually and cognitively demanding 3D task in an AR-HMD.

Methods

Participants and Preliminary Measurements

Sixteen participants (10 females, 6 males; mean age, 23.0 ± 2.6 years; range, 19–28 years) naïve to the purpose of the study were enrolled. Participants were included if they were emmetropic or had corrected-to-functional emmetropia, had monocular visual acuity of 0.0 logMAR or better, and stereo acuity of 60 seconds of arc or better. The protocol was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics in Southeast Norway and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written consent was obtained from each participant after explaining the experimental procedures. Participants underwent clinical optometric measurements before commencing the experiment. Habitual logMAR visual acuity was measured monocularly and binocularly at 6 m (TestChart 2000; Thomson Software Solutions, London, UK) and binocularly at 0.4 m (logMAR acuity chart; Good-lite, Elgin, IL, USA). Ocular dominance was determined using the hole-in-the-card test. Heterophoria was measured at 6 m and 0.4 m (cover test) and stereoacuity at 0.4 m (TNO Stereotest; Laméris Ootech, Ede, the Netherlands). Cycloplegic refraction (HRK-8000A Auto-REF Keratometer; Huvitz Co. Ltd., Gyeonggi-do, Korea) and ocular anterior chamber depth (Zeiss IOLMaster 700; Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) were measured 20 minutes after instillation of topical 1% cyclopentolate hydrochloride (Minims single use; Bausch + Lomb UK Ltd, Kingston-upon-Thames, UK).

Experimental Apparatus

The HoloLens 2 AR-HMD (Microsoft Corporation; Redmond, WA, USA) was used to display a visually and cognitively demanding 3D executive function task (Tower of London [ToL], e.g., Krikorian et al.21). This task was used due to its ability to engage the participant's attention22 and to drive accommodation and vergence for a sustained time. The task was implemented in a custom-built program using Unity (Unity Technologies, San Francisco, CA, USA), and the ToL dimensions were the same as those in previous work.21,23 A 3D-printed physical model, whose dimensions were identical to the virtual model, was used to control for the effect of task. In the task, the participant was required to move the spheres between pegs from a default configuration to match the solution displayed on a 2D screen placed 4 m from the participant (Fig. 2). The participant positioned the ToL model at a comfortable working distance, typically around 0.4 m (the distance from the participant's spectacle plane to the tip of their index finger when picking up a sphere from the middle peg). The room illumination was approximately 150 lx (Hagner Digital Luxmeter, model EC1, Solna, Sweden). The additive contrast ranged from 5:1 to 17:1 for the virtual model and 7:1 to 17:1 for the physical model.

The ToL task. The ToL consisted of three vertical pegs of different heights (13.5 cm, 9.5 cm, and 5.0 cm) attached to a rectangular baseboard (25 × 9.0 × 2.0 cm) and three spheres (red, blue, and green) with a diameter of 4.0 cm. The participant was required to stand behind an empty table and switch their gaze between the task (around 0.4 m in front of the participant) and a 2D display at 4.0 m containing the sphere configurations required to complete the task.

Experimental Procedures

The study was a 2 × 2 experimental cross-over design. There were two sessions per day on two separate days (Fig. 3). Each session lasted 25 minutes, with a 10-minute break between sessions. The session began with a 5-minute familiarization period, during which the experimenter read the instructions to the participant. For the AR task, the HMD was adjusted to fit the participant's head, ensuring that its built-in eye calibration was activated for automatic interpupillary distance adjustment. During the session, the participant was instructed to perform 12 trials of increasing complexity and to use only one hand to move one sphere at a time between the pegs. A trial was marked as a fail and restarted if the participant exceeded the target number of moves. A move involved grasping the sphere between the thumb and forefinger or middle finger, lifting it off the peg, moving it to the top of the destination peg, and releasing it to drop down the peg. The task was restarted within a session if the participant completed all 12 trials before 25 minutes had elapsed or terminated mid-trial if this set time limit was exceeded. The AR task was monitored by the experimenter viewing a wirelessly streamed video from the HMD.

The order of experimental procedures on days 1 (top) and 2 (bottom). The measurements were counterbalanced (starting with either open-loop vergence or open-loop accommodation). These measurements were used to compute TV, AC/A ratio, TA and CA/C ratio.

Measurements of Refraction and Gaze

Dynamic binocular refraction (in diopters [D]) and gaze (eye position in degrees) were measured simultaneously before and immediately after performing the task in each condition using the PowerRef 3 (PlusOptix GmbH, Nuremberg, Germany). The PowerRef 3 (operating at 50 Hz) was placed 1 m from the participant and aligned perpendicular to the line of sight. A dichroic mirror was used to reflect the infrared light from the camera.24 The room illumination was reduced to 15 cd/m2. Calibration was performed for each participant before commencing the measurements on day 1, using the recommended procedures for refraction25,26 and gaze27–29 (see Yego et al.,23 for details). Measurements used to compute TV and AC/A ratio, as well as TA and CA/C ratio, were obtained in the order specified in Figure 3.

Measurements of Tonic Adaptation of Accommodation and Vergence and Their Cross-Links

There are several methods of assessing TV accommodation.17,30,31 Here we were interested in the magnitude of changes in these parameters following the exposure to the AR task. Therefore, we used the open-field method to measure the change in parameters within a specified time. The stimulus for measuring TV was a column of five high-contrast letters (each letter was 50 pixels square, subtending 2.51 arcmin) placed in the mid-sagittal plane at 1 m. For measuring TA, the same target was used but was blurred with a Gaussian filter (radius of 18 pixels) using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), similar to Sweeney and colleagues.32 The stimuli were displayed on a fourth-generation iPad (Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA) with the bezels covered with black cardboard to eliminate peripheral visual distractions. A pilot study with three participants showed that this stimulus was effective in opening the accommodation loop. Viewing this type of target with a natural pupil facilitates an unchanged measure of accommodation aftereffect and vergence-accommodation by acting as a quasi-open-loop accommodative condition.9

The stimulus AC/A and CA/C ratios were measured using the gradient method.9,33 The AC/A ratio was measured under an open-loop vergence condition, whereby the participant viewed the TV stimulus at 1 m. They were instructed to look at the target passively for 20 seconds (to minimize the effects of voluntary effort)34 while TV was measured. Trial lenses (+1.0 D, −1.0 D, −2.0 D, and −3.0 D) were then held in front of the dominant eye at a vertex distance of 12 mm while the non-dominant eye was occluded with an infrared filter. Each lens was held in place for 5 seconds with a 15-second pause between lenses (Fig. 4). The CA/C ratio was measured under an open-loop accommodation condition, whereby the participant viewed the TA stimulus. They were instructed to look at the TA target passively for 20 seconds, while TA was measured. Prisms (6 prism diopters [PD] base in, 6 PD base out, and 12 PD base out) were then held in front of the nondominant eye for 5 seconds to minimize prism adaptation followed by 15 seconds for vergence recovery, as suggested in previous studies9,30 (Fig. 4). All measurements were completed immediately before each task and within 5 minutes of task completion.

The sequence of measurements used to compute TV and AC/A ratio (top) and TA and CA/C ratio (bottom).

Data Analysis

Data were preprocessed and analyzed using R statistical software version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).35 Before converting to accommodation and vergence, raw refraction and gaze data were visually inspected to remove blinks and data outside the PowerRef 3’s operating range for measurements of the pupil size (3.5–8.0 mm), refraction (+5.00 to −7.00 D) and gaze (15° horizontally). The accommodation in the dominant eye was computed as the difference between the refraction from the PowerRef 3 and the baseline cycloplegic refraction from the Huvitz HRK-8000A Auto-REF (adjusted by any correction worn). Vergence was calculated from the relative eye position of the right and left eyes (see Yego et al.23 for details).

TV (MA) and TA (D, computed from the dominant eye) were established from the measurements obtained in seconds 17 and 18 of the 20-second measurement period. The change in TV and accommodation was defined as the difference between baseline measurements and the measurements after performing the ToL task (in AR or physically). Positive and negative changes in TV or accommodation indicated an increase and decrease in tonic adaptation from the baseline, respectively. For AC/A and CA/C ratios, the mean accommodation and vergence responses from the middle two of each 5-second lens or prism recording were used for further analysis. The AC/A ratio was defined as the slope of the linear regression of vergence response against the induced lens. The CA/C ratio was defined as the slope of the linear regression of accommodation response against induced prism (Fig. 5). The prism demand in PD was converted to MA, assuming a 60-mm interpupillary distance. The change in AC/A and CA/C ratios was defined as the difference between the baseline measurement and the measurement after performing the AR task or the physical task each day. Positive and negative changes in the AC/A or CA/C ratio indicated an increase or decrease in cross-link alteration from the baseline, respectively.

Vergence response as a function of stimulus lenses (A) and accommodation response as a function of stimulus prisms (B) from an exemplar participant chosen to illustrate accommodation-induced vergence and vergence-induced accommodation, respectively. Both panels show measurements taken at baseline (gray), after the AR task (blue), and after the physical task (gold). The slopes of the regression lines are the AC/A ratios (A) and CA/C ratios (B).

Normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test with a significance level set at 0.05. We analyzed the effect of the exposure to each condition on a specific session (for each day), the overall effect of each condition (combining data from the two days), and the change of parameters for each day. A two-way repeated measures ANOVA was used to examine the effect of day and task on the following four parameters: (i) TA, (ii) CA/C ratio, (iii) TV, and (iv) AC/A ratio. If there was no effect of day, measurements from day 1 and day 2 for each participant were combined, and the combined mean value was analyzed using one-way repeated measures ANOVA with task as the between-factor variable, to analyze the overall effect of conditions without the confounding factor of day. The change in the four parameters was analyzed using a two-way repeated measures ANOVA (factors: day and task). This analysis was conducted to normalize data and account for variability in baseline measures. The effect size was estimated using partial eta squared (ηp2). All pairwise comparisons were conducted using post hoc pairwise t tests with Bonferroni correction to compare the specific effects of the tasks.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that vergence-driven accommodation, but not accommodation-driven vergence, was affected from performing a visually and cognitively demanding 3D task in AR for 25 minutes. The findings suggest that this specific task in AR induces an adaptive effect, altering the oculomotor balance between accommodation and vergence. The reduced CA/C ratio and TA indicate that the reduced vergence-driven accommodation effect was driven by decreased tonic accommodative adaptation.36,37

The reduced CA/C ratio after exposure to the AR task (Fig. 6) resembles findings from previous studies using other forms of stereoscopic displays.16–18 These studies reported a decrease in the CA/C ratio after viewing virtual objects in simulated 3D environments, which suggests that the decreased vergence-driven accommodation contributed to the change in oculomotor responses. It is likely that viewing virtual 3D objects in AR also can induce a decrease in vergence-driven accommodation as an adaptive mechanism to enable the accommodation system to maintain a focused retinal image. The reduction in TA appears to be another adaptive strategy to regulate the CA/C ratio in response to increased phasic vergence stimulation. These changes in TA (Fig. 6) support that adaptive mechanisms can be activated to overcome the conflict between accommodation and vergence.

Our results indicate that the vergence system, while maintaining a fused stereo percept, can reduce its influence on accommodation at the cross-link to maintain the accommodation response close to the focal plane of the HMD. The reduced influence of vergence-driven accommodation is expected to trigger an increased AC/A ratio because of the reciprocal relationship between AC/A and CA/C ratios.9,32 However, there are conflicting reports on whether there are changes in the AC/A ratio after stereoscopic viewing with VACs. Although some studies found no change,17,38–40 others have reported an increase in the AC/A ratio.16,18 One possible reason for the lack of agreement among these studies may be due to using different methodologies, such as the Maxwellian view17 and the empty-field techniques.18 For instance, measurements with empty-field techniques could potentially mask the accommodation and vergence aftereffects in reduced illumination,36,41 which may have contributed to the lower values of AC/A and CA/C reported in our study compared with other studies.17,18 Because the change in the AC/A ratio can be influenced by TV adaptation, the lack of significant change in TV might suggest little or no effect on the vergence system from exposure to the AR task.

Despite not finding changes in the AC/A ratio or TV, it cannot be ruled out that these effects may emerge with longer exposure times and greater magnitudes of VAC, as reported in some studies.16,18 For instance, whereas our study induced 2.0 D or MA of VAC (accommodation fixed at 0.5 D and vergence of 2.5 MA at near) in AR for 25 minutes, Eadie et al.18 reported a large effect of VAC on the cross-link between accommodation and vergence (VACs of 1.5–4.5 D or MA) after 1 hour of viewing a stereoscopic virtual stimulus. The exposure time and magnitude of VAC in our study can be compared with Neveu et al.,17 who had a VAC of up to 1.5 D or MA (accommodation at 1.5 D while vergence oscillated between 1 and 3 MA) for 20 minutes and reported no change in the AC/A and TV.

In natural viewing, accommodation and vergence interact mutually and without conflict.12 An experiment using ramp-tracking tasks to fatigue accommodation and vergence demonstrated that alteration of the TA or TV caused a change in the cross-link interaction.42 Reduced CA/C ratio or increased AC/A ratio was associated with fatigued TA, and increased CA/C ratio or reduced AC/A ratio with fatigued TV. Using an AR-HMD, where the accommodation demand is fixed and the vergence demand is variable, may lead to imbalanced accommodation and vergence responses (Fig. 1). This is demonstrated in our findings where the reduced CA/C ratio coincides with reduced TA, indicating accommodation fatigue. Other factors that may influence accommodation and vergence responses are depth of focus and Panum's fusional area. If accommodation is within the depth of focus relative to the accommodation demand (±0.50 D),43 the forward path of the accommodation system will not be stimulated. Vergence, being less tolerant than accommodation, will lead to a phasic vergence response and an increased CA/C ratio if exceeding the limits of the Panum's fusional area (±0.05 MA).12 An increased CA/C ratio may be associated with accommodation overshoots,44 triggering a reduction in TA. Accommodation overshoots can contribute to accommodation fatigue, leading to reduced TA over time. An accommodation response greater than the depth of focus would be expected to drive vergence through the accommodation–vergence cross-link (Fig. 1), signaling vergence to reduce its effect on accommodation. Because vergence remains stronger than accommodation,37 the low CA/C ratio and TA would be maintained. Fatigued accommodation may not drive vergence responses, thus explaining the lack of change in the AC/A ratio and TV after the task in the AR-HMD (Fig. 7).

Measures of AC/A and CA/C ratios, and of TA and vergence,45,46 could be considered as part of binocular vision evaluation before exposure to stereoscopic viewing because they may predict the adaptability of the oculomotor system under VACs. For instance, an individual exhibiting poor adaptability of accommodation (rapid change in AC/A ratio) or vergence (rapid change in CA/C ratio) may have their comfort and visual performance negatively impacted when using an AR-HMD for, say, professional training.

In our study, there were, at the group level, changes in vergence-driven accommodation, but not in accommodation-driven vergence. Although there were individual differences in which parameters were affected (Figs. 6 and 7), there were no obvious vision parameters that could account for these differences, because all participants were functional emmetropes, had normal stereo vision, and were close to orthophoria. However, other aspects of binocular vision, such as the ability to alter focus and fixation between various distances and the flexibility within the vergence–accommodation system, could be potential factors for individual variations and are worth further consideration.

Previous studies using a behavioral pointing task have shown that VACs cause depth compression in AR-HMDs47 and that prolonged exposure to VACs impairs task performance when transferred to physical environments.20 Our findings suggest that VAC adaptations cause oculomotor changes, highlighting the need to understand how oculomotor effects and behavioral outcomes, such as task performance, are related.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

The AR task was designed to mimic the physical task as closely as possible, minimizing the potential confounding effect of the task itself. Thus, the oculomotor effect can be attributed to the AR condition rather than the execution of the task. Related studies have measured the accommodation and vergence cross-links before and after performing tasks without comparing with typical physical or control conditions.17,18,40,48 A possible limitation of this study is the short period between the task exposure and the start of the measurements (approximately 1 minute). Ideally, the measurement of TV should be conducted in real time while wearing the AR-HMD due to the short vergence decay time.42 However, currently, these measures can only be obtained outside of the AR-HMD, requiring a short transition time between exposure and measurement using the PowerRef 3.

This study only considered an optical-see-through AR-HMD. Video-see-through AR-HMDs capture the physical world using a pair of front-facing stereo cameras and fuse the video stream with the virtual content. The combined stream is displayed in the HMD at the same, fixed focal distance, and thus VAC is present whether looking at real or virtual content. The VAC is now more prevalent and may affect oculomotor function differently. It would be useful, therefore, to compare the impact of video- and optical-see-through HMDs on the oculomotor system, although note that video-see-through HMDs introduce additional challenges to depth perception due to the impossibility of placing the video cameras at the user's optic centers.

Conclusions

Adaptive responses in the oculomotor system of young adults are evident through changes in the CA/C ratio and TA following a 25-minute 3D AR task. These changes were driven mainly by the VAC present in contemporary AR-HMDs during these tasks. This suggests that exposure to visually and cognitively demanding 3D tasks in AR can, at the very least, temporarily alter the oculomotor system.
