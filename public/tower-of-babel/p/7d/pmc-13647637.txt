First report and molecular characterization of gizzard erosion-associated fowl adenovirus serotype 8a in Thailand
Authors: Sittinee Kulprasertsri, Nuttawat Leeraksakiat, Sakuna Phatthanakunanan, Pattrawut Saengnual, Ploypassorn Homklinkaew, Passorn Wonnapinij, Preeda Lertwatcharasarakul, Wallaya Phongphaew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGizzard erosion (GE) and ulceration are occasionally have been occasionally associated with several serotypes of fowl adenovirus (FAdV), particularly serotype 1 (FAdV-1). Clinical signs of adenoviral GE (AGE) are often subclinical with low mortality. In Thailand, FAdV-2, 8b, and 11 have been reported in association with inclusion body hepatitis (IBH), however, AGE remains undocumented. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate FAdV-associated GE and characterize the FAdV strain detected in GE-affected chickens in Thailand.ResultsHistopathological evaluation revealed intranuclear inclusion bodies within glandular epithelial cells of eroded gizzards. In situ hybridization (ISH) demonstrated positive signals for FAdV nucleic acid within affected glandular epithelial cells, and PCR targeting the hexon gene further confirmed the presence of FAdV genetic material. Sequencing and phylogenetic analyses of the complete hexon and fiber genes identified the virus associated with GE as FAdV-8a, designated FAdV8a_NPT_KU001_25. Multiple amino acid sequence alignment of the Fiber protein showed that the Thai GE-associated isolate was more closely related to a gizzard-tropic FAdV-8a strain than to an IBH-associated FAdV-8a strain. Although amino acid variations within the knob domain may be associated with differences in tissue tropism and clinical manifestations, their biological significance and role in pathogenesis require further investigation.ConclusionsThis study represents the first report of a potential GE-associated FAdV-8a strain in Thailand. The findings suggest an association between FAdV-8a infection and GE lesions in affected chickens. However, the etiological role and pathogenic mechanisms of FAdV-8a in GE remain unclear and require further investigation, including experimental reproduction studies. The genomic characterization of this strain provides additional information that may contribute to a better understanding of the genetic diversity and evolution of FAdV-8a.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05713-3.

Background

Fowl adenoviruses (FAdVs) are non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses with a genome size ranging from 43–45 kb, belonging to the genus Aviadenovirus in the family Adenoviridae [1, 2]. According to serological analysis and phylogenetic analysis based on the hexon gene, FAdVs are classified into 12 serotypes, corresponding to genotype FAdV-1 to FAdV-11, with serotype 8 further subdivided into FAdV-8a and −8b [3–7]. Moreover, the virus can be divided into 5 species, Aviadenovirus ventriculi, Aviadenovirus quintum, Aviadenovirus hydropericardii, Aviadenovirus gallinae, and Aviadenovirus hepatitidis, based on the restriction enzyme digestion patterns [3, 7]. FAdVs are associated with poultry diseases, including IBH, hepatitis-hydropericardium syndrome, pancreatic necrosis and adenoviral gizzard erosion (AGE). However, most infections are subclinical [8, 9]. In addition, previous studies presented the occurrence of FAdV co-infection with either different FAdV strains or other viruses, particularly immunosuppressive viruses such as chicken infectious anemia virus or infectious bursal disease virus, which could significantly increase morbidity and mortality [10–14].

Since the etiology of AGE was first identified in Japan, 1993 [15], awareness has increased markedly. Subsequently, natural outbreaks of AGE caused by various FAdV serotypes were reported in several countries [16], such as the United States [17], United Kingdom [18], Poland [19], Belgium [20], Germany [21], Morocco [22], Hungary [23], China [24], Korea [25, 26], Japan [27, 28] and Iran [29]. Although FAdV-1 is widely recognized as the primary causative agent of AGE worldwide, a few cases of FAdV-4, −8a, and −11 were reported in association with GE [18, 29–32]. Furthermore, an increasing number of studies have suggested that FAdV-8a may contribute to the development of AGE, either individually or in combination with other serotypes [18, 30]. The AGE is commonly observed in broilers, ranging in age from 3–5 weeks [32]. Birds affected by AGE generally do not exhibit obvious clinical signs. However, they show low daily weight gain and low feed conversion ratio, with lesions commonly detected incidentally during postmortem condemnation in a slaughterhouse. Recently, several studies indicated that AGE-associated FAdV strains also affected egg production, resulting in economic loss in the egg production industry [18, 19, 33]. Information on AGE remains limited, as affected avians are typically asymptomatic, leading to frequent disregard of the issue. The etiology of gizzard erosion is intricate, with potential causes including amines, feed composition and fiber content, biogenic amines, congenital factors, nutritional inadequacies, mycotoxins, and bacterial infections [34]. The complex origin of the lesion occasionally results in the misinterpretation of AGE. Consequently, the genetic characterization and pathogenicity of AGE-associated FAdV strains are constrained. In the present study, White Leghorn chickens exhibiting poor growth performance were submitted to the Kamphaeng Saen Veterinary Diagnostic Center (KVDC), Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Kasetsart University, Kamphaeng Saen Campus, Nakhon Pathom, Thailand. At necropsy, the affected gizzard showed multifocal areas of erosion, hemorrhage, and discoloration of the koilin membrane, consistent with AGE lesions. We hypothesized that FAdV might be associated with the gizzard erosions in these White Leghorn chickens. However, AGE has never been reported in Thailand. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to investigate the presence of FAdV in clinical cases of GE in poultry in Thailand and to provide information on the pathological and genetic characterization of the Thai isolate. This study aims to better understand the Thai GE-associated FAdV-8a strain by characterizing its histopathological features and genetic profile.

Materials and methods

Animals

A flock of 1,040 White Leghorn chickens aged 19 days, located on a farm in Phetchaburi Province, western Thailand, exhibited stunted growth, pronounced non-uniformity in body size, and an increased mortality rate of 1.54%. Due to the affected chickens showing a subclinical presentation, morbidity rates were not documented. The chickens were reared indoors in an open-house system with limited biosecurity measures and had unrestricted access to commercial feed and tap water. The farm is managed by a local proprietor.

Gross examination and sampling

Six affected chickens were referred to the Kasetsart University Veterinary Diagnostic Center (KVDC), Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Kasetsart University, for further investigation of the disease problems. The chickens were euthanized by exposure to high concentrations of carbon dioxide in a sealed container, in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020). Unconsciousness was confirmed by assessing the absence of pulse and respiration, loss of the palpebral reflex, protrusion of the nictitating membrane, and relaxation of the vent. Subsequently, they were promptly exsanguinated by cutting the right jugular vein. The necropsy procedures were conducted in accordance with the standard necropsy protocol. Gizzard and liver fresh tissues were collected with an aseptic technique for further analyses, including PCR detection and viral isolation. Additionally, the affected tissues or organs were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for histopathological examination and in situ hybridization. This study was approved by KASETSART UNIVERSITY’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Thailand (IACUC No. ACKU66-VET-021).

Study period and location

The samples were collected in 2023 at KVDC, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Kasetsart University, Kamphaeng Saen Campus, Nakhon Pathom, Thailand.

Histological preparation and In Situ Hybridization (ISH)

Formalin-fixed tissues were trimmed and processed by a tissue processor machine, ATP140 (Amos, Australia). Subsequently, the samples were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at approximately 4–5 µm thick, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) according to standard procedures. The slides were examined by a pathologist at the Department of Pathology, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Kasetsart University, Thailand. The in situ hybridization probe was synthesized by PCR amplification using degenerate primers AdenoH1; 5'-GCACCTACTTCGACATAA-3'and AdenoH4; 5'-GCBARCATGTACTGGTARC T-3', in combination with the PCR DIG labeling mix (Roche, Germany). The reaction mixture was prepared and thermal cycling were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Viral isolation

The virus was isolated in primary chicken embryo liver cells (CEL). The CEL cells were prepared from 12 to 14-day-old embryonated chicken eggs according to a previous study [35]. Briefly, the livers were aseptically harvested and then gently trypsinized with a 0.25% trypsin–EDTA solution for 10–15 min. The cells were grown in 1X Minimum Essential Medium (Gibco™, Invitrogen Corporation, CA, USA) supplemented with L-glutamine, 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (HyClone®, Thermo Scientific, UT, USA), and Penicillin–Streptomycin and incubated at 37ºC, in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Pooled affected gizzards were ground with PBS, then filtered through a 0.2 µm pore-sized syringe filter prior to inoculation into CEL cells. Infected CEL cells were incubated at 37 ºC for 24 to 72 h post-infection until the appearance of cytopathic effects. The cell culture supernatant was harvested and subjected to PCR identification using specific primers.

Nucleic acid extraction and polymerase chain reaction

Total DNA was extracted from both tissue homogenates and cell culture supernatants using FavorPrep™ Viral Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit (Favorgen, Taiwan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The total genomic DNA was eluted in 50 µl of nuclease-free water. For PCR detection of FAdV, primers were designed based on a multiple sequence alignment using BioEdit version 7.0.5.3. The primer sequences were designed based on the conserved regions within the FAdV hexon gene, using genome sequences retrieved from the GenBank database. The sequences used for alignment included the following accession numbers: AF508959 (FAdV-11), AF339925 (FAdV-12), AF508946 (FAdV-2), AF508948 (FAdV-3), AF508956 (FAdV-8a), AF508954 (FAdV-6), AF508955 (FAdV-7), AF508958 (FAdV-9), AF508952 (FAdV-5), AY852270 (Psittacine adenovirus 1), AF339914 (FAdV-1), AF508951 (FAdV-4), Y09598 (Avian adenovirus), and AF074946 (Hemorrhagic enteritis virus).

These primers were designated as follows: AdenoH1 5'-GCACCTACTTCGACATAA-3'and AdenoH4 5'-GCBARCATGTACTGGTARC T-3'. The PCR mixture contained 10 µl of 2X PCRBIO Taq Mix Red (PCR Biosystems Ltd., London, UK), 0.2 µl of each 10 µM forward and reverse primers, 2 µl of DNA sample, and nuclease-free water up to 20 µl of the final volume. PCR amplification was performed under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 98ºC for 10 s, annealing at 53ºC for 30 s, and extension at 72ºC for 30 s, with a final extension step at 72ºC for 5 min. The PCR assay was conducted using the DNA samples, along with positive controls (FAdV-2, FAdV-11, and FAdV-8b) and a negative control (no-template control). The amplified PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis in 1.5% agarose gel stained with RedSafe™ Nucleic Acid Staining Solution (iNtRON, Korea) and visualized under UV transilluminator.

High-quality DNA extraction and whole genome sequencing

The FAdV positive supernatant was subjected to viral DNA extraction using the Quick-DNA miniprep kit™ (Zymo Research, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA sample was submitted to Azenta Life Sciences (Beijing, China). DNA concentration was measured using the Qubit fluorometer. Subsequently, metagenomic Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) was performed using the Illumina HiSeq platform, with a read length of 150 base pairs (bp).

Data processing and phylogenetic analysis

Raw sequence reads obtained from NGS were processed using bioinformatics pipelines in Galaxy, a web-based platform [36]. Read quality was assessed using FastQC [37], followed by trimming of low-quality reads and residual adaptors with Trimmomatic (version 0.36.6) [38]. Parameters were adjusted for the IlluminaClip step, with the adapter sequence set to TruSeq3 (paired-end, for MiSeq and HiSeq). Sliding window trimming was enabled, with the minimum required average quality (Phred score) set to 20. Genome assembly was performed using SPAdes (version 3.15.5 + galaxy2) [39, 40] for de novo assembly, in conjunction with BWA-MEM2 (version 2.2.1 + galaxy1) [41, 42] for reference-based mapping. The configuration was set for paired-end reads and simple Illumina for analysis mode. A sequence of FAdV TR59 strain (NC_075459), a representative of FAdV-8a, was used as the reference sequence for mapping. The mapping results were processed using iVar consensus (version 1.4.3 + galaxy) to generate a consensus sequence [43]. Key parameters for consensus generation included a minimum variant frequency threshold of 0.9 and a minimum read depth of 20. Gene annotation of the hexon, fiber, and penton base genes was performed using multiple sequence alignments via the MAFFT program (version 7) [44]. The resulting gene sequences were subjected to BLAST analysis [45] against the GenBank database [46] to confirm gene identity and ensure accurate annotation. Subsequently, the complete gene sequences were aligned with those of reference strains for phylogenetic analysis. Serotype identification was conducted based on analysis of the hexon gene. For codon-based alignment, all nucleotide sequences were translated into amino acid sequences and aligned using the MAFFT program (version 7).

The resulting amino acid alignments were then used to guide codon-based nucleotide alignment with Pal2Nal [47]. The alignments were visualized and manually inspected using AliView [48]. When sequence ends were uneven, terminal regions were trimmed so that all sequences started and ended at homologous positions. After trimming, the final alignment lengths for the hexon, fiber, and penton base genes were 2,907, 2,442, and 1,680 nucleotide positions, respectively. The trimmed alignments were subsequently used for phylogenetic tree construction. The optimal nucleotide substitution model for each gene was determined using MEGA11 [49], and phylogenetic trees were subsequently constructed using the maximum likelihood (ML) method. The Tamura-Nei model with a Gamma distribution and invariant sites (TN93 + G + I) was applied for analysis of the hexon gene. The Tamura-Nei model with a Gamma distribution (TN93 + G) was applied for analysis of the fiber gene, whereas the Tamura 3-parameter model with a Gamma distribution and invariant sites (T92 + G + I) was selected for the penton base. All phylogenetic analyses were performed with 1,000 bootstrap replicates to assess branch support. Bootstrap values ≥ 70% were considered to indicate reliable branch support, whereas values below 70% were interpreted cautiously. Duck adenovirus serotype 2 (NC_024486) was designated as the outgroup. The details about the representative FAdV sequences used to create the phylogenetic trees are in Supplementary Table 1. The trees were visualized in MEGA11 for interpretation and presentation. Pairwise alignments for percent identity and GC content were performed using the MAFFT program (version 7) and visualized using Geneious Prime (version 2025.0.3) (http://www.geneious.com).

Additionally, multiple amino acid sequence alignments of the Fiber proteins were performed using MAFFT (version 7) to investigate amino acid differences, employing the same alignment parameters as those used for phylogenetic tree construction. The similarity percentage of amino acid sequences from Fiber and their alignment were visualized in Geneious Prime (version 2025.0.3) (http://www.geneious.com).

Recombination analysis

Recombination analysis was performed using SimPlot version 3.5.1 with representative reference strains from the same FAdV species. Similarity plots were generated using the Kimura 2-parameter model with a transition/transversion ratio of 2.0, a window size of 200 bp, and a step size of 20 bp. Phylogenetic support was evaluated using the neighbor-joining method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates [50].

Gross examination of the gizzards revealed a rough, dark to black discoloration with destruction of the koilin layer (Fig. 1B). The koilin layers were difficult to remove, and the eroded gizzard mucosa was reddened and irregular in appearance (Fig. 1C). DNA Sequencing

Metagenomic NSGS was performed using DNA extracted from the supernatant of infected CEL cells. After filtering reads mapped to the host genome, 4,099,531 paired-end reads were retained for viral genome analysis. The theoretical sequencing depth was approximately 27,330, based on PE150 sequencing data and an estimated FAdV genome size of 45,000 bp, assuming that all retained reads originated from the viral genome. The initial NGS assembly generated a 43,294 bp consensus sequence containing 5,265 ambiguous bases. Therefore, 38,029 bp of the consensus sequence were resolved as defined nucleotide bases, corresponding to 87.84% of the assembled sequence. These ambiguous regions were subsequently targeted for gap filling and sequence validation. Complete sequences of the genes encoding the major structural proteins, hexon, fiber, and penton base, were obtained and submitted to GenBank under the accession numbers PV703821, PV703822, and PV703823, respectively. The lengths of the hexon, fiber, and penton base genes were 2,847 bp, 1,575 bp, and 1,659 bp, respectively. Additionally, the whole genome sequence of the viral isolate was also submitted into GenBank under the accession number PZ044074.

A comparison between the two methods was conducted to identify discrepancies and assess the accuracy of sequences assembled by BWA-MEM2 mapping and de novo assembly using SPAdes, particularly in highly variable regions, no differences were detected between the two methods (Supplementary Fig. 1). Analysis of the NGS data revealed uniform read mapping to the FAdV-8a reference genome, with no reads or assembled contigs corresponding to sequences FAdV-1 or other serotypes.

The complete sequences of the hexon, fiber and penton base genes were further analyzed by phylogenetic analysis and multiple amino acid sequence alignment.

Recombination analysis

Recombination analysis using SimPlot was conducted to evaluate whether the hexon and fiber genes of FAdV-8a_NPT_KU001_25 showed evidence of inter-serotype recombination. The similarity plot demonstrated that the Thai isolate showed consistently high similarity to representative FAdV-8a strains across most regions of the genome (Fig. 9). Although variation in similarity was observed in some genomic regions, particularly outside the major structural gene regions, the hexon and fiber gene regions did not show a distinct switch in similarity from FAdV-8a to another serotype. Discussion

FAdV-1 is widely recognized as the cause of GE in poultry worldwide [51], whereas FAdV-8a strains are commonly associated with IBH [52]. Nonetheless, FAdV-8a strains have also been detected in chickens suffering from AGE, although less frequently than FAdV-1 [30, 53]. However, there are limited studies on pathogenesis, genomic characteristics, and genetic variations of the GE-associated FAdV-8a.

The present study is the first report of FAdV-8a isolated from GE-affected White Leghorn chickens in Thailand. The association between FAdV and GE lesion was demonstrated through histopathological examination, in situ hybridization, and molecular detection. The lesion was only located in the gizzard. However, the FAdV nucleic acid was also sparsely detectable in these cells via in situ hybridization. Consistently, the previous reports suggested that the inclusion bodies were rarely observed in natural cases of AGE due to the late detection of the infection [29, 51]. The optimal time to detect inclusion bodies in the affected gizzard was only during the first week of infection [54]. In addition, Lindgren et al. reported that inclusion bodies were typically seen in the koilin membrane, peeling or loss of the koilin layer may reduce the likelihood of detecting both intranuclear inclusion bodies and also nucleic acid of the FAdV [51]. FAdV-8a serotype has been recognized as a serotype associated with IBH. However, the FAdV8a_NPT_KU001_25 strain was isolated from the affected gizzard without any macroscopic or microscopic hepatic lesions.

Phylogenetic analysis of the hexon and fiber genes demonstrated that the FAdV8a_NPT_KU001_25 strain was most genetically closely related to the TR59 strain, FAdV-8a which was originally isolated in Japan. Nonetheless, the epidemiological relationship among these strains remained unknown.

As FAdV-1 is recognized as the primary causative agent of AGE [18, 51]. Therefore, coinfection with FAdV-1 and 8a was also considered in the present study. However, the analysis of the NGS data revealed uniform read mapping to the FAdV-8a reference genome, with no reads or assembled contigs corresponding to FAdV-1 or others. These findings suggest that coinfection with FAdV-1 was not detected in the analyzed sample. Therefore, the GE lesions observed in the present study were likely associated with FAdV-8a infection. Interestingly, a previous study isolated FAdV-1 and FAdV-8a from the GE-affected chickens. Only FAdV-1 was successfully reproduced in experimental infection studies, whereas FAdV-8a inoculated chicken showed no pathological lesion [18]. Therefore, the etiological role and pathogenesis of FAdV-8a in GE remain controversial and require further investigation.

According to the recombination analysis, no evidence of recombination was clearly identified, particularly within the hexon and fiber genes. However, as FAdV-8b is one of the predominant circulating serotypes in Thailand [55, 56], the potential for recombination among FAdV serotypes should continue to be monitored. Ongoing surveillance is therefore important to track the evolution and emergence of potentially novel pathogenic FAdV strains in Thailand.

The previous studies suggested that knob region of Fiber protein might play a role in a receptor binding and tissue tropism [57, 58]. Multiple amino acid sequence alignments of the Fiber protein were performed to investigate its potential amino acid differences associated with gizzard tropism of FAdV-8a NPT_KU001_25. According to the results, the knob domain of the Thai FAdV-8a isolate was identical to that of the gizzard-tropic strain JL/170408, whereas it differed from the IBH-associated strain CH/GDLZ/201801 [24, 59]. Consistently, a previous study demonstrated a similar pattern of amino acid variations within the knob domain when non-IBH- and IBH-associated FAdV-8 strains were compared [60]. These amino acid variations were located at residues 374–524. Although amino acid variations in the Fiber protein between GE- and IBH-associated strains were identified, their potential relationship with tissue tropism remains unclear, and further functional studies are required to determine their biological significance.

Furthermore, a previous in silico study predicted that a putative epitope within the fiber knob domain may contribute to the induction of neutralizing antibodies. Amino acid variations near residues G441–R442 extending toward the C-terminal region were identified as the dominant neutralization epitope in FAdV-8a [61]. Therefore, information regarding this region may be valuable for the development of vaccines specific to FAdV-8a strains. However, further experimental studies are required to elucidate the functional significance of these mutations.

Conclusions

The present study reports the first detection of a potential GE-associated FAdV-8a strain in Thailand. Although FAdV-8a has rarely been associated with GE lesions, the identification of the FAdV8a_NPT_KU001_25 strain in GE-affected chickens is a noteworthy finding. However, the etiological role and pathogenic mechanisms of FAdV-8a in GE remain unclear and require further investigation. Experimental reproduction of the disease and re-isolation of the virus will be necessary to confirm its pathogenic role in GE in accordance with Koch’s postulates. In addition, potential predisposing factors and other possible causes of GE should also be investigated and excluded.

Given the limited availability of complete genome sequences for FAdV-8a, the characterization of this strain provides valuable data that may enhance our understanding of the genetic diversity, evolutionary relationships, and pathogenesis of this serotype. Future efforts to expand the genetic data of FAdV-8a in public databases will be essential for improving the accuracy of phylogenetic analyses and for elucidating the molecular basis of virulence associated with this virus.
