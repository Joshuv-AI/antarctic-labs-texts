Stage-dependent prognostic value of clinical and pathological T staging in resected lung adenocarcinoma: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Libing Yang, Chao Guo, Xin Du, Shanqing Li, Jiaolong Wang, Dawei Wang, Haoqiang Chi, Dianpeng Shi, Hui Zhang, Lan Song, Lei Yu, Yeye Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreoperative CT-based clinical T staging guides surgical planning in lung adenocarcinoma, whereas pathological T staging remains the postoperative reference standard. Prior studies have demonstrated discordance between clinical and pathological TNM staging in lung cancer, but stage-specific T-stage migration, comparative prognostic value, and implications for surgical decision thresholds in lung adenocarcinoma remain insufficiently defined.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter cohort study included 10,767 patients with surgically resected invasive lung adenocarcinoma. Clinical tumor size and T stage were obtained from official preoperative CT reports using the maximum reported tumor diameter, whereas pathological tumor size and T stage were obtained from final surgical pathology reports. Concordance, stage migration, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) performance were assessed across T categories. Survival analyses were performed in 6,279 patients with follow-up data, and C-index was used to compare the prognostic discrimination of clinical versus pathological tumor size.ResultsCT-based tumor size exceeded pathological measurement on average (mean, 1.83 cm vs. 1.51 cm), with downstaging predominating across cT1c–T3 tumors. Clinical–pathological T-stage agreement followed a stage-dependent pattern, with concordance highest at the extremes (T1a, T4) and lowest at intermediate stages, a finding supported by ROC analysis. Both clinical and pathological T staging stratified disease-free and overall survival. C-index analysis suggested a modest stage-dependent crossover in prognostic discrimination: clinical tumor size showed better discrimination in early-stage disease (cT1a–T1b), whereas pathological tumor size performed better in advanced stages (T3–T4).ConclusionsClinical and pathological T staging provide complementary, context-dependent information across the disease spectrum. Clinical staging showed higher concordance with pathological staging and slightly better prognostic discrimination in early-stage tumors (T1a–T1b), whereas pathological staging offered stronger discrimination in advanced disease. These findings refine the interpretation of T staging in resected invasive lung adenocarcinoma by emphasizing measurement source, disease-stage context, and uncertainty around operative decision thresholds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16494-1.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, Clinical T staging, Pathological T staging, Tumor size, Surgical decision-making, Prognostic evaluation

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. With changes in smoking patterns and the widespread use of low-dose computed tomography (CT), lung adenocarcinoma has become the predominant histologic subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in many regions [2, 3]. Accurate tumor staging is central to the management of lung adenocarcinoma. Among the TNM components, T stage is particularly relevant to thoracic surgeons because it directly affects the choice of resection extent, lymph node assessment strategy, and consideration of perioperative systemic therapy [4, 5].

In routine practice, these decisions must be made before surgery and therefore rely primarily on CT-based clinical staging. Pathological staging, although regarded as the definitive postoperative reference, is not available at the time when operative planning is performed. Discrepancies between clinical and pathological tumor size are common, especially for tumors close to critical size thresholds. Even small differences of a few millimeters may shift a tumor from one T category to another and may influence whether a patient is considered for sublobar resection, lobectomy, systematic lymph node dissection, or multimodal treatment.

Previous studies have established that clinical and pathological staging are not fully concordant. A registry-based analysis demonstrated limited agreement between clinical and pathological TNM staging in a general lung cancer population [6], and several radiologic-pathologic studies have examined tumor size discrepancy in early-stage NSCLC or selected adenocarcinoma cohorts [7–9]. However, most prior work has focused on overall concordance or size measurement differences. Less is known about whether T-stage discordance follows a stage-specific pattern in lung adenocarcinoma, whether clinical and pathological tumor size differ in prognostic utility, and how such discrepancies should be interpreted around surgical decision thresholds.

To address these gaps, we performed a large-scale multicenter cohort study of patients with surgically resected invasive lung adenocarcinoma. We quantified the magnitude and direction of clinical-pathological tumor size and T-stage discrepancies across the full T-stage spectrum, evaluated stage-specific predictive performance, and compared the prognostic discrimination of clinical versus pathological tumor size. We aimed to clarify whether CT-based and pathology-based T staging should be viewed as interchangeable measures or as complementary sources of prognostic and decision-relevant information.

Methods

Patient selection and criteria

We initially identified 11,424 patients with histologically confirmed lung adenocarcinoma who underwent surgical resection between January 2015 and December 2024 at three participating centers located in Beijing, China. Subsequently, exclusion criteria were applied. Patients with preinvasive lesions, including adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) (n = 506) and atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH) when recorded in the source pathology reports, were excluded based on histology. Patients with incomplete or missing preoperative CT reports or pathological records (n = 151) were also excluded. After these exclusions, the final study cohort comprised 10,767 patients with surgically resected invasive lung adenocarcinoma. For survival analyses, follow-up data were available for a subset of 6,279 patients, who were therefore included in the survival cohort. The patient selection process is summarized in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram of patient selection. Of 11,424 patients with resected lung adenocarcinoma, 506 with carcinoma in situ and 151 with incomplete data were excluded. The final cohort included 10,767 patients for tumor size and T-stage analysis, including 6,279 with follow-up data for survival analysis

The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [10]. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College (Approval No. S-K2117). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for the use of their clinical data for research purposes. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

Data from the lead participating center were extracted from an institutionally maintained AI-enhanced lung cancer database, whereas data from the other two participating centers were manually collected and entered into the study database. The AI-enhanced database is prospectively maintained and integrates electronic medical records, imaging archives, and pathology reports, thereby ensuring comprehensive and standardized clinical information.

Clinical tumor size and T stage were derived from official preoperative chest CT reports, which were generated using standardized institutional chest CT protocols and interpreted by certified radiologists. Tumor size was abstracted as the maximum diameter reported on thin-section CT, most commonly from axial lung-window images; when more than one dimension was reported, the largest recorded diameter was used. Routine three-dimensional volumetric measurement was not available across all centers and was therefore not included. Pathological tumor size and T stage were obtained from final surgical pathology reports. Tumor diameters were determined by certified pathologists after formalin fixation and paraffin embedding according to each center’s routine pathology workflow. Both clinical and pathological T staging were assigned according to the eighth edition of the TNM classification system [11]. Follow-up data were collected by trained clinical staff through standardized telephone follow-up procedures.

Data quality checks were performed before analysis, including range checks, review of inconsistent clinical-pathological T-stage combinations, and verification of implausible values against the original radiology and pathology reports. The study did not include retrospective central remeasurement by an independent imaging panel or central pathology review; therefore, the reported measurements reflect real-world institutional practice.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.13.1). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Correlation between clinical and pathological tumor diameters was assessed using Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients and linear regression models. Concordance between clinical and pathological T staging was evaluated using cross-tabulation and quantified with Cohen’s Kappa coefficients. Interpretation of Kappa values followed standard benchmarks for agreement strength.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess the discriminatory performance of clinical T stages in predicting corresponding pathological T stages. Area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated for each T category to evaluate stage-specific predictive value.

Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with comparisons between groups made using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to assess the prognostic significance of clinical and pathological T staging on overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS). To further compare the prognostic value of clinical versus pathological tumor size measurements, C-index was calculated. The C-index estimates the concordance between predicted and observed survival outcomes, with higher values indicating greater prognostic discrimination. Bootstrapping with 1,000 resamples was used to estimate 95% confidence intervals for C-index comparisons. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because follow-up data were not available for the entire cohort, baseline characteristics of the full analytic cohort and survival cohort were compared and are reported in Table S1; survival and C-index analyses were interpreted as secondary analyses subject to potential selection bias.

Results

Tumor size and T-staging distribution

A total of 10,767 patients diagnosed with surgically resected invasive lung adenocarcinoma after exclusion of AIS/AAH were included in this analysis. Among them, 39.3% were male, and 60.7% were female. The majority of patients were between 60 and 70 years of age.

Comparison of tumor size revealed that clinical sizes recorded from preoperative CT were generally larger than pathological sizes measured on resected specimens. The mean clinical tumor size was 1.83 cm, compared to a mean pathological tumor size of 1.51 cm (Fig. 2A).

Clinical versus Pathological Tumor Size and T-stage Distribution. (A) Distribution of tumor diameters measured by clinical and pathological assessment; (B) Distribution of clinical and pathological T-stage classifications

Marked differences were also observed in T-stage distributions. Based on clinical staging, the majority were classified as T1b (48.2%), followed by T1a (21.7%), T1c (19.3%) and T2a (7.0%). Pathological staging shifted significantly toward lower stages: T1a predominated (42.7%), T1b (37.1%), T1c (11.7%), and T2a (5.8%) (Fig. 2B).

Correlation between clinical and pathological measurements

Clinical and pathological tumor diameters showed a strong positive correlation (Fig. 3A). The fitted regression line was located below the identity line across most of the tumor-size range, consistent with the overall tendency for CT-based clinical measurements to exceed pathological measurements.

The dashed horizontal line indicates the overall κ value

The clinical–pathological T-stage heat map further demonstrated that this correlation at the continuous size level did not translate into complete agreement at the categorical T-stage level (Fig. 3B). Although large numbers of patients remained on the diagonal, indicating concordant clinical and pathological T stages, substantial off-diagonal redistribution was observed. This discordance was particularly apparent in the intermediate clinical T-stage categories, where many tumors were reassigned to lower pathological T stages.

The stage migration plot showed that pathological downstaging was the predominant direction of discordance across most clinical T-stage categories (Fig. 3C). Concordance was highest among clinical T1a tumors, whereas the proportion of downstaged cases increased markedly from clinical T1c onward. Downstaging was especially frequent among tumors classified clinically as T1c, T2a, T2b, and T3. In contrast, pathological upstaging was relatively uncommon overall and was most evident among clinical T1a tumors.

Cohen’s κ analysis confirmed the limited agreement between clinical and pathological T staging (Fig. 3D). Category-specific agreement was relatively higher for T1a and T4 tumors, with κ values of 0.406 and 0.453, respectively, whereas lower κ values were observed for intermediate categories, particularly T1b, T1c, T2a, and T2b.

ROC analysis of clinical T stage for predicting pathological T stage

ROC curve analysis further supported a stage-dependent concordance pattern, in line with the agreement pattern observed for Cohen’s κ. Clinical T-stage predictions showed relatively higher accuracy for the lowest stage T1a (AUC = 0.691), while the intermediate stages exhibited lower predictive accuracy (T1b: AUC = 0.614; T1c: AUC = 0.676; T2a: AUC = 0.656). Accuracy improved for the higher T stages (T2b: AUC = 0.704; T3: AUC = 0.706; T4: AUC = 0.875) (Fig. 4).

The AUC values indicate that prediction was weakest around intermediate T-stage thresholds and strongest for T4 disease. Survival analysis based on clinical and pathological T-stage

Because follow-up data were available for only a subset of the cohort, we compared baseline characteristics between the full analytic cohort and the survival cohort to evaluate potential selection bias in the survival analyses (Table S1). Demographic characteristics, tumor size, and clinical and pathological T-stage distributions appeared broadly comparable between the two cohorts.

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that both clinical and pathological T staging were significantly associated with DFS and OS (all log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 5A–D). Overall, both staging systems provided effective prognostic stratification. In both staging systems, higher T stage was associated with progressively worse survival outcomes.

Survival stratification and prognostic discrimination by clinical and pathological T staging. (A) Disease-free survival (DFS) according to clinical T stage. (B) DFS according to pathological T stage. (C) Overall survival (OS) according to clinical T stage. (D) OS according to pathological T stage. (E) Comparison of C-index values for clinical tumor size and pathological tumor size in predicting DFS across the overall cohort and T-stage subgroups

C-index analysis further revealed a stratified pattern. Overall, clinical tumor size and pathological tumor size showed broadly comparable discriminative ability for DFS, but this apparent similarity masked heterogeneity across T-stage subgroups (Fig. 5E). In lower-stage subgroups, particularly T1a and T1b, clinical tumor size on CT showed better prognostic discrimination than pathological tumor size, as reflected by higher C-index values. In contrast, pathological tumor size showed higher C-index values in the T3 + T4 subgroup, suggesting that the prognostic advantage of pathological staging became more evident with increasing tumor burden. In intermediate-stage subgroups, including T1c and T2 disease, the discriminative performance of the two measures fluctuated and overlapped. Because the absolute differences in discrimination were modest, these findings should be interpreted as incremental and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence for changing treatment decisions based on tumor size source alone.

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort of 10,767 patients with surgically resected invasive lung adenocarcinoma, we found that clinical and pathological T staging were closely related but not interchangeable. CT-based clinical tumor size was generally larger than pathological tumor size, resulting in frequent stage migration. This discordance was not uniform across the T-stage spectrum: clinical-pathological agreement followed a stage-dependent pattern, with relatively high concordance at the lowest and highest T categories and the greatest uncertainty at intermediate thresholds. Survival analyses further suggested that clinical and pathological tumor size provide complementary prognostic information.

These findings extend previous registry-based evidence showing limited concordance between clinical and pathological TNM staging in lung cancer [6]. Whereas prior studies primarily quantified overall staging agreement in heterogeneous lung cancer populations or focused on selected stage ranges, our study specifically evaluated surgically resected invasive lung adenocarcinoma across the full T-stage spectrum, from T1a to T4, and examined stage-specific T-stage migration, ROC performance, and the comparative prognostic discrimination of clinical versus pathological tumor size. This distinction is clinically relevant because T-stage thresholds in lung adenocarcinoma are closely linked to surgical planning, including the selection between sublobar resection and lobectomy, the extent of lymph node assessment, and the consideration of perioperative systemic therapy.

The tendency for CT to overestimate pathological tumor size is biologically and technically plausible. Resected lung specimens may undergo alveolar collapse and formalin-induced shrinkage, particularly in tumors with lepidic, aerated, ground-glass, or part-solid components ([7–8, 12, 13]). However, the present multicenter database did not uniformly capture consolidation-to-tumor ratio, ground-glass versus solid composition, lepidic-predominant histologic pattern, or invasive-component size across all centers. Therefore, direct radiologic-pathologic correlation between ground-glass/solid CT features and lepidic/invasive histologic patterns could not be analyzed in this study and remains an important direction for future work. Conversely, CT may include spiculation, irregular margins, peritumoral inflammation, obstructive changes, or adjacent vascular and bronchial structures as part of the apparent lesion extent [14]. In addition, CT and pathological measurements are performed under fundamentally different conditions: CT evaluates an inflated lung in vivo, whereas pathology assesses a collapsed, processed specimen. Differences in measurement planes, slice thickness, partial volume effects, and interobserver variability may further contribute to discordance [15]. These factors are unlikely to affect all tumors equally, which may partly explain why agreement varied substantially across T categories.

The stage-dependent pattern of concordance deserves particular attention. Clinical T1a tumors showed relatively high concordance, likely because very small lesions have limited room for measurement-induced category shifts. At the other end of the spectrum, T3 and T4 tumors are often defined by broader size ranges or anatomical invasion patterns, allowing small measurement differences to be absorbed without changing the final T category. In contrast, intermediate stages such as T1b, T1c, and T2a are defined by narrow thresholds, where modest differences between CT and pathological measurements can produce clinically meaningful stage migration. Notably, although downstaging predominated in our cohort, discordance at these thresholds may also be bidirectional, particularly when visceral pleural invasion or other adverse pathological features are identified histologically but are not confidently recognized on CT [16].

This vulnerable intermediate zone has direct implications for thoracic surgical decision-making. The boundaries between T1b, T1c, and T2a overlap with clinically important decision points used when considering segmentectomy, wedge resection, lobectomy, systematic lymph node dissection, and the intensity of perioperative evaluation. Therefore, discordance at these thresholds is not merely a statistical issue; it may alter the perceived risk category and potentially influence the treatment pathway. Our findings do not suggest that CT-based staging should be replaced by pathological staging in preoperative decision-making, which is impossible before surgery. Rather, they indicate that CT-based T stage should be interpreted with particular caution when tumors lie close to key surgical thresholds. In such cases, additional information from high-resolution imaging review, consolidation-to-tumor ratio, volumetric CT assessment, intraoperative frozen section, and multidisciplinary evaluation may help reduce the clinical consequences of staging uncertainty.

An additional observation of this study is the comparison of prognostic discrimination between clinical and pathological tumor size. In early-stage tumors, particularly T1a-T1b, clinical tumor size showed better discrimination for disease-free survival than pathological tumor size. One possible explanation is that CT preserves the in vivo architecture of the lesion, including aerated or lepidic components that may be altered or lost during fixation and specimen processing. By contrast, in more advanced tumors, pathological tumor size appeared to provide stronger prognostic discrimination, likely because it more directly reflects the final invasive tumor burden. This stage-dependent crossover supports the concept that clinical and pathological measurements capture different but clinically relevant dimensions of tumor biology, but the observed differences were modest and should not be used alone to determine adjuvant therapy.

These results have several cautious practical implications. For small early-stage lung adenocarcinomas, CT-based clinical staging remains highly relevant for operative planning and risk stratification, particularly in the current era of expanding indications for sublobar resection in selected peripheral NSCLC [17]. However, for tumors near intermediate thresholds, especially around the T1b/T1c and T1c/T2a boundaries, surgeons should be aware that small measurement differences may affect the apparent appropriateness of limited resection, the perceived need for more extensive lymph node assessment, and the threshold for more intensive perioperative evaluation. For higher-stage tumors, pathological staging remains essential for postoperative prognostication and adjuvant treatment planning, including decisions informed by lymph node status and multimodality treatment considerations [18, 19]. Taken together, our findings support a context-specific interpretation of T staging rather than a rigid assumption that clinical and pathological measurements are directly interchangeable or that size-source differences alone should dictate management.

Our study has several limitations. First, although the cohort was multicenter, all participating hospitals were located in north China, which may limit geographic and demographic generalizability. Second, the number of surgically treated T3 and T4 patients was relatively small, reducing the precision of subgroup estimates and conclusions for advanced disease. Third, clinical tumor size was abstracted from routine radiology reports rather than standardized central remeasurement. Although this approach reflects real-world clinical practice, measurement variability may have arisen from differences in slice selection, measurement plane, partial-volume effects, and identification of the maximal diameter, particularly in irregular or part-solid lesions. Fourth, pathological tumor size was also obtained from routine institutional pathology reports. Harmonized protocols for fixation, inflation status, sectioning, central pathology review, and invasive-component measurement were not available for all cases; therefore, inter-institutional variation in specimen processing may have contributed to clinical–pathological size discrepancies. Fifth, the database lacked uniform information on consolidation-to-tumor ratio, ground-glass versus solid components, volumetric or radiomic features, lepidic-predominant histologic pattern, and invasive-component size. This precluded direct radiologic–pathologic correlation between CT features and histologic growth patterns, which should be addressed in future prospective studies. Sixth, follow-up data were available for 6,279 patients rather than the entire analytic cohort; survival analyses may therefore be affected by selection bias, despite baseline characteristics are comparable between the full analytic cohort and the survival cohort. Finally, the retrospective database did not systematically capture postoperative management details, including completion lobectomy after pathological upstaging, lymph node assessment strategies, management of unexpected nodal metastasis, or adjuvant therapy decisions. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as highlighting staging uncertainty rather than prescribing postoperative management for individual patients.

In conclusion, in resected invasive lung adenocarcinoma, clinical and pathological T-stage assessments should be viewed as complementary rather than interchangeable. CT-based clinical tumor size exceeded pathological tumor size on average, and clinical–pathological agreement varied substantially across T categories. Discrepancies appeared less pronounced at the extremes of the T-stage spectrum, whereas the greatest uncertainty clustered around intermediate thresholds relevant to operative planning. Prognostic discrimination differed modestly between clinical and pathological measurements and appeared to vary by disease stage, underscoring that neither measurement source is uniformly superior across all clinical contexts. These findings support cautious, context-specific interpretation of preoperative T staging and highlight the need for future studies incorporating standardized imaging review, radiologic–pathologic component correlation, volumetric assessment, and prospective decision-impact analysis.
