Case series of patients with early psychosis presenting hypoperfusion in angular gyrus and self‐disturbance: Implication for the sense of agency and schizophrenia
Authors: Akane Yoshikawa, Youhei Obata, Chihiro Kakiuchi, Atsushi Nakanishi, Satoshi Kimura, Shigeki Aoki, Tadafumi Kato
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf‐disturbance has been considered as a core symptomatology of schizophrenia and its emergence from the prodromal phase makes it a crucial target for early detection and intervention in schizophrenia. Currently, the clinical assessment of self‐disturbance relies on the self‐report of patients, and clinicians have no diagnostic tools in clinical practice. Identifying the neural substrate of self‐disturbance would be of great clinical value by shedding light on the core dimension of schizophrenia.Case PresentationWe first introduce an adolescent patient who initially presented self‐disturbance, and clinically detectable hypoperfusion in angular gyrus (AG) was observed when early psychosis was suspected. Interestingly, the hypoperfusion in AG may correspond to improvement and exacerbation of self‐disturbance. This clinical observation led us to pursue the relationship between the decreased blood flow in the AG and self‐disturbance. Among 15 cases with suspected early psychosis in which single photon emission computed tomography was performed to exclude organic factors, we found additional 5 cases, including one prodromal patient, showing hypoperfusion in the AG and self‐disturbance with significant correlation (r = 0.79, p = 0.00025).DiscussionThe self‐disturbance has been interpreted as a reflection of disturbance of the “Sense of Agency”, the ability to attribute their action and/or thoughts to themselves. AG has been shown to play a pivotal role in the sense of agency. These cases suggest that the hypoperfusion in AG associated with the disruption in the sense of agency would be an early clinical sign of schizophrenia. Further longitudinal studies are needed to test this hypothesis.Keywords: angular gyrus, early psychosis, schizophrenia, self‐disturbance, sense of agency

INTRODUCTION

Schizophrenia is a devastating mental disorder with leading cause of years lived with disability of adolescents, a measure of the amount of life lost due to the disease. 1 Early detection is expected to improve its prognosis by minimizing the duration of untreated psychosis. 2 Three decades have been passed since the first criteria of at‐risk mental state (ARMS) were introduced to identify individuals at high risk for developing psychotic disorders. 3 However, the symptoms of ARMS are not specific and are insufficient to be an indicator for starting the drug treatment. 3 Specific diagnostic markers for early detection and intervention for schizophrenia are needed to be established.

Self‐disturbance, which refers to the disruption in the sense of ownership of action and/or thoughts, has been positioned as a core symptom of schizophrenia. 4 Previously, Nelson et al. reported that the self‐disturbance was observed even in the ultra‐high‐risk (UHR) population and its severity was higher in the UHR population who later actually developed schizophrenia than who did not develop schizophrenia, 5 supporting the specificity of the self‐disturbance in schizophrenia symptomatology. Its emergence from the prodromal phase of schizophrenia also accelerates us to search for neural substrate of self‐disturbance using clinical brain imaging as a candidate diagnostic marker for early detection of schizophrenia.

In 2020, four patients with anti‐NMDA receptor antibody encephalitis, of which psychotic symptoms were resembling with that of schizophrenia, were reported. 6 They had clinically normal brain MRI but apparent hypoperfusion in the brain 123I‐IMP SPECT (single‐photon emission computed tomography). After that, the clinical utility of the SPECT in the psychiatric field has been revisited. 7 Since then, in addition to computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging, the brain 123I‐IMP SPECT are also frequently used to exclude organic mental disorders in the cases with suspected psychosis.

In this report, we first introduce an adolescent patient who initially presented self‐disturbance and clinically detectable hypoperfusion in angular gyrus (AG) when early psychosis was suspected with her marked commentary auditory hallucination. The analysis of the SPECT data using the three‐dimensional stereotactic surface projection (3D‐SSP) analysis setting whole brain as the reference and stereotactic extraction estimation (S.E.E.) method 8 further identified that the hypoperfusion in the AG may correspond to improvement and exacerbation of her self‐disturbance. This clinical observation led us to examine whether there was a relationship between the decreased blood flow in the AG and self‐disturbance. Hence, we reviewed 15 cases of suspected early psychosis, in which 123I‐IMP SPECT was performed to exclude organic factors. We found additional 5 cases who showed the hypoperfusion in the AG and all these cases presented self‐disturbance, supporting the involvement of the AG in the symptomatology of the self‐disturbance. Other 10 cases neither showed the hypoperfusion in the AG nor the self‐disturbance.

This report complies with The Guidelines on Privacy Protection in Journal Publication and Conference Presentations Including Case Reports of the Japanese Society of Psychiatry and Neurology. Attention has been paid to protect the privacy of the patients. The SPECT scan was performed after explaining the purpose to the patients and written informed consent for the case report were obtained from all the patients. In addition, search for patients with early psychosis who underwent SPECT using medical chart is deemed as a research, based on “The Guidelines on [The Case Reports Handled as a Research] for which Ethics Approval is Required” of the Japanese Society of Psychiatry and Neurology. The Ethics Committee of Juntendo University Faculty of Medicine approved this study, which granted authorization for an opt‐out option.

CASE PRESENTATION

Patient 1 was an adolescent female with affective flattening and the loss of motivation from her age of 17. At her age of 20, she first came to our psychiatric clinic with antidepressant‐resistant depressed mood and decreased motivation. After 6 months, she claimed that she could not distinguish whether “she” was thinking or “others” were thinking, suggesting the self‐disturbance. After 8 months from her first visit, her self‐disturbance had been gradually changed to the commentary auditory hallucinations such as “You went to the restroom”. After 13 months from her first visit, continuous hallucination was found, and early psychosis was suspected, and brain 123I‐IMP SPECT was performed to exclude the organic factors. Irregular pattern of hypoperfusion in the left AG and the left superior temporal gyrus (STG) was found, which caught our attention. With antipsychotic drug treatment, her hallucination went away and the hypoperfusion in the AG had disappeared along with the amelioration of self‐disturbance, after 23 months from her first visit. Three months later, her self‐disturbance became worse and the hypoperfusion in the AG appeared again, suggesting that the hypoperfusion in the AG may reflect the severity of self‐disturbance (Figure 1). Other than the AG, hypoperfusion in several brain regions including the left inferior occipital gyrus (IOG) and the left middle frontal gyrus (MFG) was also observed (Table S1).

This case prompted us to search for other cases with early psychosis with hypoperfusion in the AG or self‐disturbance who underwent the brain 123I‐IMP SPECT to examine whether there is a relationship between the decreased blood flow in the AG and self‐disturbance. The inclusion criteria were (1) DSM‐5 diagnosis of schizophrenia, first episode or Other Specified Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorder, Attenuated Psychosis Syndrome and (2) underwent brain 123I‐IMP SPECT to exclude organic factors. Presence or absence of the self‐disturbance was assessed by the first author based on the medical chart records. We found 15 cases of suspected early psychosis in which SPECT was performed. Among them, we found additional 5 cases who showed the hypoperfusion in the AG and all these cases presented self‐disturbance, supporting the involvement of AG in the symptomatology of self‐disturbance (Figure 2). Other 10 cases neither showed the hypoperfusion in the AG nor the self‐disturbance.

Next, we describe 5 more cases with self‐disturbance, especially the subjective feelings of thoughts, presenting hypoperfusion in the AG.

Patient 2 was a 20‐year‐old male, and he could not go to school since third grade of high school. He first came to our psychiatric clinic with recurrent depressed mood. He claimed that his thoughts and actions were separated from himself, and said, “My thought has been interfered with somebody else”, suggesting thoughts interference, one of the basic symptoms seen in the prodromal phase of schizophrenia. 6 He also told us that he could not control his own thoughts despite his intention, suggesting the disturbance in subjective feelings or ownership of his own thoughts. His mother told us that his emotional reactions had been limited and felt that he had changed from what he used to be. At the examination room, he looked very sensitive to sounds around him. Considering the possible early psychosis, the SPECT scan was performed and hypoperfusion in the right AG was observed. In addition to the right AG, hypoperfusion in several brain regions including the right inferior temporal gyrus (ITG) and the right IOG was also observed (Table S1).

Patient 3 was a 34‐year‐old female with a history of depression at her age of 31. When she came to our psychiatric clinic, she said, “I cannot think freely. I cannot feel like “I” am feeling. Words come out from my mouth automatically, that is not my intention to do that. Thoughts, which are not mine, came up with one after another.” She complained that she could not feel that her thought was her origin, nor her action was based on her intention, neither. Soon after that she developed auditory hallucinations such as “The keyboard which you typed now is ‘S’.” and “The job which you are doing now doesn't suit you.” and brain 123I‐IMP SPECT showed decreased blood flow in the right AG and the right STG. Regarding the other regions, hypoperfusion in several brain regions including the right middle temporal gyrus, the right ITG, the left IOG, the left fusiform gyrus, and the left lingual gyrus was further observed (Table S1).

Patient 4 was a 17‐year‐old male. In the first grade of his junior high school, troubles with his friends in the club activities led him decrease in motivation and he started doing self‐harm. When he was 14 years old, he first visited our psychiatry department via the pediatrics department, saying, “I can hear words in my head but that are not my own. I do not know whether I am thinking or someone else is thinking in my head.” At the time he acutely developed auditory hallucinations such as “Do you want to kill this person?”, hypoperfusion in the right AG was observed in the brain 123I‐IMP SPECT. In addition to the AG, hypoperfusion in several brain regions including the right superior occipital gyrus was further observed (Table S1).

Patient 5 was a 20‐year‐old female. From her age of 13, she felt that she had lost her thoughts and her own emotions. At her first visit to our psychiatric clinic at the age of 20, she complained that she heard some voices recently but those were not her own. She could not get her thoughts together because she could not understand where the thoughts came from. She could not explain what she felt. Hypoperfusion in the right AG and the left STG was observed in the brain 123I‐IMP SPECT. Other than the AG, hypoperfusion in several brain regions including the left ITG, the left IOG, right IOG, left cuneus, left fusiform gyrus, left lingual gyrus, and right lingual gyrus was also observed (Table S1).

Patient 6 was a 26‐year‐old female. At her age of 25, she felt that her thoughts had not been her own. When she admitted to our psychiatry department at the age of 26, she said “Others are playing games in my head”, “I am forced to do the game in my head”, and “My thoughts are pulled out by someone and I cannot stay my thoughts in my head”, suggesting the self‐disturbance, forced experiences, thought withdrawal, and auditory hallucinations. She also told psychiatrists that she could not understand “who” was thinking in her head. Hypoperfusion in her right AG was observed in the brain 123I‐IMP SPECT. Regarding the other brain regions, hypoperfusion in several brain regions including the right superior parietal lobule was also observed (Table S1).

Cerebral blood flow of AG in the patients with early psychosis with and without self‐disturbance is shown in Table 1. Among the 15 patients, 5 patients with self‐disturbance showed significant hypoperfusion in the AG; however, none of the 10 patients without self‐disturbance showed the hypoperfusion in the AG. Hypoperfusion in the AG was significantly associated with the self‐disturbance in early psychosis patients (p = 0.000333 by Fisher's exact probability test). Detailed psychopathologies of the self‐disturbance and the degrees of hypoperfusion in the AG in each patient were listed in Table 2. Pearson's correlation analysis revealed that there was a significant correlation between the degree of hypoperfusion in the AG and the self‐disturbance assessed by the First Rank Symptom score provided by Tikka et al. 9 (r = 0.79, p = 0.00025, N = 16, Figure 3). There was no significant correlation between the depressive symptom and the hypoperfusion in the AG in this case series (r = −0.16). Regarding other brain regions, a significant correlation was found between the hypoperfusion of the left IOG and the negative symptom scale, especially “Alogia” (r = 0.73, p = 0.01) assessed by the Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS), 10 in 11 patients who were finally diagnosed as schizophrenia. DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first report of clinical observations suggesting the relationship between the self‐disturbance and the clinically detectable hypoperfusion in the AG noticed by the brain 123I‐IMP SPECT. In patient 1, cerebral blood flow may correspond to the improvement of the self‐disturbance. Furthermore, the hypoperfusion in the AG was observed before the occurrence of apparent auditory hallucination, suggesting that the hypoperfusion in the AG may have a possibility to reflect the pathophysiology of schizophrenia, especially the self‐disturbance, from very early stage. A significant correlation between the severity of the self‐disturbance and the extent of hypoperfusion in the AG may also support the possibility of the hypoperfusion as a state marker of the self‐disturbance.

Anatomically, the AG, corresponds to Brodmann's area 39, is located in the posterior apex of the inferior parietal lobe and is a continuous brain region with the STG 12 where the hypoperfusion was also observed in several patients of this clinical case series. Functionally, the AG has been shown to integrate the multimodal sensory information including the auditory information from the STG. 12 As a critical locus for high‐level integrative cognitive operations, the AG has been known to be one of the last brain regions to mature in humans and it continues maturing into young adulthood, 13 when schizophrenia often emerges. Marked enlargement of the AG in human brain than in non‐human primates also suggests its human‐specific higher cognitive function of the AG. 14

Self‐disturbance, a core symptom of schizophrenia, is interpreted as a context of “Sense of Agency (SoA)”, an ability to recognize their thoughts and actions as their own and the subjective feelings to control their own thoughts and behaviors. 15 Previously, it has been shown that SoA is impaired in patients with schizophrenia. 16 , 17 , 18 In six cases presented here, the SoA is considered to be disrupted especially in the light of thoughts rather than action. Self‐disturbance has been paid particular attention because of its emergence before the onset of schizophrenia 5 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 like the patients 1 and 2 in this report.

Previous neuroimaging studies revealed that the AG is one of the critical brain regions involved in the SoA. 15 , 18 , 23 , 24 , 25 In this report, five patients showed the hypoperfusion in the right AG, but one patient showed it in the left AG. According to the previous studies, the right AG was shown to be associated with self‐guided movement and the discrimination of endogenous self‐produced signals from exogenous attention. 26 , 27 Based on that notion, the right AG may play a role in the occurrence of the forced experience and the thought interference seen in the patients, even in the prodromal stage of schizophrenia. Regarding the left AG, where the hypoperfusion was observed in the patient 1, its causal involvement in perceptual experience of agency using transcranial direct current stimulation was also reported. 24

There were several studies to support the involvement of the AG in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia to date. For example, it has been reported that gray matter volumes including AG and supramarginal gyrus were reduced in patients with schizophrenia compared with healthy controls. 28 Niznikiewicz M et al. found the abnormal AG asymmetry in the chronic patients with schizophrenia. 29 Further, in patients with first‐episode schizophrenia, it has been revealed that gray matter volume of left AG was 14.8% smaller than that of the normal subjects. 30 Nierenberg J et al. 30 added further evidence that the asymmetry of the AG volume in patients with schizophrenia which exists even in the new‐onset schizophrenia. Together with our clinical observation, this AG pathology in first‐episode patients may encourage the possibility to help for prediction of psychosis with the AG. Hypoperfusion in the AG in this case series might reflect the early sign of brain atrophy. In another study, the connectivity between the right or left AG and STG measured by magnetoencephalography, responsible for the auditory sensory gating, were found to be impaired in patients with schizophrenia. 31 This is consistent with our observation that both AG and STG were disrupted in some patients (cases 3 and 5). Although decreased blood flow in the AG and supramarginal gyrus has been reported to be associated with flattening of emotion and decreased psychomotor activity, 32 disruption of SoA may also underlie those symptoms, considering the recent findings of the critical role of the AG in SoA. 15 , 17 , 23 , 24 , 25 The result from a genome‐wide association study further supports the critical involvement of the AG in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia, showing that a group of genes risk for schizophrenia were significantly enriched in the AG. 33

Although the hypoperfusion of IOG was not corresponding to the improvement and exacerbation of self‐disturbance, it was another shared picture of these early psychotic patients. Decreased cerebral blood flow in the occipital lobe in the early stages of schizophrenia is consistent with previous studies, 34 so it would be worthwhile to determine the specific psychopathology underlying the hypoperfusion observed in the IOG. In this context, we explored the relationship between hypoperfusion in the IOG and the severity of negative symptoms observed in the early stages of psychosis. A nominally significant correlation was found between hypoperfusion in the left IOG and negative symptom, especially “Alogia”. The hypoperfusion in the occipital lobe might also be helpful to discriminate the early sign of negative symptom from adolescent onset depression.

Regarding the other brain regions than the AG and the IOG, hypoperfusion in the lateral frontal and the lower temporal brain regions, such as MFG and ITG, was also observed in some patients. Regarding the lateral frontal brain region, there were four patients with schizophrenia (cases 1, 6, 11, and 12), including one treatment resistant schizophrenia, who showed hypoperfusion in the MFG, which is corresponding to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), 35 an important brain region for cognitive function. 36 Interestingly, Stoyanov et al. 37 demonstrated that uneffective connectivity of the MFG to the anterior insula was specifically observed in schizophrenia group and this might be helpful to discriminate schizophrenia from depression. They also found the aberrant connectivity from the left Precuneus to the right AG in schizophrenia, 37 supporting our notion that the right AG has a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia. Regarding the left ITG, cases 3, 4, 5, 10, and 12 of patients with schizophrenia showed hypoperfusion in the SPECT scan. Human neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that the ITG has an important role in visual perception. 38 Based on the structural MRI study, it has been shown that bilateral volume of the ITG was reduced among patients with chronic schizophrenia and deficits in ITG could lead to dysfunctions in complex visual perception. 39 It is possible that “hypoperfusion” of these areas, which did not show improvement associated with treatment, indeed reflected the reduced volume of these areas.

Originally, the Z‐graph in the brain 123I‐IMP SPECT was developed as an adjunctive diagnostic tool to distinguish Alzheimer's disease from dementia with Lewy bodies. 40 To detect the decreased cerebral blood flow in the AG in clinical practice easily, we developed a Z‐graph with regions of interest (ROI) customized for schizophrenia. AG, supramarginal gyrus, STG, IOG, and thalamus were included as ROIs based on the clinical observations including this study. There are several limitations to be aware of in this study. The database with older subjects aged 40s–80s was clinically available as a comparison group. Currently, due to the medical problem of radiation exposure, it is challenging to obtain images for SPECT scan of the database from young healthy subjects.

In conclusion, this case series is the first to describe that the patients with early psychosis accompanying with the self‐disturbance, especially, the subjective feeling of thoughts, presented the hypoperfusion in the AG, the relevant brain region for the “Sense of Agency”, even in a prodromal phase. Further study is warranted to pursue the possibility of the hypoperfusion of the AG as a state marker of impairment in the sense of agency and as a clinical practical tool to support the early diagnosis in schizophrenia.
