Diagnostic Accuracy of Size‐Based Preoperative CT Assessment for Predicting Lymph Node Metastasis in Colon Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Yuji Takayama, Tomoya Shimizu, Jun Watanabe, Shimpei Ogawa, Yukihide Kanemitsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAccurate identification of preoperative lymph node metastasis is essential for planning colon cancer treatment. Computed tomography (CT) is widely used for staging, but its diagnostic performance based on size criteria alone remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of preoperative CT for detecting lymph node metastasis in colon cancer.MethodsA systematic search of MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials was conducted on June 12, 2025. Studies comparing preoperative CT with pathological evaluation and providing 2 × 2 contingency tables were included. Pooled sensitivity and specificity were calculated using a hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic model and a bivariate random‐effects model. Subgroup analyses were performed according to cancer location (colon only vs. colon plus rectal cancer) and diagnostic criteria (size alone vs. size plus morphology). Study quality was assessed using QUADAS‐2.ResultsTwenty‐nine studies involving 5634 patients were included. The pooled sensitivity and specificity of CT for detecting lymph node metastasis were 0.693 (95% CI: 0.636–0.744) and 0.660 (95% CI: 0.581–0.731), respectively, with an area under the curve of 0.727. Meta‐regression showed no statistically significant differences in diagnostic performance between colon‐only studies and those including rectal cancer (p = 0.561 and 0.316), or between size‐only and morphologic criteria (p = 0.822 and 0.536).ConclusionsCT using size criteria alone for preoperative lymph node staging in colon cancer demonstrated only moderate diagnostic performance. These findings indicate that relying on lymph node size as the primary determinant may be insufficient for reliable clinical decision‐making.Keywords: colon neoplasms, lymphatic metastasis, meta‐analysis, systematic review, tomography, X‐ray computed

Introduction

Accurate preoperative diagnosis of lymph node metastasis (LNM) in colon cancer (CC) is a critical issue in determining appropriate treatment strategies. Numerous studies have reported that LNM is a risk factor for recurrence and is associated with poor prognosis [1, 2, 3, 4]. Furthermore, lymph node status is a key component of preoperative staging, influencing decisions regarding the extent of lymph node dissection [5] and the use of neoadjuvant treatment [6, 7]. Therefore, precise evaluation of LNM is of paramount importance in planning therapeutic strategies.

In rectal cancer, the role of preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been well established and incorporated into clinical guidelines [5, 8, 9]. In contrast, the clinical role of preoperative LNM assessment in CC is more nuanced. While colon resection and lymph node dissection are routinely performed, the recommended extent of lymph node dissection may vary depending on the preoperative staging classification. Current clinical guidelines [5] generally support D3 lymph node dissection when preoperative computed tomography (CT) suggests LNM. Conversely, D2 dissection is considered for patients without suspected LNM, particularly in early stages. Thus, preoperative LNM assessment can influence not only oncological risk stratification but also the planned extent of lymph node dissection. In a meta‐analysis by Nerad et al. [10], the sensitivity and specificity of CT for detecting LNM were reported to be 71% and 67%, respectively; however, these data were derived from studies published before 2015.

By analyzing more recent evidence, it may be possible to better assess the advantages and limitations of CT in the staging of CC and to provide updated reference values useful for both daily clinical practice and research. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of preoperative CT in detecting LNM, with a particular focus on size‐based diagnostic criteria.

Methods

Protocol and Registration

This systematic review and meta‐analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses of Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies (PRISMA‐DTA) guidelines [11]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD420251177701).

Eligibility Criteria

Studies examining LNM on preoperative CT and pathological findings, where preoperative CT included size measurements, were included in this review. Cases undergoing preoperative treatment, case studies, reviews, studies not written in English, and CT scans performed under nonstandard or non‐clinical conditions were excluded. Additional articles were identified by cross‐checking the references of the retrieved studies. Only studies in which the results were presented in a 2 × 2 contingency table, or from which an equivalent table could be extracted, were included. In studies that included both colon and rectal cancers, only the data on CC were extracted if they could be separated from those on rectal cancer. If separation was not possible, the item “Colon, Rectum” was entered in the “Location” column in (Table 1).

Literature Search

The literature search was conducted on June 12, 2025 in the following databases: MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL; Cochrane Library). The search terms used included “colon cancer” or “colorectal cancer”, “lymph node metastasis”, “computed tomography” and related terms (supplementary).

Study Selection and Data Collection

Two authors (YT, TS) independently screened the titles and abstracts of studies identified through the literature search. Duplicates were excluded by checking study titles and author names. Full texts of studies potentially meeting inclusion criteria were obtained for full‐text review. Reference lists of included studies were screened to identify additional relevant studies. Disagreements between reviewers regarding study assessment were resolved through discussion and the judgment of a third author (JW). All data extraction was performed by the same author and verified for accuracy.

Quality Assessment

The QUADAS‐2 tool [12] was used to assess the quality of included studies. Study quality was evaluated based on two categories: “risk of bias” and “applicability of concerns”. The risk of bias category comprised four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. The concerns about applicability category comprised three domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard. Risk within each domain was assessed as “high”, “low”, or “unclear”. Modification to the QUADAS‐2 signaling questions was made to enhance methodological consistency with the review objectives. These adaptations did not alter the domain structure or risk‐of‐bias judgment process.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity in diagnostic performance, subgroup analyses were conducted according to two clinically relevant factors. These included the anatomical focus of the study population (CC alone versus colorectal cancer (CRC) including rectal involvement) and the type of CT‐based assessment criteria (size‐only versus size combined with morphological evaluation). For each subgroup, bivariate random‐effects models and hierarchical summary ROC (HS‐ROC) curves were generated, and comparative performance was evaluated through meta‐regression. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the main findings. These included restricting analyses to studies using ≥ 64‐slice multidetector CT (MDCT) and, separately, to studies in which CT interpretation was performed by radiologists.

Statistical Analysis

Estimates of sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) were extracted from each study and presented in forest plots. The HS‐ROC model was used to construct a summary ROC curve and to calculate pooled estimates of sensitivity, specificity, and DOR [13]. This model accounts for heterogeneity among studies and is considered a random‐effects model. A bivariate random‐effects meta‐analysis (Reitsma model [14]) was applied to simultaneously pool sensitivity and specificity while accounting for their intrinsic correlation and between‐study variability. Furthermore, for subgroup analysis, a bivariate meta‐regression model with tumor site as a covariate was constructed to investigate whether diagnostic performance differed between the two groups. Differences in sensitivity and specificity were evaluated based on the regression coefficients, and statistical significance was assessed using Wald tests. When the estimated relative effect was imprecise, interpretation was based on the confidence interval of the regression coefficient and its directionality rather than the point estimate. Publication bias was assessed using Deeks' funnel plot asymmetry test [15]. Between‐study heterogeneity was evaluated using I2 statistics, τ [2], τ, and H indices. A p‐value of less than 0.10 for the slope coefficient was considered to indicate statistically significant asymmetry.

All analyses were performed using the mada package (version 0.5.12) within R software (version 4.5.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

The DOR was 4.607 (95% CI: 3.545–5.987), while the positive and negative likelihood ratios were 2.018 (95% CI: 1.746–2.332) and 0.505 (95% CI: 0.449–0.568), respectively, further supporting the modest discriminative ability of CT in identifying LNM.

Comparison of Diagnostic Accuracy Between Studies Focusing Solely on Colon Cancer and Those Including Rectal Cancer

To visualize potential differences in diagnostic performance, we created HS‐ROC curves for studies evaluating only CC (Figure 4a) and for studies analyzing CRC including rectal lesions (excluding studies focused solely on CC) (Figure 4b). In this review, ten studies assessed colon and rectal cancers together, whereas nineteen studies focused exclusively on CC.

HS‐ROC curves comparing diagnostic accuracy between studies focusing exclusively on CC (a) and independent studies evaluating CRC, excluding CC‐only studies (b).

The shapes and positions of the HS‐ROC curves demonstrated substantial overlap between the two groups, indicating similar diagnostic behavior. Consistently, bivariate meta‐regression showed no significant difference in sensitivity between CRC and CC (p = 0.561). The relative sensitivity ratio (CRC vs. CC) could not be precisely estimated because the regression coefficient was close to zero; however, the 95% confidence interval (0.49–1.48) encompassed unity and suggested no meaningful difference in sensitivity.

Likewise, no significant difference in specificity was detected (p = 0.316), with a wide but uninformative confidence interval (0.34–1.42). Taken together with the visual similarity of the HS‐ROC curves, these findings indicate that the inclusion of rectal cancer did not materially alter the diagnostic accuracy of CT for LNM.

Comparison of Diagnostic Accuracy Between Studies Focusing Solely on Lymph Node Size and Those Incorporating Morphological Evaluation

To examine whether incorporating morphological evaluation improved diagnostic performance, HS‐ROC curves were generated separately for studies using size criteria alone (Figure 5a) and for studies incorporating morphologic evaluation in addition to size, excluding size‐only studies (Figure 5b). In this review, 18 studies evaluated lymph node size as the sole criterion, while 11 studies included additional morphological evaluation.

HS‐ROC curves comparing diagnostic accuracy between studies using size‐based CT criteria alone (a) and studies incorporating morphologic assessment in addition to size, excluding size‐only studies (b).

Visual inspection of the HS‐ROC curves revealed considerable overlap between the two groups, suggesting comparable diagnostic behavior. Consistently, bivariate meta‐regression demonstrated no significant difference in sensitivity between size‐based and morphologic CT criteria (p = 0.822), with the relative sensitivity ratio showing a wide but non‐significant range (95% CI: 0.62–1.82).

Similarly, no significant difference in specificity was observed (p = 0.536), and the corresponding relative specificity ratio also crossed unity (95% CI: 0.62–2.54). These findings indicate that the addition of morphological evaluation to size‐based CT criteria did not materially enhance diagnostic accuracy for detecting LNM.

Sensitivity Analyses

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses. First, the analysis was restricted to studies using ≥ 64‐slice MDCT. The pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.671 (95% CI, 0.589–0.744) and 0.665 (95% CI, 0.545–0.767), respectively, which were comparable to the results of the main analysis (0.693 [95% CI, 0.636–0.744] and 0.660 [95% CI, 0.581–0.731], respectively), indicating that the diagnostic performance was not materially influenced by CT detector configuration.

Second, we included only studies in which image interpretation was performed by radiologists. This restriction yielded a modestly higher pooled sensitivity of 0.710 (95% CI, 0.651–0.762) but a lower pooled specificity of 0.605 (95% CI, 0.539–0.668) compared with the main analysis, suggesting that radiologist interpretation may enhance the detection of metastatic nodes while increasing the likelihood of false‐positive assessments. In exploratory descriptive analyses stratified by measurement axis and size cutoff, sensitivity and specificity showed substantial variability across thresholds. No single cutoff value consistently demonstrated a superior balance between sensitivity and specificity (Table S1).

Discussion

Preoperative CT is widely used as the primary imaging modality for CC and provides essential anatomical information for surgical planning and clinical decision‐making. This systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrated that CT has only moderate diagnostic accuracy for detecting LNM, with pooled sensitivity and specificity of 0.693 and 0.660, respectively. The HS‐ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.727, and the corresponding likelihood ratios and diagnostic odds ratio similarly indicated limited discriminative capability.

Importantly, technological advances have not led to substantial improvements in lymph node assessment. A previous systematic review by Nerad et al. in 201610 reported pooled sensitivity of 71% (95% CI, 59%–81%) and specificity of 67% (95% CI, 46%–83%), with a diagnostic odds ratio of 4.8 (95% CI, 2.5–9.4). Despite including studies published only up to 2014, these estimates are comparable to, or slightly higher than those observed in our analysis. Thus, even with the widespread adoption of 64‐slice or higher MDCT and notable advances such as CT colonography for tumor depth assessment [45, 46], accurate preoperative detection of LNM remains challenging.

Subgroup analyses confirmed that diagnostic limitations were not attributable to specific imaging conditions. Exclusion of studies involving rectal cancer, studies incorporating morphological evaluation, or restriction to 64‐slice or higher MDCT did not improve diagnostic performance. Restricting image interpretation to radiologists slightly improved sensitivity, but the difference was small. Size criteria were not uniformly defined across studies, indicating that achieving accurate diagnosis based solely on size is inherently difficult. Taken together, these findings suggest that despite advances in CT hardware and image reconstruction techniques, the diagnostic performance of CT for detecting LNM in CC appears to have largely plateaued.

Descriptive comparison across size thresholds demonstrated a clear trade‐off between sensitivity and specificity rather than a consistently superior cutoff value. Within the same measurement axis, ranges of sensitivity and specificity overlapped across thresholds; higher cutoffs (e.g., short‐axis ≥ 10 mm) were associated with higher specificity but markedly reduced sensitivity, whereas lower cutoffs (e.g., ≥ 5 mm) increased sensitivity at the expense of specificity. Consequently, the available data do not identify a single size threshold that provides a clearly optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity.

Therefore, alternative imaging strategies to improve accuracy are needed. Node‐RADS [47] was developed to integrate texture, margin, and shape in addition to size, but its current clinical application is limited due to significant interobserver variability reported in colorectal cancer [44]. PET/CT also holds potential for improving diagnostic accuracy. For example, Yukimoto et al. [40] reported a sensitivity of 78.6% and specificity of 96.8% when using a lymph node SUVmax cutoff value of 1.5. However, practical issues such as radiation exposure and cost limit its widespread use in routine staging.

These diagnostic challenges significantly influence treatment decisions. Even if colectomy itself is routinely performed, inaccurate preoperative nodal staging may influence the planned extent of lymph node dissection, perioperative treatment strategies, patient selection for neoadjuvant therapy, and interpretation of oncological risk. In Japan, when regional LNM is clinically suspected, Japanese guidelines [5] recommend D3 lymph node dissection, with preoperative CT findings playing a major role in determining surgical strategy. Furthermore, lymph node staging may influence decisions regarding preoperative chemotherapy. A report by Mizuuchi et al. [48] demonstrated that N3 LNM is an independent poor prognostic factor in patients with Stage III CC. While the FOxTROT [49] and OPTICAL [7] trials used only the T factor for preoperative chemotherapy eligibility, the JCOG2006 [50] trial included cT3–T4a and positive LNM (cN2 or cN3) in its eligibility criteria. Whether the cN factor is useful as an indicator for initiating preoperative chemotherapy requires further investigation.

Taken together, these findings highlight the need for an international standardized approach to LNM assessment. Limited studies have evaluated MRI for assessing LNM in CC. According to Bompou et al. [51], MRI showed a sensitivity of 100% for two interpreters, with specificities of 90.2% and 95.2%, respectively, and significantly higher AUC values than CT (CT: 0.763 and 0.751; MRI: 0.951 and 0.976; p < 0.001). On the other hand, Rafaelsen et al. [52] reported MRI sensitivity of 58.2% and specificity of 50.0%, showing no significant difference from CT. Therefore, further investigation is needed regarding the utility of MRI. Technologies such as radiomics and artificial intelligence show potential to overcome the constraints of size‐based diagnosis. In a radiomics study by Eresen [37] et al., a model derived from radiological image data demonstrated an accuracy of 81.09% and 79.49% in the training and test cohorts, respectively, with sensitivities of 83.87% and 74.36%, and specificities of 78.34% and 84.62%. In the rapidly advancing field of AI, automated CT‐based models achieved an AUC of 0.92 for predicting LNM in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, significantly surpassing the AUC of 0.65 achieved by radiologists [53]. Similarly, in bladder cancer, AI systems trained on whole‐slide pathology images demonstrated significantly higher diagnostic sensitivity than junior pathologists (0.983 vs. 0.906) and senior pathologists (0.986 vs. 0.947), while also reducing the rate of missed micrometastases [54]. These advances suggest that AI‐assisted image interpretation may pave the way for improved and standardized lymph node assessment in the future.

This review has several limitations. The included studies differed in lymph node measurement techniques, including the anatomical site of assessment, the choice of short‐ versus long‐axis diameter, and the cutoff values used to define metastatic involvement. Specifically, 9 studies used the short axis as the maximum diameter, while 20 studies used the long axis. The most common maximum diameter was 10 mm, used in 15 studies, followed by 5 mm in 9 studies and 7 mm in 3 studies. The number of such studies further decreased that measured size alone without morphological evaluation, making it difficult to compare which size yielded the highest AUC. This variability prevented standardized comparison of diagnostic performance across specific size thresholds. Furthermore, studies evaluating only morphological features without reporting size were excluded, limiting the quantitative comparison between size‐based CT criteria and morphology‐based CT criteria. However, recent studies that do not include size measurements are limited. Since the recent studies by Rollvén et al. [30] targeted Stage III and Li et al. [55] targeted T1, there may be no substantial difference in results.

In conclusion, CT using size criteria alone for preoperative lymph node staging in colon cancer demonstrated only moderate accuracy even in the era of 64‐slice or higher MDCT. These findings indicate that relying on lymph node size as the primary determinant remains insufficient for reliable clinical decision‐making. Standardized evaluation methods and advanced imaging strategies may be necessary to improve diagnostic performance.
