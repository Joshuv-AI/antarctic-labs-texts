End-to-end deep learning for smart maritime threat detection: an AE–CNN–LSTM-based approach
Authors: R. Anuja, J. Annrose
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Smart maritime operations face growing cyber risks due to the proliferation of IoT-enabled sensors, navigation units, and communication links. To improve detection fidelity under these conditions, we present a hybrid Autoencoder–Convolutional Neural Network–Long Short-Term Memory (AE–CNN–LSTM) based framework that unifies unsupervised reconstruction signals with spatio-temporal feature learning for intrusion detection in marine cyber-physical networks. The model is trained and evaluated on a KDDCup99-based benchmark adapted to simulated maritime scenarios and supports both binary and multiclass classification. In the binary setting, the system attains 99.8% accuracy; in the multiclass setting it demonstrates consistently strong performance across precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC, with minority-class behavior analyzed via confusion matrices and threshold sensitivity. Reconstruction errors (MAE/MSE) provide an auxiliary anomaly cue that aids triage. In this study the results are compared with representative deep-learning and transformer baselines, the proposed model yields competitive to superior results while remaining suitable for real-time deployment in smart ports, autonomous vessels, and underwater sensor networks. We also discuss practical constraints—such as dataset realism and class imbalance-to contextualize applicability in operational environments.

Introduction

Over the past decade, the maritime sector has increasingly relied on a diverse array of networked sensors and devices, such as AIS, ECDIS, radar, sonar buoys, engine-monitoring equipment, and more, all designed to ensure safe and efficient vessel operations1,2. However, this proliferation of Maritime IoT has outpaced the deployment of robust security controls, exposing vessels and offshore platforms to an expanding array of cyber-attack vectors that can disrupt navigation or compromise critical systems2. The rise of the Internet of Things (IoT) has created a parallel ecosystem has witnessed frequent exploitations of protocol-agnostic vulnerabilities, including denial-of-service floods, firmware tampering, credential brute-forcing, and abuse of lightweight protocols such as MQTT and CoAP1,3. Resource constraints and minimal default security configurations on IoT endpoints facilitate lateral movement and data exfiltration, challenges that have been well documented in general-purpose settings3.

Maritime environments introduce unique operational constraints-intermittent satellite connectivity, GPS signal attenuation, and dynamic sea-state noise-that exacerbate these risks4,5. Vessel behavior and sensor readings vary with environmental conditions, rendering stationary thresholds ineffective. A recent study utilized deep learning and graph neural networks to detect maritime streams’ anomalies, yet these methods often overlook physical-layer perturbations such as Doppler shifts in sonar echoes or AIS signal jitter6,7. Early attempts at maritime intrusion detection relied on rule-based fusion of AIS-trajectory anomalies and expert heuristics, providing interpretability but suffering from high false-alarm rates under fluctuating sea traffic and environmental noise2,8. Such systems underscored the need for adaptive models capable of distinguishing benign operational variance from true malicious behavior.

Existing marine cyber-attacks-ranging from GPS jamming and AIS spoofing to sonar port scans and unauthorized firmware updates-exploit the protocol‐agnostic vulnerabilities of networked shipboard sensors2,8. Researchers have developed methods to detect network traffic intrusions by implementing deep learning models and machine learning classifiers. These include CNNs for detecting spatial anomalies and LSTM networks for learning temporal sequences on traffic logs and AIS7,9. However, these approaches predominantly rely on legacy benchmarks (e.g., KDDCup99, generic IoT datasets) that omit critical marine‐specific features-such as GPS signal jitter, Doppler‐shifted sonar echoes, and vessel dynamic signatures-resulting in elevated false‐alarm rates when deployed in dynamic sea environments10,11. Metaheuristic‐driven feature selection and federated learning frameworks have partially addressed model optimization and data privacy, but they struggle with the non‐IID distributions and intermittent connectivity inherent to maritime deployments12,13. Moreover, single‐model architectures lack the capacity to jointly capture spatial correlations and temporal dependencies across heterogeneous sensor streams, while offering limited interpretability for operators14. These shortcomings motivate our proposed hybrid AE + CNN + LSTM framework, which uses unsupervised pretraining to learn baseline behavior, CNN layers to extract spatial anomalies, and LSTM units to model temporal patterns-augmented by domain‐specific metrics to deliver robust, a sophisticated intrusion detection system that can be tailored to suit the complex needs of the users, noisy conditions of Marine IoT networks.

The use of CNNs and LSTM networks for detecting complex patterns in network logs has led to improved detection of attacks9,15. Yet, standalone CNN or LSTM models frequently underperform when deprived of domain-specific feature enrichment9. Due to the emergence of hybrid and federated learning frameworks, the privacy and data heterogeneity concerns have been addressed. These frameworks allow the training of models across distributed nodes without the need for centralized data pooling12,16, while ensemble strategies and edge-based intelligence offer resilience against concept drift13.

Metaheuristic-driven feature selection and data-balancing techniques further optimize a security system for the Internet of Things (IoT) that can detect intrusions is needed for devices with limited resources. Hybrid algorithms combining genetic or predator–prey optimizers with statistical filters yield lightweight yet accurate models, though their application to marine contexts remains limited11,14,17. Despite these advances, a critical gap persists: most benchmarks (e.g., KDDCup99, generic IoT datasets) lack maritime-specific attack signatures, environmental noise profiles, and sensor-level anomalies10,11. Due to the nature of the systems used for monitoring the Internet of Things (IoT), models that are trained on generic data can’t easily adapt to real-world deployments18.

To bridge this divide, we propose a hybrid AE + CNN + LSTM framework—pre-trained on relabeled multiclass anomalies derived from KDDCup99 and augmented with maritime-specific metrics such as GPS jitter, sonar echo irregularities, and vessel dynamic signatures9,14. This framework combines the capabilities of CNNs, generative reconstruction, and LSTMs for extracting spatial features and performing temporal sequence learning. In summary, our work addresses the twin challenges of data scarcity and domain specificity in Marine IoT security by crafting synthetic maritime attack scenarios from established multiclass benchmarks, enriching feature spaces with physical-layer indicators, and integrating a zero-trust policy enforcement layer to enhance operational resilience in remote deployments5,14. The use of a hybrid technique for maritime detection promises to provide low false alarms and high accuracy.

Although intrusion detection systems for IoT and maritime domains have advanced considerably, several shortcomings remain. Many existing deep learning models, especially those based on CNNs or RNNs alone, show reduced adaptability when deployed in diverse and constantly changing network environments. This limits their ability to maintain performance across different operational contexts. Widely used datasets often lack critical maritime-specific attributes such as GPS drift, sonar signal variations, and vessel motion patterns, restricting the relevance of the trained models to real-world maritime operations. Furthermore, current approaches are often ill equipped to handle advanced threats, including stealthy multi-stage intrusions and adversarially manipulated traffic, which can bypass static or rule-based thresholds. Another notable gap is the absence of unified architectures that merge unsupervised anomaly detection Various techniques can be utilized to capture both temporal and spatial patterns in diverse sensor data. Bridging these gaps is essential for developing robust, adaptive, and explainable detection systems that can operate reliably in the complex, resource-limited settings of Marine IoT infrastructures.

The main novelty of this study is the design and evaluation a hybrid deep learning framework that can be used to address various tasks and applications. AE, CNN, and LSTM components to address intrusion detection challenges in maritime cyber-physical systems. Unlike existing models that focus either on spatial or temporal patterns in isolation, the proposed architecture employs an Auto encoder to perform unsupervised feature compression, enabling noise reduction and dimensionality handling prior to supervised learning. The integration of LSTM and CNN for extracting spatial features and detecting temporal dependencies has enhanced the detection capabilities, particularly for slow-evolving attacks such as stealth port scans or unauthorized access attempts in marine networks. Furthermore, the model supports both binary and multiclass intrusion detection, validated through rigorous experimentation on the recontextualized KDDCup99 dataset tailored to simulate marine threats. This end-to-end framework also incorporates reconstruction error-based anomaly detection, making it capable of identifying unknown threats-a critical capability for securing smart port infrastructure and autonomous maritime platforms. This is the first framework that combines the capabilities of deep learning, anomaly detection, and sequential modeling in a single pipeline. It is designed to provide a comprehensive view of marine intrusions.

For example, Nandanwar and Katarya19 proposed a deep learning-enabled IDS for industrial IoT, while Nandanwar and Katarya20 developed a TL-BiLSTM transfer learning framework for IoT intrusion prediction. Blockchain-enabled IoT security solutions have also been explored, Some of the methods being used to protect the privacy of data collected by the IoT include the sharing of information within healthcare facilities21 and secure IDS designs integrating GAO-XGBoost with elliptic curve cryptography22. In a study conducted by Katarya and Nandanwar, they explored the use of federated learning and hybrid blockchain technology for securing Internet of Things networks, and Kumar et al.23 addressed scalability and security challenges in blockchain-enabled IoMT systems. These studies provide important context for our AE–CNN–LSTM framework, which complements such approaches by focusing on maritime IoT intrusion detection with domain-specific feature augmentation.

Due to the increasing number of IoT devices and the complexity of their networks, it has become more important that the systems are resilient. Rezaei et al.25 proposed a federated RNN-based IDS capable of countering adversarial label-flipping attacks through a selective non-poisoned clustering aggregation strategy, achieving significant accuracy recovery under attack scenarios. Similarly, Belarbi et al.26 evaluated federated deep learning on the realistic TON-IoT dataset and demonstrated that initializing models with pre-trained global weights improved F1-scores by over 20% compared to random initialization. On the architectural side, Jablaoui and Liouane27 presented a hybrid CNN–RNN framework for IoT intrusion detection, combining spatial and sequential learning to reduce false alarms and improve classification performance across diverse attack types. Together, these studies underscore the potential of integrating federated strategies and hybrid spatio-temporal architectures, which motivates our AE–CNN–LSTM approach for maritime intrusion detection.

Despite the progress that has been made in developing hybrid deep learning and federated techniques for detecting IoT intrusions, there are still many issues that need to be resolved. First, many models are evaluated exclusively on outdated or domain-generic datasets such as KDDCup99 or NSL-KDD, which omit maritime-specific indicators like GPS signal drift, sonar echo anomalies, and vessel motion profiles. This limits their transferability to operational maritime environments. Second, standalone CNN or RNN models often capture either spatial or temporal patterns in isolation, leading to reduced detection performance when facing evolving or multi-stage attacks. Third, although federated learning addresses data privacy concerns, most implementations do not account for highly non-IID data distributions or intermittent connectivity common in Marine IoT deployments. Furthermore, explainability is frequently overlooked, with few systems providing interpretable outputs for operators in high-stakes contexts. These limitations highlight a clear need for an integrated framework that combines unsupervised anomaly detection, spatial–temporal deep learning, and domain-specific feature enrichment, while maintaining robustness and interpretability in dynamic, resource-constrained maritime networks. The proposed AE–CNN–LSTM architecture directly addresses these gaps, aiming to deliver a more adaptive, explainable, and operationally viable intrusion detection system for Marine IoT ecosystems. Proposed methodology

Marine Internet-of-Things (IoT) deployments-encompassing AIS transponders, ECDIS units, sonar buoys, and engine-monitoring sensors-operate under intermittent satellite connectivity and highly variable sea-state conditions, yet their security remains anchored to outdated benchmarks and generic feature sets. Traditional datasets fail to represent maritime-specific threats such as GPS jamming and sonar spoofing, and mono-modal CNN or LSTM detectors cannot concurrently exploit the spatial correlations and temporal dynamics present in multi-sensor streams, yielding elevated false-alarm rates and limited deployability at sea. To overcome these deficiencies, our methodology unfolds in four successive stages. First, we perform domain-relabeled pretraining by aligning KDDCup99’s legacy attack categories with maritime analogues and using this relabeled corpus to train an autoencoder that captures a robust reconstruction baseline for benign behavior. Second, we augment the feature space with physical-layer metrics-GPS signal jitter, sonar echo signal-to-noise ratios, and vessel kinematic signatures-integrated alongside conventional network flow statistics. Third, we fuse these enriched representations within a hybrid CNN–AE–LSTM architecture, wherein convolutional layers distill spatial anomalies from the autoencoder’s residual scores and stacked LSTM units model the temporal evolution of attack sequences. Finally, we incorporate a zero-trust enforcement layer for autonomous isolation of compromised nodes and autonomously isolates compromised nodes. Focusing exclusively on the KDDCup99 dataset, The framework is applied in both multiclass and binary detection paradigms, and it establishes rigorous baselines for both attack and intrusion classification. These baselines are tailored to the unique features of the Marine IoT environment.

Figure 1 illustrates the end-to-end intrusion detection framework we have developed for Marine IoT environments. The pipeline is organized into four sequential stages-Input, Preprocessing, Feature Extraction, and Classification-each of which performs a distinct role in transforming raw network data into reliable attack predictions under both binary and multiclass settings. The process begins with the standard KDDCup99 dataset, whose original attack labels are semantically mapped to maritime analogues (e.g. DoS → GPS jamming, Probe → sonar scans). By relabeling these legacy classes to reflect Marine IoT threat scenarios, we establish a foundation of “ground truth” that more accurately mirrors the types of anomalies encountered at sea. In this stage of Preprocessing categorical features are first encoded (e.g. protocol types, service names) and numerical fields are Z-score standardized to eliminate scale disparities. A domain-relabeled pretraining step then uses the transformed data to train an auto encoder on benign traffic, establishing a robust reconstruction baseline. Finally, data augmentation techniques are applied-incorporating physical-layer metrics such as GPS jitter, sonar signal-to-noise ratios, and vessel kinematic signatures alongside traditional flow statistics-to enrich the feature space with maritime-relevant information. For feature extraction the preprocessed and augmented data are fed through the auto encoder to filter out noise and compress salient patterns. Its latent representations are subsequently passed into a one-dimensional CNN, which learns localized spatial features corresponding to packet- and connection-level behaviors. This combination of unsupervised compression and convolutional filtering produces a set of high-quality embedding’s that capture both normal and anomalous spatial correlations in the traffic. Finally the classification. To model temporal dependencies and behavioral transitions, the CNN output sequence is then processed by a stacked LSTM network. By maintaining memory of prior events, the LSTM is able to detect evolving attack signatures that unfold over time. At the final layer, two parallel branches generate the detection outputs: a sigmoid-activated neuron for binary classification (Normal vs. Attack) and a softmax-activated layer for multiclass classification over the four mapped attack categories (DoS, Probe, R2L, U2R). The CNN’s filter outputs are treated as sequential inputs to a stacked LSTM, whose hidden state dynamics encode evolving traffic behaviors and attack progression over time.

Algorithm 1AE–CNN–LSTM-Based Marine Intrusion Detection

AE–CNN–LSTM-Based Marine Intrusion Detection

We begin by mapping KDDCup99 traffic to maritime analogues (e.g., GPS jamming, sonar scans), encoding categorical fields, standardizing numerics, and splitting into train/validation/test. Domain cues (GPS jitter, sonar signal-to-noise, vessel kinematics) are fused with standard flow features. An autoencoder is first trained on benign or mixed traffic to denoise and compress features; its reconstruction error is retained as an auxiliary anomaly signal. The compressed sequences are passed to a 1D-CNN to capture local spatial regularities and then to stacked LSTMs to model temporal evolution. Two lightweight heads produce binary (normal vs. attack) and multiclass predictions (e.g., DoS, Probe, R2L, U2R). We optimize with Adam and early stopping, saving the best validation checkpoint, and evaluate using accuracy, precision, recall, F1, and AUC, with confusion matrices for both binary and multiclass settings.

Inference time scales primarily with: (i) the sequence length processed per decision, (ii) the number and width of CNN filters and their kernel size, and (iii) the hidden width and depth of the LSTM. The autoencoder adds a modest per-timestep encode/decode cost; the classification heads are negligible. Training multiplies these costs by the number of sequences and epochs, and requires extra memory for back-propagated activations. Parameter memory is dominated by CNN filters and recurrent layers; activation memory grows with sequence length and model width. For edge deployment, latency can be reduced by capping CNN filters and kernel sizes, replacing deep LSTMs with GRUs or temporal CNN blocks, using depthwise separable convolutions, and applying quantization/pruning or distillation. Throughput improves with streaming windows and batch size of one, while threshold calibration and class weights stabilize minority-class recall without inflating false positives.

Implementation workflow

The implementation of the proposed hybrid model follows a structured five-phase pipeline encompassing data preprocessing, unsupervised feature extraction via autoencoder, sequential learning with CNN-LSTM, and comprehensive evaluation. Each phase is designed to optimize the detection of both simple and complex cyber intrusions in marine IoT systems.

Step 1 Environment setup and library installation

The initial step involves the installation of essential Python libraries including TensorFlow, Scikit-learn, NumPy, Pandas, and Seaborn. These libraries provide high-level APIs for deep learning, data manipulation, feature scaling, and visualization. The code is executed in Google Colab with GPU acceleration enabled to ensure efficient training of deep neural architectures.

Step 2 Dataset loading and preprocessing

The KDDCup99 dataset, serving as a simulation of cyber intrusions within a marine IoT context, is loaded using Scikit-learns fetch_kddcup99 function. The various features of a neural network are normalized using the Z-score standard to ensure that they are consistent. This is important for training stability.

Step 3 Feature extraction via auto encoder

An unsupervised Auto encoder (AE) is employed to learn compressed, noise-tolerant feature representations from the preprocessed input. The auto encoder consists of two dense encoding layers followed by a symmetric decoder. It can minimize the error of mean-square reconstruction between the reconstructed data and the input, thereby learning latent feature vectors that preserve critical information while discarding irrelevant noise. Once trained, the encoder part is retained to transform the original feature vectors into their low-dimensional latent representations. These encoded features serve as input to the subsequent CNN-LSTM classifier.

Step 4 Classification using CNN and LSTM

The encoded features are reshaped into 3D tensors suitable for sequence modeling. The CNN-LSTM-hybrid classifier is able to analyze the temporal and spatial correlations in the data collected from marine traffic. The 1D Convolutional Layer (Conv1D) acts as a local feature extractor by detecting patterns such as port scan bursts or repeated connection attempts. This is followed by a MaxPooling layer to reduce dimensionality and highlight dominant patterns. The output is then sent to an LSTM layer, which takes into account the temporal dependencies of the data. This can be useful in identifying stealthy attacks or unauthorized access. Finally, a Dense layer is used to generate class probabilities for multi-class and binary classification.

Step 5 Model evaluation and visualization

The model’s predictions are compared with those of ground truth labels using various classification metrics, such as F1-Score, precision, and recall. A confusion matrix, which is generated for every task, summarizes the distribution of incorrect and correct classifications across multiple categories. This feature helps the model distinguish between related types. Additionally, a classification report is printed to provide per-class performance metrics, which is particularly important in assessing the detection capability of minority classes like U2R and R2L in the multiclass setting.

Performance Evaluation

All experimental results reported in this study are averaged over 30 independent runs to account for variability due to random initialization and stochastic optimization. The dataset was split evenly across multiple runs, with training and validation occurring simultaneously. The shuffling order and weights were then re-initialized, and performance metrics were calculated and averaged. This approach is more robust than a single-run evaluation, as it allows for a more accurate assessment of model stability.

The proposed cybersecurity framework for marine networks uses performance metrics to evaluate its capabilities to distinguish between malicious and normal traffic. These performance metrics are used to analyze the model’s reliability and classification accuracy.

The Mean Squared Error is a measure of the difference between the input values and the reconstructed ones from the LSTM Autoencoder. It is different from the MAE, which only treats linear deviations. Discussion on marine intrusion insights

The results of the experiments conducted on the hybrid CNN-LSTM architecture’s ability to detect cyber intrusions in marine environments show that it is effective., such as smart ports, autonomous surface vessels, and underwater sensor networks. The Autoencoder component plays a vital role in denoising and compressing high-dimensional communication data, enabling the model to focus on meaningful representations. Subsequently, the CNN module extracts spatial feature correlations indicative of anomalous behaviors, such as abrupt packet bursts or port scanning. The temporal dynamics of attack patterns—particularly those associated with stealthy or gradual intrusions—are effectively captured through the LSTM layer, making the architecture well-suited for sequential marine telemetry data.

Despite the robustness of the proposed approach, it is acknowledged that the KDDCup99 dataset is considered outdated due to its lack of representation of modern, evolving attack vectors, especially in IoT-enabled marine ecosystems. However, it remains a mandatory and standardized benchmark in the field of intrusion detection, providing a common evaluation baseline for comparative analysis. Its use is justified in this research to validate the core functionality of the proposed architecture before deploying it on more contemporary and domain-specific datasets. The model will undergo further refinement using recently acquired cybersecurity data such as TON_IoT or custom AIS-driven attack simulations to enhance real-world applicability.

Discussion on marine intrusion insights

It is acknowledged that the current evaluation relies solely on the KDDCup99 dataset, which—despite its benchmark status—does not fully capture the complexity of real maritime traffic, including sonar signal variability, GPS jitter, and underwater communication anomalies. Although our label-mapping and feature augmentation steps simulate these conditions, evaluation on authentic maritime datasets such as TON_IoT, MAWI traffic archives, or AIS-derived logs would further validate generalization in operational contexts. Due to constraints on dataset availability and the proprietary nature of many maritime telemetry sources, such evaluations were beyond the scope of this study. However, the proposed AE–CNN–LSTM framework is inherently dataset-agnostic and can be adapted to incorporate real marine features through retraining or fine-tuning. In our future work, we will utilize the training data from the KDDCup99 model to extract lessons from contemporary maritime data, enabling the system to leverage learned representations while adapting to the domain-specific characteristics of authentic marine environments.

Another important consideration for real-world deployment is the distributed nature of Marine IoT systems, where networked components such as autonomous vessels, buoy-mounted sensors, and smart port terminals operate under intermittent connectivity and strict bandwidth limits. In such scenarios, a centralized training paradigm may be impractical or undesirable due to data privacy regulations and the cost of transmitting raw telemetry. The proposed AE–CNN–LSTM framework can be adapted to a federated learning (FL) setting, wherein model updates—rather than raw data—are exchanged between maritime nodes and a central aggregator. This approach enables collaborative training while preserving local data confidentiality and reducing communication overhead. In addition, utilizing techniques such as differential and secure privacy can help minimize the risk of information leakage resulting from model gradients. Decentralized or peer-to-peer model averaging could also be employed in environments without stable central coordination, allowing maritime nodes to share updates opportunistically. Future work will explore integrating FL and lightweight model compression into our architecture to facilitate scalable, secure, and bandwidth-efficient intrusion detection across geographically dispersed marine assets.

Computational efficiency and embedded deployment considerations

Practical deployment of intrusion detection systems in maritime environments often requires operation on resource-constrained platforms such as NVIDIA Jetson Nano, Raspberry Pi 4, or ARM Cortex-based controllers. These devices must balance detection performance with strict limits on power consumption, memory usage, and processing latency. The proposed AE–CNN–LSTM architecture contains approximately 1.42 million trainable parameters, resulting in a memory footprint of 5.7 MB for model weights. On a modern GPU (NVIDIA T4), the model achieves an average inference latency of 1.8 ms per sample in batch mode. Based on reported edge-device benchmarks for models of similar complexity, this translates to approximately 23–28 ms per sample on a Jetson Nano (in FP16 precision) and 46–52 ms per sample on a Raspberry Pi 4 (optimized with TensorFlow Lite). Power consumption during inference is estimated at 5–7 W for Jetson Nano and 3–4 W for Raspberry Pi 4. These results suggest the model can operate in near-real-time on edge devices, achieving 20–40 inferences per second on the Jetson Nano and 10–20 on the Raspberry Pi 4. Further optimization through model pruning, 8-bit quantization, and TensorRT acceleration could reduce latency by 30–40% and lower memory requirements below 4 MB, enhancing feasibility for on-board maritime intrusion detection. In future work, we plan to perform empirical benchmarking on actual embedded maritime hardware to validate these estimates under operational conditions, including network connectivity constraints and variable sea-state sensor inputs.

Conclusion

This study presented a hybrid AE–CNN–LSTM intrusion-detection framework that combines an autoencoder for denoising/feature compression, a convolutional module for spatial pattern extraction, and an LSTM for temporal dynamics to protect maritime cyber-physical systems. On a maritime-adapted KDDCup99 benchmark, the model achieves 99.8% binary accuracy and shows strong multiclass performance across precision, recall, F1, and AUC; the auto encoder’s reconstruction errors (MAE/MSE) add an auxiliary anomaly signal that improves robustness to previously unseen patterns. Compared with representative deep-learning and transformer baselines considered in this study, the approach is competitive to superior while remaining practical for real-time use in smart ports, autonomous vessels, and underwater sensor networks. We acknowledge limits related to dataset realism, sensitivity of minority-class recall under imbalance, and latency/memory budgets on embedded hardware. Going forward, we will implement and profile the detector on edge platforms, validate on newer maritime sources such as TON_IoT and AIS telemetry, enable privacy-preserving federated learning across distributed vessels and shore sites, harden communications with post-quantum cryptography or QKD, incorporate operator-oriented explanations to support accountable decisions, and apply class-imbalance remedies plus model compression for efficient on-device inference-steps intended to translate this framework into dependable, scalable maritime IDS deployments.
