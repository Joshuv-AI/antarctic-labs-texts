An open‐source pipeline for longitudinal single‐cell tracking and cell‐cycle/migration coupling analysis for neurotherapeutic screening
Authors: Neha Chandra, Matthew Yang, Hailey Wang, Miroslaw Janowski, Piotr Walczak, Cedric Allier, Yajie Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImaging‐based phenotypic assays are widely used in neuroprotection, drug discovery, and neurorepair studies, but long‐term, high‐throughput time‐lapse datasets remain difficult to analyze because of segmentation noise, photobleaching, crowded fields, cell division, and tracking errors. Fluorescent ubiquitination‐based cell cycle indicator (FUCCI) reporters enable live visualization of cell‐cycle phases, but their integration with migration behaviors remains limited. This study aimed to developed an open workflow for tracking cycle and motility.MethodsWe combined Fiji/ImageJ preprocessing, Cellpose‐based deep learning segmentation, and TrackMate‐based cell tracking. FUCCI‐expressing HEK293 cells were imaged using a Tecan Spark Cyto (Männedorf, Switzerland) every 15 min for 24 h. Cellpose models were retrained using manually corrected masks from brightfield and fluorescence images, and TrackMate parameters were optimized using manually curated tracks. Statistical analyses were conducted using Paleontological Statistics (PAST) software (version 4.0, Natural History Museum, University of Oslo, Oslo, Norway).ResultsIterative cellpose retraining improved segmentation accuracy in both brightfield and fluorescent datasets by reducing false‐positive and false‐negative errors (Brightfield Type I error improvement: t(10) = 33.323, p = 1.400 × 10−11, Brightfield Type II error improvement: t(10) = 16.066, p = 1.805 × 10−8, Fluorescent Type II errors: U = 0, p = 0.00077). TrackMate optimization improved trajectory continuity, especially through adjustment of frame‐to‐frame linking distances (Brightfield: χ 2 (2) = 10, p = 0.00077, Fluorescent: χ 2 (2) = 10, p = 0.00077). The workflow enabled single‐cell and population‐level analysis of migration distance, turning behavior, morphology, division events, and FUCCI‐defined cell‐cycle progression. Cells showed heterogeneous motility, with lower displacement trends during green‐dominant S/G2/M phases than red‐dominant G1 phases.ConclusionThis workflow links cell‐cycle state with migration dynamics and can be adapted for neuroprotective screening, neural cultures, organoids, and neuron–glia co‐culture studies.

INTRODUCTION

Longitudinal live‐cell imaging is increasingly used to quantify how candidate interventions modulate dynamic cellular phenotypes that matter for translational neuroscience and neuroprotection research 1 (e.g., survival, neurite integrity, glial responses, and regenerative cell behaviors). In parallel, imaging‐based high‐content assays have been established for human induced pluripotent cells (iPSC)‐derived neuronal cultures, enabling quantitative readouts of neuronal viability and morphology in screening‐compatible formats. 2 , 3 High‐throughput, image‐based neurite growth screening in hiPSC‐derived neurons further highlights the value of scalable microscopy pipelines for neuro‐relevant discovery workflows. 4 More recently, high‐content imaging has also been integrated into human cortical organoid screening workflows, underscoring a growing need for analysis approaches that remain robust as models become more complex and data volumes increase. 5 When paired with appropriate reporters, longitudinal imaging can further resolve cell‐state transitions (e.g., cell‐cycle phase) that may accompany functional behaviors such as motility, contact dynamics, or clonal expansion. For example, the introduction of live fluorescent reporters, such as the Fluorescent Ubiquitination‐based Cell Cycle Indicator (FUCCI), has further expanded the scope of inquiry by allowing real‐time visualization of cell cycle transitions, providing insight into proliferation patterns alongside migratory behavior. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

However, effective analysis of time‐lapse datasets faces three main challenges. First, the scale of the data is enormous: a single experiment can produce thousands of frames and hundreds of cell trajectories as cells undergo complex behaviors over time. 3 , 10 Manual annotation is therefore not only slow but also susceptible to bias and inconsistency, making automated approaches essential. 11 , 12 , 13 Second, robust segmentation and tracking remain difficult across imaging conditions and cell morphologies. A Existing tools often need tuning or retraining to handle variation in cell shape, size, signal intensity, and behavior, and segmentation errors can propagate into fragmented tracks, incorrect linkages, or faulty lineage assignments. 14 , 15 , 16 These issues are amplified in high‐throughput datasets where manual correction is impractical. Third, functional markers such as FUCCI are not routinely integrated with tracking outputs. Although FUCCI enables dynamic visualization of cell‐cycle progression, relatively few studies combine it with spatial behaviors such as velocity, turning, or migration persistence16. This integration is important for understanding how proliferation and motility co‐evolve during development, wound repair, and neuroprotective drug screening.

To address these challenges, we present an open and reproducible workflow for long‐term single‐cell tracking and joint quantification of cell‐cycle state and motility from time‐lapse microscopy. The novelty of this work lies in the structured integration of segmentation, tracking, and cell‐cycle analysis with single‐cell migration and morphology measurements. Unlike existing pipelines such as ConfluentFUCCI 17 and FUCCItrack 18 , which focus primarily on either dense tissue tracking or phase assignment, this pipeline provides a flexible and fully automated framework for long‐term, dynamic studies. First, to overcome the challenge of large, complex datasets, we incorporated automated segmentation and tracking using deep learning based Cellpose 15 and adjustable TrackMate parameters. 19 Second, to ensure accurate performance across diverse imaging conditions and cell morphologies, we retrained Cellpose on context‐specific training data and optimized tracking parameters for each experiment. Third, to integrate functional FUCCI markers with spatial behaviors, we combined fluorescence‐based cell cycle information with migration analysis, enabling simultaneous quantification of proliferation and motility. Collectively, this framework provides reusable settings, evaluation criteria, and analysis outputs that can be readily adapted to neuroprotection‐oriented imaging assays where linking cell state (e.g., cell‐cycle phase) to dynamic behavior (e.g., motility) is important for mechanistic studies and screening.

METHODS

Cell culture

Human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293) cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). For cell cycle visualization, cells were co‐transfected with a CRISPR/Cas9 plasmid targeting the AAVS1 safe harbor insertion site (Addgene #85802; Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) and a donor plasmid encoding the FUCCI construct under the CAG promoter (Addgene #136934). The donor plasmid also included a puromycin resistance gene for selection. Cells were imaged on the Tecan Spark Cyto (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland) every 15 min for 24 h, producing a multi‐channel image stack of 96 time points.

Image processing in Fiji/ImageJ

Raw time‐lapse images from the Tecan Spark Cyto were preprocessed in Fiji/ImageJ (ImageJ, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) before analysis with TrackMate. Background noise was reduced using background subtraction. To correct for drift in the microscope field of view over time, image frames were registered using the SIFT (Scale‐Invariant Feature Transform) algorithm. 20 Each fluorescent image was initially split into one of four single‐channel stacks, to proceed with registration, and then merged into a multi‐channel hyperstack for further analysis.

Automated segmentation and model training by cellpose

Two dataset views were used in this study: brightfield and fluorescent. The brightfield dataset provided clear cell boundaries with background noise, while the fluorescent dataset presented high‐contrast images with potential challenges such as photobleaching. We utilized the Python‐based algorithm Cellpose (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA; available at: https://github.com/MouseLand/cellpose) for precise cell segmentation. Cellpose is a neural network algorithm designed with a human‐in‐the‐loop approach for producing accurate segmentations (available in GitHub). 6 Uploading brightfield images and running default models produced multiple regions of interest (ROI). Default models Cyto3 and nuclei produce segmentations of low accuracy by miscounting the true number of cells as ROIs, requiring the development of custom models through training and manual correction. To train a model, we ran the default Cyto3 model on a small number of frames and manually fixed segmentation errors. Manual corrections include user annotation of default model segmentation by adding and removing ROIs in the Cellpose GUI. Models were then trained with the newly user‐annotated and error‐corrected data, which increased segmentation accuracy with each iteration of training and corrections.

Tracking analysis through TrackMate

To track the segmented cells over consecutive frames, we employed the TrackMate plugin in ImageJ. Image stacks were uploaded to ImageJ in which Cellpose segmentation was integrated, followed by the LAP Tracker in TrackMate. 19 A subset of 10 cells was chosen for detailed tracking analysis. The Trackscheme interface was used to manually annotate incorrect tracks, yielding ground truth for this subset of cells. The ground truth tracks included division events, so both daughter cells were accounted for after the division. For each selected cell, the tracks generated by TrackMate were compared with ground truth tracks for the first 50 frames of the time‐lapse sequence. A scoring system was devised to quantify tracking accuracy; the score for each cell track was based on the number of frames in which the track correctly matched the actual cell trajectory. The obtained scores were then converted into percentages to facilitate meaningful comparison and analysis.

Cell behavior and migration analysis

TrackMate allows for the export of cell and track data as a.csv file, which was exported and analyzed in Python (Python Software Foundation, version 3.x, https://www.python.org/) developed in Visual Studio Code (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) to assess cell migration alongside cell‐cycle position. These features enabled us to characterize individual and population‐level differences in cell motility and investigate how these dynamics varied over time and between different cell cycle phases.

Statistics

Data are presented as box‐and‐whisker plots, where boxes span the interquartile range from the 25th to the 75th percentile, and whiskers represent the 10th and 90th percentiles. Statistical analyses were conducted using Paleontological Statistics (PAST) software(version 4.0, Natural History Museum, University of Oslo, Oslo, Norway). 21 Normality tests were done with the Shapiro–Wilks test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an independent t‐test, when data was normal, and the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test, when data did not fit a normal distribution. For repeated measures across conditions, the Friedman test was used to assess differences. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Pipeline for longitudinal imaging of cell culture and data processing

Our study employed a flexible pipeline for high‐throughput, long‐term imaging of FUCCI‐labeled HEK293 cells, integrating automated data acquisition, segmentation, and tracking (Figure 1A). HEK293 cells, transfected with the FUCCI cell cycle indicator, were imaged using a Tecan Spark Cyto system. Brightfield and fluorescent images were acquired at 15‐min intervals over a 24‐h period. Image stacks underwent pre‐processing in Fiji/ImageJ, including background subtraction and drift correction via image registration, to ensure consistent field of view across timepoints (Figure 1B). This established pipeline enabled continuous tracking of cellular behaviors, such as mitotic events, motility, and directional changes, facilitating investigation into transitions in cell states and the interplay between cell cycle status and migratory dynamics.

Optimizing algorithms for Cellpose segmentation of live HEK293 cells

Cellular segmentation was performed using Cellpose, a deep learning‐based algorithm. 6 Initial evaluation of default Cellpose models on raw brightfield and fluorescent datasets indicated that the Cyto3 model provided the most accurate initial segmentation for both modalities. However, Cyto3 exhibited modality‐specific limitations. In brightfield images, Cyto3 frequently over‐segmented, resulting in inflated cell counts and detection of false positives, particularly in crowded regions or during mitosis (Figure 2A). Conversely, in fluorescent images, Cyto3 predominantly under‐segmented, missing a significant number of cells without generating false positives, as fluorescent signals effectively excluded debris (Figure 2C). Fluorescent imaging targets specific cellular components like nuclei (as with FUCCI), providing a high‐contrast signal against a dark background, which inherently filters out most background noise and debris. While still prone to under‐segmentation due to faint signals or highly overlapping cells, this specificity effectively mitigates the occurrence of false positives, as only labeled cellular structures produce a detectable signal.

(B) Trained models showed significant reduction in both Type I and Type II errors across multiple brightfield frames (Left: p = 1.400 × 10−11, n = 6, t‐test, Right: p = 1.805 × 10−8, n = 6, t‐test). (C) In fluorescent data, Cyto3 primarily generated false negatives. (D) While Type I errors were not produced, retraining reduced Type II errors across fluorescent frames (Right: p = 0.0021645, n = 6, Mann–Whitney U test). (E–F) Segmentation accuracy improved significantly in both (E) brightfield (p = 0.00077, n = 5, Friedman's test) and (F) fluorescent datasets after training (p = 0.00077, n = 5, Friedman's test). (G) Total error rates declined after training iterations in both modalities. *p < 0.05.

To improve segmentation accuracy, custom Cellpose models were generated through iterative manual correction (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1). For brightfield images, manual outlines of whole cells served as ground truth. Initial Cyto3 segmentation yielded 56 ROIs, while manual correction identified 47 ROIs as ground truth. Following training, the custom model identified 49 ROIs, closely approximating the ground truth. In the context of segmentation, Type I errors correspond to noncellular regions incorrectly identified as cells (false positives), whereas Type II errors reflect true cells that are missed or under‐segmented (false negatives). This iterative training significantly reduced both Type I errors (Figure 2B, t(10) = 33.323, p = 1.400 × 10−11) and Type II errors (Figure 2B, t(10) = 16.066, p = 1.805 × 10−8) in brightfield images. For fluorescent images, nuclei were outlined as ground truth. Here, Cyto3 initially identified 109 ROIs compared to a ground truth of 151 ROIs. Retraining yielded an optimized algorithm that identified 140 ROIs, a substantial improvement, and reduced Type II errors in fluorescent images (Figure 2D, U = 0, p = 0.0021645). The optimized algorithms improved segmentation accuracy in Brightfield (Figure 2E, χ 2 (2) = 10, p = 0.00077) and in Fluorescent data (Figure 2F, χ 2 (2) = 10, p = 0.0007) after training and reduced overall errors in both modalities (Figure 2G). These results support the effectiveness of iterative manual correction in refining Cellpose models for specific imaging conditions. These observations confirm that generalist models like Cellpose work best on fluorescent images, where nuclear markers create clear objects. Nonetheless, our human‐guided retraining was critical for both modalities. Because Cellpose allows easy retraining on user data, it remains a powerful, flexible tool for diverse imaging conditions.

TrackMate produces errors and correction of tracks is necessary

Following segmentation with Cellpose, cell tracking was performed using the TrackMate 19 plugin in ImageJ for both brightfield and fluorescent datasets (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2). However, TrackMate demonstrated several limitations in generating accurate tracks, particularly due to the long imaging intervals. Segmentation errors led to tracking gaps (Figure 3A), but even with correct segmentation, TrackMate sometimes began following neighboring cells in close proximity instead of maintaining the original cell track (Figure 3B). During mitotic events, the software tracked one daughter cell while assigning the second to a new lineage (Figure 3C), though errors occurred when a daughter cell was incorrectly linked to an unrelated track. Additionally, broken linkages led to missing timepoints or fragmentation of a single cell's trajectory into multiple disjointed tracks (Figure 3D). While some tracking inaccuracies stemmed from segmentation mistakes, such as merged or undetected cells, we observed tracking errors (Figure 3A–D) that occurred independently of segmentation and required manual correction. These corrections were performed using TrackMate's TrackScheme interface to reconstruct accurate lineages, which are labor‐intensive.

Frame‐to‐frame linking parameter is the major factor affecting tracking accuracy in both brightfield and fluorescent data

To evaluate tracking accuracy, we tested two key parameters in TrackMate: frame‐to‐frame linking distance and segment gap closing (Figure 3E). We assessed frame‐to‐frame linking thresholds of 15, 40, 65, 90, and 115 pixels to determine the maximum distance over which a cell could be accurately linked across consecutive frames. Similarly, segment gap closing was tested at the same pixel distances, while keeping the max frame gap constant, to evaluate TrackMate's ability to reconnect discontinuous tracks. Candidate linking distances were initially set at the TrackMate default value of 15 pixels and subsequently expanded to 115 pixels based on preliminary observations of frame‐to‐frame cell displacements across the dataset. 19 The default threshold was often too restrictive to capture true cell displacement between frames, leading to fragmented or incorrect track assignments. Smaller thresholds were sufficient for slowly migrating or spatially isolated cells, whereas larger thresholds were necessary to maintain continuity for faster‐moving cells or in crowded regions, including near mitotic events. Testing this range enabled systematic evaluation of TrackMate performance under both restrictive and permissive linking conditions.

In the brightfield dataset, tracking accuracy remained consistently high across all frame‐to‐frame linking distances (Figure 3F). The highest accuracy was observed at 65 pixels (99.1%, 553/558), closely followed by 40 pixels (98.5%, 550/558), with only minor differences between them. In contrast, segment gap closing had minimal impact, as accuracy remained stable around 98.2% (548/558) regardless of the threshold used (Figure 3F). Tracking in the fluorescent dataset resulted in lower overall accuracy compared to brightfield. The 40‐pixel linking distance achieved the highest accuracy at 98.6% (550/558, Figure 3G). All other linking distances led to performance declines, with accuracy dropping to 90.5% (505/558) at 65 pixels and as low as 84.9% (473/558) at 115 pixels. As in the brightfield condition, variations in segment gap closing had limited effect on overall performance, though a 40‐pixel setting yielded the best result at 94.4% (527/558) (Figure 3G). Together, these findings indicate that frame‐to‐frame linking distance is the most influential parameter for optimizing tracking accuracy, particularly in fluorescence data. Segment gap closing, by contrast, has minimal impact and may be less critical to adjust. Carefully tuning the linking parameter improves baseline performance and reduces the need for downstream manual correction.

Using the optimized cell tracking method to quantify cell cycle and migration

Following optimization of segmentation and tracking parameters, the refined pipeline enabled comprehensive quantification of cell cycle progression and migratory dynamics. Combined multi‐channel tracking, integrating brightfield and fluorescent data, provided superior visualization and facilitated simultaneous study of fluorescent intensity and cell trajectories over time (Figure 4A). Analysis of individual cell tracks revealed expected oscillations in green and red FUCCI fluorescence intensities, indicative of cell cycle progression (Figure 4B). Specifically, high red fluorescence during G1 decreased upon entry into S phase, accompanied by a corresponding increase in green fluorescence during S/G2/M. The green signal subsequently diminished at mitosis, followed by red fluorescence reappearance in daughter cells entering G1. This robust and interpretable temporal annotation of cell cycle phase transitions and division timing was consistently observed across multiple cells.

Assessment of cellular migratory behavior over the tracking period revealed diverse dynamics. A histogram of displacement steps indicated a wide range of step sizes, with most movements less than 50 μm (Figure 4C). Migration distance per 15‐min interval fluctuated, exhibiting alternating periods of movement and stasis (Figure 4D). Turning angle distribution peaked near 90°, suggesting frequent moderate changes in direction rather than linear or reversal movements, indicative of stochastic, constrained reorientation (Figure 4E). A scatter plot of cell speed versus directional change rate primarily showed low speeds and low‐to‐moderate directional changes (Figure 4F).

To investigate the interplay between cell cycle phase and migration, normalized FUCCI fluorescence intensities were correlated with migration distance and turning angle. Migration distance was lower during the green‐dominant (S/G2/M) phase and tended to increase as cells transitioned toward G1 (Figure 4G). In contrast, turning angle distributions remained broadly similar across phases, showing no strong dependence on cell cycle state (Figure 4H).

Quantitative summaries further illustrate the diversity of migratory behavior. Most cells in this subset migrate intermediate distances (800–1100 μm), though there is considerable spread across the cohort (Figure 5E). In the scatterplot illustrating total distance traveled versus confinement ratio, a slight downward trend is observed, indicating that cells with higher confinement ratios tend to travel shorter total distances. However, due to the broad dispersion of data points, this relationship appears weak, reflecting considerable heterogeneity in migratory behavior across the cell population despite some influence of spatial constraints (Figure 5F). The distribution of directional change rates (Figure 5G) centers around approximately 87° per frame, indicating that on average, cells display moderate turning behavior during migration. Morphological and dynamic features also varied with the cell cycle stage. Cells with higher red‐channel intensity (G1 phase) exhibited slightly higher aspect ratios, suggesting more elongated morphology compared to those in G2/M (green‐enriched) states (Figure 5H). Across the 15 tracks, the mean red fraction per track was positively associated with migration speed (Figure 5I), while net displacement showed a similar but less consistent pattern (Figure 5J). Together, these analyses reveal that cell cycle phase influences not only morphology but also migratory dynamics.

These results reveal phase‐dependent differences in migration, morphology, and cell cycle progression within a small cell population, demonstrating the effectiveness of this pipeline for integrating migration tracking with cell cycle phase analysis. While the observed trends, such as reduced displacement in S/G2/M phases, are consistent with prior literature, the small sample size and exploratory design limit the strength of biological conclusions. Rather than establishing causality, these results serve as a proof‐of‐principle demonstration of the pipeline′s ability to integrate cell cycle and migration analyses at single‐cell resolution.

DISCUSSION

Traditional segmentation techniques such as thresholding, watershed transforms, and active contour models remain in use within some workflows; however, these methods often fail under challenging imaging conditions. 6 , 22 , 23 Deep learning–based tools such as Cellpose offer greater flexibility, particularly when combined with human‐in‐the‐loop retraining. 6 In our study, iterative correction of Cellpose outputs improved segmentation in both brightfield and fluorescence datasets by reducing Type I and Type II errors. Fluorescent images were generally easier to segment because of higher contrast and lower background complexity, whereas brightfield images contained more noise, overlapping structures, and noncellular objects. These findings emphasize that even generalist models require dataset‐specific optimization, especially when applied to new imaging modalities or cell types.

Existing FUCCI analysis tools offer useful but limited functions: FUCCItrack supports single‐cell tracking and phase assignment but is optimized for sparse cultures and requires substantial manual supervision, 18 whereas ConfluentFUCCI improves automated analysis in dense cultures but provides limited customization and migration or morphology outputs. 17 In contrast, In contrast, our Cellpose–TrackMate workflow combines customizable, GPU‐accelerated segmentation with Fiji‐compatible time‐lapse tracking, enabling cell‐cycle phase to be linked with migration trajectories and morphology. Among available cell‐tracking methods (Supporting Information S1: Table 2), TrackMate performance was most sensitive to frame‐to‐frame linking distance, while gap closing had minimal influence, likely because improved segmentation reduced missed detections (Figure 3F,G). These findings reinforce that accurate tracking depends strongly on reliable segmentation and parameter tuning.

Using our optimized pipeline, we quantified both migration and cell‐cycle progression from individual tracks, with FUCCI profiles showing the expected red‐to‐green transition from G1 to S/G2/M and confirming that segmentation/tracking preserved temporal fluorescence dynamics. Cells showed mostly short step lengths, occasional longer displacements, and broadly distributed turning angles, consistent with stochastic migration without strong directional cues. 24 When stratified by FUCCI state, displacement tended to be higher in red‐dominant G1 and lower in green‐dominant S/G2/M, paralleling reports of reduced movement in G2 and supporting dynamic coupling between cell‐cycle progression, motility, and morphology. 25 , 26 , 27 Broad variation in directional change and weak relationships between confinement ratio and migration distance further highlighted heterogeneity within the small dataset. Overall, these findings support integrating cell‐cycle readouts with migration metrics to capture multiparametric single‐cell dynamics. 25 , 26 , 27

Several limitations remain. Segmentation and tracking accuracy were sensitive to imaging quality, cell morphology, temporal resolution, and cell density. Fluorescence tracking was more error‐prone because nuclear labeling provides limited shape information for frame‐to‐frame association, whereas brightfield tracking became more difficult after repeated division events. In addition, tracking accuracy was evaluated using a manually curated subset of 10 well‐segmented cells, and population‐level analysis was limited to 15 cells. Therefore, the reported results should be interpreted as proof‐of‐principle estimates of optimized tracking performance rather than full end‐to‐end validation across all segmentation failure modes. Larger datasets and additional cell types will be needed to confirm robustness and generalizability. Despite these limitations, the workflow is reproducible and transferable. We included a detailed description of the procedure of Cellpose model creation (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1) and Trackmate usage (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2). Second, we provided python code to enable reproducible implementation (Supplemental information). Third, we uploaded raw image data and tracking file to figshare: https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.31634557. This framework is adaptable to neurons, glia, organoids, and mixed neural cultures for neuroprotection studies or migration analysis. Core steps, including FUCCI reporter labeling, time‐lapse imaging, ImageJ‐based channel registration, Cellpose segmentation, and TrackMate linking, are broadly transferable (Figure 1). However, neural and glial cells often show complex morphologies, including neurites, ramified processes, variable fluorescence, and overlapping somata; therefore, dataset‐specific Cellpose retraining and TrackMate parameter tuning will likely be required. To improve robustness, representative “hard cases” should be included during training and validation, and tracking quality should be reported using objective metrics such as fragmentation rate, identity switches, and manual correction burden. For microglia‐like cells, defining the tracked structure, such as soma, process centroid, or skeleton‐derived feature, is important because each captures distinct motility behaviors. 29 , 30 Because FUCCI‐type reporters have been used in mouse and neural tissue contexts, extending this “track + phase” framework to neural progenitors or injury‐responsive populations may help distinguish treatment effects on proliferation, activation state, migration, and morphology. 28 , 29 , 30

CONCLUSION

In summary, we demonstrate an open and adaptable workflow that integrates Cellpose‐based segmentation, TrackMate tracking, and fluorescence readouts to jointly analyze cell‐cycle progression and migration in time‐lapse microscopy. By combining model retraining with parameter optimization, this pipeline addresses common sources of error in long‐term imaging datasets and provides a practical framework for linking cellular state with dynamic behavior in neuroprotection‐oriented assays.
