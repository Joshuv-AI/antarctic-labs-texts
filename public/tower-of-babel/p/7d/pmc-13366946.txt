The insulin receptor inhibitor BMS-754807 alleviates neuroinflammation and Alzheimer’s disease pathologies across human cellular and mouse models
Authors: Hyun-ju Lee, Jaewoo Seok, Sora Kang, Seokjun Oh, Jeong-Woo Hwang, Yu-Jin Kim, Jinsoo Seo, Hyang-Sook Hoe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBMS-754807 is a dual inhibitor of insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-1R) and insulin receptor (IR) that is in phase II clinical trials for the treatment of HR-positive and HER2-negative breast cancer. Because IGF-1R signaling regulates inflammatory responses, pharmacological modulation of IGF-1R may have therapeutic potential for Alzheimer’s disease (AD); however, the effects of BMS-754807 on neuroinflammatory responses/AD pathology and cognitive function have not been fully investigated.MethodsWe examined whether BMS-754807 modulates neuroinflammation and AD pathologies in multiple in vivo animal models and in vitro human models. BMS-754807 (20 mg/kg, i.p.) was systemically administered in wild-type mice challenged with LPS, 5xFAD mice, and PS19 transgenic mice. In addition, human-induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived microglia challenged with LPS and AD hiPSC-derived neurons were treated with 2.5 µM BMS-754807. For all models, the effects of BMS-754807 treatment were analyzed by real-time PCR, immunofluorescence staining, western blotting, ELISA, and/or activity assays.ResultsBMS-754807 treatment significantly decreased p-IGF-IR (on-target) levels, LPS-induced proinflammatory cytokine production, and reactive oxygen species levels; restored HO-1 expressions; and inhibited AKT/STAT3 signaling in BV2 microglial cells. Similarly, BMS-754807 treatment reduced LPS-evoked proinflammatory cytokine expressions in primary microglial cells and primary astrocytes. In addition, BMS-754807 administration mitigated LPS-stimulated gliosis, microglial/astrocyte-associated dynamics, STAT3/NF-κB phosphorylation, and potentially NLRP3 inflammasome in vitro and/or in WT mice. Moreover, BMS-754807 treatment suppressed LPS-mediated proinflammatory responses through IGF-1R and NLRP3 in BV2 microglial cells. In 5xFAD mice, BMS-754807 administration downregulated IGF-1R phosphorylation, microgliosis/astrogliosis-related dynamics, and AKT/P38/STAT3 pathway. Notably, BMS-754807 treatment also diminished LPS-induced proinflammatory cytokine levels and STAT3/NF-κB signaling in human microglial models. Furthermore, BMS-754807 treatment decreased Aβ40/Aβ42 levels in hiPSC-derived AD neurons, and increased short-term spatial memory and reduced Aβ plaque accumulation by decreasing β-secretase (BACE1) activity in 5xFAD mice. Finally, in hiPSC-derived AD neurons and PS19 mice, BMS-754807 treatment significantly attenuated tau hyperphosphorylation, CaMKIIα phosphorylation, and tau-mediated astroglial activation.ConclusionsTaken together, our results suggest that BMS-754807 exerts anti-inflammatory and potential disease-modifying effects by attenuating LPS/Aβ/tau-evoked glial activation and reducing Aβ and tau pathologies in both human cellular and mouse models of neuroinflammation and AD. Furthermore, BMS-754807 administration improved specific domains of cognitive function in vivo. These findings support pharmacological inhibition of IGF-1R as a potential therapeutic approach for neuroinflammation-associated diseases including AD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03855-7.

Background

Neuroinflammation is an intrinsic immune response in the central nervous system (CNS) that plays a critical role in maintaining brain homeostasis by detecting and eliminating pathogens and cellular debris [1]. Transient inflammatory responses are essential for neuroprotection and tissue repair, but prolonged or dysregulated neuroinflammation might lead to progressive synaptic dysfunction and cognitive decline, thereby contributing to Alzheimer's disease (AD) and other neurodegenerative diseases [2–4]. These chronic inflammatory conditions are effectively mimicked by stimulating microglia and astrocytes with endotoxins such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS). LPS robustly hyperactivates glial cells and disrupts their homeostasis, triggering the massive release of proinflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [5, 6]. Therefore, suppressing microglial/astrocyte hyperactivation is considered a promising therapeutic strategy to ameliorate CNS inflammatory responses and subsequent neurodegeneration [7].

The accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ), a hallmark of AD, also stimulates microglial/astrocyte activation, leading to the production of proinflammatory mediators [8]. Aβ is generated through the sequential cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP) by β- and γ-secretases, primarily within endocytic compartments [9, 10]. The resulting Aβ40 and Aβ42 peptides aggregate into Aβ oligomers which contribute to synaptic dysfunction, impaired neuronal connectivity, and ultimately resulting in cognitive decline [9, 10]. In addition, Aβ and hyperphosphorylated tau exhibit pro-oxidative properties that disrupt mitochondrial function [11]. The oxidative stress further exacerbates AD pathology by promoting Aβ production through activation of β- and γ-secretases and by enhancing tau hyperphosphorylation [11–13]. Moreover, proinflammatory cytokines and activated microglia promote tau phosphorylation by tau kinases such as GSK-3β, CDK5, and CaMKII, thereby accelerating neurofibrillary tangle formation and synaptic dysfunction [14, 15]. Consequently, targeting inflammatory pathways that modulate both Aβ accumulation and tau pathology may be a promising therapeutic strategy for AD.

Insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-1R) is a receptor tyrosine kinase that is broadly expressed in neuronal and glial cells and is involved in neuroprotection, synaptic function, and inflammatory responses [16–18]. Consistent with these roles, the IGF-1R inhibitor DKD-IGF alleviates inflammation, proinflammatory cytokine expression, and inflammatory cell infiltration in a mouse model of diabetic kidney disease [19]. In addition, the IGF-1R antagonist JB1 reduces the expression of proinflammatory mediators (e.g., COX-2 and iNOS) in primary astrocytes from a Parkinson's disease (PD) mouse model [20]. Given that chronic neuroinflammation is a critical driver of AD pathogenesis, pharmacological inhibition of IGF-1R may mitigate neuroinflammation-associated pathology in AD.

In this study, we investigated the effects of the dual IGF-1R/IR inhibitor BMS-754807 on AD pathology using a comprehensive array of in vitro and in vivo models: LPS-challenged mouse glia and wild-type mice, human iPSC-derived microglia and AD neurons, and 5xFAD and PS19 transgenic mice. In these diverse models, BMS-754807 treatment consistently exerted robust anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing LPS/Aβ/tau-mediated glial hyperactivation, oxidative stress, AKT/STAT3/NF-κB signaling, and, potentially, the NLRP3 inflammasome. Furthermore, pharmacological IGF-1R/IR inhibition by BMS-754807 significantly ameliorated Aβ plaque deposition by decreasing BACE1 activity, attenuated tau hyperphosphorylation and associated astrogliosis, and ultimately improved short-term spatial memory in AD mouse models and/or AD hiPSC-derived neurons. Collectively, these findings indicate that pharmacological modulation of IGF-1R is a promising disease-modifying strategy to alleviate neuroinflammation and AD pathogenesis.

Methods

Ethics statement

All experiments were approved by the institutional biosafety committee (IBC) of the Korea Brain Research Institute (KBRI) and Yonsei University and performed in accordance with approved animal protocols of KBRI (KBRI, approval no. IACUC-19—00049 and IACUC-24—00004; Yonsei University, approval no. IBC-D-202509—447—91). The institutional review board (IRB) of Yonsei University approved the use of hiPSCs (approval no. 7001988—202,505-BR-2742—01E).

IGF-1R/IR Inhibitor, BMS-754807

BMS-754807 was originally developed as an anticancer drug, and thus we selected doses (10 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg) lower than those used for anticancer efficacy to minimize the potential for cytotoxic and systemic adverse effects associated with anticancer dosing (25 to 75 mg/kg) [21–23]. Although comprehensive pharmacokinetic profiling and plasma-to-brain ratio measurements were not performed in the present study, a recent study reported that orally administered BMS-754807 (32.5 mg/kg) significantly reduces p-IGF-1R levels in the cerebellum in SHO mice, confirming that BMS-754807 crosses the blood–brain barrier (BBB) [24]. In addition, Prabhakaran et al. demonstrated penetration of radiolabeled [18F] BMS-754807 into the BBB in mice and rats using micro-PET imaging [25].

For in vivo experiments, BMS-754807 was purchased from MCE (Cat. No. HY-10200, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), dissolved in vehicle [5% DMSO + 10% polyethylene glycol (PEG) 300 + 20% Tween 80 + 65% deionized water (DW)], and administered by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection. For in vitro assays, BMS-754807 was purchased from Selleckchem (Cat. No. S1124, Houston, TX, USA), dissolved in DMSO, and used at a final concentration of 2.5 or 5 µM.

BV2 microglial cells, primary microglia, and primary astrocytes

BV2 microglial cells were obtained from Elabioscience Biotechnology Inc (Houston, TX, USA) and cultured as previously described [26]. Primary glial cells were isolated from C57BL/6 mice on postnatal day 1 according to an established protocol with minor modifications [5]. Briefly, whole brains were mechanically dissociated and filtered through 70-μm nylon mesh. Mixed glial cultures, mainly composed of astrocytes and microglia, were maintained in low-glucose DMEM (1000 mg/L glucose) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin in a 5% CO₂ incubator at 37 °C for 3 weeks. To obtain mouse primary astrocytes, T75 flasks containing mixed glial cells were sealed with foil and shaken at 250 rpm on a rotary shaker at room temperature (RT) overnight. Next, the conditioned culture medium was discarded, and the cells were dissociated using trypsin–EDTA and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 30 min. The resulting pellet was collected as primary astrocytes and used for subsequent experiments. To isolate primary microglial cells, mixed glial cells were subjected to mild trypsin digestion (0.5 M EDTA, 1 M CaCl2, and 0.25% trypsin EDTA in low-glucose DMEM with 1% penicillin and streptomycin) for 40 min in the incubator. After removal of the astrocytic layer by washing, microglial cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA and centrifuged twice at 2000 rpm for 10 min. To investigate the effect of BMS-754807 on LPS-induced neuroinflammatory responses, primary microglial cells or primary astrocytes were treated with LPS (200 ng/ml, L2630, Escherichia coli, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) or PBS for 30 min, followed by treatment with BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) for 5.5 h.

CCK assay

To assess the cytotoxicity of BMS-754807 treatment in vitro, the D-Plus™ CCK cell viability assay kit (Dongin biotech, Seoul, Korea) was used. BV2 microglial cells were incubated with vehicle (0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, or 0.5% DMSO) or BMS-754807 (0.1, 1, 5, 10, 25, or 50 µM) for 6 h. Then, the treated cells were incubated with CCK solution (0.5 mg/ml) for 30 min, and the absorbance at 570 nm was measured using a SPECTROstar Nano microplate reader (BMG, Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany).

Real-time PCR (qPCR)

The effects of BMS-754807 on LPS-mediated proinflammatory cytokine expression, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and neuroinflammatory dynamics were evaluated in BV2 microglial cells, primary microglial cells, and/or primary astrocytes. For prevention conditions, BV2 microglial cells were treated with BMS-754807 (2.5 or 5 µM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) for 30 min, followed by LPS (200 ng/mL) or PBS for 5.5 h. For curative conditions, BV2 microglial cells were challenged with LPS (200 ng/mL) or PBS for 30 min, followed by BMS-754807 (2.5 or 5 µM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) for 5.5 h. To investigate whether BMS-754807 downregulates LPS-evoked proinflammatory responses in primary microglial cells and primary astrocytes, cells were treated with LPS (200 ng/mL) or PBS for 30 min followed by BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) for 5.5 h. Real-time quantitative (qPCR) analysis of proinflammatory cytokine expression, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and neuroinflammatory dynamics was then performed as previously described [27]. The mRNA expression of each target was normalized using the cycle threshold (Ct) values for Gapdh. Fold changes were calculated relative to the control treated with 1% DMSO. Detailed information on primer sequences for real-time qPCR is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting in BV2 microglial cells

To investigate whether BMS-754807 treatment reduces on-target IGF-1R phosphorylation in vitro, BV2 microglial cells were treated with BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) for 6 h. Then, the cells were lysed, and Western blotting was performed using an anti-p-IGF-1RY1135/1136 antibody and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA). To examine the impact of BMS-754807 on LPS-induced oxidative stress (HO-1), LPS-associated downstream signaling (AKT and P38), and inflammation-associated molecular target (NLRP3) expression in vitro, BV2 microglial cells were treated with LPS (200 ng/mL) or PBS for 30 or 45 min, followed by treatment with BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) for 45 min, 5.5 h, or 23.5 h. Protein extraction, protein quantification, and Western blotting analysis (using 10 or 20 µg of protein per lane), including immunodetection and image analysis, were then performed as previously described [28]. Detailed information on the primary and secondary antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

ROS production

To evaluate the effects of BMS-754807 treatment on LPS-induced intracellular ROS levels, BV2 microglial cells were stimulated with LPS (200 ng/mL) or PBS for 30 min, followed by BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) treatment for 23.5 h. After discarding the media, the cells were stained using the DCFDA/H2DCFDA-Cellular ROS Assay Kit (Cat. no. ab113851, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) as instructed by the manufacturer. Finally, the intensity of DCF fluorescence was measured using a fluorescence plate reader with excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 535 nm, respectively.

Nuclear fractionation

To assess the effect of BMS-754807 treatment on LPS-induced nuclear STAT3 phosphorylation, BV2 microglial cells were stimulated with LPS (200 ng/mL) or PBS for 30 min followed by treatment with BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) for 5.5 h. Then, nuclear fractionation was performed as previously described [29]. The nuclear fraction was analyzed by Western blotting with anti-p-STAT3Y705, anti-p-STAT3S727, or anti-PCNA antibodies. Detailed information on the primary and secondary antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Immunofluorescence staining

To validate the effects of BMS-754807 administration on LPS-evoked STAT3 phosphorylation, immunofluorescence staining was performed. BV2 microglial cells were stimulated with LPS (200 ng/ml) or PBS and then treated with BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) for 5.5 h. Next, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde containing 0.1% (w/v) sucrose for 10 min and incubated with anti-p-STAT3Y705, anti-p-STAT3S727, and anti-CD11b antibodies in GDB buffer overnight at 4 °C. The cells were washed 3 times with PBS, incubated with Alexa 555- or Alexa 488-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT, washed 3 times with PBS, and mounted with DAPI-containing solution (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA). Images of the cells were obtained by inverted fluorescence microscopy and analyzed as previously described [29]. Detailed information on the primary and secondary antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Animals

5xFAD mice (a mouse model of AD) exhibit differential pathology between sexes, with female 5xFAD mice showing greater variability and more severe pathology than males [30]. In addition, hormonal fluctuations in females may influence AD progression and thereby increase experimental variability. Therefore, to minimize physiological and behavioral variability, only male mice were used in the present study.

Wild-type mice

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6N mice (wild-type (WT); Orient-Bio Company, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) were housed as previously described and randomly assigned to three experimental groups: vehicle, LPS, or BMS-754807 + LPS [26]. These groups were subsequently subjected to two independent experiments. The first experiment was designed to examine whether daily treatment with BMS-754807 for 5 days modulates microglial/astroglial activation under acute immune stimulation (single higher-dose LPS injection). For this experiment, WT mice were injected (i.p.) with BMS-754807 (10 mg/kg) or vehicle (5% DMSO + 10% polyethylene glycol (PEG) 300 + 20% Tween 80 + 65% DW) daily for 5 days. On day 5, the mice were challenged with LPS (10 mg/kg) or PBS (i.p.) 30 min after the BMS-754807 or vehicle injection. The mice were sacrificed 8 h after the LPS or PBS administration on day 5. The second experiment investigated the effects of BMS-754807 on microglial/astroglial activation and neuroinflammatory dynamics, STAT3/NF-κB signaling, and NLRP3 inflammasome activation induced by repeated immune stimulation (daily LPS injections for 3 days at a lower dose). To test this, WT mice were injected (i.p.) with BMS-754807 (20 mg/kg) or vehicle daily for 7 days. On days 5—7, the mice were challenged with LPS (2.5 mg/kg) or PBS (i.p.) 30 min after the BMS-754807 or vehicle injection. The mice were sacrificed 8 h after the final LPS or PBS administration on day 7, a time point previously shown to correspond to the early neuroinflammatory phase characterized by robust cytokine induction and glial activation in the mouse brain [31–34].

5xFAD mice

5xFAD mice [Stock No. 34848-JAX; B6. Cg-Tg (APPSwFlLon, PSEN1*M146L*L286V) 6799Vas/Mmjax; Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME] were used as an Aβ-overexpressing AD mouse model. These mice carry five familial AD mutations under the control of the Thy1 promoter. For treatment, 3.5-month-old male 5xFAD mice were injected (i.p.) daily with BMS-754807 (10 or 20 mg/kg) or vehicle for 14 days. The mice were sacrificed 3 h after the final injection of BMS-754807 or vehicle.

PS19 mice

PS19 mice [Stock No. 008169-JAX; B6; C3-Tg (Prnp-MAPT*P301S) PS19Vle/J; Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME] were used as an AD mouse model of tauopathy. These mice express human mutant tau (P301S) under the control of the prion protein (Prnp) promoter. For treatment, 3.5-month-old male PS19 mice were injected (i.p.) daily with BMS-754807 (20 mg/kg) or vehicle for 14 days. The mice were sacrificed 3 h after the final injection of BMS-754807 or vehicle.

Western blotting in WT mice and 5xFAD mice

To investigate the effects of BMS-754807 on IGF-1R (on-target) phosphorylation and LPS/Aβ-induced downstream signaling or transcription factor expression, the cortical and hippocampal regions were dissected from WT mice and 5xFAD mice that had been injected with BMS-754807 and/or LPS. The dissected brain tissues were homogenized and lysed as described previously [33]. After centrifugation of the brain lysate at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 20 min, the supernatant was collected and stored at −20 °C until analysis. Western blotting was performed using 10 µg of protein, and the intensity of the immunoblots was analyzed using Fusion-Capt Software (VILBER LOURMAT, Collégien, France), with normalization to GAPDH, β-actin, or PCNA. Detailed information on the primary and secondary antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Immunofluorescence staining in WT mice, 5xFAD mice, and PS19 mice

To assess whether BMS-754807 treatment affects cytotoxicity, LPS/Aβ/tau-mediated neuroinflammatory responses, Aβ/tau pathology, and neuronal integrity, 30-μm-thick brain slices were prepared from WT, 5xFAD, or PS19 mice as described previously [33]. After permeabilization in PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 and 10% normal goat serum (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) for 2 h at RT, the sections were immunostained with primary antibodies at 4 °C for 1–3 days, washed, and incubated with Alexa 594- or Alexa 488-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at RT. Tissue mounting and fluorescence image acquisition were then performed as previously described [29]. For the quantification of immunofluorescence images, four to six brain sections per mouse were selected and analyzed at 400-μm intervals across the brain region of interest. The quantified values from the four to six sections were averaged to obtain a single mean value per mouse for statistical analysis. Thus, each mouse was treated as one independent biological replicate. Detailed information on the primary and secondary antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Real-time PCR (qPCR) in WT mice and 5xFAD mice

To examine the effects of BMS-754807 administration on LPS/Aβ-induced proinflammatory cytokine levels, neuroinflammation-associated molecular targets, and glial dynamics in vivo, WT mice were injected (i.p.) with BMS-754807 (20 mg/kg) or vehicle daily for 7 days. On days 5—7, the mice were challenged with LPS (2.5 mg/kg) or PBS (i.p.) 30 min after the BMS-754807 or vehicle injection. In addition, 5xFAD mice and PS19 mice were treated with BMS-754807 (20 mg/kg) or vehicle daily for 14 days. After the final injection, cortical and hippocampal tissues were dissected, and total RNA was extracted and analyzed by real-time qPCR as described previously [29]. The mRNA expression of each target was normalized to the Ct values for Gapdh, and the fold change was calculated relative to expression in vehicle-treated control mice.

IL-1β ELISA in WT mice and 5xFAD mice

To assess whether BMS-754807 treatment affects LPS/Aβ-induced proinflammatory cytokine IL-1β protein levels, WT mice or 5xFAD mice were treated with BMS-754807 and/or LPS, and cortical and hippocampal tissues were dissected, homogenized, and lysed as described previously [33]. After centrifugation of the lysate at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 20 min, protein assay reagents (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) were used to determine the protein concentration in the supernatant. Then, IL-1β protein levels were measured using an IL-1β ELISA kit (88—7013A-88, Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions as described previously [33].

siRNA transfection in BV2 microglial cells

To investigate whether BMS-754807 treatment downregulates LPS-induced proinflammatory responses in an Igf-1r-dependent (on-target) manner in vitro, BV2 microglial cells were transfected with scramble or Igf-1r small interfering RNA (siRNA, 40 nM) for 41 h as previously described [34]. Next, the cells were starved in serum-free media for 1 h and then treated with LPS (200 ng/ml) or PBS for 30 min, followed by BMS-754807 (2.5 μM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) for 5.5 h. Finally, the cells were harvested, and real-time PCR was performed to determine the efficiency of Igf-1r knockdown. After confirming Igf-1r siRNA transfection, proinflammatory cytokine mRNA levels were measured by real-time PCR.

To examine whether BMS-754807 treatment downregulates LPS-induced neuroinflammatory responses in an NLRP3-dependent manner in vitro, Nlrp3 gene expression in BV2 microglial cells was silenced by transfection with scramble or Nlrp3 siRNA (40 nM) for 41 h as previously described [34]. The cells were then incubated in serum-free media for 1 h, treated with LPS (200 ng/ml) or PBS for 30 min, and treated with BMS-754807 (2.5 μM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) for 5.5 h. Finally, the cells were harvested, and real-time PCR was performed to determine Nlrp3 knockdown efficiency. After validating NLRP3 siRNA transfection, proinflammatory cytokine mRNA levels were measured by real-time PCR.

hiPSC culture

Healthy hiPSCs were generated from fibroblasts of a 75-year-old female donor unaffected by AD (Coriell #AG09173, Coriell Institute, Camden, NJ, USA) by Dr. Yankner’s laboratory at Harvard Medical School as previously reported [35]. These cells were maintained in mTeSR1 medium (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) on plates coated with Geltrex (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), with medium replaced daily. An isogenic AD hiPSC line harboring the APPswe mutation (APP KM670/671NL) was generated from control iPSCs (Coriell #GM23720) using CRISPR/Cas9 as described previously [28]. Differentiation protocols for hiPSC-derived excitatory neuron (iNEU) and hiPSC-derived microglia-like cells (iMGLs) are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Real-time PCR (qPCR) in hiPSC-derived iMGLs

To investigate the effect of BMS-754807 treatment on LPS-mediated proinflammatory responses in human microglia, hiPSC-derived iMGLs were treated with BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (0.1% DMSO) for 24 h, followed by stimulation with LPS (1 µg/ml) or DW for 24 h. RNA was extracted using Qiazol (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and cDNA was synthesized with Primescript™ Reverse Transcriptase (Takara Bio, San Jose, CA, USA) in the presence of recombinant RNase inhibitor (Takara Bio, San Jose, CA, USA). The cDNA was used in RT-qPCR with iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) in a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using a 40-cycle program. Detailed information on primer sequences for real-time qPCR is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Immunofluorescence staining of hiPSCs-derived iNEUs and iMGLs

To investigate whether BMS-754807 treatment affects LPS-mediated neuroinflammation or Aβ/tau pathology, hiPSC-derived iMGLs were treated with BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (0.1% DMSO) for 24 h and then challenged with LPS (1 µg/ml) or DW for 24 h. In addition, hiPSC-derived iNEUs were treated with BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (0.1% DMSO) for 48 h. Next, the cells were stained following the protocol provided in the supplementary materials and mounted with ProLong Diamond antifade mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Imaging was performed using an LSM800 (Zeiss, Jena, Germany) or a STELLARIS 5 (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) confocal microscope, and images were analyzed using Fiji (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and AIVIA 15.0.0 (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Detailed information on the primary and secondary antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Cognitive behavioral tests in 5xFAD mice

Y-maze

To examine the effect of BMS-754807 treatment on short-term spatial working memory, the Y-maze test was performed. To test this, 3.5-month-old 5xFAD mice were injected with BMS-754807 (20 mg/kg, i.p.) or vehicle daily for 2 weeks. For the Y-maze test, one mouse was placed in one of the three arms (35 cm × 7 cm × 15 cm) of the maze, which met at an angle of 120°, and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Spontaneous alternations were recorded and analyzed using SMART video tracking software (Panlab, Barcelona, Spain). The alternation percentage was calculated by dividing the number of alternations by the number of alternation triads.

Novel object recognition test

To evaluate whether BMS-754807 administration affects recognition memory, the novel object recognition test was performed. For this experiment, 3.5-month-old 5xFAD mice were treated with BMS-754807 (20 mg/kg, i.p.) or vehicle daily for 2 weeks. In the training phase, a mouse was placed in an open-field box (40 cm × 40 cm × 25 cm) containing two identical objects for 5 min. Between trials, odor cues were eliminated by thoroughly swabbing the apparatus and objects with 70% ethanol. Twenty-four hours after the training phase, the mouse was returned to the same apparatus containing one familiar object and one novel object for a 5-min retention testing phase. The locations of the two objects in the apparatus were counterbalanced. The trials were recorded, and the time of exploratory behavior, defined as pointing of the mouse's nose toward an object, was manually counted. Object/novelty preference (%) was calculated using the formula [Preference (%) of object = TNovel/(TFamiliar + TNovel) × 100], where Tnovel is the time of exploration of the novel object, and TFamiliar is the time of exploration of the familiar object.

BACE1 activity in 5xFAD mice

To examine the underlying mechanisms by which BMS-754807 treatment reduces Aβ plaque deposition in 5xFAD mice, the activity of BACE1, a β-secretase involved in amyloidogenic APP proteolytic processing, was analyzed. For this experiment, 5xFAD mice were injected with BMS-754807 (20 mg/kg, i.p.) or vehicle daily for 2 weeks. Three hours after the final BMS-754807 or vehicle injection, the cortical and hippocampal tissues were dissected, homogenized, and lysed. The supernatant obtained after three sequential centrifugations of the lysate at 20,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C was used to analyze BACE1 activity. Specifically, the SensoLyte® 520 β-Secretase (BACE1) activity assay kit (cat. no. AS-71144, AnaSpec, Fremont, CA, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cortical and hippocampal homogenates, along with a β-secretase-specific fluorogenic substrate, were loaded into a 96-well plate and incubated for 3 h. Then, the stop solution was added, and the fluorescence intensity was measured using excitation and emission wavelengths of 490 and 520 nm, respectively.

Aβ ELISA in hiPSC-derived excitatory neurons (iNEUs)

To investigate the effect of BMS-754807 treatment on Aβ levels in iNEUs, AD iNEUs were treated with BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (0.1% DMSO) for 48 h. Then, the iNEU culture medium was collected, and 1 mM PMSF (Tocris, Bristol, UK) was added. The levels of Aβ40 and Aβ42 in the culture medium were measured using an Aβ40 ELISA kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) and an Aβ42 ELISA kit (FUJIFILM Wako Chemical, Osaka, Japan), respectively, according to the manufacturers' instructions. Prior to analysis, the culture medium was diluted 1:4 in the standard diluent. The ELISA data were normalized to the total cell numbers.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used to create graphs and perform statistical analyses. Data are described as individual data points and the mean ± SEM. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test in GraphPad Prism. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied to normally distributed data (p > 0.05), whereas a non-parametric test was used when normality was not satisfied (p ≤ 0.05). For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc tests was used for normally distributed data. Asterisks indicate significance: * p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, and **** < 0.0001. Detailed statistical results are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

Results

BMS-754807 decreases LPS-induced proinflammatory cytokine levels and oxidative stress in BV2 microglial cells

Prior to investigating the effect of BMS-754807 on LPS-induced proinflammatory responses, we first assessed its cytotoxicity in vitro. We then examined whether BMS-754807 treatment modulates the phosphorylation of IGF-1R, which is the on-target of BMS-754807. To address this, BV2 microglial cells were treated with BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) for 6 h, and Western blotting was conducted with anti-p-IGF-1RY1135/1136 and anti-β-actin antibodies. Next, we assessed whether BMS-754807 pretreatment (prevention condition) or posttreatment (curative condition) differentially regulates LPS-mediated proinflammatory cytokine expression in vitro. The results of qPCR analysis showed that pretreatment with 2.5 or 5 µM BMS-754807 significantly decreased LPS-induced Cox-2 and Tnf-α mRNA levels but not Il-1β mRNA levels in BV2 microglial cells (Fig. 1C, D). In addition, posttreatment with 2.5 or 5 µM BMS-754807 significantly diminished LPS-induced Cox-2, Il-1β and Tnf-α mRNA levels in BV2 microglial cells (Fig. 1E, F).

Given that LPS-mediated proinflammatory responses are closely associated with oxidative stress, including ROS production [5, 6], we evaluated whether BMS-754807 treatment modulates LPS-induced ROS and HO-1 expression. Notably, posttreatment with BMS-754807 significantly reduced LPS-induced intracellular ROS production and HO-1 expression in BV2 microglial cells (Fig. 1G, H). These data suggest that BMS-754807 treatment could effectively mitigate LPS-induced oxidative stress in vitro.

BMS-754807 inhibits LPS-induced AKT and STAT3 signaling in BV2 microglial cells

Since AKT, P38, and STAT3 are major downstream signaling molecules for LPS-induced inflammatory responses [36], we investigated the effects of BMS-754807 treatment on LPS-mediated phosphorylation of AKT and P38 in vitro. Western blotting results showed that BMS-754807 treatment significantly inhibited LPS-induced AKT phosphorylation but not P38 phosphorylation in BV2 microglial cells (Fig. 1I-J). Moreover, Western blotting of the nuclear fraction demonstrated that BMS-754807 treatment significantly diminished LPS-induced nuclear STAT3 phosphorylation at both Tyr705 and Ser727 in BV2 microglial cells (Fig. 1K-M). To confirm these findings, we performed immunocytochemical staining with anti-p-STAT3Y705, anti-p-STAT3S727, and anti-CD11b antibodies and found that BMS-754807 treatment significantly decreased LPS-induced nuclear STAT3 phosphorylation at Tyr705 and Ser727 in BV2 microglial cells (Fig. 1N-Q). These results suggest that BMS-754807 treatment suppresses LPS-induced AKT and STAT3 signaling in BV2 microglial cells.

BMS-754807 attenuates LPS-induced upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines in primary microglia and astrocytes

Since BMS-754807 treatment significantly reduced LPS-mediated inflammatory responses in BV2 microglial cells, we further assessed its anti-inflammatory effects against LPS in primary glial cells. For this experiment, primary microglia or astrocytes were treated with LPS (200 ng/ml) or PBS for 30 min followed by BMS-754807 (2.5 µM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) for 5.5 h. Real-time PCR analysis showed that BMS-754807 treatment significantly reduced LPS-induced Cox-2, Il-1β, and Tnf-α mRNA levels in primary microglia (Fig. 1R). However, in primary astrocytes, BMS-754807 treatment significantly diminished LPS-induced Il-1β mRNA expression but not Cox-2 or Tnf-α mRNA expression (Fig. 1S). These results suggest that BMS-754807 differentially downregulates LPS-evoked proinflammatory cytokine expression in primary microglia and astrocytes.

We then assessed whether this cognitive improvement involves structural neuronal changes. Next, we investigated whether BMS-754807 modulates Aβ pathology in 5xFAD mice and found that 10 mg/kg BMS-754807 treatment significantly decreased 4G8-positive Aβ plaque numbers in the hippocampal CA1 region but not in the hippocampal DG region or cortex (Supplementary Fig. 8). Given that Aβ pathology exacerbates tau hyperphosphorylation, we further evaluated the effects of BMS-754807 on tau hyperphosphorylation in 5xFAD mice. We found that BMS-754807 treatment did not affect tau hyperphosphorylation at Ser202/Thr205, Thr212/Ser214, or Thr231 compared with vehicle treatment (Supplementary Fig. 9A-I). We next examined the in vivo efficacy of BMS-754807 against tau pathology in transgenic PS19 mice. We found that BMS-754807 treatment significantly reduced tau hyperphosphorylation at Thr231 in the cortex and hippocampal DG region compared to vehicle treatment (Fig. 14E-G). However, BMS-754807 administration did not affect tau hyperphosphorylation at Ser202/Thr205 and Thr212/Ser214 in PS19 mice (Supplementary Fig. 11).

To examine the underlying mechanisms of tau pathology in PS19 mice, the phosphorylation of various tau kinases was assessed by immunofluorescence staining. Importantly, BMS-754807 treatment significantly downregulated CaMKIIα phosphorylation at T286 in both the cortex and hippocampus of PS19 mice compared to vehicle treatment (Fig. 14 H-J). However, BMS-754807 administration did not alter the phosphorylation of other tau kinases, including GSK-3β, CDK-5, DYRK1A, and P38 (Supplementary Fig. 12—13). Taken together, these results suggest that BMS-754807 selectively ameliorates tau hyperphosphorylation in human and mouse models, likely by inhibiting CaMKIIα.

Discussion

Several studies have demonstrated that IGF-1 and IGF-1R are involved in cancer cell proliferation, tumor invasion, and inflammatory responses in vitro and in vivo [17, 38, 39]. However, the effects of the IGF-1R/IR inhibitor BMS-754807 on neuroinflammation, Aβ/tau pathology, and cognitive function and the associated molecular mechanisms have not been fully investigated. In the present study, we demonstrated that the effects of BMS-754807 administration on LPS-induced proinflammatory cytokine expression, oxidative stress, glial hyperactivation, and AKT, STAT3, and NF-κB signaling occur through IGF-1R and NLRP3 in BV2 microglial cells, primary glia, hiPSC-derived microglia, and WT mice. In 5xFAD mice, BMS-754807 treatment reduced IGF-1R phosphorylation, gliosis, and proinflammatory cytokine levels; increased Sirt1/3 expression; and downregulated AKT, P38, and STAT3 signaling. In addition, BMS-754807 treatment improved domain-specific short-term spatial memory and alleviated Aβ pathology by inhibiting BACE1 activity in both 5xFAD mice and hiPSC-derived AD neurons. Finally, BMS-754807 treatment attenuated tau-mediated cortical astrogliosis, tau phosphorylation at specific epitopes (Thr217, Thr181, and Thr231), and tau kinase CaMKIIα phosphorylation in PS19 mice and human iPSC-derived AD neurons. Taken together, these results indicate that BMS-754807 administration mitigates neuroinflammatory responses and AD-related pathology and thus represents a potential disease-modifying strategy across multiple in vitro and in vivo models.

BMS-754807 suppresses LPS/Aβ/tau-induced proinflammatory responses and gliosis

Prolonged exposure to pathogenic stimuli (e.g., LPS, Aβ, and tau aggregates) activates microglia and astrocytes, leading to excessive release of proinflammatory cytokines in the CNS. This response contributes to neuronal dysfunction and neurodegenerative disease progression [40–42]. Interestingly, several studies have implicated IGF-1/IGF-1R signaling in regulating glial activation, although the results have been inconsistent across contexts. For example, pharmacological inhibition of IGF-1R reduces levels of the proinflammatory mediators IL-6 and INF-γ in splenic T cells of arthritic mice [43], whereas IGF-1R activation attenuates MPP+-induced COX-2 and iNOS levels in primary astrocytes [20]. In addition, genetic knockdown of IGF-1R decreases hippocampal microgliosis in arthritic mice [44]. Moreover, APPswe/IGF-1R± mice (APPswe/Presenilin1ΔE9; IGF-1R± mice) exhibit reduced Aβ-evoked astroglial activation [45, 46]. These contrasting findings suggest that the role of IGF-1R in inflammation may depend on the cell type and specific pathogenic stimulus.

We therefore investigated whether BMS-754807 differentially regulates LPS-induced proinflammatory responses across glial cell types. We found that BMS-754807 administration markedly reduced LPS-induced proinflammatory cytokine mRNA expression (Cox-2, Il-1β, and Tnf-α) in microglial models (BV2, primary, and iMGLs) (Fig. 1 and Fig. 11). By contrast, in primary astrocytes, BMS-754807 treatment significantly decreased only Il-1β mRNA expression without altering Cox-2 or Tnf-α mRNA levels (Fig. 1). Because microglia are the principal innate immune effector cells in the CNS [47], they are likely directly responsible for the rapid amplification of cytokine production following LPS stimulation. Astrocytes frequently acquire reactive phenotypes in response to microglia-derived cytokines [48]. Consequently, the broader cytokine suppression observed in microglia compared with astrocytes may reflect the absence of essential microglial-astrocytic crosstalk under monoculture conditions. Future studies will investigate whether BMS-754807 exerts more comprehensive effects on astrocytes in microglia–astrocyte co-culture systems. In addition to cell type-specific differences, we observed distinct in vivo effects depending on the stimulus. BMS-754807 significantly attenuated both microgliosis and astrogliosis in LPS-treated WT mice and 5xFAD mice (Fig. 2 and Fig. 7). However, in PS19 mice, BMS-754807 treatment significantly reduced tau-mediated astrogliosis but not microgliosis (Fig. 13 and Supplementary Fig. 10). Because reactive astrogliosis is closely associated with tau hyperphosphorylation and neurofibrillary tangle accumulation [49], this astrocyte-specific effect in PS19 mice may parallel the selective attenuation of tau hyperphosphorylation (e.g., Thr231) (Fig. 14).

BMS-754807 differentially regulates neuroinflammatory dynamics and exhibits region-specific efficacy in mouse models of AD

IGF-1 signaling regulates systemic inflammation by controlling the recruitment and activation of peripheral immune cells [18, 50]. Genetic ablation of IGF-1R markedly reduces lymphocyte, neutrophil, and eosinophil infiltration in models of allergic or pathogen-induced inflammation, implying that IGF-1R is critical for the recruitment and activation of immune cells in vivo [50]. In addition, IGF-1 regulates glial immune surveillance by shifting homeostatic microglia/astrocytes to reactive states in response to pathogenic stimulation (e.g., LPS or Aβ) [51]. Here, we demonstrated that BMS-754807 treatment suppressed LPS-induced microglial/astroglial-associated inflammatory dynamics, including DAM and RA phenotypes and DAM–RA interactions, in WT mice (Fig. 3). However, BMS-754807 selectively suppressed AD-associated astroglial dynamics (Gbp2, Neat1, and Cxcl10) in 5xFAD mice without altering AD-related microglial dynamics (Fig. 9).

Why does BMS-754807 treatment differentially modulate LPS- and Aβ-mediated microglial/astrocyte-associated neuroinflammatory dynamics, despite significantly downregulating both LPS- and Aβ-induced microglial and astroglial activation? One plausible explanation is that under chronic Aβ pathology, astrocytes are more susceptible than microglia to IGF-1R inhibition by BMS-754807. Another possibility is that chronic Aβ exposure induces long-term reprogramming of microglial signaling to sustain NF-κB and related inflammatory pathways through compensatory circuits (e.g., TREM2-APOE or inflammasome inputs), reducing their reliance on IGF-1R [52]. By contrast, astrocytic activation might be more tightly associated with IGF-1R–STAT3 signaling, resulting in selective responsiveness of astrocytes to IGF-1R inhibition. Although we did not assess tau-associated glial inflammatory dynamics in the present study, the differential regulation of astrogliosis and microgliosis observed in PS19 mice suggests that stimulus-specific (i.e., Aβ vs tau) modulation of microglia and astroglial-associated neuroinflammatory dynamics may also occur in this mouse model of tauopathy. Therefore, future studies will determine whether BMS-754807 selectively regulates AD-associated microglial and astroglial neuroinflammatory dynamics in PS19 mice.

These cell-type and stimulus-specific differences in glial dynamics were accompanied by pronounced region-specific efficacy of BMS-754807. Notably, BMS-754807 predominantly reduced on-target activity and AD pathology in the cortex, with comparatively limited effects in the hippocampus. For instance, the reductions in IGF-1R phosphorylation, BACE1-mediated Aβ plaque accumulation, astroglial neuroinflammatory dynamics, and neuroinflammation-associated downstream signaling in 5xFAD mice treated with BMS-754807 were largely restricted to the cortex (Fig. 7, Fig. 9, Fig. 10 and Fig. 12). Similarly, in PS19 mice, BMS-754807 administration suppressed GFAP-positive astrogliosis only in the cortical region (Fig. 13). This regional divergence raises the question of why pharmacological IGF-1R inhibition exerts stronger effects in the cortex. This discrepancy might reflect differential baseline IGF-1R expression levels in brain regions (cortex vs hippocampus) or varying degrees of blood–brain barrier permeability to BMS-754807. Furthermore, it is also possible that Aβ/tau pathology is more advanced in the cortex than in the hippocampus, making cortical glia more responsive to IGF-1R inhibition at this specific disease stage, and thus, we will address these issues in future studies.

Parallel modulation of neuroinflammation-associated signaling cascades by BMS-754807

IGF-1R regulates downstream signaling cascades, including AKT, MAPK, STAT3, and NF-κB, which are involved in cellular survival, metabolism, and inflammatory responses [53]. Specifically, IGF-1 directly phosphorylates STAT3 in HEK-293T cells co-transfected with IGF-1R and STAT3, suggesting that IGF-1R can act as an upstream regulator of STAT3 [54]. In addition, STAT3 is activated by LPS stimulation, thereby contributing to the production of proinflammatory cytokines in primary microglia [55]. Conversely, pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 by Stattic significantly reduces LPS-induced proinflammatory responses in the mouse brain [51]. Several studies have indicated that NF-κB is also involved in LPS-induced inflammatory signaling in the CNS. For example, systemic LPS administration enhances NF-κB phosphorylation, Iba-1/GFAP expression, and IL-1β and TNF-α levels in the cerebral cortex in WT mice [54]. IGF-1 treatment also enhances NF-κB phosphorylation and exacerbates inflammation in models of hyperoxia and hyperreactive airway disease [56]. Another study found that pharmacological inhibition of IGF-1R reduces NF-κB expression and blocks its nuclear localization in lens epithelial cells, suggesting that IGF-1R regulates NF-κB signaling [57]. Consistent with previous reports, we found that IGF-1R inhibition suppressed LPS-induced phosphorylation of STAT3 and NF-κB in WT mice and human iMGLs (Fig. 5 and Fig. 11). In 5xFAD mice, however, BMS-754807 treatment inhibited AKT, P38, and STAT3 signaling without altering NF-κB levels (Fig. 10). It is important to note that our current data supports the parallel modulation of these pathways rather than a clearly defined causal linear axis. Given that IGF-1R is positioned upstream of multiple signaling cascades, the simultaneous suppression of AKT, P38, and STAT3 by BMS-754807 treatment in this study reflects a broad attenuation of neuroinflammatory signaling networks rather than a sequential, linear signaling cascade among these kinases. Further evidence is required to establish direct mechanistic coupling between these specific nodes under chronic neuroinflammatory conditions.

BMS-754807 attenuates neuroinflammation by suppressing the IGF-1R/NLRP3 priming axis

The NLRP3 inflammasome is closely regulated by IGF-1R signaling. For example, in HeLa cells, IGF-1 treatment increases intracellular production of ROS, which subsequently activates NLRP3 and, in turn, proinflammatory cytokine production [58]. IGF-1R expression is elevated in orbital fibroblasts from patients with thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy (TAO), and this upregulation is associated with increased NLRP3 expression [59]. Conversely, suppression of IGF-1R expression via miR-143 overexpression or pharmacological inhibition downregulates NLRP3 expression [59]. In addition to regulating the NLRP3 inflammasome, several studies have demonstrated that IGF-1R signaling modulates mitochondrial anti-inflammation-associated molecules. For example, activation of IGF-1 by swimming exercise modulates AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α survival pathways, thereby inhibiting inflammation and apoptosis in a D‑galactose-induced aging mouse model [59]. In LPS-stimulated macrophages, SIRT1 inhibits NF-κB signaling by deacetylating its p65 subunit at lysine 310, thereby reducing proinflammatory responses [60]. Similarly, SIRT3 promotes autophagy-mediated degradation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in THP-1 monocytes, attenuating proinflammatory cytokine release [61]. Together, these observations suggest that IGF-1R regulates neuroinflammation in vitro and in vivo not only by suppressing canonical NLRP3 inflammasome activation but also by enhancing mitochondrial resilience through sirtuin and antioxidant pathways.

In this study, we found that BMS-754807 administration reduced LPS-evoked NLRP3 expression and mRNA levels of essential NLRP3 inflammasome components (Il-1β, Asc, Caspase-1) in microglial models and WT mice (Fig. 4). Moreover, BMS-754807 downregulated LPS-evoked proinflammatory responses in an IGF-1R- and NLRP3-dependent manner in BV2 microglial cells (Fig. 6). Although cleavage of caspase-1 and ASC speck formation were not directly assessed, our findings suggest that BMS-754807 suppresses the priming phase of the NLRP3 inflammasome, which is required for NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Interestingly, while BMS-754807 suppressed NLRP3 priming in models of acute inflammation induced by LPS, it selectively upregulated Sirt1 and Sirt3 in 5xFAD mice, a model of chronic inflammation. It is possible that under acute inflammation, IGF-1R signaling primarily engages ROS generation and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, resulting in rapid and transient immune stimulation by LPS. However, under chronic Aβ pathology, prolonged oxidative stress and persistent cytokine exposure may induce adaptive reprogramming of IGF-1R signaling, shifting its downstream outputs toward mitochondrial metabolic regulation via sirtuins. Collectively, the literature and our findings highlight the need to determine whether IGF-1R inhibition directly regulates NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Thus, future studies will assess NLRP3 inflammasome activation in human/mouse microglia transfected with IGF-1R-targeting siRNA by measuring caspase-1 cleavage or ASC speck formation in the presence/absence of BMS-754807.

Disease-modifying effects of BMS-754807 on learning and memory and Aβ/tau pathology

Chronic neuroinflammation disrupts synaptic plasticity and immediate early gene expression required for memory encoding, leading to spatial memory impairments [62]. Therefore, attenuation of neuroinflammation is associated with improvement of cognitive function in AD models. For example, genetic deletion of Nlrp3 in APP/PS1 mice reduces IL-1β production and improves spatial memory performance [63]. In addition, pharmacological depletion of microglia using the CSF1R inhibitor PLX3397 ameliorates synaptic deficits and enhances cognitive performance in APP/PS1 mice [64]. Consistent with previous findings, we observed that BMS-754807 administration resulted in domain-specific improvements in short-term spatial memory in 5xFAD mice (Fig. 12). Because 5xFAD mice with early-stage inflammation (3.5 months old) do not yet exhibit robust neurodegeneration, these cognitive enhancements are most appropriately framed as functional, disease-modifying outcomes, likely stemming from synaptic recovery and reduced neuroinflammation rather than from definitive structural neuroprotection. Future studies will assess whether BMS-754807 modulates hippocampal synaptic function, structural plasticity, and network dynamics to rescue memory deficits in younger and aged 5xFAD mice and PS19 mice.

Aβ and tau hyperphosphorylation represent the core pathological hallmarks driving synaptic dysfunction and neurodegeneration in AD, and both are regulated by IGF-1 signaling. Previous studies suggest that the influence of IGF-1 on Aβ metabolism is highly context-dependent: while systemic IGF-1 infusion significantly reduces brain Aβ levels in aging rats [65], chronic pathological states, such as sleep restriction, shift IGF-1R signaling toward amyloidogenic APP processing and increased BACE1 activity [66]. Here, we found that pharmacological inhibition of IGF-1R by BMS-754807 effectively suppressed Aβ secretion in human iPSC-derived AD neurons and attenuated Aβ plaque burden in 5xFAD mice by downregulating BACE1 activity (Fig. 12). However, our findings do not establish whether the reduction in BACE1 activity was directly mediated by IGF-1R inhibition or occurred indirectly through modulation of neuroinflammatory pathways. To address this limitation, future studies will employ targeted genetic approaches, including BACE1 knockdown (e.g., BACE1 shRNA), to determine whether the effects of IGF-1R inhibition on Aβ pathology are directly mediated by BACE1. Furthermore, NLRP3-deficient (Nlrp3⁻/⁻) 5xFAD mice will be utilized to clarify whether loss of NLRP3 inflammasome signaling alters Aβ plaque deposition and BACE1 activity.

Alongside these anti-amyloidogenic effects, BMS-754807 treatment reduced tau phosphorylation at Thr217 and Thr181 in human AD neurons and selectively decreased tau phosphorylation at Thr231 in tau transgenic PS19 mice (Fig. 14). Notably, this effect was accompanied by reduced tau kinase CaMKIIα phosphorylation in PS19 mice, whereas other major tau kinases (i.e., GSK-3β, CDK-5, DYRK1A, and P38) were not significantly altered by treatment with BMS-754807 (Supplementary Figs. 12—13). Recent studies have demonstrated that AKT decreases GSK-3β activity in 5xFAD mice by phosphorylating GSK-3β at Ser9, thereby reducing tau hyperphosphorylation [67, 68]. Because BMS-754807 significantly reduced AKT signaling in LPS-treated BV2 microglial cells and 5xFAD mice (Fig. 1 and Fig. 10), a potential concern is that BMS-754807-mediated AKT inhibition could alleviate the inhibitory phosphorylation of GSK-3β at Ser9, thereby exacerbating GSK-3β-driven tau phosphorylation. However, in the present study, BMS-754807 did not alter p-GSK-3βSer9 expression or increase phosphorylation of GSK-3β at Y216 (p-GSK-3α/βY216), a site associated with enhanced kinase activity, in PS19 mice (Supplementary Fig. 12). These findings suggest that the inhibitory effect of BMS-754807 on tau hyperphosphorylation is not mediated by the canonical AKT-GSK-3β axis in PS19 mice. Instead, the selective reduction of CaMKIIα activity implies that CaMKIIα may be a more proximal regulator of tau phosphorylation under pharmacological IGF-1R inhibition by BMS-754807 in this tau-overexpressing AD model.

In contrast to the findings in PS19 mice, BMS-754807 treatment did not alter tau phosphorylation and GSK-3α/β activity in 3.5-month-old 5xFAD mice (Supplementary Fig. 9). Previous studies have reported that tau hyperphosphorylation progresses in an age-dependent manner in 5xFAD mice, with robust p-Tau (Ser202/Thr205) expression emerging at 7–8 months of age (late stage of AD) [69, 70]. The 3.5-month-old 5xFAD mice used in this study represent an early stage of AD with relatively minimal tau hyperphosphorylation. Thus, the lack of tau modulation by BMS-754807 administration in 5xFAD mice may reflect the early pathological stage rather than an absence of therapeutic potential. Future studies will evaluate the effects of BMS-754807 on tau hyperphosphorylation in aged 5xFAD mice (e.g., 7–8 months old).

Clinical implications and therapeutic potential of the IGF-1R/IR inhibitor BMS-754807

The clinical and therapeutic relevance of IGF-1R/IR inhibition in AD is determined by the mode, magnitude, and cell-type specificity of signaling modulation rather than by receptor suppression alone. Several studies have revealed that glial cell-specific genetic ablation of IR/IGF-1R significantly influences AD pathology. For instance, microglia- or astrocyte-specific IR/IGF-1R knockout in 5xFAD mice increases amyloid plaque burden, enhances neuroinflammatory responses, and impairs cognitive performance [71]. In contrast to the results observed in these genetic models, we found that pharmacological inhibition of IGF-1R/IR by BMS-754807 significantly suppressed Aβ levels and neuroinflammatory responses in human AD neurons and microglia (Fig. 11 and Fig. 14). Moreover, administration of BMS-754807 (10 or 20 mg/kg) daily for 2 weeks attenuated Aβ-, tau-, and LPS-induced neuroinflammatory responses and improved short-term spatial memory in 5xFAD, PS19, and LPS-treated WT mice without causing neurotoxicity. Because IGF-1R signaling has intrinsic neurotrophic functions, including promotion of neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive maintenance through PI3K/AKT-dependent mechanisms [16, 17], complete ablation of IR/IGF-1R signaling may disrupt essential homeostatic and neuroprotective pathways in the CNS, potentially exacerbating AD-related pathology and decreasing viability. Therefore, compared with complete genetic ablation (e.g., IR/IGF-1R knockout mice), dose- and duration-controlled pharmacological modulation of IR/IGF-1R by BMS-754807 treatment may offer a more balanced disease-modifying strategy for mitigating AD-associated pathology while preserving essential neurotrophic functions of IGF-1R in the CNS.

While our findings support the therapeutic potential of pharmacological modulation of IR/IGF-1R, a comprehensive mechanistic interpretation requires the explicit acknowledgment of several limitations. First, BMS-754807 exhibits multiple off-target interactions with various kinases, including TrkA/TrkB, CAMKK2, GSK-3β, and AurA/B. Among these, GSK-3β and Trk family receptors have been implicated in tau phosphorylation, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive function [72–74]. Although we confirmed that GSK-3β phosphorylation was not altered in 5xFAD and PS19 mice (Supplementary Fig. 9 and 12), we cannot entirely exclude the possibility that other off-target interactions contributed to the observed anti-inflammatory and cognitive benefits, either independently or synergistically with on-target IGF-1R blockade. Future studies will systematically deconvolute these on- and off-target network effects. Second, only male mice were used in the in vivo studies. Given the well-documented sex differences in AD progression, microglial reactivity, and systemic immune responses, the efficacy and neuroinflammatory profile of IGF-1R inhibition may vary significantly between sexes. Future preclinical investigations must evaluate this intervention in female cohorts to ensure the broad clinical translatability of this therapeutic strategy.

Conclusions

This study is the first to examine whether BMS-754807, a dual IGF-1R/IR inhibitor, modulates neuroinflammatory dynamics and Aβ/tau pathology and to elucidate its mechanisms of action in vitro and in vivo. BMS-754807 treatment suppressed LPS-induced upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines and the activation of downstream AKT and STAT3 signaling through IGF-1R and the NLRP3 priming phase in BV2 microglial cells, primary microglia, and hiPSC-derived microglia. In WT mice, BMS-754807 treatment decreased microgliosis/astrogliosis, neuroinflammatory dynamics, NLRP3 expression, and phosphorylation of STAT3 and NF-κB. In 5xFAD mice, BMS-754807 administration significantly downregulated glial neuroinflammatory dynamics and downstream AKT, P38, and STAT3 signaling while increasing SIRT1 and SIRT3 expression. Additionally, BMS-754807 treatment improved short-term spatial memory and decreased Aβ burden via reduced BACE1 activity in 5xFAD mice and reduced Aβ secretion in hiPSC-derived AD neurons. Moreover, BMS-754807 reduced astrogliosis, tau hyperphosphorylation, and CaMKIIα activation in PS19 mice and decreased tau hyperphosphorylation in human AD neurons (Fig. 15). Taken together, these results support the potential of BMS-754807 as a novel therapeutic agent for neuroinflammation and Aβ/tau-related diseases. Although we observed no overt cytotoxicity in our models, the potential risks of chronic IGF-1R inhibition for neuronal metabolism and viability cannot be overlooked, given the neurotrophic functions of IGF-1 signaling.
