When neck pain isn’t muscular: A case report on TIPIC syndrome responding to NSAIDs
Authors: Habib Bellamlih, Ayman El Farouki, Soufiane Belabbes, Brahim Zinoun, Taoufik Africha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transient Perivascular Inflammation of the Carotid Artery (TIPIC) syndrome is an infrequent and often underdiagnosed etiology of acute unilateral neck pain. Characterized by localized inflammation of the carotid artery and its surrounding tissues, TIPIC syndrome typically presents with tenderness over the carotid bifurcation. The condition is self-limiting and generally responds well to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. This report discusses a 24-year-old male with sudden-onset right-sided neck pain, diagnosed with TIPIC syndrome through imaging studies, and effectively managed with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.

Introduction

Transient Perivascular Inflammation of the Carotid Artery (TIPIC) syndrome, previously known as carotidynia or Fay syndrome, was first described in 1927 [1].

In 1988, the International Headache Society (IHS) recognized carotidynia as a distinct clinical entity, classifying it as an atypical headache syndrome within the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD) [2]. The diagnostic criteria included : 1) Unilateral neck pain, potentially radiating to the head, 2) Focal tenderness over the carotid artery, with possible edema or increased pulsation, 3) Absence of structural lesions, 4) Spontaneous recovery within 14 days of symptom onset [3,4]. However, in 2004, the IHS excluded carotidynia from the ICHD due to controversial evidence regarding its diagnostic criteria. Consequently, carotidynia was reclassified as a nonspecific symptom associated with various conditions, including vasculitis, carotid dissection, sialadenitis, trigeminal neuralgia, and oropharyngeal infections [[5], [6], [7]].

Recent advancements in imaging techniques have revealed characteristic radiological findings associated with carotidynia, suggesting it may represent an isolated diagnosis. This condition is now referred to as Transient Perivascular Inflammation of the Carotid Artery (TIPIC) syndrome [8].

TIPIC syndrome remains a rare and underdiagnosed disorder. Reported cases indicate a slight female preponderance and a higher incidence in individuals in their fifth and sixth decades of life. The etiology is believed to involve a transient inflammatory process affecting the vessel wall, particularly the adventitia, and the pericarotid tissues. However, the exact pathogenesis remains poorly understood, and ongoing debates persist regarding whether carotidynia and TIPIC syndrome are identical entities [9,10].

Case report

A 24-year-old previously healthy male presented with a 3-day history of progressively worsening, non-radiating, constant right-sided neck pain. The pain was unresponsive to over-the-counter paracetamol. He denied any recent infections, trauma, or dental issues.

Physical examination revealed localized tenderness over the right carotid artery at the level of the upper border of the thyroid cartilage. No palpable masses or bruits were noted. Mild right cervical lymphadenopathy was present. Vital signs were within normal limits.

Laboratory tests showed mild eosinophilia (4.1%) and thrombocytopenia (platelets 132 × 10⁹/L). The white blood cell count was 6.7 × 10⁹/L, C-reactive protein (CRP) was 12 mg/L, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 19 mm/hr. Given the absence of significant inflammatory markers and the localized nature of the symptoms, empirical antibiotic therapy was not initiated.

Subsequent imaging studies were performed. A focused ultrasound of the neck revealed increased echogenicity surrounding the distal common carotid artery, carotid bulb, and proximal external and internal carotid arteries on the right side. Perivascular tissue planes appeared disrupted compared to the contralateral side. No plaques, vessel wall thickening, or flow abnormalities were noted (Fig. 1). A subsequent CT scan of the neck confirmed soft tissue thickening around the same vascular segments (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). There were no signs of dissection, aneurysm, or neoplastic processes. Based on the clinical presentation and imaging findings, a diagnosis of TIPIC syndrome was established. The patient was started on NSAID therapy. Although adherence was inconsistent due to gastrointestinal side effects, his symptoms resolved completely within two weeks of onset. A follow-up ultrasound at six weeks demonstrated normal perivascular tissue planes and no residual echogenicity. The patient remained symptom-free during a three-month follow-up period.

Discussion

TIPIC syndrome, formerly referred to as carotidynia, typically presents as acute, unilateral neck pain accompanied by localized tenderness over the carotid bifurcation. Involvement of the pericarotid sympathetic plexus may explain associated referred symptoms such as head and neck pain, vertigo, and, in some cases, diplopia. Though the clinical presentation may mimic other pathologies, TIPIC syndrome remains a distinct inflammatory condition of the carotid artery and surrounding tissues [11].

The exact pathogenesis of TIPIC syndrome remains unclear, largely due to the absence of tissue biopsy in routine diagnosis. However, one histological study by Upton et al. [12] on a patient who developed symptoms prior to elective carotid endarterectomy revealed lymphocytic infiltration, scattered neutrophils, vascular proliferation, and early fibrosis, suggesting a chronic inflammatory process.

Laboratory investigations in TIPIC syndrome are often nonspecific, and inflammatory markers such as ESR and CRP are usually within normal limits or only mildly elevated.

Imaging is pivotal in the diagnosis of TIPIC syndrome. High-resolution ultrasound typically reveals hypoechoic mural thickening and perivascular echogenicity at the carotid bifurcation, correlating with the point of maximal tenderness. Importantly, these changes often occur without any luminal narrowing, and Doppler flow is typically preserved. Although luminal narrowing has been described in isolated cases, it is not a consistent feature [13].

MRI proves to be an invaluable second-line option when ultrasound results are inconclusive. While contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) may occasionally reveal similar findings, MRI is typically the preferred technique due to its superior soft-tissue resolution and enhanced ability to characterize tissue composition [10,13].

It is essential to rule out other causes of carotid pain, such as carotid dissection, aneurysm, large vessel vasculitis, or autoimmune disease, before establishing a diagnosis of TIPIC syndrome. This makes TIPIC largely a diagnosis of exclusion [14].

Currently, there are no standardized treatment guidelines. NSAIDs are the most commonly used first-line therapy and are often effective in relieving symptoms, as seen in our case [13]. In rare instances, corticosteroids may be required, particularly in NSAID-refractory patients. Antibiotics are occasionally administered early on, typically when an infectious etiology is initially suspected. Adjunctive therapies such as heat application may offer symptomatic relief [15].

TIPIC syndrome is usually self-limiting, with resolution typically occurring within several weeks. However, recurrence has been reported in a minority of cases, emphasizing the need for awareness and follow-up. A recent PET-CT study showed no residual inflammation by the third week, supporting the transient nature of the condition [13].

Notably, the interdisciplinary nature of TIPIC syndrome can create challenges in management. This ambiguity reinforces the need for general practitioners and emergency physicians to be familiar with TIPIC syndrome as a differential diagnosis in patients presenting with unexplained unilateral neck pain.

Conclusion

TIPIC syndrome is a rare but important consideration in the differential diagnosis of focal neck pain, particularly in the absence of systemic signs. It represents a transient inflammatory process affecting the carotid artery and perivascular tissues. Diagnosis relies heavily on imaging, particularly ultrasound and MRI, as clinical and laboratory findings are often nonspecific. Early recognition is crucial to avoid unnecessary investigations and anxiety for the patient. Most cases respond well to NSAIDs and resolve within weeks, though recurrent episodes have been documented. Awareness among clinicians is essential for timely diagnosis and effective management of this self-limiting but potentially distressing condition.
