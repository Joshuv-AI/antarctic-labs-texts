Navigating the Complexities of Pulmonary Hypertension Care: Unique Challenges in PAH Management
Authors: Karla Cruz Morel, Namita Sood, Roberto Bernardo, Kimberly Jackson, Gillian P. Leung, Charles Burger, Jean M. Elwing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary hypertension (PH) comprises a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by elevated pressure in the pulmonary arteries. Despite advances in awareness, utilization of non‐invasive screening with echocardiography, increasing number of targeted therapies, and evolving clinical guidelines emphasizing referral to PH specialty centers, barriers to timely diagnosis and treatment persist. Key challenges span patient‐related and system‐level domains that hinder the effective diagnosis, management, and treatment of PH, particularly pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). In this article, the obstacles to PH care are examined and actionable recommendations are proposed. By integrating patient‐related and system‐level perspectives, this review provides a framework to bridge persistent gaps in PH care delivery in the United States.

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) comprises a heterogeneous group of disorders defined by elevated pressure in the pulmonary arteries. The disease involves pulmonary vascular narrowing, fibroproliferative remodeling, and inflammation of the pulmonary vascular endothelium, leading to increased pulmonary arterial pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance and, ultimately, to right heart failure and death [1, 2]. Irrespective of etiology, PH significantly increases morbidity and mortality [3] and imposes a substantial burden on healthcare systems worldwide [4].

Over the last five decades, there have been significant efforts to increase pulmonary arterial hypertension awareness and education regarding evaluation and management. This has occurred through international collaborations initially with the World Health Organization (WHO) and subsequently with the World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension (WSPH). Additionally, patient‐centered groups such as the Pulmonary Hypertension Association (PHA) have played a vital role in advocacy and public education.

Despite advances in awareness, utilization of non‐invasive screening with echocardiography, increasing number of targeted therapies, and evolving clinical guidelines emphasizing referral to PH specialty centers, barriers to timely diagnosis and treatment persist. Key challenges span patient‐related and system‐level domains that hinder the effective diagnosis, management, and treatment of PH, particularly pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) (Figure 1) [5]. PAH is a precapillary subtype of PH characterized by sustained elevations in pulmonary vascular resistance in the absence of significant left heart, lung, or thromboembolic disease. The current review highlights the primary obstacles to PAH care and proposes solutions to bridge gaps in care delivery.

Barriers to care for pulmonary arterial hypertension patients.

Patient‐Related Barriers

Access to Care

Barriers to care access in the United States (U.S.) are not unique to PAH but are emblematic of broader systemic issues. According to the U.S. Census Bureau (2023), 8% of the population lacks health insurance. Adults aged 19 to 64 are particularly vulnerable as their eligibility for public programs such as Medicaid and Medicare may be more limited [6]. These vulnerabilities are particularly relevant to the PAH population, with a mean age of 53–56 years and approximately 75% of patients being women, as reported in the Registry to Evaluate Early and Long‐term PAH Disease Management (REVEAL) and the United States Pulmonary Hypertension Scientific Registry (USPHSR) [7, 8].

Racial and ethnic disparities further exacerbate access issues. Approximately 26.3% of Hispanic adults (aged 19–64) are uninsured, nearly double the rate for Black adults (11.1%) and more than triple that of non‐Hispanic White (7.0%) and Asian (6.8%) adults. Nonetheless, possessing insurance does not necessarily guarantee access [9]. A Kaiser Family Foundation (KFF) survey reported that 58% of insured adults encountered challenges using their coverage such as denied claims, narrow provider networks, or authorization delays with many issues left unresolved, potentially leading to serious consequences [10, 11]. It has also been reported that there is a higher proportion of PAH patients with Hispanic ethnicity who are either uninsured or have more restrictive health insurance. Hispanic patients with PAH, despite similar severity of illness, are more likely to be hospitalized or receive their care in the emergency department, which could suggest that they do not have the means of satisfying their medical needs in a traditional outpatient setting and rely more heavily on emergency department or inpatient services [12]. Moreover, data from the Pulmonary Hypertension Association Registry (PHAR) shows that Hispanics had a disadvantageous socioeconomic profile as compared with non‐Hispanic Whites. Hispanics had lower level of educational attainment, lower annual incomes, lower rates of employment or retirement, and reduced access to Medicare or commercial insurance [13].

Cost remains a major deterrent, as four in ten insured adults reported delaying or skipping care due to unaffordable out‐of‐pocket expenses or premiums [11]. These access issues contribute to delayed diagnoses; in a study by Small et al., the longest diagnostic delays were reported in the U.S., where patients waited an average of 17 months between symptom onset and PAH diagnosis, compared to 7.8 months in Europe and 5.1 months in Japan [14]. In a prospective, questionnaire‐based cohort study of WSPH Group 1 PAH patients in the U.S., one‐third of patients reported monthly out‐of‐pocket costs exceeding USD 1,000 and nearly half required some form of financial assistance. Thirty percent of patients reported modifying their medication regimen, often by skipping doses to reduce costs [15].

Current PH guidelines recommend patients with PAH be evaluated and treated at a PH Center of Care [16]. Leary et al reported that only 37.1% of patients receive PAH‐specific medications from a PH Care Center. Patients cared for by non‐PH specialists were more likely to be on monotherapy despite guideline recommendations for combination therapy [17]. Accredited centers must meet specific criteria including nursing and support staff trained to assist in the diagnosis, education and management of patients with PH, as well as facilities with diagnostic capability and access to specialists (Figure 2) [18]. To improve the quality of care and outcomes, the PHA established a program for accreditation of centers with special expertise in PH and their resources available for the care of PH patients. Approximately 100 accredited PH centers exist nationwide; however, to date, 13 states lack a PHA‐accredited center. Many of these centers are in metropolitan areas, which further underscores the geographic and logistical access limitations for PAH patients seeking specialized care [19].

While PH Care Centers offer the advantage of coordinated, multidisciplinary care, the patient may lack access to the center because it falls outside their insurance network. Furthermore, many providers are discouraged from referring outside of their system. Additionally, if the PH program is outside the network, limited services may be covered at that center. Many of the guideline‐recommended support services required for accreditation are not adequately reimbursed by third‐party payers. As a result, maintaining critical support services remains challenging. In an international survey by Doyle‐Cox et al., directed to PH clinics meeting the 2009 European Society of Cardiology/European Respiratory Society criteria for PH centers across 32 countries in Europe, Asia, Australia, and the U.S., 10% of clinics lacked a full‐time PH nurse, coordinator, or allied health professional, and 35% had only one full‐time nurse, which was inadequate given the reported clinic volumes [20].

Similarly, the integration of pharmacists into PH Care Center teams has been shown to improve both clinical and economic outcomes. In PH clinics, pharmacists can play a vital role with providing medication‐focused patient education, creating patient‐specific schedules for frequently dosed medications, and in ensuring medication safety by managing drug interactions and side effects which may improve adherence to PH therapies. Often, they can facilitate faster medication authorizations and support the inclusion of updated PH targeted therapies in formularies. Their involvement can help reduce hospital length of stay, reduce drug‐related problems and lower hospitalizations costs [21, 22]. Unfortunately, the lack of appropriate reimbursement for these services creates significant challenges despite institutional acknowledgement of these critical roles.

Socioeconomic Burden

Cohort studies have demonstrated significantly higher healthcare costs for patients with PAH compared with unaffected individuals. Hospitalizations, particularly readmissions, and medication expenses generally drive the increased direct costs [23]. Others that may contribute include diagnostic evaluations, as well as outpatient and emergency department visits. Research has also demonstrated elevated indirect costs, such as lost productivity, disability, housing insecurity, travel for care, and food insecurity [24].

In a retrospective analysis of MarketScan Commercial and Health and Productivity Management datasets (2016–2018), Ogbomo et al. reported that on a per‐patient‐per‐month (PPPM) basis, prevalent PAH patients had higher total healthcare costs ($9,915 vs $359), longer inpatient stays (0.63 vs 0.02 days), more short‐term disability (STD) days (6.0 vs 1.5), and higher STD‐related costs ($1,226 vs $277). Similar trends were observed in incident PAH patients, with increased costs ($9,353 vs $336), hospital stays (0.92 vs 0.01 days), STD days (8.1 vs 1.5), and STD‐related costs ($1,706 vs $263) (all p < 0.0001) [23].

Similarly, Watzker et al. analyzed data from multiple national datasets (2018–2020), including Medicare, Medicaid, and commercial insurance, and reported an estimated annual national payer burden of $3.1 billion for PAH. PPPM costs ranged from $6,500 to $14,742, with out‐of‐pocket expenses between $341 and $907. Inpatient utilization rates ranged from 435 to 770 per 1,000 patients, further underscoring the intensive healthcare needs of this population [4].

The financial and psychosocial barriers that impact access to subspecialty PAH care are also significant. Talwar et al. found that patients with lower socioeconomic status presented with more advanced disease and experienced poorer outcomes [25]. Similarly, Nadipelli et al. interviewed clinicians at PH expert centers who acknowledged the detrimental impact of socioeconomic factors on care delivery [26]. Other patient‐related factors such as racial disparities, language barriers, and substance use disorders may also limit care access.

Healthcare System Barriers

Delayed Diagnosis

Despite increased awareness and therapeutic advances, diagnostic delays remain a persistent problem in PH. Since the establishment of the first U.S. PAH registry in 1987, the average time to diagnosis remains 2 years or more. Importantly, the extended delay in correct diagnosis has not improved significantly (Figure 3) [27].

Diagnosis journey roadblocks of pulmonary hypertension patients.

For example, a retrospective review by Didden et al. (2007–2020) reported a median delay of over 2 years from the onset of chronic unexplained dyspnea to a formal PAH diagnosis, with patients often undergoing multiple echocardiograms before referral for confirmatory right heart catheterization (RHC), delayed by a median of 1.7 years [28]. This is especially concerning given the median untreated survival of approximately 2.8 years [29]. High risk populations, such as those with scleroderma or congenital heart disease (CHD), may present at advanced stages. Khou et al. found that 77% of patients with scleroderma‐associated PAH were in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class III or IV at diagnosis, despite established screening recommendations [30]. Similarly, Burger et al. noted persistent delays when reviewing time to diagnosis in the REVEAL (2006–2007) and USPHSR (2015–2018) registries, was 2.8 years and 1.9 years, respectively [27].

Delayed diagnosis of PAH is associated with higher mortality and more frequent PAH‐related rehospitalizations. Patients diagnosed within 3 months of symptoms onset had less severe disease and higher event‐free survival than those with delayed diagnosis [30]. Overall, delayed diagnosis results in disease progression, need for more complex therapies, increased hospitalizations, and worse survival.

Misdiagnosis

The WSPH classifies PH into five diagnostic groups based on underlying cause and associated conditions [31]. The diagnosis requires confirmatory RHC and entails a series of diagnostic tests to appropriately phenotype the patient [16]. It is critical to accurately determine the most appropriate diagnostic group as therapeutic strategies are aligned with the group classification [32]. Completing the recommended work‐up can be challenging, and several tests require prior authorizations that may be approved only with select providers; these authorization barriers complicate timely completion of the diagnostic evaluation for both patients and providers.

Targeted therapies are primarily approved for group 1 (PAH), select group 3 (interstitial lung disease‐associated PH), and group 4 chronic thromboembolic PH (CTEPH). Misclassifying PH may lead to inappropriate therapy, risking adverse outcomes and unnecessary expenses. Diagnostic errors often stem from limited provider experience and misinterpretation of echocardiography or RHC data. For example, diagnosis of group 2 PH, which is the most common cause of PH, relies on pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) measurement during RHC, but the PCWP is not always accurately captured. Halpern et al. found that, among 310 patients diagnosed with PAH based on a PCWP < 15 mmHg, 53.5% had a left ventricular end‐diastolic pressure (LVEDP) > 15 mmHg, indicating misclassification of postcapillary PH as PAH [33].

When comparing the diagnosis and treatment of PAH at a PH Care Center versus a non‐PH Specialty Center, Saunders et al. reported that 68.4% of patients were diagnosed with a PH group different from what was initially suspected, and 12.4% received no PH diagnosis. These inaccuracies had a significant impact on patient care, given the prohibitive cost of PAH‐targeted therapies and their potential side effects [34]. Accurate diagnosis is particularly critical for group 1 PAH, where the median survival without treatment is approximately 2.8 years [29].

Physician Training and Education

Despite advancement in PH management over the past two decades, expertise remains concentrated in specialized centers. The majority of patients with PAH are initially evaluated by primary care physicians (78.1%), with smaller proportions first seen by pulmonologists, cardiologists, emergency physicians, and rheumatologists [14].

However, primary care physicians' training often lacks sufficient exposure to PH, leading to under‐recognition. In addition, many specialists also graduate with limited experience in PH care. de Belen et al. conducted a cross‐sectional simulation study assessing the care of patients with PH among practicing pulmonologists and cardiologists. PH was correctly suspected in fewer than 50% of cases. Even when PH was correctly identified, 30% of providers failed to perform or recommend RHC [35]. Similar findings were reported in the Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension Quality Enhancement Research Initiative (PAH‐QuERI) where only 6% of the patients had undergone complete recommended diagnostic testing [36], which is of utmost importance to accurately classify and appropriately treat PH.

This knowledge gap is concerning, as a recent observational study by Leary et al. highlighted that most PH care is delivered by non‐PH Specialty Programs rather than PHA‐accredited PH Care Centers.

PH is widely acknowledged as a highly specialized field of medicine; however, there remains a lack of standardized specialist training. This has led to the foundation of the National Consortium on Pulmonary Vascular Disease (PVD) Fellowship Training (APOLLO). The goal (APOLLO) is to establish a common curriculum and set training requirements across PH programs and to promote excellence in clinical training for future PH focused health care providers, educators, and researchers in the field [37]. However, currently, only a few PVD Fellowship Programs exist, and these are not accredited through the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education [38].

Delayed Referral to PH Centers of Care

Both the 2022 European guidelines for diagnosis and management of PH and the more recent 7th World Symposium on PH recommendations emphasize timely referral to PH Centers of Care for subspecialty expertise for accurate diagnosis and access to PH‐specific therapies [16, 32]. In addition, multidisciplinary teams provide comprehensive care and access to clinical research. There is evidence that patients treated at a PH care center have better outcomes, including improved survival and fewer hospitalizations [39].

Unfortunately, referrals often occur only after patients have progressed to advanced disease stages. The RePHerral study demonstrated that more than 60% of patients referred for evaluation of PAH were already in NYHA class III or IV and 57% were inappropriately receiving PAH medications [40].

A study examining the incidence of PAH and CTEPH in the Massachusetts All‐Payer Claims Database from 2015 to 2017 found that only 5.6% of the cohort (n = 12,505) were seen at a PH Care Center. Factors associated with reduced likelihood of referral included Medicaid enrollment, a mental health diagnosis and distance to the nearest PH care center [41].

Inappropriate Use of Targeted Therapies

The expanding PAH targeted therapies have introduced new complexities into patient care. While only a subset of PH patients benefit from selective pulmonary vasodilator therapies, these medications are increasingly prescribed under the care of non‐PH Specialty providers. Inappropriate use not only exposes patients to potential harm but also leads to significant financial burden. For example, a retrospective analysis of veterans prescribed phosphodiesterase‐5 inhibitors for PH between 2005 and 2012 revealed that two‐thirds of these prescriptions were inconsistent with established PH guidelines. The associated cost ranged from $10,000 to $13,000 per patient per year [42].

Similarly, findings from the Giessen PH registry, published in 2017 by one of the world's leading PH centers, reported that 40% of patients referred with a diagnosis of group 2 PH were already receiving PAH therapies despite a lack of proven efficacy for such treatment. Similar practices have been documented in the U.S. [43]. Saunders et al. showed that 40.2% of patients with PAH diagnosed by non‐PH specialists were reclassified upon PH‐Specialist evaluation with 75% reclassified as Group 2 PH and 7.6% without any PH diagnosis. Nearly 11.2% had medications discontinued, saving $232–$245 per patient per day [34].

The recent FDA approval of sotatercept, a novel activin signaling inhibitor (ASI) for group 1 PAH, adds both promise and complexity to the treatment landscape. While clinical trials have demonstrated its efficacy in patients already receiving background therapy [44], concerns remain regarding its use outside guideline‐recommended indications. Although sotatercept may offer the advantage of less burdensome administration, its need for frequent monitoring and its side‐effect profile underscore the importance of prescribing within specialized care. Sotatercept is not intended to replace standard combination regimens, and its integration into clinical practice requires updated treatment algorithms and careful monitoring protocols.

Furthermore, insurance coverage for PAH therapies varies widely across U.S. commercial health plans. Coverage restrictions, step therapy requirements, and prescriber limitations create additional barriers. A study by Rucker et al. examining 17 commercial health plans from 2017 to 2022 found that the proportion of PAH coverage policies with no restrictions dropped from 62% to 27%, while those with at least one restriction nearly doubled from 38% to 73%. Notably, step therapy requirements increased from 29% to 46%. The study also highlighted variability in payer response [45]. Such restrictions reduce patient access to medications and further delay appropriate disease management, perpetuating downstream complications stemming from diagnostic delay. These restrictions also limit prescriber options and increase office administrative burden.

Access to Care

Ideally, a single‐payer healthcare system would ensure universal access to care and would facilitate the referral of patients with PAH to regional centers and comprehensive centers of excellence. Since this does not exist in the United States, other strategies require consideration.

The PHA has stressed the importance of alliances between regional and comprehensive accredited centers, which could be expanded with greater emphasis on strengthening collaboration between non‐PH specialty providers and expert centers. From a tactical perspective, one approach would be the development of multidisciplinary teams to provide seamless care for the patient, both within and outside of the respective individual healthcare systems. This would facilitate seamless care between the local provider and the specialist at a comprehensive care center.

This collaborative model between PH Care Centers and non‐PH focused centers has the potential to enhance access to specialized care, decrease delays and misdiagnosis, improve medication management, encourage participation in clinical research, and provide broader psychosocial support for patients with PH. Through such collaboration, patients could receive necessary care closer to home. However, forming these partnerships requires significant time and effort. Ensuring that e‐consult services are properly reimbursed and making it easier for patients to access PH centers when needed would support these collaborations. Such alliances may play a crucial role in raising the standard of care for PH patients across various medical environments.

Easing regulations on cross‐state telemedicine, especially for rare diseases, could further increase access, particularly when remote monitoring tools such as remote 6‐minute walk testing are used for patients in areas with few PH care options. Unfortunately, the requirement of insurance approval prior to physician referral remains a barrier. Spreading awareness about specialized centers through patient support groups and social media can also help.

For these models to be effective, the establishment of new reimbursement codes specifically tailored to PH multidisciplinary consultations and related services is needed, with support from advocacy groups and professional societies.

Reducing Social Barriers to Care

Additionally, it is important to leverage patients' perspective to identify and understand specific barriers to care by proactively assessing their socioeconomic challenges. The Montefiore social determinants of health 10‐point questionnaire is one of the tools that can be used during the patient's evaluation to easily identify issues that may need attention.

Social workers are essential to help patients navigate social barriers and improve their care. However, due to cost and reimbursement challenges, many practices are unable to provide this support. Establishing alliances between comprehensive PH Care Centers and other healthcare systems may help address this gap.

Leveraging Resources

Building a PH Care Center requires substantial resources to provide quality care and not all needed services are reimbursed. Lobbying policymakers and third‐party payers for appropriate reimbursement should be an ongoing focus of the entire PH community. Active engagement with organizations such as the PHA and the American Thoracic Society provides opportunities for collective action. There is a critical need for sustainable reimbursement models that align with the complexity and high cost of managing PAH, to ensure appropriate care for this vulnerable, chronic population and to support the long‐term viability of fully resourced PH centers of care. Additional research evaluating the cost‐saving impact of fully staffed PH care centers could help build a stronger case for administrative support and investment in these programs (Figure 4).

Proposed solutions for current challenges in pulmonary hypertension.

Streamlining Management

The treatment guidelines for patients with PAH have evolved substantially; however, many insurance plans require initiation on the least costly medications, making adherence to current PH treatment guidelines difficult for prescribers. Streamlining prior authorization requirements and aligning coverage decisions with established clinical guidelines can reduce administrative burden, improve care efficiency, and support better health outcomes for this high‐risk population.

Education

Physician education and familiarity with PH remain a challenge. A survey of all U.S. pulmonary and critical care fellows and program directors from February to October 2024 found there was no change in the pulmonary and critical care medicine fellows' comfort in managing PH over the last two decades. Approximately 40% of fellows were uncomfortable interpreting key diagnostic data, and only 27% felt comfortable initiating PH targeted therapy [46]. This highlighted a need for incorporating an introduction to pulmonary vascular disease in the core curriculum for medical school and residency. Incorporation of a more structured curriculum should be mandatory in pulmonary and cardiology fellowship programs.

Recently, the PHA developed a Pulmonary Hypertension Boot Camp which is an asynchronous online learning course that is designed to address this educational gap (https://phassociation.org/education-programs/medicalprofessionals/ph-boot-camp). Additionally, the National Consortium on Pulmonary Vascular Disease Fellowship Training (APOLLO) has made significant strides in advancing education and training in PH. Their efforts have included developing standardized core curricula, offering structured educational resources, and promoting advanced fellowship training opportunities to improve clinical competency and ensure a pipeline of well‐prepared specialists [37]. Despite this progress, notable gaps in training persist. These deficits highlight the need for continued investment in PH education, including expanded clinical training opportunities, simulation‐based learning, collaboration across institutions, and broader integration of PH content in general pulmonary and cardiology training programs.

Developing Simplified Guidelines

The development of simplified guidelines tailored for non‐PH focused centers and rural settings is important to improve PH care. Current treatment guidelines, while comprehensive, are complex and geared towards specialized centers. There is a need for streamlined protocols tailored for non‐specialized providers practicing in rural or resource‐limited environments. These simplified guidelines should focus on early recognition of PH, clear diagnostic algorithms, basic management principles and clear referral pathways.

Advocate to Reduce Cost of PH Targeted Therapy

The high cost of PH targeted therapies remains a major barrier to access and medication adherence. Continued advocacy should focus on lowering medication costs through improved generic access, increased price transparency, and expanded manufacturer assistance programs.

Advocate for Payers' Coverage in Alignment With Current Guidelines

Insurance payers often delay recommended treatments through prior authorization and step therapy. Stakeholders, including healthcare systems, professional societies, and patient advocacy groups should advocate for payer policies aligned with clinical guidelines so that treatment decisions reflect evidence and clinical need.

Advocate for Social Worker and Pharmacy Reimbursement

Social workers and pharmacists play critical roles in the management of patients with PH; however, the lack of reimbursement limits the ability of many practices to integrate these essential resources. Policy changes that enable billing for pharmacy‐led medication management and social work services are crucial to support comprehensive, team‐based care.

Conclusion

Pulmonary hypertension remains a complex, multifaceted syndrome with significant diagnostic and management challenges. Addressing barriers related to healthcare access, physician training, and timely referral to expert centers is essential to improving outcomes. As therapies become more sophisticated, ensuring that patients receive appropriate, evidence‐based care from specialists is imperative. Systemic reform and targeted interventions can advance the standard of care for individuals living with PAH and reduce the broader burden on healthcare systems.
