Understanding Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular disease in the light of Ayurveda
Authors: Maanasi Menon, Sushma Naranappa Salethoor, Thushara Madathil, Akhilesh Shukla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is the malady that claims most human lives around the world by literally cutting off their heart's blood supply. Unwholesome diet, sedentary lifestyle and psychological stress are the major risk factors leading to this condition. Even with technological and medical advancements, the burden of this disease has only aggravated further especially with the COVID pandemic also contributing to trigger this condition. There is an urgent need to find better solutions to combat this disease in such circumstances. The Indian traditional healthcare system, Ayurveda has a unique perspective of the heart and heart disease which when understood parallelly along with modern medical knowledge may offer better solutions. The descriptions of Kaphaja Hridroga in Ayurveda can be correlated to ASCVD, and this article is an effort to develop a synergistic understanding of the aetio-pathogenesis of ASCVD in the light of Ayurveda. Ayurveda management principles give more emphasis on prevention and non-pharmacological approaches such as diet and lifestyle modifications along with medical management and these principles of management of Hridroga can be tactfully integrated into conventional therapy for providing a more wholesome care.Keywords: Hridroga, Dhamani Pratichaya, Avarana, Atherosclerosis, Abaddha Medas, Dyslipidemia, Ischemic heart disease (IHD), Coronary heart disease, Hritshoola

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is a collective term comprising a group of disorders of the heart and blood vessels. These diseases are the largest cause of morbidity and premature death worldwide [1]. Modern technology-driven culture especially with the advent of Artificial Intelligence (AI) has made life more convenient for man but has led him to be physically inactive thereby causing many lifestyle diseases. Unhealthy diet, low physical activity, psychological stress, smoking, alcohol intake etc lead to lifestyle disorders such as dyslipidemia, obesity, hypertension, and diabetes. These, along with hereditary or genetic predisposition and exposure to certain infections are the prime risk factors for developing ASCVD. Despite all medical advancements, ASCVD remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide and its burden has only risen further. The recent COVID pandemic has also contributed to it because, now it has come to light that SARS COV-2 also triggers atherosclerosis [2,3] In such a scenario, there is an urgent need to explore new avenues to find solutions for this life-threatening condition. Ayurveda has described heart disease and its management in its classical texts mainly under the heading- hridroga (heart disease). There is a need to understand these descriptions parallelly along with the conventional medical knowledge to be able to come up with solutions that may help tackle this problem better in a collaborative way. Ayurveda has very uniquely understood the importance of the heart, not just its anatomical and physiological aspects, but it is very interesting to note that it has also explored the psycho-spiritual aspects. This is now found to be very significant, as modern stressful lifestyle and other psychological factors are known to be a major risk factor for heart disease [4]. This highlights the need for non-pharmacological approaches like diet, lifestyle modification and psycho-spiritual therapy to manage this condition. Ayurveda has given equal importance to these approaches apart from medical management. Based on the modern understanding of ASCVD, it can be compared to the description of kaphaja hridroga (heart disease caused by kapha imbalance) in Ayurveda. Kapha, being responsible for the structural integrity of the organs, any impairment of kapha dosha may lead to structural damage or dysfunction. In this case it affects the main arteries that supply blood to the heart causing dhamani pratichaya [5] or Atherosclerosis which in turn blocks the flow of blood to the heart leading to ASCVD. The various etio-pathogenic pathways leading to this condition are explored in this article to provide a collective understanding of ASCVD through the lens of Ayurveda. This convergence of ancient wisdom with modern scientific understanding will help develop an integrative approach and may pave ways to better help manage this problem (see Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Materials and methods

Heart and its pathologies from the major Ayurveda texts such as the Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, Ashtanga Hridaya, Sharngadhara Samhita and Bhela Samhita were collected and parallelly compared to the modern descriptions from published research articles and books related to cardiology. Concepts that are proposed in this article were thoroughly discussed among the Ayurveda and modern counterparts to come to a parallel understanding of the topic.

Existing theories

Ayurveda understands ASCVD as a kapha dominant hridroga as it begins due to accumulation of kapha dosha and medas (lipids) in the major dhamani (artery) that supplies rasa- rakta dhatus (blood along with nutrients derived from food) to the heart for its nourishment and proper functioning. Dhamani pratichaya, described under kaphaja nanatmajavikara (diseases caused exclusively by Kapha Dosha), is akin to Atherosclerosis [5]. Dhamani pratichaya is a condition described as upalepa [Charaka Samhita Sutra Sthana 20/17–19 Chakrapani] or coating of the internal wall of the dhamani by excessive medas and kapha causing sanga or obstruction in the dhamani affecting the normal flow of vyana vata which is responsible for the pumping action of the heart that propagates the circulation of blood in the body [Charaka Samhita Chikitsa Sthana 15/36]. Impairment of vata dosha due to avarana (occlusion) is also a major pathological component of this disease. Some scholars also use the term dhamani kathinya (hardening of arteries) [6] referring to the ongoing process of Srotodushti (impairment of the channels) in the rasa-raktavaha dhamani (vessels carrying rasa and rakta). In the later stages it becomes a sannipataja hridroga where the involvement of all the Tridoshas, leads to severe complications and even death.

Physiology of Cardiovascular system in Ayurveda

It is notable that scientific and precise descriptions of anatomy and physiology of the heart and blood vessels have been written in ancient ayurvedic texts (when there was absolutely no sophisticated technology to understand the internal body structures and their functions). The circulatory function of the heart and CVS was described in Ayurvedic literature before William Harvey was known to have discovered it [7]. The ancient text Bhela Samhita, contemporary to the Charaka Samhita clearly describes the circulatory function of the heart [8]. The word for heart in Ayurveda is hridayam which comprises three syllables: ‘hri’ which denotes harana-receiving/taking away, ‘Da’ denotes Dana-giving and ‘Ya’ stands for Ayana-constantly circulating. Yam’- also means control and regulation. This clearly conveys the circulatory function of the heart [9]. Hridaya is described as Chetana Sthana (Charaka Samhita Sharira Sthana 7/8)- the seat of consciousness, Pranayatana (Charaka Samhita Sutra Sthana 29/3) -seat of life and a sadyopranahara marma [10] -vital organ, injury to which leads to immediate death. It is the abode of prana (vital energy), ojas (essence of all dhatus imparting vigour, vitality & immunity) (Charaka Samhita Sutra Sthana 17/74), rasa (nourishing/fluid component of blood) and rakta (cellular component of blood). The vascular structures in the CVS are denoted by three terms namely dhamani, sira and srotas which may be understood as arteries, veins and capillaries respectively. The word dhamani is defined as- “dhmanat anilapoornatvat dhamanyah” which means they are called dhamani due to their pulsating nature propelled by vata. (Charaka Samhita Sutra Sthana 30/12). This a very scientific and functional definition as compared to etymology of the word ‘artery’, which is basically a misnomer because ancient greeks thought they were channels for circulating air [11]. The word dhamani indicates active flow of blood, pushed by the pressure of Vata. The word sira is defined as “saranat sirah'' is indicative of a passive flow of blood through the veins. (Charaka Samhita Sutra Sthana 30/12) srotas broadly means channel and is defined as- “Sravanat Srotamsi”(Charaka Samhita Sutra Sthana 30/12), which means that they are called srotas because fluid oozes out of them into the surrounding tissue and back. This is a befitting definition for capillary circulation because we now know that capillaries are single cell layered structures through which contents of the blood are exchanged with the tissues. In this context we can understand that even cells are Srotases (micro channels) due to their semi permeability and not so-called closed structures as the name “cell” suggests. Their functions as channels were inferred even before Robert Hook first saw the cells arranged like a honeycomb under a microscope [12]. Hridaya and the connecting Dhamanis are the srotomoolas of rasavaha and pranavaha srotas (root of the channels carrying rasa and prana). (Charaka Samhita Vimana Sthana 5/7–8) This clearly establishes the heart as the center of the circulatory system.

The involvement of Doshas in the functions of the heart and CVS are discussed as follows. Vata dosha, through its five subtypes Vvyana, prana, udana, samana and apana, controls all types of movement and transportation at every level and thus is basically in charge of the physics of the body. Vyana vata is responsible for rasa samvahana (blood circulation) (Charaka Samhita Chikitsa Sthana 15/36) which can be understood as the self-excitatory function of the heart through the auto-rhythmicity of the Sinoatrial (SA) node, since it is the SA node that gives the necessary impulses for the heart to start beating and initiate circulation. It is also responsible for prasarana (Abduction/relaxation), akunchana (Adduction/contraction) and gati/gamana (all motor functions) movements in the body which may be understood as the contraction and relaxation of cardiac and endothelial muscles which propels the movement of blood within the CVS towards the periphery, thus enabling proper movements of the extremities. Prana Vata which is primarily located in the head is responsible for hridaya and dhamani dharana [13] which means control of the functioning of the heart and vascular structures. This can be understood as the autonomic nervous system control of the heart rate and vasoconstriction and dilation mediated mainly through the vagus nerve. Prana Vata also controls buddhi (intellectual functions), chitta (mental functions) and indriya (Sensory Functions) (Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 12/4–5). This explains why any sensory or emotional stimulus has a direct effect on the heart rate.Udana Vata resides in the chest and is responsible for expiration and vak (speech), prayatna, urja, bala (effort, strength and vitality) and some higher mental functions like smriti (memory) (Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 12/5) which mainly indicate that it connects the functions of the heart, respiratory system (through pulmonary circulation) and the CNS. Samana vata assists the jataragni in the process of digestion of ingested food and leads it further through the gut (Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 12/8) [14]. After the Ahara rasa (essence of digested food) is formed, it is taken to the hridaya by the Samana Vata (Sharangadhar Samhita Purva Khanda 6/9). Apana vata moves downwards and is responsible for maintaining the normal blood volume through the process of elimination of excess liquid through the urine and feces.(Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 12/8). Blood volume decides the cardiac load and so the proper functioning of Apana vata is important. It may be understood as an autonomic nervous system control of the kidneys, bladder and bowels. If its downward movement is obstructed due to urine retention or constipation, it travels upwards and may cause discomfort to the heart (Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 4/2).

The functions of all the enzymes and hormones and all chemical transformations within the body may be understood as the function of pitta dosha and thus it is responsible for the chemistry of the body. Pitta Dosha, through its subtypes, pachaka, ranjaka, bhrajaka, alochaka and sadhaka is responsible for the process of digestion of food, formation of ahara rasa and metabolism at the dhatu (tissue) level. The sadhaka pitta resides in the heart (Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 12/13). The role of chemical substances that control the functioning of the heart like hormones, cardiac enzymes, neurotransmitters which control blood pressure, heart rate etc and the exchange of chemical ions in terms of influx of Na and Ca ions and efflux of K ions across the membrane of the SA node [15], may be considered as the function of Sadhaka Pitta. Ranjaka Pitta does Rasa Ranjana (Ashtanga Hridaya, Sutra Sthana 12/13), it imparts the red colour to the blood which may be correlated to formation of haemoglobin which eventually affects the volume and viscosity of the blood and this in turn affects the cardiac function. Pachaka Pitta is responsible for digestion of food and formation of ahara rasa (Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 12/10–12). All the digestive enzymes and hormones that play a role in digestion and intermediate metabolism of food, especially lipids in the gut and the liver can be understood as functions of Pachaka Pitta. It determines the basal metabolic rate of the body which has a direct effect on the pulse rate and pulse pressure of the heart based on Read & Gale's formula [[16], [17], [18]].

Kapha Dosha through its five subtypes, avalambaka, kledaka, sleshaka, tarpaka and bodhaka is responsible for all the structural components and protective mechanisms of the body. Avalambaka kapha is situated in the heart and is responsible for its structural integrity (Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 12/15–16). The pericardial fluid around the heart and the resistance offered by the heart musculature and the blood vessels to the flow of blood to maintain the diastolic blood pressure can be considered as the function of Avalambaka Kapha [19]. Kledaka kapha helps in anna sanghata and kledana (∼moistening of the ingested food with mucous secretions) (Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 12/16) and assists in the proper formation of Ahara Rasa which then turns into rasa dhatu.

The involvement of dhatus (tissues) along with their respective Srotases (Channels) are being discussed as follows. The pranavaha srotas may be considered as the central and autonomic nervous system control of the CVS and the respiratory system. Rasa dhatu is the first formed dhatu after digestion of food. It may be understood as the liquid/plasma portion of the blood that carries essential nutrients to the heart and other organs, nourishing them. Thus, the function of rasa is preenana (nourishment of Dhatus). (Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 11/4). It is carried out through the Rasavaha Srotas, with the heart and major arteries (Rasavahini Dhamanis) as its stromoolas (Charaka Samhita Vimana Sthana 5/8). The CVS may be considered as the rasavaha srotas. Rakta dhatu may be considered as the cellular component of the blood mainly erythrocytes (RBCs). Its function is jeevana karma (Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 11/4) - which can be understood as the life-giving oxygen supply to the heart through the RBCs in the blood. Thus, the hematopoietic system is to be understood as the rakta vaha srotas with yakrut (liver) and pleeha (spleen) as the srotomoolas (Charaka Samhita Vimana Sthana 5/8) as they are the two major organs involved in the life cycle of the red blood cells (RBCs). The liver is the main erythropoietic organ in the fetus [20] and the spleen is the graveyard of the RBCs [21]. Mamsa Dhatu in this context, can be considered as the heart musculature and the endothelial smooth muscles in the blood vessels. Medo Dhatu both in the form of baddha medas (adipose tissue) and abaddha medas (lipids in circulation) in their normal levels are important to maintain proper functionality of the CVS. When in excess they lead to obesity and atherosclerosis respectively and are thus detrimental to the health of the CVS. Heart is also the seat of ojas which is responsible for bala (∼immunity). Components of the blood responsible for immunity, namely WBCs, immunoglobulins, antibodies etc can be understood as the components of Ojas. The heart and mind are closely connected because it is also the seat of the manas (mind). Emotional fluctuations in the mind directly affect the heart through vagal and hormonal stimulations [22,23].

Etiology of Kaphaja Hridroga – causative factors (Nidana)-

Dhamani Pratichaya (Atherosclerosis) is described as a Kaphaja Nanatmaja Vyadhi (Charaka

Samhita Sutra Sthana 20/17) and thus etiological factors that aggravate all Doshas especially Kapha and Medas, contribute to causing this condition and are discussed as follows:

Aharaja (Dietary factors)- Foods that are Guru (heavy), Atisnigdha (high in fat content), Abhishyandi (which causes clogging of channels), Madhura Rasa (sweet, high sugar foods) and those that aggravate all Doshas, especially Kapha and Medas are the dietary causes. (Charaka Samhita Sutra Sthana 17/34) (Charaka Samhita Sutra Sthana 21/4)

Viharaja (Behavioral factors)- Avyayama (lack of exercise), Diwaswapna (day sleeping), Alasya

(laziness), Shayya and Asana Sukha (sedentary lifestyle) are the behavioral factors that aggravate.

Kapha and Medas. (Charaka Samhita Sutra Sthana 17/34) (Charaka Samhita Sutra Sthana 21/4). Alcohol intake and smoking are also contributing factors.

Manasika (Psychological factors)- Chronic stress (Atichinta), anxiety (Udvega), anger (Krodha),

phobias (Bhaya), depression (Shoka) are factors that vitiate all Doshas especially Pitta and Vata. (Charaka Samhita Chikitsa Sthana 3/115) Achinta-idle mind and Harshanityatva (continuous exultation) aggravate Kapha (Charaka Samhita Sutra Sthana 17/4)

Beeja Doshaja (Genetic factors) – Family history of Hridroga, hypertension, dyslipidemia and other genetic factors are responsible for Vata vitiation which leads to defects in Srotas and fat metabolism (Charaka Samhita Sutrasthana 21/4) [24].

Vyadhijanya - (pre-existing medical conditions)- Prameha (diabetes), Sthoulya (obesity), Vyana.

Vaishamya (hypertension), excessive Abaddha Medas (dyslipidemia) etc. are the diseases that lead to atherosclerosis [25].

Agantuja (exogenous causes)- Exposure to toxins, tobacco products and pollutants may also cause Sroto Dushti (defect in the channels) and Dosha vitiation and these may be classified as Vishaja Nidana (toxicological causes) [26]. Apart from these factors, it has been observed that infections both bacterial and viral may lead to cardiovascular diseases by provoking atherosclerosis [27]. Atherosclerotic CVD is also a common comorbidity found to be associated with the recent COVID- 19 pandemic [28]. These infections may be understood as the Krimija Nidana (pathogenic cause) of ASCVD. Thus, all the etiological factors described above lead to vitiation of all Doshas especially Kapha and Medas causing Srotodushti in the Dhamanis and accumulation of excessive Abaddha Medas in Rasa- Rakta Dhatus leading to Dhamani Pratichaya and eventually Hridroga.

Origin and development of the disease- A parallel understanding

Pathogenesis of Dhamani Pratichaya and atherosclerosis

Over-indulgence in dietary and lifestyle related etiological factors, genetic predisposition or a combination of both genetic and environmental factors may lead to one having Hypertension (HTN), Dyslipidemia or both. Habitual smoking or history of certain infections are other exogenous factors apart from HTN and dyslipidemia that may be involved in initiating the process of Kha- Vaigunya (structural changes/dysfunction) of the main Rasavaha Dhamani that supplies Rasa- Rakta to the heart. HTN leads to turbulent flow of Rasa-Rakta through the Dhamani causing injury to the endothelial lining of the artery [29] which in this context is a main coronary artery supplying blood to the heart. This may be understood as Vata vitiation resulting in injury/dysfunction because HTN is essentially a Vata predominant condition.

A second pathway involves the presence of certain exogenous substances in circulation (homocysteine, nicotine or other toxins resulting from smoking and systemic infections (∼ Vishaja factors) which react with the endothelial lining and cause a localised inflammation of the wall of the artery leading to its dysfunction [30,31].

Infectious agents (∼Krimija factors) known to provoke Atherosclerosis are some bacteria such as Chlamydia pneumoniae, Helicobacter pylori and viruses such as Hepatitis viruses, Influenza viruses, HIV, Human Papillomavirus HPV, Herpes simplex virus and even the recent SARS CoV2 virus [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. The possible mechanisms of these Vishaja and Krimija factors leading to ASCVD may be through overexpression of cytokines, adhesion and chemoattractant molecules, growth factors after the infection, increased uptake of LDLs, hyper oxidation and hyper resistance against apoptosis [31]. This may be understood as vitiation of Pitta Dosha as it involves chemical reactions and inflammation. The third and the most crucial pathway involves dyslipidemia, which is the presence of excessive lipids, mainly LDLs, in the blood causing endothelial dysfunction [36]. This may be understood as vitiation of Kapha Dosha and Medo Dhatu which may also directly interact with the wall of the artery to cause Kha-vaigunya or defect in the arterial wall. Dyslipidemia is the most imperative cause of this condition, because Abaddha Medas can cause damage/dysfunction of the Dhamani directly and further Abaddha Medas, mainly LDL itself starts accumulating at the site of endothelial damage (∼Khavaigunya) [37] This is the reason why we consider Dhamani Pratichaya as a Kapha predominant condition.

In all the three pathways we can see that the Doshas involved cause Khavaigunya or damage to

the Srotas/channel which in this case is the dhamani. The excessive /abaddha medas in circulation starts accumulating in the site of khavaigunya and this is how the fatty streak starts forming [38].

An immune response is then triggered attracting leukocytes, mainly macrophages to the site which convert to foam cells and get stuck to the site after ingesting some of the LDL particles [38].This process may be understood as dosha- dooshya sammoorchana where kapha (in the form of leukocytes) and Abaddha Medas interact with the wall of the dhamani at the site of khavaigunya and starts forming the Atherosclerotic plaque [38] or dhamani upalepa (coating of the arterial wall) (Charaka Samhita Sutra Sthana 20/19). The mamsa dhatu in the dhamani (∼smooth muscles of the endothelium) then grows onto the plaque [39] causing further Upalepa, as Lepana (to cover) is a function of mamsa dhatu. (Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 11/4). Ultimately, this leads to the condition called Dhamani Pratichaya (atherosclerosis) leading to narrowing of the lumen of the coronary artery or Rasavaha Dhamani supplying the heart.

Pathogenesis of Kaphaja Hridroga due to Dhamani Pratichaya (Fig. 1)

As the inflammatory process continues, the plaque becomes unstable. The mechanical stress caused by HTN (Vata), causes rupture of the unstable plaque, exposing its contents to the bloodstream, vitiating the Rakta Dhatu by causing agglutination of platelets and RBCs over the ruptured plaque [40].This leads to thrombus formation causing further narrowing of the coronary artery by occlusion [41]. This can be understood as marga avarana (occlusion of the passage) which leads to impaired flow of vyana vayu responsible for transporting rasa- rakta to the heart. Thus, the normal flow of rasa- rakta is partially or completely occluded by the plaque which at this stage involves Abaddha Medas (∼LDL), macrophages-foam cells (∼Kapha), smooth muscles of endothelium (∼Mamsa) ,platelets and RBCs (∼Rakta). The decreased blood flow to the heart because of this process leads to Hridroga. As the main source of the block is the plaque formed by kapha and medas, this type of Hridroga is classified as Kaphaja Hridroga. The narrowing of the Rasavaha Dhamani causes Rasa Kshaya (decreased flow of Rasa) to the heart leading to Ischemic heart disease (IHD).

Hritshoola ∼ Angina pectoris

Rasakshaya in the heart is what causes Angina (Hritshoola) as the myocardium struggles to pump due to lack of oxygen. If the process is gradual, the patient will have episodes of stable angina, whereas, when there is severe and acute MI, the patient will suffer from unstable angina and may collapse [42]. This condition is called Marmabhighata where we see the patient losing consciousness. Angina or Hritshoola presents differently based on the dominant Dosha involved. Sushrutha Samhita explains this in detail. In Hritshoola caused by Vata the patient feels an expanding, pricking, twisting, tearing, exploding or splitting kind of pain. (Sushruta Samhita Uttara Tantra 43/6) When caused by Pitta, there is a sucking pain associated with localized and generalised burning sensation, feeling of hot fumes emerging from within, cardiac exhaustion, thirst, fainting, profuse perspiration and dryness of mouth. (Sushruta Samhita Uttara Tantra 46/7) Hritshoola caused by dominance of Kapha presents as a feeling of heaviness (of the body and in the chest), excessive mucous secretions, anorexia, stiffness of the chest, weak digestive fire and sweet taste in the mouth. (Sushruta Samhita Uttara Tantra 46/8)

Sannipataja Hridroga and its complications (Fig. 2)

Rasakshaya in the heart (ischemia) causes myocardial infarction which leads to simultaneous vitiation of all Tridoshas in the heart. Each Dosha causes distinct effects on the functioning of the heart which may be understood as described below.

Effects of ischemia

Coronary atherosclerosis is a focal process that usually causes non-uniform ischemia which may lead to disturbances of the mechanical, biochemical and electrical functions of the myocardium [43].

Mechanical effects [44]- Myocardial pump action is reduced because of ventricular akinesia or dyskinesia due to regional disturbances in ventricular contractility caused by ischemia. Total or subtotal occlusion of the coronary artery due to abrupt or severe ischemia leads to transient LV failure, mitral regurgitation and angina pectoris. When prolonged it may also lead to myocardial necrosis, septal and free-wall rupture and scarring. The mechanical effects of ischemia may be understood as due to vitiation of Kapha Dosha causing Marga Avarana and consequent Vata Dushti.

Biochemical effects [45]- With severe oxygen deprivation, fatty acids cannot be oxidized and glucose is converted to lactate; intracellular pH is reduced and so are ATP and creatine phosphate. Impaired cell membrane function leads to leakage of potassium and the uptake of sodium by myocytes as well as an increase in cytosolic calcium finally leading to cell lysis and associated release of myocardial enzymes.The biochemical effects may be attributed to vitiation of Pitta Dosha as it involves chemical reactions and inflammation.

Electrical effects [46]- Ischemia causes characteristic changes in the ECG such as repolarization abnormalities seen as inversion of T waves and displacement of ST segments indicating the localization of the affected regions of the heart. Another consequence of myocardial ischemia is electrical instability, which may lead to Ventricular tachycardia or Ventricular fibrillation. Most patients who suddenly die of IHD do so because of ischemia induced Ventricular tachyarrhythmias. These electrical effects may be attributed to vitiation of Vata Dosha causing irregular electrical activity.

Thus, what may have initially started as a Kaphaja condition becomes a Tridosha/Sannipataja Hridroga when all the doshas get involved simultaneously. Based on the severity of respective Dosha vitiation, the complications of this condition can be classified into Kaphaja -Congestive cardiac failure - due to mechanical causes, Pittaja - Cardiogenic shock-due to biochemical causes and Vataja - Ventricular arrhythmias - due to electrical malfunctions. If treatment is not administered promptly, it leads to Marma Bheda (irreversible damage to the heart) leading to sudden death.

Pathophysiology based on Shatkriyakala - 6 stages

The pathogenesis of diseases is described by Ayurveda in six stages based on the vitiation of Doshas and the impairment of the respective Srotas (Sushruta Samhita Sutra Sthana 21/36). ASCVD probably occurs as a result of dhamani pratichaya (Atherosclerosis) in the rasavaha srotas and here the doshas are in the sthanasamshraya stage where they get lodged at the site of Kha-vaigunya. Due to the blockage of the passage of rasa dhatu towards the heart, it becomes ischemic leading to ASCVD. The analysis of ASCVD based on Shatkriyakala may be interpreted as below-

1.Sanchaya - The kapha pradhana tridoshas get vitiated due to the influence of the respective etiological factors leading to the accumulation of Doshas within their own sites, especially the urah pradesha (Chest region).

2.Prakopa- Doshas aggravate further and fill up their sites resulting in formation of Ama due to impaired Agni.

3.Prasara- Doshas overflow from their site and start circulating all over the body along with Rakta. At this stage there may be hypertension or hyperlipidemia or both leading to Kha-vaigunya of rasa vaha dhamani supplying the heart which may be understood as endothelial dysfunction of the coronary artery.

Thus, ASCVD manifests completely in the Vyakta stage of the whole pathogenic process while hypertension and dyslipidemia are the prasara stages and dhamani pratichaya is the sthasamsharaya stage of pathogenesis. When ASCVD progresses further into complications leading to severe irreversible damage to the heart causing sudden death, it is known as the bheda avastha or stage.

Principles of management

Nidana Parivarjana- Avoidance of etiological factors

Ayurvedic guidelines for proper diet and lifestyle modifications can significantly prevent complications of ischemic heart disease. Avoiding all the dietary and lifestyle causes of ASCVD is the first and foremost step towards its treatment. Wholesome diet avoiding those that increase kapha and medas, regular physical activities, healthy sleep habits, avoiding smoking, alcohol and other narcotics are the lifestyle modifications that need to be adopted. Apart from this, it is important to maintain a pleasant environment and practice Yoga and meditation to avoid stress and anxiety.

Yukti Vyapashara Chikitsa - Medical Management

While treating ASCVD, it is important to note that acute and severe manifestations should be treated according to established modern medicine protocols for emergency management of Acute Coronary syndrome. Chronic conditions and patients who have been stabilized after an acute phase can be rehabilitated effectively integrating Ayurveda treatment principles which are discussed below.

Chikitsa sutra of Kaphaja and Sannipataja Hridroga as mentioned in Trimarmiya Chikitsa Adhyaya

In the case of kaphaja hridroga, swedana (Sudation therapy), vamana (therapeutic vomiting), langhana (reducing therapy), kaphahara annapana (kapha reducing food and drinks) are indicated. For sannipataja hridroga, langhana (reducing therapy) and hitanna (wholesome food) are indicated. One must also consider the dominant dosha involved and treat accordingly. (Charaka Samhita Chikitsa Sthana 26/96–99)

Treatment for Angina/Hrit Shoola

Pain in the chest is the predominant symptom in this case and treatment is suggested based on the time of onset of pain with respect to digestion. If the pain occurs during ingestion of food, devadaru, kushta, tilvaka, saindhava and sauvarchala lavana with vidanga in hot water along with ativisha is indicated. If pain occurs after digestion, Sneha Virechana or purgation with eranda taila (castor oil) should be administered. If there is pain during digestion phala virechana, purgation with aragwadha fruit is given and if it is always there, mula virechana, purgation with root of trivrit is the drug of choice. (Charaka Samhita Chikitsa Sthana 26/101–102)

Shodhana Chikitsa

Shodhana procedures must be done mildly with caution or may be avoided in patients who have advanced CVD. The heart being a vital marma, all shodhana procedures especially vamana is normally contraindicated (Charaka Samhita Siddhi Sthana 2/8). In certain conditions when the patient has good strength to withstand the treatment procedure and when the disease is not very severe and acute, Shodhana procedures may be administered. In vataja and pittaja conditions - virechana is preferred as the disease originates from amashaya. In kaphaja conditions (Charaka Samhita Chikitsa Sthana 26/81–91), Vamana (therapeutic emesis) may be administered, but with caution (Sushruta Samhita Uttara Tantra 43/17). Basti can be administered in vataja hridroga in early stages and for prevention. (Sushruta Samhita Uttara Tantra 43/14)

Shamana Chikitsa

After Shodhana Karma or for patients who cannot withstand shodhana procedures, shamana chikitsa (pacification) or long-term maintenance therapy is advised. Medicinal herbs are known to have beneficial effects on CVD and drugs that have hridya (cardioprotective) property such as arjuna, sthira, bilwa are specially indicated [47]. These hridya drugs can be classified based on their action on doshas such as sthira and dashamoola are vatahara- hridya, padma, kooshmanda, vidari and yashtimadhu are pittahara-hridya and arjuna, guggulu and lashuna are kaphahara-Hridya drugs. In a nutshell, the main treatment protocol involves the following components- Deepana (drugs which increases appetite), pachana (digestive), vata anulomana (mild laxatives)- to correct the course of Vata, Sroto Shodhaka-to cleanse channels - in atherosclerosis, rakta prasadaka (blood purifiers), medho hara- (hypolipidemics), mutrala (diuretics) like gokushura to decrease blood volume, medhya andmano dosha hara (nootropic/psycho supportive drugs) such as brahmi, kooshmanda etc, brimhana/Rasayana-(nourishing/rejuvenative) and vishahara (antitoxic) measures.

Satvavajaya Chikitsa

Psychological and spiritual counselling for mind and stress management is a vital aspect of treatment. Dhee-to develop proper thinking, dhairya - to develop mental courage and atma vidya-to gain self-knowledge, awareness and spiritual insight are the important aspects of satvavajaya chikitsa. (Ashtanga Hridaya Sutra Sthana 1/26)

Sadvritta and Rasayana Chikitsa

Following healthy daily and seasonal regimen- dinacharya & ritu charya will help prevent recurrence of CVD. Ideal social behavior (Sadvritta & Achara Rasayana) and Rasayana Chikitsa especially- Hridya Rasayana according to predominant Dosha can be continued for long term maintenance.

Conclusion

ASCVD being the leading cause of death worldwide despite advancement in modern medicine, there is a dire need to explore new solutions to tackle this disease. In Ayurveda, this condition can be correlated to Kaphaja Hridroga which occurs because of dhamani pratichaya/atherosclerosis.

Excessive indulgence in etiological factors that vitiate kapha pradhana tridosha, like unwholesome diet and lifestyle, psychological stress or even certain infections may lead to conditions like HTN, dyslipidemia or inflammatory conditions which in turn lead to endothelial dysfunction/Kha-vaigunya. This then provokes atherosclerosis or dhamani pratichaya. The major blood vessels that supply blood to the heart get blocked and this may lead to ischemia of the heart causing severe complications that can be fatal. At this stage it becomes a sannipataja vyadhi, vitiating all the tridoshas and needs emergency management. Ayurveda explains that prevention is always the first line of treatment and thus gives utmost importance to avoiding etiological factors and adopting proper diet and lifestyle modifications. While the acute and severe complications like MI should be managed as an emergency by the conventional medicine system, Ayurveda can offer solutions for management of chronic, dormant ASCVD and for rehabilitation after the initial emergency management of the acute phase. Pharmacological management of this condition through Ayurveda involves drugs and therapies that are deepana, pachana, kapha - medo hara, vatanulomana, Srotoshodhaka, hridya, rakta prasadaka, manodosha hara and vishahara in properties. For long term management appropriate hridya rasayanas may be administered. It is also very crucial to manage psychological stress through appropriate satvavajaya chikitsa. Collaborative research and efforts to integrate these principles into the conventional ways of managing ASCVD may be an effective solution to reduce morbidity and mortality due to this condition.
