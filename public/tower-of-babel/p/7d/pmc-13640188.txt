RNA-binding protein RBM47 promotes endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition and neointimal hyperplasia in arteriovenous fistulas
Authors: Zongda Li, Xiaoyu Lan, Chengwei Zhang, Tingting Hu, Shuang Yao, Yao Zhao, Xinying Yu, Xiaozhu Sun, Huimin Ma, Qin Wang, Haihai Liang, Jundong Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArteriovenous fistula (AVF) is the preferred vascular access for hemodialysis in patients with end-stage renal disease. However, AVF stenosis, a severe complication, significantly impairs the patient’s quality of life. The mechanisms underlying AVF stenosis are multifactorial, with neointima (NI) hyperplasia within the fistula being a key contributor. While the involvement of RNA-binding proteins in the pathogenesis of various vascular diseases has garnered increasing attention, their role in autogenous AVFs remains poorly understood.MethodsDifferentially expressed genes in a mouse AVF model were identified through public database analysis, and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) confirmed RBM47 as the most significantly altered gene. Endothelial-specific RBM47 overexpression was achieved by local delivery of adeno-associated virus (AAV)-RBM47 using F127 hydrogel. Hematoxylin-eosin staining was used to assess neointimal thickness in human and mouse AVF tissues. Background

In recent years, chronic kidney disease (CKD) has emerged as a significant global public health concern. Patients with end-stage renal disease rely on renal replacement therapies to sustain their quality of life, with hemodialysis being the most widely used option [1]. Arteriovenous fistula (AVF) is the preferred vascular access method recommended by clinical guidelines for blood purification treatments [2]. Despite its advantages, including high patency and low infection rates, AVF is still prone to the serious complication of stenosis, which severely impacts patient well-being. neointima (NI) hyperplasia in AVF is a major contributor to this stenosis [3–5]. Factors such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and preoperative arterial function may influence endothelial cells undergoing endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), thereby promoting neointima hyperplasia in the fistula [6–8].

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) are key regulators of RNA molecules, influencing their post-transcriptional processing, stability, localization, and translation [9, 10]. Increasing evidence highlights the critical role of RBPs in various cardiovascular diseases, including coronary atherosclerosis, cardiac fibrosis, and pulmonary hypertension [11–14]. RBPs are central to mRNA alternative splicing (AS), a process in which precursor mRNA (pre-mRNA) selectively retains or omits specific exons during splicing, generating diverse mRNA isoforms that translate into functionally distinct proteins [15–17]. This mechanism significantly enhances the complexity and diversity of gene expression, playing a pivotal role in tissue development, cell differentiation, and stress responses [18]. Dysregulation of AS is closely linked to numerous diseases. Advances in high-throughput sequencing have led to the identification of numerous splice variants, which may exert various functions within the organism. AS not only serves as a key node in the gene expression regulatory network but also responds to environmental stimuli, such as hypoxia, inflammation, and shear stress, to rapidly modulate cellular functions, making it an essential mechanism in the dynamic regulation of pathological processes [19].

This study analyzed transcriptomic data from preoperative veins and postoperative AVFs, with a focus on RBPs. A significant increase in the expression of RBM47 was observed in the AVF group, suggesting its potential role in modulating EndMT through AS. This discovery provides insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying neointima hyperplasia in AVF and offers potential targets for intervention and therapeutic strategies aimed at preventing and treating AVF stenosis. Furthermore, it expands the clinical applicability of RBPs and AS in vascular diseases.

Method

Data access

The data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The data (Agilent submission) supporting the findings of this study are openly available in the GEO repository at the following links: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE119296 and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE220796 [20, 21]. The RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data for this study have been published in the GEO database and can be accessed via the following link: http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/1336413. The code used for the analyses is provided in the Supplemental Material.

WGCNA network construction and module identification

Gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed with modifications using the R package, as previously described. First, samples were clustered, and three outlier samples were identified and removed based on the clustering results. A stepwise approach was then used to construct co-expression networks in WGCNA [22]. The R function pick SoftThreshold was applied to determine the optimal soft-thresholding power (β) for network construction. Modules were identified through hierarchical clustering and the dynamic tree cut function. Gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM) were calculated to relate the modules to clinical traits. Relevant module gene information was extracted for further analysis, and the network of the pink module was visualized.

Identification of differentially expressed genes and alternative splicing events in the dataset

Differential gene expression analysis and visualization were performed using R version 4.2.1. The R packages DESeq2 and edgeR were employed. Differential expression analysis of the raw count matrix was carried out using the DESeq2 package, following standard procedures. The raw count matrix was normalized using the Variance Stabilizing Transformation (VST) method from DESeq2 [23]. For the identification and quantification of AS events, rMATS software (version 4.1.1) was used, operating in Python. rMATS is a statistical model-based tool designed specifically to detect differential splicing events between RNA-seq sample groups [24]. The software calculates the P-value and false discovery rate (FDR) for each splicing event and assesses changes in exon inclusion levels (ΔPSI, percent spliced in) between groups to identify differential splicing events. rMATS categorizes five major types of AS: skipped exons (SE), 3’ alternative splice sites (A3SS), 5’ alternative splice sites (A5SS), mutually exclusive exons (MXE), and intron retention (IR). In this study, the criteria for selecting differentially spliced events were FDR < 0.05 and |ΔPSI| ≥ 0.1.

Human sample collection

Sample collection was conducted by the Blood Purification Center of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University, and the experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the hospital. All procedures involving human samples adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to surgery. Preoperative venous samples were collected from uremic patients scheduled for AVF creation, whereas AVF vessel samples were obtained from excised stenotic segments of patients undergoing revision surgery for AVF stenosis. Control venous samples and stenotic AVF samples were unpaired and derived from different patients (n = 4 per group). This non-paired design was mainly due to the clinical difficulty in obtaining paired human AVF specimens, as stenotic AVF tissues are rarely surgically excised because percutaneous transluminal angioplasty is commonly used for AVF stenosis. To reduce potential sampling and processing bias, all samples were collected according to consistent inclusion criteria and processed using standardized procedures. The available baseline clinical characteristics of the included patients are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Some samples for immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Cat# P0099, Beyotime, China), whereas samples for PCR and Western blotting were frozen at − 80 °C for further analysis.

Animals model

Male C57BL/6 mice (22–25 g, Animal Center of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University) were housed under a 12-hour light/dark cycle at 22–24 °C with 50 ± 10% humidity, with free access to food and water. Mice were acclimated for at least one week prior to experimentation. Anesthesia was induced using 2%–3% isoflurane, and once adequate anesthesia was achieved, the mice were positioned supine and secured on a small animal surgical platform. The inferior vena cava and aorta were surgically exposed. In the experimental group, an AVF was created by puncturing both the distal inferior vena cava and the aorta using a 25-gauge needle. In the control group, only the aorta was punctured [25]. The success of the AVF model was confirmed by observing arterial blood flow in the inferior vena cava. Postoperatively, the mice were kept warm, and analgesia was administered with buprenorphine (1 mg/kg) twice daily at 12-hour intervals. Fourteen days after surgery, mice were euthanized. Briefly, animals were deeply anesthetized with 3%–5% isoflurane until complete loss of consciousness was confirmed by the absence of reflex responses. Cervical dislocation was then performed as a secondary method to ensure death. This combined approach was used to minimize animal suffering and is consistent with institutional and international guidelines for animal welfare. Tissue samples were subsequently collected for further analysis.

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) -RBM47-overexpressing mice

An AAV vector carrying green fluorescent protein (GFP)-RBM47 was constructed by Cyagen Biosciences (China). A volume of 10 µL of the AAV solution (titer: 1012) was mixed with 50 µL of Pluronic F-127 hydrogel (Cat#P2443, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 4 °C. The resulting mixture was locally applied to the exposed vessels. After four weeks, vascular tissues were harvested, and transfection efficiency was assessed through quantitative Western blot.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Human and mouse tissues were rinsed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Cat# C0221A, Beyotime, China) and fixed in PFA. The tissues were then embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 3 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Cat# C0105S, Beyotime, China) was performed to assess cellular morphology. The mean neointimal area, luminal area, ratio of luminal area to neointimal area, and the ratio of neointima to media plus adventitia were quantified using ImageJ software. For immunohistochemistry, sections were blocked with 10% goat serum (Cat# C0265, Beyotime, China) for 40 min and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C. After washing with PBS containing 0.5% Tween-20 (Cat# 9005-64-5, Solarbio, China), sections were incubated with biotin-labeled secondary antibodies at room temperature. Following additional washes, the sections were treated with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogenic solution using a peroxidase substrate kit (Cat#ZLI-9017, ZSGB-BIO, China). Counterstaining was performed with HE. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, sealed with neutral resin, and mounted with coverslips. Primary antibodies included anti-RBM47 (1:100, Cat# 23902-1-AP, ProteinTech, China), anti-α-SMA (1:100, Cat# 14395-1-AP, ProteinTech, China), anti-CD31 (1:150, Cat# 11265-1-AP, ProteinTech, China) and anti-VIMENTIN (1:50, Cat# 10366-1-AP, ProteinTech, China).

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) via microwave heating. After blocking with immunostaining blocking solution, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Cat# P0131, Beyotime, China), and the slides were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium for fluorescence microscopy. For cell immunofluorescence, cells cultured on coverslips were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked, and then incubated with primary and secondary antibodies as described above. After DAPI nuclear staining and mounting, images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. Primary antibodies included anti-RBM47 (1:50, Cat# 23902-1-AP, ProteinTech, China) and anti-CD31 (1:100, Cat# 11265-1-AP, ProteinTech, China).

Cell culture and treatments

The human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) line was purchased from Shanghai Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology (China). Cells were cultured in HUVEC-specific medium (Cat#ZQ-1304-B, Shanghai Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. To induce HUVEC migration, proliferation, and EndMT-related phenotypic changes, cells were treated with 5 ng/mL transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) (Cat#HY-P7118, MedChemExpress, USA) for 48 h, after which RBM47 expression was evaluated.

Cell migration

For wound healing assays, HUVECs were seeded into culture inserts (ibidi, Germany). After 24 h, the inserts were removed, and images were captured at 0 and 24 h using a Leica microscope to assess wound closure. For Transwell migration assays, HUVECs suspended in 200 µL of serum-free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) were seeded into the upper chambers of 24-well Transwell inserts. The lower chambers were filled with 600 µL of DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). After 24 h, the migrated cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with crystal violet. Cells remaining on the upper surface were gently removed using a cotton swab, and images of the migrated cells were acquired using a Leica microscope.

Preparation of plasmid expression vectors and small interfering RNA (siRNA)

RBM47 siRNA and corresponding negative control siRNA were synthesized by OBiO Technology (China). Overexpression plasmids, including FLAG-RBM47 (OE-RBM47), FLAG-SEC31A-△13 (OE-SEC31A-△13), and a negative control vector (OE-NC), were also designed and synthesized by OBiO Technology. Transfections of siRNA or pcDNA plasmids into HUVECs were performed using Lipo8000 reagent (Cat#C0533, Beyotime, China).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using Trizol reagent (Cat#ET101-01-V2, TransGen Biotech, China). After measuring RNA concentration, cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Cat#R323-01, Vazyme, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed with AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Cat#R323-01, TransGen Biotech, China). Each sample was analyzed in duplicate, and GAPDH was used as an internal reference for normalization. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Agarose gel electrophoresis

For agarose gel electrophoresis, a 1.5% gel was prepared by dissolving agarose powder (Cat# YZ-YZQZT, Solarbio, China) in TAE (Cat# T1060, Solarbio, China) electrophoresis buffer, then heating it in a microwave until fully dissolved. After cooling to 50–60 °C, Gel-Red (10 µL per 100 mL, 1:10,000 dilution, Cat#D0139, Beyotime, China) was added, and the solution was poured into a casting tray with combs inserted. Once solidified, the gel was placed in an electrophoresis tank filled with TAE buffer. Samples were loaded into the wells, and electrophoresis was carried out at 120 V for 40 min until the bromophenol blue dye front approached the bottom of the gel. DNA bands were visualized under UV light, and images were captured using a gel documentation system.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from tissues or cells using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Cat#P0013B, Beyotime, China). Protein concentrations were determined with a protein quantification kit (Cat#P1045, Beyotime, China), and equal protein amounts were loaded for each sample. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) was performed using a Protein A/G Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit (Cat# PB201-01 Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Cat# PG112, Epizyme Biotech, China) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cat#ZB-2305 & Cat#ZB-2306, ZSGB-BIO, China). Protein bands were visualized using a Bio-Rad imaging system. Both target and reference proteins were detected on the same membrane. Primary antibodies included anti-RBM47 (1:500, Cat# 23902-1-AP, ProteinTech), anti-α-SMA (1:1000, Cat# 14395-1-AP, ProteinTech), anti-CD31 (1:1000, Cat# 11265-1-AP, ProteinTech), anti-p-SMAD3 (1:1000, Cat# AP0727, ABclonal), anti-SMAD3 (1:1000, Cat# A16913, ABclonal), anti-DYKDDDDK tag antibody (1:2000, Cat# 66008-4-Ig, ProteinTech), anti-SNAIL (1:1000, Cat# 340942, ZenBio, China), anti-SLUG (1:1000, Cat# 213286, Biodragon, China) and anti-GAPDH (1:50000, Cat# 60004-1-Ig, ProteinTech).

RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation assay (RIP)

HUVECs were lysed for 10 min using cell lysis buffer supplemented with protease and RNase inhibitors (P0102, Geneseed, China). The lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with magnetic beads conjugated to either RBM47 antibody (RBM47 group) or control IgG (IgG group). After immunoprecipitation, RNA was extracted using a spin-column-based method, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and analyzed by PCR.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0. For two-group comparisons, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, Welch’s corrected t-test, or Mann–Whitney U test was used according to data distribution and variance equality. For comparisons among more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc tests was performed. Unless otherwise stated, in vitro experiments were independently repeated at least three times. n represents independent biological replicates, including patient samples, individual mice, or independent cell culture experiments; technical replicates were used only to ensure measurement reliability and were not treated as independent samples. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Upregulation of RBM47 expression in human and mouse models

Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the GSE119296 dataset from the GEO database. As this study focused on RBPs in the context of AVF remodeling, the top ten differentially expressed genes annotated as RBPs in GeneCards were identified for further analysis (Fig. 1A-C, Figure S1A). The expression of these genes was subsequently validated through quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) in both sham and AVF mouse models, revealing a significant upregulation of RBM47 and YBX2 in AVF tissues (Fig. 1D, Figure S1B). Motif analysis suggested that RBM47 likely interacts with splicing-related exonic motifs (Fig. 1E). Based on these findings, RBM47 was selected for further investigation.

In human tissue samples, AVF and control venous tissues were obtained from patients, with results showing a significant elevation of RBM47 expression in AVF tissues compared to control veins (Fig. 1F). Western blot analysis confirmed this upregulation at the protein level (Fig. 1G). H&E staining revealed marked neointimal formation in AVF tissues compared to normal veins (Fig. 1H). Considering the role of EndMT in neointima hyperplasia during AVF stenosis, the cellular localization of RBM47 was further examined. Immunofluorescence co-staining with the endothelial marker CD31 demonstrated that RBM47 expression was localized to the neointimal region and co-localized with CD31 (Fig. 1H), suggesting that RBM47 may be critical in regulating AVF remodeling through endothelial cell modulation.

In the AVF mouse model, RBM47 expression was also significantly upregulated compared to the sham group. Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the co-localization of RBM47 and CD31 in the neointimal region (Fig. 1I-J). Collectively, these results suggest that RBM47 plays a pivotal regulatory role in neointima hyperplasia during AVF remodeling.

RBM47 knockdown inhibits proliferation, migration, and EndMT-related changes in HUVECs

The above findings suggest that RBM47 plays a pivotal role in AVF remodeling via endothelial cells; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Thus, RBM47 may regulate EndMT in endothelial cells, contributing to neointima hyperplasia.

To test this hypothesis, EndMT was induced in HUVECs using TGF-β. Initially, this study assessed the dose-dependent effects of TGF-β and observed that 5 ng/mL TGF-β significantly upregulated RBM47 expression (Fig. 2A-B), as confirmed by qRT-PCR(Fig. 2C). Subcellular fractionation revealed that RBM47 primarily localized to the nucleus, with expression further elevated upon TGF-β stimulation, as confirmed by confocal microscopy (Fig. 2D-F). Next, this study knocked down RBM47 in HUVECs using siRNA and confirmed knockdown efficiency by qRT-PCR and Western blot (Fig. 2G-H). Given the critical roles of cell proliferation and migration in AVF remodeling, the impact of RBM47 knockdown on these processes was evaluated. CCK-8 assays showed a significant reduction in HUVEC proliferation following RBM47 silencing (Fig. 2I). Scratch wound and Transwell migration assays confirmed that RBM47 knockdown impaired HUVEC migration (Fig. 2J). At the molecular level, TGF-β treatment reduced endothelial marker CD31 expression and upregulated mesenchymal marker α-SMA, SNAIL and SLUG, while RBM47 knockdown restored CD31 expression and reduced α-SMA, SNAIL and SLUG expression (Fig. 2K). These results suggest that RBM47 knockdown not only inhibits HUVEC proliferation and migration but also attenuates TGF-β1-induced EndMT-related molecular and phenotypic changes, highlighting its potential role in regulating endothelial cell function during neointima hyperplasia.

Upregulation of RBM47 enhances proliferation, migration, and EndMT in HUVECs

To further explore the functional role of RBM47 in endothelial cells, its impact on cell proliferation, migration, and EndMT was examined through overexpression experiments. RBM47-overexpressing plasmids were transfected into HUVECs, and overexpression efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blot (Fig. 3A and E). Subsequently, the effects of RBM47 overexpression (OE-RBM47) on HUVEC biological behavior were assessed. Compared to the control group, OE-RBM47 significantly enhanced both HUVEC proliferation and migration (Fig. 3B-D). At the molecular level, OE-RBM47 significantly reduced CD31 expression and increased α-SMA, SNAIL and SLUG expression, indicating that RBM47 may promote EndMT (Fig. 3E). These results suggest that RBM47 upregulation enhances HUVEC proliferation, migration, and EndMT, implying that RBM47 may play a pro-fibrotic role in the development of neointima hyperplasia.

RBM47 promotes neointimal hyperplasia in the RBM47 overexpression mouse AVF model

To investigate the role of RBM47 in vivo, a mixture of AAV-RBM47 and Pluronic gel was applied to the surface of the inferior vena cava in mice. In AAV-RBM47-treated AVF tissues, RBM47 protein expression was significantly upregulated (Fig. 3F). H&E staining revealed that the neointimal thickness in AAV-RBM47 mice was significantly increased compared to the control group (Fig. 3G). Additionally, immunohistochemical staining showed that, compared to the control group, the expression levels of α-SMA and vimentin were significantly elevated in AAV-RBM47 mice, while CD31 expression was notably reduced (Fig. 3H). These results suggest that RBM47 overexpression promotes neointimal formation in vivo.

RBM47 may regulate EndMT and AVF remodeling through alternative splicing

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying RBM47’s role in AVF remodeling, WGCNA was performed on the GSE220796 dataset from the GEO database (Figure S2, Fig. 4A–B). The analysis identified the pink module as the most strongly correlated with postoperative AVF remodeling (Fig. 4C–E). To validate the regulatory effects of RBM47 on endothelial cell gene expression, RNA-seq was conducted on normal HUVECs and RBM47-overexpressing (OE-RBM47) cells (Fig. 4F). Gene enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes revealed significant involvement in biological processes such as endothelial cell proliferation, nuclear DNA replication, and SMAD protein signal transduction (Fig. 4G). Given the predominant nuclear localization of RBM47 in HUVECs and the role of nuclear RBPs in regulating AS (Fig. 4H), AS events in both the GSE220796 dataset and OE-RBM47 cells were analyzed. Five major types of AS events were identified, with SE being the most prevalent (Fig. 4I and L). Further intersection of the genes undergoing SE events in the GSE220796 dataset, OE-RBM47 HUVECs, and those associated with the pink module revealed several candidate genes — PSME3IP1, MYL12A, LCORL, SEC31A, and HDLBP — that may be regulated by RBM47 through SE events (Fig. 4M). These results suggest that RBM47 may promote AVF remodeling by modulating AS events that drive EndMT in vascular endothelial cells.

RBM47 promotes EndMT by regulating SEC31A-Δ13 expression and activating the phosphorylated SMAD3 (p-SMAD3) pathway

Previous studies have shown that SEC31A interacts with RBM47 and influences lipid metabolism in vascular endothelial cells. To validate this interaction further, RIP assays were performed in HUVECs, confirming direct binding between SEC31A mRNA and RBM47 (Fig. 5A). Binding structure prediction and visualization using AlphaFold3 revealed distinct interaction sites between RBM47 and SEC31A mRNA (Fig. 5B). Visualization of RNA-seq data from normal and RBM47-overexpressing (OE-RBM47) HUVECs using rMATS2 sashimi plots demonstrated a significantly reduced skipping event of exon 13 in SEC31A in OE-RBM47 cells, a finding that was experimentally validated by agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 5C–D). To further investigate, an SEC31A-Δ13 expression plasmid was constructed, and its expression efficiency was confirmed through agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 5E). Functionally, CCK-8 assays showed that overexpression of SEC31A-Δ13 significantly inhibited TGF-β-induced HUVEC proliferation (Figure S3A). Scratch wound healing and Transwell migration assays confirmed that SEC31A-Δ13 significantly suppressed TGF-β-induced HUVEC migration (Fig. 5F). At the molecular level, SEC31A-Δ13 overexpression under TGF-β stimulation restored CD31 expression while reducing α-SMA, SNAIL and SLUG expression (Fig. 5G). Additionally, co-overexpression of SEC31A-Δ13 in OE-RBM47 HUVECs significantly attenuated the pro-proliferative and pro-migratory effects induced by RBM47 overexpression (Fig. 5H, Figure S3B). SEC31A-Δ13 overexpression also reversed the RBM47-induced downregulation of CD31 and upregulation of α-SMA, SNAIL and SLUG. RNA-seq enrichment analysis revealed a strong association with the SMAD protein signal transduction pathway, prompting further investigation into the classical p-SMAD3 pathway. Western blot analysis demonstrated that RBM47 overexpression significantly increased p-SMAD3 expression, whereas co-overexpression of SEC31A-Δ13 suppressed this activation (Fig. 5I). To further explore the biochemical relationship between SEC31A-Δ13 and SMAD3, we performed a co-immunoprecipitation assay in HUVECs expressing FLAG-tagged SEC31A-Δ13. SMAD3 was detected in the anti-FLAG immunoprecipitated complex but not in the IgG control, suggesting that SEC31A-Δ13 physically associates with SMAD3 under cellular conditions (Figure S3C). Collectively, these results suggest that RBM47-mediated SEC31A alternative splicing is associated with reduced SEC31A-Δ13 generation, increased p-SMAD3 activation, and EndMT-related phenotypes. These findings support p-SMAD3 as a major downstream pathway involved in RBM47–SEC31A-Δ13-mediated endothelial phenotypic transition.

Discussion

This study identified, for the first time, the significant upregulation of the RBP RBM47 in both human and murine AVF models. Using an AAV-mediated RBM47 overexpression mouse model, this study revealed that RBM47 promotes neointima hyperplasia during AVF remodeling. Vascular endothelial cells, which form a single-layer structure lining the vessel lumen, play a critical role in sensing and integrating hemodynamic, mechanical, and biochemical signals [26]. During AVF remodeling, hemodynamic disturbances, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress induce EndMT, which is recognized as a key source of neointimal cells [6, 8, 27, 28]. Our results reveal that RBM47 accelerates neointimal formation by promoting EndMT in vascular endothelial cells. Mechanistically, RBM47 regulates the AS of its downstream gene SEC31A, particularly suppresses SEC31A-Δ13 generation, thereby modulating the progression of EndMT. These findings uncover a novel molecular mechanism underlying neointima hyperplasia in AVF (Fig. 6).

Schematic illustration of the mechanism by which the RBM47-regulated SEC31A exon-skipping event mediates AVF neointimal hyperplasia through the SMAD3 signaling pathway. RBM47 regulates the exon-skipping event of SEC31A, thereby influencing the post-transcriptional alternative splicing process in endothelial cells. This regulation activates the TGF-β/SMAD3 signaling pathway, promoting endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition and leading to neointimal hyperplasia in AVF. This mechanism highlights the critical regulatory role of the RBM47–SEC31A-△13–SMAD3 axis in AVF remodeling

AVF stenosis develops from the combined effects of hemodynamic abnormalities, chronic inflammation, and dysregulated vascular remodeling. Epidemiological data indicate that the one-year patency rate after AVF surgery remains only 60%–70% [29, 30]. Although endovascular interventions, such as balloon angioplasty, can temporarily restore blood flow, restenosis occurs frequently, highlighting the urgent need for new biomarkers and molecular therapeutic targets [31, 32]. RBPs play critical roles in RNA splicing, stabilization, and complex formation and have emerged as significant regulators and potential drug targets in cardiovascular biology [33–36]. By binding to target RNAs, RBPs exert post-transcriptional control over gene expression, profoundly influencing cell fate and disease progression. Transcriptomic profiling of AVF tissues identified RBM47 as a differentially expressed RBP that is significantly upregulated during AVF remodeling.

RBM47 was initially identified in RNA screening studies of genes differentially expressed during embryonic development. It contains three classical RNA recognition motifs and exhibits a broad but tissue-specific expression pattern in humans and mice [37]. As an RNA-binding protein, RBM47 has been shown to regulate multiple layers of RNA metabolism, including alternative splicing, mRNA stability, translational efficiency, and RNA editing-related processes, thereby participating in the pathogenesis of several diseases, such as diabetic nephropathy, systemic lupus erythematosus, pancreatic cancer, and colorectal cancer [38, 39]. In the vascular field, RBM47 has mainly been identified through transcriptomic or genetic association analyses, including studies of peripheral vascular atherosclerosis [40]. However, whether RBM47 has a functional role in AVF remodeling, particularly in endothelial cell phenotypic transition and neointimal hyperplasia, has not been previously defined. In the present study, we found that RBM47 was markedly upregulated in both human stenotic AVF tissues and murine AVF tissues. In vivo, local AAV-mediated overexpression of RBM47 aggravated neointimal hyperplasia, whereas in vitro, TGF-β stimulation increased RBM47 expression in HUVECs, and functional assays demonstrated that RBM47 promoted endothelial proliferation, migration, and EndMT-related phenotypic changes. Thus, our study extends the known functions of RBM47 from EndMT and vascular disease-associated transcriptomic signatures to AVF-related endothelial remodeling.

Alternative RNA splicing is a key mechanism through which RBPs exert their biological functions. As understanding of transcriptomic complexity grows, AS has been recognized as a critical determinant of cell fate and disease progression [41]. In cardiovascular diseases, RBPs regulate selective splicing events that influence pathological outcomes. For example, hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) modulates the splicing of CaMK2γ, affecting calcium signaling in hypoxic cardiomyocytes, while RBM25 regulates CHOP-related apoptotic gene splicing, promoting cardiomyocyte apoptosis after myocardial infarction [42]. However, the role of AS in AVF remodeling remains poorly understood. rMATS analysis identified SE as the predominant form of AS in human AVF samples. To further explore RBM47-mediated splicing regulation, RNA-seq was performed on HUVECs with and without RBM47 overexpression, followed by rMATS analysis, which also revealed SE as the most prevalent event. Integration with WGCNA pinpointed key AS events closely associated with AVF remodeling. Under TGF-β stimulation, RBM47 binding to SEC31A was significantly increased, while expression of the SEC31A-Δ13 isoform was markedly reduced, suggesting that RBM47 may promote EndMT by modulating SEC31A-Δ13 splicing. It should be noted that our mouse AVF model was established under a normal physiological background, whereas most clinical AVFs develop in patients with CKD. The uremic environment is characterized by the accumulation of uremic toxins, chronic micro-inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic disturbance, all of which may influence endothelial function and phenotypic transition. Previous studies have shown that uremic toxins contribute to endothelial dysfunction and can induce inflammatory and oxidative stress responses in human endothelial cells [43, 44]. In addition, oxidative stress and tissue injury have been linked to alternative splicing regulation, suggesting that stress-related microenvironmental cues may reshape RNA splicing programs by affecting RNA-binding proteins and spliceosome-associated regulatory networks [45]. Thus, the uremic microenvironment may not only provide a pro-EndMT background but also act as an upstream modifier of RNA splicing regulation, potentially influencing the magnitude or pattern of RBM47-mediated SEC31A exon 13 splicing and downstream SMAD3 activation.

SEC31A is a critical component of the coat protein complex II (COPII) complex, involved in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)–to–Golgi transport pathway [46]. Previous studies have reported that RBM47 regulates SEC31A exon retention to modulate lipid metabolism and protein secretion [47]. Importantly, although RBM47 has been reported to regulate SEC31A alternative splicing, its role in AVF stenosis, EndMT, and downstream SMAD signaling has not been previously defined. Our data support a previously unrecognized RBM47–SEC31A-Δ13–SMAD3 axis in AVF-associated neointimal hyperplasia, thereby establishing a mechanistic link between RNA alternative splicing and AVF-specific vascular remodeling. Because TGF-β signaling involves multiple canonical and non-canonical downstream branches, we further examined additional signaling molecules. RBM47 overexpression predominantly enhanced SMAD3 phosphorylation under our experimental conditions. Moreover, SEC31A-Δ13 restoration attenuated RBM47-induced SMAD3 activation. These results suggest that SMAD3 is the major downstream signaling branch of the RBM47–SEC31A-Δ13 axis identified in this study. Nevertheless, the possible involvement of other pathways, cannot be completely excluded and warrants further investigation.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the limited number of human AVF samples may reduce the statistical robustness of our conclusions, and larger, ideally paired or longitudinal clinical cohorts are needed to further validate the clinical relevance of the RBM47–SEC31A-Δ13–SMAD3 axis. Second, this study did not establish an AVF model under CKD or uremic conditions, and our in vivo evidence was based on an RBM47 overexpression strategy without a complementary in vivo RBM47 loss-of-function model, such as endothelial-targeted RBM47 knockdown or endothelial-specific RBM47 knockout. Therefore, the effects of the uremic environment on RBM47 expression, SEC31A splicing regulation, and the therapeutic relevance of RBM47 suppression remain unclear. Future studies using CKD AVF models combined with endothelial-targeted RBM47 inhibition approaches are required to determine whether RBM47 suppression can attenuate or reverse AVF neointimal hyperplasia under clinically relevant conditions. Interestingly, previous studies suggest that RBM47 may inhibit epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in certain tissues, indicating a potential tissue-specific context for its function, which requires future exploration [48, 49]. Third, although SEC31A-Δ13 rescue attenuated RBM47-induced SMAD3 phosphorylation and EndMT, and our Co-IP data provide preliminary biochemical support for the physical association between SEC31A-Δ13 and SMAD3 in endothelial cells, this study did not directly determine whether this association represents a direct protein–protein interaction or whether endogenous SEC31A-Δ13 reduction alone is sufficient to activate SMAD3 signaling. Because SEC31A-Δ13 is an exon 13-skipping isoform, isoform-specific loss-of-function approaches require careful validation. Future studies using rigorously validated isoform-specific and complementary mechanistic approaches are needed to further clarify how SEC31A-Δ13 regulates SMAD3 activation and how this splice isoform may affect SEC31A-related ER–Golgi transport function.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study reveals a novel molecular mechanism by which RBM47 promotes AVF neointimal formation by regulating EndMT in vascular endothelial cells. Furthermore, the alternatively spliced isoform SEC31A-Δ13 was identified as a protective factor that suppresses TGF-β- and RBM47-induced EndMT. A deeper understanding of the RBM47–SEC31A–p-SMAD3 signaling axis may provide new molecular targets and mechanistic insights for the prevention and treatment of AVF-related neointima hyperplasia.
