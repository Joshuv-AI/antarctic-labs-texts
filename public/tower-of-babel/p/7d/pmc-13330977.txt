Shengjiangsan Upregulates miR-328-3p to Inhibit MAP3K9 in the Treatment of Sjogren Disease: A Bioinformatics Analysis
Authors: Yating Ren, Yihan Wang, Tao Hong, Wenyue Jiao, Dingqi Lu, Xinyi Yao, Donghai Zhou, Xinchang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveConventional treatments for Sjögren’s disease (SjD) have limited therapeutic efficacy, whereas Shengjiangsan (SJS) has been reported to ameliorate SjD, although further research is lacking. The aim of the present study was to investigate the effects exerted by, and underlying mechanisms of, SJS in the treatment of SjD through miRNA sequencing, bioinformatics analysis, and in vitro and in vivo experiments.MethodsKey miRNAs and their target genes involved in the effects of SJS on SjD were identified through microRNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis, and their targeting relationships were verified using a dual-luciferase assay. Animal and cell experiments were conducted to demonstrate the efficacy and underlying mechanisms of SJS in treating SjD.ResultsRNA sequencing showed that the expression of miR-328-3p in the model group with SjD was low and upregulated after SJS treatment, reaching levels comparable to those in the control group. Expression of its target gene, MAP3K9, was elevated in the model group and decreased after treatment. Simultaneously, dual-luciferase assays verified the targeting relationship between miR-328-3p and MAP3K9, and cell transfection experiments demonstrated that SJS inhibited MAP3K9 expression by increasing miR-328-3p levels. Additionally, experiments showed that SJS significantly increased the salivary flow rate in SjD mice, reduced inflammatory infiltration of SG lymphocytes, enhanced AQP5 protein expression, and decreased serum levels of inflammatory factors such as IgG, BAFF, IL-6, and IFN-γ.ConclusionSJS may targets miR-328-3p to inhibit MAP3K9 expression, alleviate inflammation in NOD/Ltj mice, reduce pathological damage in the SG, increase saliva secretion, and improve SjD symptoms.

Introduction

Sjögren disease (SjD) refers to a chronic autoimmune disease distinguished by the progressive impairment of the exocrine glands. It primarily manifests as dryness and multisystem damage caused by gland dysfunction.1 Currently, the etiology and pathogenesis of the disease have not been fully clarified, and disease-modifying drugs specifically for this condition have not been developed.2,3 Clinical management relies mainly on symptomatic treatment or therapies adapted from other autoimmune diseases, whose efficacy is often limited by drug-related adverse effects.4,5 Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop safer and more effective therapies. As a complementary approach, Traditional Chinese Medicine has been attracting growing attention.6–9

Shengjiangsan (SJS) is a classical traditional Chinese medicine formula comprising Rhei Radix et Rhizoma, Curcumae Longae Rhizoma, Bombyx Batryticatus, and Cicadae Periostracum, first documented during the Qing Dynasty, originally used for the treatment of febrile diseases.10 In recent years, SJS has been widely applied in clinical practice for diseases of the respiratory system, endocrine system, immune system, urinary system, and digestive system, with significant therapeutic effects. Modern pharmacological studies have demonstrated that those herbal components possess immunomodulatory, antihyperlipidemic and antitumor, as well as antibacterial and anti-infective properties effects.11–16 Our previous researches have demonstrated that SJS has a substantial therapeutic effect on SjD in long-term clinical practice,17 but its effect and mechanism lack of experimental evidence and requires further investigation.Recent researches indicated MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are non-coding RNA molecules (about 22 nucleotides in length) that regulate gene expression in a negative manner by either suppressing mRNA translation or enhancing its degradation. Dysregulated miRNA expression during SjD progression directly influences disease developments and prognosis, positioning miRNAs as promising therapeutic targets.5

This study performed miRNA sequencing and bioinformatics prediction, followed by multilevel experimental validation(in vivo animal models and in vitro cell transfection). It aimed to investigate the therapeutic effect of SJS on SjD mice and its underlying miRNA-related mechanisms. The results demonstrate that SJS produces a definite therapeutic effect in SjD mice. The mechanism is likely mediated through the upregulation of miR-328-3p, which inhibits MAP3K9 expression. These findings provide a scientific basis for the clinical application of this formula.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of SJS

SJS consisted of four crude drugs: Rhei Radix et Rhizoma (3g), Curcumae Longae Rhizoma (6g), Bombyx Batryticatus (3g), and Cicadae Periostracum (6g) (Table 1), obtained from the Outpatient Department of the Zhejiang Second Affiliated Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Hangzhou, China). Based on a standard 60-kg adult weight, the clinical dose of SJS (18 g/day) is equivalent to 0.3 g/kg/day. Mouse doses (2.73 and 5.46 g/kg/day of crude SJS) were calculated from the human clinical dose (0.3 g/kg/day) using a body surface area conversion factor of 9.1, representing the equivalent clinical dose (ECD) and twice the ECD, respectively. Soak the above herbs in water for 1 hour, then decoct twice, each for 1 hour. High Performance Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (HPLC-MS) Analysis of SJS

The concentrated water-soluble decoction was prepared as described above, and then lyophilized to obtain a dry powder. 0.1 g of the lyophilized SJS powder was completely dissolved in 1000 µL of 80% methanol. The mixture was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was then analyzed using an UltiMate 3000 RS HPLC coupled with a Q Exactive high-resolution mass spectrometer.

Animals

Twenty-four NOD/Ltj mice (female, 7 weeks old, specific pathogen-free) and six ICR mice (female, 7 weeks old, specific pathogen-free) were obtained and housed at Animal Experimental Research Center of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University. All mice were maintained under controlled conditions (temperature: 21–23°C; humidity: 50–60%; 12-h light/12-h dark cycle) with access to food and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Zhejiang Chinese Medical University Laboratory Animal Research Centre (ethical number: IACUC-202201-19), and were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 and the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Animals Experiments

ICR mice served as the Control group. NOD/Ltj mice were randomly divided into four groups (n=6 per group) using a random number table: the Model group, the Positive Control group (Pred group), and two SJS treatment groups (the SJSint group and SJShig group). The Control and Model groups received distilled water via oral gavage. The SJSint group and SJShig groups were administered SJS at doses of 2.73 g/kg and 5.46 g/kg, respectively. The Pred group received prednisone at 5 mg/kg. Each mice was administered once daily with a volume of 0.2mL. After 8 weeks of continuous administration, the mice were humanely euthanized via gradual exposure to increasing concentrations of carbon dioxide (CO2) at a displacement rate of 30% chamber volume per minute, followed by confirmation of cardiac and respiratory arrest, and the SG, spleen and serum samples were collected for further experiments.

Saliva Flow Rate Detection

After inhalation anesthesia with isoflurane, mice were intraperitoneally injected with pilocarpine (0.5 mg/kg; Yuanye, Wuhan, China). Saliva was collected using sterile cotton balls for 15 min, weighed before and after collection, and used to calculate the salivary flow rate. Salivary flow rate (μL/min) = saliva volume (μL) / 15 min.

Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining (HE)

The SGs were processed for HE staining, and histopathological alterations in SG tissue were visualized microscopically using a digital pathology scanning system to capture images. The focal index was scored. The number of lesions per 4 mm2 was calculated as the focal index score (FS).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded SG tissue sections were subjected to xylene dewaxing, ethanol hydration. After antigen retrieval and incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide, the sections were blocked via incubation with goat serum (room temperature, 30 min), then reacted with primary antibody AQP5 (4 °C, overnight) and thereafter with the secondary antibody (room temperature, 10 min). DAB development and hematoxylin counterstaining were performed. Slides were mounted, images captured using the digital pathology scanning system, and positive results interpreted. ImageScope software was used to calculate the positive area ratio as (positive area + strongly positive area)/(negative area + weakly positive area + positive area + strongly positive area) × 100.

Elisa

Mouse serum, HSG cells, and cell supernatants were collected. Levels of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) in cells were quantified via ELISA kits (Kedinuo, Fujian, China), while IL-6, BAFF, IgG, and IFN-γ in mouse serum were quantified via ELISA kits (MeiMian, Jiangsu, China).

The miRNA-Seq and Bioinformatics Analysis

SG samples (n = 3) from the Control, Model, and SJShig groups were selected for miRNA-sequencingat (including RNA purification, reverse transcription, sequencing and library construction) at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw data were processed through Fastx-Toolkit (https://github.com/agordon/fastx_toolkit) to obtain clean date, then the known miRNA and novel miRNA of each sample were identified with miRBase22.0 database (http://www.mirbase.org/). To establish each sample’s miRNA expression level, the transcripts per million (TPM) method was utilized. Differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMs) were identified through an absolute value of log2 ≥ 1 and false discovery rate <0. 05. Target gene predictions of miRNAs was executed via miRanda and RNAhybrid software, The criteria for determining candidate target genes are are as follows:score cutoff ≥160, energy cutoff ≤-20 kcal/moll (miRanda); number of hits per target ≥100, energy cutoff ≤-20 kcal/mol, p-value cutoff ≤0.01 (RNAhybrid). Venn, correlation, and principal component (PCA) analyses were performed between samples. Statistical analysis of inter-group DEMs and volcano plot analysis were carried out. KEGG and GO analysis were performoned on the DEMs and their target genes.

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay

Four groups of 293T cells were maintained in 24-well cell culture plates and transfected with respective plasmids, as detailed below. (three replicate wells per group): ① Mimic NC + Map3k9 NC, ② mmu-miR-328-3p + Map3k9 WT, ③ Mimic NC + Map3k9 MUT, and ④ mmu-miR-328-3p + Map3k9 MUT. Prepare 1× PLB cell lysis buffer. Resuspend Luciferase Assay Substrate in Luciferase Assay Buffer II (1 vial:10mL) to yield the LAR II detection buffer. Add 20μL1× PLB cell lysis buffer into each well, followed by 100μL of LARII solution. After mixing, load the sample into the luminescence detector to measure the initial luminescence value (corresponding to firefly luciferase activity).Then add 100μL Stop&Glo detection solution to terminate the first luminescence and initiate the second. After mixing, load the sample into the luminescence detector to measure the second luminescence value (corresponding to the detection of Renilla luciferase activity). The result is calculated as Renilla luciferase divided by firefly luciferase.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) Assay

96-well plates were used for inoculating HSG cells at a density of 1 × 104 per well. 48 h after SJS administration, added 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (BS350A, Biosharp, Anhui, China) to each well, and placed the plate at 37 °C for 1 h. Optical density at 450 nm was measured by a microplate reader. Based on the instructions, cell viability was determined.

Cell Culture and Transfection

HSG cells (HaoKe, Hangzhou, China) were cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C (with RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin). After the cells reached 70–80% confluence, they were transfected with PolyTrans (Sunya, Hangzhou, China) and miR-328- 3p inhibitor (Sunya, Hangzhou, China) and combined with SJS. After 48 h transfection, RNA and proteins were extracted, and supernatants were collected for following testing.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT–PCR)

Using TRIzol to extract total RNA from HSG cells and SG tissue,as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit and HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR served for the synthesis of miRNA and mRNA cDNAs. ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix and ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix were then utilized for subsequent quantification process. U6 and β-actin were used for miRNA and mRNA normalize, correspondingly. miRNA and mRNA relative expression levels of HSG cells and SG tissues were calculated via 2−ΔΔCT method. Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All quantitative data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. For data that conformed to a normal distribution and showed homogeneity of variance, comparisons between two independent groups were conducted using an unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons among three or more groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test for multiple comparisons. In cases where data did not follow a normal distribution or variances were unequal, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U-test (for two groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test (for multiple groups) was used. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Identification and HPLC-MS Analysis of SJS

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) was used to analyze and identify the active components of SJS, aiming to characterize its active components and establish quality control measures. The collected data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.3 software and cross-referenced against the mzCloud database. The results revealed that in both positive and negative ion modes, 886 compounds were identified in the mzCloud database from the SJS decoction, with 487 compounds scoring above 60 points. Based on TIC chromatograms and matching scoring criteria, multiple key active components were identified, including dl-arginine, l-isoleucine, l-norleucine, adenine, 4-indolecarbaldehyde, palmitoleic Acid, 1-stearoylglycerol, L-threonic acid, 2-furoic acid, gallic acid, oryzalin, and phenylacetaldehyde. The results are provided in Supplementary Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 1.

Based on the quantitative expression results, intergroup differential miRNA analysis was conducted to identify differentially expressed miRNAs(DEMs) between groups. The results demonstrated that, compared with the blank control group, 333 differentially expressed miRNAs were identified in the model group, including 153 significantly upregulated and 180 significantly downregulated. In contrast, compared with the model group, 286 differentially expressed miRNAs were detected in the treatment group, with 157 significantly upregulated and 129 significantly downregulated (Figure 2D–F).

Using cluster analysis, we examined the overlap of miRNAs that were downregulated in the model group and upregulated after SJS treatment. (Figure 2G) The differentially expressed miRNAs were clearly divided into several clusters. Among these clusters, one exhibited significant downregulation in the model group but was markedly upregulated after SJS treatment. This finding suggests that this cluster of miRNAs may play an important role in the process by which SJS improves SjD and includes mmu-miR-328-3p, which is among the top ten most abundant miRNAs.

Based on the DIANA-miRPath database, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that these DEMs were mainly enriched in the MAPK and AMPK signaling pathways and were closely related to biological functions such as protein binding and regulation of cellular processes (positive and negative) (Figure 3A and B). Furthermore, the KEGG and GO analyses of the target genes corresponding to these EDMs showed that they were also most enriched in the MAPK and AMPK signaling pathways and were related to single-organism cellular processes, protein binding, membrane-bound organelles, and other functions (Figure 3C and D). Since miR-328-3p exhibits high and trending (significant downregulation in the model group but markedly upregulation after SJS treatment) expression in the SG, we analyzed its target genes and identified 7 target genes (specifically, Nol4l, Map3k9, Plch2, Lsm14b, Slc2a1, Grin2b, Usb1). Among them, Map3k9 was directly related to MAPK signaling pathway (Supplementary Table 4). Meanwhile, the KEGG results of DEMs’ target genes showed that genes enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway also include MAP3K9 (Supplementary Table 5). Integrating differences in miRNA expression levels, variation trends, target gene prediction, and GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, we focused on miR-328-3p and its target gene MAP3K9. These results suggest that in the treatment of SjD with SJS, miR-328-3p is likely targeted to regulate MAP3K9 expression, thereby exerting therapeutic effects.

Therapeutic Effects of SJS on SjD Mice

SJS Increases the Salivary Flow Rate (SFR) in SjD Mice, Improves Histopathological Features of SGs, Reduces the Secretion of Inflammatory Factors, and Enhances AQP5 Protein Expression

Measurement of the salivary flow rate showed that the SFR of SjD mice at 8 and 12 weeks of age was significantly lower than that of the control group (P < 0.01). After 8 weeks of treatment with SJS and Pred, the SFR in the SJSint, SJShig and Pred groups increased significantly compared with the model group (P < 0.05) and approached the level of the control group (Figure 4A–C). SJS Reduces Serum IgG, BAFF, IL-6 and IFN-γ Levels in SjD Mice

ELISA results (Figure 4K–N) showed that serum IgG, BAFF, IFN-γ, and IL-6 levels in the model group were significantly higher than those in the normal control group (P < 0.01). After medium- and high-dose SJS treatment, the levels of these inflammatory factors were significantly reduced (P < 0.05), and serum BAFF, IFN-γ, and IL-6 levels were also significantly reduced in the Pred group (P < 0.01).

Discussion

Sjögren disease (SjD) is a refractory inflammatory autoimmune disease; however, there remains a significant unmet need for the treatment of a large SjD population.1–3 Our previous study showed that SJS has a definite therapeutic effect on SjD.17 In this study, animal experiments demonstrated that SJS improved the salivary flow rate of SjD mice (Figure 4A–C), improved SG histopathology (Figure 4D and E), increased AQP5 protein expression (Figure 4F and G), reduced the secretion of inflammatory factors (Figure 4H–J), and alleviated dryness symptoms. Serological examination showed that SJS reduced the level of peripheral inflammatory factors (Figure 4K–N). To a certain extent, SJS has been shown to be effective in treating SjD in animal models.

Recently, several miRNAs have been proposed as potential therapeutic targets in SjD.5 For example, inhibiting IFN-β-induced ISR restored hsa-miR-145-5p levels, indicating that ISR modulation may be a potential strategy to restore LSG cellular homeostasis in SjD patients.18 Multiple significantly differentially expressed miRNAs are present in pSjD patients’ T and B cells, such as hsa-let-7d-3p in T cells and hsa-miR-30b-5p in B cells. Their potential target mRNAs were enriched in disease-related pathways, indicating that abnormal miRNA expression in pSjD patients’ T/B cells may be linked to disease pathogenesis.19 The microRNA-125b derived by LGMSC-Exos may exert its effects by binding to its target gene PRDM1 (PR domain zinc finger protein 1), thereby providing a potential therapeutic target for SjD and a new direction for the application of exosomes.20 IFN-γ-dependent levels of hsa-miR-424-5p and hsa-miR-513c-3p affect the expression of key factors involved in cellular protein inhibition, and these factors regulate the secretory function of labial salivary glands (LSG) in patients with SjD.21

Here, miRNA-seq and bioinformatic analyses were performed on SGs from the control, model, and SJShig groups. A total of 397 co-expressed miRNA genes were identified (Figure 2C), and two groups of differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMs) were identified through intergroup comparisons. One group showed increased expression in the model group but decreased expression in SJSint, whereas the other group showed decreased expression in the model group but increased expression in SJSint (Figure 2D). Because miRNAs inhibit transcription or promote degradation of target genes, we focused on DEMs that were downregulated in the model group and upregulated after SJS treatment. GO and KEGG analyses of these DEMs showed enrichment mainly in the MAPK and AMPK signaling pathways and in biological functions such as protein binding and regulation of cellular processes (positive and negative) (Figure 3A and B). Furthermore, predicted target genes of these DEMs were also enriched in the MAPK and AMPK signaling pathways and were associated with single-organism cellular processes, protein binding, membrane-bound organelles, and other functions (Figure 3C and D).

We noticed this early in the study that miR-328- 3p was among the top twenty miRNAs with altered expression abundance, belonging to the group upregulated after SJS treatment (Figure 1). Further analysis revealed that miR-328-3p and its target gene, MAP3K9, were also enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway (Figure 3B and D). The MAPK signaling pathway plays an important role in diverse cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, survival, apoptosis, and immune responses.22–24 Based on these findings, we selected miR-328-3p and MAP3K9 as targets for follow-up studies and conducted dual-luciferase experiments to verify their interaction (Figure 5A).

Studies have shown that miR-328- 3p plays a role in various diseases. For example, inhibiting miR-328-3p represents a novel strategy for the efficient elimination of cancer stem cells (CSCs), which may help prevent tumor metastasis and recurrence in patients with epithelial ovarian cancer.25 AC008 exerts a critical role in OA pathogenesis through the miR-328-3p‒AQP1/ANKH pathway.26

As the direct target of miR-328- 3p in our study, MAP3K9 is an important upstream component of the MAPK pathway. As a mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase, it triggers the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), p38, and extracellular signal–regulated kinase (ERK) pathways to regulate cell growth, differentiation, migration, and apoptosis.27–29

To validate the miRNA-seq results, we first measured miR-328-3p and MAP3K9 mRNA levels in mouse SGs using RT-qPCR and found that SJS upregulated miR-328-3p and downregulated MAP3K9 expression (Figure 5B and C). Furthermore, we used miR-328 - 3p inhibitors in HSG cells and confirmed that miR-328-3p expression was inhibited and MAP3K9 expression was increased (Figure 5E and F). At the same time, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels in HSG cells and their supernatants were measured, and these inflammatory factors were found to decrease after SJS intervention (Figure 5G–L).

Overall, this study is the first to use miRNA sequencing to analyze the effect of SJS on the miRNA expression profile of SG tissues in SjD mice, revealing a possible molecular mechanism of SJS in the treatment of SjD. Animal and cell experiments verified the regulatory effect of SJS on the miR-328-3p/MAP3K9 signaling pathway. SJS may exert synergistic effects by inhibiting inflammation, alleviating glandular damage, and restoring secretory function through this pathway. This study new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying SjD treatment with traditional Chinese compounds and lays a theoretical foundation for developing miRNA-targeted intervention strategies with promising clinical translation potential.

Our study had some limitations. Firstly, miR-328- 3p and MAP3K9 were not verified in patients due to difficulties in obtaining clinical samples after treatment and limitations in antibody availability. Second, SJS treatment alters miR-328-3p expression in SjD mice and HSG cells, but its specific mechanism of action remains unclear. Furthermore, MAP3K9 was associated with ERK/JNK activation and was highly correlated with the SjD inflammatory phenotype. No experiments were conducted to further explore the effect of SJS on these pathways. Therefore, we plan to conduct more focused and in-depth studies along these lines in the future.

Conclusion

In conclusion, SJS ameliorated Sjögren disease primarily by upregulating miR-328-3p to inhibit MAP3K9, thereby modulating inflammatory and metabolic pathways, restoring salivary gland function, and reducing key inflammatory factors, thus revealing a novel miRNA-dependent mechanism underlying its therapeutic effects.
