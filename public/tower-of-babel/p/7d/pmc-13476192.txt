Descending Necrotizing Mediastinitis Across Age and Host-Status Groups: Diagnosis, Treatment Phases, and Outcomes in the Literature, with an Illustrative Case
Authors: Constantin Ionescu, Claudia Simona Cambrea, Ionuţ Bulbuc, Elena Neagu, Bogdan Marian Caraban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDescending necrotizing mediastinitis (DNM) is a rare but life-threatening infection originating from odontogenic, tonsillopharyngeal, or deep cervical sources, and propagating through the cervical fascial planes into the mediastinum. Despite advances in cross-sectional imaging, thoracic surgery, intensive care medicine, and antimicrobial therapy, DNM remains associated with mortality ranging from 11 to 40% across contemporary series. The evidence base is fragmented and lacks a unified framework integrating anatomical, physiological, microbiological, and critical care dimensions. The aims of this study are to map the DNM literature across five predefined age and host-status subgroups, to synthesize differences in source infection, diagnostic pathway, mediastinal extent, treatment phases, ICU burden, microbiological evolution, and outcomes, to appraise methodological quality, and to identify future research priorities.MethodsA scoping review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA-ScR, with incorporation of a prespecified illustrative institutional case for contextual comparison and pregnancy subgroup representation. Structured searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science were supplemented by systematic citation chaining and revised eligibility criteria, allowing the inclusion of mixed infectious mediastinitis studies when the DNM subgroup could be clearly distinguished, yielding 18 studies (881 patients) in the final synthesis. A prospectively documented institutional case of DNM in pregnancy was analyzed separately from the PRISMA-tracked literature and used only to contextualize the pregnancy subgroup and to compare the proposed phase-based framework with a high-complexity observed clinical course.ResultsThe methodological quality of the PRISMA-tracked studies was predominantly Fair to Good; the illustrative institutional case was appraised separately as Fair. The reviewed literature suggested a hypothesis-generating, generally recurrent, phase-based clinical trajectory across subgroups. The institutional case was broadly consistent with the literature-derived pattern in seven of eight phases and differed mostly in the degree of multidrug-resistant microbiological complexity during Phase 7, an exploratory finding that may extend, rather than contradict, the proposed framework. Six recurrent adverse inflection points were identified, while evidence density varied across phases.ConclusionsDNM may be more usefully interpreted as a staged infectious syndrome rather than as an exclusively anatomical or surgical entity. The proposed phase-based framework, derived from structured synthesis of heterogeneous literature and contextualized by a prospectively documented severe case, should be interpreted as a conceptual, hypothesis-generating complement to existing anatomical staging systems rather than as a validated clinical model. Prospective multicenter registries, standardized reporting frameworks, and reproducibility testing should be regarded as the highest-priority research needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01397-2.

Introduction

Descending necrotizing mediastinitis (DNM) is a rare but life-threatening infection that most commonly originates from odontogenic, tonsillopharyngeal, or deep cervical foci, and propagates into the mediastinum through the cervical fascial planes. Since the landmark clinicopathological description by Estrera and colleagues in 1983, DNM has been recognized as a distinct entity in which a cervicofacial infectious source progresses to necrotizing mediastinal involvement with substantial morbidity and mortality [1].

The published literature consistently identifies several pillars of effective management: early contrast-enhanced cervicothoracic computed tomography (CT), rapid and adequate source control, repeated reassessment of disease extent, and broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy adapted to microbiological and clinical evolution [2–8]. However, DNM is not a uniform entity; it has been described across a wide spectrum of clinical substrates: previously healthy adults, immunocompromised patients, children, elderly patients, and, more rarely, pregnant women [8–16]. These differences in host substrate may influence the pace of disease progression, the severity of systemic involvement, the microbiological profile, and the intensity of intensive care support required.

Anatomical staging has historically dominated surgical decision-making in DNM. The Endo classification (1999) and the subsequent Guan classification (2021) link radiologic extent to operative strategy and prognosis [1, 13]. While these frameworks are important contributions, they do not capture the temporal dynamics of disease progression, the physiological burden of systemic involvement, or the need for iterative microbiological reassessment, dimensions that are increasingly recognized as determinants of outcome in contemporary series [4, 7, 8, 14].

Although several reviews and retrospective series on DNM have been published, the evidence remains fragmented across study designs, host substrates, and reporting frameworks. Prior reviews have mainly emphasized surgical strategy, mortality, or general treatment principles, whereas contemporary series increasingly highlight the importance of multidisciplinary management, reintervention burden, intensive care support, and dynamic microbiological evolution. In addition, broader infectious mediastinitis cohorts suggest that DNM should sometimes be interpreted within a wider mediastinal infection context when subgroup-specific DNM data can be clearly separated. However, to our knowledge, no previous synthesis has specifically organized DNM across predefined age and host-status groups while simultaneously integrating diagnosis, radiologic staging, source-control escalation, ICU burden, microbiological evolution, and outcome into a single clinically interpretable framework. The methodological characteristics of the available evidence have also rarely been explicitly appraised, and pregnancy-associated DNM remains markedly underrepresented in the published literature.

The aim of this study was therefore to: (1) map the DNM literature across five predefined subgroups; (2) synthesize differences in diagnosis, treatment phases, ICU course, microbiological adaptation, and outcomes; (3) appraise methodological characteristics and study quality; and (4) identify future research priorities. In addition, we sought to develop a conceptual, hypothesis-generating phase-based synthesis of DNM progression based on recurring patterns identified across the literature. A prospectively documented institutional case has been incorporated as an illustrative comparator only, to contextualize the pregnancy subgroup and to compare a contemporary, severe observed clinical course with the literature-derived framework.

The institutional case was included on explicitly limited terms. It was not treated as a primary evidentiary unit for deriving the framework, nor as confirmatory evidence of framework validity, but as a prespecified illustrative case used to contextualize an otherwise underrepresented host subgroup and to compare the proposed phase model with a high complexity clinical course. This approach was chosen to preserve the primacy of the literature synthesis while making transparent the additional interpretive value contributed by the institutional case.

Methods

Study Design

This study was designed as a scoping review with incorporation of a prespecified illustrative institutional case and was conducted in accordance with PRISMA-ScR [17]. A scoping methodology was selected because the DNM literature is heterogeneous and predominantly case-based. The objective was to map available evidence, characterize methodological features, identify recurring patterns and inflection points, and describe knowledge gaps rather than to generate pooled effect estimates. The illustrative case was included for two limited purposes: to provide contextual material for the underrepresented pregnancy subgroup and to compare the literature-derived phase-based framework with a prospectively documented severe clinical trajectory. Because scoping reviews are designed to map evidence and generate interpretive structure rather than establish causality or validate predictive models, the phase-based model proposed in this study was conceived from the outset as a conceptual and hypothesis-generating synthesis.

This literature synthesis did not require ethics approval. The illustrative institutional case was prospectively documented by the treating team and is presented as contextual material only. Written informed consent for publication of the illustrative institutional case and accompanying images was obtained from the patient.

Review Question

Primary question: How does DNM differ across age and host-status groups with respect to source infection, diagnostic pathway, mediastinal extent, treatment phases, ICU burden, microbiological evolution, and outcomes? Secondary questions addressed: the most common primary infectious sources per subgroup; the frequency and consequences of diagnostic delay; the relationship between radiologic extent and operative strategy; subgroup differences in antimicrobial adaptation and ICU support; the methodological quality of the evidence base; the areas of consistency or difference between the institutional illustrative case and the literature-derived trajectory; and the major research priorities.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible if they described patients with DNM arising from an odontogenic, pharyngeal, tonsillar, retropharyngeal, cervical, or other descending cervicofacial infectious source and provided sufficient extractable clinical information across relevant domains to permit meaningful inclusion in the structured synthesis. These domains included, where available, source infection, diagnostic evaluation, imaging extent, surgical or procedural management, antimicrobial therapy, intensive care support, complications, and outcome. Mixed infectious mediastinitis studies were considered eligible when the descending cervicofacial mediastinitis subgroup could be clearly distinguished and DNM-specific clinical data could be extracted. Studies were excluded if they focused primarily on post-sternotomy mediastinitis, esophageal perforation without descending cervical spread, traumatic mediastinitis unrelated to deep neck infection, or publications lacking sufficient clinical detail for structured data charting. No language or date restrictions were applied. The institutional illustrative case was prespecified in the design, assessed separately using the same appraisal framework for contextual transparency, and listed distinctly in the evidence tables with an institutional case (IC) designation.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

Structured searches were performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science. Core search terms included 'descending necrotizing mediastinitis', 'descending necrotising mediastinitis', 'deep neck infection', 'odontogenic infection', 'retropharyngeal abscess', 'cervical abscess', combined with subgroup terms ('healthy', 'immunocompromised', 'immunosuppressed', 'pediatric', 'elderly', 'pregnancy'). Reference lists of landmark papers were hand-searched, and forward citation tracking was performed for key studies [1, 4, 13].

The search was last updated on 15 January 2026. Database-specific search strategies were developed for each platform and are provided in Supplementary Appendix 1 to ensure transparency and reproducibility. No language or date restrictions were applied. In the revised review process, broader infectious mediastinitis studies were also screened when they contained clearly separable descending cervicofacial mediastinitis cases, allowing inclusion of contemporary mixed-population cohorts relevant to DNM interpretation. Although the review protocol was not registered in PROSPERO or OSF, the methodology was predefined and conducted in accordance with PRISMA-ScR recommendations, ensuring a systematic and structured approach to evidence identification and selection.

Study Selection Process

Study selection was performed in two sequential stages: title/abstract screening after duplicate removal, followed by full-text assessment against predefined eligibility criteria. Of 30 records identified, 5 duplicates were removed, 25 underwent title/abstract screening, 22 full texts were assessed, and 18 studies met inclusion criteria for the PRISMA-tracked synthesis. The institutional illustrative case was prespecified and did not enter the PRISMA screening process; it is listed separately in the evidence tables with an IC designation and is excluded from the PRISMA-tracked n = 18 total. Reviewer Process and Calibration

Two reviewers (I.B. and E.N.) independently screened titles and abstracts and subsequently evaluated full-text articles for eligibility. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, adjudication by a third reviewer.

Prior to formal screening, a calibration exercise was conducted on a random sample of ten records to ensure consistency in the application of eligibility criteria. Inter-rater agreement during this phase was high (Cohen’s κ = 0.82), indicating substantial agreement.

Following calibration, screening proceeded independently, with consensus reached for all studies included in the final literature synthesis.

Population Framework and Subgroup Assignment

Included cases were assigned to one of five predefined subgroups: immunocompetent adults (no major systemic predisposition); immunocompromised adults; pediatric patients (age < 18 years); elderly patients (age ≥ 65 years); and pregnant patients. Predefined prioritization rules prevented double-counting: pregnancy > pediatric > immunocompromise > elderly age. The institutional illustrative case was assigned to the pregnant subgroup.

Data Charting

A standardized data-charting form was developed a priori. Extracted domains included: bibliographic data; age, sex, subgroup; source of infection; symptom duration and diagnostic delay; imaging modality; mediastinal extent (Endo or Guan classification when reported); microbiology and antibiotic therapy; airway management; cervical and thoracic source-control procedures; reintervention; ICU admission, organ support type and duration; survival; hospital and ICU length of stay; and functional sequelae. Data were extracted by one reviewer and independently verified by a second. The institutional illustrative case was charted using the same form for contextual comparison.

Methodological Quality Assessment

Methodological quality was assessed using a structured appraisal framework adapted from the NIH Study Quality Assessment Tool for case series and observational studies. Because the included evidence base comprised case reports, case series, retrospective cohorts, a narrative review, and one classification study, this appraisal was used to provide interpretive context rather than strict cross-design comparability. Seven criteria were evaluated per study: (1) clear objective; (2) population defined; (3) consecutive or representative sample; (4) objective outcome measurement; (5) adequate follow-up; (6) data completeness; and (7) confounders addressed. Ratings were categorized as Good (≥ 5 criteria fully met), Fair (3–4 criteria met), or Poor (≤ 2 criteria met). The institutional illustrative case was appraised separately using the same framework for transparency, but was not included in the PRISMA-tracked totals.

Phase-Based Analytical Framework

Included studies were mapped onto an eight-phase framework developed as a conceptual analytical scaffold: (1) predisposition and source infection; (2) early cervical disease and recognition; (3) mediastinal spread and radiologic staging;(4) initial therapeutic control; (5) advanced surgical source control; (6) ICU phase and organ support; (7) microbiological evolution and antimicrobial adaptation; and (8) recovery, sequelae, and outcome.

The framework was derived through structured pattern recognition during data charting and synthesis. Specifically, during extraction, we identified recurring clinical transition points repeatedly described across reports and series, including initial source infection, failure of containment, radiologic progression, escalation of source control, systemic critical illness, microbiological change during prolonged treatment, and final outcome. These recurring transitions were iteratively organized into the eight proposed phases.

The phases should therefore be interpreted as a conceptual synthesis of recurrent clinical patterns, not as a formally validated analytic model, prognostic score, or prescriptive treatment algorithm. The institutional illustrative case was then compared with the literature-derived model at each phase as a contextual, hypothesis-generating exercise rather than as a validation procedure.

Provisional Operational Definitions

For the purposes of this review, a phase was defined as a recurring clinical segment of disease evolution characterized by a dominant pattern of pathophysiology, diagnostic activity, therapeutic intervention, or outcome. ICU burden was defined provisionally as the degree of intensive care dependency reflected by need for mechanical ventilation, vasopressor support, tracheostomy, prolonged ICU stay, or other major organ-support interventions. An adverse inflection point was defined as a recurring clinical transition associated in the literature with worsening disease control, increased treatment burden, or poorer outcome, such as diagnostic delay, underestimation of disease extent, incomplete source control, extensive mediastinal spread, sepsis-related organ dysfunction, or marked host vulnerability.

These definitions are intended as provisional interpretive tools to improve transparency and reproducibility of the present synthesis. They should not be interpreted as consensus definitions or formally validated clinical criteria. The provisional operational definitions of the eight proposed phases are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Synthesis

Data were synthesized descriptively. Findings are summarized in structured evidence tables and subgroup narratives, followed by a phase-based comparative synthesis. Because the included evidence base was heterogeneous in study design, patient population, disease extent classification, reporting depth, microbiological detail, and ICU-related metrics, the resulting synthesis emphasizes recurrent patterns rather than uniformity. Cross-study comparisons were interpreted more cautiously where reporting was sparse or inconsistent.

The institutional illustrative case is presented alongside—but analytically distinct from—the literature-derived synthesis. It contributes contextual material for the pregnancy subgroup and for comparison with the proposed framework, but is not used to derive the primary conclusions of the review and should not be interpreted as confirmatory evidence of model validity.

Literature-Derived Phase Model of DNM

Across the 18 included studies, DNM appeared to follow a broadly recurrent, hypothesis-generating phase-based trajectory beginning with a cervicofacial source infection, progressing through mediastinal extension, source-control escalation, intensive care support, microbiological evolution, and either recovery or death. Pooled mortality ranged from 11% [7] to 40% [3, 4] across series, with a pooled estimate of approximately 19.4% in the largest systematic review [8]. Contemporary additions to the corpus reinforced this interpretation. A recent 15-year DNM series emphasized complications, repeat operations, and prolonged critical care support in severe disease [18], while a broader infectious mediastinitis cohort with a separable DNM subgroup supported the relevance of considering DNM within a wider infectious mediastinitis framework when subgroup-specific data are extractable [19]. The same broad sequence was identifiable across study types and geographic settings, although the depth and precision of reporting varied considerably.

The eight-phase framework structured the synthesis as follows (see Fig. 2). Phase 1 (predisposition and source infection): the primary infectious source was odontogenic or pharyngeal in the large majority of cases across all subgroups; host vulnerability modified disease speed and severity but not the fundamental trajectory. Phase 2 (early cervical recognition): consistently identified as the most consequential point of diagnostic vulnerability, with deep cervical infections frequently presenting with apparently localized findings despite active fascial propagation [2, 8]. Phase 3 (mediastinal spread and CT staging): contrast-enhanced CT was central to both diagnosis and procedural planning; the Endo and Guan classifications linked radiologic extent to surgical access requirements and prognosis [1, 13]. Phase 4 (initial therapeutic control): broad-spectrum antibiotics and cervical drainage were universally required but frequently insufficient once mediastinal spread was established [2–4, 14]. Phase 5 (advanced surgical source control): thoracic access—preferentially via VATS—and reintervention were required in more than 50% of advanced cases [4, 8, 14]. Phase 6 (ICU phase and organ support): severe cases transitioned to systemic critical illness requiring sustained multidisciplinary management. Phase 7 (microbiological evolution): pathogen profiles shifted from community-acquired polymicrobial oral flora to nosocomial MDR organisms in patients with prolonged ICU exposure. Phase 8 (recovery): survival was achievable even in severe cases when serial CT-guided reassessment, escalated source control, and ICU-based organ support were maintained.Fig. 2Proposed eight-phase clinical model of descending necrotizing mediastinitis. The consistency of evidence across phases was not uniform. Support was strongest for the importance of cervicofacial source infection, CT-based recognition of mediastinal extension, need for source-control escalation, and the contribution of critical care support in severe disease. By contrast, evidence for later microbiological evolution, subgroup-specific ICU burden, and phase-level distinctions in rare host groups was more limited and more dependent on uneven reporting across studies. The framework should therefore be interpreted as a structured conceptual synthesis of unevenly distributed evidence rather than a uniformly supported progression model.

Illustrative Institutional Case in the Context of the Phase Model

Detailed phase-by-phase comparison is presented in Table 4. The case was broadly consistent with the literature-derived pattern in seven of eight phases. Convergent phases: Phase 1 confirmed that incomplete early source control (premature treatment discontinuation after initial drainage) constitutes the upstream inflection point enabling mediastinal extension, consistent with the diagnostic delay literature [2, 4, 8]. Phases 2 through 6 followed the literature-predicted trajectory, including the transition to Endo IIB disease, the insufficiency of initial cervical-only control, the requirement for bilateral VATS, and the ICU phase characterized by septic shock, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and tracheostomy.

Divergent phase: Phase 7 (microbiological evolution) differed from the typical literature-described pattern in the degree of complexity. The sequential isolation of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, carbapenemase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae, Klebsiella oxytoca, and Providencia stuartii across multiple anatomical compartments represents a more advanced MDR profile than reported in most published series, likely reflecting the combined effect of prolonged ICU exposure, multiple invasive procedures, and antimicrobial selection pressure. This divergence may extend rather than contradict the phase model, suggesting that Phase 7 complexity may scale with ICU duration and cumulative exposure.

Phase 8 (recovery): despite the accumulation of multiple adverse inflection points—Endo type IIB spread, septic shock, prolonged ventilatory dependence, complex MDR microbiological evolution, and pregnancy-associated physiological constraints—the patient achieved progressive clinical and radiological recovery. This outcome is consistent with the literature-described range of Phase 8 trajectories and illustrates that recovery remains possible even in a highly adverse host–substrate combination. Common Features and Key Inflection Points Across Host Groups

Across the 18 PRISMA-tracked studies and, separately, the institutional illustrative case, several features were consistently shared (Tables 5, 6, 7). The disease invariably originated in a cervicofacial focus and descended through fascial planes into the mediastinum. CT-based serial reassessment was critical for recognizing progression and guiding escalation. Successful treatment required a combination of systemic antibiotic therapy, repeated source control, and—in severe cases—ICU-based organ support. Six recurrent adverse inflection points were identified across the literature and were also observed in the institutional case (Table 8). A seventh potential inflection point—MDR microbiological complexity during prolonged ICU courses—was observed as an exploratory pattern and was documented in its most detailed form in the institutional case. Subgroup-Specific Observations

Host-status subgroups shared the same broad progression model but differed in vulnerability, microbiological context, and recovery reserve (Table 5). Immunocompetent adults and pediatric patients demonstrated greater physiological reserve, though pediatric cases remain too sparse for definitive conclusions [12, 16]. Elderly and immunocompromised patients appeared more susceptible to rapid progression and adverse outcomes [9–11]. Pregnant patients remain the most underrepresented subgroup in the published literature; the institutional illustrative case provides the only contemporaneous phase-level documentation for this group, and its contribution to the synthesis is explicitly restricted to subgroup contextualization and comparison with the proposed framework.

Discussion

The present study suggests that the clinical logic of DNM may be better captured by a phase-based infectious model than by a purely anatomical or surgical framework. However, this framework should be interpreted as a conceptual, hypothesis-generating synthesis derived from a heterogeneous scoping review rather than as a stable, validated, or generalizable clinical model. Across host-status groups, the literature suggested a broadly recurrent progression in which anatomy, microbiology, physiology, and timing interact continuously, but the evidentiary support for individual phases was uneven. The institutional illustrative case was broadly consistent with this pattern across seven of eight phases and therefore provides contextual support—but not formal validation—for the clinical interpretability of the proposed framework.

The inclusion of the institutional case as a prespecified illustrative comparator rather than as an informal anecdote was intended to make its role transparent and limited. Specifically, it was used to contextualize an otherwise undocumented pregnancy subgroup and to compare the proposed framework with a prospectively documented real-world trajectory. Where the case aligned with the literature, it increased the plausibility of the framework as a conceptual synthesis; where it differed, particularly in Phase 7 microbiological complexity, it suggested possible extension of the model rather than confirmation of it. The case should therefore be interpreted as illustrative and contextual, not confirmatory, and not as evidence of framework validity. The microbiological dimension of DNM requires dynamic, longitudinal interpretation. Some reports suggest that pathogen profiles may shift from community-acquired polymicrobial oral flora to nosocomial multidrug-resistant organisms during prolonged ICU courses, but microbiological reporting in the literature is inconsistent and likely affected by reporting bias, selective culturing, and differences in ICU exposure. The Phase 7 trajectory documented in the institutional case provides a particularly detailed example of late-stage microbiological complexity, but it should be interpreted as an exploratory illustration rather than as evidence of a generalized temporal rule. Accordingly, antimicrobial management in advanced DNM should be interpreted cautiously and in relation to culture data, clinical trajectory, and source-control status at each decision point.

The structured methodological appraisal adds context not previously emphasized in DNM reviews. Of 18 PRISMA-tracked studies, 8 were rated Good and 10 were rated Fair, confirming that the evidence base remains predominantly retrospective and case-based, with limited control of confounding and marked variability in reporting standards. This appraisal should not be read as a formal ranking across incompatible study designs, but rather as a transparent description of the evidentiary context within which the proposed framework should be interpreted. For that reason, the framework should be regarded as conceptually informative but not validated.

The ICU phase is an integral—not secondary—component of DNM pathophysiology in advanced disease. In severe cases, local mediastinal infection transitions to systemic inflammatory escalation, respiratory failure, septic shock, and MDR microbiological evolution under antimicrobial selection pressure. Surgical, infectious disease, and critical care decisions remain interdependent throughout the clinical course.

Limitations and Mitigation Strategies

First, the evidence base is dominated by case reports and small retrospective series (10 of 16 studies rated Fair), limiting the strength of causal inference. Mitigation: prospective multicenter registries represent the only pathway to higher-quality evidence (Table 3, priority 1).

Second, heterogeneity in disease definitions, extent classifications, and outcome definitions precluded formal cross-study comparison or meta-analysis. Mitigation: adoption of a standardized reporting framework—incorporating both Endo and Guan classification, phase-based documentation, and a minimum outcome dataset—should be a consensus priority for the field. In addition, heterogeneity in phase-relevant reporting means that some components of the proposed framework are supported by denser and more consistent evidence than others.

Third, the pregnancy subgroup is represented only by the institutional illustrative case, which limits generalizability for this host category. Mitigation: an international registry dedicated to pregnancy-associated DNM is the highest-priority research need for this subgroup (Table 3, priority 2).

Fourth, the institutional illustrative case was prospectively documented by the treating team; its inclusion as contextual material, while explicitly bounded methodologically, does not eliminate the risk of reporting bias inherent to non-blinded case documentation. Mitigation: future studies should use standardized, prespecified phase-assignment forms completed by reviewers independent of the treating team.

Fifth, search dates were not prospectively registered and no PROSPERO or OSF registration was performed. Mitigation: future updates of this review should include prospective registration and formal search documentation as supplementary material.

Sixth, the proposed phase-based framework has not been prospectively validated as a prognostic scoring instrument. Mitigation: a prospective cohort study with blinded phase assignment at clinical decision points, correlated with mortality and ICU outcomes, would constitute a formal validation (Table 3, priority 3).

Despite this limitation, the study provides a structured appraisal of the DNM evidence base, a prospectively documented phase-level dataset for pregnancy-associated DNM, and a research agenda directed at the most important methodological gaps. These contributions should be interpreted as framework-building and hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive validation.

Future Research Directions

The methodological quality appraisal and evidence synthesis reveal eight research priorities summarized in Table 3. The three highest-priority needs are: (1) a prospective multicenter registry with standardized phase-based and Endo/Guan documentation, which would transform the current case-based evidence into a dataset capable of supporting subgroup analysis and prognostic factor studies; (2) a dedicated international registry for pregnancy-associated DNM, for which only isolated case reports and the present institutional case exist; and (3) prospective validation of the phase-based framework as a scoring instrument, correlating phase assignments with mortality, reintervention rate, and ICU length of stay.

At moderate priority, a propensity-matched or prospective comparative study of VATS versus open thoracotomy in Endo type IIB/Guan type III disease would address a critical gap in surgical effectiveness data. Evidence-based guidelines for antimicrobial duration and de-escalation thresholds in MDR-complicated DNM—directly motivated by the Phase 7 observations in the present synthesis—represent a second moderate-priority need. A multicenter pediatric registry would address the sparsity of subgroup-specific data for children.

The proposed phase-based framework could serve as an organizing scaffold for both registry design and future prospective studies, facilitating standardized data collection and enabling comparative synthesis across institutions and geographic settings. The Phase 7 divergence observed in the institutional illustrative case—greater MDR complexity than described in most published series—generates a specific, testable hypothesis: that later microbiological complexity may be a function of ICU duration and cumulative antimicrobial exposure, independent of host substrate. This hypothesis can be formally tested in a registry that systematically documents both antibiotic exposure data and serial microbiological results.

Conclusions

Descending necrotizing mediastinitis may be more usefully interpreted as a staged, dynamic infectious syndrome rather than as a discrete surgical entity. Across age and host-status groups, the disease appears to follow a broadly recurrent phase-based progression from cervicofacial source infection to mediastinal extension, source-control escalation, ICU-dependent systemic support, and either recovery or death. Six recurrent adverse inflection points were consistently identified in the literature: diagnostic delay, underestimation of mediastinal extent, incomplete source control, extensive mediastinal spread, sepsis-related organ dysfunction, and host vulnerability. A seventh potential inflection point—multidrug-resistant microbiological complexity during prolonged ICU exposure—was observed as an exploratory signal and should be interpreted cautiously.

The proposed phase-based framework should be regarded as a conceptual, hypothesis-generating synthesis, not as a validated clinical model. The institutional illustrative case was broadly consistent with the literature-derived trajectory in seven of eight phases and provided contextual support for the framework’s plausibility while also suggesting that later microbiological complexity may be more variable than the current literature captures.

The highest-priority research needs remain a prospective multicenter registry with standardized phase-based documentation, a dedicated international registry for pregnancy-associated DNM, and prospective evaluation of the proposed framework for reproducibility and clinical usefulness. The present synthesis and illustrative case together provide a conceptual foundation for these next steps.
