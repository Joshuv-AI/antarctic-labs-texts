Temporal diversity of phytochemicals in Tinospora cordifolia (Giloy), in accordance with seasonal variations: comparative analysis using UHPLC-PDA and HPTLC
Authors: Acharya Balkrishna, Monali Joshi, Meenu Tomer, Pardeep Nain, Anurag Varshney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeasonal variation influences the biosynthesis and accumulation of phytochemical compounds in plants, thereby affecting the qualitative and quantitative composition of phytochemicals. Investigation of these trends is crucial for ensuring the optimal collection time and consistency of metabolites. Although time specific collections of plant materials have been well-defined in classical Indian ancient literatures, however robust scientific validation has been rather limited.Methods Tinospora cordifolia stems were collected over a period of 24 months (Year 2022 to 2024) for phytochemical investigation. Bioactive constituents were extracted using hydro-methanol as the solvent and characterized by UHPLC-PDA analysis. Quantitative analytical method was validated in accordance with ICH-Q2 (R1) guidelines. Chemical fingerprinting was performed using HPTLC.ResultsUHPLC-PDA analysis of Tinospora cordifolia stems facilitated the identification and quantification of three key bioactive compounds including cordifolioside A, magnoflorine and β-ecdysone, whereas HPTLC generated a distinct chemical fingerprint for the studied samples. The chromatographic method was found to be suitable, specific, linear, accurate, and robust for quantitative analysis. The concentration of magnoflorine, β-ecdysone and cordifolioside A were found to be range between 5.0 and 54.5 ng/mg, 5.5 to 28.0 ng/mg and 154.0 to 289.0 ng/mg, respectively. Seasonal variation study revealed that the concentration of these three phytochemical compounds was highest during monsoon (August), lowest in winters (December to February), and moderate in spring and summer (rest of the year). Therefore, monsoon season seems like an appropriate time for the collection of Tinospora cordifolia stems, as advised in classical Indian medicinal text.ConclusionThis study underscores the significance of temporal diversity of phytochemical profiles with response to seasonal variation in herbs. This study also provides a systematic harvesting framework for determining optimal collection period of Tinospora cordifolia in order to maximize the consistency of pharmacological active phytochemical compounds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07532-4.

Background

Ayurveda is a traditional system of medicine that offers a holistic approach to health and wellness. In recent years, traditional medicine systems have gained significant growth and renewed interest, reflecting a global shift toward overall wellbeing [1]. Ayurveda utilizes herbal plants for their medicinal properties. Herbal plants contain phytochemical compounds which are responsible for their therapeutic properties and also serve as the key indicators for evaluating quality of herbs [2]. The quality and quantity of these natural metabolites is largely affected by biotic and abiotic factors, therefore continuous observation of these pharmacologically active compounds in response to the environmental factors become rather crucial [3]. These natural variations tend to be influenced by the collection time, therefore optimization of the collection time for specific plant material is central to ensure maximal harvesting efficiencies [4]. In ancient Indian classical texts, the method for plant collection, form of collection, specific time of harvesting and collection of plant according to their potency has been well documented [5].

Charaka Samhita and Raja Nighantu, the classical Indian texts on herbal medicine reads, “Teṣāṃ śākhāpalāśamacirapraruḍhaṃ varṣāvasaṃtayogrāhyāṃ, grīṣme mūlāni, śiśire vā śīrṇapraruḍhaparṇānāṃ, śaradi tvakkaṃdakṣīraṇi, hemaṃte sārāṇi, yathartupuṣpaphalamiti” [5], “Kandaṃ himartau śiśire ca. mūlaṃ puṣpaṃ vasaṃte phaladaṃ vadanti. Pravālapatrāṇi nidāghakāle syuḥ paṃcajātāni śaratprayoge” [6], transliterated as: “the quality of plant material is largely affected by the season of collection; therefore plant materials should be collected as per the specific season. Accordingly, roots should be collected in summer and winter; tuber, bark and milk in winter; hardwood in autumn; leaves in summer; stems during rainy and spring seasons and fruits and flowers in their respective growing season. For the plants that grow in the mud, best time of the collection is winters.” Although Ayurvedic texts advocate the season based collection of plant material for medicinal use, however comprehensive scientific validation with modern analytical core has been rather limited. One of the interesting instances are the reports on Terminalia ivorenisis, that indicate that cinnamic acid concentration was highest during rainy season [4], whereas in Artemisia afra the phytochemical concentrations was lowest during rainy season [7].

In the present study, we scientifically validated the seasonal variations in phytochemical composition of Tinospora cordifolia, with the primary objective of determining the optimal collection time in order to maximize the harvesting efficiency of phytochemicals. By investigating the temporal diversity of phytochemical with response to seasonal changes, we established a scientific basis of collection of Tinospora cordifolia at the most optimal season, ensuring the maximum concentration of desired phytochemicals, and rationalizing sustainable harvesting practices.

Materials and methods

Collection of plant material and Preparation of hydro-methanolic extract

The stems of Tinospora cordifolia (Wild.) Hook.f. & Thomson (Family: Menispermaceae) were collected from the herbal garden of Patanjali Research Foundation, Haridwar, India. The plant material was then authenticated by a registered taxonomist at Patanjali Research Foundation Herbarium (Collection no.: 9869), located (WX3X + G26) at Haridwar, India. To analyse the seasonal variation of phytochemicals, across four different seasons of Indian subcontinent, the samples were collected in every alternate month for a period of 24 months (Year 2022 to 2024). For hydro-methanol (30 W: 70 M v/v) extraction of Tinospora cordifolia, 100 g of fresh plant material was ground and extracted at 65 0C for 3 h. The extracted material was filtered through 10 μm filter cloth, the remaining material was then re-extracted twice under the same extraction conditions. Filtrates from all the three extractions were pooled and concentrated using Buchi R-300, rotary-evaporator (Switzerland), under reduced pressure.

Ultra-high-performance liquid (UHPLC-PDA) analysis

Preparation of reference standard solution

Reference standard solutions of magnoflorine (Chem faces, China, potency: 98.00%, cat # CFN98071), cordifolioside A (Chem faces, China, potency: 98.00%, cat # CFN95040) and β-ecdysone (PHY-proof, Germany, potency: 99.90%, cat # 89651) were prepared by separately dissolving accurately weighed standards in methanol to obtain stock solutions at a concentration of 1000 µg/mL. From the stock solutions, a mixed reference standard working solution of 50 µg/mL was prepared for analysis.

Preparation of T. cordifolia sample solution

500 mg of hydro-methanolic extract of T. cordifolia stems was dissolved in 10 mL of water: methanol (30: 70 v/v), sonicated for 30 min, centrifuged at 9000 rpm and then filter through 0.45 μm nylon filter to obtain a clear solution for analysis.

Instrumentation and chromatographic conditions

Quantitative analysis of phytochemicals was performed in Prominence-XR UHPLC system (Shimadzu, Japan). The system was equipped with a quaternary pump (Nexera XR LC-20AD XR) along with a degasser unit (DGU-20 A 5R), an auto sampler (Nexera XR SIL-20 AC XR) and diode array detector (PDA) (SPD-M20 A). The chromatographic separation of phytometabolites were performed using VDSPher PUR 120 C18-U (5 μm, 4.6*250 mm) column (Germany) along with a binary mobile phase. The mobile phase consists of 0.10% v/v of orthophosphoric acid in water (pH: 2.50 adjusted by diethylamine) (Solvent A) and acetonitrile (Solvent B). The solvent gradient system was as follows: 5% solvent B from 0 to 5 min, 10% solvent B from 5 to 10 min, 12% solvent B from 10 to 20 min, 20% solvent B from 20 to 35 min, 20% solvent B from 35 to 40 min, 40% solvent B from 45 to 45 min, 55% solvent B from 45 to 46 min, 5% solvent B from 46 to 50 min. The flow rate was maintained at 1.00 mL/min throughout the analysis with the column temperature of 30 0C. The injection volume was 10 µL for both the reference standard (50 µg/mL) and sample solution. The compounds were detected at 247 nm (β-ecdysone) and 265 nm (magnoflorine and cordifolioside A) by PDA detector as per the wavelength of maximum absorbance.

Ultra high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) method validation

The method for quantification of phytometabolites namely, magnoflorine, cordifolioside A, and β-ecdysone was validated as per International Council on Harmonisation [ICH-Q2 (R1)] guidelines [8]. The method validation was performed in terms of system suitability, linearity, precision, recovery, intermediate precision, robustness, limit of detection, and limit of quantitation.

System suitability

System suitability was determined by calculating the percentage relative standard deviation (% RSD) of observed area, retention time, tailing factor and theoretical plate count from replicate injection (n = 6) of working standard solution.

Specificity

Specificity was defined on the basis of selectivity and was evaluated by injecting solvent blank along with the working standard mix solution.

Linearity

Linearity was plotted by injecting different concentrations of standard solution. These concentrations were prepared separately by diluting the standard stock solution. The correlation coefficient (r2) was then calculated by plotting the calibration curve area (x – axis) verses concentration (y – axis).

Range

The range of an analyte is calculated by normalizing the lowest and highest concentration within the linearity interval based on sample dilution. The range is the interval between the lowest and the highest concentration of an analyte where the method reproduces reliable and consistent results.

Precision

The repeatability was determined in terms of system precision and method precision (intra-day and inter-day precision). System precision was determined by calculating the % RSD of six replicate injections of working standard solution (50 µg/mL). For intra-day precision six individual sample solutions were prepared from the same sample batch. Same concentration of six sample solutions was applied and the % RSD for each marker compound was calculated. Similarly, the inter-day precision was performed on the next day and % RSD was calculated.

Limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantitation (LOQ)

The LOD and LOQ was determined by signal-to-noise ratio. This was evaluated by comparing the measured signals of a low concentration of analyte to those of a blank sample in order to establish the minimum concentration at which the analyte can be reliably detected and quantified. LOD and LOQ was confirmed by six replicate injections of the lowest concentration followed by calculating % RSD of the peak area of individual markers.

Accuracy

The accuracy of method was determined by percentage recovery, by spiking the known concentration of working standard in sample at three different percentage levels (80%, 100% and 120%). The recovery was calculated by comparing the observed value with the theoretical value, and % RSD of the % recovery was subsequently determined.

Robustness

The robustness of method was determined by embodying some deliberate variations in method parameters like change in flow rate of mobile phase and column temperature. The flow rate of the mobile phase was changed from 1.0 mL/min to 0.95 mL/min and 1.05 mL/min and the column temperature was modified from 30 0C to 33 0C and 37 0C.

High performance thin layer chromatography (HPTLC) analysis

Preparation of T. cordifolia sample solution

500 mg of hydro-methanolic extract of T. cordifolia stems was dissolved in 10 mL of water: methanol (30: 70 v/v), sonicated for 30 min, centrifuged at 9000 rpm and then filter through 0.45 μm nylon filter to obtain the clear solution for analysis. The clear sample was then used for HPTLC analysis.

Instrumentation and chromatographic conditions

The CAMAG HPTLC system (Muttenz, Switzerland) equipped with an automatic TLC sampler (ATS4), TLC scanner 4, TLC Visualizer and integrated software winCATS (Version 1.4.10) was used for the analysis. HPTLC was performed on a pre-coated silica gel 60 F254 (Cat. No.: 1.05554.0007) aluminium backed TLC plate. For HPTLC fingerprinting analysis, 10 µL of sample solution was applied as 8 mm band using spray-on technique. The TLC plate was then developed up to 70 mm, using CAMAG twin trough chamber pre-saturated (15 min) with mobile phase (chloroform: methanol: water: formic acid (8: 2: 0.2: 0.2). The developed TLC plate was dried using warm air and visualized under UV 254 nm and 366 nm. TLC images was documented and the same TLC plate was then scanned at 254 nm and 366 nm, respectively.

Results

Variation of extractive yield of T. cordifolia stems

To assess the impact of seasonal variation of extractive yield, the hydro-methanolic extract of T. cordifolia stems was evaluated for 24 months, from August 2022 to August 2024. In the present study, the mean extractive yield (%) was calculated for same month across two consecutive years (Year 2022–2023 and year 2023–2024). The extractive yield ranged from 3.79% w/w to 5.10% w/w (Fig. 1). Notably, the mean extractive yield % values aligned well with the overall extractive yield % across studied period. Statistical analysis (ANOVA) revealed non-significant variations in extractive yield % across the studied months, in this study.

UHPLC-PDA method validation

UHPLC-PDA was used to determine the phytochemicals present in the hydro-methanolic extract of T. cordifolia stems. Cordifolioside A, magnoflorine and β-ecdysone were identified and further quantified against reference standards by using a validated method as per ICHQ2(R1) guidelines. The optimal chromatographic separation was achieved by using the gradient elution of 0.10% orthophosphoric acid in water (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile (mobile phase B). System suitability was evaluated to ensure the proper functioning of the system. The observed % RSD for area percentage and retention time were within the acceptance criteria of not more than 2% (NMT 2%) and the tailing factor was also found within the limit (< 2). Sharp and well resolved peaks (> 2000) indicated the presence of high theoretical plate, as observed for magnoflorine (71407), β –ecdysone (138993) and cordifolioside A (51135). Furthermore, no peak was observed in the solvent blank corresponding to the reference standard, confirming the absence of interference at the same RT of reference standard, which indicates the specificity of the method. A calibration curve with a five-point linearity range (1, 5, 10, 50 and 100 µg/mL: for magnoflorine and β-ecdysone, 1, 10, 50, 100 and 500 µg/mL: for cordifolioside A) was plotted. The result indicated a correlation coefficient NLT 0.99 for all three phyto-compounds under investigation. Furthermore, the working range for this method was determined from the linearity and found to be 20–2,000 µg/g for magnoflorine and β -ecdysone and 20- 10,000 µg/g for cordifolioside A.

The precision of developed method was confirmed by system precision and method precision (intra-day and inter-day precision) analyses. These results indicated that the % RSD for system precision (n = 6) ranged from 0.29 to 0.42%, which is within the acceptable limit (% RSD: NMT 2.00). Similarly, the % RSD was in the range of 0.47 to 1.57% and 0.55 to 3.96%, respectively, for intra-day and inter-day precisions. All the observed values were found within the permissible range, confirming the precision of the developed method (Table 1).

UHPLC-PDA method for quantitative analysis of phytochemicals in hydro-methanolic extract of T. cordifolia

The validated analytical method was used for the quantitative analysis of magnoflorine, β-ecdysone and cordifolioside A in T. cordifolia stems hydro-methanolic extract. In UHPLC analysis, cordifolioside A, magnoflorine, and β-ecdysone were eluted at retention time of 14.98 min, 17.54 min and 24.89 min (Fig. 2), respectively, under optimized conditions.

The concentration of the analytes varied depending on the season/month of collection. It was observed that the concentration ranged from 5.0 to 54.5 ng/mg for magnoflorine, 5.5 to 28.0 ng/mg for β-ecdysone and from 154.0 to 289.0 ng/mg for cordifolioside A. Notably, the concentration of all three compounds were found significantly highest during August month of collection (Monsoon season, in India), specifically 54.5 ± 1.5 ng/mg for magnoflorine, 28.0 ± 4.0 ng/mg for β-ecdysone and 284.0 ± 94.0 ng/mg for cordifolioside A (One-way ANOVA, p < 0.05). In autumn season, the concentration started decreasing gradually and reached the lowest during winters (December to February): magnoflorine at 5.0 ± 4.0 ng/mg, β-ecdysone at 5.5 ± 1.5 ng/mg and cordifolioside A at 154.0 ± 29.0 ng/mg (Fig. 3). Subsequent analysis indicated a progressive increase in the concentration of all three compounds from winter to spring (April). A similar pattern continued during the summer season as well. During the next monsoon season, the concentrations of all three compounds were elevated compared to other seasons (magnoflorine: 289.0 ± 89.0 ng/mg, β-ecdysone: 18.0 ± 14.0 ng/mg, cordifolioside A: 46.0 ± 10.0 ng/mg) (Fig. 3).

Chemical fingerprinting of hydro-methanolic extract of T. cordifolia stems using HPTLC

HPTLC method was used to develop the chemical fingerprints of phytochemicals present in T. cordifolia stems. HPTLC analysis was performed for the samples collected during August and December, as these months showed distinct phytochemical variations. However, UHPLC-PDA analysis was performed for all the collected samples.

HPTLC fingerprinting was used to visualize the bands of phytochemical compounds at 254 nm and 366 nm. A visual inspection of TLC plate revealed prominent bands in the month of August compared to December (Fig. 5a, c). Further densitometric scanning of the respective TLC plates at 254 nm and 366 nm, confirmed the presence of distinct peaks. As indicated in figure (5b and 5 d) the compounds corresponding to peak numbers 2, 6, 8, and 9 were detected only in T. cordifolia stems extract from August and were not detectable in December months (winters). Similarly, at 366 nm the compounds corresponding to peak number 2, 3, 4, and 5 were not detected in December months (winters). These HPTLC results were consistent with UHPLC-PDA findings and demonstrated that HPTLC is a quick and efficient method for the phytochemical screening of T. cordifolia.

Discussion

Tinospora cordifolia commonly known as Giloy, is a well-known traditional medicinal plant. In Ayurveda, it is described as the Rasayana herb, which is believed to promote overall health, longevity and vitality [9, 10].

Present study demonstrated the seasonal variations in phytochemical constituents of T. cordifolia, by utilising UHPLC-PDA and HPTLC analytical techniques. The results revealed that the concentration of magnoflorine, β-ecdysone and cordifolioside A, was significantly elevated during monsoon season (August) (p < 0.05). However, lowest concentration was found during winters (December and February) (p < 0.05), and moderate levels were observed during spring (April) and summer (June). The lower concentration of phytochemical compounds might be attributed to plant growth under lower temperatures, which trigger significant adjustments in various physiological and biochemical processes, leading to alterations in the synthesis and accumulation of phytochemical compounds [11]. The highest concentration of phytochemicals in plants during the monsoon season is likely the result of a combination of factors, including reduced biomass production, temperature stress and activation of plant defence mechanism, as these factors can contribute to the biosynthesis of phytochemical compounds [12]. Thus the combined use of UHPLC-PDA and HPTLC enabled the real time analysis of phytochemical compounds across different seasons. Incidentally, in India the monsoon season (July to August) has been marked for plants driven celebrations, for example Harela in northern India, and Jadi Booti Divas (August 4th) all over the country.

In line with the present study, a previous report indicated that a significantly higher amount of cinnamic acid was found in Terminalia ivorensis samples collected from rainy season compared to other seasons [4]. A similar pattern in phytochemicals has also been reported in Artemisia afra jacq. For instance, it was observed that scopolin, scopoletin and quercetin gradually decreased in samples collected during winter and increased again in those collected during rainy season [7]. Similarly, study on Breonadia salicina showed significant seasonal variation in phytochemicals, with higher concentrations observed in samples collected during autumn and winter seasons, while the lowest levels during spring and summer [13]. In Diosoyros rekoi as well, the seasonal variation significantly affects the phytochemical content. The results indicated highest antioxidant activity in samples collected during spring season, which might be attributed to elevated levels of phenolic and flavonoid compounds [14]. In another study on Valeriana jatamansi the amount of valepotriates was remarkably higher in samples from January month compared to the samples from February to June. This was further increased in July and remained significantly higher during October and November [15]. Another study revealed that the bark of Lannea discolor showed higher concentrations of total phenolic content, total flavonoid content and total antioxidant capacity in samples collected during summer season. In contrast, the leaves of Senna singueana, Melia azedarach and Moringa oleifera exhibited higher level of these phytochemicals during rainy season [2], in line with the observations of present study.

The observed variations in phytochemical content may have implications for the therapeutic efficacy of T. cordifolia, as the analysed compounds have been reported for potent biological activities. Magnoflorine has been reported to possess antioxidant [16], anti-inflammatory [17], immunomodulatory [18], and anti-fungal activities [19], β-ecdysone reported to exhibit anti-inflammatory, anti-microbial, anti-oxidant, and immunomodulatory effects [20]. Similarly, cordifolioside A has demonstrated immunomodulatory [18], radioprotective and cytoprotective [21] profiles. Overall the findings of the present study emphasize the importance of season based collection of medicinal plants, to ensure the maximum concentration of phytochemicals. The clear pattern of seasonal variations served as a guideline for the collection of T. cordifolia in both wild and cultivated conditions.

While this study describes the temporal diversity of phytochemical compounds in across seasons, it has some limitations such as the sample collection and analysis being conducted over a relatively short period of two years. A long-term study would help draw more robust conclusions and provide a better understanding of the impact of seasonal changes on phytochemical profiles. Additionally, the study focused on a single species of Tinospora specific to one geographical location in India. Further studies could expand by including multiple species of Tinospora, from diverse geographical regions of India, and extending the duration of observation to capture long-term variations, in general.

Conclusion

Collection of T. cordifolia stems during different seasons, significantly influences the phytochemical profiling. In the present study, three key compounds were selected and analysed by UHPLC-PDA; and the HPTLC fingerprints were also developed for T. cordifolia stems for accounting the variations. Both the analytical results revealed a clear seasonal trend of variation in phytochemical levels of T. cordifolia stems. These findings demonstrated that the stems collected during monsoon season have higher concentration of phytochemicals in comparison to those collected during other seasons. This might be attributed, due to biotic and abiotic factors, which may induce synthesis and storage of phytochemical compounds. By examining the temporal diversity of phytochemical in T. cordifolia, the present study identifies the optimal harvesting time to maximise the yield, thereby enhancing the therapeutic efficacy and standardization of herbal formulations. Moreover, the present study supports evidence based use of traditional medicinal plants, ensuring consistency and sustainable utilization. Conclusively, the findings highlight the enormous capability of nature to pack phytochemical in herbal plants in the most optimal manner.
