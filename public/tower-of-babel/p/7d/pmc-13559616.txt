A model of cellular proliferation and mitochondrial biogenesis predicts prognosis and immunotherapy response in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Mi Zou, Guangda Zheng, Yanju Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), which is the leading subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), poses considerable difficulties in accurate prognostic assessment and targeted therapeutic options. Cell proliferation-related genes (CPGs) and mitochondrial biogenesis-related genes (MBGs) play critical roles in tumor metabolic reprogramming; however, their prognostic value and molecular mechanisms in LUAD are poorly understood. This study aims to construct a CPG/MBG-based prognostic risk model for LUAD, evaluate its clinical utility in predicting prognosis and immunotherapy response, and experimentally validate the functional role of key model genes in LUAD progression.MethodsBy utilizing The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-LUAD and GSE72094 datasets, this investigation formulated a risk scoring model through differential expression screening combined with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-Cox regression analysis. The molecular characteristics and clinical implications of the risk model were investigated via immune microenvironment evaluation, genomic alteration analysis, and drug sensitivity prediction. The functional contributions of key genes were further substantiated using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), commercial assay kits, the JC-1 fluorescent probe, the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), Transwell invasion assays, and wound healing assays.ResultsA risk model based on seven CPGs and MBGs (PLK1, HMMR, CYP27A1, LDHA, NPAS2, KRT17, CIDEC) showed reliable predictive performance in both GSE72094 and the TCGA-LUAD cohorts. Enhanced tumor heterogeneity and an immunosuppressive microenvironment were observed in the high-risk group. Drug sensitivity analysis indicated that the risk model could guide personalized treatment strategies; for instance, high-risk patients showed increased susceptibility to agents such as docetaxel and 5-fluorouracil. In vitro experiments demonstrated that the key gene CIDEC exhibited upregulated expression in LUAD tissues and cells. Knockdown of CIDEC led to enhanced cellular energy metabolism and increased mitochondrial membrane potential, while also effectively suppressing cell invasion, proliferation, and migration.ConclusionsThe established MBGs/CPGs prognostic model provides a novel tool for stratified treatment planning in LUAD, underscoring the crucial roles of cellular proliferation and mitochondrial biogenesis in tumor progression. Functional validation of CIDEC offers experimental support for the development of potential therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), cellular proliferation and mitochondrial biogenesis, prognostic model, immune signature, drug sensitivity

Highlight box

Key findings

• A seven-gene prognostic model (PLK1, HMMR, CYP27A1, LDHA, NPAS2, KRT17, CIDEC) based on cell proliferation-related genes (CPGs) and mitochondrial biogenesis-related genes (MBGs) was constructed and validated in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD).

• High-risk patients exhibited an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, elevated tumor mutation burden, and distinct drug sensitivity profiles.

• CIDEC knockdown suppressed LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while enhancing mitochondrial oxidative metabolism.

What is known and what is new?

• CPGs and MBGs play critical roles in tumor metabolic reprogramming, yet their integrated prognostic value in LUAD remains poorly characterized.

• This study establishes a novel CPG/MBG-based risk model for LUAD prognostic stratification and reveals that CIDEC regulates LUAD malignant phenotypes through lipid-metabolic reprogramming and mitochondrial modulation.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The risk model provides a practical tool for patient stratification and personalized treatment selection in LUAD.

• CIDEC represents a potential metabolic therapeutic target, warranting further preclinical and clinical investigation.

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC), one of the globally deadliest forms of cancer, has an overall 5-year survival rate of less than 30% (1). Based on histopathological characteristics, LC is classified into two major types: small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and the more common non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). NSCLC comprises three major subtypes according to differentiation features: lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), large cell lung carcinoma, and lung squamous cell carcinoma (2). Notably, LUAD represents the predominant subtype among NSCLC cases, with a steadily rise in incidence (3,4). Advances in molecular marker research have significantly altered the treatment landscape of LUAD through the identification of tumor subgroups with distinct genetic profiles. Although the overall prognosis remains unsatisfactory for most patients, studies have successfully uncovered key molecular mechanisms driving tumorigenesis (5,6). Abnormalities in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene are relatively common in LUAD patients. Research demonstrated that these patients exhibit significant therapeutic responses to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), establishing EGFR as a critical clinical target (7,8). Therefore, elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms of LUAD and identifying specific molecular markers have pivotal implications for achieving personalized diagnosis and patient care.

A hallmark of cancer cells is their uncontrolled proliferation. To sustain rapid growth, tumor cells undergo metabolic reprogramming to fulfill elevated energy demands (9). Mitochondria, serving as essential bioenergetic and biosynthetic hubs, are crucial for normal cellular function and human health. Mitochondria constitute a highly dynamic intracellular network and perform “quality control” functions through processes such as fission, biogenesis, fusion, and autophagic degradation (mitophagy) (10,11). Furthermore, mitochondria are involved in various biological processes (BPs), including reactive oxygen species generation, redox metabolism, cell signaling, regulation of cell death, and biosynthetic metabolism (12,13). As central organelles in cellular stress response, mitochondria render cells vulnerable to oncogenic transformation when they fail to adapt to bioenergetic changes induced by environmental cues. This dysfunction leads to altered cellular metabolism, ultimately accelerating tumor initiation and progression (14,15). Thus, mitochondrial biogenesis is closely involved in LUAD development by accelerating tumor cell proliferation. Cell proliferation-related genes (CPGs) and mitochondrial biogenesis-related genes (MBGs) may therefore offer a promising strategy for cancer intervention.

Integrating high-throughput sequencing with molecular experimental validation, this investigation systematically elucidates the molecular mechanisms by which CPGs and MBGs drive malignant progression in LUAD, providing a theoretical foundation for developing metabolism-targeted therapies and refining prognostic stratification. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and TRIPOD reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1-2721/rc).

Methods

Data collection

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) provided mRNA expression data, mutation profiles, and clinical information from 541 LUAD samples and 59 normal samples. A total of 505 samples possessing complete survival records and overall survival time >30 days were enrolled as the training set for prognostic model construction. Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) provided the GSE72094 dataset, and 385 LUAD samples with survival time exceeding 30 days were included as the validation set.

A total of 690 MBGs were sourced from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), with the search keyword “Mitochondrial Biogenesis” used. Two functional gene sets were derived from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp): (I) 96 MBGs (search term: REACTOME_MITOCHONDRIAL_BIOGENESIS), and (II) 706 CPGs (search terms: HALLMARK_G2M_CHECKPOINT, KEGG_CELL_CYCLE). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Identification of CPGs and MBGs in LUAD

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in TCGA-LUAD were screened through the “edgeR” package (16) with thresholds of |logFC| >1 and false discovery rate (FDR) ≤0.05. Through intersection analysis with MBGs and CPGs, a total of 384 differentially expressed MBGs and CPGs were determined in LUAD.

Construction and validation of the prognostic model

The TCGA-LUAD dataset served as the training cohort to establish the prognostic model. Patients with an overall survival time of >30 days were included to ensure clinical relevance. First, we implemented univariate Cox regression analysis to determine 159 survival-associated genes. We introduced the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) method to refine the set of survival-related genes. The optimal tuning parameter λ (λ=0.05) was determined via 10-fold cross-validation based on minimum mean error, resulting in the selection of 7 survival-related genes. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was subsequently conducted on these genes, leading to a final set of 7 feature genes enrolled to build the prognostic model. We stratified patients into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median risk score. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves were generated using the “survminer” package (https://github.com/kassambara/survminer), and differences in survival across the groups were assessed by the log-rank test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival were plotted with the “timeROC” package (17), and the area under the curve (AUC) values were computed. Finally, expression profiles of the feature genes, along with patient survival status and risk score distributions, were visualized for both the TCGA-LUAD and GSE72094 datasets.

Development and validation of the prognostic nomogram

We evaluated the independent prognostic efficiency of the risk score through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. A prognostic nomogram was generated by integrating clinicopathological parameters [age, tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage, tumor stage, and sex] with the risk score from the prognostic model. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) curves were plotted to examine the predictive performance of the model. Additionally, ROC curves were generated for the clinicopathological parameters and the prognostic model, and AUC values were calculated to compare their predictive accuracy.

Immune analysis

The tumor microenvironment (TME) of each sample in the high- and low-risk groups was evaluated using the “estimate” package (18). Immune scores, stromal scores, and ESTIMATE scores were calculated for each sample, and violin plots were generated to visualize the results. We introduced single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) to quantify the prevalence levels of immune cell subsets across risk groups. To assess the potential response to immunotherapy, tumor gene expression data were submitted to the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) website (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/), and TIDE scores were obtained for each sample. Immunophenoscore (IPS) for LUAD was downloaded from The Cancer Immunome Archive (TCIA) database (https://tcia.at). Violin plots were applied to compare TIDE and IPS scores between risk groups. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to all assessments of statistical differences.

Enrichment analyses of DEGs between risk groups

The “edgeR” package was introduced to determine the DEGs between risk groups in the TCGA-LUAD dataset, applying thresholds set at |logFC| >1 and FDR ≤0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were subsequently implemented on the DEGs using the “clusterProfiler” (19) and “enrichplot” R packages (20) (adjusted P <0.05).

Tumor mutation burden (TMB) analysis

Genomic alteration profiles between the high- and low-risk groups were systematically compared through the “maftools” R package (21). Mutation spectrum differences—including variant types, single nucleotide variant (SNV) classes, and mutation rates—were visualized by waterfall plots. The distribution landscape of the 20 highest-ranking genes by mutation frequency was further analyzed. Additionally, the mutational profiles of the model genes were illustrated utilizing bar plots. TMB scores were calculated for each sample, and variations in genomic instability between risk groups were examined by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Correlation analysis and drug screening

We carried out drug sensitivity analysis based on data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer 2 (GDSC2) database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). We introduced the “oncoPredict” R package (22) to evaluate the correlation between risk groups and susceptibility to chemotherapeutic agents. We compared the variations across groups through the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Cell culture

The human normal bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B (SNL-203) and the human LUAD cell line A549 (SNL-089) were purchased from SunnCell (Wuhan, China). Both BEAS-2B and A549 cells underwent culture in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (11965118, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10099141C, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS) (C0222, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). We maintained all cells in an incubator (37 ℃, 5% CO2).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

We isolated total RNA from cells using TRIzol reagent (15596018CN, Thermo Fisher Scientific). We measured RNA concentration and purity through a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was reverse transcribed from RNA by the HiScript III RT SuperMix kit (R323-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qRT-PCR was implemented using the SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (CL132-01, Vazyme) on StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Measurement of oxygen consumption rate (OCR)

A total of 1×104 A549 cells were seeded into Seahorse XF 96-well microplates (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and incubated overnight. Prior to OCR assessment, the routine culture medium was replaced with XF base medium (Agilent), followed by a 2-h incubation. Subsequently, oligomycin (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), FCCP (MCE), as well as rotenone (MCE) and antimycin A (Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA), were injected sequentially according to the designated time points. Finally, OCR was measured using a Seahorse XF-96 extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, North Billerica, MA, USA).

Measurement of intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP)

Upon completion of cell culture, lysis buffer was added, and cells were lysed for 10 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected. Reagent I and working solution were then added to the supernatant in accordance with the instructions provided with the assay kit (BC0300, Solarbio, Beijing, China), and absorbance was detected using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential in A549 cells was evaluated using the 5,5',6,6'-tetrachloro-1,1',3,3'-tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) fluorescent probe (T3168, Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells subjected to different treatments were first washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then incubated with 5 µM JC-1 probe for 30 min at 37 ℃ in the dark. After an additional PBS wash, cell images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) for subsequent analysis. Red fluorescence corresponds to JC-1 aggregates, whereas green fluorescence represents JC-1 monomers; the mitochondrial membrane potential is expressed as the ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) cell viability assay

Cells from the experimental group (sh-CIDEC) and the control group (sh-NC) were plated into 96-well plates (2×103 cells/well) and cultured (37 ℃, 5% CO2) for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. Each well was treated with 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (C0037, Beyotime) and incubated for 2 h at each time point. Absorbance at 450 nm was then quantified by a microplate reader to examine cell proliferation activity.

Transwell invasion assay

Following resuspension in 200 µL of DMEM medium (4×104 to 5×104 cells/well), we placed A549 cells in the upper chamber of a Matrigel-precoated Transwell insert (354230, Corning, Corning, NY, USA). The lower chamber was filled with 600 µL of complete medium. After incubation (24 h, 37 ℃, 5% CO2), the cells underwent fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and stained with crystal violet solution for 2 h. Five randomly selected fields per sample were imaged using an inverted microscope, and cell counts were carried out for quantitative analysis.

Wound healing assay

When A549 cells reached approximately 80% confluency in the culture plate, a mechanical wound was created by vertically drawing a sterile 200 µL pipette tip across the bottom of the plate. Data and statistical analyses

Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). We introduced one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Student’s t-test, or two-way ANOVA to evaluate variations between two or multiple groups/time points. Data analysis was carried out using Microsoft Excel, GraphPad Prism 8.0, and ImageJ. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Construction and validation of a prognostic model based on MBGs and CPGs in LUAD

Leveraging the TCGA-LUAD dataset and MBGs and CPGs, we detected 384 differentially expressed MBGs and CPGs in LUAD (Figure 1A). To establish a robust prognostic model, the TCGA-LUAD dataset constituted the training cohort. A total of 159 survival-associated genes were determined by univariate Cox regression analysis (P<0.05) (Table S1). The LASSO regression analysis was applied, and the optimal tuning parameter λ (λ = 0.05) was established via 10-fold cross-validation, retaining seven genes with the highest biological relevance (Table S2; Figure 1B,1C). Multivariate Cox regression further refined the model, yielding seven independent prognostic predictors and their respective risk coefficients (Figure 1D). To validate the prognostic model in a clinical context, the efficiency of the TCGA-LUAD and GSE72094 datasets was calculated. We generated ROC curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival. The AUC values for the TCGA-LUAD cohort were 0.75, 0.72, and 0.71, respectively (Figure 1E), while those for the GSE72094 cohort were 0.70, 0.68, and 0.78 (Figure 1F), indicating consistent predictive performance across time points and independent datasets. Survival analysis confirmed that high-risk patients exhibited remarkably poorer overall survival in both the TCGA-LUAD and GSE72094 cohorts (P<0.0001; Figure 1G,1H), indicating in a higher risk of death in this group. Heatmap analysis revealed similar expression patterns of seven model genes in both datasets (Figure 1I,1J). Furthermore, the arrangement of survival status and risk scores indicated that high-risk patients contribute to diminished clinical outcomes in both the TCGA-LUAD and GSE72094 datasets (Figure 1K,1L).

Construction and validation of a risk score-based prognostic nomogram

To evaluate the risk score in a clinical context, we integrated patient risk scores with clinical characteristics (TNM stage, sex, tumor stage, age) from the TCGA-LUAD dataset. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses pointed out that the risk score was remarkably linked to LUAD prognosis (P<0.001; Figure 2A,2B) and served as an independent prognostic factor. A nomogram incorporating the risk score and other clinicopathological variables was constructed to estimate survival probability in LUAD patients (Figure 2C). Calibration curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival showed a high level of concordance between predicted and actual results (Figure 2D-2F), indicating high predictive accuracy and supporting its use in guiding individualized clinical decision-making. Furthermore, ROC analysis served to examine the discriminative ability of the nomogram and individual clinical variables. The nomogram achieved an AUC value of 0.77 (Figure 2G), demonstrating its superior prognostic discrimination ability.

Analysis of immune characteristics and functional annotation of DEGs between risk groups

To systematically characterize differences in the TME between risk groups, we comprehensively analyzed immune cell infiltration patterns and potential response to immunotherapy. Immune infiltration analysis pointed out that, relative to the low-risk group, high-risk patients had remarkably diminished recruitment of anti-tumor immune cells—such as dendritic cells, B cells, mast cells, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) (P<0.01)—suggesting impaired immune responsiveness (Figure 3A). Evaluation through the ESTIMATE algorithm showed that stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores were all remarkably lower in the high-risk group (P<0.05; Figure 3B), indicating decreased immune and stromal cell infiltration and an elevated concentration of tumor cells in the TME of high-risk patients. These features may collectively contribute to poorer clinical outcomes in this group. To assess the linkage connecting risk groups and immunotherapy sensitivity, TIDE and IPS scores were calculated. The high-risk group exhibited prominent immune evasion features (P<0.001; Figure 3C), suggesting potentially poorer response to immunotherapy. Further IPS analysis pointed out that high-risk patients in the cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4)-positive/programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)-negative subgroup had significantly lower scores (P<0.01; Figure 3D), indicating a likely reduced response to CTLA-4 inhibitor therapy.

Based on strict criteria (|logFC| >1, FDR ≤0.05), we determined a total of 1,256 DEGs (online supplementary table: https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2025-1-2721-1.xlsx). We introduced GO and KEGG enrichment analyses to elucidate transcriptomic differences between risk groups. GO analysis pointed out that these DEGs were primarily linked to key BPs related to cell proliferation and mitochondrial biogenesis. In BP terms, DEGs were markedly enriched in regulation of membrane potential and sodium ion transmembrane transport, reflecting their close association with cellular energy metabolism. For cellular component (CC), remarkable enrichment was observed in chromosome-related structures such as nucleosomes and centromeric core domains, suggesting potential roles in cell cycle regulation through chromatin assembly. Molecular function (MF) analysis further indicated involvement in metal ion transmembrane transporter activity and other mitochondrial functions (Figure 3E). KEGG pathway analysis revealed remarkable enrichment in retinol metabolism and steroid hormone biosynthesis, both closely linked to the regulation of cell proliferation (Figure 3F). These results not only highlight metabolic heterogeneity between risk groups but also clarify the molecular mechanisms by which MBGs and CPGs influence LUAD prognosis.

Assessment of mutation characteristics across risk groups

Systematic analysis of mutational profiles in LUAD patients revealed widespread genomic alterations in both risk groups (Figure 4A,4B). Further investigation into the distribution patterns of the top 20 most frequently mutated genes showed that, although the overall mutation profiles were similar, the high-risk group exhibited increased mutational burden in several driver genes such as TP53, TTN, and MUC16 (Figure 4C,4D). Analysis of the seven model genes indicated higher mutation rates in KRT17 and NPAS2 in the high-risk group (Figure 4E). TMB analysis demonstrated that the high-risk group had remarkably elevated TMB scores (P<0.05; Figure 4F), suggesting greater genomic instability and immunogenicity in these patients.

Effects of CIDEC on metabolic flux, mitochondrial membrane potential, and malignant phenotypes in LUAD cells

CIDEC, a key member of the cell death-inducing protein family, can trigger programmed cell death through a caspase-independent pathway (23). Recent studies have linked CIDEC to the outcomes of various malignancies, including bladder cancer, pancreatic cancer, and gastric adenocarcinoma (24,25); however, its role in LUAD remains unclear. To elucidate the influence of CIDEC on metabolic flux, mitochondrial membrane potential, and malignant phenotypes in LUAD cells, a series of functional assays were conducted in vitro. Initially, CIDEC expression levels were assessed in BEAS-2B and A549 cell lines. qRT-PCR revealed a marked upregulation of CIDEC mRNA in A549 cells compared with BEAS-2B cells (Figure 6A), suggesting that its overexpression may facilitate malignant progression in LUAD. Subsequently, we knocked down CIDEC in LUAD cells. qRT-PCR confirmed high knockdown efficiency (Figure 6B), supporting its use in subsequent functional experiments. Assessment of OCR and ATP levels demonstrated that LUAD cells with CIDEC knockdown exhibited elevated respiratory rates and higher ATP content (Figure 6C,6D). Furthermore, changes in mitochondrial membrane potential were evaluated using JC-1 staining; the results indicated that CIDEC knockdown led to an increased mitochondrial membrane potential, reflected by a higher red-to-green fluorescence intensity ratio (Figure 6E). CCK-8 cell viability assays indicated that the growth potential of A549 cells in the sh-CIDEC group was significantly attenuated (Figure 6F). Furthermore, wound healing and Transwell invasion assays indicated that CIDEC knockdown in A549 cells remarkably impaired the migration and invasion capacities of LUAD cells (Figure 6G,6H). In summary, these findings determined that CIDEC contributed to the aggressiveness of LUAD by fueling cellular proliferation, migration, and invasion.

Discussion

LUAD is among the most lethal subtypes of LC globally. Current mainstay therapeutic strategies for LUAD patients include surgical resection, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted agents, and immunotherapy (26,27). In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have led to breakthrough advances in the clinical management of LUAD, significantly improving long-term survival outcomes in advanced-stage patients (28,29). ICIs—including monoclonal antibodies targeting PD-1, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and CTLA-4—act by alleviating T-cell inhibitory signals, reversing T-cell exhaustion, and activating anti-tumor immune responses. Nevertheless, the key molecular mechanisms underlying LUAD prognosis remain incompletely understood, which considerably limits the accuracy of clinical outcome prediction. Thus, elucidating the molecular pathways driving LUAD progression and establishing robust prognostic models are integral for advancing patient stratification and informing personalized treatment.

Based on the TCGA-LUAD dataset and MBGs and CPGs, this investigation systematically determined and formed a prognostic risk model for LUAD consisting of seven key genes (PLK1, HMMR, CYP27A1, LDHA, NPAS2, KRT17, and CIDEC). The model exerted dependable predictive generalizability in both the TCGA-LUAD and GSE72094 cohorts. By integrating the risk score with clinicopathological characteristics, a highly applicable nomogram was developed, which showed strong concordance between predicted and actual survival rates at 1, 3, and 5 years, outperforming conventional clinical staging metrics. These findings not only confirmed the robustness and generalizability of the model but also provided a reliable quantitative tool for individualized prognostic assessment in LUAD patients.

TME analysis displayed that high-risk patients exhibited remarkably reduced infiltration of anti-tumor immune cells—such as dendritic cells and B cells—along with enhanced immune escape mechanisms. These findings align with previously reported mechanisms through which an immunosuppressive TME promotes tumor progression (30). Furthermore, decreased infiltration of mast cells and TILs was observed in the high-risk group, suggesting their potential participation in modulating anti-tumor immune responses within the TME. Fan et al. (31) reported that mast cells, as sentinel immune regulators, can enhance clinical outcomes in LUAD by specifically recruiting CCR2+ T-cell subsets. Additionally, Jackson et al. (32) demonstrated that impaired TILs function facilitates advanced cancer progression, and that METRNL induces bioenergetic exhaustion in CD8+ T cells via metabolic reprogramming, thereby contributing to tumor aggressiveness. The ESTIMATE algorithm further supported lower stromal and immune cell infiltration alongside higher tumor purity in high-risk patients. This “cold tumor” phenotype may underlie their poor response to immunotherapy, particularly CTLA-4 inhibition (33,34). Together, these results expand our understanding of immune evasion mechanisms in LUAD and offer valuable insights for selecting immunotherapeutic strategies in high-risk populations.

Functional enrichment analyses confirmed that the DEGs between the risk groups were closely linked to energy metabolism and metabolic reprogramming. Specifically, GO analysis indicated remarkable involvement of DEGs in energy-related processes such as regulation of membrane potential and sodium ion transmembrane transport, while KEGG analysis highlighted notable enrichment in retinol metabolism and steroid hormone biosynthesis pathways. These findings suggested that tumor cells in high-risk patients may drive metabolic reprogramming through mitochondrial dysfunction (35,36). Of note, significant enrichment of nucleosome and centromeric chromatin components at the cellular level implies that altered chromatin stability may underpin the malignant phenotype in high-risk tumors (37,38). Additionally, TMB analysis showed a remarkably elevated accumulation of somatic mutations in the high-risk group, indicating greater tumor heterogeneity and neoantigen potential. Although elevated TMB theoretically contributes to enhanced immune recognition, the observed immunosuppressive features suggest that high-risk tumors may evade immune clearance through immunosuppressive mechanisms, ultimately contributing to poor clinical outcomes (39,40). This aligns with the recently proposed hypothesis that mutational accumulation drives tumor heterogeneity (41). These results uncover key molecular networks through which MBGs and CPGs contribute to LUAD pathogenesis, providing a theoretical basis for dual targeting of metabolic and proliferative pathways. Furthermore, drug sensitivity predictions revealed distinct responses to chemotherapeutic and targeted agents across the risk groups. The low-risk group exerted increased sensitivity to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors (olaparib) and histone deacetylase inhibitors (vorinostat). Dong et al. (42) reported that olaparib enhances survival in RB1-mutant LUAD patients by suppressing activation of the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway. Vorinostat induces tumor cell death by increasing acetylation of autophagy-related proteins such as LC3-II and Beclin-1 through targeting histone deacetylase (HDAC) (43). The high-risk group exhibited better responses to taxanes (docetaxel) and 5-fluorouracil. A clinical study by Matsumoto et al. (44) confirmed that combination therapy with docetaxel and ramucirumab is effective against LUAD with choroid plexus metastasis. Shan et al. (45) demonstrated that synergistic treatment with 5-Fluorouracil and ginsenoside Rg3 suppresses LUAD proliferation and metastasis in both in vitro and in vivo models by specifically inhibiting phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) p65 subunit. These findings lay theoretical groundwork for risk-stratified precision therapy in LUAD.

In the constructed prognostic model, a total of seven key genes significantly associated with patient outcomes were identified. Among these, CIDEC was selected for functional validation due to its relatively limited characterization in LUAD and its potential involvement in metabolic reprogramming. CIDEC (also known as FSP27) is a member of the CIDE protein family. Under normal physiological conditions, it is predominantly expressed in white and brown adipose tissues, where it promotes lipid droplet fusion and enlargement by localizing to lipid droplet contact sites, thereby maintaining cellular lipid homeostasis and orderly triglyceride storage (46,47). In cancer cells, lipid metabolic reprogramming represents a hallmark of malignant progression. Aberrant lipid droplet accumulation mediated by CIDEC can reshape the metabolic landscape of tumor cells through effects on energy supply, lipid signaling, and oxidative stress, consequently influencing proliferative and invasive capacities (48,49). In the present study, CIDEC was identified as a positive risk factor (risk coefficient: +0.06831), and its expression level correlated positively with unfavorable clinical outcomes in LUAD patients. A previous investigation by Mu and colleagues identified CIDEC as a prognosis-related gene in LUAD (50); however, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. Therefore, a series of in vitro experiments was conducted to systematically characterize the functional role of CIDEC in LUAD malignant phenotypes. qRT-PCR results confirmed that CIDEC was markedly overexpressed in LUAD cell lines, suggesting that its upregulation is closely associated with tumor progression. Of note, a recent study by Chen et al. (51) reported downregulation of CIDEC in NSCLC tissues and showed that CIDEC overexpression suppressed malignant phenotypes, which is somewhat inconsistent with the findings of the current work. This discrepancy may stem from molecular heterogeneity between LUAD and other NSCLC subtypes (e.g., lung squamous cell carcinoma), or from differences in sample sources, detection methodologies, and cohort characteristics, warranting further investigation. To explore the mechanism of CIDEC action in LUAD, CIDEC was knocked down in LUAD cells. Functional assays revealed that CIDEC knockdown significantly reduced cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while concurrently elevating OCR and ATP levels; moreover, JC-1 staining demonstrated a marked increase in mitochondrial membrane potential. Collectively, these findings indicate that CIDEC may play a pivotal role in regulating tumor cell energy metabolism. Mechanistically, CIDEC (FSP27), as a member of the CIDE family, has been established to promote lipid droplet fusion and growth, thereby driving intracellular triglyceride accumulation (23). This process may sequester fatty acids in an inert storage form, reducing their entry into mitochondria for β-oxidation and consequently suppressing oxidative phosphorylation levels (52). Based on this, we hypothesize that upon CIDEC knockdown, lipid substrates may be redirected into mitochondrial metabolic pathways, leading to compensatory increases in OCR, ATP content, and mitochondrial membrane potential. Prior studies have also shown that AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a key regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, participates in modulating FSP27 protein stability, and that FSP27 deficiency results in aberrant lipid droplet accumulation accompanied by enhanced mitochondrial activity (53,54). Furthermore, mitochondrial functional status has been demonstrated to be closely linked to the migratory and invasive abilities of LUAD cells (55). Thus, CIDEC-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming may influence tumor cell invasive behavior, at least in part, through modulation of mitochondrial function. Additionally, lipids are closely involved in energy storage, maintenance of membrane integrity, and signal transduction; however, excessive lipid synthesis has been shown to accelerate tumor initiation, colonization, and metastasis (56). Huang et al. (57) reported that CIDEC facilitates triglyceride accumulation by interacting with AGPAT (1-acylglycerol-3-phosphate O-acyltransferase), thereby accelerating the synthesis of phosphatidic acid. In addition, lipid catabolism and anabolism can modulate the activity of stromal and immune cells through processes such as energy metabolism and oxidative stress, further contributing to tumor progression (58). Taken together, these findings lead us to postulate that CIDEC may contribute to LUAD malignant progression through an axis involving lipid accumulation, suppression of mitochondrial metabolism, and promotion of malignant phenotypes. This discovery offers a potential theoretical foundation for metabolic intervention strategies targeting CIDEC. Nevertheless, the precise molecular intermediates that link lipid droplet dynamics to mitochondrial reprogramming remain to be further elucidated.

Through the integration of high-throughput sequencing and functional experiments, this investigation successfully formed and examined a prognostic risk model for LUAD leveraging MBGs and CPGs. The model exerted dual capability in both strong predictive performance and effective patient stratification in terms of immune features, mutational profiles, and drug sensitivity, thereby providing an innovative theoretical foundation and practical tool for precision medicine in LUAD. The current investigation is subject to several limitations. First, the prognostic model was constructed using retrospective cohorts, and its clinical applicability requires further validation through prospective studies. Second, analyses of the TME and drug sensitivity relied predominantly on bioinformatic predictions, necessitating additional in vitro, in vivo, and clinical sample-based experiments to confirm their reliability. Furthermore, the functional in vitro experiments in this study were conducted exclusively using the A549 cell line; therefore, the generalizability of the conclusions remains to be validated across additional LUAD cell lines and in animal models. Moreover, although this study preliminarily reveals a potential role for CIDEC in regulating mitochondrial metabolism and malignant phenotypes, the precise molecular mechanisms and the upstream and downstream signaling pathways involved have yet to be fully elucidated. It should also be noted that direct measurements of metabolic flux were not performed in the current study. Nevertheless, transcriptional alterations in MBGs can, to some extent, reflect reprogramming of cellular metabolic states, and mitochondrial biogenesis, a key process governing mitochondrial abundance, oxidative capacity, and energy output, is considered an important indicator of metabolic remodeling in tumors. Future investigations will integrate metabolic flux analysis with systematic assessments of mitochondrial function to further clarify the underlying molecular mechanisms. In addition, this study lacked clinical validation. Accordingly, future investigations should enroll larger cohorts of LUAD tumors and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues with long-term follow-up data to further verify the reliability and clinical applicability of this prognostic model. In addition, comparisons of baseline CIDEC expression in biopsy specimens from patients receiving distinct therapeutic regimens are warranted to explore its potential as a predictive biomarker for treatment selection and therapeutic response.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study systematically delineates the potential roles of MBGs and CPGs in the prognosis of LUAD, and the functional characterization of CIDEC provides novel insights into the mechanisms underlying metabolic reprogramming in this malignancy. Further research addressing these limitations is essential to facilitate the translation of these findings into clinical practice.
