Developing the risk prediction model (ProlncSig) from lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRNAs for prognosis prediction in breast cancer
Authors: Xiaoyu Fu, Weixing Wang, Bradley M Downs, Juanjuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlterations in cellular metabolism are known to play a crucial role in the development and progression of cancer. The lipoxygenase pathway, which controls unsaturated fatty acid metabolism, has been shown to regulate tumour progression and is commonly altered in breast cancer. In this study, we first obtained 11 lipoxygenase pathway genes from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). We then explored the lipoxygenase pathway-related long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in breast cancer tissues from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Information from our analysis was used to construct a risk prediction model (ProlncSig) to predict breast cancer prognosis. We found that ProlncSig could accurately identify breast cancer patients that had significantly shorter overall survival from those that had longer overall survival. Moreover, ProlncSig performs better in predicting prognosis than the clinicopathological features. Furthermore, GO and KEGG enrichment analysis showed that ProlncSig risk score had a high correlation with immune signature. We developed and validated an accurate prognostic risk prediction model (ProlncSig) based on lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRNAs, which has strong potential to provide prognostic information and may provide novel guidance for immunotherapeutic strategies for breast cancer patients.KEYWORDS: Lipoxygenases, LncRNA, prognostic signature, tumour immunity, Immunotherapy, breast cancer

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women worldwide [1]. Although, early detection and effective therapies can improve the prognosis of patients with breast cancer, the high heterogeneity presented at diagnoses can lead to therapeutic challenges and can make predicting clinical outcomes difficult. To help overcome these challenges, validated prognostic models and predictive biomarkers are required [2].

Lipids are an essential building block of life, playing a crucial role in membrane structure, cell signalling and energy production. The lipoxygenase pathway is involved in lipid metabolism and has been shown to modify a large range of lipids. Lipoxygenase is a non-haem iron dioxygenase that has been shown to catalyse the peroxidation of unsaturated fatty acids (such as arachidonic acid or linolenic acid) into the corresponding short-lived 5-hydroperoxy eicosatetraenoic acid (5-HPETE). 5-HPETE, in response to dehydrating enzymes, produces multiple types of leukotrienes (LTs) and lipoxins (LXs). These metabolites can then be further broken down through enzymatic activity and has been shown to mediate tumour progression [3,4]. The lipid mediators that are produced by lipoxygenases are involved in activation of pro-inflammatory signal transduction pathways such as the nuclear factor κB pathway, which illustrates the intimate link between lipid signalling and transcription factor activation. Lipoxygenase metabolites (e.g. leukotrienes) promote tumour-associated inflammation and angiogenesis [5]. Lipoxygenase pathway dysregulation is linked to immunosuppressive microenvironments in multiple cancers [6]. Lipoxygenase and its metabolites have been shown to play a dual role in the development and progression of various cancers [7]. For example, the lipoxygenase gene ALOX15B has been shown to have an inhibitory action on tumorigenesis in prostate cancer [8] and breast cancer [9]. Conversely, ALOX15B has also been shown to associate with tumour progression and poor outcome in colorectal cancer [10,11]. Overall, multiple genes of the lipoxygenase pathway have been shown to affect tumour progression and can likely provide information that can be used to predict prognosis and help guide treatment strategies for cancer patients.

LncRNAs (long non-coding RNAs) are non-protein coding transcripts that are longer than 200 nucleotides in length. Although lncRNAs do not directly encode proteins, there is innumerable evidence that lncRNAs act as the organizational framework of subcellular structures and have been found to play key roles in multiple cellular processes [12]. LncRNAs are involved in regulating almost all biological processes such as apoptosis, cell cycle, migration, differentiation, proliferation and immune response [13,14]. Additionally, lncRNAs also regulate cellular physiology, the epigenome, transcriptional levels, post-transcriptional modifications, can encode polypeptides and can act as signal transducers [15–17]. In addition, the stability and detectability make lncRNAs a promising diagnostic, prognostic and predictive biomarker. In this manuscript, our goal was to investigate if lncRNAs that associate with lipoxygenase pathway can be used to make a prognostic signature to predict survival outcome for individuals with breast cancer.

In summary, we identified 13 lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRNAs and constructed the risk model, named ProlncSig, for prognostic prediction for breast cancer patients. We found that ProlncSig could accurately identify breast cancer patients that had significantly shorter overall survival from patients with longer overall survival. Furthermore, we found that the genes differently expressed between high- and low-risk score individuals were highly correlated with pathways important for tumour immune microenvironment. This report has unprecedentedly revealed the strong potentiality of lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRNAs as prognostic markers that can likely be used to help guide treatment strategies for breast cancer patients.

Materials and methods

Data collection

RNA-seq data, single nucleotide variation data and clinical information for a total of 1105 tumours and 112 benign controls from 1082 breast cancer cases were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets (Download on 2023–1–4, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The clinical information included gender, age, TNM stage, survival outcome and survival duration. The 1082 breast cancer cases were randomly divided into the training set (N = 541) and the test set (N = 541) using the R function (Table 1). Eleven lipoxygenase pathway genes were obtained from human gene set GOBP_LIPOXYGENASE_PATHWAY in the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp). RNA-seq data were normalized using TMM (trimmed mean of M-values) normalization. Batch effects were corrected via the sva R package. LncRNA expression was log2-transformed and z-score standardized.

Study design

The study design (Figure 1) was as follows: First, 11 lipoxygenase genes were screened using the package ‘limma’ in R software (version 4.1.3) in the training set to identify a multi-gene panel. Among the 1438 lncRNAs which co-expressed with the 11 lipoxygenase genes (r > 0.3, p value <0.05) [18], we performed univariate cox regression analysis to select the candidate lncRNAs. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was implemented to identify minimal lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRNAs to predict the survival. The regression coefficient β value (coef) of each gene was analysed using the multivariate cox regression. Finally, lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRNAs were selected to construct prognostic lncRNAs signature (ProlncSig) to predict the survival outcome in breast cancer. The signature was defined as risk score=∑ni = 1 (coefi×Expri), and Expri is the relative expression of the gene in the signature for patient i and coefi is the LASSO coefficient of gene i. The positive sign of coef indicates higher mortality risk. Using the median risk score as the cut-off, patients were classified as low-risk group (RS < median number) and high-risk (RS ≥ median number) group. We then evaluated the efficacy of the prognostic function of ProlncSig in the test set. The Kaplan Meier survival analysis and the log-rank analysis were used to determine the survival rate difference between the high-risk and low-risk groups. Finally, we performed an external validation of the ProlncSig using fresh-frozen breast cancer tissues approved by Institutional Review Boards of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University. The study was conducted in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki. Patients provided informed consent to use excess tissue for research.

The workflow of this study.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis

The patients were divided into low-risk and high-risk groups based on the median risk score of the ProlncSig. Then, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) for 1-year, 3-year and 5-year overall survival. The ROC curve was used to determine the prediction function of the lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRNAs model. The AUC analysis was conducted using the ‘timeROC’ package in R software.

Building and validating a predictive nomogram

A nomogram was developed to predict the overall survival rate at X-year. The risk of survival for an individual patient could be calculate by the points associated with each risk factor. The nomogram provided a graphical representation of the factors through ‘rms’ R package. Furthermore, calibration curves were applied to visualize the proximity between the predicted and factual OS [19]. The closer the calibration curve is to the diagonal, the better the nomogram prediction performance will be.

Gene ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis

Gene ontology (GO) analysis and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Enrichment Analysis are practical resources for research gene functions. The biological process (BP), cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF) of potential targets were clustered based on ‘clusterProfiler’ package in R software. In the enrichment result, p < 0.05 or p.adjust <0.05 is considered to be a meaningful pathway.

Immune signature

Immune cell infiltration is analysed by CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm [20] through ‘limma’ R package. The ssGSEA (gene set enrichment analysis) was used to quantify tumour-infiltrating immune cell subgroups during immune responses and immune functions between high-risk and low-risk groups. We used TIDE (Tumour Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion) to predict response to immunotherapy (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/). Based on tumour pre-treatment expression profiles, this TIDE module can estimate multiple published transcriptomic biomarkers in the high-risk and low-risk groups. The lower TIDE score, the greater the probability of immune escape, suggesting that the immunotherapy effect is worse. Immune checkpoints [21] expression was compared between high-risk and low-risk groups. And these candidate checkpoints encompassed PDCD1 (PD-1), CD274 (PD-L1), CTLA4, TIGIT, LAG3, HAVCR2 PDCD1LG2 and SIGLEC15.

TMB

Tumour mutation burden (TMB) was calculated as the total of nonsynonymous variants in each sample including deletion, splice site, nonsense mutation, missense mutation and insertion. The ‘ggplot2’ R package was used to visualize the relationship between TMB and gene mutations. The difference of TMB between high-risk and low-risk groups was analysed.

Clinical tissue samples

Breast tumour tissues and normal tissues of 22 breast cancer patients undergoing surgical treatment for breast cancer were obtained from the Department of Breast and Thyroid Surgery, Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University. Before surgery, all of the patients did not receive chemotherapy or other systemic treatment. Then, all the involved samples were transported in dry ice and kept in a − 80°C refrigerator until real-time PCR (qRT-PCR).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total lncRNAs from breast tumour tissues and normal tissues was extracted with the use of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen 15,596,026). Reverse transcription for cDNA synthesis using the MonScript RTIII Super Mix with dsDNase kit (Monad Biotech, MR05201). qPCR was carried out based on MonAmp ChemoHS qPCR Mix (Monad Biotech, MQ00401) by adopting the CFX Connect real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). qRT-PCR primer sequences are presented in Table 2. We calculated relative quantification values of RNA with the application of the 2−ΔΔCt method. The risk score of each patient was calculated according to the expression of lncRNAs in tumour tissues. All the qRT-PCR results and risk scores are in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3) and Perl. The unpaired Student’s t-test and the Wilcoxon test were used respectively to evaluate the normal distribution variables and the non-normal distribution variables. A class of logistic regression (OCLR) was used to evaluate mRNAs of each sample expression profile, which can be used to measure the degree of dryness of the sample. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard models were generated to estimate the hazard ratios of prognostic factors and to select independent prognostic factors. In analysing the differential genes, we used the Benjamini–Hochberg method to convert the p value to FDR. For all analyses, p ≤ 0.05 or false discovery rate (FDR) q ≤ 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Identification of lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRnas and establishment of ProlncSig

To identify the significant lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRNAs, we first identified which of the lipoxygenase pathway genes become altered after tumorigenesis. To identify the altered lipoxygenase pathway genes, we compared the expression levels of the 11 lipoxygenase pathway genes from MSigDB database between normal and breast tumour tissues. Of the 11 genes identified, 7 were lipoxygenases (ALOX12, ALOX12B, ALOX15, ALOX15B, ALOX5, ALOX5AP and ALOXE3) [22–24]. We also identified HPGD [25] and PLA2G3 [26], which participate in the lipoxygenase-catalysed pathway, and glutathione peroxidase GPX1 and GPX4, which silence lipoxygenase [27–29]. We found that eight lipoxygenase genes (ALOX5, ALOX5AP, ALOX12, ALOX15, ALOX15B, GPX1, GPX4 and HPGD) showed significant differences in expression between normal and breast tumour tissues (Figure 2(A-K)). Furthermore, we found that the expression of ALOX5AP in tumour tissues was significantly higher than that in normal tissue (Figure 2(A)), while the expressions of the other seven lipoxygenase genes in tumour tissues were significantly lower than those in normal tissues (Figure 2(B-H)). We found that ALOXE3, GPX1and PLA2G3 were not differently expressed between normal and tumour tissues (Figure 2(I-K)).

To identify the lncRNAs that are co-expressed with the eight identified differentially expressed lipoxygenase genes, a Sankey diagram was generated using the whole TCGA cohort. We identified 1438 lncRNAs that were co-expressed with the eight lipoxygenase genes (Figure 3(A)). Using the training set data, we used both Cox regression (Figure 3(B)) and LASSO regression (Figure 3(C)) to identify 13 lncRNAs (SIDT1-AS1, AP000350.6, AC027097.1, AC068491.2, USP30-AS1, AC034102.8, Z68871.1, AC009554.2, RNF213-AS1, LINC02615, LINC02649, AC002094.4, ZBTB40-IT1) to include in the lipoxygenase gene prognostic signature. The correlation analysis between the 8 lipoxygenase gene and 13 lncRNAs (Figure 3(E-F)) showed that ALOX12 had the strongest correlation with each lncRNA, and USP30-AS1 had the strongest correlation with every lipoxygenase.

Predicting the prognosis of breast cancer patients using ProlncSig

After the prognostic lncRNAs signature (ProlncSig) was generated using data from the training set, Kaplan Meier plots were generated to explore the prognostic function of ProlncSig in the training set (Figure 4(A)), test set (Figure 4(B)) and the whole population (Figure 4(C)). The patients in the high-risk group, risk score greater than the median score, had worse outcome than those in the low-risk group in the training set and test set (p < 0.05). Moreover, the ProlncSig risk score was informative across TNM stages (Figure 4(D-E), p < 0.001). Overall, the survival analysis indicated that the risk score of ProlncSig could predict the prognosis of breast cancer patients, although there is only a weak association between survival time and ProlncSig (Figure 4(F-H)).

Next, we measured the expression levels of the 12 lncRNAs with known transcriptome sequences (SIDT1-AS1, AP000350.6, AC027097.1, AC068491.2, USP30-AS1, AC034102.8, Z68871.1, RNF213-AS1, LINC02615, LINC02649, AC002094.4 and ZBTB40-IT1) in tumour and normal tissues from 22 breast cancer patients. We found that six of the lncRNAs (AP000350.6, AC068491.2, Z68871.1, RNF213-AS1, LINC02649 and AC002094.4) were significantly lower expressed in tumour tissues than in normal tissues (Figure 4I). Ten out of twelve signature lncRNAs showed expression trends consistent with TCGA, confirming their detectability and differential expression patterns consistent with our training cohort.

Then, we wanted to investigate if ProlncSig risk score could be used as an independent prognostic factor for overall survival irrespective of other clinical variables. Using Cox regression analyses, we found that ProlncSig risk score could be used as independent prognostic factors for overall survival, irrespective of the age, tumour size, nodal status and distant metastasis (Figure 5(A-B)). Furthermore, using ROC analysis, the ProlncSig risk score could distinguish individuals that had less than a 1-year OS from BC patents with more than 1-year OS: AUC of 0.808 (Figure 5(C)). Furthermore, the ProlncSig risk score performs better than the clinicopathological features in predicting survival outcome (Figure 5(D)). Additionally, the prediction accuracy was estimated by measuring the consistency index, and the results showed the model risk score had the highest prediction accuracy, >0.65 (Figure 5(E)). A nomogram was constructed with the combination of clinicopathological characteristics and ProlncSig risk score. We found that the clinicopathological characteristics could enhance the predictive value of the ProlncSig risk score of OS at 1, 3 and 5 years and could distinguish the patients with different risk (Figure 5(F-G)).

Association of lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRNA signature and immune microenvironment

In order to explore the association of ProlncSig and the immune system, we conducted a differential analysis of the Infiltration of immune cells in the established high-risk and low-risk groups (Figure 7(A)). Compared with the low-risk group, the infiltration of humoral immunity and cellular immunity related immune cells was significantly inhibited in the high-risk group. We analysed 13 immune-related gene sets representing different immune cell types, functions and pathways [30] (Table S4). The correlation between immune cell sub-populations and related functions was evaluated by ssGSEA. The scores of APC co-inhibition, APC co-stimulation, CCR, check point, cytolytic active, HLA, inflammation promoting, MHC class 1, parainflammation, T cell co-inhibition, T cell co-stimulation, type I IFN response and type II IFN response in high-risk group were significantly lower than those in low-risk group in the TCGA cohort (Figure 7(B-C)). It was suggested that the activities of immune cells and immune function of high-risk group were inhibited. We found that the expressions of the eight candidate immune checkpoint genes (PDCD1 (PD-1), CD274 (PD-L1), CTLA4, TIGIT, LAG3, HAVCR2, PDCD1LG2 and SIGLEC15) were significantly lower in the high-risk group than those in the low-risk group (Figure 8(A)). To investigate if the risk score has the potential to predict the efficiency of immunotherapy, we compared the TIDE scores for the high- and low-risk patients (Figure 8(B)). We found that the high-risk group and significantly lower TIDE scores, suggesting that they are more likely to evade immunotherapy.

Tumor mutation burden (TMB)

Finally, we were interested in investigating if the high- and low-risk score patients had a different amount of tumour mutation burden. We found a little higher number of mutations in the 13 risk lncRNAs in patients in the high-risk group (Figure 8(C), p = 0.041) than in the low-risk group. Next, we selected the top 15 mutated genes for visualization to investigate if they were enriched in the high-risk group. We found that mutations in the 15 selected genes occurred in 86.15% (423/491) of patients in high-risk group and 82.9% (383/462) of patients in low-risk group. Most of the mutation types were missense mutation, followed by frame shift delete, nonsense mutation, frame shift inserts, splice site and multi hit appeared in individual genes.

Discussion

It has been well characterized that tumour cells and cells in the tumour microenvironment undergo metabolism dysregulation and that these alterations are key factors for tumorigenesis and progression [31]. Moreover, natural inhibitors of lipoxygenase have been shown to inhibit tumour growth in a variety of cancer models [32,33]. LncRNAs are extremely intricate in tumorigenesis and cancerous metastasis. Tang et al. discovered that a protective lncRNA LYPLAL1-DT impedes the progression of triple-negative breast cancer via Mediating hnRNPK/β-Catenin Complex [34]. A study has confirmed the downregulation of LncRNA SNORD3A in breast cancer cells and tissues. And SNORD3A can specifically make breast cancer cells sensitive to 5-FU by enhancing the expression of UMPS and improve the efficacy of 5-FU chemotherapy in breast cancer patients [35]. Since lipoxygenase plays an important role in tumour growth, we wanted to investigate if lncRNAs that associate with lipoxygenase activity could also be important for tumour progression and can be used to predict tumour prognosis for breast cancer patients.

Many studies have shown that prognostic signatures, based on gene sets associated with specific biological traits, can be used to predict tumour prognosis. Predicting prognosis through the establishment of reliable risk models can help establish personalized therapeutic treatment strategies [36,37]. In this study, a panel of 13 lncRNAs was used to make a prognostic prediction signature (ProlncSig). At present, there are relatively few studies on these LncRNAs. In breast cancer specimens, the expression of USP30-AS1 is decreased, which is associated with a smaller tumour size, lower tumour grade and stage, and the absence of lymphatic and vascular invasion [38]. USP30-AS1 plays the role of an immunomodulator in influenza A virus infection [39]. The cell experiment and subcutaneous tumour model verified that the arginine methylation-related LncRNA z68871.1 in the model had a significant effect on the proliferation and invasion of breast cancer cells [40]. A study suggests that LINC02615 downregulation may be related to the risk of breast cancer in Iranian patients [41]. LncRNA ZBTB40-IT1 regulates the differentiation of human bone marrow mesenchymal cells through the microRNA-514a-3p/FOXO4 axis [42]. More research is needed in the future to clarify these LncRNAs.

We performed targeted validation using prospectively collected clinical specimens (n = 22, breast cancer tissues). Expression of all signature lncRNAs was measured via qRT-PCR with custom-designed primers. 10/12 signature lncRNAs showed expression trends consistent with TCGA, confirming their detectability and differential expression patterns consistent with our training cohort. This approach provides the first experimental evidence supporting the biological relevance of these lncRNAs in breast cancer.

We showed that ProlncSig could be used to predict prognostic outcomes for breast cancer patients. Next, we investigated the practicality of the ProlncSig risk model through evaluation of the C-index and calibration chart. We found that the nomogram showed satisfactory predictive discrimination ability in monitoring overall survival. Moreover, this ProlncSig risk model was superior to the model based on conventional clinical features and was an independent prognostic factor.

Although many studies have shown that metabolites can regulate cell proliferation, apoptosis and differentiation [43], recent studies have shown that they can also affect immune function. For example, arachidonic acid has been shown to suppress the immune response by inhibiting Th1 differentiation, NK cell function and T cell activation [44]. Multiple studies have shown that the regulation of lncRNAs on lipoxygenase genes, such as ALOX15 and ALOX12, has been shown to affect the function of the immune system. For example, Chen et al. showed that lncRNA ENST00000538705.1 regulates the expression of ALOX15 [45]. Similarly, antisense overlapping lncRNA ALOX12-AS1 can regulate the encoding of ALOX12 gene by interfering with translation [46].

Moreover, according to GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of the differential genes of the ProlncSig predicted high- and low-risk groups, we found high correlation with pathways important to the immune microenvironment. Furthermore, through the immune cell infiltration analysis and functional score, we found that the high-risk group was more likely to show immunosuppressive state. In the past decade, immunotherapy for breast cancer has made great progress with the emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors, including inhibitors targeting programmed cell death protein −1 (PD-1), programmed cell death protein ligand-1 (PD-L1) and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein (CTLA-4) [47]. Immune cells can induce cytotoxic responses while continuously activating checkpoints [48]. At present, immune checkpoint inhibitors have become an important therapeutic way of tumour treatment. To explore if the expression levels of genes known to be important for immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as PDCD1 (PD-1), CD274 (PD-L1), PDCD1L (PD-L2) and CTLA4, we further investigated the expression levels of immune checkpoints in patients with high- and low-risk ProlncSig scores. The expression levels of these immune checkpoint genes were statistically different between the high- and low-risk groups (p < 0.001), and the expression levels in the high-risk group were lower than those in the low-risk group. TMB is generally considered to be an important factor affecting the effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitors [49]. The gene with the highest frequency of mutation in the high-risk group was TP53 (35%), while in the low-risk group was PIK3CA (35%). The high-risk group had slightly higher TMB rates than the low-risk group, but not by much. There was also little difference in the mutational pattern between the two groups.

In summary, we developed a prognostic model of breast cancer based on lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRNAs named ProlncSig and showed evidence that this model may be helpful for prognosis and individualized treatment strategies. In addition, we also described a relationship between ProlncSig score and the expression of immune checkpoint genes.

Although ProlncSig shows great potential as a prognostic model, this study also has several limitations. First, all training and validation data were derived exclusively from the TCGA dataset – a rigorously processed but homogeneous resource. We believe that including data from other real-world samples will improve the accuracy and prediction ability of the model. Second, this study was based on bioinformatic analysis, more experiments in vivo and in vitro are needed to confirm the expression of these lncRNAs in breast cancer and to explore the detailed biological functions. Finally, the predictive capacity of ProlncSig for immunotherapy response warrants clinical validation in breast cancer cohorts receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study constructed a reliable predictive signature based on lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRNAs. This signature was identified as an independent prognostic factor and a highly practical nomogram was established. Moreover, lncRNAs model signature was highly correlated with immunosuppression. In short, lipoxygenase pathway-related lncRNAs have strong potential as prognostic markers and therapeutic targets for breast cancer. However, the specific mechanisms of these lncRNAs regulating breast cancer progression need to be further studied.
