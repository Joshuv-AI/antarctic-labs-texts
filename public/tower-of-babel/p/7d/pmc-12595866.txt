Healthy lifestyle reduces frailty-associated overall and site-specific cancer risks: a prospective large cohort study in Chinese adults
Authors: Hurong Lai, Caifeng Liao, Yansong Tu, Yazhi Yang, Wanwan Bao, Shan Zhang, Chuyang Lin, Huaijun Tu, Ling He, Jian Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty and unfavorable lifestyle factors are associated with elevated cancer risk. However, whether adherence to a healthy lifestyle is associated with a reduction in frailty-related cancer risk remains unclear. This study aimed to examine the associations between frailty, healthy lifestyle, and overall cancer risk, and to assess the extent to which a healthy lifestyle is correlated with attenuated risks of overall and site-specific cancers associated with frailty.MethodsThis prospective cohort study utilized data from the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB), enrolling 484,225 adults aged 30–79 years from 10 geographically diverse regions across China. The frailty index (FI) was calculated using 28 baseline variables, all of which represented health status deficits measured through questionnaires and physical examinations. Lifestyle behaviors were determined by smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, diet score, and body shape. The primary outcome was incidence of all cancer types during follow-up (ICD-10: C00-C97). Multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate associations between frailty, healthy lifestyle, and cancer risk.ResultsAmong 484,225 participants in the China Kadoorie Biobank cohort, 288,531 (59.6%) were women. After 4,774,217 person-years of follow-up, 21,459 incident cancer cases were identified. Participants with high frailty showed significantly increased risk of overall cancer (hazard ratio [HR] 1.31, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.23–1.38). Conversely, adherence to a favorable lifestyle was associated with reduced cancer risk (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.71–0.78). Additive interaction analysis demonstrated a significant negative interaction between frailty and favorable lifestyle regarding overall cancer risk (RERI: − 0.50, 95% CI: − 0.75, − 0.24). Most notably, we found that adherence to a healthy lifestyle was significantly associated with a reduction in the detrimental effects of frailty on overall cancer risk. Specifically, Participants with robust status and a favorable lifestyle exhibited the lowest cancer risk (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.51–0.63, P < 0.001). Furthermore, similar protective associations were observed for site-specific cancers, including lung, colorectal, and liver cancers.ConclusionIn this large-scale prospective cohort study of Chinese adults, we demonstrated that adherence to a healthy lifestyle was significantly associated with a reduced risk of frailty-related cancer. Our findings highlight the importance of integrating healthy lifestyle factors into cancer prevention strategies for frail populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07186-8.

Backgrounds

The increasing trend of global population aging has made cancer a major social, public health, and economic issue in the twenty-first century. Worldwide, an estimated 20.0 million new cancer cases and nearly 10.0 million cancer deaths occurred in 2022. The global cancer burden is expected to reach 35.0 million by 2040, a 77% increase from the 20.0 million cases estimated in 2022 [1]. Notably, with persistent population growth, the cancer burden among younger populations has increased substantially. Recent studies demonstrate that cancers in adolescents and young adults represent a substantial and growing global health burden [2]. Consequently, early implementation of effective intervention measures for cancer prevention is essential for alleviating the global health burden.

Frailty, characterized by diminished physiological reserve and multisystem impairment, is closely associated with increased cancer incidence and mortality [3, 4]. More importantly, frailty shows a strong association with increased risks of postoperative complications [5–7], chemotherapy intolerance [8, 9], and adverse outcomes [10–12] in cancer patients. Frailty assessment has been extensively employed to categorize cancer patients at risk of poor outcomes and evidence has shown that it can be involved in treatment decision-making in clinical practice [13, 14]. However, studies on the association between frailty and the overall cancer risk in adults remain sparse. To date, no large-scale cohort studies have investigated the association between frailty and overall cancer incidence in Asian populations.

Unhealthy lifestyles include smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, poor dietary habits, and obesity, are known to be associated with an increased risk of cancer [15–18]. The study revealed that in 2019, 40% of cancer cases among U.S. adults aged 30 or older were attributable to modifiable lifestyle factors [19]. Conversely, substantial evidence indicates that cancer risk can be reduced by adhering to a healthy lifestyle, which includes regular physical activity [20], a diet rich in fruits and vegetables [21], and maintaining a healthy weight [22]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that adherence to a healthy lifestyle may counteract the adverse effects of both biological aging and genetic risk on cancer incidence [23–25]. However, whether adherence to a healthy lifestyle is associated with a reduction in the adverse effects of frailty on overall cancer risk remains unclear.

In view of the above, we analyzed data from the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB) to examine the independent and joint associations between frailty, healthy lifestyle factors, and the risk of incident cancer. We also evaluated the correlation between a healthy lifestyle and the reduction in overall and site-specific cancer risks among frail individuals.

Methods

Study design and participants

Our study is an independent analysis using data from the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB). Detailed information on the design, survey methods, and population characteristics of the CKB has been described previously [26–28]. Briefly, CKB is a prospective cohort study involving over 0.5 million adults from ten geographically diverse regions across China, with 512,724 participants aged 30 to 79 years enrolled between June 2004 and July 2008. At baseline, trained health workers administered a laptop-based questionnaire to collect information on sociodemographic characteristics, dietary and lifestyle factors, and personal and family medical histories, while also measuring anthropometric indexes (details at http://www.ckbiobank.org). The CKB study was approved by the ethics review committee of the Oxford University Tropical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. 025-04, 3.2.2005), the Chinese Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Ethical Review Committee (Approval No. 005/2004, 9.7.2004), and the local CDC ethical committees in each study region. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study. All participants provided written informed consent prior to their enrollment [26]. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

In the current study, participants were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) no available data for assessing frailty (n = 19); (2) no available data for assessing healthy lifestyle (n = 0); (3) diagnosis of cancer at baseline (n = 2577); (4) missing covariate data on age, gender, education level, marital status, hip circumference, study sites, annual household income, and family history of stroke, diabetes, heart attack, and cancer (n = 25,903). Ultimately, a total of 484,225 participants were eligible for subsequent analysis. Definition of frailty

Frailty was assessed using the frailty index (FI), which was calculated based on the accumulation of multiple age-related health deficits. In this study, we constructed the FI following standard procedures described in previous studies [4, 29, 30]. Based on the health measures available in the CKB, we selected 28 variables to calculate the FI, including medical conditions (based on self-reported diagnoses by a doctor or physical measurements), symptoms, signs, and anthropometric measures (Additional file 1: Table S1). Since the primary outcome of the study was the incidence of overall cancer, we replaced the cancer-related variables in the references with psychiatric disorder variables. Each variable was dichotomized or mapped onto the 0.00–1.00 interval, with 0.00 representing the absence of a deficit (the healthiest state) and 1.00 representing the maximal expression of the deficit (the unhealthiest state). The FI was calculated for each participant as the number of deficits present in a person divided by the total number of 28 deficits that were considered. The FI is a continuous variable that ranges from 0.00 to 1.00, with a higher value indicating a poorer (or frailer) health status. As suggested by previous studies [4, 29, 31], we further categorized the FI into three levels of frailty: robust (FI ≤ 0.10), pre-frail (0.10 < FI < 0.25), and frail (FI ≥ 0.25).

Assessment of healthy lifestyle factors

Based on the previous study, five modifiable lifestyle factors were incorporated into this study, including smoking, alcohol intake, physical activity, dietary habits, and body shape (a reflection of balance between energy intake and energy expenditure) [32]. Each lifestyle factors was assigned a score of 0 or 1, with 1 representing a favorable lifestyle behavior (Additional file 1: Table S2). Notably, based on recommendations from the Chinese Dietary Guidelines [33] and consistent with previous studies [32, 34], we developed a simple diet score using the following criteria: daily intake of fresh vegetables, daily intake of fresh fruits, red meat consumption 1–6 days per week, legume consumption ≥ 4 days per week, and fish consumption ≥ 1 day per week. Each fulfilled criterion contributed one point, while unmet criteria received zero points. Thus, the total diet score ranged from 0 to 5, with scores of 4–5 classified as representing favorable lifestyle behaviors [34]. Overall, the healthy lifestyle score (HLS) ranged from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating healthier lifestyle behaviors. Based on the previous study, participants were categorized into three groups: favorable lifestyle (HLS ≥ 4), intermediate lifestyle (HLS 2–3), and unfavorable lifestyle (HLS 0–1) [24].

Follow-up and outcome measures

Incident cancer cases were identified from the time of the participants' enrollment into the cohort through linkage with local disease and death registries, the national health insurance system, and active follow-up [26]. Nearly all of the CKB population is now covered by the health insurance system, which records detailed information on all hospitalizations, including International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes, as well as details of coded examinations and treatment procedures. In this study, the primary outcome was the incidence of overall cancer (ICD-10: C00-C97). As of December 31, 2016, 21,459 incident cancer cases (4.4% of participants) had been identified in the study cohort. In addition, we further analyzed the eight most prevalent site-specific cancers, including lung (C33-C34), colorectal (C18-C20), stomach (C16), esophageal (C15), liver (C22), pancreas (C25), lymphoma (C81-C85), and leukemia (C91-C95). Due to the extreme imbalance observed in the distribution of participants across sex-stratified subgroups, sex-specific cancers (such as prostate, breast, and ovarian cancers) were excluded from the analyses.

Covariates

Data on the covariates were gathered by trained health workers through questionnaires and physical measurements during the baseline survey. The covariates in this study included age, gender, hip circumference (continuous), education level, marital status, study sites, annual household income, and family history of stroke, diabetes, heart attack, and cancer. Age was categorized into three groups: under 50 years old, 50 to 64 years old, and over 65 years old [35]. Gender was classified into two categories: man and woman. Education level was divided into five groups: less than primary school, primary school, middle school, high school, and college or above. Marital status was classified into four groups: married, widowed, separated or divorced, and never married. The study sites included five urban and five rural areas geographically spread across China: Harbin, Qingdao, Suzhou, Liuzhou, Haikou, Gansu, Hen, Sichuan, Zhejiang, and Hun [26]. Annual household income was categorized into four groups: less than $1,307, $1,307–$2,614, $2,614–$4,575, and greater than $4,575. Family history of stroke, diabetes, heart attack, and cancer was recorded as either “yes” or “no”.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean (standard deviation, SD), whereas skewed continuous variables are expressed as the median (interquartile range, IQR). Between-group differences were compared using Student's t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages), with between-group comparisons performed using chi-square tests or Fisher's exact tests. To assess the impact of multicollinearity among covariates on the results, we conducted a collinearity diagnostic test. Importantly, we did not identify any significantly collinear covariates (Additional file 1: Table S3).

Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed to estimate the cumulative incidence of overall cancer. Cumulative hazard curves were constructed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and between-group differences were evaluated using the log-rank test. Cancer incidence rates were calculated and reported as the number of events per 1000 person-years. Multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to assess the associations of frailty and healthy lifestyle with the risk of incident cancer, including an assessment of dose–response relationships, with results reported as hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). In addition, we assessed the interaction between frailty and healthy lifestyle on the risk of incident cancer. Stratified analyses were performed to examine associations between incident cancer risk and both frailty status and lifestyle categories, using unfavorable lifestyle and robust status as reference categories, respectively. Schoenfeld residuals was used to test the assumption of proportional hazards, and no evidence of violation was detected. Additive interaction analyses between frailty status and lifestyle categories were conducted using two indices: the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and the attributable proportion due to interaction (AP) [36].

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the results: (1) participants were categorized into five groups according to their HLS (0–1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 points); (2) using robust status and favorable lifestyle as the reference group, we further assessed the association between frailty, lifestyle, and overall cancer risk; (3) we excluded incident cancer cases diagnosed within the first year of follow-up; (4) the main analyses were repeated after excluding participants with a family history of cancer at baseline; and (5) participants with a family history of stroke, diabetes, or heart attack were further excluded to minimize residual confounding.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.4.3), SPSS (version 27.0.1), and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). Cox regression analyses were performed using the 'survival' package, while additive interaction analyses used the 'interactionR' package. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the study population, stratified by whether the participants developed incident cancer. Among the 484,225 participants enrolled in our study, 288,531 were women and 195,694 were men. The median age of the participants was 51 years (IQR 43–59 years). During a median follow-up of 10.12 years (IQR 9.16–11.11; totaling 4,774,061 person-years), 21,459 incident cancer cases occurred, yielding an incidence rate of 4.49 per 1000 person-years in the total population. The incidence rate was approximately 1.4-fold higher in men than in women (5.43 vs. 3.87 per 1000 person-years). Our analysis demonstrated that participants diagnosed with incident cancer were more likely to be middle-aged or elderly, female, widowed, or have a family history of diseases (e.g., stroke, heart attack, and cancer) compared with cancer-free participants. Compared to participants who did not develop cancer, those with incident cancer showed significantly higher proportions of frailty (8.61% vs. 5.12%) and unfavorable lifestyle (14.07% vs. 10.28%). Furthermore, participants who developed incident cancer exhibited more unfavorable lifestyle factors compared to those without incident cancer, including higher rates of smoking (37.39% vs. 28.65%) Independent associations of baseline frailty status and lifestyle factors with incident cancer risk

After fully adjusting for covariates, Fig. 2A demonstrates a gradual increase in the association between frailty and overall cancer risk. Compared with robust individuals, those classified as frail had a 1.31-fold increased risk of overall cancer (95% CI 1.23–1.38, P < 0.001), whereas those classified as pre-frail had a 1.15-fold increased risk of overall cancer (95% CI 1.11–1.19, P < 0.001). We further examined the associations between frailty status and overall cancer risk, stratified by sex (men and women) and age (< 50, 50–64, and ≥ 65 years), and observed consistent results in subsequent analyses (Fig. 2C, D and Additional file 1: Fig. S1A–C). Conversely, we observed a gradual decrease in the association between healthy lifestyle and overall cancer risk. As shown in Fig. 2B, adherence to a favorable lifestyle was associated with a decreased risk of overall cancer (intermediate vs. unfavorable HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.79–0.86; favorable vs. unfavorable HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.71–0.78, P < 0.001 for trend) in contrast to those with an unfavorable lifestyle. Similarly, we examined the associations between adherence to a healthy lifestyle and overall cancer risk, stratified by sex (men and women) and age (< 50, 50–64, and ≥ 65 years), and consistent results were observed in subsequent analyses (Fig. 2E, F and Additional file 1: Fig. S1D–F). Furthermore, individuals who reduced cigarette consumption (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.71–0.76, P < 0.001), limited alcohol intake (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.83–0.89, P < 0.001), and engaged in higher physical activity levels (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.92–0.97, P < 0.001) would be more likely to have a lower overall cancer risk (Additional file 1: Table S4). Interaction association of frailty, lifestyle, and incident cancer risk

Table 2 displays the association between lifestyle factors and overall cancer risk in the frailty-stratified analysis with unfavorable lifestyle as the reference. We observed a significant interaction between lifestyle factors and frailty status (P < 0.001 for interaction). Specifically, the strength of the association between favorable lifestyle and overall cancer risk varied significantly according to frailty status. In frail individuals, a favorable lifestyle was associated with a 28% reduction in cancer risk (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.59–0.87, P < 0.001), whereas in robust individuals the reduction was only 17% (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.74–0.92, P < 0.001). Similar associations were observed for smoking, alcohol consumption, and body shape. Additionally, we further examined the additive interaction between frailty and favorable lifestyle on overall cancer risk (Additional file 1: Table S5). The results showed a negative interaction effect, with a RERI of -0.50 (95% CI − 0.75, -0.24) and an AP of − 0.54 (95% CI − 0.84, − 0.24). Intermediate lifestyle and frailty observed a similar pattern, with a RERI of -0.47 (95% CI − 0.68, − 0.25) and an AP of − 0.41 (95% CI − 0.59, − 0.23). Discussion

Frailty and lifestyle factors interact to influence cancer risk. In our analysis, both lower frailty levels and adherence to a healthy lifestyle were independently associated with a reduced overall cancer risk. Furthermore, adherence to a healthy lifestyle was associated with lower incidence of overall cancer, even among individuals with higher frailty levels (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Most importantly, we found that adherence to a healthy lifestyle was significantly associated with a reduced risk of frailty-related cancer. Specifically, frail individuals who adhered to a favorable lifestyle was associated with a 28% reduction in cancer risk (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.59–0.87, P < 0.001). Similar protective associations were observed for site-specific cancers, including lung, colorectal, and liver cancers.

The accelerating global trend of population aging has led to a rising incidence of frailty, resulting in diminished quality of life and increased healthcare burdens [31]. Data from 21 cohorts involving 61,500 participants showed that 10.7% of community-dwelling older adults were frail, while another 41.6% were pre-frail [37]. Growing evidence suggests that frailty significantly increases the risk of both cancer incidence and mortality [38–40]. As cancer remains a leading cause of death worldwide, a deeper understanding of the relationship between frailty and cancer would significantly benefit both clinical practice and public health initiatives [1]. In the current study, our findings demonstrate that frailty significantly increases overall cancer risk, which is consistent with previous studies [38, 41]. Moreover, similar results were observed in sex-stratified and age-stratified (< 50, 50–64, and ≥ 65 years) analyses. Notably, this association was more pronounced in men (HR 1.41, 95% CI 1.30–1.54, P < 0.001) and young adults aged under 50 years (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.32–2.00, P < 0.001). This implies that although frailty prevalence is lower in younger populations, cancer risk stratification based on frailty status remains necessary (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated a monotonically increasing dose–response relationship between FI and overall cancer incidence risk, with consistent patterns observed across different lifestyle categories (Additional file 1: Fig. S5).

Promoting healthy lifestyles constitutes a key element of integrative oncology and plays a critical role in cancer prevention [42]. Smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, poor dietary habits, and obesity represent the predominant risk factors for the global cancer burden [43–45]. Previous study has demonstrated that nearly four in ten (37.7%) cancer cases in the UK in 2015 could have been prevented by modifying common lifestyle factors [46]. Our study further indicated that adherence to a healthy lifestyle was associated with a reduced overall cancer risk, with consistent results observed in sex-stratified and age-stratified analyses. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline analyses revealed a monotonically decreasing dose–response relationship between HLS and overall cancer incidence risk, with consistent patterns observed across different frailty statuses (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). These findings align with previous studies, demonstrating that adherence to a healthy lifestyle is significantly associated with a reduced risk of multiple cancers,, including pancreatic [47], liver [48], and colorectal cancer [49]. Additionally, we also observed that reduced cigarette consumption, limited alcohol intake, and higher levels of physical activity were associated with lower overall cancer risk. However, we found no statistically significant associations of dietary habits or body shape with overall cancer risk. This discrepancy might result from over-adjustment for confounders in the model, as the crude analysis demonstrated that maintaining an optimal body shape was significantly associated with reduced cancer risk.

Several potential biological mechanisms may explain the association between lifestyle factors and frailty-related cancer risk. Frailty is associated with chronic inflammation [50], immunosuppression [51], and metabolic dysregulation [52], all of which are recognized as carcinogenic factors. Conversely, healthy lifestyle factors can mitigate these adverse effects by reducing inflammation levels [53], improving immune surveillance [54, 55], and optimizing metabolic function [56, 57]. Research on reducing the adverse effects of frailty on diseases through modifying healthy lifestyle factors remains very limited. A large-scale nationwide cohort analysis from the UK revealed that adhering to a healthy lifestyle can substantially attenuate the impact of frailty to respiratory diseases mortality [58]. Another large-scale prospective cohort study has demonstrated that adhering to a healthy lifestyle could potentially reduce the risk of dementia by decreasing frailty levels [59]. Here, we provided new insight by demonstrating that adherence to a healthy lifestyle is significantly associated with a reduction in the adverse effects of frailty on overall cancer risk. Similar risk patterns were also observed for lung, colorectal, and liver cancers. However, no significant associations were observed for gastric, esophageal, and pancreatic cancers, nor for leukemia or lymphoma. This may be attributed to the insufficient number of incident cancer cases during the follow-up period, as well as potential unadjusted specific risk factors or unknown confounders. Additionally, additive interaction analysis revealed a significant negative interaction between frailty and healthy lifestyle in relation to overall cancer risk. Specifically, the protective association between favorable lifestyle and overall cancer risk significantly varied by frailty status, with frail individuals demonstrating a 28% risk reduction compared to a 17% reduction in robust individuals. These findings demonstrated that the protective effect of healthy lifestyles on cancer risk was modified by frailty status, with maximized benefits observed in frail populations. This may be attributed to the intrinsically lower baseline cancer risk in non-frail populations, consequently resulting in limited absolute benefits from lifestyle modifications. We further examined the incidence of frailty across different lifestyle subgroups and found that the favorable lifestyle subgroup had the lowest frailty incidence (Additional file 1: Fig. S6). These associations remained consistent in both sex-stratified and age-stratified analyses, suggesting that adherence to a healthy lifestyle may reduce overall cancer risk by lowering frailty levels.

Previous studies have confirmed that adherence to a healthy lifestyle can mitigate the adverse effects of both metabolic risks [60] and genetic factors [61, 62] on cancer risk. In this novel investigation integrating frailty and lifestyle factors, we examined their joint effects and interactions on cancer risk, demonstrating that lifestyle modifications may attenuate cancer risk among highly frail individuals. We further examined the association between lifestyle factors and overall cancer risk across varying frailty statuses (Additional file 1: Table S11). Specifically, smoking and alcohol consumption remained significantly associated with an increased risk of cancer across all frailty subgroups. However, no significant association was observed between dietary factors and cancer risk. Interestingly, we found that higher levels of physical activity were associated with lower cancer risk exclusively in pre-frail individuals. Additionally, compared with participants having a BMI of 18.5–27.9 kg/m2 without abdominal obesity, underweight individuals showed a significantly increased cancer risk. Conversely, both general obesity and abdominal obesity demonstrated potential protective effects against cancer in frail and pre-frail populations. Obesity manifests a dual effect in cancer, facilitating tumorigenesis and disease progression while enhancing patient response to immunotherapy [63, 64]. In this study, we present novel evidence suggesting that obesity may reduce frailty-associated cancer risk. To our knowledge, our study provides the first evidence that adherence to a healthy lifestyle is significantly associated with a reduction in the adverse effects of frailty on overall cancer risk. Moreover, a series of sensitivity analyses further validated the robustness of the primary findings. Consistent results were observed across all analyses, including when participants were categorized into five groups based on their HLS scores (0–1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 points).

The current study has several notable strengths that should be highlighted. First, this represents the first study to comprehensively evaluate both the joint effects and interactions between frailty and healthy lifestyle factors in relation to cancer risk. Our findings emphasize the importance of incorporating healthy lifestyle factors into cancer prevention strategies for frail populations. Second, the study utilized data from a large prospective cohort comprising over 500,000 adults across ten geographically diverse regions in China, providing robust evidence for associations between frailty, healthy lifestyle, and incident cancer risk. Additionally, although younger adults had the lowest prevalence of frailty, those with comorbid frailty exhibited the most elevated cancer risk. These findings provide novel evidence for optimizing preventive interventions and enhancing precision risk stratification for cancer in young adults.

Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge several limitations of the current study. First, frailty was assessed only at baseline, without repeated measurements during follow-up. This single-timepoint assessment may have resulted in non-differential misclassification, which would likely bias the observed associations toward the null. Second, the FI did not incorporate functional measures such as activities of daily living or instrumental activities of daily living, potentially limiting its ability to fully capture age-related decline. However, previous research suggests that the FI appears to be insensitive to the inclusion of specific items [65]. Third, lifestyle behaviors were self-reported in the CKB, which may have led to overestimates in the prevalence of low-risk lifestyle factors. Fourth, we only assessed lifestyle factors at baseline and did not account for potential changes during follow-up. However, a previous study using resurvey data from a subset of the CKB population demonstrated that most participants maintained relatively stable lifestyle patterns over extended periods [66]. Fifth, although our diet score captured major dietary components relevant to cancer risk, it did not include specific assessment of dairy products and seeds consumption, which have been associated with various health outcomes including gastrointestinal health. Future studies should incorporate the consumption of dairy products and seeds into more comprehensive dietary assessment systems to further elucidate their potential role in modifying frailty-associated cancer risk. Sixth, Despite the strengths of our prospective design and extensive adjustment for confounders, the observational nature of this study remains an important limitation. Although we observed a significant interaction, residual confounding or reverse causality cannot be entirely excluded. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as highlighting robust associations rather than definitive causal relationships. Future intervention studies are needed to confirm whether lifestyle modification causally reduces cancer risk in frail individuals. Finally, since the study population consisted exclusively of Chinese adults, the generalizability of these findings to other ethnic populations, different age ranges, or diverse lifestyle contexts requires further validation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in this large-scale prospective cohort study of Chinese adults, we demonstrated that adherence to a healthy lifestyle was significantly associated with a reduced risk of frailty-related cancer. Furthermore, similar protective associations were observed for several site-specific cancers, including lung, colorectal, and liver cancers. Our findings provided compelling evidence supporting the incorporation of frailty assessment into cancer prevention strategies, while highlighting the critical importance of lifestyle interventions in frail populations. Future research should focus on identifying optimal intervention approaches and quantifying the potential population-level impact of these strategies across diverse demographic groups.
