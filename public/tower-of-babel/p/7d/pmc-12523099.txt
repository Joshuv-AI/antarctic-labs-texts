Trends of opioids consumption for cancer patients treated by the palliative care department at the last week of life in a tertiary care center
Authors: Abdullah Alawlah, Raghib Abusaris, Ihab Sharha, Deepak Khorana, Mohammad Alkaiyat, Abdullah Algarni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPain is one of the most distressing symptoms for cancer patients. It remains a public health problem, and there is a need for better pain management at the societal level.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study is to characterize the opioid dosages and its type administered to adult cancer patients during their last week of life and to investigate the factors that may have contributed to it by the palliative care team at King Abdulaziz Medical City in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was performed at the King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC), utilizing charts of adult cancer patients admitted to palliative care. We studied the use of opioids in the last week of the patient’s life (day 7 before patient’s death and day 1 before patient’s death). We studied all cancer patients who were admitted to the palliative care department at KAMC and died in the hospital from 1/1/2022 to 1/1/2023 (one year).ResultsThe study included 178 patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Of our sample, 47.2% were male and 52.8% were female. The average age at death was 67.82(± 14.03) years. The average length of last admission to palliative care department was 22.25(± 14.25) days. The most frequent tumor types were gastrointestinal cancers (30.9%), then pancreatic cancers (12.9%), breast cancers and hepatocellular cancers (10.1% for each one of them). Morphine was the most common opioid prescribed at 32.0% 7-days prior to death and increasing to 37.6% 1-day prior to death. Hydromorphone was second most common opioid prescribed, at 17.4% 7-days prior to death and increasing to 24.2% 1-day prior to death. Using a morphine to hydromorphone conversion ratio of 5:1, the mean opioid equivalent to oral morphine 7 days prior to death was 32.01 mg, and on the day prior to death it was 27.78 mg.ConclusionOur study showed that the use of opioids in palliative inpatient care is common in Saudi Arabia, with morphine and hydromorphone being the most commonly used opioids. Our study results emphasize the need for an individualized approach to palliative care, considering patient-specific factors such as comorbidities and pain management.Keywords: Neoplasms, Pain, Opioids, Analgesic, Palliative care

Background

Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage [1]. It is a distressing symptom for any patient but can be particularly challenging for patients with advanced cancer nearing the end of their life [2]. Pain management is an essential component of end-of-life care for cancer patients, with opioids being the most robust pharmacological treatment for pain [3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has developed guidelines for managing pain, which include assessing patients thoroughly to provide timely and effective pain management to improve patient’s quality of life [4].

Opioids are medications derived from opium (which is the extract of the juice of the poppy plant) and synthetic derivatives [5]. Opioids are considered the cornerstone of pain management in end-of-life care, alleviating moderate to severe pain [6]. Many studies showed that policies restrictiving access to opioids do affect rates of opioid prescribing generally [7], however, there is no clear evidence indicating regulations on opioid prescription have an effect on prescribing specifically within palliative care [7], . Additionally, there is a significant global variation in opioid consumption. In a recent study, the ten richest countries worldwide, including Austria, Canada, Denmark, and the USA, account for 90% of the world’s morphine-equivalent opioids consumption [8]. On the other side, there is a rising epidemic of opioid overdoses in the United States and Canada [8, 9]. The opioid crisis in these two countries, however, due to poisoning of the recreational opioid supply rather than medical use of opioid and is not seen in other high-income nations (like Germany) with similar prescribing patterns or in low- and middle-income nations (LMICs), where access to these medications is extremely limited [10]. Just 1% of the yearly supply of opioid medications (measured in morphine milligrams equivalents) is available to the poorest 50% of the global population [10].

Palliative care is an approach that improves the health of patients and their families who are facing problems associated with their terminal illnesses [11]. It includes pain management through the relief of suffering using early identification, assessment, and treatment of pain, as well as patients’ physical, psychosocial, and spiritual aspects [11]. Palliative care aims to enhance the quality of life for patients facing terminal illnesses [12]. The care provided involves early identification, assessment, and treatment of pain and physical, psychosocial, and spiritual issues arising from the illness [13]. Pain management is a crucial component of palliative care, and palliative care specialists work to address specific pain issues that occur in patients’ lives [14].

Opioids are essential for pain management in Saudi Arabia; they are used for end-of-life care for cancer patients, but several studies suggest that opioids are under-prescribed [15]. The Saudi Arabian National Cancer Center (NCC) at the Saudi Health Council has developed guidelines for palliative care, which outline the importance of pain management, including the use of opioids in end-of-life care [16]. Also, the Saudi Ministry of Health - General Administration of Pharmaceutical Care have provided protocols for Palliative Care Patients - End of Life Care to provide adequate pain management and access to opioids for patients that are in need [17]. However, in comparing opioid medical use with other related studies conducted in developed countries such as USA and Canada, it is clear that opioids are under-prescribed in Saudi Arabia [18].

Furthermore, studying the needs of patients in their last days of life can help the stakeholders accommodate their needs. Indeed, all parties involved, including patients, families, and clinicians, can benefit from looking into the needs of terminally ill cancer patients. This includes helping to clarify the goals of care, encouraging shared decision-making, preventing needless investigations and aggressive care, ensuring goal-concordant care, and enabling patients and their families to finish unfinished business and have a good death [19–21].Therefore, our aim in this study was to elucidate patterns of opioid usage throughout the final week of life in a country where opioid prescriptions are underprescribed.

Methods and materials

A retrospective cross-sectional study was performed in King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia - Palliative Care department utilizing charts of adult cancer patients admitted to palliative care. We studied the use of opioids in the last week of the patient’s life (day 7 before death and the day before death: day 1,). We studied all cancer patients who were admitted to the palliative care department at KAMC and died in the hospital from 1/1/2022 to 1/1/2023 (one year). Therefore, all adults (above 18 Y/O) who were cancer patients under palliative care in KAMC at that time were added to the study. We excluded patients who died before completing 8 days under the palliative care department in the hospital and patients without clear documentation of cancer diagnosis.

The data collection sheet was designed to collect information about demographics, date of admission and death, type of opioids, and doses converted to the equivalent oral morphine. Information about the opioid-induced respiratory depression (OIRD) development after opioid usage were also collected. Comorbidities that is the occurrence of a chronic illness with a primary disease of interest [22]. In our case we have taken cancer as a primary disease and all other comorbidities were grouped as “Yes” or “No”. Data were taken from the KAIMRC data centre, missing information from the KAMC computer system (BestCare), and all data were transferred to Excel. After data cleaning, we transferred the data to SPSS for statistical analysis. We included the dosage of opioids on day 7 before death and the day before death.

Data analysis

Data were collected from the KAIMRC data centre, cleaned, and exported to the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS). As seen below, categorical variables were summarized by count and percentages and the desired prevalence was estimated using a 95% confidence interval. In addition, depending on the distribution, continuous variables were summarized by mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range. Furthermore, an association between categorical variables was assessed by the Chi2 test, whereas the association between categorical and numerical variables was evaluated using nonparametric tests. Logistic regression analysis was performed using the bivariate variable gender as an independent variable and the remaining variables as dependent variables. Variables found significant in univariate regression were analysed further in a multivariable regression model to assess the combined effect. P value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Discussion

Palliative care is an essential component of cancer treatment, and opioids are widely used to manage the pain of cancer patients in the terminal stages of their illness. However, the potential for respiratory depression, addiction, and other adverse effects from opioid use in palliative care may affect prescribing. This retrospective study was conducted on all cancer patients admitted to palliative care in their last week of life in a tertiary care centre in Saudi Arabia between 2022 and 2023 (178 patients). A different study conducted by Mohammad Al-Shahrion and his team at King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Center – Riyadh – looked at opioid prescribing patterns before and after an inpatient palliative care consultation [23]. However, this study included all kinds of inpatients who were referred to palliative care for pain management and they were receiving an opioid before referral [23]. In 2014, there were approximately 15 palliative care centers in KSA, limited to only cancer institutions. However, there are now more than 75 palliative care services across the KSA [24].

The current study results showed that the most commonly used opioid for pain in palliative care in KAMC was morphine, and the second most frequently used opioid was hydromorphone. Similarly, a study of 631 patients reported that morphine was the most prescribed opioid before and after referral to palliative care [23]. The percentage of patients receiving opioids regularly increased from 31.9% before referral to palliative care to 49.9% after referral, and very similar to our study opioid use percentage on day 7 (53.9%) [23]. Likewise, gastrointestinal cancers were the most common type of cancers (27.4%) [23], similar to the 30.9% in our patients. Lung cancer has low prevalence in Saudi Arabia. In fact, the prevalence of lung cancer among young adults in Saudi Arabia is 4.1% [25]. Furthermore, in 2014, the Saudi Cancer Registry recorded 452 instances of lung cancer, a low incidence when compared to global rates. Lung cancer was the fourth most prevalent cancer in males and the seventeenth most common in women, with these cases making up 3.9% of all cancer cases reported that year [26].

Opioid-induced respiratory depression (OIRD), the most serious opioid-related adverse event, is a “Life-threatening ADE” [27]. The results show that respiratory depression in our sample was experienced by 2.2% on day 7 and 6.7% on day 1. Similarly, a study conducted in Jeddah on opioid-induced respiratory depression (OIRD) in all hospital wards reported that only 0.57% of patients experienced OIRD [28]. The difference in the incidence can be explained. The sample for this study was from all wards, while our study include all cancer patients admitted during the last week of their lives. Also, OIRD was significantly associated with receiving a highest daily morphine milligram equivalent (MME) dose of ≥ 150 MME to 82.4% of patients) compared to those who had received 50 to < 150 MME (33.3%) and < 50 MME (50.0%, p < 0.0001) [28]. It was also noted that those patients who received the highest dose of morphine were admitted in surgical department. However, in our study no significant difference (p-value = 0.057) in the proportions of OIRD between day 7 and day 1, even though the mean oral morphine equivalent dose was reduced by 4.23 mg. These results suggest that we should reduce the fear of respiratory depression when the opioid dose is low.

Our findings also revealed that most of our cohort of patients had other comorbidities (91.6%), which may affect the management of pain and opioid titration. The presence of comorbidities may affect the treatment plan, or medications used, and this may lead to reducing prescribing of on opioids, limiting their use. However, our results indicated insignificant association between the use of opioids in patients with comorbidities. In comparison, the presence of comorbidities and the age of patients can affect opioids’ usage [29]. To improve pain management in palliative care, the use of opioids should be tailored to the patient’s individual needs, with consideration given to comorbidities, pain severity and patient preference [30]. In terms of population pharmacokinetics, age, body weight, body surface area, sex, and creatinine clearance were all found to be significant variables in the oxycodone clearance of 89 cancer pain patients. Of these, body surface area was found to be the most significant factor [31]. Healthcare providers need to actively provide information and strategies to recognized and prevent adverse effects associated with opioids, and to address them promptly when they manifest [30].

The distributions and numbers of opioids differ significantly from day 7 to day 1. Our results indicate that most patients significantly received single opioid both on 7 and 1 day prior to death. Opioid use in KSA is restricted and was given according to WHO’s analgesic ladder guidelines [17]. In the KSA more than one opioid is only allowed to be used in moderate to severe pain conditions in palliative care and end-of-life pain relief [17, 32]. Opioids, such as morphine, oxycodone, fentanyl, and others, might lessen pain from cancers, particularly so-called breakthrough pain, a sometimes severe type of cancer pain with a very quick onset, even if they are not always sufficient as the only analgesics for all patients [32]. The use of more than one opioid can be justified as some are long-acting opioid and some are rapid acting. For instance, the rapid parenteral fentanyl titration technique aims to quickly reduce extreme pain. When the patient’s pain is sufficiently managed, the physician may transition to a longer-acting opioid, like intravenous morphine or hydromorphone, if necessary [33].

Our study revealed that the mean dose of opioid equivalent to oral morphine administered to cancer patients in their last week of life on a palliative care unit was was 32 mg on Day 7 and 27.7 mg on Day 1. These doses are less than those documented in previous studies conducted in Saudi Arabia and globally [15]. A survey by Alqarni and colleagues investigated the use of opioids in last week of life in cancer patients admitted in palliative care between 2014 and 2015. The study indicated that the average dose converted to oral morphine equivalence was 49.1 mg on 7 days before death and 39.4 mg on the day before death, and our findings are lower [15]. Another study by Bailey FA and colleagues was conducted in USA in 2012. This study revealed that pain was documented in 82% of their subjects, while only 64% had an active opioid prescription at the time of their death [34], which is consistent with our findings. In our study, 61.8% received regular opioids before their death, however, it is reasonable to assume that the prevalence of pain would be similar in both patient groups. The initiation of opioids should be based on the lowest effective dose required to provide satisfactory pain relief and align with patient objectives with regular adjustment [30]. Healthcare professionals should work with specialists in palliative care to formulate an optimal pain management strategy. Vigilant monitoring of opioid adverse effects is essential, with clear measures for their prevention and treatment.

Multivariate analysis shows that the chances of increased opioid usage were higher in male than female cancer patients, and with increased age. This was also reported by Buonora et al., whose study about outpatients who were on opioid therapy for chronic pain (conducted in 2012). They found that male gender was associated with increased dosage with OR 1.82, 95% CI 1.22–2.70 [35]. In contrast, another Saudi study reported that with increasing age at admission, the percentage of patients prescribed opioids decreased significantly [15]. It is also noted that although the primary emphasis of our study was pain management, it is important to note that opioid may reduce minute ventilation and/or the respiratory response to chemoreceptor stimulation brought on by hypoxemia and hypercapnia, thus easing dyspnea [36–39].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. It was conducted in a single tertiary care centre, limiting the generalizability of the results to other settings. We only collected data on the last week of life, which may not provide a complete picture of palliative care for cancer patients in the department. A key limitation of this study is that comorbidities were recorded only as present or absent, without quantification using a standardized comorbidity scale (e.g., Charlson Comorbidity Index) or stratification by the number or severity of comorbid conditions. This limits the ability to assess the impact of comorbidity burden on the study outcomes. Future research should incorporate validated comorbidity indices and more granular analysis to better evaluate their potential influence. Another limitation of this study is the absence of pain assessment data. Pain scores were not recorded in the BestCare database, and a considerable proportion of patients were unable to communicate their pain level due to their clinical condition. Consequently, we were unable to evaluate the potential impact of pain on the outcomes analyzed. Future studies should consider incorporating standardized pain assessment tools where feasible to better understand the role of pain in this patient population. Lastly, due to retrospective nature of the study we did not know the causality of respiratory depression. Therefore, all the respiratory depression developed in cancer patient after the administration of opioid drugs were labelled as “opioid-induced respiratory depression”. Despite these limitations, our study however, adds to the existing literature on opioid use in palliative care for cancer patients in Saudi Arabia. Previous studies in Saudi Arabia have reported the pattern of opioid use in palliative care but very few studies have examined the trend of opioid consumption for cancer patients in the last week of life in a tertiary care centre. Our research has provided insights into current opioid consumption trends in palliative care for cancer patients in a tertiary care centre in Saudi Arabia.

Our study has certain strengths. Firstly, we collected data from a specialized palliative care unit, which may provide an accurate picture of opioids in the last week of life. Secondly, we used a standardized measurement, which has been used in previous studies to improve results comparability, which may provide a more comprehensive understanding of opioid consumption trends in palliative care.

Conclusion

Our study showed that the use of opioids in palliative care for cancer patients in their last week of life is common in Saudi Arabia, with morphine and hydromorphone being the most used opioids. The use of opioid was age and gender dependent. Male were > 3 times more likely to received opioids other than morphine or hydromorphone than women. OIRD was rarely seen, and only at high doses, while most of our patients had comorbidities. The use of opioids is restricted in KSA. Our study results show significant reduction of oral morphine dose from day 7 to day 1 before death. These results emphasize the need for an individualized approach to opoiod therapy, considering patient-specific factors such as comorbidities and pain assessment. Additional studies are needed to investigate the effectiveness of different opioids in palliative care and their potential side effects, particularly in the context of comorbidities. Directions for future research may include a larger-scale study that examines opioid consumption trends in multiple tertiary-care centres in Saudi Arabia to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Future studies may also explore reasons for the reduction in dose as death approaches and examine the factors contributing to individualized opioid titration. Future studies may also examine the patient’s and their families’perspectives on the decision-making process for opioid use.
