Client perceptions and satisfaction with HIV care clinic services in post-conflict Tigray, Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hafte Kahsay Kebede, Lillian Mwanri, Mengistu Welday Gebremichael, Fisseha Ashebir Gebregizabher, Gebremeskel Mamu Werid, Paul Ward, Hailay Abrha Gesesew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe 2020–2022 Tigray conflict affected health infrastructure, antiretroviral therapy (ART) supply chains, and continuity of HIV care. Assessing patient satisfaction during the recovery period is important to monitor service quality and inform service improvement. This study aimed to (i) measure the level of patient satisfaction among people living with HIV (PLHIV) receiving ART in post-conflict Mekelle City, (ii) model the relationships between four service-quality dimensions and overall satisfaction, and (iii) identify determinants of overall satisfaction with HIV care clinic services.MethodsThe study was conducted from October to November 2024 in Mekelle City, Tigray (eight public facilities and three operated by non-governmental organizations (NGOs)). Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) on ART for at least 12 months. Patient satisfaction was measured using a 21-item, 5-point Likert-scale questionnaire covering four domains: provider–client relationship (PCR), availability and accessibility of care (AAC), quality of care services (QCS), and facility and service environment (FSE). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to validate the four-factor measurement model, a second-order structural equation model (SEM) examined associations between the four domains and overall satisfaction, and binomial logistic regression identified determinants of dichotomised overall satisfaction (mean composite score > 3.69 = satisfied).ResultsOf 631 eligible patients, 569 consented (90.17%); 521 were retained after exclusions. Mean age was 42.4 years and 60.7% female. Overall, 62.4% reported satisfaction with HIV care clinic services. Domain-level satisfaction was: 64.5% for PCR, 31.5% for AAC, 57.2% for QCS, and 57.4% for FSE. The second-order SEM showed strong, statistically significant standardised associations between overall satisfaction and each domain (PCR β = 0.920; AAC β = 0.963; QCS β = 0.925; FSE β = 0.985; all p < 0.001). In multivariable logistic regression, clients at tertiary-level facilities (OR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.37–0.67) and at public facilities (OR = 0.64, 00195% CI 0.54–0.76) had significantly lower odds of satisfaction; sociodemographic and clinical variables were not independently associated with satisfaction.ConclusionsOverall satisfaction with HIV care clinic services in post-conflict Mekelle City was 62.4%, below Ethiopia’s Health Sector Transformation Plan-II (HSTP-II) target of 80% and at the lower end of the Ethiopian pooled estimate of 69.7% (95% CI 63.8–75.5%). All four service-quality domains were strongly associated with overall satisfaction, and tertiary-level and public facilities had lower odds of satisfaction. Targeted quality improvement is warranted across access, environment and quality-of-care dimensions, particularly at tertiary and public facilities. Whether changes in satisfaction translate into ART interruption, virologic failure, or drug resistance was outside the scope of this cross-sectional analysis and should be examined in longitudinal research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13031-026-00807-z.Keywords: Conflict, HIV/AIDS, Patient satisfaction, Structural equation modelling, Tigray, Quality of care

Background

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV/AIDS from a fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition, significantly reducing morbidity and mortality worldwide [1, 2]. As people living with HIV (PLHIV) live longer, attention has shifted from survival to optimal quality of life (QoL) through effective care, sustained adherence, and viral suppression [3, 4]. ART success depends on a continuum of care; that includes testing, linkage, retention, and adherence, with viral suppression as the clinical goal that benefits individuals and reduces community transmission4. Treatment interruptions, poor adherence, and inadequate monitoring continue to drive virologic failure and the emergence of resistant strains [5–7], particularly in resource-limited and conflict-affected settings.

The COVID-19 pandemic and concurrent humanitarian crises, including the Tigray War in northern Ethiopia, have affected health systems. Between 2020 and 2022, more than 80% of health facilities in Tigray were damaged or rendered non-functional [8], and a prolonged blockade restricted supply chains, limiting access to ART and diagnostics for over a year [9]. ART stockouts persisted for more than 12 months, ART enrolments fell by 76%, and follow-ups by 80% [10, 11]. This disruption affected continuity of care and created a high-risk environment for virologic failure, drug resistance, and avoidable deaths.

Post-conflict populations face increased HIV-related risks due to trauma-related vulnerabilities [12–14] and disrupted health systems, although evidence indicates that ART continuity is achievable with targeted interventions [15, 16]. The lasting impacts of war on health infrastructure and social determinants of health may shape patient experience of HIV care, indicating a need to examine how care is perceived in such settings [17]. Patient-reported outcomes (PROs), including satisfaction surveys, provide insights into care delivery during recovery and can inform feedback mechanisms, adaptive service-delivery models, and patient-centred quality improvement [18, 19]. This study aimed to evaluate patient satisfaction with HIV care services among PLHIV receiving ART in post-conflict Mekelle City, Tigray. Specifically, it: (i) measured the level of overall satisfaction; (ii) modelled the relationships between four HIV-care service quality dimensions and overall satisfaction using structural equation modelling (SEM); and (iii) identified determinants of overall satisfaction with HIV care clinic services.

Conceptualisation of patient satisfaction in HIV care

The conceptual framework guiding this study (Fig. 1) was developed in three integrated steps to ensure both theoretical grounding and contextual fit. First, a targeted review of the patient-satisfaction and service-quality literature was conducted, with a focus on HIV-care research from sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and Ethiopia, to identify dimensions of care empirically associated with patient satisfaction [20–30]. Second, the SERVQUAL model of Parasuraman, Zeithaml and Berry [31], the most widely applied service-quality framework in healthcare, was used as the guiding theoretical model. Third, the framework was integrated with Donabedian’s structure–process–outcome triad [32, 33], positioning patient satisfaction as a process-related outcome that links service-delivery characteristics to downstream HIV health outcomes (adherence, virologic suppression, drug resistance, morbidity and mortality).

Conceptual framework of patient satisfaction with HIV care services, integrating SERVQUAL and Donabedian’s structure–process–outcome triad. Solid arrows represent relationships tested in this study (H1–H5); dashed arrows represent hypothesised downstream pathways (H6 – H8) recommended for longitudinal research

SERVQUAL identifies five canonical dimensions of perceived service quality: tangibles (physical facilities, equipment, appearance of personnel), reliability (dependable and accurate service delivery), responsiveness (willingness to help and provide prompt service), assurance (knowledge, courtesy, and trust-inspiring behaviour of staff), and empathy (caring, individualised attention) [34, 35]. Following established practice in healthcare-services research [34, 35] and prior SSA HIV-care satisfaction studies [20–30], the five dimensions were consolidated into four conceptually coherent first-order constructs that better map onto chronic ART care. Specifically, empathy, assurance and responsiveness were grouped under provider–client relationship (PCR); the access component of reliability was operationalised as availability and accessibility of care (AAC); the technical component of reliability (clinical competence, counselling, confidentiality) was operationalised as quality of care services (QCS); and tangibles were operationalised as facility and service environment (FSE). Overall patient satisfaction was modelled as a second-order latent construct reflectively indicated by the four first-order constructs. The empirical adequacy of this four-domain structure was then tested by confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using the present data and is reported in the Results.

Within Donabedian’s framework, AAC and FSE correspond to structural attributes (resources, staff availability, geographic distribution, physical environment); PCR and QCS correspond to the process of care (interpersonal interaction, technical competence, counselling, confidentiality); and patient satisfaction is the proximal process-related outcome, theorised to mediate the link between care characteristics and the distal clinical outcomes of ART adherence, virologic suppression and onward transmission [32, 33, 36, 37]. This integration is consistent with previously published SEM applications in chronic-disease care, including the Donabedian-based SEM of integrated chronic disease management leveraged from HIV/ART programmes in rural South Africa [36], and the SEM of patient satisfaction and adherence in HIV care reported by Wilson and colleagues in two Houston HIV clinics [37].

Hypotheses

The framework is operationalised as five tested hypotheses (H1–H5; solid arrows in Fig. 1) and three conceptual downstream hypotheses (H6–H8; dashed arrows in Fig. 1; not directly testable with the present cross-sectional data and recommended for future longitudinal research).

H1. Provider–client relationship Overall satisfaction. Higher perceived quality of the provider–client relationship, encompassing provider responsiveness, communication, language accommodation, explanation of test results and medication side effects, and involvement in care decisions, is positively associated with higher overall patient satisfaction with HIV care [20, 24, 37–39].

H2. Availability and accessibility of care Overall satisfaction. Higher perceived availability and accessibility of HIV care, encompassing provider availability during clinic hours, free access to medical care, geographic accessibility, off-hours contact, and phone support, is positively associated with higher overall satisfaction [23, 40–42].

H3. Quality of care services Overall satisfaction. Higher perceived quality of HIV care services, encompassing counselling, availability of educational materials, illness-prevention advice, ART adherence support, and protection of privacy during disease counselling, is positively associated with higher overall satisfaction [21, 22, 36, 43].

H4. Facility and service environment Overall satisfaction. Higher perceived quality of the facility and service environment, encompassing cleanliness and comfort of the waiting area and consultation rooms, friendliness of greeting from staff, brief waiting time, and the provider’s undistracted focus during the visit, is positively associated with higher overall satisfaction [22, 24, 44, 45].

H5. Second-order structure of overall satisfaction. Overall patient satisfaction with HIV care is best modelled as a second-order latent construct reflectively indicated by its four first-order constructs (PCR, AAC, QCS, FSE), with acceptable convergent and discriminant validity within a confirmatory measurement model [31, 34–37].

H6–H8 (conceptual). Higher overall patient satisfaction is hypothesised to be associated with higher ART adherence (H6); higher ART adherence is associated with sustained virologic suppression (H7); and sustained virologic suppression contributes to reduced HIV drug resistance, reduced HIV-related morbidity and mortality, and reduced onward transmission (H8). These pathways are grounded in the treatment-as-prevention paradigm and the UNAIDS 95-95-95 cascade [9, 37, 40, 46–48] but were not directly tested with the present cross-sectional data; they are recommended for dedicated future longitudinal research.

Methods

Study setting, and design

This facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Mekelle City, Tigray, Ethiopia’s, between 1 October and 30 November 2024. The study took place at eleven health facilities providing HIV care services during the post-war period. All selected facilities were actively providing comprehensive HIV care throughout the study period. The study was conducted approximately two years after the end of the Tigray conflict (November 2020 to November 2022), which severely disrupted HIV care services across the region. At the time of data collection, ART services had been partially restored under the post-conflict recovery effort led by the Tigray Regional Health Bureau and partners, although facilities continued to operate under significant resource constraints.

Study population, facility selection, and sampling

The study population comprised adults (≥ 18 years) living with HIV who were receiving ART at HIV care facilities in Mekelle City. Eligible participants had been on ART for at least 12 months at the respective facility and had accessed HIV care services between 1 October and 30 November 2024. Exclusion criteria were: severe mental or physical conditions impairing the capacity to consent, ART duration of less than 12 months, or being critically ill or hospitalized during data collection.

At the time of the study, Mekelle City had an estimated 11,600 PLHIV on ART distributed across 24 ART-providing facilities, based on records from the Tigray Regional Health Bureau. Of these 24 facilities, 13 were excluded: 4 private facilities declined to participate, 2 military hospitals had ceased ART services at the time of the study, and 7 public health centers were excluded due to very low active patient volumes (22 to 40 patients each), which would have limited their statistical contribution to facility-level analysis. The remaining 11 facilities (45.8%) were all included, comprising 2 medium clinics, 4 health centers, 2 primary hospitals, 2 general hospitals, and 1 referral hospital; one primary hospital and two clinics were NGO-managed. Together, these 11 facilities served more than 90% of all PLHIV active on ART in Mekelle City, with active cohort sizes per facility ranging from 174 to 3,833 patients. Individual per-facility cohort sizes were obtained directly from each participating facility but are not reported here because data-sharing agreements preclude disclosure of facility-identifiable patient counts. The proportion of facilities included substantially exceeds the minimum 15% recommended by the USAID/DELIVER Project’s Logistics Indicators Assessment Tool (LIAT) [49].

The sample size was calculated using the single-population proportion formula [50], assuming a 50% satisfaction rate, a 4% margin of error, and 95% confidence, yielding 601 participants. After adjustment for a 5% non-response rate, the planned sample size was 631. Given the homogeneity of HIV care services across facilities [51], the sample was distributed equally across the 11 selected facilities. Equal allocation was chosen to ensure adequate representation of each facility level and ownership category for facility-level comparison, consistent with WHO/UNAIDS quality assessment standards [52, 53], Proportional allocation was considered but rejected on practical grounds: with active cohort sizes ranging from 174 to 3,833 patients per facility, proportional sampling would have allocated approximately 230 respondents to the largest facility and fewer than 15 respondents to the smallest, the latter being below the minimum cell size required for stable facility-level comparisons in our planned analyses.

A pre-specified sensitivity analysis using inverse-probability weights proportional to each facility’s active ART cohort size (N_i divided by n_i) was conducted to address the over-representation of smaller facilities in any pooled estimate of overall satisfaction. Weighted estimates were obtained within a stratified single-stage survey design with finite population correction, treating facility as the stratum. The unweighted estimate is reported as a sample-level descriptive statistic and as the basis for stratum-level comparisons; the weighted estimate is reported alongside it for population-level interpretation. The analysis was implemented using the survey package in R.

Recruitment and data collection

Participants were recruited using consecutive sampling HIV-positive peer educators identified potential participants in waiting areas of the selected facilities and referred interested individuals to trained research assistants, who screened for eligibility and provided detailed study information (objectives, procedures, risks, benefits, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw without penalty). Participants were approached before their appointments and invited to complete the survey after their consultation or clinic visit, to avoid disrupting clinical services. Face-to-face interviews were conducted in Tigrigna and electronic responses were uploaded in real time to an encrypted, cloud-based Qualtrics XM server.

Data collection was undertaken by 11 data collectors and 2 supervisors. Data collectors held qualifications ranging from BSc to MSc in nursing or public health and were experienced HIV care providers. To reduce social-desirability bias, each data collector was assigned to a study facility located in a sub-city different from the one in which they were employed, minimizing the likelihood that participants would recognize them or have prior clinical contact with them. All data collectors and supervisors completed a four-day pre-fieldwork training that covered the study protocol, the informed-consent procedure (including the criteria for documented oral consent; see below), use of the Qualtrics XM platform, interviewer techniques to minimise social-desirability bias, and identification and management of psychological distress. Supervisors performed daily review of a random subset of completed questionnaires for completeness and consistency, and provided on-site refresher training where required.

Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before any study procedure, with documented oral consent accepted in two specified circumstances: (i) when a participant had limited literacy and could not read or sign the written consent form; or (ii) when a participant declined to sign a written document because of privacy concerns related to HIV status. In these cases, the consent form was read aloud in Tigrigna, the participant verbally confirmed understanding and agreement, and the data collector documented the oral consent.

A distress-management protocol was implemented. Data collectors were trained to recognise signs of psychological distress, to pause or terminate the interview if distress was apparent, and to notify the facility’s HIV care providers for further support through the facility’s standard care pathways. No adverse events or distress episodes requiring escalation were recorded during the data collection period.

Measurement and variables

As no single validated instrument adequately captured the post-conflict care dimensions relevant to Tigray, the patient-satisfaction instrument was developed from tools previously used in HIV-care quality studies across sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia [27, 29, 30, 54–57]. A targeted literature review yielded 35 draft items. A two-phase expert consultation involving eight multidisciplinary specialists (HIV clinicians, public health experts, health-services researchers, and mental-health professionals) refined the items for relevance, clarity, contextual fit, and alignment with post-conflict healthcare challenges in Tigray. In the first phase, experts independently reviewed each item; in the second phase, a virtual consensus workshop finalised the item pool, reducing it to 21 items, Guided by SERVQUAL [31] and empirical SSA HIV-care satisfaction literature [27, 29, 30, 54–57], the 21 items were organised into the four first-order constructs (PCR, AAC, QCS, FSE), with overall satisfaction modeled as a second-order construct (see Table 1).

Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Extremely Dissatisfied to 5 = Extremely Satisfied), with an additional “Don’t know” option. Domain scores were the mean of items within each domain (range 1–5). The composite mean served as the primary outcome for logistic regression and was dichotomised as satisfied (> 3.69) versus Dissatisfied (≤ 3.69) domain scores served as latent constructs in the SEM. The questionnaire also collected sociodemographic, clinical, and facility-level data. The English instrument was translated into Tigrigna using a forward-backward procedure following WHO (2009) and EORTC (2017) guidelines, with discrepancies resolved by translator-researcher reconciliation, and pilot-tested with 55 ART clients (excluded from the final sample) for clarity and cultural appropriateness. Revisions included simplifying wording for items on waiting time and appointment frequency and recontextualising items on privacy and provider communication. PLHIV were not formally involved in initial item generation; this is acknowledged as a limitation. The English and Tigrigna versions are provided as Supplementary File S3.

Quality control procedures (Bias)

Several measures were implemented to ensure data quality. The instrument was forward- and back-translated; pilot-tested with 55 ART clients; and digitized in Qualtrics XM. The Qualtrics XM dashboard enabled real-time monitoring, and the principal investigator and supervisors reviewed daily submissions for completeness and consistency, addressing flagged issues promptly. Recruitment was distributed across weekdays and clinic hours to enhance representativeness and minimize selection bias. Interviewers provided clarification where required to accommodate varying literacy levels.

Data analysis

Data were cleaned in Microsoft Excel and analyzed in R version 4.4.3. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) used the weighted least squares mean and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator on polychoric correlations, with the NLMINB optimization algorithm and robust standard errors. Delta parameterisation was used (the lavaan default with WLSMV for single-group models): the residual variance of each latent response variable was fixed to 1 at all levels of any grouping variable, while factor loadings and thresholds were freely estimated, permitting consistent estimation of standardised loadings on ordinal indicators. Model fit was assessed against established criteria (WRMR ≤ 1.0, CFI and TLI > 0.90, RMSEA < 0.08, SRMR) [58, 59]. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α [60].

A second-order SEM (specified in lavaan) was then fitted to assess association between the four first-order constructs (PCC, AAC, QCS, FSE) and overall satisfaction, with sociodemographic and facility-level exogenous covariates regressed onto the second-order factor.

To identify determinants of dichotomised satisfaction (> 3.69 = satisfied) [61], binomial logistic regression was fitted within a generalised-linear-model (GLM) framework with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals categorical variables were dummy-coded and continuous variables standardised. Multicollinearity was within acceptable limits (GVIF^(1/2*Df) < 1.32) [62]. Model fit was assessed using AIC (671.21), residual deviance (635.21 on 503 df), and a non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test (χ² = 9.63, p = 0.292) [63]. A generalised linear mixed-effects model (GLMER) with random intercept facility was fitted as a robustness check. A pre-specified weighted sensitivity analysis using inverse-probability weights proportional to each facility’s active ART cohort size was conducted for clustering the overall satisfaction estimate (Supplementary File 5). The full R code (estimator, parameterisation, threshold handling, bootstrap settings, and exact model statements) is provided as Supplementary File S2.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of 631 eligible patients, 569 consented (response rate 90.17%). Fourteen records were excluded for incompleteness and 34 for non-engagement, defined a priori as a within-respondent standard deviation of < 0.25 across the 21 items.

Demographic characteristics revealed that the majority of participants were adults aged 30–49 years (67%), with a mean age of 42.4 ± 10.2 years (range: 18–86). Females accounted for 60.7% of the sample, and 53.6% were married. Educationally, 38.6% had completed primary school, while 30.3% reported self-employment as their primary occupation. Most participants (85.6%) resided in Mekelle City.

Regarding HIV treatment experience, the mean duration of antiretroviral therapy (ART) was 9.6 ± 4.9 years, with 42.0% having received ART for 5–10 years, 37.6% for > 10 years, and 20.4% for ≤ 5 years. The distribution of ART service levels was as follows: primary (59.1%), secondary (27.1%), and tertiary (13.8%) level facilities. Public healthcare facilities provided care to 79.5% of participants, while private facilities served the remaining 20.5%. Complete demographic and facility-level characteristics are provided in Supplementary File S1.

Validation of the measurement model

CFA demonstrated excellent fit for the four-factor measurement model, with all indices exceeding recommended thresholds: CFI = 0.996, TLI = 0.994, SRMR = 0.044, RMSEA = 0.082. All standardized factor loadings were statistically significant (p < 0.001) and substantively meaningful, ranging from 0.61 to 0.92. Internal consistency was acceptable across satisfaction domains (Cronbach’s α = 0.78–0.90) and strong overall (α = 0.89). Convergent validity was demonstrated by average variance extracted (AVE) values of 0.56–0.79); covariances between constructs ranged from 0.64 to 0.99) see Fig. 2 & Supplementary File S1 for complete results).

Path analysis of client satisfaction

The second-order SEM showed excellent model fit (CFI = 0.996, TLI = 0.997, RMSEA = 0.055 [90% CI: 0.047–0.063], SRMR = 0.044). All first-order factor loadings were statistically significant (p < 0.001), ranging from 0.601 to 0.919 across the four domains: PCR 0.855–0.919, AAC 0.601–0.884, QCS 0.862–0.884, FSE: 0.780–0.897. The second-order overall satisfaction construct showed strong standardised associations with each domain (PRC β = 0.920; AAC β = 0.963, 95% CI 0.930–1.088; QCS: β = 0.925, 95% CI 0.862–1.036; FSE β = 0.985, 95% CI 0.921–1.098; all p < 0.001), explaining 84.6–97.0% of the variance (R²=0.535–0.970). Patient satisfaction outcomes

Using the pre-specified mean cutoff of 3.69, 62.4% of participants reported overall satisfaction with HIV care clinic services. A pre-specified inverse-probability-weighted sensitivity analysis, using weights proportional to each facility’s active ART cohort size within a stratified single-stage survey design with finite population correction, produced a population-weighted satisfaction estimate of 60.1% (95% CI 55.4% to 64.8%), approximately 2% points lower than the unweighted estimate. Satisfaction varied by facility characteristics: primary-level facilities had a higher proportion of satisfied clients (66.2%) than tertiary/referral-level facilities (37.5%), and NGO/private facilities had a lower proportion (48.6%) than public facilities (66.7%). The highest proportions of satisfied clients were observed among younger participants (18 to 29 years: 68.9%), women (64.9%), married/never-married participants (63.4% to 64.6%), and those with higher education (college: 68.1%) or stable employment (self-employed: 69.0%). ART duration of 5 to 10 years was associated with the highest unadjusted satisfaction (64.8%).

Domain-level analyses showed PRC as the highest-rated domain (64.5% satisfied), AAC as the lowest (31.5% satisfied), QCS (57.2%) and facility FSE (57.4%) at intermediate levels (Supplementary File 4).

Multivariable logistic regression confirmed facility-level factors as the principal determinants: tertiary-level facilities had 50% lower odds of satisfaction (OR = 0.50, 95% CI:0.37–0.67, p < 0.001) and public facilities had 36% lower odds 36% lower odds (OR = 0.64, 95% CI 0.54–0.76, p < 0.001) than their reference categories. Diagnostics confirmed model robustness (Hosmer-Lemeshow p = 0.29; VIF < 1.32), with consistent findings across the GLMER and weighted sensitivity analyses (Supplementary File S1, Fig. 4).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study describes patient-reported satisfaction with HIV care clinic services in post-conflict Mekelle City, Tigray in late 2024. Internationally reported overall satisfaction with HIV care has ranged from approximately 50% to over 90% [20, 56, 64–66]. Within Ethiopia, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of HIV-care satisfaction studies reported a pooled estimate of 69.7% (95% CI 63.8–75.5%) [67]. The present Mekelle City estimate of 62.4% falls within the lower range of this pooled meta-analytic confidence interval, and the apparent difference may not be statistically or clinically meaningful given differences in study periods, settings, instruments, and dichotomisation cutoffs. The estimate is also below Ethiopia’s HSTP-II programmatic target of 80% [68]; this comparison is descriptive rather than evidence of crisis. Two pre-war studies from Tigray reported substantially higher satisfaction with ART services: 89.6% across Tigray Region ART health centres and 75.2% at Midre-Genet referral hospital in Northwest Tigray, both in 2013 [29, 69]. The 62.4% observed in post-war Mekelle therefore represents a marked decline relative to this pre-war baseline, consistent with the documented disruption of HIV care infrastructure during the 2020–2022 war. However, causal attribution to the conflict is not possible from this cross-sectional design, and the findings should be interpreted as a descriptive snapshot of the post-conflict care environment.

Among the four latent service-quality domains, AAC and FSE received the lowest ratings. Patients cited long wait times, difficulty contacting providers, repeated service interruptions, and unwelcoming staff behavior. The broader service-delivery context is described in published reports, which indicate that more than 80% of health facilities in Tigray were damaged or rendered non-functional during the conflict [8], with prolonged ART stockouts of up to nine months [11] and ART interruption among a substantial proportion of PLHIV [10]. The HeRAMS Tigray baseline report (2023) further documented persistent infrastructure damage, human-resource shortages, and stockouts of ART medications and diagnostics through mid-2023 [70]. Whether and how these structural factors translate into the patient-reported AAC and FSE deficits observed here cannot be determined from this cross-sectional design and would require longitudinal or comparative studies, which were beyond the scope of this analysis.

The lower odds of satisfaction at tertiary-level (OR = 0.50) and public (OR = 0.64) facilities is a notable finding. Three plausible mechanisms warrant consideration. First, referral hospitals in Tigray function as the regional centres for second-line and third-line ART, drug-resistance management, and complex treatment-failure cases requiring virological and immunological monitoring; this concentrates the most clinically demanding caseload at a single facility, compounding throughput pressures that reduce consultation time, limit provider responsiveness, and lengthen waiting times. Second, complex case presentations require additional investigations and inter-departmental referrals, increasing perceived service fragmentation. Third, post-conflict recovery of tertiary facilities may lag behind primary-level facilities in staffing normalisation and supply-chain restoration, consistent with evidence from other post-conflict health systems [15–17]. Standardised facility-level patient-load and case-complexity data were not consistently available across the 11 sites and could not be included as covariates; future studies should prospectively capture these. These findings challenge the assumption that higher-level facilities inherently deliver better-perceived care, and suggest that decentralisation, task-shifting, and targeted quality improvement at referral facilities warrant attention.

The strong, statistically significant standardised SEM associations between overall satisfaction and each of the four domains (PCR, AAC, QCS, FSE; all β > 0.92) indicate that overall satisfaction is closely tied to all four domains rather than to any single dimension. This is consistent with second-order reflective conceptualisations of multidimensional patient satisfaction in healthcare-services research38,62,63 [34, 35] and with previously published HIV-care SEMs64,65 [36, 37]. Targeted quality-improvement interventions are therefore unlikely to be effective if focused on a single domain; integrated improvement across all four domains is required.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. (1) on-site interviewing carries a residual risk of social-desirability bias despite mitigation through cross-facility deployment of experienced HIV care providers as data collectors, explicit confidentiality assurances, and a service-improvement (rather than provider-evaluation) framing; the 62.4% reported may modestly overestimate true satisfaction. (2) the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; two pre-war Tigray studies provide contextual benchmarks but used non-identical instruments and different facility samples, and no non-conflict control was included. (3) The lack of comparable HIV-care satisfaction studies in conflict-affected settings limits external benchmarking. (4) The focus on HIV care excludes broader health-system factors (staffing levels, resource availability) that may influence satisfaction. (5) PLHIV were not formally involved in initial item generation; the instrument may therefore have missed dimensions particularly salient to conflict-affected PLHIV, including experiences of displacement and return to care, continuity of treatment during supply interruptions, trauma-informed provider interactions, confidentiality concerns in communities with heightened scrutiny following mass displacement, and perceived cultural or gender responsiveness of services. Future studies should begin with formative qualitative work with conflict-affected PLHIV to identify and validate additional items. (6) Standardised facility-level patient-load data (daily patient-to-provider ratios) were not consistently documented across the 11 sites, precluding their inclusion as an independent covariate, even though patient load is a plausible confounder of the association between facility level and satisfaction. (7) For stable clients on extended (up to 6-monthly) appointment schedules, responses reflected their most recent completed visit, introducing potential recall bias relative to participants interviewed on the same day as their consultation. Despite these limitations, the study identifies actionable areas for quality improvement and provides a psychometrically validated Tigrigna-language instrument that can be adapted for similar settings.

Conclusion

In post-conflict Mekelle City, 62.4% of PLHIV on ART reported overall satisfaction with HIV care clinic services, below Ethiopia’s HSTP-II benchmark of 80%. Satisfaction was driven by all four service-quality domains, with availability and accessibility of care performing worst. Tertiary-level and public facilities were associated with lower odds of satisfaction, while sociodemographic and clinical variables were not. Targeted, integrated quality improvement is therefore warranted across all four service-quality domains, with particular focus on access and on tertiary-level and public facilities. Whether changes in patient satisfaction translate into ART interruption, virologic failure, or drug resistance was outside the scope of this cross-sectional analysis and should be examined in longitudinal research linking satisfaction data to treatment and laboratory outcomes. These findings are specific to Mekelle City, the urban capital of Tigray, and may not generalise directly to rural or remote conflict-affected areas where facility infrastructure, staffing, and ART service-recovery trajectories may differ.
