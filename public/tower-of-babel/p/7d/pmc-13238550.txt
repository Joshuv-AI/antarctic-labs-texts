An iEEG investigation on sex‐specific differences in seizure duration
Authors: Paula Albert I Gracenea, Alexandra Muss, Magdalena Seethaler, Jan Rémi, Elisabeth Kaufmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMale people with epilepsy (mPWE) are at higher risk for bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (BTCs) and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) compared to female people with epilepsy (fPWE). Despite major clinical and personal implications, little is known about sex‐specific differences in seizure propagation and termination. Characterization of seizure duration in men versus women with unifocal epilepsy.MethodsAdults with unifocal epilepsy and available intracranial EEG (iEEG) recordings (September 2006 to March 2022) were identified retrospectively. Up to 20 clinical seizures were analyzed per person, excluding individuals with status epilepticus or lateralized periodic discharges. Seizure duration was determined based on iEEG recordings. In focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (FBTCs), durations of the focal and BTC phases were assessed based on EEG and seizure semiology. Multiple linear regression was used to assess sex‐specific differences in seizure duration, adjusting for age, epileptic hemisphere, frontal lobe epilepsy (FLE), temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), lateralizing signs, onset during sleep, and focal seizures with preserved consciousness and aura phenomena (FPC‐a). A restricted‐cohort sensitivity analysis was performed, excluding PWE in whom clinical seizure onset preceded iEEG onset.ResultsA total of 100 PWE (m/f = 50/50; age: 33.6 ± 12.2 years) and 758 seizures (120 FBTCs) were analyzed. While fPWE had significantly longer focal seizures (FSs) (p = 0.015), mPWE revealed significantly longer FBTCs (p < 0.001), driven by a prolonged focal phase (p = 0.002). No significant difference was observed for the BTC phase. In the restricted cohort (n = 77 PWE), the prolongation of the focal phase during FBTCs in mPWE remained robust (p = 0.006).SignificanceOur findings demonstrate sex‐specific differences in seizure duration, suggesting that seizure propagation may differ between men and women. This may contribute to a better pathophysiological understanding of the sex‐specific differences in seizure manifestation and associated risks and underline the yet unmet need for a sex‐specific approach in epilepsy research and patient management.Plain Language SummarySex‐related differences in epilepsy are increasingly recognized but remain poorly understood. In this study, we compared seizure duration between women and men with unifocal epilepsy using intracranial brain recordings. We found that women had longer focal seizures, whereas men had longer focal phases in focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures. Further research is needed, as these differences may be relevant for clinical care and risk assessment in epilepsy.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is one of the most prevalent chronic neurologic diseases and is characterized by recurrent seizures. It affects men and women alike 1 ; however, knowledge regarding sex‐specific clinical manifestation and therapeutic aspects remains limited. This is surprising, considering the well‐established sex‐specific differences in brain morphology and neurochemistry, which are likely to influence seizure manifestation and termination. For example, men have on average 8%–13% larger brain volumes, 2 whereas women seem to have a higher percentage of gray matter including a thicker parietal cortex. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Further, functional brain lateralization is thought to be more pronounced in men, although convincing evidence is still lacking. 2 , 7 , 8 Beyond structural differences, men and women also differ in neurochemical profiles, including neurosteroid levels and neurotransmitter signaling involving serotonin, GABA, and dopamine. 9 , 10 , 11 How these biological differences affect seizure propagation and termination, particularly at the level of large‐scale epileptic networks, remains largely unknown.

Studying sex‐specific mechanisms of seizure propagation and termination not only allows us to deepen our functional neuroanatomical understanding but may also provide insights into sex‐specific disease associated risks like SUDEP. MPWE have a 1.4‐fold increased SUDEP risk compared to fPWE. 12 , 13 This difference has been attributed, at least in part, to the higher incidence of BTCs in men, 14 , 15 as BTCs are recognized as the main risk factor (OR 18.7 [7.6–45.8]) for SUDEP. 12 , 13 These observations suggest that sex‐specific fundamental differences may exist in seizure evolution.

Against this background, the present study aimed to systematically analyze sex‐specific differences in seizure manifestation in adults with unifocal epilepsy.

METHODS

Participants

PWE (≥16 years of age) were retrospectively identified from the database of the local epilepsy monitoring unit. Only PWE who underwent an intracranial EEG (iEEG) recording were considered, as iEEG recordings allowed for a more accurate determination of the seizure duration compared to surface EEG recordings. Further, only PWE with at least one clinically observable seizure and identifiable EEG seizure duration were included.

Focal seizures with preserved consciousness and aura phenomena were defined as seizures presenting exclusively with sensory phenomena, cognitive phenomena, autonomic phenomena, affective phenomena, or indescribable aura. For ease of readability, this seizure type is hereafter referred to as FPC‐a. PWE who experienced exclusively isolated FPC‐a were not enrolled. No PWE included in this study had either status epilepticus or continuing lateralized periodic discharges after the clinical end of the seizure, that is, scenarios with an undeterminable seizure end. Further, PWE whose clinical seizure onset preceded the EEG seizure pattern onset by at least 10 s were excluded, as this might indicate an electrode placement distant from the seizure focus and result in mislocalization of the seizure onset zone. In addition, a restricted‐cohort sensitivity analysis was performed for the subgroup of PWE in whom the EEG seizure pattern onset preceded the clinical onset, to ensure precise localization of the seizure onset and thus precise detection of the seizure duration.

Additional exclusion criteria comprised a multifocal or unknown seizure origin, missing consent, lack of relevant clinical information, or unavailable MRI or ictal EEG data.

ASM tapering was performed on an individualized basis upon the treating epileptologist's discretion, depending on the patient's clinical history and current epileptic activity. Rescue medication (lorazepam) was administered according to clinical protocol, specifically in case of two FBTCs in close succession, a BTC phase lasting ≥5 min, or in case of frequent recurrent FSs with clinical concern for impending status epilepticus.

The following demographic and clinical data were extracted from the patient files: sex, age at monitoring, age at disease onset, epilepsy classification, epileptic hemisphere, and handedness. Handedness was assessed using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory. 16 The individual epilepsy type was determined in an interdisciplinary patient management conference based on the EEG data, video recordings, as well as neuropsychological testing and available functional and structural imaging data.

The study was approved by the local institutional ethics committee and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All subjects gave written informed consent to the scientific use of their clinically acquired data.

Seizure semiology

The seizure semiology was determined based on the video recordings and classified according to the latest ILAE classification 17 as well as the semiological seizure classification. 18 Lateralizing signs like dystonia, sign of four, and version were noted, as well as FPC‐a, the time of the day, and the vigilance state at seizure onset. Seizure semiology during FPC‐a was assessed using a standardized testing protocol conducted by trained clinical staff, including an assessment of motor signs, speech, consciousness, as well as questions regarding alterations in sensory perception. All periictal semiological features were documented based on subject self‐report and behavioral observations on synchronized video recordings.

Seizure duration

The seizure duration was determined based on the iEEG recordings. The iEEG was acquired using subdural and/or stereotactically implanted electrodes (sEEG) from AD‐Tech Medical Instrument Corporation, Racine, WI, USA. The intracranial electrodes were individually placed, depending on the expected localization of the seizure onset zone and the seizure propagation pattern. The EEG signal was recorded using XLTEK NeuroWorks software and an XLTEK EMU128FS amplifier (Natus Medical Incorporated, San Carlos, CA, USA) with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz and 16 bit A‐D conversion.

The seizure duration was defined as the time difference (in seconds) between the EEG seizure pattern onset and end and confirmed by at least two experienced epileptologists. The end of the seizure pattern was thereby set at the end of the ictal rhythmic EEG activity. In case of an asynchronous seizure pattern termination, the seizure end was set after the latest detectable EEG seizure pattern. For FBTCs, not only the overall seizure duration but also the duration of the focal phase and the BTC phase were documented. The beginning of the BTC phase was determined based on the evolution of the EEG seizure pattern together with the seizure semiology. The “sign of four” and “version” were included in the BTC phase.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28). Mean and standard deviation were calculated for quantitative parameters. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to analyze continuous or ordinal data, while the chi‐square test was used to compare group differences in categorical data.

A multiple linear regression analysis was performed to examine the association between sex and seizure duration. Separate models were constructed for the following conditions: (a) all seizures, (b) FSs, (c) FSs without preceding FPC‐a, (d) FBTCs, and (e) the focal phase of FBTCs as well as (f) the BTC phase of FBTCs. Thereby, seizure duration was always used as the dependent variable, while age, epileptic hemisphere, frontal lobe epilepsy (FLE), temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), lateralizing signs, seizure onset during sleep, and preceding FPC‐a were included as independent variables.

Additionally, exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted for both TLE and FLE, using the same regression model with seizure duration as the dependent variable and the previously mentioned independent variables. In these subgroup models, epilepsy classification was omitted because it was constant within each subgroup.

A restricted‐cohort sensitivity analysis was performed using the same regression models and covariates as in the primary analysis. In this restricted cohort, PWE were excluded if the clinical seizure onset preceded iEEG seizure onset (i.e., also <10s), to ensure a precise detection of the seizure onset and thus precise determination of the seizure duration from iEEG seizure onset to iEEG seizure termination.

A p‐value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Bonferroni correction was applied to the six predefined primary comparisons in the main cohort (adjusted α = 0.05/6 = 0.0083). The restricted‐cohort analysis was conducted as a sensitivity analysis. Subgroup analyses were considered exploratory.

RESULTS

Study cohort

Between September 2006 and March 2022, 132 PWE undergoing iEEG monitoring were identified. After exclusion of PWE with isolated FPC‐a (n = 1), clinical seizure onset preceding EEG onset by ≥10 s (n = 15), multifocal or unknown seizure origin (n = 15), and missing consent or incomplete clinical, MRI, or ictal EEG data (n = 1), the final study cohort comprised 100 PWE. Seizure duration with regard to sex‐specific differences

The average duration of all seizures (n = 758), as well as FSs (n = 638) and FBTCs (n = 120), is given in Table 2. Male and female PWE did not differ in the total duration of their seizures (p = 0.144). However, fPWE revealed significantly—that is, on average 18.53 s—longer FSs than mPWE (p = 0.015). This analysis was repeated after exclusion of the FSs with preceding FPC‐a (n = 145), as FPC‐a were more prevalent in fPWE than mPWE (f:m = 17/50:11/50). Also, hereafter, fPWE revealed significantly longer durations of their FSs than mPWE (p = 0.041). On average, FSs without preceding FPC‐a lasted 13.82 s longer in fPWE than in mPWE. In contrast, mPWE had significantly longer FBTCs than fPWE (p < 0.001). FBTCs lasted on average 53.99 s longer than in the fPWE. This difference was mainly driven by the focal phase, which was on average 47.43 s longer than in fPWE (p = 0.002), as illustrated in Figure 2. No significant difference, though, was found for the BTC phase (p = 0.157).

In multiple linear regression analyses, increasing age was associated with longer seizure duration in the models for all seizures and FSs, but not for FBTCs. FLE was associated with shorter FS duration, whereas TLE was associated with longer FBTC duration. Seizure onset during sleep was associated with shorter FBTC duration but not with FS duration. Other prespecified covariates, including epileptic hemisphere and lateralizing signs, did not show significant associations with seizure duration.

Restricted‐cohort sensitivity analysis

To control for a potential bias in EEG‐based seizure duration estimates related to discrepancies between clinical and electrographic seizure onset, a restricted‐cohort sensitivity analysis was performed excluding all remaining PWE in whom clinical seizure onset preceded iEEG seizure onset. This yielded a restricted cohort of 77 PWE (fPWE/mPWE: 38/39). In total, 540 seizures, including 91 FBTCs, were available for analysis.

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the restricted cohort are summarized in Table S2. The restricted cohort remained well balanced with respect to age at monitoring, age at epilepsy onset, and disease duration. The distribution of epilepsy types was similar between sexes, with TLE being the most frequent diagnosis (fPWE/mPWE: 21/23), followed by FLE (fPWE/mPWE: 14/14) and POLE (fPWE/mPWE: 3/2); no insular epilepsies were present. The lateralization of the epileptic hemisphere was evenly distributed in fPWE (left/right: 19/19) and showed a predominance of left‐hemispheric epilepsies in mPWE (left/right: 27/12) without a significant sex difference (p = 0.09). The proportion of PWE with FPC‐a (fPWE/mPWE: 19/15), FBTCs (fPWE/mPWE: 22/16), and lesional etiology (fPWE/mPWE: 23/26) did not differ between sexes.

Using the same multiple linear regression approach and covariate set as in the main cohort, sex‐specific differences in seizure duration were reassessed in the restricted cohort. With respect to seizure duration, mPWE and fPWE did not differ in the total duration when considering all seizures (p = 0.983). No significant sex difference was observed for FSs overall (p = 0.204) or for FSs without preceding FPC‐a (p = 0.254). In contrast, mPWE continued to show significantly longer FBTCs than fPWE (p = 0.007). FBTCs lasted on average 55.06 s longer in mPWE, which was primarily driven by a prolonged focal phase (on average 53.72 s longer; p = 0.006), whereas no significant sex difference was found for the BTC phase (p = 0.726) (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis of seizure duration in temporal and frontal lobe epilepsy

Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed within TLE and FLE, comparing seizure duration between fPWE and mPWE within each epilepsy classification. In TLE, no consistent sex‐related pattern in seizure duration was observed across seizure types or phases. In FLE, several sex‐related differences were observed, including longer FS duration in fPWE and longer FBTC duration in mPWE. To evaluate whether lateralizing semiological features contributed to these findings in FLE, version and dystonia were examined and added to hierarchical regression models; neither differed by sex, and their inclusion did not attenuate the observed sex‐related associations. Overall, these subgroup findings are exploratory and should be interpreted with caution.

DISCUSSION

This cohort study systematically analyzed the sex‐specific differences in seizure duration in adults with focal epilepsies, based on intracranial EEG recordings. Of note, significantly shorter FSs as well as significantly longer focal phases during FBTCs were found in men compared to women. In other words, men seem to have stronger inhibitory mechanisms enabling them (1) to terminate FSs quicker and (2) to delay the BTCs in case of FBTCs. The latter proved to be robust also in a restricted‐cohort sensitivity analysis controlling for discrepancies between clinical and electrographic seizure onset.

Sex‐specific differences in seizure propagation

Seizure duration reflects the interplay between epileptic activity and mechanisms limiting its spread and eventual termination. 19 , 20 In focal epilepsies, seizures originate within circumscribed brain regions, from where epileptic activity may evolve to neighboring areas and, in some cases, propagate to distributed cortical and subcortical networks, including thalamic structures. 21

However, studies investigating sex‐specific differences in seizure manifestation and propagation are yet rare. 15 , 22 Women were, for example, described as being more likely to experience isolated FPC‐a and to reveal lateralized EEG seizure patterns in case of mesial temporal lobe epilepsy. 15 Further, women—but not men—have significantly different seizure durations in right versus left‐hemispheric epilepsies. 22 It was hypothesized that these sex‐specific differences are due to differences in brain connectivity, with a stronger intrahemispheric versus interhemispheric connectivity in men versus women. 23 Further, mPWE might have a stronger ictal activation of the basal ganglia which is known to be associated with a stronger inhibition of focal seizure activity. 24 These differences might contribute to the stronger ability of mPWE to inhibit focal epileptic activity, leading to the observed shorter FSs and longer latencies before BTCs in mPWE than in fPWE. This effect was predominantly driven by PWE with FLE and may reflect sex‐related differences in the seizure propagation pathways and termination mechanisms. The rate of BTCs, though, did not differ between mPWE and fPWE in our study.

Pathophysiological considerations on sex‐specific differences in seizure termination mechanisms

Seizure termination is thought to result from the combined effects of multiple processes that progressively reduce neuronal excitability during ongoing ictal activity. Among the best characterized mechanisms, ictal acidosis has been shown to contribute to seizure termination by inhibiting excitatory NMDA receptor function and enhancing GABA‐mediated inhibition. 25 In addition, acid‐sensing ion channels, particularly ASIC1a, are activated under acidic conditions and are predominantly expressed on inhibitory interneurons, thereby facilitating inhibitory recruitment and seizure cessation. 25

Another endogenous mechanism implicated in seizure termination is adenosine signaling. Increased extracellular adenosine concentrations have been shown to correlate with seizure termination in human intracranial recordings, supporting its role as an intrinsic inhibitory brake during ictal activity. 26 Together, these findings suggest that termination of established ictal activity relies on cellular constraints that emerge during sustained network engagement.

In addition to cellular mechanisms, seizure termination can also be considered at the network level. Once widespread cortical and subcortical networks are engaged, termination may reflect a global network state that constrains ongoing ictal activity rather than continued focal propagation. In this context, thalamo‐cortical synchrony has been proposed to characterize network states associated with the stabilization and eventual cessation of seizures, highlighting the role of large‐scale network interactions in seizure termination. 27 Whether sex‐specific differences in large‐scale network organization influence such termination‐related network states remains unknown.

Neurosteroids are known to affect both synaptic and extrasynaptic GABAA receptors and can influence inhibitory tone at the synaptic level. 28 Sex‐based differences in endocrine regulation and neurosteroid signaling are well documented 29 , 30 ; however, their specific contribution to seizure termination remains poorly understood and has not been systematically investigated. 31 Accordingly, such factors may modulate established termination pathways without currently allowing conclusions regarding their precise role in ictal cessation.

In the present study, sex‐specific differences in seizure duration predominantly affected the focal phase preceding FBTCs, whereas the duration of the BTC phase itself did not differ between sexes. This pattern suggests that sex‐related modulation primarily influences seizure propagation rather than seizure termination mechanisms.

Taken together, we observed that sex‐specific differences in seizure duration exist. Our findings indicate that sex‐specific differences primarily affect seizure propagation rather than seizure termination.

LIMITATIONS

The study's retrospective design may have led to an increased error rate in the data collection. Moreover, individuals undergoing iEEG recording constitute a highly specific subset of drug‐resistant epilepsy patients. While they are representative of the epilepsy population evaluated in tertiary epilepsy centers, this preselection restricts the generalizability of our findings to a wider patient cohort, as drug‐responsive patients and those with multifocal or generalized epilepsy syndromes have not been included in our investigation. Furthermore, hormone levels and menstrual cycles were not examined and should be included in future studies to enhance our comprehension of sex‐specific differences in seizure duration.

In addition, ASM serum levels and tapering strategies were not systematically recorded. Although the impact of ASM withdrawal on seizure duration remains uncertain, the study by Hartl et al.—conducted in a population closely comparable to ours undergoing iEEG monitoring—found no significant effect of withdrawal on seizure duration. 32 Other work has suggested that lower serum levels of some ASMs may be associated with longer seizures. 33 While these findings indicate that a major influence on seizure duration is unlikely, we cannot fully exclude that ASM changes during monitoring may have affected the results.

Finally, the application of a conservative restricted‐cohort sensitivity analysis excluding patients with a temporal discrepancy between clinical and electrographic seizure onset reduced the overall sample size and the number of analyzable FSs. While this approach strengthened confidence in the robustness of the FBTC‐related findings, it may have limited the detection of sex‐specific differences in FS duration in the restricted cohort.

CONCLUSION

The observed sex‐specific differences in seizure duration suggest stronger ictal inhibitory mechanisms in men, leading to a more efficient prevention of seizure spread. The study findings shed light on neurophysiological distinctions between mPWE and fPWE and highlight the need for a sex‐specific approach in epilepsy research and patient management. Prospective studies should be conducted to better understand sex‐specific differences and the underlying mechanisms, including structural and hormonal disparities.
