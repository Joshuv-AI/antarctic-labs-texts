Mitral Valve Blood Cyst Discovered as an Incidentaloma in Trauma Evaluation
Authors: Fox Bravo, Jacob Ref, Nanda Pullela, Tushar Acharya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital blood cysts of the cardiac valves are rare incidental findings most commonly affecting the mitral valve and can be diagnostically challenging when discovered during evaluation of concurrent cardiac pathology or trauma.Case SummaryA 31-year-old woman presented as a level 1 trauma activation after sustaining multiple stab wounds to the left chest. Computed tomography identified pneumopericardium and a left ventricular hypodensity concerning for thrombus or laceration. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mitral valve mass. Transesophageal echocardiography further characterized it as a 2.0 × 1.5 cm cystic structure attached to the ventricular aspect of A2/A3 scallops of the mitral leaflet. Cardiac magnetic resonance confirmed a water-based cystic lesion with no perfusion or enhancement, establishing the diagnosis of a congenital blood cyst.DiscussionThis case illustrates the critical role of multimodal imaging in differentiating congenital blood cysts from traumatic cardiac lesions.Take-Home MessagesMultimodal imaging is essential to distinguish congenital blood cysts from traumatic cardiac injury, preventing unnecessary surgical intervention. Conservative management with surveillance imaging is appropriate for asymptomatic blood cysts without significant valvular dysfunction.

History of Presentation

A young woman presented to our level 1 trauma center as an interhospital transfer following an assault in which she sustained multiple stab wounds to the left chest, left shoulder, and left upper extremity. On arrival, the patient was alert and oriented. Primary survey revealed a stable airway, audible bilateral breath sounds, and palpable pulses in all extremities, with a blood pressure of 104/68 mm Hg and heart rate of 86 beats/min. Secondary survey identified multiple penetrating wounds to the left chest and a deformed left arm. A left-sided chest tube placed at the transferring facility became dislodged during transport and was promptly replaced in the trauma bay.Take-Home Messages•In trauma patients with concurrent cardiac masses, multimodal imaging including TEE and CMR is essential to distinguish congenital blood cysts from traumatic cardiac lesions, enabling accurate diagnosis and avoiding unnecessary surgical intervention.•Conservative management with echocardiographic surveillance is appropriate for carefully selected asymptomatic patients without obstruction, embolic events, or significant valvular dysfunction.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history was not contributory. No prior cardiac diagnoses, structural heart disease, or cardiac procedures were reported.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the clinical context of penetrating chest trauma with a concurrent mitral valve mass, the differential diagnosis included: 1) traumatic myocardial laceration with associated thrombus; 2) preexisting left ventricular thrombus; 3) congenital blood cyst of the mitral valve; 4) myxoma; 5) papillary fibroelastoma; and 6) infective endocarditis vegetation.

Investigations

For further anatomic characterization, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was performed and revealed an ovoid cystic mass with central echolucency measuring 2.0 × 1.5 cm, attached to ventricular aspect of A2 and A3 cusps of the anterior mitral leaflet (Figure 3, Videos 1 to 3). The mass did not impede mitral leaflet mobility with trace physiologic mitral regurgitation (MR) (Video 4). All other cardiac valves were structurally and functionally normal. The mass was well circumscribed with smooth thin walls and central echolucency, and lacked independent chaotic mobility; features that distinguished it from a vegetation or thrombus. Blood cultures were also negative. Location of the mass on the ventricular aspect of the mitral leaflet favored papillary fibroelastoma over myxoma, however, its cystic, well-circumscribed appearance made it more likely to be a blood cyst rather than a fibroelastoma, which is typically solid, pedunculated, and frond-like. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) was performed on hospital day 5 for tissue characterization. The mass measured 2.2 × 1.9 cm and was again noted to be attached to the anterior mitral leaflet (Figure 4, Video 5). It was T1 hyperintense and had a mixed T2 signal intensity (hyperintense interspersed with hypointensity), consistent with a water-based structure. Further, there was no loss of signal on fat-suppressed images, suggesting absence of lipomatous content. It did not perfuse on dynamic perfusion imaging with gadolinium contrast. On late gadolinium enhancement imaging, it exhibited high-signal on the magnitude (MAG) images and a hypointense signal on phase-sensitive inversion recovery (PSIR), in keeping with fluid content.Figure 4Cardiac Magnetic ResonanceCMR steady-state free precession short-axis views at the mitral valve level in (A) diastole and (B) systole showing the mass (red arrows) attached to the anterior mitral leaflet. Overall, the findings of T1 and T2 hyperintense signal, absent perfusion, and complete absence of enhancement on PSIR were pathognomonic for a cystic lesion containing proteinaceous material and/or blood products, strongly consistent with the diagnosis of a congenital blood cyst. Because histological confirmation was not available, the diagnosis was made on convergent multimodal imaging features rather than pathology. In particular, the cystic morphology on TEE, absence of perfusion enhancement on CMR and hypointense signal on PSIR, lack of valvular dysfunction, and unchanged CT appearance at 4 years supported conservative management in this asymptomatic patient.

Management

The patient was admitted to the surgical intensive care unit for hemodynamic monitoring. She remained hemodynamically stable and did not require blood transfusions. The orthopedic surgery team recommended nonoperative management of the left upper extremity injury. Given the incidental nature of the blood cyst, no obstruction, absence of mechanical complications, lack of significant MR, and preserved ventricular function, conservative management was recommended. The patient was counseled on the benign natural history of blood cysts and the importance of surveillance imaging.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was lost to follow-up after discharge. Four years later, she presented to the emergency department for an unrelated issue of right-sided costovertebral angle tenderness. CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis demonstrated a stable hypoattenuating mass-like structure at the level of the mitral valve (Figure 5), with no interval growth or change in CT imaging characteristics over 4 years. Discussion

This case presents a unique diagnostic challenge: distinguishing a congenital blood cyst from acute traumatic cardiac pathology. Blood cysts of the cardiac valves have a reported autopsy prevalence of 0.4% to 1.5%, though clinical detection is less common.1,2 Most are asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally during echocardiography for unrelated indications.3 Blood cyst pathogenesis involves ventricular endothelial infoldings forming endothelium-lined channels containing blood or serous fluid.1,2 Most regress during infancy, although some may persist into adulthood. The mitral valve is most commonly affected (60%-80% of cases), with predilection for the anterior leaflet.2, 3, 4 Reported complications include valve dysfunction, systemic embolization, and rarely, left ventricular outflow tract obstruction.5,6

In this case, multimodal imaging proved essential in establishing the diagnosis and preventing unnecessary surgical intervention. CT initially identified the left ventricular hypodensity, prompting echocardiographic evaluation. TTE, despite poor acoustic windows from subcutaneous emphysema, and with the help of echo-contrast, was able to confirm the presence of a mitral valvular mass. TEE provided critical anatomic detail. Morphologic features including circumscription, smooth thin walls, central echolucency, and movement with leaflet motion helped distinguish the blood cyst from papillary fibroelastoma (solid, frond-like, independent motion), vegetation (irregular borders, other signs of infection), and thrombus (noncystic, not typically on valve leaflets).4,5 Intact anterior cardiac structures further argued against traumatic etiology.

CMR provided definitive tissue characterization. T1 hyperintensity, T2 mixed signal, absent first-pass perfusion, and complete absence of gadolinium enhancement confirmed the diagnosis.7 This profile differentiated the cyst from thrombus (typically isointense or hypointense on T1 and hypointense on T2).7,8 Advanced CMR parametric mapping may offer additional diagnostic refinement for equivocal cases.9 T2 imaging to detect iron content within the blood cyst was nondiagnostic in this case, presumably because of small size and high mobility with anterior mitral leaflet motion causing signal averaging with the blood pool.

The rarity of cardiac blood cysts precludes consensus guidelines for the management of asymptomatic cases. The evidence base consists almost entirely of case reports and small case series. Management should therefore be individualized, guided by the presence of symptoms, valvular dysfunction, and associated complications.3 For left-sided cysts, the literature leans more toward an active approach with surgical removal given the availability of minimally invasive surgery options with low risk.2 For right-sided cysts, the approach is generally more conservative; in one reported case of tricuspid valve cyst, the lack of echocardiographic evidence of tricuspid valvular dysfunction suggested a benign course and therefore the patient was monitored with echocardiography.2,10

Although surgical removal remains the predominant recommendation for mitral valve blood cysts, our case illustrates that conservative management may be appropriate in select patients. For carefully selected asymptomatic patients without significant valvular dysfunction, hemodynamic compromise, or obstruction, a strategy of close surveillance is a defensible alternative. In this patient specifically, 4 years of CT-confirmed stability without interval growth, new valvular dysfunction, or embolic events provides reassurance and supports continuation of a nonoperative strategy. Based on the available case-level evidence,3,5 we would recommend annual TTE as the preferred surveillance modality given its accessibility, lack of radiation, and ability to assess both cyst morphology and mitral valve function in real time. CT, although useful for incidental reassessment as demonstrated in our case, is not recommended as a primary surveillance tool given radiation and limited use in soft tissue characterization. Repeat CMR should be saved for cases in which echocardiographic windows are inadequate, or new features raise prompt concerns for alternative diagnosis.8 Criteria that should prompt multidisciplinary surgical reassessment include new or progressive MR, cyst enlargement, evidence of systemic embolization, or the onset of symptoms attributable to the lesion. Absence of these features, as seen in our patient, supports a conservative and surveillance-based approach as a safe long-term approach for asymptomatic individuals.

Conclusions

We present an incidentally discovered congenital blood cyst of the anterior mitral valve in a young woman with penetrating chest trauma. Sequential CT, TTE, TEE, and CMR were essential to differentiate this congenital anomaly from traumatic cardiac injury, confirm the diagnosis, and guide conservative management. Stability was confirmed 4 years later on incidental surveillance imaging. This case underscores the importance of systematic evaluation of incidental cardiac findings in trauma patients and the complementary roles of echocardiography and CMR in the characterization of cardiac masses.
