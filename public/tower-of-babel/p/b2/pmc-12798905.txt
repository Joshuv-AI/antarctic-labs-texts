Intra-Petrous Carotid Artery Dissection and Calcific Emboli from a “Petrous” Aortic Valve: A Case Report
Authors: Michele G Croce, Elisa Coloberti, Isabella Canavero, Natascia Ghiotto, Carlo Asteggiano, Valentina Mantovani, Carlo De Vincentiis, Elvis Lafe, Sabrina Ravaglia
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Calcific aortic stenosis (CAS)—the most common valvular disorder—is not formally listed among stroke risk factors, and routine antithrombotic prophylaxis is, therefore, not recommended.[1]

We describe here a 38 year old man with a congenital bicuspid CAS, who developed right intracranial carotid artery dissection causing ischemic stroke, along with multiple calcific brain emboli that increased in number over 10 months.

The patient presented to the emergency room (ER) with transient loss of consciousness, headache, and mild left hemiparesis. Imaging revealed approximately four punctate intracerebral calcific emboli and a dissection-related critical stenosis of the intra-petrous internal carotid artery (ICA), confirmed by angiography [Figure 1], resulting in a middle cerebral artery (MCA) infarct [Supplementary Figures 1 and 2]. Thoracic computerized tomography (CT), performed to exclude pulmonary embolism, revealed severe calcification of a bicuspid aortic valve [Supplementary Figure 3], while echocardiography showed moderate stenosis (peak/mean gradient 40/30 mmHg). Cardiac monitoring for 72 h revealed no atrial fibrillation. The coexistence of arterial dissection and aortic valve disease prompted us to screen for genetic vessel disorders, with negative results.

Cerebral angiography of the right internal carotid artery (ICA). Oblique (a), anteroposterior (b), and lateral (c) views show severe irregular stenosis of the intra-petrous ICA due to arterial dissection, with delayed yet maintained distal flow. The intracranial ICA is S-shaped, with multiple tortuosities and acute angles. The dissection starts in the first posterior bend (A1) of the intra-petrous tract, which, in our patient, looks more obtuse (or more open)—an anatomy that may have increased the linear flow velocity of emboli, potentially raising the mechanical impact of emboli on the intima. ICA: internal carotid artery

The patient was started on aspirin 100 mg. Follow-up transcranial Doppler (TCD) continued to detect microembolic signals [Supplementary Figure 4], even outside the right MCA/ICA territories, and a follow-up CT 10 months later showed that the cerebral calcific emboli had tripled (≈15) [Supplementary Videos 1, 2], even though the carotid lesion had fully recanalized [Supplementary Figure 5]. After excluding other potential embolic sources, and suspecting the valve itself as the ongoing source, surgeons replaced the valve, which was found to be completely calcified [Figure 2]. No further embolic lesions were detected on follow-up brain CT after surgery.

In this patient, the progression of calcified brain emboli before surgery, as well as the direct evidence of persistent micro-emboli on follow-up TCD, suggests CAS as an active embolic source, although none of these emboli produced ischemic symptoms, whereas the index stroke was attributable to carotid dissection. This case highlights two rarely appreciated associations between CAS—especially bicuspid CAS—and cerebrovascular disease: (1) calcific emboli and (2) intracranial arterial dissection.

(1) Although autopsy series have demonstrated calcium microemboli in multiple vascular beds—including cerebral vessels—in CAS, symptomatic stroke is rare; accordingly, current guidelines do not recommend primary antithrombotic prophylaxis for CAS. Supplementary Table 1 reviews reported cases of stroke associated with CAS: they are very uncommon, particularly when excluding elderly patients with tricuspid valves, in whom additional vascular risks factors (e.g. atherosclerosis) are often present. Proposed mechanism of stroke in CAS include: direct embolization consistent with calcium deposits (five reported cases); noncalcific thrombus formation on the valve surface; hemodynamic turbulence leading to platelet activation and microemboli; atrial fibrillation due to left atrial enlargement; and periprocedural embolization during valve replacement. In most cases, additional risk factors co-occur; patients under 40 all show bicuspid aortic valve.

In all reported cases, brain CT/ magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) could detect a single or at most two [11] intravascular calcifications.

We therefore suggest that even “moderate” CAS in young adults may warrant closer neurological surveillance and earlier surgical consideration when calcific emboli accumulate.

(2) We hypothesize that calcific emboli could represent a plausible mechanical trigger for intracranial carotid artery dissection. Intracranial carotid artery dissections are rare; particularly within the rigid intrapetrous segment[2] and typically occur in older patients or in association with trauma or connectivetissue disorders, none of which applied here. We therefore propose that a calcium embolus originating from the valve may have struck the intima just distal to the first intrapetrous bend, where flow velocity remains high and thus exerts relatively strong mechanical force.[3] Because intracranial arteries lack external elastic laminae and have a thinner, less elastic media, the initial intimal tear produced by such an impact could propagate subintimally, generating dissection. A relatively obtuse carotid bend in this patient may have further increased linear flow velocity and wall stress [Figure 1]; some authors suggests that ICA tortuosity influences both hemodynamics and vessel wall resistance.[4]

With regard to treatment, there is no consensus on the optimal antithrombotic strategy for intracranial dissections. In our patient, aspirin prevented further ischaemic lesions but did not halt ongoing calcific embolization, which ceased only after valve replacement.

We are not aware of previous reports linking intracranial ICA dissection with CAS-related calcific emboli. However, because calcified emboli are often overlooked and their clinical relevance remains debated,[5] such associations may be underreported. Our case may provide reference data for an otherwise unsuspected adult population and supports further exploration of how calcific particles interact with vessels geometry and hemodynamics. We conclude that CAS may be may an under-recognized contributor to both intracranial calcific emboli and arterial dissection, and that acknowledging this link could prompt earlier cardiac evaluation in patients with unexplained cerebral calcific emboli or intracranial carotid dissection.

Review of published cases of calcific aortic stenosis (CAS) and embolic stroke

We included English/French/Spanish publications with the keywords: aortic valve, calcific brain emboli, stroke. We annotated patient age and sex; type of stroke (large vs small vessels; single vs multiple; calcific vs non-calcific embolization); evidence for a cardiac source (known or newly discovered at the time of the stroke; type of valve disease and severity of stenosis); presence of additional cerebrovascular risk factors; treatment given. The presumed associated stroke is usually spontaneous; stroke may be the first clinical manifestation of a previously unknown CAS references (13812); in most cases, calcific embolization may be directly demonstrated. Some patients references (6, 7, 9, 10) are elderly with tricuspid aortic valve; patients under 40 all show bicuspid aortic valve, with or without stenosis. In all cases, one single or a maximum of two (10) intravascular calcifications are described on imaging; no documentation of progression of the embolic load in follow-up imaging has been demonstrated in the available literature.
