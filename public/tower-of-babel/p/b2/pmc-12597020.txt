Understanding the characteristic behaviour of the wild-type and mutant structure of FLT3 protein by computational methods
Authors: Saleena Younus, Özge Tatli, Ahmad Nasimian, Julhash U. Kazi, Lars Rönnstrand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
FLT3 is a critical prognostic marker in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) due to its high frequency of mutation. Primary mutations in FLT3 include point mutations or deletions in the tyrosine kinase domain (TKD) and internal tandem duplications (ITD) in the juxtamembrane (JM) region. These mutations lead to ligand-independent activation, disrupting the JM region and kinase domain interaction, resulting in autophosphorylation of FLT3 and activation of signalling pathways such as STAT5, MAPK, and AKT, which promotes increased proliferation and resistance to apoptosis. So developing effective therapeutics against these mutant FLT3 proteins is crucial for AML treatment. Here we employed computational approaches, including Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulation, cluster analysis, and Normal Mode Analysis, to investigate the dynamic behaviours of FLT3 mutant structures (TKD mutants Y842C/F in FLT3-WT, FLT3- ITD, and ITD mutant in FLT3-WT). The molecular dynamics (MD) simulation studies revealed that all these mutations significantly affect the optimized state, flexibility, and compactness of the FLT3 protein. Cluster analysis also confirmed that these mutations significantly impacted protein flexibility. Specifically, the ITD mutation in the JM region increased the overall flexibility of the FLT3 structure, whereas the point mutations in the TKD domain (TKD; Y842C and Y842F) and the combined mutations of ITD and TKD in the FLT3 protein (ITD + Y842C/F) resulted in more rigid conformations. These findings highlight the distinct conformational effects of FLT3 mutations that contribute to kinase deregulation and underscore their potential as therapeutic targets in AML.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous hematological malignancy originating from hematopoietic stem cells in the blood and bone marrow [1], [2]. The prevalence of AML typically varies between the pediatric and adult cases based on age. In pediatric leukemia, AML accounts for approximately 15–20 % of cases, while in adults, it represents a higher proportion, accounting for over 33 % of all leukemia cases [3]. Key etiological factors for the development of AML include genetic mutations, prior chemotherapy, and exposure to chemicals or radiation. Multiple gene mutations are associated with the development of AML [4]. However, mutations in genes such as FLT3, NPM1, CEBPA, RUNX1, ASXL3, and TP53 are considered key drivers of the disease [5]. Among these, FLT3, NPM1, and CEBPA have been thoroughly investigated for their roles in the treatment and disease progression of AML [6].

Mutation rates among these genes vary [5], and FLT3 is recognized as one of the most frequently mutated, correlating with a poor prognosis [5]. The mutation rate of FLT3 is approximately 30 % in AML cases [5], [7]. Two main types of activating mutations are frequently found in FLT3: point mutations or deletions in the tyrosine kinase domain (FLT3-TKD), which occur in about 7–10 % of AML cases, and internal tandem duplication (ITD) mutations in the juxtamembrane (JM) region, which account for 20–30 % of all AML cases [8], [9]. FLT3-ITD is an in-frame duplication of the nucleotides, typically within exons 14 and 15 of the JM domain, which can vary in size from 3 up to ∼400 base pair [10]. FLT3-ITD mutations are associated with a more aggressive disease phenotype and a high risk of relapse [11]. These mutations induce ligand-independent activation by disrupting the autoinhibitory interaction between the JM region and the kinase domain [12]; this triggers autophosphorylation of FLT3, thereby activating multiple downstream intracellular signalling pathways, such as STAT5, MAPK, and AKT. Ultimately, these cascades promote cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis [13], [14]. Additionally, secondary TKD mutations may appear in the activation loop of FLT3-ITD proteins during treatment with tyrosine kinase inhibitors. The emergence of these secondary mutations often results in the development of therapy resistance in AML patients [15], [16], [17].

Developing more effective treatments targeting these mutants is therefore of the utmost importance. This requires a detailed understanding of the characteristic behaviours of wild-type (WT) and mutated FLT3 proteins. Structure-based drug design (SBDD) is a vital component of target-based drug discovery, now widely employed by the pharmaceutical companies and researchers. SBDD encompasses a collection of computational techniques, including structure-based virtual screening of the molecules (SBVS), molecular docking, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. These computational techniques reduce the time and cost of drug discovery and contribute to identification of more specific and effective drugs [18], [19]. Within SBDD, MD simulation is a particularly powerful technique for elucidating the characteristic behaviour of the protein in a biological system. This approach provides molecular-level insights into protein behaviour in both normal and diseased states, expanding our understanding of disease pathways and furthering the drug discovery process. Modern SBDD approaches now often incorporate new computational techniques, including machine learning, alongside MD simulation to gain further insight into protein dynamics [20]. Understanding protein dynamics is crucial, as it directly correlates with conformational stability and flexibility, which in turn influence the function of the proteins and the cellular phenotype [21], [22].

In this study, our objective is to understand how the TKD point mutations of phosphorylation site (Y842C/F), the internal tandem duplication (ITD) of seven amino acids in the JM region, and the combined mutations of ITD and TKD (Y842C/F) affect the characteristic behaviour of the FLT3 protein. To address this, we performed comparative computational analyses on FLT3-WT and its mutant structures, including (i) Point mutations of the tyrosine residue Tyr842 (Y842-phosphorylation site) in the kinase activation loop to cysteine (Cys) and phenylalanine (Phe) residues (Y842C/F) (ii) The internal tandem duplication (ITD) mutation (FLT3-ITD) in JM region (iii) A combination mutation of ITD with the Y842C/F mutations in JM and TKD’s.

Point mutations at the phosphorylation site Y842C/F in the TKD of FLT3-WT and FLT3-ITD proteins have been previously reported [16], [17], [23]. Previous studies indicate that the Y842C mutation is rare in AML, but leads to constitutive phosphorylation of FLT3 and tyrosine phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT-5) [23]. In contrast, the Y842F mutation in FLT3-ITD has been shown to impair the activity of the RAS/ERK pathway by reducing the interaction between FLT3 and SHP2, thereby diminishing SHP2 activation [16], [17]. However, the structural and dynamic consequences of these mutations remain poorly characterized. To address this gap, we employed a range of computational techniques in this study, including sequence analysis, protein modelling, MD simulations, and normal mode analysis (NMA). Through these investigations, we aim to provide a deeper understanding of the distinct structural impacts induced by various FLT3 mutations. The structural information presented herein will inform and accelerate the development of more effective, broadly applicable therapeutic strategies targeting these mutations in the treatment of AML.

Materials and methods

For this study, we utilized the primary online resources were NCBI, NCBI-CDD, PDB, AlphaFold database, UniProt, VectorBuilder, and the SWISS-MODEL server [23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28]. Here the GROMACS software was employed for MD simulation studies of the protein structure [29]. The mutation of the structure was done with Pymol software, and visualization of the protein was done using Discovery Studio software and Pymol [30], [31]. The generation of images for RMSD, RMSF, Rg, H-Bond, and SASA were accomplished using Qtgrace (https://sourceforge.net/projects/qtgrace/) software. Cluster analysis of the structures, along with their visualization, was conducted through Rstudio software [32], [33]. The dynamic motion of both wild-type (WT) and mutated structures was generated using Dynamut2 software [34].

Sequence comparison and protein modelling

For this study, we used the FLT3-ITD sequence data derived from an AML patient, as previously described (W51 mutation [35]). This sequence has a 7 amino acids long duplication in the JM region. Based on this FLT3-ITD sequence, we performed multiple sequence comparisons of wild-type (WT) and FLT3-ITD protein sequences using a tool from VectorBuilder website (https://en.vectorbuilder.com/tool/sequence-alignment.html). This tool shows 99.30 % sequence identity of the FLT3-ITD sequence with FLT3-WT protein sequence and highlights the duplicated sequence regions of the FLT3-ITD protein. The details of the protein sequence alignment of FLT3-WT and FLT3-ITD are shown in the SupplementaryFile_S1. From the protein sequence alignment of both WT and FLT3-ITD, we could identify that there is a duplication of seven amino acid residues “REYEYDL” occurring from 595 to 601 in the JM region of the FLT3-ITD sequence. Using this FLT3-ITD sequence, we modelled the protein structure of FLT3-ITD by homology modelling techniques using the SWISS–model server. Nowadays, several computational modelling software are available for the homology modelling of proteins. However, due to the easiness of handling software and considering the reliability of the modelled structures from the SWISS-model server, we used the SWISS-model server for modelling the FLT3-ITD structure.

In SWISS- Model server, the Template search was performed using the FLT3-ITD sequence, and the final model was selected from five template structures based on sequence identity, GMQE, and coverage. After running the build model, we selected the best model, which was based on the SWISS-Model template structure of FLT3 protein P36888 (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/search/text/P36888) from the AlphaFold database. In the homology modelling server, the quality of the resulting modelled structure was evaluated by GMQE, QMEAN, sequence identity, coverage, Molprobity, and Ramachandran plot (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/docs/help#model_evaluation). The GMQE (Global Model Quality Estimation) indicates the expected quality of the protein model at the tertiary and quaternary structural levels, with values typically ranging between 0 and 1, where higher values signify better quality [51]. The highest GMQE value among the generated structures was considered the most reliable. The QMEAN score provides both global (entire structure) and local (per-residue) quality estimates of the protein model, with values ranging from 0 (high quality) to –4.0 (low quality). However, in this study, the selected structure did not include a QMEAN score because it was based on an AlphaFold template. Instead, the AlphaFold method reports a predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) score, which assesses per-residue confidence on a scale of 0–100. A pLDDT > 90 indicates the highest accuracy, with reliable backbone and side-chain predictions. A pLDDT > 70 suggests correct backbone prediction but potential side-chain inaccuracies, while pLDDT > 50 and < 50 indicate low and very low confidence, respectively [52]. We calculated the average pLDDT score of the modelled FLT3-ITD structure from its B-factors using the R programming language. The in-house code developed for this analysis is available on GitHub (https://github.com/SaleenaYounus/pLDDT_Modelled_Protein/blob/main/pLDDT_Residue_No.R). To validate our script, we first calculated the pLDDT scores for four reference structures from the AlphaFold database. The results (75.19 for AF-P36888-F1-v4, 75.19 for AF-Q00342-F1-v4, 86.21 for AF-Q9ZTA9-F1-v4, and 75.31 for AF-E7ER61-F1-v4) were consistent with their reported values, confirming the accuracy of our implementation. After validation, we applied the script to compute the pLDDT score of the FLT3-ITD modelled structure. A detailed summary of the selected AlphaFold models, their statistics, and pLDDT plots is provided in Supplementary File S1. The stereochemical quality of the FLT3-ITD model was further evaluated using a Ramachandran plot, which assesses the energetically favoured regions of backbone dihedral angles against amino acid residues. In addition, the local and global quality of the modelled structure was estimated using the MolProbity score, where lower values indicate better model quality. On the Swiss-Model server, both the Ramachandran and MolProbity evaluations are performed using the MolProbity software (https://github.com/rlabduke/MolProbity) [53].

However, our current study does not aim to explore the membrane-associated dynamics of the full-length (Full length: 993aa) FLT3 protein. Since our future work is centered on therapeutic drug identification targeting these mutations, we limited our computational study to the JM and kinase regions of the FLT3-WT and FLT3-ITD structures (572-958aa). The acquired TKD mutations usually occur in the FLT3-ITD protein during the treatment of AML with TKIs (Tyrosine kinase inhibitors) [17], [36]. For this reason, we added TKD mutation in the FLT3-ITD structure at phosphorylation site Y842 with C/F. Molecular dynamic (MD) simulation

The GROMACS 2023.2 version was employed to perform molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the FLT3 protein to evaluate its stability, flexibility, and compactness [37]. This study analyzed six different FLT3 protein structures, including the wild type (FLT3-WT), two point-mutated structures of FLT3-WT (FLT3-Y842C and FLT3-Y842F), FLT3-ITD, and a combination mutation of FLT3-ITD with two TKD point mutations (FLT3-ITD-Y842C and FLT3-ITD-Y842F). Before initiating the MD simulations, parameters for the force field, solvation, energy minimization, and equilibration were systematically established. The CHARMM27 force field was applied to the protein structure [38]. While newer force fields, such as CHARMM36m and AMBER99SB, exist, CHARMM27 was selected for its extensive validation and proven accuracy in simulating protein stability and dynamics, particularly in studies involving TKD domains of FLT3 protein [39], [40], [41], [42]. Additionally, the TIP3P water model, fully compatible with CHARMM27, was utilized for solvation to ensure consistency and accuracy [43]. The protein structure was solvated within a triclinic box (dimensions 11.66765 × 11.66765 × 11.66765 nm), ensuring a minimum distance of 1.0 nm between the protein and the box edges. To neutralize the entire system and replicate physiological conditions, a salt concentration of 0.15 M was maintained by adding appropriate numbers of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm for 5000 steps to achieve a stable configuration. A constant temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1.0 bar were applied to the system using the Berendsen thermostat and the Parrinello-Rahman barostat, respectively, to maintain equilibration at constant temperature and pressure [44], [45]. Electrostatic interactions of the system were calculated using Particle-Mesh Ewald (PME) summation [46]. Both the NVT and NPT ensembles were applied during equilibration and production runs [47], [48]. The following protocol was employed during the Equilibration and production steps.

Temperature equilibration (Heating/cooling): The system was gradually heated from 0 K to 300 K over a period of 100 ps using the V-rescale thermostat [45].

Constraints and restraints

Positional restraints: During the initial heating phase, positional restraints were applied to the heavy atoms of the protein to keep the structure fixed while allowing the water molecules and ions to move freely. This was achieved using the -DPOSRES definition. These restraints were gradually released during the equilibration phase, allowing the protein to attain full flexibility.

Bond constraints: The LINCS algorithm was employed to apply bond constraints, particularly to bonds involving hydrogen atoms (constraints = h-bonds). The algorithm was configured with 1 iteration (lincs_iter = 1) and 4th order accuracy (lincs_order = 4).

Following the equilibration, the production run was performed under the NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 bar pressure using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat. The production simulation was conducted for 300 ns using the leap-frog integrator method [45]. Periodic Boundary Conditions (PBC) were applied in all directions to replicate the simulation box, eliminating edge effects and simulating bulk properties. A time step of 2 fs was used for the simulations, with trajectories recorded every 10 ps. Over the 300 ns simulation, a total of 30,000 conformations were generated for trajectory analysis. The trajectory of the MD simulation study was analyzed using built-in GROMACS tools, such as rms, rmsf, hbond, gyrate, and sasa [29].

Evaluation of the amino acid level stability of the WT and mutant type FLT3 protein by cluster analysis

To evaluate amino acid residue-level flexibility, we utilized an additional computational technique, Cluster analysis, which is a widely used statistical technique to group similar objects or data points into clusters [32]. The main aim of cluster analysis is to partition a set of observations into subsets or clusters based on similarities between the data points within each group and dissimilarities between data points in different groups. Cluster analysis techniques now have been widely used in the structural biological studies of protein to understand the conformational changes of a protein by using the trajectories of MD simulation analysis of protein or protein complex. Cluster analysis uses different algorithms to divide the data set into clusters of similar properties. In the present work, we used the K-means algorithm to sort the objects from the data set into different groups. K-means indicate the sum of squared deviations (SSD) from the cluster’s centroid. By cluster analysis methods, we determined the optimal number of clusters in terms of RMSF using the elbow method. The “elbow” region of the objective function versus the number of cluster plots was sketched by R studio 4.2.2 [33].

Evaluation of the stability of WT and mutant FLT3 protein using Dynamut2

Machine learning tools are based on computational techniques that enable systems to learn patterns and relationships from data without explicit programming and making predictions based on input features. These types of tools are widely used across various fields, including biology, to analyse large and complex datasets [49]. In structural biology, they are used to model protein dynamics, predict the impact of mutations, and classify conformational states from simulation data. These tools enhance understanding of biological systems by automating data analysis and uncovering hidden insights [50].

In the current study, in addition to using GROMACS for molecular dynamics simulations, we employed another software package, DynaMut2, to validate the dynamic motion and stability of both the wild-type (WT) and mutated FLT3 protein structures. DynaMut2 is a web-based server that utilizes graph-based signatures and partially incorporates machine learning techniques to evaluate the dynamic behaviour and functional impact of mutations on protein structures, based on static input structures [34]. DynaMut2 integrates two main tools for this purpose: (i) a Normal Mode Analysis (NMA) approach, which analyzes and visualizes protein dynamics by sampling conformational changes, and (ii) a Prediction tool based on Ran-dom Forest algorithm, which assesses the impact of mutations on protein stability and dynamics by estimating vibrational entropy changes using machine learning methods. In the proposed work, we used the NMA approach to analyze the protein dynamic motion. NMA is a computational approach that models the vibrational modes of a protein based on its 3D structure, then predicting its intrinsic motions and conformational flexibility based on its atomic structure. NMA uses the atomic coordinates of the protein's 3D structure, including both WT and mutant forms, as input. These coordinates represent the arrangement of all atoms in the protein. Then the protein is approximated as a harmonic oscillator, with atoms treated as masses connected by springs representing chemical bonds. Then the potential energy of the protein is calculated as a quadratic function of atomic displacements from the equilibrium structure, leading to the computation of the Hessian matrix (a matrix of second derivatives of the potential energy). This matrix characterizes the stiffness of the protein and the coupling of atomic movements. Decomposition of the Hessian matrix yields eigenvalues and eigenvectors: eigenvalues correspond to the frequencies of vibrational modes (with lower values indicating biologically relevant motions), and eigenvectors define the directions of these motions. By this method, NMA provides insights into the flexibility and potential conformational movements of the protein structure.

The structural features of FLT3-WT and FLT3-ITD protein models

The FLT3-WT protein structure is composed of a total of 993 amino acid residues with a molecular weight of about 150–160 kDa. The protein structure encompasses an extracellular N-terminal region, a transmembrane region (TM), a juxtamembrane region (JM), and an intracellular C-terminal region. The extracellular N-terminal region includes five immunoglobulin-like domains (D1 (27−161), D2 (162−244), D3 (245−346), D4 (347−434), and D5 (435−541)), encompassing amino acid residues from 27 to 541. Among these, first three domains (D1, D2 and D3) are involved in ligand binding, and other two domains (D4 and D5),which are involved in protein dimerization. The extracellular region consists entirely of beta-strand structures. The TM domain adopts a helical structure composed of amino acid residues 544–563, anchoring the protein in the cytoplasmic region. The 564–571 region is considered a linker region (LR) [12]. The JM (comprising amino acid residues 572–603) controls the FLT3 protein activation by steric inhibition and maintains the protein in an inactive form. The JM region consists of three sub-motifs: the JM binding motif (JM-B, 572–578), the JM switch motif (JM-S, 579–592), and the JM zipper motif or linker motif (JM-Z, 593–603). The intracellular kinase domain (amino acid residues 604–958) is highly conserved. Within the kinase region of the FLT3 protein, there are two substructures, an N-lobe and a C-lobe, containing two sub-kinase domains (TKD1 (604−828) and TKD2 (859−958)) which are interconnected by another kinase substructure known as the activation loop (A-Loop, amino acid residues 829–858). In TKD1, there is a large loop region between positions 723 and 762, which is missing in most of the crystallized structures due to its inherent flexibility and dynamic nature, making it difficult to resolve through X-ray crystallography techniques. In the modelled FLT3-ITD protein structure includes 1000 amino acids with continuous numbering, the duplicated region appears from positions 595–601, while the original position of the seven amino acid residues is numbered as positions 602–608 in the JM. Our study focused on the Y842 phosphorylation site in both FLT-WT and FLT3-ITD. For maintaining consistent numbering in the FLT3-ITD protein structure as in FLT3-WT, we revised the entire numbering of the FLT3-ITD structure and adjusted the numbers of both the duplicated region and the original region of the JM-Z motif with the same numbering (“REYEYDL” 595–601). MD simulation

Protein functionality is directly correlated with its conformational stability and flexibility [34], [55], [56]. This type of information will be helpful for understanding the nature of different mutations and their impact on the diseased pathway of AML. Based on this information, we can find more effective therapeutics against target proteins to control deregulation of pathophysiological pathways. In receptor tyrosine kinases, the Y842 residue, located within the activation loop, is a conserved tyrosine that plays a critical regulatory role in modulating kinase activity and downstream signalling cascades. However, in FLT3, Y842 is not required for receptor activation or ubiquitination, but it is essential for maintaining downstream RAS/ERK signalling, receptor stability, and cellular transformation, primarily through SHP2-mediated pathways [16].

In this study, our primary objective was to elucidate the characteristic behaviours of the FLT3 protein when it undergoes point mutations of TKD (Y842C/F), ITD, and combined mutations of ITD with TKD (Y842C/F). To achieve this, we analysed the MD simulation trajectories, focusing on RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration (Rg), H-bond, and SASA. Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA)

The Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) of a protein is used to estimate the total surface area that is directly accessible to surrounding solvent molecules, typically water. Based on SASA plots, the protein’s dynamic stability and conformational behaviour in solution can be evaluated [60], [61], [62]. A lower SASA value indicates increased folding stability and compactness, reflecting the burial of hydrophobic residues and reduced solvent exposure of the protein surface. The comparison of SASA plots of FLT3-WT with FLT3-Y842C and FLT3-Y842F reveals that (Fig. 7A), throughout the 300 ns simulation, FLT3-Y842C consistently maintains lower SASA values compared with FLT3-WT, suggesting that the cysteine substitution at position Y842 in FLT3-WT promotes a more compact and stable fold. Toward the end of the trajectory (275–300 ns), FLT3-Y842C shows a marginal increase in SASA, indicating a minor relaxation of the structure. In contrast, FLT3-Y842F displays higher SASA values than FLT3-Y842C across most of the simulation and surpasses FLT3-WT after ∼250 ns, reflecting greater solvent exposure and enhanced conformational flexibility. Overall, these observations indicate that the Y842C and Y842F substitutions alter the conformational flexibility of FLT3 compared with the WT protein, resulting in distinct effects on structural compactness and solvent accessibility. In the SASA plots of FLT3-WT with mutated structures FLT3-ITD, FLT3-ITD-Y842C, and FLT3-ITD-Y842F (in Fig. 7B) reveals distinct solvent-exposure patterns throughout the 300 ns simulation. Both FLT3-ITD and FLT3-ITD-Y842C exhibit higher SASA values (∼215–230 nm²) than FLT3-WT across the entire trajectory, indicating reduced compactness and greater solvent accessibility of the mutated proteins. In contrast, FLT3-ITD-Y842F maintains lower SASA values (∼215–220 nm²) than FLT3-WT until approximately 200 ns, suggesting transient structural compactness. However, beyond this point, its SASA fluctuates more markedly alternating above and below the WT curve until 300 ns, reflecting dynamic changes in surface exposure and conformational flexibility. Fig. 7C, indicates the overall comparison of SASA profiles among all six FLT3 structures demonstrates distinct differences in solvent exposure patterns. All mutant structures, including FLT3-Y842C, exhibit higher SASA values than FLT3-WT throughout the simulation, indicating reduced compactness and increased solvent accessibility. The average SASA values for FLT3-WT, FLT3-Y842C, FLT3-Y842F, FLT3-ITD, FLT3-ITD-Y842C, and FLT3-ITD-Y842F were 198.52, 208.71, 213.39, 219.73, 218.50, and 213.62 nm², respectively. Among these, FLT3-ITD shows the highest solvent accessibility, suggesting pronounced structural loosening, while FLT3-Y842C exhibits moderate SASA values, indicating a modest reduction in compactness compared with FLT3-WT. Intra hydrogen bond (H-Bond) interaction

Intra-molecular hydrogen bonds between amino acid residues play a critical role in maintaining protein conformational stability, as first demonstrated by the Fersht group in the 1980s [63], [64]. Since then, several studies have reaffirmed that these interactions are central to structural integrity and protein folding. In this study, we examined the intra-molecular hydrogen-bonding stability of FLT3-WT and its mutant forms through 300 ns molecular-dynamics simulations. A higher number of hydrogen bonds generally indicates improved conformational stability.

From the hydrogen-bond interaction plots of FLT3-WT, FLT3-Y842C, and FLT3-Y842F (Fig. 8A), the FLT3-Y842C mutant shows slight deviations in hydrogen-bond count, while FLT3-Y842F consistently maintains a marginally higher number of hydrogen bonds across most of the trajectory. The elevated hydrogen-bond population in FLT3-Y842F suggests stronger intramolecular stabilization and reduced local fluctuations relative to both WT and Y842C. Conversely, FLT3-Y842C displays minor variations in bond number, reflecting moderate flexibility in the activation-loop region. Overall, the increased hydrogen bonding in FLT3-Y842F corresponds to a more rigid, compact conformation, whereas the lower and more variable hydrogen-bond count in FLT3-Y842C indicates greater flexibility more dynamic than both FLT3-Y842F and FLT3-WT. In contrast, FLT3-ITD-Y842C exhibits a slightly reduced and more variable hydrogen-bond count, suggesting localized flexibility and minor disruption of the hydrogen-bond network relative to WT. Computational assessment of conformational dynamics in WT and mutant FLT3 using DynaMut2

To investigate the dynamic behaviour of the WT and FLT3-ITD structures, we performed Normal Mode Analysis (NMA) using Dynamut2 webserver. NMA provides an efficient approximation of protein dynamics based on harmonic motion around a native conformation. NMA uses a consensus method DUET that combines the statistical potential function for energy calculations (SDM), structural pattern mining approach (mCSM-stability), and ENCoM (Elastic Network Contact Model), to predict the energetic and conformational impact of mutations. By modeling protein motion through low-frequency normal modes, this method allows us to infer how mutations may affect structural flexibility and overall dynamics. Fig. 10A-L display the harmonic movement and the steady state images of the movement direction of WT and mutant type FLT3 protein structure by NMA analysis. From these analyses, we observed notable differences in the directionality of motion among the variants. Discussion

The highly dynamic nature of proteins directly correlates with their conformational stability. Protein stability plays a crucial role in regulating function, as the three-dimensional folded structure of a protein is one of its defining features and often reflects the functional capabilities and phenotype of an organism. However, protein conformational stability can be altered by mutations, which may lead to structural alterations. Such modified protein structures often undergo several post-translational modifications, which are the main hallmarks of several diseases, including cancer. In cancer, the altered structure can result in altered protein function through various mechanisms, including activating the protein function in oncogenes or inactivating the function in tumor suppressors. All of these will promote cancer formation and a cancer phenotype [34], [65], [66], [67], [68]. Therefore, understanding the dynamic behaviour of proteins is essential in structural biology for obtaining an in-depth understanding of the molecular mechanisms of diseases. This knowledge is key to identifying clinically relevant information and discovering more effective therapeutics against diseases.

In receptor tyrosine kinases, the Y842 residue, located within the activation loop, is a conserved phosphorylation site that often plays a regulatory role in modulating kinase activity and downstream signalling cascades. However, in the case of FLT3, Y842 is not essential for receptor activation or ubiquitination. Instead, it plays a critical role in maintaining downstream RAS/ERK signalling, receptor stability, and cellular transformation, primarily through SHP2-mediated pathways [16]. In this study, we investigated the structural and dynamic behavior of the FLT3 protein carrying point mutations at the Y842C/F phosphorylation site in both FLT3-WT and FLT3-ITD backgrounds. Additionally, we analyzed the impact of an internal tandem duplication (ITD) mutation in the juxtamembrane (JM) region, involving the duplication of seven amino acid residues (REYEYDL, positions 595–601), referred to as the W51 ITD mutation. Although the Y842C mutation in the tyrosine kinase domain (TKD) of FLT3-WT and FLT3-ITD has been characterized previously [15], [21], the Y842F mutation and its combination with ITD remain underexplored. Here, we endeavor to comprehend the characteristic behaviours of all the above mutations of FLT3 protein through computational studies including MD simulation, cluster analysis, and NMA analysis method for further therapeutic identification against this mutation. Before incorporating the computational techniques, we first modelled the FLT3-ITD protein structure using the SWISS-MODEL server and compared it with the FLT3-WT structure. In our homology model, the duplicated region at the beginning of the JM-Z domain (which is normally a β-strand region [12]) exhibited a tendency to adopt a short helical conformation. We present this as an observation from our model, consistent with the known structural disruption caused by ITDs in the JM region, rather than as a novel structural finding. Additionally, structural changes were observed in the actual JM-Z region of the FLT3 protein, where half of the strand has been converted into a loop. Further structural alterations were noted in the inter-kinase region (activation loop) between the C- and N-lobe kinase domains due to this duplication. The JM domain in FLT3 plays a crucial role in controlling activation, inactivation, and stabilizing the inactive kinase conformation. The ITD mutation in the JM-Z motif induces conformational changes in both the JM and kinase regions of the FLT3 protein, disrupting the auto-inhibitory conformation and promoting constitutive activation of downstream effectors, including the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK kinases, STAT5, and PI3-kinase [69].

To understand the dynamic behaviour and conformational stability of the mutated FLT3 protein structures, we applied computational techniques to both the WT and mutated forms and then performed a comparison study of the mutated structure based on its FLT3-WT. This analysis aimed to elucidate the characteristic behaviours of the mutated structures compared to their native form. In this study we considered four mutated structures of FLT3 (FLT3-Y842C, FLT3-Y842F, FLT3-ITD-Y842C, and FLT3-ITD-Y842F) along with its WT (FLT3-WT) and FLT3-ITD protein structures. Based on all six structures, we first performed the computational technique of MD simulation analysis. Here, we mainly focused on the comparison studies of structural deviation (RMSD), fluctuation (RMSF), structural compactness (Rg), intra-H-bond interactions, and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) of the mutated structure of FLT3 with WT protein. In RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and SASA analysis studies, we usually focus on the lowest values, and in the H-Bond interaction analysis study, we examine the highest values relevant to the protein structures. All these values indicate the protein structures' good optimization, conformation stability, and compactness.

The RMSD (Fig. 4) and RMSF (Fig. 5) analyses collectively reveal distinct dynamic behaviours among the FLT3 variants, highlighting how specific substitutions and duplications affect JM and kinase stability and flexibility. FLT3-WT displayed an average RMSD of 0.85 nm and RMSF of 0.27 nm, representing a balanced conformation with moderate flexibility across key regulatory domains such as the juxtamembrane (JM) and activation-loop regions. The FLT3-Y842C mutant exhibited a slightly higher RMSD (0.86 nm) but lower RMSF (0.17 nm), indicating minor global destabilization yet increased local rigidity within the activation loop due to the loss of the phosphorylatable hydroxyl group (Tyr842) by addition of cysteine [16], [36]. The Y842C substitution therefore represents a mutation at a phosphorylation site rather than a phospho-mimetic change, eliminating the residue’s ability to be phosphorylated and thereby altering activation-loop dynamics [16], [36]. The FLT3-Y842F mutant showed the lowest RMSD (0.72 nm) and reduced RMSF (0.20 nm), reflecting a more compact but conformationally restricted state arising from the bulky aromatic phenylalanine side chain. Because phenylalanine retains the aromatic ring but lacks the hydroxyl group, this substitution abolishes phosphorylation at Tyr842, preventing normal activation-loop phosphorylation and altering intramolecular interactions critical for kinase activation [16], [17]. In contrast, the FLT3-ITD mutant demonstrated a lower RMSD (0.78 nm) than FLT3-WT but the highest RMSF (0.30 nm), signifying that although its global structure remains relatively stable, it undergoes enhanced local fluctuations, particularly in the JM region, consistent with the loss of autoinhibitory interactions and constitutive activation by ITD duplication [16]. When Y842C/F (FLT3-ITD-Y842C and FLT3-ITD-Y842F) substitutions were introduced into the ITD mutant structure (FLT3-ITD), the resulting FLT3-ITD-Y842C (RMSD = 0.82 nm; RMSF = 0.19 nm) and FLT3-ITD-Y842F (RMSD = 0.87 nm; RMSF = 0.16 nm) mutants exhibited additive effects: ITD-driven flexibility combined with Y842C/F-induced local rigidification. Overall, these results indicate that FLT3-WT maintains a dynamic equilibrium, Y842C/F in FLT3 substitutions restrict local flexibility by disrupting phosphorylation-dependent regulation at Tyr842, FLT3-ITD enhances regional mobility, and their combination promotes conformational rearrangements that destabilize the inactive form and favour an open, active-like kinase conformation [12], [14], [16], [17], [70]. The FLT3 phosphorylation is known to activate multiple downstream signalling pathways, including PI3K/Akt, Ras/MAPK, STAT5, and ERK1/2, which are crucial for regulating cell survival, proliferation, and apoptosis inhibition. Dysregulation of these pathways contributes to the development and progression of leukemia and other cancers [70].

Analysis of the radius of gyration (Rg) and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) (Fig. 6, Fig. 7) provided further insights into the global compactness and stability of the FLT3 variants. The Rg parameter reflects the overall distribution of atomic mass around the protein’s center of gravity and serves as a sensitive indicator of structural compactness and conformational stability, while SASA quantifies solvent exposure and complements, Rg in evaluating the degree of structural openness. Together, these metrics help to interpret how point mutations alter the packing of regulatory domains and thereby influence protein activation.

When compared with FLT3-WT (Rg = 1.93 nm; SASA = 198.52 nm²), the FLT3-Y842C mutant exhibited the lowest Rg (1.88 nm) with moderately increased SASA (208.71 nm²), indicating a more compact but locally stabilized activation loop that retains solvent accessibility around the phosphorylation site. The FLT3-Y842F mutant displayed slightly higher Rg (1.92 nm) and SASA (213.39 nm²), consistent with partial loss of compactness and increased loop flexibility arising from the absence of the hydroxyl group at Tyr842, which disrupts normal phosphorylation-dependent stabilization. The FLT3-ITD mutant showed the highest SASA (219.73 nm²) and elevated Rg fluctuations (1.91 nm), suggesting loss of juxtamembrane (JM) autoinhibitory contacts and enhanced global flexibility associated with constitutive activation.

Interestingly, the combined mutants (FLT3-ITD-Y842C and FLT3-ITD-Y842F) exhibited intermediate Rg values (1.86 nm and 1.84 nm) but maintained high SASA (218.50 nm² and 213.62 nm²), reflecting additive effects of ITD-driven conformational expansion and Y842-site-dependent rearrangements within the activation loop. These observations suggest that Y842C favours a compact and locally stabilized fold, whereas Y842F and ITD mutations promote a more open, solvent-exposed conformation, weakening JM–kinase domain coupling. The combined ITD–Y842 substitutions further enhance solvent exposure while maintaining partial compactness that stabilizes an active-like kinase conformation. Functionally, such Rg–SASA relationships indicate that mutations reducing overall compactness and increasing solvent accessibility favour transition toward the active state, consistent with enhanced FLT3 signalling activity.

Hydrogen-bond analysis (Fig. 8) revealed modest reductions in the average number of intramolecular H-bonds relative to FLT3-WT (292). The largest decreases occurred in FLT3-Y842C (275) and FLT3-ITD-Y842C (283), suggesting weakened internal stabilization and enhanced local flexibility, particularly around the activation loop. Although all mutant systems exhibited fewer hydrogen bonds than the WT, comparative analysis among the mutants showed that FLT3-Y842F (284), FLT3-ITD (288), and FLT3-ITD-Y842F (290) retained relatively higher H-bond numbers, implying greater internal cohesion and rigidity compared with other mutant forms. Functionally, these patterns suggest that FLT3-Y842C-containing mutants promote localized flexibility that may preserve partial regulatory motion, whereas FLT3-Y842F and FLT3-ITD containing mutants exhibit greater structural rigidity and activation-loop stabilization, more rigid than other mutants but still more flexible than WT. The combination mutant structures (FLT3-ITD-Y842C and FLT3-ITD-Y842F) demonstrate additive effects, balancing global flexibility relative to WT and local rigidification within the activation loop. Functionally, such conformational rearrangements weaken autoinhibitory constraints and promote constitutive activation of FLT3, consistent with its known role in aberrant signalling and leukemogenesis [16], [70].

To further assess the conformational stability and flexibility of the FLT3 variants, cluster analysis based on backbone RMSF values was performed (Fig. 9). The total number of residues adopting stable conformations was 199, 294, 264, 114, 289, and 313 for FLT3-WT, FLT3-Y842C, FLT3-Y842F, FLT3-ITD, FLT3-ITD-Y842C, and FLT3-ITD-Y842F, respectively, with corresponding average RMSF values of 0.27 nm, 0.17 nm, 0.20 nm, 0.30 nm, 0.19 nm, and 0.16 nm. The Y842C mutation at the phosphorylation site markedly increased the number of stable residues from 199 (WT, 0.27 nm) to 294 (0.17 nm), indicating enhanced structural rigidity. This stabilization likely arises from the smaller cysteine side chain, which limits loop mobility while supporting local secondary-structure packing. In contrast, the Y842F substitution reduced the number of stable residues to 264 (0.20 nm), implying partial destabilization and increased activation-loop flexibility due to loss of the hydroxyl group necessary for phosphorylation-dependent hydrogen bonding.

The FLT3-ITD mutant exhibited the fewest stable residues (114 with 0.30 nm RMSF), consistent with pronounced structural instability and elevated flexibility caused by disruption of juxtamembrane–kinase domain interactions that normally maintain the inactive conformation. When Y842C or Y842F substitutions were introduced into the ITD background, the number of stable residues increased substantially to 289 (0.19 nm) and 313 (0.16 nm) for FLT3-ITD-Y842C and FLT3-ITD-Y842F, respectively. These findings suggest that, although ITD enhances global flexibility, the additional Y842 substitutions impose compensatory local rigidity within the activation loop, restoring partial stability to the overall structure. Collectively, the cluster analysis supports the RMSF observations: FLT3-ITD drives overall flexibility, Y842C and Y842F mutations locally stabilize the activation loop, and their combined presence yields a more rigid and structurally stable conformation that likely favors an open, catalytically active state of the kinase.

To comprehend the dynamic nature of WT and mutated structures of FLT3, we employed an additional computational tool, DynaMut2, which provides information on the dynamic behaviour of the protein based on the harmonic movement by NMA analysis. The visual representation results of the NMA analysis (Fig. 10) reveal that the point mutation in the phosphorylation site Y842C of FLT3-WT does not alter its movement direction, rather, it changes its frequency (energy) in movements. Nevertheless, it is possible to discern that the harmonic movement of the proteins proceeds in opposite directions in all other mutated structures.

Overall, these integrated results demonstrate that the FLT3-ITD mutation disrupts JM-domain autoinhibition and enhances global flexibility, while the Y842C/F substitutions remodel the activation loop, modulating local rigidity and hydrogen-bonding at a critical phosphorylation site. The combined ITD-Y842C/F mutations exert additive effects, simultaneously increasing global mobility and reinforcing local structural stabilization. This dual behavior destabilizes the inactive kinase conformation, favours the open, catalytically competent state, and thereby supports constitutive FLT3 activation and oncogenic signalling, consistent with prior biochemical findings [16], [70].

Limitation of the study

In this study, we aimed to investigate the characteristic behaviours of point mutations in the phosphorylation site Y842C/F in FLT3-WT and FLT3-ITD, as well as the ITD mutation in the FLT3 protein, using computational methods. While these approaches provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying mutated FLT3 proteins, several inherent limitations must be acknowledged.

For the computational study, we selected the FLT3-ITD modelled protein structure from the Swiss-Model server, which was based on the FLT3-WT structure derived from AlphaFold (P36888). The quality of this model depends on input data and available reference models. While AlphaFold provides accurate predictions, its performance may vary, especially for regions with low sequence conservation or flexibility. We validated the models using the experimental structure (1RJB) and pLDDT scores, but uncertainties remain due to the limitations of computational modelling. MD simulations were performed for 300 ns with the CHARMM27 force field, providing insights into the dynamic nature of FLT3; however, further validation is required to compare the flexibility of FLT3-WT and mutant structures using extended simulations with the CHARMM36 force field. Hydrogen bond analysis revealed the global stability of the mutated structures, but C-alpha positional fluctuations and RMSF confidence intervals were not assessed. Future studies will incorporate these analyses for a deeper understanding of the dynamic stability of FLT3 mutants. Another major limitation of this computational study is the challenge of translating computational findings into experimental validation. While computational models provide valuable hypotheses, experimental confirmation through techniques like X-ray crystallography, cryo-EM, or NMR spectroscopy is essential to verify structural predictions. Experimental validation of the full-length FLT3 protein, especially in its mutant forms, is particularly difficult due to the protein's size, complexity, and the dynamic nature of its conformational changes, especially in the long loop region of the TKD1. Many crystallized structures are missing these regions due to difficulty in capturing them. Additionally, simplifications made to ensure the feasibility of simulations, such as using truncated structures, may not fully represent the protein's complexity in its native form, limiting the accuracy of our computational models.

From our analysis, we observed that substitution of Y842 with non-phosphorylatable residues (C or F) alters the local dynamic behaviour of the protein. Based on our simulations, we speculate that such substitutions may influence its phosphorylation-dependent regulatory mechanism. While our current study focuses on the apo forms, the findings underscore the dual impact of Y842 mutations, stemming from both side-chain biophysical alterations and the loss of a critical phosphorylation site. This highlights the importance of future studies involving phosphomimetic variants or ATP/Mg²⁺-bound simulations to fully elucidate the regulatory role of Y842 in FLT3 activation. Furthermore, our ongoing drug identification studies will describe how the dynamics and functional behaviour of the activation loop change in response to different FLT3 mutations.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully revealed the impact of specific mutations on the FLT3 protein through comprehensive computational analysis. Notably, we investigated the effects of point mutations at the phosphorylation site Y842C/F in the FLT3-WT and FLT3-ITD structures, as well as the internal tandem duplication (ITD) mutation involving seven amino acid residues in the JM-Z motif of FLT3. Using the advanced modelling techniques, we generated the first structural model of FLT3-ITD, using protein sequence of ITD mutation identified AML patient sample. This FLT3-ITD modelled structure has been deposited in the public repository of the modelled protein database “ModelArchive,” where it can serve as a resource for future studies aimed at understanding the structural consequences of ITD mutations. The availability of this structural model will facilitate future research into targeted therapeutics for FLT3-ITD-driven malignancies, specifically addressing the ITD mutation of seven amino acid residues “REYEYDL” in the JM-Z motif. Apart from this, the presented work also highlights critical insights into the structural and functional consequences of FLT3 mutations, which have implications for both understanding the molecular mechanisms driving AML and developing more effective therapeutic strategies. By providing a detailed analysis of mutation-induced structural changes, we pave the way for further exploration of clinically relevant therapeutics targeting deregulated pathways associated with FLT3.

Building on this foundation, future studies should aim to identify a specific kinase inhibitor targeting the altered regions of FLT3 protein, with a particular focus on deregulated pathways using the CADD approach. Wet-lab validation of the identified kinase molecule inhibitors also will be a critical next step of the future work.
