Innate and T-cellular immune responses to sequential vaccination with chimeric hemagglutinin split influenza virus vaccines in mice☆
Authors: Kirill Vasilev, Eduard Puente-Massaguer, Irene Hoxie, Kaitlyn Bushfield, Florian Krammer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric hemagglutinin (cHA)-based vaccines represent a promising strategy toward the development of a broadly protective universal influenza virus vaccine. Previous studies show that sequential administration of cHA split influenza vaccines stimulates broadly cross-reactive antibodies targeting the HA stalk and induces systemic and localized CD4+ T-cell responses to the HA, neuraminidase (NA), and nucleoprotein (NP). However, the exact role of the T-cellular immune response in the protection after vaccination with cHA constructs remains under-investigated. Here we provide an in-depth characterization of the innate immune response and antigen-specific T-cellular immune response in mice after sequential immunization with the group 2 cH15/3HK14N2HK14 and cH4/3HK14N2HK14 virus vaccine candidates with or without CpG 1018 adjuvant and during a heterologous virus challenge. The non-adjuvanted split vaccine induced mixed Th1/Th2 T-cellular immune response associated with interferon-γ, interleukin (IL)-2, tumor necrosis factor α, IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, and IL-10 production whereas the adjuvanted vaccine (split/CpG) induced a Th1-polarized T-cellular immune response mediated by IL2+TNFα+ and IFNγ+IL2+TNFα+ polyfunctional CD4 effector memory T-lymphocytes. An increase in the Th2-associated cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) and the eosinophil accumulation was shown in the lungs of mice immunized with a non-adjuvanted split vaccine after a challenge with a phylogenetically distant influenza virus. We also investigated the ability of T-cells to induce protection against the heterologous virus challenge in passive-transfer and T-cell depletion experiments. Passive transfer of sera from mice immunized with split/CpG vaccine reduced weight loss, inflammatory cytokine production, and viral replication in lungs after challenge. Passive transfer of T-cells from immunized to naïve mice did not reduce weight loss and viral replication but affected the innate immune cell population dynamics and cytokine response in mouse lungs after the challenge. The depletion of CD4+ T-cells did not affect viral replication in mouse lungs but influenced cytokine production and innate immune cell population dynamics.Keywords: Influenza, T-cells, HA stalk, Universal vaccine, ODN 1018, CpG 1018

Introduction

The effectiveness of current seasonal influenza virus vaccines exhibits considerable variability ranging from 10 % to 60 % [1,2]. Conventional vaccines primarily target the immunodominant head domain of the hemagglutinin (HA) glycoprotein, which undergoes frequent antigenic drift, requiring regular vaccine formulation updates. Different approaches for the development of a broadly protective universal influenza virus vaccine are being investigated in preclinical and clinical settings [3]. Efforts are mainly focused on the induction of immune responses to conserved regions of the influenza virus such as the HA stalk domain. Chimeric HA (cHA)-based vaccines, engineered by replacing the head domains of seasonal influenza virus strains with those from non-human subtypes represent a promising strategy for the redirection of the immune response toward the conserved HA stalk region. Previous studies have demonstrated that sequential immunization with cHA constructs effectively stimulates the production of broadly cross-reactive antibodies in various animal models [4–6] as well as in human subjects [7–9]. Until recently these studies have been mainly focused on group 1 cHA constructs, formulated as recombinant proteins, live-attenuated influenza vaccines (LAIVs), vectored vaccines, or inactivated split vaccines [10,11]. In a previous study, we investigated the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of group 2 cHA virus vaccines, cH15/3HK14N2HK14 and cH4/3HK14N2HK14, formulated as LAIVs or inactivated split vaccines with or without the Toll-like receptor 9 agonist adjuvant CpG 1018 [12]. Immunization with these group 2 cHA split vaccines in combination with CpG 1018 elicited broadly cross-reactive antibodies that effectively target all group 2 HAs. The antibodies induced through sequential vaccination were primarily directed toward highly conserved regions of the HA, including the stalk and the trimer interface, and also to the N2 neuraminidase (NA). Furthermore, this immunization approach induced both systemic and localized CD4+ T-cell responses specific to the HA, NA, and nucleoprotein (NP) [12]. T-cells play an important role in the immune response to the influenza virus. Multiple studies in mouse and ferret models have demonstrated that CD8+ T-lymphocytes can provide cross-protection during heterologous virus infection [13–17] by directly killing virus-infected cells. CD4+ T-lymphocytes contribute to the antiviral immune response by facilitating the antibody-secretory function of B-cells, modulating the activity of the innate immune system and CD8+ T-lymphocytes, and also performing cytotoxic functions [13]. Postvaccinal T-cellular immune response and the contribution of T-cells in overall protection depends on multiple factors, such as vaccine type, vaccination regimen, adjuvants, and route of administration. LAIVs and vectored influenza vaccines generally induce higher T-cellular immune responses, which mediate cross-protection during heterologous virus infections as shown in mice, ferrets, and clinical trials [18–25]. In contrast, inactivated influenza vaccines usually induce weaker T-cellular immune responses than natural infection or LAIV vaccination [20,26–28]. The contribution of the T-cellular immune response to protection after vaccination with adjuvanted cHA split vaccines remains under-investigated.

Materials and methods

Viruses

Group 2 cH15/3HK14N2HK14 and cH4/3HK14N2HK14 viruses were generated using reverse genetics techniques [11]. The cH15/3HK14N2HK14 virus incorporates the H15 head domain from the HA of the A/wedge-tailed shearwater/Western Australia/2576/1979 (H15N9) virus, while the H4 head domain of the cH4/3HK14N2HK14 virus is derived from the A/duck/Czechoslovakia/1956 (H4N6) virus. Both viruses display the HA stalk domain and the NA from the A/Hong Kong/4801/2014 (H3N2) virus. For inactivated split influenza virus vaccines, the internal genes originate from the donor vaccine virus strain A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (A/PR/8/34, H1N1). The B-cH5/1 virus used for priming infections was also generated via reverse genetics [29,30]. The head domain of this chimeric B-cH5/1 virus is derived from the A/Vietnam/1203/2004 (H5N1) virus, while the HA stalk domain belongs to A/PR/8/34 (H1N1). Both the NA and the internal genes for the B-cH5/1 virus are derived from the B/Yamagata/16/1988 virus. The A/Philippines/2/1982 (H3N2, X-79 6:2 A/PR/8/34 reassortant, mouse-adapted) challenge virus and the aforementioned influenza viruses were grown in 10-day old embryonated chicken eggs at 37 °C for 48 h. They were then cooled at 4 °C overnight (O/N), allantoic fluid was harvested and centrifuged at 4000g, 4 °C, 20 min to remove cell debris, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C. Viral titers were determined by the plaque assay method on Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells [31].

Production of inactivated split influenza virus vaccines

The inactivated split influenza virus vaccine was produced as described previously [32]. Briefly, viruses were inactivated using 0.05 % (v/v) β-propiolactone (Millipore Sigma) and centrifugated at 4000g, 4 °C for 30 min. The supernatant, containing the inactivated virus was concentrated using a 30 % (w/v) sucrose cushion prepared in 1 × NaCl-Tris-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (NTE) buffer consisting of 1 M NaCl, 100 mM Tris-HCl, and 10 mM EDTA in water for injection with the pH adjusted to 7.4. Samples were centrifugated at 25000 rpm in an SW 32 Ti rotor, 4 °C for 2 h and the pellets were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) (Gibco). Virus splitting was performed with 1 % (v/v) Triton X-100 (Fisher Bioreagents). To remove the detergent, samples were incubated with 0.2–0.3 g of Bio-Beads SM-2 (Bio-Rad) per milliliter of inactivated split virus. Total protein concentration in supernatants was determined using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad). Inactivated split virus vaccine aliquots were stored at −80 °C until use.

Recombinant proteins

Recombinant headless miniH3 from influenza virus strain A/Wisconsin/67/2005 (H3N2) was produced in High Five cells and purified from cell culture supernatant using a Ni2+–nitrilotriacetic acid resin chromatography [33]. Purified protein was used as a target antigen in enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

ELISA

96-well Immulon 4HBX plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated with recombinant protein at a concentration of 2 μg/ml (50 μl per well) in PBS with a pH of 7.4 and incubated O/N at 4 °C. The following day, the plates were washed three times with PBS containing 0.1 % (v/v) Tween 20 (PBS-T). Subsequently, the plates were blocked by incubating them in a blocking solution composed of 3 % (v/v) goat serum and 0.5 % (w/v) nonfat dry milk in PBS-T for 1 h at room temperature (RT). After blocking, mouse serum was added into the first well at a 1:30 dilution (150 μl per well) and then serially diluted 1:3 in blocking solution. This mixture was incubated for 2 h at 20 °C. Following this incubation period, the plates were subjected to three washes with PBS-T. For quantification of total IgG, a 1:3000 dilution of sheep anti-mouse IgG (H&L) peroxidase conjugate (Rockland) in a blocking solution was applied. For development, 100 μl of σ-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride (OPD) substrate (SigmaFast OPD, Millipore Sigma) was added to each well. After a 10-min incubation, the reaction was stopped by adding 50 μl of 3 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) to each well. A Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek) was used to obtain optical density measurements at 490 nm (OD490). The titration curves were built based on the obtained OD490 values, areas under curves (AUCs) were calculated in R using the AUC() function from the DescTools library. Obtained AUC values were used for statistical analysis and visual representation.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were performed with 6- to 8-week-old female BALB/c mice (Jackson Laboratories) under protocols approved by the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Four weeks before vaccination, mice were inoculated with 50 μl of 105 plaque-forming units (PFU) of the chimeric B-cH5/1 virus to simulate preexisting immunity to the H1 stalk. Priming was performed intranasally under ketamine/xylazine anesthesia. Vaccination with cH15/3HK14N2HK14 split influenza virus vaccine was performed 4 weeks later in the right leg muscle with 50 μl of group 2 cHA split vaccine (1 μg of HA) prepared in PBS with or without the CpG 1018 adjuvant (Dynavax Technologies). Mice were boosted with cH4/3HK14N2HK14 split vaccine after another 4 weeks. Mice immunized with PBS (without B-cH5/1 virus priming) and mice immunized with an irrelevant protein (ovalbumin (OVA)) adjuvanted with CpG 1018 and primed with the B-cH5/1 virus) were used as negative controls. For the T-cell memory recall assay, mice were inoculated with 0.1 × 50 % lethal dose (LD50) of X-79 in 50 μl of PBS under ketamine/xylazine anesthesia.

Passive transfer experiments

For T-cell and serum transfer studies, 6- to 8-week-old female BALB/c were primed and boosted as described above. Six weeks after the second boost, blood and spleens were collected. Spleens were homogenized to obtain a single-cell suspension. Erythrocytes were removed using red blood cell (RBC) lysis solution (Biolegend). T-cells were enriched using a Pan T-Cell Isolation Kit and MACS Separator (Miltenyi Biotec). For passive transfer of T-cells, 107 T-lymphocytes in 100 μl were transferred to naïve mice via retroorbital injection. The serum was passively transferred at a volume of 200 μl via intraperitoneal injection. Three hours after the passive transfer, mice were challenged with 0.5 × LD50 or 0.1 × LD50 of X-79 in 50 μl of PBS under ketamine/xylazine anesthesia. Mice were euthanized on days 3 and 6 after the challenge, and lungs and bronchoalveolar lavage fluids (BALFs) were collected to assess viral titers and the concentrations of inflammatory cytokines.

T-cell depletion

CD4+ T-lymphocytes were depleted by sequential intraperitoneal administration of 25 μg GK1.5 anti-CD4 monoclonal antibodies in a volume of 200 μl on days 5, 4, and 3 before the challenge and on the day of the challenge with 0.1 × LD50 of the X-79 virus. The efficiency of the depletion was confirmed by flow cytometry.

Viral titers in the lungs

To collect the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, mice were euthanized, and lungs and trachea were exposed. An 18G needle attached to a 1 ml syringe was inserted into the trachea and the lungs were instilled with 1 ml of cold PBS. The fluid was aspirated and transferred into 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes. Samples were spun down at 1500 g for 5 min to remove the cells and debris, aliquoted, and frozen to −80 °C until use. To assess viral titers, BALFs were serially diluted at a 1:10 ratio in minimal essential medium (MEM). These diluted samples were subsequently incubated for 1 h on MDCK cells that had been seeded at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well on the previous day. After the 1h incubation period, an agarose overlay was applied to the cells. This overlay consisted of a final concentration of 0.64 % (w/v) agarose (Oxoid) in MEM supplemented with 2 mM l-glutamine, 0.1 % (w/v) sodium bicarbonate, 10 mM 2-(4-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazin-1-yl)ethanesulfonic (HEPES), penicillin (100 U/ml), streptomycin (100 μg/ml), 0.2 % (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA), L-(tosylamido-2-phenyl) ethyl chloromethyl ketone (TPCK)-treated trypsin (1 μg/ml), and 0.1 % (w/v) diethylaminoethyl-dextran. Subsequently, the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h, and visible plaques were counted after fixation with 3.7 % (v/v) formaldehyde in PBS, followed by visualization through immunostaining. All virus titers are reported in units of log10 plaque-forming units (PFU) per milliliter. To measure viral titers in lung homogenates, harvested lungs were disrupted (2 cycles, 10 s/cycle) using tubes with zirconium beads (Andwin Scientific) and 1 ml of PBS in a BeadBlaster 24 Microtube Homogenizer (Eppendorf). Samples were centrifuged to remove the cell debris and the supernatants were titrated in the manner described above.

Flow cytometry

For the collection of lung samples, mice were humanely euthanized, and their chest cavities were opened. The right ventricle was perfused with 10 ml of cold PBS. The lungs were processed using gentleMACS™ Octo Dissociator for tissue homogenization and Lung Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) for preparation of single-cell suspensions. After digestion, the tissue was filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer (BD Biosciences). Spleens and lymph nodes (inguinal and popliteal pooled together) were mechanically disrupted using pestle homogenizers designed for 1.5-ml tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then filtered similarly. Erythrocytes were lysed using RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To stop the lysis reaction, 10 ml of a solution containing 2 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) in PBS was added to the tubes. The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged for 8 min at 400g and 4 °C and the pellets were reconstituted in 5 ml of complete media (Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (RPMI 1640) supplemented with 10 % (v/v) FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin). Cell count and viability were assessed with a C-Chip™ Disposable Hemacytometers (Incyto) using a 0.4 % (v/v) Trypan blue solution to discriminate between live and dead cells. Subsequently, 2 × 106 cells per sample were seeded in 96-well cell culture-treated plates for antigen-specific stimulation to analyze T-cellular immune responses or were immediately subjected to staining with fluorescently labeled antibodies to analyze the percentages of different innate and adaptive immune cell populations in the lungs.

For the analysis of T- and B-cell populations in the spleen and lymph nodes, cells were stained with antibody cocktail, including the following antibodies: CD38-AF488 (0.125 μl, BioLegend), PD-1-PE/Dazzle (0.5 μl, BioLegend), GL7-APC (0.25 μl, BioLegend), CD185-BV421 (0.5 μl, BioLegend), CD3-BV711 (0.5 μl, BioLegend), B220-BV785 (0.25 μl, BioLegend), CD4-PerCP/Cy5.5 (0.25 μl, Tonbo Biosciences).

For the analysis of antigen-specific T-cellular immune responses, cells were stimulated with a peptide pool library containing 15 amino acid peptides with an 11 amino acid overlap, at a concentration of 5 μg/ml. These peptides covered the entire sequences of HA and NA proteins of the A/Perth/16/2009 (H3N2) influenza virus strain (NR-19266 for HA and NR-19267 for NA, BEI Resources), as well as the whole sequence of the NP protein of A/PR/8/34 (H1N1) virus (PepMix™, JPT). The stimulation was carried out at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 6 h in the presence of Brefeldin A (5 μg/ml; BioLegend), Monensin (2 μM, BioLegend), and costimulatory anti-CD28 antibodies (25 μg/ml; BioLegend). Negative control samples were incubated with all the aforementioned reagents except the specific antigens. Following stimulation, cells were stained using fluorescently labeled antibodies (0.5 μl per sample of CD3-BV711, 0.125 μl per sample of CD4-PerCP/Cy5.5, 0.25 μl per sample of CD8-BV785, 0.125 μl per sample of CD62L-APC/Cy7, and 0.25 μl per sample of CD44-PE/Cy7). Intracellular staining of cytokines was performed using the Cytofix/Cytoperm Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit (BD Biosciences) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Antibodies for tumor necrosis factor (TNF)α-AF488 (0.25 μl per sample), interferon (IFN)γ-BV605 (1 μl per sample), and interleukin (IL)-2–phycoerythrin (0.5 μl per sample) (BioLegend) were used to identify the corresponding cytokines. Statistical differences between groups were calculated by subtracting the percentage of cytokine-producing cells in non-stimulated samples from the corresponding values for peptide-stimulated samples. For the analysis of cell populations in the lungs, cells were stained with the following antibody cocktail: CD117-AF488 (0.25 μl BioLegend); Ly6C-PerCP/Cy5.5 (0.25 μl, BioLegend); CD103-APC (1 μl, BioLegend); Ly6G-AF700 (0.25 μl, BioLegend); CD11b-APC/Cy7 (0.25 μl, BD Bioscience); CD64-BV421 (1.5 μl, BioLegend); CD11c-BV605 (1 μl, BioLegend); MHC-II (IA/IE)-BV650 (0.25 μl, BioLegend); CD24-BV711(0.25 μl, BioLegend); CD45-BV785 (0.125 μl, BioLegend); FCeRI-PE (0.5 μl, BioLegend); SiglecF-PE/CF594 (0.5 μl, BD Bioscience). The gating strategy is shown in the supplementary materials (SFig. 1). Data were collected using an Attune flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed using FlowJo_v10.8.1 software.

Cytokine analysis

A mouse LEGENDplex kit (Biolegend) was used to measure the concentration of proinflammatory and Th1/Th2/Th17-associated cytokines in BALFs or lung homogenates in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Data were collected using an Attune flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and processed with LEGENDplex™ data analysis software (https://legendplex.qognit.com).

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded left lung tissues obtained from mice were cut into 5 μm sections and stained by the Biorepository and Pathology Core (ISMMS). The presence of X-79 NP antigen was probed with an influenza A virus NP polyclonal antibody at a 1:5000 dilution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Briefly, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed using a VENTANA DISCOVERY ULTRA automated slide staining instrument (Roche, Basilea, Switzerland). Single staining (automatic or semi-automatic) was performed using the selected primary antibody and the Discovery OMNIMap anti-host-HRP (Roche) as secondary antibody. The signal was obtained using Discovery Chromo-Map DAB RUO (760–2513) (depicted in brown signal). Tissues were counterstained with hematoxylin to visualize the nuclei. Whole tissue sections on the slide were converted into high-resolution digital data using a NanoZoomer S210 Digital slide scanner (Hamamatsu). The HALO image analysis platform was used for quantitative tissue analysis (Indica Labs, Inc.), using a random forest algorithm classifier. Multiplex IHC module and color deconvolution were used to separate chromogenic stains together with nuclei segmentation to set up the system for quantitative analysis and determination of unsupervised pixelwise H-score values.

Statistical analysis

Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s correction for multiple comparisons in RStudio software (R version 4.2.0, RStudio Inc). Differences of P-values equal to or less than 0.05 were considered as statistically significant.

Results

Vaccination with cHA split vaccines stimulates the formation of follicular helper T-cells (Tfh) and germinal center B-cells (GCB) in spleens and draining lymph nodes

BALB/c mice were primed with chimeric B-cH5/1 virus to mimic preexisting anti-HA stalk immunity in humans which is mostly group 1 focused (Fig. 1a). Four weeks later, mice were sequentially vaccinated with cH15/3HK14N2HK14 cHA split influenza vaccine and boosted with cH4/3HK14N2HK14 cHA influenza vaccine after another 4 weeks (Fig. 1b). On day 14 after the second boost, spleens, popliteal, and iliac lymph nodes were collected to assess the percentage of T follicular helper (Tfh) and germinal center B (GCB) cells (Fig. 2). Immunization with split/CpG and split vaccines as well as the irrelevant protein (ovalbumin, OVA) induced a substantial increase in the level of Tfh cells in the draining lymph nodes compared to the PBS control (Fig. 2a, b). A tendency toward an increase in the percentage of GCB cells was also observed in the immunized groups. However, only the mice that received the split/CpG vaccine demonstrated a statistically significant difference with the PBS control. The presence of CpG 1018 adjuvant stimulated the formation of Tfh cells in the spleen. In contrast, no significant changes were observed in the split group and in the animals that received OVA instead of the vaccine (Fig. 2c). The increase in the percentage of GCB and Tfh cellular subpopulations was associated with the generation of an antigen-specific IgG-mediated humoral immune response in the mice that received split influenza virus vaccine with or without CpG 1018 adjuvant (Fig. 2d).

Vaccination with cHA split vaccines induces an HA-specific systemic T-cellular immune response

To assess the longevity of the anti-HA T-cellular immune response, mice were sacrificed 6 weeks after the boost. The presence of T-cellular immunity and the Th1/Th2/Th17 polarization were analyzed using intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) and a multiplex cytokine assay (Fig. 3). Prime-boost vaccination with cHA vaccines induced the formation of CD4+ T-cellular immune responses lasting for at least 6 weeks after the boost (Fig. 3a). Most of the antigen-specific T-cells were located in the spleen and consisted of IFNγ− IL2+TNFα− single-producers. Polyfunctional IFNγ− IL2+TNFα+ and IFNγ+IL2+TNFα+ subpopulations were also detected but at a lesser frequency. In the split/CpG group, the overall frequency of polyfunctional T-cells was significantly higher compared to the split-only, OVA/CpG, and PBS groups (Fig. 3b, c.). The HA-specific T-cellular immune response in the lungs was significantly lower compared to that in the spleen. However, the split/CpG group was shown to form polyfunctional IFNγ− IL2+TNFα+ and IFNγ+IL2+TNFα+ T-lymphocytes. No significant increase above the background cytokine production was shown for CD8+ T-cells in both spleens and lungs (SFig. 2). To analyze the Th1/Th2/Th17-polarization, splenocytes from the vaccinated animals were stimulated with an overlapping peptide library covering the H3 protein sequence from A/Perth/16/2009 (H3N2) influenza virus for 24 h. After the stimulation, the concentration of the cytokines in the supernatants was measured using the LEGENDplex multiplex assay. As shown in Fig. 3d, prime-boost vaccination with a non-adjuvanted split vaccine led to Th2 polarization of the T-cellular immune response as suggested by the elevated levels of Th2-associated cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13). The levels of Th1-associated IFNγ, IL-2, and TNFα and Th17-associated IL17A/F and IL-22 were similar in both split/CpG and split-only groups.

Vaccination with cHA split vaccines decreases the inflammatory response in the lungs after challenge with heterologous A/Philippines/2/1982 (H3N2; X-79) virus

To assess the impact of vaccination with cHAs on the immune response after the heterologous virus challenge, mice immunized as described above were infected with 0.1 × LD50 X-79 influenza virus. The concentrations of proinflammatory cytokines as well as viral loads were measured in BALFs on day 3 and day 6 after the challenge. The dynamics of different cellular populations and the local T-cellular immune response were analyzed in lung tissue. The percentage of immunopathology-associated innate immune cell populations such as neutrophils, exudative macrophages, and inflammatory monocytes was substantially increased in challenged mice compared to unchallenged control animals (non-vaccinated, non-challenged) (Fig. 4a). In the split/CpG group, the recruitment of neutrophils, exudative macrophages, and inflammatory monocytes on day 3 was less prominent when compared to the other experimental groups, particularly the non-vaccinated PBS control group. By day 6, the neutrophil levels had equalized across all four groups due to a reduction in their percentage in the PBS group. However, the differences in the levels of inflammatory monocytes between the split/CpG group and the other groups persisted. Furthermore, the percentage of exudative macrophages was significantly higher in the PBS group when compared to the split/CpG group. On day 6, a statistically significant increase in the levels of NK cells and mast cells was observed in the PBS group. In mice vaccinated with the split vaccine without adjuvant, a substantial increase in the percentage of eosinophils was evident on day 6 following the virus challenge. The accumulation of these cells in the lungs during respiratory infections is associated with Th-2 polarization of the immune response. Vaccination substantially reduced virus proliferation in the lungs and infection-induced lung pathology (Fig. 4b–d). The combination with the CpG 1018 adjuvant slightly increased the efficiency of viral clearance but did not have a substantial effect on the lung pathology score since both groups that received the split vaccine demonstrated a low level of infection-associated lung damage (Fig. 4c, d).

Local T-cellular immune responses in the lungs were observed primarily on day 6 after infection and were dominated by H3-specific CD4+ T-lymphocytes. The formation of polyfunctional T-cell subpopulations (TNFα+IL2+, TNFα+IFNγ+, and TNFα+IL2+IFNγ+) was shown in the split/CpG group whereas the group that received the non-adjuvanted split vaccine was characterized by the presence of monofunctional IL2+ T-cells. A significant increase in the percentage of this population was observed on day 6 after infection in response to H3 and N2 peptide library stimulation (Fig. 6a, b).

Systemic T-cellular immune responses in the spleen were characterized by a broader range of antigen specificity and a higher percentage of cytokine-producing T-cells (Fig. 7). The most prominent T-cellular immune response associated with the formation of polyfunctional T-cells was generated in the split/CpG group in response to restimulation with the peptides covering the sequences of H3, N2, and NP proteins, being H3-specific response predominant. The tendency toward the increase in the level of antigen-specific T-cells compared to the control groups was also observed in mice that received the non-adjuvanted split vaccine.

The contribution of humoral and T-cellular immune response to the overall heterologous protection in passive transfer and T-cell depletion experiments

To examine the contributions of antibodies and T cells elicited by cHA split vaccines to protection against heterologous challenge with the X-79 virus, we conducted passive transfer and CD4+ T-cell depletion experiments (Fig. 8a, b). For the passive transfer study, serum and splenocytes were collected from immunized mice at day 42 post-boost. T cells were isolated from the splenocytes using a magnetic separator, pooled, and transferred into naïve recipients. Corresponding serum samples from vaccinated animals were also pooled and administered intraperitoneally to a different set of recipient mice. Two hours after serum or T-cell transfer, the recipient mice were challenged intranasally with 0.5 or 0.1 × LD50 of the X-79 virus. BALFs were collected 6 days post-challenge to assess proinflammatory cytokine levels. Lungs were collected 3 and 6 days post challenge to assess viral titers and innate immune cell populations.

Mice receiving sera from donors vaccinated with the split/CpG vaccine exhibited statistically significant improvements in body weight dynamics compared to PBS controls following the challenge with 0.5 × LD50 of the X-79 virus. In contrast, no improvement in weight loss dynamics was observed in mice that received sera from animals vaccinated with the non-adjuvanted split vaccine (Fig. 8c). Passive transfer of sera from split/CpG vaccine donors also reduced viral replication in the lungs of mice challenged with 0.1 × LD50 of the X-79 virus. Furthermore, levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNFα, IL-6, and MCP-1, were decreased in mice that received sera from both split/CpG and non-adjuvanted split-vaccinated donors. Interestingly, serum from non-adjuvanted split vaccine donors reduced proinflammatory cytokine levels but did not significantly lower viral titers (Fig. 8d, e).

Passive transfer of T cells had no significant effect on body weight dynamics after a challenge with 0.5 × LD50 of the X-79 virus or on viral replication after a challenge with 0.1 × LD50 of the same virus. However, an increase in Th2-associated cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13) was observed in the BALFs of mice that received T cells from donors immunized with the non-adjuvanted split vaccine (Fig. 8c–e).

A CD4+ T-cell depletion experiment further confirmed that T cells do not directly contribute to heterologous protection against the X-79 virus. Viral titers did not differ between CD4+ T-cell-depleted and nondepleted groups (Fig. 7f). Both the split/CpG and split groups showed reduced viral titers compared to the OVA/CpG and PBS control groups. However, CD4+ T-cell depletion altered innate immune cell dynamics and cytokine production in the lungs of infected animals. As shown in the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) biplots (Fig. 8g), non-T-cell depleted mice vaccinated with adjuvanted or non-adjuvanted split vaccine displayed pronounced differences in postvaccinal immune responses to the virus infection. Specifically, split-only-vaccinated animals had elevated levels of Th2-associated cytokines (IL-4, IL-5) and a higher percentage of mast cells and basophils in the lungs. CD4+ T-cell depletion diminished these differences between the split/CpG and non-adjuvanted split groups (Fig. 8f, SFig. 3).

Discussion

cHA constructs are a promising vaccine candidates that have been proven to be immunogenic and protective in multiple preclinical studies in different animal models [4,6,10,11,34,35]. Of note, group 1 cHA vaccines have been successfully evaluated in different clinical trials [7–9]. Recently, we demonstrated that group 2 cHA vaccines combined with CpG 1018 adjuvant induce broadly cross-protective antibodies and antigen-specific CD4+ T-cellular responses. In the present study, we performed an in-depth characterization of the postvaccinal immune response to the sequential vaccination with group 2 cHA split vaccine with or without CpG 1018 adjuvant.

Split cHA vaccine was shown to induce the formation of GCB and Tfh lymphocytes in the draining lymph nodes. These cellular populations play an essential role in antibody class switching and affinity maturation. Higher levels of GCB have been shown to correlate with improved protection in different influenza virus challenge models [36–38]. In the present study, the combination of CpG 1018 and group 2 cHA split vaccines increased the levels of GCB in the draining lymph nodes compared to the non-adjuvanted split vaccine. This finding is in line with the previously published data on the immunogenic properties of the CpG 1018 adjuvant [36–38] and can help to explain the higher cross-protective potential of the split/CpG vaccine compared to the non-adjuvanted formulation [12].

Both adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted split cHA vaccines induced the formation of Tfh cells in the lymph nodes. However, only the CpG 1018-adjuvanted formulation led to a significant increase in the percentage of this population in the spleen. Thus, the combination with this adjuvant stimulates the systemic T-cellular immune response which was further confirmed by the analysis of cytokine production after antigen-specific restimulation with overlapping peptides. The ability of the cHA vaccines to induce a T-cellular immune response was shown previously [12,39–41]. However, the role of the T-cellular immune response after a challenge with a phylogenetically-distant virus is poorly investigated. It is widely accepted that CD8+ T-cells contribute in a major way to the anti-influenza virus response and to cross-protection [17,42–49]. The role of CD4+ T-cells, on the other hand, is controversial. Some evidence suggests that depleting CD4+ T cells after vaccination impairs the recall response of CD8+ T cells [50]. McKinstry et al. showed that the adoptive transfer of CD4+ T cells did not directly reduce viral loads but increased survival. Interestingly, the protective effect in the T-cell passive transfer experiments was associated with Th1 or Th17 polarization of the T-cellular immune response, whereas Th2-polarized cells did not protect mice from challenge [50]. Mice vaccinated with FluMist showed a 50 % survival rate after CD4+ T-cell depletion and subsequent lethal viral challenge compared with a lower 25 % survival rate following CD8+ T-cell depletion [51]. However, other research showed that CD4+ T cell depletion did not affect protection in mice vaccinated with whole inactivated virus vaccines despite the presence of IFNγ-producing CD4+ T cells post-vaccination [52]. In summary, based on current research, the role of CD4+ T cells in the post-vaccination immune response to the influenza virus encompasses the following mechanisms: facilitation of an early antibody response and cytokine production, the recruitment of CD8+ T lymphocytes and innate immune cells to the infected tissues, and stimulation of the differentiation of long-lived memory CD8+ T cells [53,54]. Additionally, memory CD4+ T cells collaborate with B cells to enhance the secretion of virus-neutralizing antibodies. The synergistic effect of non-neutralizing antibodies and IFNγ produced by CD4+ T-cells was shown to be essential for cross-protection against homologous and heterologous influenza virus challenges: IFNγ downregulates the expression of IgG1-binding FcγRIIb receptors on macrophages which leads to the enhancement of the signals from IgG2b/c antibodies with higher antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) effector potential [55]. Finally, memory CD4+ T cells can clear viruses in an antigen-specific manner through mechanisms involving IFN-γ and perforin [50].

In the present study, we have demonstrated the formation of polyfunctional CD4+ T-cells in both lungs and spleens after sequential vaccination with group 2 cHA split vaccines with and without the CpG 1018 adjuvant. The antigen-specific T-cells were present in mice for at least 6 weeks after the secondary immunization and participated in the immune response to infection with the heterologous X-79 virus. In the mice immunized with the non-adjuvanted split cHA vaccine, CD4+ T-cells were responsible for the production of Th2-associated cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) in response to the viral challenge and the recruitment of eosinophils and basophils to the lung tissue. Immunization with split/CpG cHA vaccine substantially reduced the accumulation of neutrophils, exudative macrophages, and inflammatory monocytes in mouse lungs after challenge with X-79 virus. Neutrophils and macrophages establish the first line of antiviral defense during influenza virus infection [56,57]. These cells support the pathogen clearance by the phagocytosis of viral particles and infected cells. Also, they produce a broad spectrum of proinflammatory factors, inducing the influx of different cell populations, including the antigen-specific T-lymphocytes, to the lung tissue [58]. On the other hand, excessive accumulation of neutrophils, exudative macrophages, and inflammatory monocytes is associated with immunopathology and lung injury during the influenza virus infection [59–61]. T-cell depletion experiments showed that the elimination of CD4+ T-lymphocytes increases the percentage of alveolar macrophages and neutrophils in the lungs. However, no direct impact on viral replication was associated with the T-cellular immune response as was also confirmed by the passive transfer experiments. Thus, the observed protection against challenge with the X-79 virus is primarily mediated by antigen-specific antibodies. In a previous study, we demonstrated that the split cHA vaccine, particularly when formulated with CpG 1018, elicited an IgG antibody response targeting a broad range of both group 1 and group 2 HA antigens. However, these antibodies did not show detectable neutralization activity against the X-79 influenza virus, which was also used for the challenge experiments in the current work [12]. Non-neutralizing antibodies (nnAbs) have been demonstrated to play a significant role in protecting against influenza virus infections in mice despite not preventing the virus from entering cells [55,62–64]. These antibodies can engage immune effector functions such as ADCC and ADCP. These functions involve natural killer cells and macrophages that eliminate infected cells via Fc-receptor-dependent mechanisms [65,66]. Additionally, nnAbs can activate the classic complement pathway, leading to the formation of the membrane attack complex, which induces membrane permeabilization in opsonized viral particles or infected cells [67].

In the present study, the T-cellular immune response on its own as induced by the cHA split vaccine was not sufficient to protect mice against challenge with the X-79 virus. However, passively transferred antibodies reduced the body weight loss and decreased the viral replication in the lungs of infected animals, suggesting the predominant role of the nnAbs in the heterologous protection induced by cHA split vaccines. This data does not exclude the possibility of a synergistic effect of CD4+ T-cells and antibodies described earlier in the work by Shibuya et al. [55]. Future studies will clarify the mechanisms of protection mediated by nnAbs in the context of split cHA-induced postvaccination immunity. Another limitation of this study was the use of BALB/c mice, which are less efficient at generating a robust T-cell immune response compared to other mouse strains, such as C57BL/6 mice. The BALB/c model was selected to align with a previously published study on the cHA vaccine [12]. The results obtained in this study show that the observed cross-protection is achieved without the direct antiviral activity of antigen-specific T cells. However, it remains possible that the cross-protective effects of T-cell immunity induced by the cHA vaccine could be more effectively demonstrated using an alternative mouse model.
