fMRI-guided V1-targeted rTMS improves depressive symptoms in adolescents and young adults with bipolar disorder: a double-blind randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xudong Zhao, Hetong Zhou, Xiaomei Zhang, Ruoyu Tang, Yimei Gan, Tongjie Zhuang, Yiyi Zhu, Zeyu Qin, Yiqing Chen, Yaoyang Fu, Danhua Zhang, Le Xu, Shiliang Wang, Zhongxia Shen, Shaohua Hu, Minmin Wang

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBipolar depression (BD-D) in adolescents and young adults is associated with disrupted neural circuits underlying affective regulation, particularly those involving the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC). Despite the promise of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) as a non-invasive intervention, effective targeting strategies that engage these dysfunctional circuits remain insufficiently explored. This study investigates the clinical efficacy of a novel rTMS protocol targeting the primary visual cortex (V1) node of the V1-OFC functional circuit in adolescents and young adults with BD-D.MethodsWe conducted a double-blind randomized controlled trial. Fifty-two adolescents and young adults BD-D participants were randomized to active rTMS group (10 Hz, 100% RMT) or sham rTMS group (20% RMT) targeting the V1 region that exhibited the strongest functional connectivity with the OFC (MNI: − 12, − 81, 6). rTMS was administered over 3 weeks (5 sessions/week, 15 sessions in total), with all participants receiving adjunctive lurasidone (40–80 mg/day). The primary outcome was the change in depressive symptoms measured by the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) at baseline, week 3, and week 8. Background

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a severe, chronic psychiatric illness characterized by recurrent episodes of mania or hypomania and depression [1–3]. In adolescents and young adults, bipolar depression (BD-D) not only disrupts emotional regulation but also impairs neurodevelopment and psychosocial functioning [4, 5]. Despite pharmacological advances [6, 7], the depressive symptoms of BD-D remain challenging to manage, highlighting the urgent need for effective, rapid-onset, and well-tolerated therapeutic interventions.

The orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) plays a pivotal role in emotion evaluation and reward-based decision-making and has been increasingly recognized as a key node in the pathophysiology of mood disorders [8, 9]. Neuroimaging studies have revealed OFC dysfunction in BD across mood states, with abnormal connectivity and activation patterns associated with both manic and depressive symptoms [10–12]. Several preliminary trials have shown that repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) targeting the OFC can produce rapid and substantial symptom improvement in patients with treatment-resistant depression, including those who failed to respond to traditional dorsolateral prefrontal cortex stimulation [13, 14]. One notable study reported promising response and remission rates in preliminary trials of 1 Hz OFC-rTMS in patients with major depression who were unresponsive to conventional treatment protocols [15]. Despite these promising findings, direct OFC stimulation poses technical and physiological challenges due to its ventral location. Patients often report adverse effects such as visual phosphenes, which may reduce compliance and limit clinical applicability [16]. Moreover, even when direct stimulation is achieved, the magnetic field often spreads to adjacent superficial cortical pathways rather than selectively engaging OFC circuits, thereby limiting the focality and clinical applicability of this approach.

To overcome these limitations, circuit-based neuromodulation targeting functionally connected cortical areas has become an attractive alternative [17–19]. Rather than directly stimulating deep or ventral regions such as the OFC—which poses technical and tolerability challenges—indirect modulation through upstream cortical nodes provides a feasible and potentially safer strategy. Prior work from our group has demonstrated the feasibility of targeting interconnected regions through primary visual cortex (V1)-mediated stimulation [20, 21]. The V1 region, located on the occipital surface, is easily accessible for TMS and exhibits robust functional connectivity with several higher-order brain regions involved in emotional processing, including the OFC [22, 23]. Anatomical and physiological evidence supports the existence of fronto-occipital pathways and functional coupling between the OFC and visual cortices, which may mediate bidirectional influences on affective perception through higher-order visual areas and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculi [24–26]. Human fMRI studies further support the presence of strong functional coupling between prefrontal and visual cortices during affective perception tasks [27]. Therefore, stimulation of V1 may offer a novel, non-invasive route to modulate OFC-related mood circuits.

Based on this evidence, we hypothesize that high-frequency rTMS over V1 is expected to modulate OFC-coupled valuation circuits via fronto-occipital connectivity, thereby influencing the broader emotional regulatory network. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy and explore the underlying neural mechanisms of fMRI-guided V1-targeted rTMS in adolescents and young adults with BD-D. Using a double-blind, randomized controlled design, we defined the primary clinical endpoint as the change in Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) score at week 3, immediately following completion of the intervention, with durability of effect further assessed at week 8. Clinical symptomatology and resting-state functional connectivity were evaluated to elucidate potential circuit-level modulation effects.

Methods

Trial design

This study was designed as a double-blind, randomized controlled trial conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Hangzhou, China). Randomization codes and group assignments were generated using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Each participant was randomly assigned a unique code corresponding to either the active or sham stimulation group based on a pre-generated randomization list. Participants had an equal probability of being allocated to either group. Both participants and clinical evaluators responsible for assessing emotional symptoms remained blinded to group allocation throughout the study to ensure unbiased data collection.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, participants were randomly divided into two groups: Group A (Active rTMS) and Group B (Sham rTMS). In the active rTMS group, participants received 10 Hz high-frequency rTMS delivered at 100% of their resting motor threshold (RMT), guided by individual MRI data. The stimulation target was the V1 cortical node with the strongest functional connectivity to the OFC, thereby indirectly modulating the V1-OFC circuit. In the sham rTMS group, participants received sham stimulation at the same anatomical target, but at a subtherapeutic intensity of 20% RMT, delivered with the same frequency (10 Hz). Treatment allocation awareness was assessed among participants, and blinding efficacy was evaluated to confirm adequate masking between the active and sham conditions. A robotic navigation system (Black Dolphin Navigation Robot, Solide Medical Sci. & Tech.Co., Ltd., Xi’an, China) was used to ensure consistent coil positioning across sessions.

Clinical trial design and intervention paradigm. A Participants underwent a three-week multimodal intervention using transcranial magnetic stimulation. Each week consisted of daily sessions, totaling 15 sessions. Emotional and imaging examinations were conducted before and after the intervention. B Flow diagram of participant selection and intervention

rTMS sessions were conducted once daily, 5 days per week, for a total of 3 consecutive weeks (15 sessions in total, 3000 pulses per day, the total number of pulses is 45,000). Each session lasted 20 min. During the trial, all participants received lurasidone monotherapy as the standardized pharmacological treatment, and efforts were made to maintain a single-medication regimen throughout the intervention period. Both the active and sham rTMS groups followed an identical titration schedule: lurasidone was initiated at 20 mg/day during days 1–6, increased to 40 mg/day from day 7 onward, and subsequently adjusted to a target dose of 40–80 mg/day based on clinical response and tolerability. Short-acting benzodiazepines or β-blockers were permitted for transient symptoms such as insomnia or anxiety, following clinician discretion.

Emotional symptoms were assessed using standardized rating scales at multiple time points during the intervention. Adverse events were monitored and recorded throughout the trial. Neuroimaging assessments were conducted at baseline (week 0, W0) and post-treatment (week 3, W3).

Participants

This study was conducted at the outpatient psychiatry department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Preparatory work, including training for consistency in clinical rating scales, began in September 2022, and formal participant recruitment commenced in November 2022. The study continued until June 30, 2023, with participants enrolled from the outpatient clinic. An a priori power analysis was performed based on previous rTMS studies reporting a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.6) for clinical improvement. Assuming α = 0.05 and 1–β = 0.80, the required sample size was estimated at 45 participants. Considering a 20–25% attrition rate, the target enrollment was set at approximately 55 participants. As shown in Fig. 1B, a total of 75 patients were initially enrolled. After screening and initial dropout, 43 participants completed the rTMS intervention (23 in the active rTMS group and 20 in the sham rTMS group). Of these, 37 participants completed the full trial protocol, including all follow-up assessments (22 in the active rTMS group and 15 in the sham rTMS group).

All procedures involving human participants were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Reference ID: IIT20220081C-R1) and adhered to the ethical standards of national and institutional committees as well as the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study. This trial was prospectively registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (Registration number: NCT05929183).

The inclusion criteria included the following: (i) 14–28 years of age; (ii) diagnosis of BD depressive phase established by two professional psychiatrists, in accordance with the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) and DSM-5 diagnostic criteria; (iii) the score of the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) ≤ 6; (iv) Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale score between 12 and 30; (v) right-handedness; (vi) education ≥ 9 years.

The exclusion criteria included the following: (i) history of severe somatic or brain organic diseases and craniocerebral trauma; (ii) abnormal brain structure or any MRI contraindications were found by magnetic resonance examination; (iii) those who do not cooperate or cannot effectively complete the experiment; (iv) drug, alcohol, or other psychoactive substance abusers; (v) pregnant, lactating, or planned pregnancy; (vi) severe suicidal ideation and behavior; (vii) ECT or rTMS treatment was performed within 6 months.

Treatment protocol of navigated rTMS

Based on resting-state functional connectivity analysis, the strength of the functional connection between the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC, MNI coordinates: [34, 36, − 12]) and the primary visual cortex (V1, MNI coordinates: [− 12, − 81, 6]) was found to be significantly associated with baseline depression severity, as measured by the MADRS (Pearson correlation coefficient R = − 0.4258, p = 0.002). Given this significant negative correlation, the V1 node at MNI [− 12, − 81, 6]—the site of strongest connectivity with the OFC—was selected as the stimulation target for rTMS.

Participants in the active stimulation group received high-frequency rTMS at 10 Hz, delivered at 100% of their individual RMT. Each session lasted for 20 min and included 60 stimulation trains per session. Each train consisted of 50 pulses (5 s duration), with inter-train intervals of 15 s, totaling 3000 pulses per session and 45,000 pulses over the entire 3-week treatment course (15 sessions in total). Participants in the sham group received the same stimulation schedule and auditory feedback, but at a subthreshold intensity of 20% RMT, insufficient to induce neuromodulatory effects. All stimulation parameters (frequency, train number, inter-train interval, coil positioning) were kept consistent between the two groups to maintain effective blinding.

All rTMS sessions were delivered using the Black Dolphin Navigation Robot System (Model S-50, Solide Medical Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Xi’an, China), equipped with a figure-of-eight coil (Solide Brain Control Medical Technology, Xi’an, China). The rTMS procedures utilized the NTK-TMS-II 300 TMS device (JiangXi Brain Modulation Technology Co., Ltd., Nanchang, China). This robot-assisted navigation platform is capable of sub-millimeter precision in coil positioning. Prior to stimulation, each participant’s high-resolution structural MRI data was coregistered to the standard brain space using anterior commissure–posterior commissure (AC–PC) alignment. The imaging data were used to construct individualized three-dimensional models of the scalp and cortex. The stimulation site was projected onto the scalp using a visual positioning system, a rigid head fixation mask, and dedicated planning software. Coil orientation and position were dynamically adjusted in real time by the navigation system to ensure accurate delivery of stimulation at the prescribed target and angle. The detailed procedural workflow can be found in the Additional file 1: Section 1.

Imaging

Imaging acquisition

Imaging was performed on a 3.0 Tesla scanner (GE SIGNA) with a standard whole-head coil at the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The participants were asked to lie on the scanner with their eyes closed. Foam cushions were positioned on either side of the head to restrict head motion, and earplugs were used to reduce the noise. High-resolution 3D anatomical images were captured through T1-weighted magnetization prepared rapid acquisition gradientecho (MPRAGE) sequence. The parameters used were: TR = 7.1 ms; TE = 2.9 ms; FOV = 260 × 260mm2; matrix size = 256 × 256; slices = 146; slice thickness = 1 mm; flip angle = 8°. Functional images were acquired by the echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence, with the following parameters: TR = 1800 ms; TE = 30 ms; FOV = 240 × 240mm2, gap = 0.8 mm; matrix size = 64 × 64; slices = 28; slice thickness = 4 mm; time points = 180.

fMRI analysis

MATLAB (R2021b, MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) software and Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF) Advanced Edition V9.0 were used to preprocess the data. The data from the first 10 time points for each participant were removed to avoid scanner instability and allow subjects to adapt to scanner noise. The key preprocessing steps included slice-timing correction, head movement correction, spatial standardization, spatial smoothing (6 mm × 6 mm × 6 mm Gaussian kernel), removal of linear drift, low-frequency filtering (0.01 ~ 0.08 Hz) and removal of physiological confounding factors (the 24 head motion parameters, whole brain signal, white matter signal and cerebrospinal fluid signal). To ensure data quality, any participant with maximum head motion exceeding 2.5 mm of translation or 2.5° of rotation was excluded, and all included participants met this stringent criterion.

Based on preprocessing, we selected the V1 (x = -12, y = -81, z = 6, radius = 8 mm) as seed region for FC analysis and analyzed the correlation between seed region and brain areas at the voxel level to observe the changes in functional connectivity with other brain regions after intervention. The mean time series of each ROI was extracted. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated between the mean time series of each ROI and each voxel’s time series of the whole brain to generate correlation maps. Finally, Fisher’s r-to-z transform was applied for each correlation coefficient to fit the normal distribution.

Clinical assessment

All clinical assessments were conducted by trained research personnel who underwent standardized rater reliability training to ensure consistency and minimize inter-rater variability. Throughout the treatment period, all adverse events were monitored and documented in accordance with established safety protocols.

Depressive symptoms were evaluated using three validated scales: the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale, the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24), and the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology – Self-Report (QIDS-SR). Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale.

The MADRS was administered at five time points: baseline (week 0), and at the end of weeks 1, 2, and 3 (during the intervention), and week 8 (follow-up). HAMD-24 and Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA) were evaluated at baseline and at the end of weeks 1, 2, and 3. QIDS-SR was self-administered at baseline (day 0) and daily on days 1, 2, and 3 (D1, D2, and D3) after the initiation of rTMS treatment to capture early symptom changes, and again at week 8 to assess longer-term treatment outcomes.

The primary clinical outcomes were defined as the change in MADRS scores from baseline to each post-treatment time point, with particular focus on changes at week 3 and week 8. The secondary outcomes included changes in HAMD-24, QIDS-SR, and HAMA scores between baseline and post-treatment, as well as alterations in whole-brain functional connectivity with the V1 seed region, as derived from resting-state functional MRI.

This multimodal clinical and neuroimaging assessment protocol was designed to comprehensively capture both subjective and objective treatment effects, enabling an integrated analysis of behavioral outcomes and circuit-level neuromodulation.

Clinical outcome analysis

All statistical analyses of clinical scale data were conducted using SPSS 26.0. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For continuous variables in baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, independent sample t-tests were used for parameters with normal distribution, while Mann–Whitney U tests were applied to parameters with non-normal distribution. Clinical scale scores (MADRS, HAMD-24, QIDS-SR, HAMA) were analyzed using repeated-measures ANCOVA to examine within-group and between-group differences over time. Specifically, a two-way mixed-model ANCOVA was conducted for key time points, with group (active vs. sham) as the fixed factor and baseline score, age, and gender as covariates. Paired t-tests were used to investigate specific changes between baseline and post-treatment or follow-up assessments. To control for potential inflation of Type I error due to multiple comparisons, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to all post hoc tests. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

In addition, intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses were conducted including all randomized participants (n = 52). Longitudinal changes in clinical scale scores (MADRS, HAMD-24, QIDS-SR, and HAMA) were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with restricted maximum likelihood estimation, which allow inclusion of participants with incomplete follow-up data under the missing-at-random assumption. Fixed effects included group (active vs. sham), time (W0, W1, W2, W3, W8), and their interaction, with subject-specific random intercepts to account for within-subject correlations. Baseline score, age, years of education, sex, disease course, height and weight were included as covariates. Post hoc contrasts were adjusted using the FDR correction.

Resting-state fMRI statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB R2021b and the DPABI toolbox. Two-way mixed-model ANCOVA was performed using DPABI to examine the changes in FC before and after treatment between the active and sham rTMS stimulation groups, with age, gender, and years of education as covariates. Subsequently, the results of the ANCOVA were corrected for multiple comparisons using Gaussian Random Field (GRF) theory. This correction involved a voxel threshold of p < 0.005 and a cluster threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Clinicodemographic characteristics of patient

A total of 75 participants were initially recruited for this study. Of these, 23 were excluded due to failure to meet inclusion criteria or withdrawal of consent, resulting in 52 patients with bipolar disorder who were ultimately enrolled and randomly assigned in equal numbers to one of two treatment groups: Group A (active rTMS, n = 26) and Group B (sham rTMS, n = 26). Among these, 43 participants (Group A: n = 23; Group B: n = 20) completed all 15 planned rTMS sessions and clinical assessments at baseline (week 0, W0) and post-treatment (week 3, W3). However, only 37 participants completed the full 8-week protocol, including the final follow-up assessment (Group A: n = 22; Group B: n = 15), and were therefore included in the final analysis of clinical efficacy.

The clinicodemographic characteristics of these 37 participants—including age, sex distribution, and baseline severity of depressive symptoms—did not significantly differ between the two groups (all p > 0.05), indicating successful randomization and group comparability at baseline. Affective symptoms

Per-Protocol (Completer) Analysis

As shown in Fig. 2, among participants who completed all the study assessments, independent samples t-tests were conducted to compare changes in depressive symptom severity between the active rTMS and sham stimulation groups at multiple time points, as measured by the MADRS, QIDS-SR, and HAMD-24 scales.

Clinical intervention results. A Results for the HAMD-24. B Results for the HAMA. C Results for the MADRS. D Results for the QIDS-SR. Each data point represents participants with recorded scores at that time point. W0: week 0, W1: week 1, W2: week 2, W3: week 3, W8: week 8, D0: day 0, D1: day 1, D2: day 2, D3: day 3, ***p < 0.001

For the primary outcome, MADRS, a significant group difference emerged at the 8-week follow-up (Week 8), with the active rTMS group showing significantly lower scores compared to the sham group (t(35) = − 3.595, pFDR < 0.01). The mean difference in MADRS scores between groups was − 5.76 (95% CI [− 9.01, − 2.51]), indicating a greater reduction in depressive symptoms following active rTMS. No significant between-group differences were observed at baseline (W0) or at intermediate time points (weeks 1–3) (all p > 0.05).

For the QIDS-SR, a similar pattern was observed. At week 8, the active rTMS group demonstrated significantly greater symptom reduction compared to the sham group (t(35) = − 3.653, pFDR < 0.01), with a mean difference of − 4.65 (95% CI [− 7.24, − 2.07]). No significant group differences were found at baseline (Day 0) or during the early post-treatment assessments on days 1–3 (all p > 0.05).

For the HAMD-24, a significant group difference was detected earlier, at the end of week 3 (t(35) = − 3.921, pFDR < 0.01), with the active rTMS group exhibiting a greater reduction in depressive symptoms compared to the sham group. The mean difference was − 6.35 (95% CI [− 9.64, − 3.06]). No significant differences were observed between groups at baseline or other time points (all p > 0.05).

These per-protocol findings indicate that while early effects of rTMS may not be detectable within the first week, significant improvements in depressive symptoms become evident by Week 3 (HAMD-24) and are most pronounced by week 8 (MADRS and QIDS-SR).

Intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis

In the intention-to-treat analysis including all randomized participants (n = 52), linear mixed-effects models revealed significant group × time interactions were observed for MADRS (F4,175.78 = 7.49, p < 0.001), HAMD (F3,139.33 = 15.72, p < 0.001), and QIDS-SR (F4,185.33 = 8.94, p < 0.001), but not for HAMA (F3,141.36 = 0.15, p = 0.931). Post hoc comparisons with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction revealed that the active group had significantly lower scores than the sham group at week 8 for MADRS (mean difference = − 6.03, t167.98 = − 3.75, p < 0.001, q = 0.001) and QIDS-SR (mean difference = − 4.30, t82.99 = − 3.36, p = 0.001, q = 0.006), and at week 3 for HAMD (mean difference = − 6.01, t117.75 = − 3.85, p < 0.001, q < 0.001). No significant group differences were found at other time points for any scale, and no significant differences were observed for HAMA at any time point (all q > 0.05).

In contrast, the sham stimulation group showed a significant increase in FC between V1 and the left anterior cingulate gyrus (t(14) = 4.934, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.27) and a significant decrease in FC between V1 and the right superior occipital gyrus (t(14) = − 3.7, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = − 0.96) after treatment (Fig. 3B). Adverse effects

Adverse effects were mostly seen during and after active rTMS treatment. Of the 6 participants who experienced mild and temporary adverse effects included mild cephalgia, slight dizziness, nausea, transient earache, and pain at the site of stimulation. During treatment, no symptoms of mania were found through the monitoring of the YMRS. No severe adverse effects were reported in all participants.

Discussion

In this study, we propose a novel neuromodulation mode based on indirect, circuit-level modulation of the OFC via stimulation of the V1. Our findings provide promising clinical evidence that rTMS targeting the V1 node was associated with a time-dependent improvement in depressive symptoms in adolescents and young adults with bipolar depression. While a significant between-group difference was observed on the HAMD-24 at week 3, group differences on the MADRS and the self-reported QIDS-SR became statistically significant only at week 8 after FDR correction, indicating a delayed yet sustained antidepressant effect.

Interestingly, anxiety symptoms measured by the HAMA scale did not exhibit significant group-by-time interactions. This pattern suggests that the therapeutic effects of V1-targeted rTMS in the present study may be more specific to depressive symptom dimensions rather than generalized affective distress. The OFC is centrally involved in reward processing, valuation, and affective regulation—functions closely linked to depression—whereas anxiety symptoms are primarily mediated by amygdala-centered and extended limbic networks [28–30]. Thus, V1-OFC stimulation may preferentially influence depressive circuits without substantially affecting anxiety-related pathways [31], and highlights the potential specificity of the V1-OFC pathway in mediating antidepressant effects [26]. Future studies could explore circuit-specific targets for anxiety modulation and investigate whether combined or sequential interventions could address comorbid symptom domains.

Compared to direct OFC stimulation, targeting the V1-OFC circuit via V1 may offer unique advantages. While the OFC is increasingly recognized as a key node involved in emotion regulation, reward processing, and decision-making, its anatomical location near the orbit and eyes makes it a challenging direct stimulation target. Adverse effects such as visual phosphenes and increased intraocular pressure have been reported, particularly when stimulating ventral prefrontal regions, which may reduce tolerability and compliance—especially in adolescents and young adults [15, 16, 32]. By stimulating the functionally connected V1 node instead, we bypass these drawbacks while still modulating OFC-related circuits.

The observed antidepressant effects may be better interpreted within a framework of network-level modulation involving fronto-occipital functional coupling. Previous studies reported that the OFC and V1 are functionally linked through multisynaptic fronto-occipital pathways and higher-order visual regions that support bidirectional information flow between sensory and affective systems [26, 33]. Additionally, this fronto-occipital network is embedded within broader large-scale systems, including the default mode and salience networks, both of which are known to show dysregulation in mood disorders [34–36]. Thus, rTMS targeting the V1 node may serve as a strategic entry point for modulating fronto-occipital network coupling. Consistent with this interpretation, our fMRI-guided approach revealed treatment-related changes in functional connectivity between V1 and emotion-related cortical regions: active rTMS enhanced coupling between V1 and the right superior occipital gyrus, while reducing connectivity between V1 and the left anterior cingulate cortex. These findings support a network-level mechanism of rTMS-induced neuroplasticity.

Several factors may contribute to the variability in treatment response observed in this study. Although no significant between-group differences were detected at baseline, we systematically assessed and compared baseline depressive severity (MADRS, HAMD-24, QIDS-SR), anxiety symptoms (HAMA), age, sex, illness duration, and medication dose, all of which were comparable between the active and sham rTMS groups. To minimize pharmacological confounding, all participants received lurasidone as monotherapy throughout the study period, with doses flexibly titrated to a target range of 40–80 mg/day, according to clinical response and tolerability. The total stimulation dose (45,000 pulses across 15 sessions) was approximately half that of the Stanford accelerated protocol, which was chosen to balance efficacy and safety given the higher risk of mood switching in adolescents and young adults. Nevertheless, given the broad age range of 14–28 years, our sample includes both late adolescents and young adults, who may differ in neurodevelopmental stage, cortical plasticity, and responsiveness to neuromodulation. Exploratory age-stratified analyses suggested no significant differences in MADRS outcomes between participants < 18 years and those ≥ 18 years, although larger studies are needed to confirm potential developmental effects.

Despite the promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, owing to the rigorous trial design, only participants who completed the full 8-week follow-up were included in the final analysis. While this approach ensured data quality and consistency, it inevitably reduced the final sample size, limiting statistical power and generalizability. Larger-scale trials with prolonged follow-up are warranted to establish the longevity of clinical benefits and broader applicability across diverse BD-D cohorts. Second, all participants received concomitant pharmacotherapy with lurasidone during the study period. Both active and sham groups followed the same medication titration protocol and dosage range to minimize pharmacological bias. However, as lurasidone is an effective treatment for bipolar depression, its concurrent administration may have contributed to overall symptom improvement and limits the ability to fully isolate the independent therapeutic effects of rTMS. Third, although our stimulation coordinates were derived from group-level resting-state fMRI–based functional connectivity analyses, the current study did not employ individualized target modeling, which may further optimize treatment precision in future studies. Finally, sham stimulation at 20% RMT matched the auditory cues of active rTMS but did not replicate scalp sensations, as no tilted coil or additional electrodes were employed, potentially allowing participants to differentiate it from real stimulation. Additionally, occipital rTMS may elicit phosphenes, introducing a residual risk of functional unblinding. Although post-intervention blinding assessments indicated that participants were generally unable to reliably identify their treatment allocation, future studies should adopt more sophisticated sham protocols to better emulate the complete sensory profile of active stimulation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that rTMS targeting the V1 node may be a feasible and safe neuromodulation approach associated with symptomatic improvement in adolescents and young adults with bipolar depression. These results support further investigation of circuit-based neuromodulation approaches as potential adjunctive strategies for precision treatment of affective disorders.
