Effects of a yoga-based intervention program on psychological immunity and life satisfaction among armed forces personnel of India: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Aarsh Ojas Parasar Pandey, Niharranjan Mishra, Santosh Kumar Vishvakarma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersonnel in the armed forces experience high levels of stress, danger, and trauma that can adversely impact their mental and emotional health. The armed forces organisations must prioritise preventive and proactive measures to improve the mental and emotional health of their personnel. Improving the psychological immunity of the armed forces personnel will help them handle the stress they experience daily and enhance their life satisfaction. The main objectives of this study were to assess the effects of a yoga-based intervention program on psychological immunity and life satisfaction among active armed forces personnel in India and to study the relationship between psychological immunity and life satisfaction.MethodsA pre-test-post-test control group design was adopted for the study. We have screened 50 samples for this study, and 42 (Mage 33.62 ± 4.12) were selected. Participants were randomly assigned to two groups: control and intervention. Psycho-Immunity Scale (PIS) and Life Satisfaction Scale (LSS) were used to assess the participants' psychological immunity and life satisfaction, respectively. A yoga intervention was provided to the yoga group, comprising pragyayogasana (Pragyayogasana is a series of 16 asanas which must be performed in the mentioned sequence for physical, mental, and spiritual well-being), pranayama (mindful and controlled breathing), and meditation, administered six days a week for one month.ResultsA significant enhancement in psychological immunity (p < 0.01) and life satisfaction (p < 0.01) was observed in the yoga group. Also, a substantial improvement in all the components of psychological immunity, viz., self-confidence (p < 0.01), total adjustment (p < 0.01), emotional maturity (p < 0.01), and psychological well-being (p < 0.01), was found in the yoga group. In contrast, no such difference was obtained in the control group. Also, a significant positive correlation was found between total adjustment and life satisfaction (r = .32).ConclusionThese results revealed that the yoga-based intervention program significantly enhanced the armed forces personnel's psychological immunity and life satisfaction. Results also suggest promising implications for policymakers aimed at improving armed forces personnel’s overall health and optimal functioning.Trial registrationThis study was registered at the Institute Ethics Committee-National Institute of Technology Rourkela, India (IEC-NIT Rourkela). Introduction

Military life is associated with a high degree of uncertainty and adversities. Armed forces personnel face more job-related stress because they also face some unique stressors. Literature shows that stressors such as combat, exposure to heavy casualties, deployment of units in a warzone, and unexpected mobilisation of reserve units positively correlate with high levels of psychological distress [1–3]. The highly stressful nature of disaster relief missions becomes the cause of various psychological problems [4]. The extreme nature of military life hinders the achievement of a satisfactory level of satisfaction with life and the overall well-being of the personnel [5].

It is always a challenging and essential task to keep soldiers physically, psychologically, and emotionally fit, especially in the face of unique occupational stressors encountered by armed forces personnel. In such a high-stress environment, preventive and promotive measures become crucial to ensure treatment, long-term maintenance of overall health, and operational readiness [6]. This emphasises the growing recognition of the importance of enhancing psychological immunity, a construct that represents one’s adaptive psychological resources for handling stress and hardship.

Olah has defined the psychological immune system as "an integrated system of cognitive, motivational and behavioural personality dimensions that provide immunity against stress, promote healthy development and serve as stress resistance resources or psychological antibodies" [7]. Physical immunity protects the body from all types of diseases. Similarly, psychological immunity protects from all mental and emotional disorders or problems. It is a complex mechanism that works in parallel to physical or biological immunity [8]. Psychological immunity acts as both a protective and a promotive mechanism [9]. It protects from psychological and emotional issues from occurring and promotes better mental and emotional health. Like biological immunity, psychological immunity also provides autonomous and adaptive mechanisms that protect and promote health outside human awareness [10]. Whenever the psychological immunity of a person decreases, mental problems start appearing, which may further turn into disorders.

Psychological immunity can be considered as a subsystem within the framework of resilience, but has a distinct theoretical foundation. Resilience is the ability to recover from adversities, while psychological immunity is a more structured, proactive subsystem within the resilience framework [11]. Resilience framework emphasises the dynamic interaction between inner strengths and external resources such as social support, peers, and socio-cultural factors [12]. Psychological immunity is a more structured system of adaptive, cognitive, motivational, and personality traits that act as internal resources against adversities [7].

Unremitting exposure to stressful situations such as intense physical training, irregular schedules, unexpected deployment, potentially traumatic and violent situations, etc., impacts cadets’ physiological and psychological health [13] and thereby reduces the level of satisfaction with life in the military.

Life satisfaction is an individual’s cognitive and affective evaluation of his/her life [14]. It is a person’s attitude and feelings about life. Life satisfaction is positively correlated with overall mental health [15], quality of life [16], self-esteem, and happiness [17].

Intervention programs or training modules for improving psychological immunity and life satisfaction for the armed forces of India have not been developed to date. There is a paucity of published research evidence in the Indian context. Through this study, we have developed an intervention program and assessed its efficacy in enhancing the psychological immunity and life satisfaction of the armed forces personnel of India.

Yoga has emerged as an effective therapeutic tool for various physiological and psychological problems. Yogic practices generally include the mindful practice of specific postures, slow, deep, and highly mindful breathing, and meditation [18]. Yoga is now widely used to achieve good physical and mental well-being [19]. However, little is known about the promotive health advantages of yoga on armed forces personnel.

The ultimate goal of yoga is to achieve a state of self-realisation, but in between, the yoga practice provides various health-related benefits. It creates harmony among the mind, body, and soul. Various systematic reviews and meta-analyses have been done to authenticate the role of Yoga therapy in many psychological disorders. Yoga is found to be very beneficial in treating stress [20], OCD [21], anxiety [22], and depression [23]. The practice of yoga also improves overall well-being [24]. Research studies conducted to assess the efficacy of yogic techniques advocate in favour of using yogic practices for better mental health and well-being.

Through the practice of yoga, one can develop a profound sense of tranquillity and mindfulness (being present at the moment), which can be applied to other situations in life.

Even though studies on the impact of yoga on armed forces personnel's mental health are becoming more numerous, less is known about how it affects psychological immunity and life satisfaction, particularly in the Indian armed forces. The majority of current research focuses on symptom reduction in PTSD [25] and other psychological issues [26, 27], but it ignores the development of psychological strengths and overall well-being [27]. Furthermore, culturally grounded constructs like psychological immunity and life satisfaction in armed forces contexts have received little empirical attention [28]. Therefore, we developed a yoga intervention to enhance the psychological immunity and life satisfaction of the armed forces personnel.

The main objective of this study was to analyse the effects of a yoga-based intervention program on the psychological immunity and life satisfaction of the active armed forces personnel of India and to study the relationship between psychological immunity and life satisfaction.

Based on theoretical and empirical grounds, the expected effect sizes in the study are hypothesised to be moderate to large. Yogic practices are known to produce promising physiologic and psychological benefits by enhancing emotion regulation, stress reduction, and promoting self-awareness and resilience [29, 30]. Given the structural and integrative nature of the yoga protocol used in this study, it is reasonable to expect moderate to large effect sizes across the outcome measures. The following hypotheses were made to conduct the study:

H1: Yoga group participants will exhibit significantly higher psychological immunity scores compared to the control group participants after the intervention.

H1: Yoga group participants will exhibit significantly higher psychological immunity scores compared to the control group participants after the intervention.

H2: Yoga group participants will report significantly greater life satisfaction than participants in the control group after the intervention.

H2: Yoga group participants will report significantly greater life satisfaction than participants in the control group after the intervention.

H3. There will be a significant improvement from pre-test to post-test scores in the yoga group participants.

H3. There will be a significant improvement from pre-test to post-test scores in the yoga group participants.

H4. There will be no significant difference between pre-test and post-test scores in control group participants.

H4. There will be no significant difference between pre-test and post-test scores in control group participants.

Methods

Study design and procedure

We used a pre-test-post-test control group design for this study. This is a single-blinded study (an armed forces cadet helped in the data collection). Participants were equally divided into the intervention and the control group. Eligible participants were randomised into intervention and control groups. Randomisation was done by using a computer-generated randomisation list to ensure unbiased and unpredictable assignment of the participants to either of the groups. Allocation concealment was maintained through the use of sequentially numbered, opaque, and sealed envelopes, which were prepared in advance by the cadet who helped in the data collection. The whole procedure was designed in a way to ensure allocation concealment and reduce selection bias.

The study period was 30 days, during which all the study participants were assessed before and after the intervention group (experimental group) was provided with the yoga-based intervention program. Informed consent was obtained from all the participants before inclusion in this study. The pre-intervention (baseline) assessment was conducted three days before the first intervention session, and the post-intervention check was performed three days after the intervention was completed.

An expert yoga trainer provided the yoga-based intervention program to the yoga group six days a week for one month, with a time duration of one hour each day in the morning from 6:30 to 7:30. The yoga group participants had to attend a minimum of 20 yoga sessions. During the intervention period, participants of the control group were instructed not to participate in or practice any such activity.

Participants

Fifty armed forces personnel from the two battalions of the CAPF (Central Armed Police Forces) of India were selected for the study. According to the computer-generated randomisation list, participants (n = 42) were randomly assigned to the control and intervention groups (Fig. 1). The inclusion criteria were: (a) the age range of 20–40 years old, (b) being physically fit enough to practice a yoga-based intervention program regularly. Exclusion criteria were (a) personnel belonging to the ‘services’ (personnel who provide material and maintenance support services and are not involved in actual fight situations) of the armed forces, (b) female cadets due to their minimal representation, (c) personnel suffering from any significant physical or psychological disorder, and (d) personnel having experience of previous regular yoga practice. Two participants from the intervention group have discontinued the study because of some other assignment. Finally, 20 participants in each group were analysed (Fig. 1).

Sample size

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 software to determine the approximate sample size for this study. A one-tailed independent samples t-test was chosen based on the directional hypothesis that the yogic intervention would significantly enhance psychological immunity and life satisfaction among armed forces personnel, compared to a control group. Previous studies have consistently reported positive effects of yoga practice on mental health and well-being [31, 32], having no theoretical or empirical evidence for assuming a negative outcome.

The effect size was set at 0.7, indicating a larger effect. This value is based on prior yogic intervention studies reporting large effect sizes for similar psychological outcomes [31, 33]. Thus, a sample size of 52 (26 in each group) was required to achieve a power study of 80%, with an α level of 0.05.

In the present study, we have selected 50 participants, which is closely aligned with the obtained sample size, suggesting the study was adequately powered to detect the expected effects.

Measures

Psycho-Immunity Scale (PIS)

The psychological immunity of the study's participants was measured by PIS. PIS was developed by Bhardwaj et al. in 2015 [34]. PIS measures overall psychological immunity (PI) and its four components separately. Four components of psychological immunity that this scale measures are self-confidence (SC), total adjustment (TA), emotional maturity (EM), and psychological well-being (PWB). PIS consists of 60 items. Twenty-five of them are positive, and 35 are negative. 15–15 items were prepared to measure each psychological immunity component separately. Likert’s five-point scale was used for analysing the items: strongly disagree, disagree, undecided, agree, and strongly agree.

Validation of the Psycho-Immunity Scale (PIS)

Data was collected from 336 samples across India for the purpose of validating the scale.

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to conduct Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) for exploring the latent structure of the Psycho-Immunity Scale. The analysis yielded ten factors having eigenvalues greater than 1. The first four factors explained 43.82% of the total variance. These findings support the construct structure of psychological immunity, which includes psychological well-being (PWB), emotional maturity (EM), total adjustment (TA), and self-confidence (SC).

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)

CFA was used to evaluate the scale’s convergent and discriminant validity. All four constructs had Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values more than 0.50, indicating strong convergent validity.

Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio was used to test the discriminant validity. Discriminant validity was effectively established as all the inter-construct HTMT values fell below 0.90.

The scale also has face and content validity. Face validity was obtained through experts’ ratings. The content validity of the scale was established by grounding its items in the theoretical framework of the psychological immune system proposed by Olah (2000). Furthermore, all four components of the psychological immunity, SC, TA, EM, & PWB, were in line with positive psychology theories of optimal functioning and overall well-being.

Composite Reliability (Cr)

The composite reliability for SC is 0.92, 0.91 for TA, 0.918 for EM, and 0.927 for PWB. The Cronbach’s Alpha for the entire questionnaire (all 60 items across SC, TA, EM, & PWB) is 0.937. These results show excellent internal consistency, suggesting that the items consistently measure the underlying constructs.

Predictive validity of psychological immunity in relation to life satisfaction

A multiple regression analysis was performed to assess whether the four components of psycho-immunity, SC, TA, EM, & PWB, significantly predict life satisfaction. Findings suggested that three components, TA, EM, & PWB, were statistically significant predictors of life satisfaction. Self-confidence was not a statistically significant predictor of life satisfaction, indicating that self-confidence alone may not directly predict one’s life satisfaction. These findings suggest that psychological immunity is a useful framework for understanding and enhancing life satisfaction.

Life Satisfaction Scale (LSS)

The participants' life satisfaction was assessed using the LSS. It was prepared by Alam & Shrivastava in 1996 [35]. It consists of 60 items and measures six aspects of satisfaction, viz., health satisfaction, personal satisfaction, economic satisfaction, marital satisfaction, social satisfaction, and job satisfaction. The reliability of the test was 0.84 (Cronbach's α). The scale has face validity as all the items are closely related to the covered areas and judged by the experts. It also has content validity.

Intervention

A yogic intervention module for improving psychological immunity and life satisfaction was developed after studying various yogic texts, practices, and discussions with some subject experts. The developed module was sent to some experts for their opinions regarding the relevance and modifications, if any. After approval from these experts, the developed module was finalised for the intervention. It is comprised of some specific asanas (specific bodily postures), pragyayogasana, pranayama (slow, deep, and mindful breathing), and dhyana (meditation). Pragyayogasana is a series of 16 asanas, developed by Acharya Shriram Sharma [36]. These asanas are easy to practice but have a tremendous effect on the mind and body. Pragyayogasana is highly effective as a preventive strategy, as well as a way to maintain or restore physical and mental health. The yoga module thus developed was used for intervention purposes. The intervention started with a warm-up, followed by specific asanas, shavasana (corpse pose), and pranayama. Finally, meditation was practised.

Data analysis

The preliminary data follow the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance. Normality of the data was checked by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Significance value (p-value) for the data of psychological immunity and life satisfaction was greater than 0.05, failing to reject the null hypothesis of normality, which means the data is normally distributed. Homogeneity of variance was checked by Laven’s test. Here again significance value (p-value) was much greater than 0.05. That indicates the homogeneity of variance.

Between-group comparisons of the participants at the pre- and post-intervention levels were made using an independent sample t-test. Within-group comparisons between pre- and post-intervention data of both groups were conducted using a paired sample t-test. Pearson correlations were calculated between the outcome measures. The correlation results led to running the linear regression analysis to ascertain the determinants of life satisfaction of the personnel. SPSS version 23.0 was used to conduct the above data analyses (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Discussion

The primary aim of the present study was to assess the effects of the yoga intervention on the psychological immunity and life satisfaction of the active armed forces personnel of India. We found that a yoga-based intervention has significantly enhanced the intervention group's life satisfaction, psychological immunity, and its four components, namely self-confidence, emotional maturity, and psychological well-being. Further, no significant changes were observed in the control group participants. A significant difference was observed in overall psychological immunity, self-confidence, emotional maturity, psychological well-being, and life satisfaction when comparing the intervention group to the control group after the intervention.

Our results are comparable to those of Bhardwaj (2016) [37], who assessed the effects of pragyayoga on psychological immunity and found significant improvement in the psychological immunity of the participants. Psychological immunity is a relatively new concept in the field of psychology. Only a little research has been done to develop an intervention program to enhance psychological immunity. Various studies have been carried out to assess the effects of yoga on the components of psychological immunity separately. Our findings are in line with those of previous studies. In agreement with similar earlier studies, the yoga-based intervention programs significantly improved self-confidence [38], adjustment [39], emotional maturity [40], and psychological well-being [24].

The study’s findings also revealed that the yoga-based intervention program significantly enhanced the life satisfaction of the intervention group participants. Our findings are consistent with previous studies [41, 42].

Moreover, regarding the correlation between psychological immunity and life satisfaction, only the total adjustment component of psychological immunity has a significant positive association with life satisfaction. This finding is in congruence with earlier studies like Stanley et al., 2012; Whisman and Uebelacker, 2013, etc. [43, 44]. In the regression analysis, it was obtained that total adjustment significantly predicts the life satisfaction of the personnel. The yoga-based intervention enhanced the total adjustment of the personnel, and improved adjustment supported the yoga-based intervention to enhance the life satisfaction of the participants. Better adjustment in every aspect of life results in enhanced life satisfaction.

The physiological and psychological effects of yoga have been demonstrated in multiple studies on different populations; the literature in this regard is sparse on armed forces personnel of India. This is the first study that has developed a yoga-based intervention program to enhance the psychological immunity and life satisfaction of the active armed forces personnel of India.

Participants in the yoga group also reported decreased stress and anxiety and improved positive mood and sleep. The participants in our study reported no adverse effects of yoga practice.

The intervention program used in the present study comprises Pragyayogasana, pranayama, bandha, and dhyana (meditation). The beneficial effects of yoga-based intervention in enhancing psychological immunity and life satisfaction lie in its health-protective and promotive ability at both physiological and psychological levels.

In voxel-based morphometric studies, it was found that regular yoga practice is associated with various cortical and sub-cortical areas of our brain that are associated with sustained attention and self-control [45]. Yogic asanas and pranayama reduce the activity of the sympathetic nervous system and stimulate the parasympathetic nervous system, resulting in physiological reactivity to stress [46]. These changes could have contributed to the enhancement of the psychological immunity and life satisfaction of the participants.

Yogic practices increase the inhibitory effects of GABA in the brain pathways as measured by MR spectroscopy, which is crucial for stress reactivity, threat perception, and emotion regulation [47]. Although these neurobiological mechanisms were not measured in this study, previous studies provide hypothetical explanations for the psychophysiological benefits of yoga practice. Participants in our study reported that daily yoga improved their sleep quality and helped them handle unpleasant emotions more flexibly and effectively.

It has been found that the practice of yoga helps individuals develop better stress-coping mechanisms, thereby decreasing the adverse health effects of chronic stress [48] and increasing the psychological immunity of the practitioners. Yoga-based intervention is beneficial in enhancing emotion-focused stress coping, which includes a reduction in physiological arousal through relaxation, a cognitive reappraisal of stressors, and acceptance of what cannot be changed [49].

Through the cautious and persistent practice of yoga, they learn to direct their attention to present bodily sensations, perceptions, thoughts, changes in the breath pattern, and emotions when experiencing stress, which increases the mindfulness and psychological health of the practitioners [50]. These changes in stress coping improved the psychological immunity and life satisfaction of the practitioners.

Limitations and future research

Although this study has several strengths, we are also aware of some limitations. First, in our study, we did not take female participants. This decreases the generalisability of the study. Second, although this is a single-blinded study, double-blinding was not ensured. Third, all the outcome measures are self-report, which may be subject to recall bias. Future studies should incorporate behavioural assessments wherever possible. Fourth, while our sample size met the minimum requirement based on G*Power sample size calculation, it should be larger to ensure more generalisability and statistical power. We recommend that future studies lengthen the intervention period and include female participants while replicating the study design.

Conclusion

The results from this study support the use of yoga intervention among the armed forces to enhance psychological immunity and life satisfaction. Yoga is an effective tool for achieving complete mental health. Armed forces can use the yogic module developed in this study in their training programs. Yoga should be part of the routine of the armed forces personnel to attain complete health and optimal functioning. Future research should investigate the effects of yoga on various other variables among the armed forces population.
