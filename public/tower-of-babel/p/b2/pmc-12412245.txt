Corynebacterium amycolatum: an underestimated pathogen in early-onset neonatal sepsis—a case report
Authors: Wenjing Wu, Yongmei Jiang, Wei Zhou, Linghan Kuang, Jiaji Ling, Yu Tang, Xingxin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly-onset neonatal sepsis (EOS) is a critical condition primarily caused by maternal–fetal transmission of bacterial pathogens during delivery, with Escherichia coli and Group B Streptococcus being the most prevalent. However, neonatal sepsis can also involve other rare bacteria, including Corynebacterium amycolatum, which was first described in 1988 and is widely recognized as an emerging pathogen in infectious diseases.Case presentationA male infant was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) due to premature birth and tachypnea. His mother had experienced premature rupture of membranes (PROM) 20 days prior. The infant’s procalcitonin (PCT) level was significantly elevated, reaching 1.51 ng/ml. Chest X-ray revealed increased and blurred lung markings, slightly decreased lung transparency in both lung fields, with scattered ground-glass opacities, and a few fine granular shadows. Corynebacterium amycolatum was isolated from the blood cultures and identified by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) with 99.9% confidence. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed that the isolate was sensitive to penicillin, cephalosporins, and vancomycin, yet resistant to ciprofloxacin, clindamycin, and erythromycin. Empirical treatment with ampicillin and cefoperazone/sulbactam was initially started. Followed by ceftazidime based on susceptibility results, the infant's condition significantly improved, leading to discharge.ConclusionThis case emphasizes the importance of recognizing C. amycolatum as a potential pathogen in neonatal sepsis, especially in preterm infants, and underscores the necessity of accurate identification methods such as MALDI-TOF MS to avoid misidentification. Given the severe clinical outcomes associated with this condition, heightened awareness and diagnostic precision are crucial for optimizing treatment strategies in neonatal intensive care units. Background

Corynebacterium spp. is a genus of Gram-positive, non-spore-forming, rod-shaped bacteria belonging to family Corynebacteriaceae. It is widely distributed in environments such as soil, water, and plant and animal material [1]. Species of Corynebacterium, other than C. diphtheriae, commonly colonize human skin and mucous membranes and are often considered clinically insignificant contaminants when isolated from clinical specimens [2]. C. amycolatum was first identified by Collins and Burton on human skin in 1988 [3] and has been linked to various human infections, including septicemia, endocarditis, mastitis, and peritonitis [4–7]. Early-onset neonatal sepsis (EOS) is a systemic infection occurring within the first three days of life, generally caused by bacterial pathogens transmitted vertically from the mother to the infant in utero (before delivery) or during delivery [8, 9]. The most common pathogens are Escherichia coli and group B Streptococcus (GBS) [10]. However, neonatal sepsis can also involve other rare bacteria, including C. amycolatum. In this report, we describe a case of EOS caused by C. amycolatum in a low-birthweight, very premature infant. Empirical therapy with ampicillin and cefoperazone/sulbactam was initiated, and treatment was later adjusted to ceftazidime based on antimicrobial susceptibility testing, resulting in a successful outcome.

Case presentation

A male infant, born at a gestational age of 31 weeks and 1 day and weighing 1580 g, was delivered vaginally to a 32-year-old mother, gravida 2, para 2. The mother had no fever before and during delivery, with normal results from her prenatal routine blood test and vaginal secretion test. Screening for Group B Streptococcus and testing for Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Mycoplasma genitalium, and Chlamydia trachomatis (RNA) were all negative. Notably, she had experienced premature rupture of membranes 20 days prior and was hospitalized at that time. During hospitalization, she was also administered magnesium sulfate for fetal neuroprotection, dydrogesterone for pregnancy support, dexamethasone to promote fetal lung maturation, and cefoxitin for infection prevention. Postpartum, severe chorioamnionitis and umbilical vein vasculitis were confirmed through histological examination of the placenta. The newborn was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) due to the premature birth and tachypnea. Apgar scores were recorded as 8, 9, and 9 at 1, 5, and 10 min, respectively. Upon NICU admission, the infant had an oxygen saturation of 90%, a temperature of 36.5 °C, a heart rate of 144 beats/min, a respiratory rate of 64 breaths/min, and a blood pressure of 66/28 mmHg. Physical examination revealed intercostal, subcostal, and suprasternal retractions during inspiration, along with coarse breath sounds in both lung fields. His white blood cell count and C-reaction protein concentration were 18.7 × 109/L (60.0% neutrophils) and < 0.5 mg/L. However, the procalcitonin (PCT) level was significantly elevated, reaching 1.51 ng/ml. Chest X-ray revealed increased and blurred lung markings, slightly decreased lung transparency in both lung fields, with scattered ground-glass opacities, and a few fine granular shadows (Fig. 1). A single aerobic blood culture was collected from peripheral venipuncture, and sputum and ear swab cultures were obtained immediately after birth. Empiric antibiotic therapy with ampicillin and cefoperazone/sulbactam was initiated. Treatment included non-invasive mechanical ventilation and pulmonary surfactant. After 12 h of incubation, gram-positive bacilli were detected in the blood culture. At the same time, a repeat peripheral blood culture and CSF culture were performed, with no growth observed after 5 days of incubation. The isolate, identified as C. amycolatum strain 045007 by MALDI-TOF MS (bioMérieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France) with a 99.9% confidence level, was also isolated from the newborn’s sputum and ear swab, as well as the mother’s uterine secretion. Genomic DNA from strain 045007 was extracted and sequenced using the HiSeq X10 platform (Illumina; San Diego, CA, USA). For the whole genome sequence of strain 045007, 10,566,986 reads and 1.59 GB of bases were generated (coverage, 400), which were assembled into a 2.6 Mb draft genome containing 69 contigs ≥ 200 bp in length (N50, 98,841 bp) with a 58.87 mol% GC content. The pair-wise average nucleotide identity (ANI) between strain 045007 and the type of strain of C. amycolatum strain DSM 6922 T (GenBank accession number CP046975) was determined using the JSpecies program based on BLAST [11]. The ANI value was 95%, which was above the cutoff to define a bacterial species [12]. After 5 days of antimicrobial and supportive treatment, the infant’s condition improved. Subsequently, cefoperazone/sulbactam was replaced with ceftazidime based on susceptibility results, and ampicillin was discontinued. This therapy was administered for 7 days, after which the baby was discharged following recovery. This therapy was administered for 7 days, after which the baby was discharged following recovery. At discharge (day 26), the child showed no signs of shortness of breath or cyanosis when not receiving oxygen and passed newborn hearing screening.

Discussion

Corynebacterium spp. predominantly colonize the microbial flora of the skin and mucous membranes. Historically, the pathogenic potential of this organism was underestimated. Recently, with an increase in infection cases and resistance profiles among clinical isolates, greater attention is now directed towards these organisms, which are no longer viewed as mere contaminants [13]. Corynebacterium amycolatum is considered a potential multidrug-resistant opportunistic pathogen that can cause various infections, such as bacteremia, peritonitis, endocarditis, and wound infections, especially in nosocomial settings and among immunocompromised patients [4, 14, 15]. C. amycolatum has been identified as the most frequently isolated species in nosocomial surgical site infections in North India [16] and is also prevalent in central venous catheter-related infections among pediatric patients with hematological malignancies [14]. Based on our literature search in PubMed, A total of 24 cases of C. amycolatum bacteremia were reported (Table 2). In the reported cases, the proportion of adults with C. amycolatum bacteremia is higher than that of children (16 vs 8). The majority of infections due to C. amycolatum occured in either immunocompromised patients (especially those with hematologic malignancy) or patients with intravascular devices [7, 13, 14, 17]. To date, only one case of neonatal sepsis caused by C. amycolatum has been reported, where the pathogen was initially misidentified as C. xerosis through biochemical testing. Unfortunately, despite aggressive treatment, the extremely premature newborn died from septic shock syndrome [5]. Traditional bacterial identification methods, such as phenotypic and biochemical techniques, often fail to reliably identify Corynebacterium spp. at the species level. Recently, MALDI-TOF MS has emerged as a reliable method for identifying Corynebacterium spp [18]. In this case, the pathogen was accurately identified as C. amycolatum by MALDI-TOF. Neonatal sepsis is a significant health threat to newborns. The incidence is 2,202 per 100,000 live births, with mortality rates ranging from 11 to 19% [26]. In China, the recommended empirical treatment for EOS includes ampicillin (or penicillin) and a third-generation cephalosporin, pending blood culture and other test results, according to expert consensus [27]. Consequently, the newborn in this case was treated with ampicillin and cefoperazone/sulbactam, which were initially used empirically.

Treating C. amycolatum infections is challenging due to frequent multidrug resistance. As a member of the Corynebacterium genus, most isolates (including C. amycolatum) are resistant to penicillins, clindamycin, erythromycin, and fluoroquinolones, but only susceptible to vancomycin and linezolid [6, 28]. Vancomycin is recommended for the treatment of C. amycolatum infections, and in previous reports, vancomycin was used in more than half of the patients, particularly among adults (Table 2).

In the case, strain 045007 demonstrated an atypical susceptibility profile – showing sensitivity to penicillin, cephalosporins, linezolid, and vancomycin. This favorable sensitivity pattern likely contributed to the positive outcome, as cephalosporin therapy (cefoperazone/sulbactam) was initiated from the outset.

This contrasts markedly with the neonatal sepsis case reported by Berner et al. [5], where the C. amycolatum isolate was resistant to ampicillin, piperacillin, netilmicin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (though susceptible to vancomycin), leading to a standard regimen of piperacillin and netilmicin [5]. The differing resistance patterns between these two isolates likely contributed to the differences in clinical management and outcomes. The differing resistance patterns between these two isolates may explain the difference in clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this report details a case of early-onset neonatal sepsis caused by C. amycolatum in a low-birthweight, premature infant. This case underscores the importance of recognizing C. amycolatum as a potential pathogen in neonatal sepsis—particularly in preterm infants—and highlights the necessity of accurate identification methods such as MALDI-TOF MS to prevent misdiagnosis. Given the severe clinical implications, heightened awareness and diagnostic precision are crucial for optimizing treatment strategies in neonatal intensive care settings.
