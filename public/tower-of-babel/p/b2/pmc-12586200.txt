The challenge of robotic surgery in oncology: a feasibility study with the Senhance™ Surgical System
Authors: Burghard Abendstein, Michael Prugger, Vivianda Menke, Ludger Staib, Narimantas Salamavicius, Tomislav Kulis, Frank Willeke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Robotic-assisted surgery is an evolution of minimally invasive techniques, though its application in complex oncological cases requires further investigation to verify feasibility and safety. Consequently, this study aimed to assess the feasibility and safety of the robotic platform Senhance™ Surgical System across various oncological procedures. We retrospectively analyzed data from the prospective multi-center TRUST registry, including 966 patients who underwent gastrointestinal, gynecological, or urological cancer surgeries at eight European sites. Investigated parameters included procedural times, conversion rates, instrument usage, and perioperative complications. The results demonstrate the system’s viability, with low overall rates of unplanned conversions (4.8%), adverse events (2.9%), and serious adverse events (2.3%), which compare favorably to other published oncological studies. Procedural characteristics varied by discipline; urology constituted the largest cohort, while gynecology had the shortest operative times but the highest rate of instrument changes, suggesting a unique complexity. Most technical issues were manageable warnings rather than critical system malfunctions. A limited 1-year follow-up of gynecological patients showed expected oncological outcomes. In conclusion, this large-scale analysis provides substantial evidence that oncological surgery with the Senhance™ Surgical System is safe and feasible, demonstrating low complication rates and adaptable workflows that support its expanding role in cancer care.

Introduction

Minimally invasive surgery has revolutionized the surgical landscape since the beginning of the twenty-first century, becoming the standard of care in numerous fields and potentially replacing open surgery [1]. The emergence of robotic-assisted surgery has further expanded the possibilities of minimally invasive procedures by offering enhanced precision, ergonomics, and control, thereby benefiting both the surgeon and the patient [2].

Though still under evaluation in many oncologic contexts, robotic-assisted surgery has shown promising potential to match or surpass laparoscopic results [3]. One system that offers notable advantages in this regard is the Senhance™ Surgical System (Asensus Surgical US, Inc., Durham, NC, USA). FDA-approved since 2017, the Senhance™ Surgical System integrates innovative features such as eye-tracking camera control and an ergonomically designed cockpit. Notably, this robotic system maintains standard laparoscopic trocar placement, a notable achievement for robotic procedures and concepts. This compatibility with conventional techniques, such as alternatively performing robotic and laparoscopic approaches, minimizes the additional risks associated with placing new trocars, even in high-risk patients. In addition, the Senhance™ Surgical System elegantly integrates into existing surgical expertise and workflow, by, e.g., typically utilizing three robotic arms alongside familiar laparoscopic instruments with standard 5 mm diameters.

Besides these phenomenal advantages, a lasting hesitation exists in adopting robotic surgery in oncological cases. This finding is often attributed to the increased complexity of cancer-related procedures, with oncological surgeries usually recognized as particularly challenging as they face a higher risk of adverse events such as bleeding, embolism, infections, and overall morbidity [4]. However, up to 80% of patients with solid tumors will eventually need surgery as part of the treatment for the disease [5]. To shed further light on the feasibility of robotic surgery in these procedures, we aim to investigate the application of the Senhance™ Surgical System. Here, we strive to explore not only standard procedure parameters but also the number of instruments used, as these may offer a more precise indicator of procedural complexity and challenge than, e.g., surgical duration or conversions. In addition, we aim to add investigations on limited follow-up data to explore potential long-term outcomes.

This study evaluates the feasibility and safety of robotic-assisted oncology procedures with the Senhance™ Surgical System across multiple European centers.

Methods

Patient inclusion and sites

We collected data from eight European centers (Feldkirch State Hospital, Feldkirch, Austria; Klaipeda University Hospital, Klaipeda, Lithuania; Sana Hospital Offenbach, Offenbach, Germany; St.-Marien Hospital Siegen, Siegen, Germany; University Hospital Centre Zagreb, Zagreb, Croatia; Evangelical Hospital Wesel, Wesel, Germany; Hospital Esslingen, Esslingen, Germany, and Presidential Hospital Minsk, Minsk, Belarus) participating in the international study group TRUST (The TransEnterix European Patient Registry for Robotic-assisted Laparoscopic Procedures in Urology, Abdominal Surgery, Thoracic and Gynecologic Surgery, Ethics Approval Number: 2017-463-f-S, Study has been published at the Deutsches Register Klinischer Studien (english: German Clinical Trials Register DRKS) in Germany and Clinicaltrails.gov in the US).

Oncological procedures across three surgical disciplines (gastrointestinal surgery, gynecology, and urology) were registered at the described sites. For the main investigation, data were collected from all patients undergoing respective cancer-related surgeries. The inclusion criteria followed the guidelines of the TRUST study. They were defined as eligibility for laparoscopic surgery and, therefore, robotic surgery, while presenting no absolute contraindications for minimally invasive surgery, such as cardiopulmonary diseases or expected severe intra-abdominal adhesions. The criteria for exclusion from respective surgeries included, therefore, any inability to perform laparoscopic surgery, an estimated life expectancy of less than 12 months, and failure to provide informed consent. Patient-related data were documented after each patient provided written informed consent, and respective data were securely saved in a pseudonymous format.

Patient demographics were collected before surgery, including age, sex, comorbid conditions, and history of previous abdominal surgery.

In addition to the registered data, we conducted an exploratory investigation of the 1-year outcomes at a single site and single discipline.

Procedures

All surgical procedures were completed using the Senhance™ Surgical System if not otherwise stated. As the procedures varied across the three disciplines, we opted not to present detailed procedure descriptions. Respective details can be found in related literature (e.g., [6–9]). All surgeries were conducted in accordance with oncological standards, encompassing optimal preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative care.

Data related to procedure times were recorded as the duration of the surgery, defined as the time in minutes from incision to closure; the duration of the console, defined as the time in minutes the cockpit surgeon controlled the robotic system’s handles; and the duration of setup, defined as the time in minutes the surgical team needed to adjust and prepare the robotic arms. Blood loss during the procedure was documented and categorized as either < 0 mL or, if greater than 0 mL, an approximate volume was recorded. If the procedure required conversion, indicating a continuation via laparoscopic surgery, open surgery, or a combination of both, this change was registered in the database. It is important to note that only “unplanned conversions” were recorded and initially differentiated from conversions planned due to a procedural strategy. Regarding technical aspects, device deficiencies such as robotic malfunctions were documented and specified. For better understanding, the procedure details will be presented only for the most frequently performed procedure per discipline.

Complications categorized as adverse event (AE) or serious adverse event (SAE) were recorded and judged in severity (mild, moderate, or severe) and causality to the robotic system (unassessable, unlikely/not related, possible, probable, or certain). The surgeon who recorded the incidence performed the initial assessment. An independent safety committee of the registry reviewed the assessments regularly and either approved the initial assessment or reassessed, entering a discussion with the surgeon based on the patient’s details in the hospital files. Causality is defined as: “not related” indicating no plausible connection (event explained by other causes), “possible” representing a weak or uncertain connection with alternative causes being likely, but insufficient info available for ruling out a connection completely, “probable” referring to a connection that seems relevant and not explained by other causes and “causal” meaning a strong link beyond a reasonable doubt (incident fits timing or presents with a known response patterns with no alternative cause). The AE and SAE categories were used because they were common at the time the registry was designed in 2017, when, for example, the Clavien–Dindo classification [10] was not yet widely adopted. AEs are defined as “any unintended medical occurrence, disease, injury, or clinical sign (including abnormal lab findings) in subjects, users, or others during a clinical investigation, regardless of its relation to the investigational device,” while SAE follow the definition of “an AE that results in death, life-threatening illness or injury, permanent impairment, hospitalization or its prolongation, medical or surgical intervention to prevent serious harm, chronic disease, or fetal distress, death, or congenital defect” [MDR Article 2(57/58)] [11]. For better overall classification, the start date and duration of the AE/SAE were registered.

The robotic setup data baseline consisted of three arms, including two instruments and one camera. Each instrument used in the procedure was documented (i.e., type of instrument), and any additional instrument (more than two) was counted as an instrument change. The respective data are only evaluated for the most frequent procedure per discipline.

Follow-up

As an exploratory extension, we conducted a follow-up investigation specifically for gynecological patients to assess longer term outcomes after robotic surgery with the Senhance™ Surgical System in oncological cases. Over a 12-month period, we documented data on late complications, receipt of adjuvant therapies (chemotherapy or radiotherapy), and the occurrence of tumor relapse.

Data extraction and statistical analysis

Database registrations began in January 2018, and data extraction occurred in February 2025.

Statistical evaluation was performed with statistical software SAS® 9.4 (TS1M6) for Microsoft Windows” with the support of RQM + Europe [12, 13]. Metrics were calculated based on the distribution of the data. Parametric tests were applied to data following a normal distribution; non-parametric tests were used for all other data.

Results

Procedure details

Surgical times showed explicit variation across the three disciplines. For a better understanding, we focused on the details of the most frequently performed procedure per discipline. Radical hysterectomy presented the shortest average duration at 111.7 min (SD: 32.2), while urological surgeries showed the most extended average duration at 204.4 min (SD: 49.8). Gastrointestinal procedures exhibited the broadest range, varying from 75.0 to 403.8 min. In addition, console time correlated with the total surgical duration: gynecology registered the shortest average console time (51.9 min, SD: 36.2), urology the longest (125.2 min, SD: 62.3). Finally, docking time was short for all disciplines, ranging on average from 3.7 to 7.6 min. Importantly, entries with a minimum value of 1 min (docking time and console duration) should be interpreted carefully, as they typically resulted from either very short docking times or console durations actually matching the total duration of surgery. Across these three procedures, a total of 37 cases required conversion. Regarding all procedures, 46 cases (4.8%) required a change in approach. These conversions relate exclusively to “unplanned conversions”, where surgical requirements needed the use of non-robotic instruments. Notably, radical prostatectomy accounted for most of the conversions, with 26 cases converted to laparoscopic surgery (4.2%) and two (3.4%) to open surgery, as detailed in Table 3. In contrast, rectal resection recorded minimal numbers of conversions (three cases (1.7%) to open surgery, and one case (0.3%) to laparoscopy and open). Radical hysterectomy presented the highest relative conversion rate to laparoscopy (four cases, 11.4%) and open surgery (one case, 2.9%). Table 3 provides an overview of the details recorded. Malfunction

Most recorded malfunctions were categorized as “limited motion” or “collision”, as shown in Table 4. It is essential to note that the limited motion is a warning message, not a system malfunction. The limited motion and arm collisions were primarily caused by suboptimal trocar placement, patient anatomy and positioning (e.g., degree of Trendelenburg and table height), arm setup, and the positioning of the robot relative to the patient and the overall operation room layout. True malfunctions included console malfunctions (0.2%), monitor/camera malfunctions (0.6%), and other malfunctions (1.9%), such as failures of surgical instruments (ultrasonic device, bipolar devices, and scissors), as well as problems with the eye-tracking system and the robotic arms. Complications

Adverse events (AEs)

Overall, we found 28 AEs in the overall case cohort, representing an AE ratio of 2.9%. The 28 AEs were found related to radical prostatectomies (14 AEs), rectal resection (9 AEs), hemicolectomy right (4 AEs), and sigmoid resection (1 AE). The AEs, therefore, mainly occurred in urology and gastrointestinal surgery. Evaluation of each AE’s severity concluded 21 AEs as mild, six as moderate, and one as severe. One of the serious AE cases was a rectal resection that was followed by low anterior resection syndrome (LARS) and incontinence. The patient needed a sacral nerve stimulation (SNS) system implantation and a rectal resection with terminal descending colostomy. Regarding the relationship to the robotic system, 24 cases were judged as unlikely or unrelated, while 1 case was considered probable, 3 cases were considered possible, and one case was deemed unassessable. The case judged probable was the same case, where the patient needed an SNS system implantation. The three possible cases were derived from radical prostatectomy and were described with a subcutaneous emphysema of the neck, haematuria, and a distended abdomen with obstipation.

Serious adverse events (SAEs)

In the 966 surgery cases, a total of 23 serious adverse events were recorded, with a respective SAE ratio of 2.3%. The findings revealed significant differences between specialties and specific procedures, specifically related to the following procedures: radical prostatectomies (3 SAEs), gastrectomy (1 SAE), liver surgery (1 SAE), sigmoid resection (1 SAE), and rectal resection (17 SAEs). Hence, most SAEs were identified again in gastrointestinal surgery, and a smaller number were identified in urological surgery, whereas gynecology reported none. Investigating the SAEs’ severity revealed ten SAEs to be considered severe, 11 were considered moderate, while 2 resulted in the death of the patient. These two patients were a) an esophageal melanoma with a secondary robotically assisted gastrectomy; in the aftermath of this procedure, the patient developed a multi-organ failure, and b) a liver tumor that was treated with a robotically assisted partial liver resection with consequent bleeding and multi-organ failure. Regarding the relationship between the SAEs and the robotic system, the safety committee’s assessment identified two cases that may be connected to the robotic procedure. At the same time, 20 events were deemed unlikely or unrelated to the robotic procedure, and one case was considered unassessable. The two cases with an assumed possible relationship were both rectal resections, with one bladder injury and one anastomosis leak.

The overall average time between the procedure and the diagnosis of the SAE was 5.33 days (0–18 days), with a possible causality to the robot averaging 8 days.

Follow-up outcome

Data from 27 patients undergoing oncological procedures in gynecology were used to investigate follow-up outcomes. As a late complication requiring surgical intervention, we recorded one patient who developed a suture insufficiency of the vaginal blind pouch, and the same patient also experienced lymph node metastasis. Regarding adjuvant therapies, 13 patients (48.15%) required further treatment: one patient received chemotherapy alone (6 cycles carboplatin/ebetaxel), and three patients were treated with a combination of chemo- and radiotherapy (1. patient: 6 cycles carboplatin + Paclitaxel + 4 cycles cisplatin (Interlace) + percutaneous radiotherapy of the pelvis and pelvic lymphatic pathways up to the aortic bifurcation combined with intrauterine brachytherapy; 2. patient: 6 cycles carboplatin/ebetaxel + vaginal brachytherapy 3 × 7 Gy; 3. patient: radio-/chemotherapy according to PORTEC-3 + Radiotherapy according to PORTEC-3). Finally, nine patients received treatment with radiotherapy alone (eight patients: vaginal brachytherapy 3 × 7 Gy; one patient: once vaginal brachytherapy 14 Gy + percutaneous radiotherapy to the pelvic region 50 Gy).

Discussion

This study provides a detailed examination of a substantial cohort of 966 oncological patients undergoing robotic surgery with the Senhance™ Surgical System across three distinct surgical disciplines. The large sample size enables meaningful observations regarding procedural patterns, temporal aspects, technical considerations, and early outcomes.

Our findings highlight urology as the specialty contributing the most significant number of cases, underscoring its established role as a frequent adopter of robotic surgery in oncological settings, which may potentially pave the way for other disciplines. [14] Furthermore, our results indicate the existence of a dominant surgical procedure within each discipline, suggesting expertise in these frequently performed interventions. While some of these procedures, such as radical prostatectomy in urology or rectal resection in gastrointestinal procedures, can still be technically demanding, specialized skills, a supportive robotic system, and efficient workflows among the surgical teams may mitigate some of the inherent challenges associated with complex oncological cases.

The analysis of surgical duration and console times provides valuable context for performing oncological surgery with the Senhance™ Surgical System. Several intriguing aspects emerge when examining procedure time, conversion rates, and instrument changes together across specialties. While gynecological procedures such as radical hysterectomy demonstrated the shortest operative times, this efficiency appeared to come at the same time with the highest conversion and instrument change rates. This might potentially indicate a concentrated period of heightened technical demand, such as adhesions due to previous abdominal surgery, necessitating specialized instruments. The fact that gynecology also presented the smallest patient cohort could further imply that specific gynecological procedures remain particularly challenging for robotic adoption. For instance, ovarian carcinoma would be another indication for surgery. However, because the respective diagnosis is often made at an advanced stage, open surgery is typically the only viable option. Interestingly, despite the high instrument changes, the overall duration of radical hysterectomy did not appear to be significantly compromised when compared to other findings. Takmaz and Güngör found an average time for radical hysterectomy at 150 min (SD: 180) [15]. Our result therefore indicates a short time for instrument change. In contrast, the more experienced urological surgeons exhibited longer procedure times but demonstrated minimal conversions and fewer instrument changes than their gynecology counterparts. Gastrointestinal surgery displayed a wide range in procedure times for rectal resection, likely reflecting the diverse complexity of cases. However, their remarkably low conversion rate and below-average instrument changes suggest a relatively low challenge of this procedure and, at the same time, a strong potential for future robotic implementation. The occurrence of intraoperative conversions requires nuanced interpretation. It is essential to note that all conversions were unplanned conversions. Planned conversions might be strategically incorporated into the procedure, representing a hybrid approach rather than a conversion. In colorectal surgery, a “planned conversion” to enable stapler use is frequently part of the surgical strategy. This is possible and even entirely feasible given the shared trocar access between the Senhance™ Surgical System and laparoscopic surgery. This enables the leading surgeon to halt an ongoing robotic procedure at any time and switch to laparoscopic instruments, for example, to perform anastomoses. It is hence crucial to emphasize that conversions (planned and unplanned) with the Senhance™ Surgical System, executed without the need for additional trocar placements, should not inherently be viewed as adverse outcomes but rather as proof of the surgeon’s adaptability in ensuring optimal patient care.

The relatively minimal blood loss observed across the cohort, even in longer procedures, additionally highlights the precision and controlled nature of robotic surgery in these oncological settings. The reported real malfunctions of technical equipment, while rare, underscore the reality of working with advanced surgical systems. Optimal trocar placement, patient positioning (e.g., the degree of Trendelenburg and the height of the table), arm setup, and positioning of the robot to the patient are aspects that need to be delicately fine-tuned for each patient individually in the pre-surgical setting to eliminate limited motion or collision of the robotic arms. Consequently, these incidents are more likely to occur in the early stage of a surgeon’s or the entire team’s learning curve, and experienced cockpit surgeons can usually manage or prevent these situations without further restrictions. However, rushed pre-surgical settings can compromise careful preparation, increasing the likelihood of technical difficulties during the procedure and potentially prolonging operative time or impacting surgical efficiency. Anticipating and effectively managing such events is a critical component of surgical safety protocols. The distinction made between “limited motion” as a warning and actual instrument failures (e.g., ultrasonic knife, bipolar, scissors) is essential to emphasize. While warnings can be addressed without significant disruption, true malfunctions necessitate prompt troubleshooting and, in some cases, instrument exchange.

The consistent use of 5 mm standard laparoscopic instruments across the most frequently selected options suggests a degree of standardization and familiarity within the robotic platform for these oncological procedures. These also build on the experiences collected in laparoscopic surgeries.

The rate of AE and SAEs observed in the present investigation compares favorably with those reported in the broader oncologic surgery literature [16–18]. While SAE rates in cancer surgery vary considerably and typically range from 12.5% to 20.1% depending on cancer type, patient characteristics, and expertise [16], some cohorts might even reach lower rates as illustrated at 5–15% for gynecological cancer surgery [17]. However, our data consistently highlight lower complication rates. SAEs’ rates also depend on the surgical approach (minimally invasive vs. open surgery), and minimally invasive approaches, such as laparoscopic or robotic-assisted techniques, have demonstrated reduced (S)AE rates compared to open surgery [18]. Within this context, the presented low SAE incidence underscores the potential safety benefits of the Senhance Surgical System, especially when benchmarked against national databases such as the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) [19].

The early follow-up data, indicating an overall normal recovery and oncological outcome in the vast majority of patients, are encouraging. The comparability of these outcomes to standard oncological follow-ups in gynecology suggests that robotic surgery did not cause a negative impact on the long-term outcome [20].

Limitation

A limitation of this study is the lack of data on the total number of surgeons within each surgical discipline, as well as the absence of a subjective assessment by the surgeons regarding the perceived difficulty or complexity of the respective procedures. Consequently, interpreting the observed outcomes without this subjective context warrants caution. In addition, a major limitation is represented by the follow-up data being only present for the smallest discipline cohort, marking an imbalance which limits the generalizability. A primary reason for the unavailability of follow-up data from gastrointestinal procedures and urology is that the registry was not designed to provide extended follow-up. However, we had the advantage of accessing data from one site with a rigorous follow-up program, allowing us to analyze outcomes from a fractional perspective. Lastly, patient selection across the centers likely varied depending on their experiences with the system, and some probably prioritized diagnosis or patients deemed well suited for the benefits of robotic surgery.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present investigation provides valuable insights into the application of the Senhance™ Surgical System, indicating that robotically assisted surgery for oncologic surgery with the Senhance™ Surgical System is safe and feasible. The overall representable procedure times with intraoperative instrument changes, the relatively low blood loss and conversion rates, the manageable nature of technical challenges and malfunctions, and the early positive follow-up data contribute to our understanding of the role of robotic surgery in these complex cases. The low number of AE and SAE indicates an important role for robotics and the Senhance™ Surgical System in oncological surgery. Further research, including surgeon-reported assessments of case complexity and longer term oncological outcomes, would further enhance the understanding and optimize the application of robotic surgery in oncological care and treatment.
