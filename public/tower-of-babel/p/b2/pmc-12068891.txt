Rupture mechanics of blood clots: Influence of fibrin network structure on the rupture resistance
Authors: Ranjini K Ramanujam, Farkhad Maksudov, Rebecca A Risman, Rustem I Litvinov, John W Weisel, John L Bassani, Valeri Barsegov, Prashant K Purohit, Valerie Tutwiler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmbolization is a leading cause of mortality, yet we know little about clot rupture mechanics. Fibrin provides the main structural and mechanical stability to blood clots. Previous studies have shown that altering the concentration of coagulation activators (thrombin or tissue factor (TF)) has a significant impact on fibrin structure and viscoelastic properties, but their effects on rupture properties are mostly unknown. Toughness, which corresponds to the ability to resist rupture, is independent of viscoelastic properties. We used varying TF concentrations to alter the structure and toughness of human plasma clots. We performed single-edge notch rupture tests to examine fibrin toughness under a constant strain rate and we assessed viscoelastic mechanics using rheology. We utilized fluorescent confocal and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to quantify the fibrin network structure under varying TF concentrations. Our results revealed that increased TF concentration resulted in increased number of fibrin fibers with a reduction in network pore size, thinner and shorter fibrin fibers. Increasing TF concentration yielded a maximum toughness at mid-TF concentration, such that fibrin diameter and number of fibers underlie a complex role in influencing the rupture resistance of blood clots, resulting in a nonmonotonic relationship between TF and toughness. A simple mechanical model, built on our findings from our Fluctuating Spring (FS) computational model, adopted to estimate the fracture toughness (critical energy release rate) as a function of TF predicts trends that are in good agreement with experiments. The differences in mechanical responses point to the importance of studying the structure-function relationships of fibrin networks, which may be predictive of the tendency for embolization.Keywords: Fibrin, Rupture, Biomechanics, Fracture, Thrombosis

Introduction

Blood coagulation is a critical component of the protective hemostatic system that stems blood loss following vascular injury and prevents hemorrhages. Vessel wall damage is followed by the expression of a transmembrane protein named tissue factor (TF) on the surfaces of perturbed endothelial cells and other activated cells within and outside the blood vessel [1]. Upon exposure to blood, TF initiates enzymatic reactions (extrinsic blood clotting pathway), leading to generation of thrombin, a serine protease [1]. Thrombin catalyzes partial cleavage and the conversion of fibrinogen, a blood plasma glycoprotein, into fibrin monomers. These monomers polymerize spontaneously in a half-staggered manner into double-stranded protofibrils, which then grow longitudinally and aggregate laterally into longer and thicker fibrin fibers. During elongation and thickening, the fibers branch to form a three-dimensional fibrin network. Thrombin-activated factor XIIIa, a plasma transglutaminase, catalyzes covalent intermolecular crosslinking within fibers, imparting mechanical and chemical stabilization to the fibrin network. Thus, fibrin provides the necessary mechanical stability to blood clots, enabling them to resist the dynamic intra- and extravascular forces in vivo, which is essential for maintaining hemostasis and preventing bleeding [2,3].

Under many pathological conditions, excessive blood clotting in vivo contributes to cardiovascular diseases (CVD), which is the primary cause of death worldwide. It is estimated that roughly 127.9 million Americans (48.6 %) ≥20 years of age have CVD, including coronary heart disease, heart failure, stroke, or hypertension [4]. Blood clotting disorders in CVD usually manifest as thrombosis, the unwarranted formation of obstructive intravascular blood clots, which disrupt blood flow causing life-threatening conditions, such as myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke. A serious complication of thrombosis is embolization, where a portion of the clot ruptures and lodges in downstream vessels, inducing ischemia and tissue damage. Venous thromboembolism, a combination of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolization, is a major global cause of mortality and morbidity, affecting 60,000–100,000 Americans annually [5]. Cerebral artery obstruction and ischemic stroke can result from the embolization of arterial or cardiac thrombi, while pulmonary embolism arises from deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in the lower limbs or pelvis. Pulmonary embolism has been demonstrated to raise the mortality rate by 10-fold when compared to DVT alone [5]. In addition to CVD, viral and bacterial infections, active malignancy, pathological pregnancy, major surgery, inflammation, etc., are among the most significant risk factors for the incidence of thromboembolism [6–10]. Despite the high clinical importance, it is unclear what factors and conditions affect a blood clot’s ability to resist rupture and prevent embolization. They must be a priori related to mechanical properties of blood clots and thrombi.

The mechanical characteristics of blood clots and thrombi are primarily determined by the structure and amount of fibrin [2,3]. Fibrin structure is modulated by the concentration of fibrinogen, enzymatic and non-enzymatic kinetics of fibrin formation, biochemical microenvironment, and the presence of blood cells, namely platelets, erythrocytes, and leukocytes [2]. In (pro)thrombotic conditions, usually associated with elevated fibrinogen levels in blood and/or increased intravascular thrombin generation, fibrin clot structure has been reported to be severely altered [11–15]. For example, clots formed from the blood of patients with thrombotic disorders had stiffer fibrin networks made up of thin, highly branched fibers [11,13–15]. We and others have shown correlations between altered fibrin content of clots, and other changes in fibrin structure and the rupture toughness of clots [16–18]. Despite reported evidence linking fibrin structure of in vitro blood plasma clots to thrombotic risks in patients, the mechanisms underlying the relationship between clot structure and altered rupture properties remain largely unknown. Therapeutic interventions in thrombosis are limited, primarily relying on the enzymatic dissolution of fibrin networks, mechanical removal of a thrombus (thrombectomy), and prevention of new clot formation (anticoagulants and anti-platelet drugs), all of which carry a significant risk of bleeding and may not be effective [6,19]. Exploring the relationship between the mechanical rupture response of the fibrin network and its structural organization can offer a foundation for understanding the biomechanics of fibrin-containing blood clots and thrombi and can inform the development of new prophylactic and treatment modalities in thrombosis.

From a theoretical perspective, the process of blood clot embolization is fundamentally mechanical, and clots embolize or fracture when hemodynamic and other forces surpass the clot’s resistance to rupture, called fracture toughness. The fracture toughness of fibrin, a crucial parameter influencing its mechanical integrity, refers to the material’s ability to resist crack propagation and tolerate defects. Examined through established fracture mechanics techniques, fibrin’s rupture behavior involves the release of strain energy and dissipation due to fluid motion through pores as a crack extends. Fibrin’s toughness is determined by the critical energy release rate (Gc), which is independent of bulk viscoelastic properties [20]. Our previous studies have revealed that fibrin’s toughness is a fundamental property, independent of variations in specimen size or shape [17,20,21]. Studies have demonstrated that in vitro fibrin clots are less resilient to rupture than other biological hydrogels, such as collagen, cartilage, and others, despite being extremely deformable [20]. This renders the clot vulnerable to damage, further accentuating the need to understand blood clot rupture mechanisms. Moreover, clots are heterogeneous [22] and inevitably predisposed to forming cracks and defects. Such defects can propagate and cause clot rupture, thereby increasing the risk of embolization. Hence, it is paramount to study the mechanical behavior of fibrin in the presence of cracks to understand the mechanism and progression of thrombotic embolization.

In our previous studies, we demonstrated that the structural modifications in fibrin from elevating fibrinogen concentration from 1 to 10 mg/ml result in a 5-fold increase in fracture toughness [16,17]. However, since altering fibrin(ogen) concentration induces complex changes in fibrin network structure, we could not distinguish between the contributions of fibrin volume fraction and other changes in network structure, such as fiber diameter, pore size, fiber length, number of fibers and branch point density to the fracture toughness. Thus, the rupture mechanism remains uncertain, prompting an investigation into the contributions to toughness from modified protein content and other structural alterations. The present study aims to delineate the microstructural mechanisms governing resistance to rupture under a constant fibrinogen concentration, while varying other network structure parameters. To induce modifications in the organization of the fibrin network, while also maintaining a constant protein content, we manipulated the exogenous TF concentration ([TF]) from femtomolar to nanomolar to initiate blood plasma clotting [1]. Here, we aim to delineate the links between fibrin structure and rupture mechanics to understand blood clot (in)stability.

Collectively, the results obtained underscore the influence of fibrin network structure on the rupture resistance of blood clots, highlighting the contribution of fiber diameter, length and number of fibers, in regulating this mechanism. It is shown that the fracture toughness of fibrin gels is not a monotonic function of [TF] and that there is a maximum in fracture toughness due to competing effects at the network and fiber levels. Our experiments indicate that the fracture toughness of clots depends not only on the volume fraction of fibrin but also on the micro-mechanics of the fibers themselves. The micro-mechanics and flaw tolerance of fibers depends on their geometry, and this affects their critical strain to rupture which is an input into our micro-mechanical model for fracture toughness. The fiber critical strain eventually manifests in a critical strain for the whole network and this is captured by our Fluctuating Spring (FS) model for the force-stretch response which accounts for fiber rotation, stretching and Weibull statistics of failure.

Materials and methods

Clot preparation

Frozen citrated pooled human blood plasma from healthy donors was obtained from a commercial source (Cone Bioproducts Inc., Texas, USA). The fibrinogen concentration in the pooled plasma was 2.9 mg/ml. Clotting was activated by adding 25 mM CaCl2 (final) and lipidated recombinant human TF (thromboplastin, Dade Innovin reagent, Henry Schein, NY, USA) at final concentrations 30, 75, 150 and 600pM to plasma samples pre-warmed at 37 °C. The clots were allowed to form for at least 1 h at 37 °C.

Confocal microscopy of unstrained and strained plasma clots

Unstrained clots

Fibrin was labeled by supplementing plasma with Alexa Fluor 488-labeled fibrinogen (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) at 0.03 mg/ml final concentration (labeled fibrinogen ~1 % of unlabeled fibrinogen) prior to clot initiation. Clotting was activated with TF as previously described above (Section 1) and clots were allowed to form at 37 °C for 1 h. The mature clots were imaged using a Zeiss LSM 780 fluorescent confocal microscope (Zeiss, White Plains, New York) with a 63 × magnification with an oil immersion objective with a numerical aperture of 1.2. The pixel size was 2048 × 2048. For image analysis, z-stack images of at least 30 slices were taken at a 1-μm slice thickness. At least three replicates per condition were imaged to ensure data reliability.

Strained clots

Plasma samples were supplemented with fluorescently labeled fibrinogen and clotting was initiated as described previously. Clots were formed in a flat Teflon mold 20 × 20 × 4 mm. Velcro strips were placed on both the top and bottom surfaces of the mold before adding 3 ml of TF-initiated plasma. Clots were formed with Velcro on the top and bottom of the testing region to ensure a stable grip in the stretching device (see Fig S1). The clots were allowed to polymerize at 37 °C for 1 h before being transferred to 4 °C overnight. Clots were clamped in a custom-made tensile stretching apparatus and a single-edge crack was incised into one of the edges. The samples were manually stretched to 20 % perpendicular to the crack direction, followed by fixing for 30 min while still under strain in 2 % glutaraldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline. The fixed clots were removed from the stretching apparatus, and confocal microscopy images of the clots at various regions were captured, as mentioned above. Unstrained clots served as controls. At least two replicates per condition were imaged.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of unstrained plasma clots

Plasma clots were formed as previously described, allowed to polymerize at 37 °C for 1 h, and washed 3 times, 15 min each, in a 50 mM sodium cacodylate buffer containing 150 mM NaCl (pH 7.4). Clots were then fixed in 2 % glutaraldehyde in the cacodylate buffer overnight at room temperature. Increasing concentrations of ethanol (30–100 % v/v) were used to dehydrate the clot samples and hexamethyldisilazane was applied to replace ethanol, followed by air drying of the sample. Further, a layer of gold-palladium was sputter coated onto the samples using an EMS 150T ES and imaged using a Hitachi S-4800 FE-STEM-EDS obtained with 20 kV at 10,000 × magnification. At least two replicates per condition and nine images from randomly selected areas per replicate were taken.

Image analysis

The line measure tool in FIJI (ImageJ) software was used to measure fiber diameters in SEM and fiber length and pore size from confocal microscopy images of fibrin clots. Fiber alignment was measured as changes in the angle of individual fiber fibers with respect to the horizontal edge of the image corresponding to 0° using the line measure tool. The orientational alignment parameter was determined by taking cos(2θ). Branch points, which are defined as the intersection of two or more fibers (Y), were manually counted per area on SEM images (N10μm2). In order to prevent bias and human error, we had two separate experimentalists to assess the imaging data. The number of fibers per region in confocal image was measured manually using FIJI software.

Dynamic turbidimetry of plasma clots

For dynamic turbidimetry, clotting was initiated as described and 100 μl of TF-activated plasma was immediately transferred to a standard 96-well plate. Clot formation in a well was tracked using a detector moving SpectraMax Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, California) at a wavelength 405 nm, in a humid chamber at 37 °C. Clotting kinetics reflected by changes in the optical density (OD) were recorded for at least 30 min with 15-s intervals until a plateau was reached. Turbidity was normalized to the first point taken as zero to remove background. Optical density was divided by the maximum optical density to normalize that the peak is a fully formed clot. The lag time was measured as the time taken for OD to reach 0.015 after normalization to the first point, as described in [23]. The rate of clot formation was measured as the slope of the linear region in the normalized to maximum optical density curves.

Dynamic rheometry of plasma clots

To assess the overall dynamic viscoelastic properties of the polymerizing clots, plasma was activated by adding 25 mM CaCl2 and [TF] ranging from 30 to 600pM and the samples were immediately transferred to form a clot between the horizontal plates of an oscillatory rheometer (Mcr 302e, Anton-Paar, Graz, Austria). Measurements were taken every 2 s for 20 min using a 20-mm parallel plate, a gap of 0.35 mm, a 4.28 % strain, and a frequency of 7.8 Hz during fibrin polymerization at 37 °C. The storage (G′) and loss (G′′) moduli were recorded. At least three replicates per condition were tested.

Fracture testing of uncracked and cracked plasma clots

Mechanical tests were performed to determine the toughness, mechanical strength, and extensibility of plasma clots without or with perpendicular cracks. Clots in the mold were allowed to form as mentioned previously. The clots were removed carefully and loaded to a Biomomentum mechanical tester (Mach-1 v500cs, Biomomentum Inc., Quebec, Canada) using clamps. Tensile testing was performed at a 2 mm/min displacement rate, corresponding to a nominal strain rate of 0.1/min. Corresponding force vs. displacement curves were analyzed to determine ultimate tensile strength (maximum force prior to rupture) and extensibility (maximum extension prior to rupture). To calculate the fracture toughness of clots (see below), cracks (10–35 % of the clot width) were incised at a single edge of the clot prior to stretching. Flat, small, reflective particles made up of polyethylene terephthalate were sprinkled on selected clots prior to loading to measure the strain field distribution in the rupture region. Continuous high-definition videos were recorded in parallel for all tests. For all conditions, at least ten samples with varying crack sizes were tested over three days and analyzed to ensure mitigation of differences in day-to-day variability.

Fluctuating spring model of tensile deformation of fibrous networks

To elucidate the mechanical response of fibrin networks under deformation, we utilized the Fluctuating Spring (FS) model [25]. The FS model described in detail in the SI. Recent experiments and computer simulations on single fibrin fibers [26–29], along with our previous research [25], indicate that the mechanical response of the fibrin network is inherently non-linear. The origin of this nonlinearity is the mechanical properties of fibrin fibers, which first align due to the straightening of fibrin protofibrils forming fibers [26] and then stretch [25]. This process ultimately results in the elongation of fibrin fiber strands and extension of fibrin fiber network. The FS model builds in these mechanical effects. Fiber alignment regimes I and stretching regime II – Upon application of external force F→, the fibers align along the direction of force. As strain ε increases, the fraction of aligned fibers increases with increasing ε, as evident from SEM and TEM images [25]. As summarized in the SI, the FS model conceptualizes the network as N interconnected assembly of 20-mm long fibrin fiber strands, formed by aligned fibrin fibers. As increasingly more fibrin fibers become aligned, the number of aligned fiber strands increases as N(ε)=Nst1−exp−ε/ε0, where ε0 is the characteristic strain for alignment and Nst is the total number of fibrin fiber strands forming the fibrin network. The Young’s modulus of the bulk fibrin network Y(ε) is directly proportional to y – the average stiffness of individual fibrin fibers, i.e. Y(ε)=yN(ε). The point ε=ε0 marks the transition from the fiber alignment regimes I to the fiber stretching regime II. As ε>ε0 keeps increasing, the stiffness of the network increases linearly approaching the maximum value Ymax=yNst. In the linear regime II, the network’s response to applied force is primarily attributed to the elongation (stretching) of individual fibrin fiber strands (Fig. 6). Fiber rupture regime III – For ε<ε*, where ε* is the critical strain threshold, the fibrin fibers undergo purely stretching deformation [25]. Above this threshold (ε>ε*), the fibrin fiber strands begin to rupture (Fig. 1B). The point ε=ε* marks the transition from the fiber stretching regime II to fiber rupture regime III. At ε*, which corresponds to the stress threshold σ(ε*), the derivative (dσ/dε vs. ε) starts to decrease rapidly (Fig. 6), and the stress-strain (σε) curve shows a stress peak (stress maximum on the σε-curve). The statistical distribution of the maximum stresses experienced by the fibrin network defines the network failure. To model the survival probability of the fibrin network s, we employ the Weibull distribution [30] s=exp−(σ/Δσ)m=exp−(ε/2Δε)m, in which the mechanical cooperativity parameter m describes mechanical coupling among fibrin fibers through branch points they form, and the stress range Δσ (or strain range Δε=Δσ/2Ymax)describes the mechanical rupture of the fibrin fiber strands. Probabilistic fracture mechanics commonly employs the Weibull distribution to model damage accumulation in materials like fibrous networks [31–34]. Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.4.0 software package was used for all analyses. D’Agostino and Pearson’s test was first performed to verify normality of data arrays. Outliers were identified and removed using the Grubbs’ test. All data are represented as mean ± standard error of the mean unless otherwise specified. One-way ANOVA and Tukey post hoc tests were performed to detect significant statistical group differences between groups. Kruskal-Wallis test was used for non-parametric analysis, and Welch’s corrected ANOVA was used for samples with different standard deviations. The correlation matrix was generated using Pearson’s correlation test to find the relationships between parameters. Best-fit regression lines were obtained using a simple linear regression test. For all statistical tests alpha=0.05 was set as a significance level.

Results

Structure of fibrin clots and kinetics of fibrin formation as a function of [TF]

To characterize the structure of plasma clots formed at increasing [TF] (30, 75, 150, 600pM), (Equivalent thrombin concentrations, calculated as given in [35], are provided in the supplement) we performed confocal microscopy experiments, which showed hydrated fibrin fibers as straight rod-like structures forming a 3D network (Fig. 1A–D). Confocal microscopy revealed a significant decrease in average fibrin fiber length and pore size when [TF] was increased from 30 to 600pM (16 to 7.6 μm, p < 0.001, and 6.7 to 2.3 μm, p < 0.0001, respectively) (Fig. 1E and F).

SEM of fibrin networks was carried out to capture high-resolution images and perform 2D structural quantifications (Fig. 2A–D). SEM images showed a significant decrease in average fiber diameter (0.185 to 0.135 μm, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2E) and an increase in the number of branch points per unit area (N10μm2) (4 to 8, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2F) as the [TF] increased from 30 to 600pM. The average fiber diameter of [TF] at 75 and 150pM clots was significantly lower than that of 30pM samples (p < 0.01). An increase in average number of fibers (9 to 16 p < 0.0001) when [TF] was increased from 30 to 600pM was observed (Fig. 2G). We see fewer number of fibers at low [TF] because these fibrin fibers show increase in diameter due to delayed polymerization rate. Thus, fewer but thicker fibers are formed at low [TF] whereas more, but thinner fibers are seen at high [TF].

Mechanical characteristics of plasma clots as a function of [TF] and fibrin ultrastructure

Viscoelastic characteristics of fibrin clots formed at various [TF]

The dynamic rheological characteristics of plasma clots formed at [TF] = 30, 75, 150, and 600pM are shown in Fig. 4. A clotting period of 15 min was sufficient for the full development of clot stiffness. While the storage modulus (G′) represents the elastic or storage component of a material’s response to deformation, the loss modulus (G′′) represents the viscous or dissipative component. G′ and G′′ were determined at the final points of dynamic turbidimetry. Storage and loss moduli decreased when [TF] concentration was increased from 30 to 600pM (Fig. 4A and B). Quantification of average storage and loss moduli revealed a sharp decrease from 60 to 18 Pa, p < 0.0001, for G and from 6 to 3 Pa, p < 0.01, for G′′ (Fig. 4C and D). Thus, clots formed at low and intermediate [TF] are stiffer (higher storage modulus). Indeed, thicker and longer fibers typical for the lower [TF] provide more resistance to deformation and bending, resulting in a greater overall stiffness of fibrin clots.

Dependence of clot rupture mechanics on [TF]

Plasma clots formed at increasing [TF] without or with varying perpendicular crack lengths (10–35 % of the width) were stretched in the axial direction at a constant strain rate (Fig. 5A). Force vs. extension curves for all conditions were plotted in parallel with high-definition video recordings. Fig. 5B shows representative force-extension curves of individual clots formed at [TF] = 30, 75, 150, and 600pM with ~20 % crack length. Cracked samples ruptured through the progression of a crack that propagated horizontally, perpendicular to the direction of force applied. Cracked samples were not subjected to quantification of maximum force (Fmax) and critical displacement (Δc), which is the maximum displacement at Fmax prior to rupture, as both parameters are contingent on the crack lengths incised prior to the application of force. Instead, the cracked samples were analyzed for fracture toughness, shown in Fig. 5C. The highest toughness was observed at the intermediate [TF] = 75 and 150pM [TF], underscoring a non-monotonic profile as [TF] increases. The Fmax and Δc, were quantified for uncracked samples, where uncracked samples broke at the clamp, and plotted in Fig. 5D–E, which revealed a non-monotonic relationship between [TF] and both Fmax and Δc, highlighting that mid-range [TF] exhibits higher strength (higher Fmax) and greater extensibility (Δc) compared to both low and high [TF]. Consistent with the mechanics of uncracked clots, expectedly, fracture toughness values showed no correlation with crack lengths at a given [TF] (p > 0.05), corroborating that toughness is a material property and is independent of the size and shape of materials, as illustrated in Fig S3. Furthermore, the strain field distribution at 30pM [TF] exhibited higher non-uniformity (indicated by a wider spread of data) compared to samples at 600pM [TF], particularly in the rupture region as shown in Fig S4.

Theoretical analysis

Fracture toughness of fibrin clots as a function of [TF]

To estimate the fracture toughness of the fibrin gels, we idealize them in the undeformed (reference) configuration as a meshwork of regular cubes filled with liquid. We refer to these cubes as representative volume elements (RVEs) for a continuum model. The cubes are of side L and each edge of the cube is made of a fibrin fiber of diameter D. Then the volume fraction of solid in the cube (or gel) is ϕSref=AD2/L2 where A is a constant that depends on the geometry of the network [36]. Note that ϕsref remains fixed in all our experiments although D and L change as the [TF] changes. Fig S5 shows that the ratio D/L remains nearly constant irrespective of the [TF]; this is expected since the solid volume fraction ϕsref is held fixed. Now to estimate the fracture toughness, we imagine a cube right ahead of the crack tip in the reference configuration. When the fibers in this cube that are perpendicular to the crack face break, then the crack advances by distance L and the crack face area increases by L2 in the reference configuration. To estimate the critical energy released when crack advances by one cube (RVE) ahead of the crack tip, we consider three parts, viz. (a) the energy released in the crack-tip RVE due to loss of elastic energy stored in the fibers when they break, Gcnet (b) the energy released in that RVE due to liquid from the gel escaping into the bath when the deformed cube holding it disintegrates, Gcmix and (c) the energy dissipated as liquid diffuses away from that RVE as it is deformed to the critical strain for failure, Gcdiff. Our goal is to determine how each of these contributions scales with D, L etc., and therefore deduce the dependence of the fracture toughness Gc on the geometry of the network, which in turn is determined by the [TF]. Mechanical regimes of forced deformation and rupture of a cracked fibrin network

Consistent with our previous findings at [TF] = 75pM [16,38] and [TF] = 30pM, stress-strain curves and their derivatives revealed the following three distinctly different mechanical deformation regimes in the fibrin fiber networks (Fig. 6). For the case of fibrin gel formed at [TF] = 30pM with a α=22% crack (red curves in Fig. 6), these regimes are: 1) the weakly non-linear regime I of network deformation for ε<7 %, with an increasing slope (dσ/dε), primarily characterized by fibrin fibers straightening; 2) the linear regime II of deformation at intermediate strains for 7%<ε<33%, with a roughly constant slope, dominated by fiber stretching; and 3) the fiber rupture regime III for ε>ε*=33%. This last regime is defined by a peak stress (critical stress σcr) corresponding to a critical strain (σcr=σεcr). Subsequently, the stress rapidly declines to zero for ε>εcr.

The strain threshold ε* (dashed curves in Fig. 6) can be determined from experimental stress-strain curves as described in Materials and Methods. The strain range, Δε=εcr−ε* (approximately half the width at the strain peak), can also be identified from the stress-strain profiles in regime III (Fig. 6). Similar mechanical regimes I-III were observed in experimental σε-profiles collected for fibrin gel samples obtained for fibrin gel samples at [TF] = 75pM and 150pM, with varying single-edge crack lengths (see Fig. 6B and Fig. S6). While there is a clear separation of the experimental σε-curves into mechanical regimes I, II, and III for cracked fibrin gels formed at low [TF] = 30pM and intermediate [TF] = 75pM and 150pM, there is no initial non-linear response to the applied load in σε-curves, i.e., regime I of the σε-curves for uncracked and cracked fibrin gels obtained at the high [TF] = 600pM (see Fig. 6 and Fig. S6). For [TF] = 600pM, the characteristic strain for alignment ε0=0%; this is true for < 50 % of the curves obtained at lower [TF] (see Supplementary Results and Fig. S6). All σε-profiles obtained at [TF] = 600pM have a constant slope (dσ/dε), starting from ε=0% to ε=ε*. This suggests that deformation mechanisms for a fibrin network formed at high and low-to-intermediate values of [TF] are different. While fibrin fibers initially straighten and then stretch at low-to-intermediate [TF] values, they stretch directly from the beginning (at ε=0%) at high [TF] values.

We accounted for this by calculating the critical stress per strand, σcr,st, for each sample. This was done by dividing the critical stress, σcr, by the number of fiber strands (1−α)Nst. We assumed a uniform fiber strand density across the sample, and thus, the number of fibrin fiber strands was determined by multiplying the number of strands in an uncracked sample, Nst, by (1−α) to account for the strands that were cut out. The detailed procedure for the calculations of Nst is given in Supplementary Results. Briefly, using the results of our previous independent work on a single fibrin fiber [26], we assume that the stiffness of the protein component (fibrin network) correlates with the volume fraction of this component. Using the values of Young’s modulus for uncracked fibrin gels (Table 3), we estimated the stiffness of the fibrin network Knet (i.e., protein without water). Using the stiffness of individual fibrin fibers kf, forming a fibrin network at different values of [TF] and their lengths (see Fig. 1F), we calculated the stiffness of a single fiber strand Kst spanning the 20 mm length of the fibrin network. Then, by dividing Knet by Kst, we obtain Nst.

Fiber size effect on the critical strain to rupture of fibers

A consequence of the Weibull statistics for the critical stress (or strain) to rupture of fibrin fibers (see the FS model analysis and Supplementary Methods) is the ‘size effect’ [36]. As the volume of a fiber increases, there is a greater probability of the presence of critical flaws in it and this results in a decrease of εcr. On the other hand, it is known that thicker fibers are more extensible than thinner fibers due to the quasi-helical packing of protofibrils in a fiber [39,40]. Taking these factors into consideration, we assume that the critical stretch λcrit=1+εcrit of the fibers is given by λcrit=λc1+pL/L0−L/L0q where λc, L0, p, are constants. In this expression, the second term inside the square brackets captures the increase in λcrit as the fiber length L increases (which implies an increasing D for constant ϕsref), while the third term captures the decrease in λcrit at long lengths due to the size effect of Weibull statistics. We assume that the variation of λcrit with L mirrors the variation of εcr with L. We have performed such an exercise in Fig. 8 A using parameters p=0.087,q=53.8,L0=17.3μm to capture the trend of εcr vs. L. These parameters are used to predict the trend for λcrit which then allows us to make a qualitative prediction of the variation of Gc with L. The expression for the toughness takes the form Gcnet+Gcmix+Gcdiff=D1Lλcrit−12+D2L+D3. We sed λc=1.01, D1=104.6J/m3, D2=8.38J/m3, D3=0.96 in Fig. 8 B to capture the trend in the experimental data. The important result of this analysis is that the fracture toughness first increases, then reaches a maximum, and then decreases as L increases. The increase of Gc with increasing L would occur even if all material properties, including εcr, were independent of L, but the decrease of Gc at long lengths is a consequence of the size effect. The trend in the dependence of λcrit on fiber length is captured by the simple expression although the discrepancy between the experimental data and the analytical form is seen and is likely due to uncertainties in the measurement. However, and importantly, this simple expression leads to an analytical form for the fracture toughness vs. fiber length that captures the experimental data very well in Fig. 8B.

Discussion

Thrombin generation at the site of injury in the vascular system is one of the most important enzymatic processes that regulate the hemostatic system. Previous studies have shown a strong association between cardiovascular diseases and pathological activity of thrombin [41–43]. A clinical bleeding tendency was observed when thrombin generation was impaired [41], while a greater potential to produce thrombin is related to an increased risk of thromboembolism [44]. Increase in thrombin activity in blood plasma was also shown to profoundly affect the architecture of the fibrin network, where low thrombin activity produced clots with thick fibers and increased porosity, while clots formed with high thrombin levels were composed of a dense network of thin fibrin fibers [43,45,46]. The most potent trigger of thrombin generation in blood is TF, a transmembrane phospholipoprotein, which forms a highly active enzymatic complex (extrinsic tenase), promoting activation of factor X and the conversion of inactive prothrombin to active thrombin.

In this study, we characterized the ultrastructure of plasma clots formed at increasing exogenous [TF] of 30, 75, 150 and 600pM at a fixed normal fibrinogen concentration of ~3 mg/ml. In parallel, we investigated the viscoelastic properties and rupture mechanics of these clots to elucidate the underlying structural mechanisms of clot rupture. The chosen TF concentrations of 30, 75, 150, and 600pM were selected to examine their impact on fibrin network structure and the resulting fracture toughness of plasma clots. These concentrations are not intended to mimic in vivo [TF] but rather to serve as a tool to induce significant variations in fibrin network structure. Our primary objective is to explore the relationship between fibrin structure and function, aiming to gain a comprehensive understanding of how these structural changes influence hemostasis and the prevention of clot rupture.

Structural mechanisms governing the resistance of fibrin clots to rupture

A balance between number of fibers and fiber diameter determines the rupture resistance of blood clots

The structural analysis revealed that, as [TF] increases, there is an increase in number of fibers (Fig. 2G) but a reduction in fiber diameter (Fig. 2E). Notably, at the intermediate [TF] = 75 and 150pM, a unique combination of both relatively high number of fibers and large diameter, as shown in the SEM images (Fig. 2E and 2G respectively) was observed. The fracture toughness of clots was also seen to be the highest at 75 and 150pM values of [TF] (Fig. 5C). This suggests a non-monotonic relationship between [TF] and clot toughness, thus highlighting the significant impact of fibrin structural changes on rupture resistance.

It is known that material failure occurs at points of concentrated stress or points of mechanical weakness [47]. Fibrin clot rupture occurs when the first, second, etc. multifiber thread breaks progressively under strain, exposing the remaining fibers or fiber threads to increasing loads and causing cascading catastrophic failure of the entire network. We and others have demonstrated that the strain fields within the fibrin network are non-uniform, concentrated in the region ahead of the crack tip [17,20,21,48], and that crack propagation occurs predominantly in a direction perpendicular to the maximum stretch of fibers, which is seen directly ahead of the crack tip [17,21]. In our previous study, we showed the importance of mechanical cooperativity (parameter m in the FS model; see Master Eq. (3)), which characterizes mechanical coupling among fibrin fibers through branch points, and that this cooperativity is directly related to the number of branch points [16,25]. Lower cooperativity m, indicative of fewer branch points, corresponded to independent failure of individual fiber strands at lower stress and strain thresholds. At [TF] = 30pM, the cooperativity is low (few branch points) (Fig. 2F), which explains the lowered strength and extensibility under strain (Fig. 5D–E), causing expedited rupture of fibers. The exacerbation of this effect at [TF] = 30pM is attributed to the presence of a porous fibrin network (Fig. 1F), further reducing the fiber-fiber connections. In addition, the 30pM-[TF] samples are characterized by the presence of thicker and longer fibers (Fig. 2E and 1E respectively). Thick fibers have been observed to exhibit a heterogeneous protofibril packing characterized by a denser core within individual fibers [49,50], leading to a higher prevalence of weak regions, thus increasing their propensity to rupture. This is also observed in the non-uniform distribution of the strain fields (with high standard deviation; see Fig. S4), which further reduces the strength of the fibrin network.

The increased susceptibility to rupture observed at [TF] = 600pM can be ascribed to diminished extensibility (Fig. 5E), primarily induced by the presence of thinner and shorter fibers (Figs. 2E and 1E respectively). Our previous findings, in conjunction with those of other studies, indicate that fibers undergo alignment, thinning, and bundling as strain increases, particularly at the crack tip [17,18,20,21,51]. Fig. 3 illustrates that maximum fiber alignment is attained at 600pM [TF], suggesting that fibers reach a critical failure stretch at lower overall strains, thereby reducing their strength and extensibility. As the strength of a material is dependent on the maximum strain individual fibers can endure, the overall network strength is reduced. Further, the strain field distribution was observed to be more uniform (lower standard deviation, Fig. S4) due to increased density and cooperativity (many branch points). This would predispose fibrin networks to sequential and coordinated rupture, causing complete network failure. Note that the force-displacement curves in Fig. 5B are almost linear rather than strain-stiffening, as would be expected for fibrin gels. A likely reason is progressively increasing damage in the network. High stresses near the crack tip can cause fibrin fibers near the crack tip to break and hence reduce the tangent modulus of the material.

Viscoelastic properties of a fibrin clot are not related to fracture toughness

Fibrin is a viscoelastic polymer constituting both elastic and viscous characteristics [3]. While viscosity (plasticity or creep compliance) is characterized by irreversible deformation brought on by force, elasticity (or stiffness) is defined by reversible mechanical deformation. For fibrin clots, the elastic component is typically roughly an order of magnitude higher than the viscous component [3]. Fibrin has a high extensibility of up to 300 %, the largest for protein fibers despite its low mass fraction of protein (~0.3 % when formed from normal blood plasma) [3]. Consequently, blood clots tend to stretch under stress rather than break. Factor XIIIa-stabilized plasma clots could be stretched by an average of 2.7-fold before breaking, exceeding their relaxed length [3]. The aforementioned clot rupture becomes apparent when external forces exceed fibrin extensibility and resistance to rupture.

The effects of fibrin network structure on viscoelastic properties have been extensively studied over the decades [28,52–54]. For example, a study by Ryan et al.[52]. demonstrated that thrombin clots with a balance between high fiber diameter and high branching density exhibit large stiffness. In our study, we conducted similar experiments to measure the rheological properties of plasma clots using TF as the clotting reagent. Our results indicate that thicker fibers with low branching, formed at low [TF], exhibited maximum stiffness compared to clots with thinner fibers and high branching (Fig. 4). While this finding contrasts with the results obtained in the previous study, these differences can likely be attributed to the distinct network structure induced by TF as opposed to thrombin. Additionally, a study by Li et al. [28] on single fibrin mechanics found an inverse relationship between fiber stiffness and fiber diameter. These variations suggest a complex system and intricate relationship with structure and viscoelastic mechanics, that we are still trying to understand. Nevertheless, while viscoelastic parameters explain how a material deforms in response to an applied force, they do not provide information about the rupture or fracture mechanism (Fig. 9).

Correlation analysis.

The correlation matrix represents weak Pearson correlation coefficients observed between the bulk viscoelastic properties and fracture toughness.

Fracture toughness is the tolerance to defects and a material’s ability to resist the growth of flaws, such as a crack. It is examined here using traditional fracture mechanics techniques that have been extensively utilized to study the rupture of materials such as bone, ceramics and metals [20]. The mechanical rupture of material corresponds to the release of strain energy during fracture as a crack propagates [20]. Toughness is described by the critical energy release rate, Gc. We have previously shown that toughness is a fundamental property of clots for a given network structure and is essentially independent of the size and shape of the specimen [17,20,21]. It is defined as the amount of energy released per unit increase in crack length. Our previous study demonstrated an unexpectedly low toughness (~6.8 N/m at 2.7 mg/ml fibrinogen concentration) for fibrin hydrogels [20]; the majority of hydrogels tested for rupture are synthetic rather than biological, with toughness values of more than 10 and up to 1000 J/m2 [20]. While it is known in general that viscoelastic properties do not describe rupture mechanics, here we show experimentally for the first time for fibrin the lack of correlation between viscoelastic properties (storage and loss moduli, Fig. 4 C and D) and toughness (Fig. 5E) across all four [TF] applied (r=−0.13 and −0.31, respectively) (Fig. 8). This absence of correlation underscores the significance of investigating toughness in addition to viscoelastic mechanics.

Scaling of fracture toughness with micro-structural parameters can reveal variation in fiber level properties

In our previous studies [17,20], we used the J*-integral to calculate the fracture toughness. This method relies on computing the stress and strain fields all over the specimen using finite elements, and then using a conserved integral to compute the toughness, which includes contributions arising from the mechanical rupture of fibers as well as fluid dissipation due to flows through the network. In this paper, we adopt a micro-structural view that focuses on the crack tip and not the fields over the entire specimen. While earlier the micro-structural details informed the continuum model that was used to compute the stress and strain fields, and then the J*-integral, here the micro-structural (fiber level) information directly enters analytical expressions for the fracture toughness, allowing us to make comparisons between scaling results and experimental data.

In earlier work [20,21] it was shown that a critical strain (or stretch) ahead of the crack tip can predict the macroscopic load for crack advance, as well as the direction of crack advance in fibrin gel specimens. This critical strain was assumed to be independent of fibrin volume fraction and fiber geometry and it could give a good estimate of the critical energy release rate even in a different crack geometry [20]. In this work, it is shown that the assumption of a critical strain independent of fiber geometry may not always be realistic. For short to moderate fiber lengths, the assumption that fiber modulus and critical strain are independent of fiber geometry gives a linear dependence of fracture toughness on fiber length (or diameter, since solid volume fraction is held fixed) and this agrees with experimental trends (Fig. 8B). However, this linear dependence fails to hold for longer (and thicker) fibers and a decline in fracture toughness is seen in experiments (Fig. 8B). This could happen because fibers of larger volume may have a greater propensity to rupture due to the higher probability of flaws, and therefore a lower critical strain to failure. Furthermore, the complex helical geometry of the protofibrils forming a fiber may lead to differences in extensibility of fibrin fibers [39], even up to the critical strain for rupture. Within the theoretical analysis, we account for these factors by assuming that the dependence of the critical strain to rupture of fibrin fibers on fiber length has a maximum and mirrors that of the critical strain to rupture of the whole network, which also shows a maximum as a function of fiber length. This allows us to capture the macroscopic toughness behavior of our fibrin gels. The need for a fiber level analysis of critical strain was necessitated by the failure of the scaling law, which was based on a simplistic view of fibrin fibers.

Conclusions

We show through experiments that fracture toughness shows a non-monotonic dependence on [TF] and corresponding fibrin structural modifications. Our findings indicate that fiber diameter, length and number of fibers of the clot drive the rupture resistance of blood clots. These findings add to our understanding of the structure-function relationships in the 3D fibrin network by elucidating the relationships between structural characteristics and the fibrin mechanical response. These results provide a basis for understanding and predicting the tendency for thrombotic embolization by offering a mechanistic understanding of how variations in fibrin structure can influence clot stability under physiological conditions. Additionally, by elucidating these structure-function relationships, our study offers valuable insights that can be applied to the design of mechanically stable scaffolds for tissue engineering, where maintaining structural integrity under dynamic conditions is essential. As a future direction, we recognize the conflicting evidence regarding the role of RBCs in clot rupture. Liu et al. [51] found that whole blood clots are nearly six times tougher than fibrin-only clots, while Fereidoonnezhad et al. [18] observed decreased toughness with higher RBC volume fractions. This underscores the need for a systematic study to explore how blood cells affect clot rupture mechanisms, which we plan to investigate.

Statement of significance

Fibrin, a naturally occurring biomaterial, is the main mechanical and structural scaffold of blood clots that provides the necessary strength and stability to the clot, ensuring effective stemming of bleeding. The rupture of blood clots can result in the blockage of downstream vessels thereby blocking blood flow and oxygen supply. The fibrin network structure has been shown to influence the viscoelastic mechanical properties of clots, but has not been explored for fracture mechanics. Here, we modulate the fibrin network structure by varying the concentration of Tissue Factor (TF). Interestingly, the association between TF concentration and maximum toughness of the clots is non-monotonic. The variations in mechanical responses highlight the importance of studying the structure-function relationships of fibrin networks, as these may predict the tendency for embolization.
