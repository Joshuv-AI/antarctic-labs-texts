Associations Between Socioeconomic Status and Adult Congenital Heart Disease Mortality in the United States: Insights From the Global Burden of Disease Study
Authors: Vasupradha Suresh Kumar, Nicholas Kassebaum, Bryanna Schwartz, Jennifer Klein, Dorothy Nakiwala, Craig Sable, Anitha S. John
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe GBD (Global Burden of Disease) study provides a unique opportunity to better understand the socioeconomic and geographical drivers of clinical outcomes and mortality in adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) in the United States.MethodsWe performed a secondary data analysis using a population‐based observational study design that studied state specific data on mortality and disability‐adjusted life years from 1990 to 2021 for patients with ACHD aged 20 years and older in the United States. These data were acquired from the GBD study. We subsequently correlated these data with median household income and percentage of uninsured people in each state by using the US census bureau poverty and income data.ResultsIn 2021, the GBD study identified 292 624 (95% CI, 258 956–342 906) adults ≥20 years with CHD living in the United States with 1074 (95% CI, 964–1331) ACHD related deaths and 62 244 (95% CI, 51 820–76 301) disability‐adjusted life years. The state with the highest mortality and disability‐adjusted life years (West Virginia) also has one of the lowest median household incomes ($51 122). States with lower ACHD related mortality have higher median household incomes (r 2=0.62, P < 0.001). There is a lower correlation between mortality and the percentage of patients uninsured in each state (r 2=0.14, P=0.0068).ConclusionsThe GBD study provides unique insight into ACHD mortality and disability in the United States. Although mortality and disability rates have decreased from 1990 to 2021, patients with ACHD living in states with lower median household income and higher percentage of uninsured people have higher rates of mortality and disability. Ensuring adequate access to specialized care is warranted for this population.

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the leading cause of death from congenital defects in the United States, accounting for around 31% of deaths in individuals with birth defects in 2021. 1 Innovations in surgical and catheter‐based interventions, along with advances in medical management, have resulted in a significant increase in the survival rate of children with CHD into adulthood. 2 As a result, there is a growing population of patients with adult CHD (ACHD) with unique and complex health care needs that differ from those of pediatric patients. Access to and delivery of high‐quality, specialized care for patients with ACHD remains limited due to several barriers, including a shortage of ACHD‐trained providers, inadequate insurance coverage, and geographic inaccessibility of subspecialty cardiac centers. 3 Historically, much of the data regarding mortality and prevalence have been extrapolated from Canadian data. 4 Understanding the burden of ACHD specifically within the United States and identifying the key factors influencing mortality and long‐term outcomes are of critical public health importance.

The GBD (Global Burden of Disease) is a large‐scale research initiative that uses available population‐level data to generate population health metrics, offering a unique approach to analyze disease trends on a national and global scale. Although there have been recent publications on the burden of CHD, literature focusing on ACHD outcomes regionally in the United States is limited. Using GBD data provides an opportunity to better understand long‐term outcomes in the population with ACHD as well as define limitations in current modeling that can inform future GBD analysis. To our knowledge, no prior studies have used the GBD database to examine ACHD trends in the United States, either nationally or at the state level. Additionally, this is the first study to use the GBD database to analyze ACHD outcomes and their associations with socioeconomic factors.

We provide an analysis of 2021 GBD data to describe outcomes, including mortality, in the population with ACHD within the United States. We also correlate GBD data to information about median household income and insurance status from the US Census Bureau by state to better understand the potential impact of socioeconomic factors. This article was produced as part of the GBD Collaborator Network and in accordance with GBD protocols. 5

METHODS

For the GBD data used, the University of Washington Institutional Review Board approved the GBD study (STUDY00009060) up to July 26, 2026. All supporting data are available within the article and its online supplementary files. Some of the data are also available publicly at the US Census Bureau and the rest can be requested from the GBD database through the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation.

Case Definition

ACHD was defined by: (1) age ≥20 years; (2) presence of CHD as defined by the 2018 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) guidelines; and (3) residence in the United States. 6 The International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes for the various CHD subtypes as previously described. 7

Overview

The global health database with the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation was used for analysis in this study. All input data are available online via the GBD Input Data Sources Tool of the Global Health Data Exchange. 1 Data sets for estimating CHD epidemiology were informed by 3 mechanisms: systematic literature review for prevalence, excess mortality, and with‐condition mortality; congenital birth defects registries; and hospital administrative data. For mortality, available data through death registration and global health database's estimates of population were used. Each model's data and fit can be examined in the GBD Causes of Death Visualization for mortality analysis and GBD online Epi Visualization tool for other metrics. 8 , 9

Data Sources

Availability of data for fatal and nonfatal cases of ACHD varied widely across states and regions. 5 The Institute of Health Metrics and Evaluation has 8 online GBD tools and visualizations (GBD results tool, GBD Compare, GBD Foresight, Mortality Visualizations, Causes of Death Visualization, Epi Visualization, Burden of Proof Visualization, and GBD sources tool). All reporting is compliant with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting. 10 Deaths in the adult population due to CHD were estimated using a common cause‐specific mortality framework developed for GBD 2021. Each death was assigned to a single underlying cause. Deaths assigned to ill‐defined or nonspecific causes or intermediate causes were reassigned to probable causes of death, including CHD, with the use of statistical redistribution algorithms. This study analyzed publicly accessible, deidentified, state‐level data from the US Census Bureau and the GBD database. Because no individual‐level information was used, informed consent was not required.

Mortality Estimates in ACHD

Mortality was estimated using the Cause of Death Ensemble model, a tool specifically for GBD that runs different models (analyzing out of sample predictive validity of different statistic models and combining it to provide cause specific estimates of mortality) on the same data and chooses an ensemble of models that best reflects all the available input data. 11 The covariates used in Cause of Death Ensemble models include total congenital birth defects and congenital heart disease. 5 To account for the disparity between the deaths from individual diseases versus the overall mortality level, GBD uses cause of death correct. The cause of death correct process was used to correlate all‐cause mortality estimates with cause‐of‐death estimates. 11 Years of life lost (YLLs) were calculated by taking the difference between the longest possible life expectancy for an individual of a certain age and the age at death. 12 Mortality estimates are reported as age‐ and sex‐specific mortality rates per 100 000 population. Prevalence estimates represent the total number of individuals living with ACHD within each age‐sex group at the national level. Estimates are presented with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) reflecting uncertainty inherent to the GBD modeling process rather than sampling variability.

Estimating Disability and Disease Burden in ACHD

The Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, in collaboration with GBD experts and pediatric cardiology specialists, reorganized the classification of nonfatal CHD estimates. This reorganization considered both anatomical and clinical relevance, drawing from literature, registry data, and trend analyses. 5 The 5 CHD categories defined were (1) single ventricle and single ventricle pathway congenital heart anomalies; (2) severe congenital heart anomalies excluding single ventricle heart defects; (3) critical malformations of great vessels; (4) congenital valvular heart disease; and (5) patent ductus arteriosus, ventricular septal defect and atrial septal defect, and other congenital heart anomalies. The GBD study uses ICD codes to map causes of death and nonfatal conditions in its disease burden analysis. 7

To assess the impact of nonfatal CHD, the study used 3 key metrics: (1) YLLs due to premature death; (2) years lived with disability (YLDs), and disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs), which are a summation of YLDs and YLLs. 13 YLDs were estimated by multiplying the prevalence of each CHD sequela (by age, sex, location, cause, and year) with the corresponding disability weight. 14 These estimates are expressed per 100 000 population and include 95% UI. DisMod‐MR 2.1, a Bayesian meta‐regression tool, was used to generate consistent estimates across geography and time. It employs a geographical cascade approach, balancing differential equations to produce internally consistent values by age, sex, location, and year. 15 For all congenital conditions, incidence was set to 0 because they occur at birth, with no new cases developing afterward. 5 This distribution was derived from the updated GBD 2021 analysis using the same approach of proportional allocation based on cause‐specific mortality.

YLLs are a secondary measure of how much earlier a specific population dies before the expected age of death of the population. YLDs is a measure of years a population lives with an illness (or comorbidity).

Income and Insurance Status Assessment

State‐level data from 2021 on the percentage of uninsured individuals and median household income were obtained from the US Census Bureau. 16 According to the Census Bureau, individuals are considered “uninsured” if they lacked health coverage for the entire calendar year or were covered only through the Indian Health Service. 16 The Census Bureau collects data at the county, state, and national levels through the American Community Survey, using methods such as mail canvassing, online responses, and centralized local data collection. These data are compiled and reported by state agencies.

Statistical Analysis

All the analysis for this study was done using appropriate statistical models, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. The data from GBD all come with UIs which provides estimates of precision. 14 To assess the impact of socioeconomic factors on health outcomes, the relationship between median household income, insurance coverage, and mortality in ischemic heart disease and stroke were analyzed in adults over the age of 20 across states. This analysis aimed to compare the findings in other adult diagnoses. Using this data, an unpaired 2‐sided t test was performed on mortality and median household income by state and mortality and percentage uninsured by state. An unpaired 2‐sided t test was also performed to find the correlation between prevalence rate (per 100 000 population) and median household income and percentage uninsured by state. The P value was calculated (with statistical significance labeled as P‐value <0.05).

Mortality rate, prevalence, and DALYs were compared in men and women between the ages of 20 and 44 years old (childbearing age) to provide insight into potential impact of pregnancy on ACHD outcomes. Of note, there are no existing GBD data on mortality rates due to CHD in adults above the age of 69 years.

This study used state‐level, population‐aggregated data from the GBD study. Analyses were intended to describe national and state‐level patterns and to explore population‐level associations between ACHD outcomes and socioeconomic indicators. Multivariable modeling was not performed because data on additional potential confounders were not available.

RESULTS

The number of deaths in 2021 was 0.43/100 000 (95% UI, 0.38–0.53) and in 1990, 0.79/100 000 (95% UI, 0.69–0.83). Sex‐ and Age‐Specific Patterns in ACHD

In 2021, absolute mortality was lower in women than in men. There were 505.04 deaths among women (95% UI, 439.93–586.01) and 568.59 deaths in men (95% UI, 480.27–756.12). In 2021, the mortality from CHD in the youngest adult age group (20–24 years) was 81.72 (95% UI, 71.22–104.14) in 2021, which was lower than in older age groups such as the 55 to 59 age group, where mortality reached 126.72 (95% UI, 110.59–172.13). In 2021, ACHD was responsible for 62 243.97 DALYs (95% UI, 51 820.31–76 300.51). In 1990, ACHD was responsible for 79 216.23 DALYs (95% UI, 68 341.01–87 959.58). In 2021, women aged ≥20 years accounted for 29 463.68 DALYs (95% UI, 24 044.91–35 633.47) and men accounted for 62 243.98 DALYs (95% UI, 41 109.85–73 780.28). In 1990, in women aged ≥20 years accounted for 36 273.51 DALYs (95% UI, 3229.43–40 347.94) and men accounted for 32 780.28 (95% UI, 26 565.82–41 109.85). This indicates an overall decrease in disease burden over time among women (Table S1).

Socioeconomic Correlations With Outcomes

There was considerable variation across states in mortality rates, as well as in DALYS and YLLs (Table). In 2021, West Virginia recorded the highest mortality rate, YLLs, and DALYs, and Washington, DC had the lowest (Table).

Specifically, West Virginia had a mortality rate of 0.70/100 000 population (95% UI, 0.56–0.89), whereas in Washington, DC, the morality rate was 0.24/100 000 population (95% UI, 0.18–0.31) (Figure 1). Similarly, the DALY and YLL rates in West Virginia were 37.18 per 100 000 population (95% UI, 29.68–45.78) and 30.68/100 000 population (95% UI, 24.67–39.16), respectively whereas in Washington, DC, the DALY and YLL rates were 16.49/100 000 population (95% UI, 11.90–21.04) and 10.90/100 000 population (95% UI, 8.36–13.93), respectively.

State median household incomes in the United States in 2021 ranged from $51 122 to $91 072 (Table S2). West Virginia, which had the highest ACHD mortality rate, also had one of the lowest median household incomes at $51 122 (90% CI, $50 059–$52 185). 16 Conversely, Washington, DC had the lowest mortality rate and had the highest median income at $91 072 (90% CI, $88 170–$93 974) (Table S2). The percentage of uninsured residents also varied widely across states, from 2.9% (margin of error percentage of 0.2) in Massachusetts to 20.3% (margin of error % 0.4) in Texas (Table S2). 16

A statistically significant inverse correlation was observed between ACHD mortality and median income (R 2=0.62, P<0.001) whereas a weaker but statistically significant correlation was found between mortality and the percentage of uninsured residents (R 2=0.14, P=0.0068) (Figures 2 and 3). This relationship between mortality and socioeconomic indicators is not unique to ACHD; however, the correlation observed for stroke and ischemic heart disease in 2021 were much weaker than that with ACHD and socioeconomic indicators. In 2021 in the United States, for ischemic heart disease, the correlation between mortality and median income was R 2=0.18 (P=0.0023), and for stroke, R 2=0.11 (P=0.016). In 2021 in the United States, for ischemic heart disease, the correlation between mortality and percentage of uninsured individuals was R 2=0.02 (P=0.37) and for stroke, R 2=0.003 (P=0.69).

In contrast to mortality, ACHD prevalence demonstrated weaker association with socioeconomic indicators. A statistically significant but relatively weak direct association was observed between ACHD prevalence and median household income (R 2=0.12, P<0.05). Prevalence was inversely associated with the percentage of uninsured residents (R 2=0.21, P<0.05), with similar statistically significant but weaker association.

DISCUSSION

The GBD study uses advanced modeling techniques for estimating the burden of hundreds of diseases and holds significant promise for improving our understanding of trends in the burden of ACHD in the United States. From 1990 to 2021, mortality and DALY rates for ACHD decreased, most likely attributable to substantial advances in surgical, interventional, and medical treatment in addition to the emergence of ACHD as a recognized specialty with established care models in the United States. Although this is consistent with previously published data, there is significant variability in outcomes based on type of CHD. 17 Measures such as mortality, YLLs, and DALYs may be more instructive in gaining insight into the needs of the population with ACHD, especially highlighting regional needs. 11 , 13 , 15

Similar to previous studies that demonstrated the association between low socioeconomic status and CHD mortality in children, our study found that patients with ACHD residing in states with lower median household income and higher uninsured population have higher rates of mortality and DALYs. 18 , 19 Our analysis revealed a strong inverse correlation between median household income and ACHD mortality across US states, indicating that states with higher income levels tend to have lower mortality rates among patients with ACHD. This correlation between median household income and mortality specifically can also be seen in other chronic noncongenital cardiovascular diagnoses in adults like ischemic heart disease and stroke, although interestingly, the correlation was weak or not statistically significant in our data. 12 , 20 , 21 , 22 As these patients can be cared for by most adult cardiologists and neurologists, these results may imply that the specialized care adult patients with ACHD need is not readily available, especially to those with lower incomes. Data from Canadian centers show patients treated at ACHD centers have 22% lower mortality; additionally, 2025 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guidelines recommend that adults with CHD be cared for by specialized providers. 6 , 23 Investment into improved access to specialized care would likely improve outcomes for this population. 24 , 25

In contrast, the relationship between uninsured status and ACHD mortality was statistically significant but notably weaker. This may be due to the type of insurance coverage and variability in services covered, supported by the higher mortality rates in states with lower income where more patients will be on public insurance programs. Public insurance programs often have limited provider networks, with fewer health care systems accepting these plans, especially in rural or lower income states. 18 , 26 This can result in delayed or fragmented care, even for insured patients. In contrast, private insurance plans may offer better access to ACHD‐certified centers and subspecialty services. This is an area for future investigation into implications on long‐term outcomes and survival.

Although state‐level median household income had a strong correlation with ACHD mortality, its association with ACHD prevalence was comparatively weak as was the association between ACHD prevalence and insurance status. This pattern may suggest that socioeconomic conditions exert a substantially greater influence on outcomes following diagnosis than on disease occurrence itself. However, it may also be possible that lack of access to subspecialized care may affect disease detection and diagnosis. This is especially true as many patients with ACHD may have fallen out of subspecialty care and may not be aware of their medical history.

There is considerable geographic variability within the US population with ACHD in mortality and DALY rates, underscoring the unequal distribution of health care resources. A major concern raised by the findings is the shortage and regional variation of ACHD cardiologists across the United States, creating implications for workforce needs. 27 , 28 Many states do not have ACHD‐certified cardiologists, creating significant care gaps for individuals living with ACHD in these regions. Interestingly, the area with the lowest mortality rate (Washington, DC) also has been reported to have the best ACHD physician to patient ratio further implying access to care with adequate number of providers is associated with improved outcomes. 23 , 25 ACHD specialized physicians have knowledge in both CHD as well as in diseases of older age like acquired cardiovascular disease. 29 , 30 As the population with ACHD continues to grow due to increasing survival rates, the demand for specialized care will only rise, highlighting an urgent need for a robust workforce pipeline. 27 , 28 , 31 This not only includes increasing fellowship training opportunities but also creating more telehealth cross‐state collaborations that expands access across rural and underserved areas. Thus, there is a critical need for strategic workforce planning to meet the demands of this growing patient population.

Although mortality rates decreased from 1990 to 2021, there remained a higher rate among men versus women across all age groups. In addition, there were higher DALYs in men versus women. Although mortality was lower in women of childbearing age than men of the same age, GBD modeling does not allow for direct correlation between pregnancy and ACHD. 11 Mortality trends in ACHD have changed over time due to the heterogeneity of disease and evolving medical, interventional, and surgical therapies. 32 , 33 With the development of cardiac surgery in infants in the late 1970s and with surgeries like the arterial switch and Norwood operation in the 1980s, the populations of young adult patients with CHD in the 1990s were very different than previous. 2 , 31 , 33 As therapies have improved, mortality rates will shift as has been previously described. 33 The relationship between pregnancy in women with ACHD is important to explore in future studies in addition to further exploration of sex specific differences in outcomes.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, this analysis relied on population‐aggregated, state‐level estimates from the GBD study rather than individual‐level clinical data. As a result, observed associations between ACHD outcomes and socioeconomic indicators should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis generating, and may be influenced by unmeasured factors such as disease complexity, comorbid conditions, access to specialized ACHD care, and health care use.

There is a notable discrepancy between GBD‐derived prevalence estimates and those reported in prior studies, which have suggested ACHD prevalence in the United States to be similar to that observed in Canadian populations. 4 Current GBD modeling may underestimate the true burden of ACHD by overestimating the number of individuals classified as being “in remission” from CHD, such as those with repaired septal defects.

Mortality attributable to CHD may also be underestimated. ACHD frequently coexists with other comorbidities that contribute to death, which can obscure the role of CHD as the underlying cause. Cause‐of‐death assignment may therefore attribute mortality to related conditions, such as stroke or arrhythmia, rather than to congenital heart disease itself. This limitation may be particularly relevant for 2021, during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Additionally, deaths without a specific assigned cause but categorized as cardiovascular are redistributed through modeling, which may lead to under‐ or overrepresentation of ACHD‐related mortality. 5

Furthermore, ACHD‐specific mortality data were not available for individuals older than 69 years, potentially underestimating disease burden in older adults. Socioeconomic variables, including median household income and percent uninsured, were obtained from US Census Bureau data, which rely on surveys and local government reporting. These data may be subject to misclassification or incomplete reporting, particularly in rural areas.

Finally, prior work has demonstrated an increase in avoidable mortality in the United States, suggesting that access to health care and broader socioeconomic factors may influence mortality outcomes across conditions. 21 , 22 In this context, our findings highlight several areas for future implementation research aimed at improving ACHD care, including geographic disparities, workforce shortages, and access to specialized services. The GBD VizHub platform already incorporates select socioeconomic indicators into predictive modeling, and future inclusion of state‐level ACHD subtype data could further improve the precision of burden estimates and better inform targeted interventions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the field of ACHD is continually evolving, and it is important to have a better understanding of the long‐term outcomes of ACHD in the United States. The GBD study provides a novel way to better understand these trends in this unique patient population. Future modeling that allows for analysis of ACHD subtypes will provide better understanding of disease prevalence in the United States. Additional analysis on outcomes and proximity to ACHD specialty centers can continue to inform policy and workforce needs and improve access to care.
