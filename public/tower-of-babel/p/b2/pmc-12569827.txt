Human Papillomavirus Infection as a Risk Factor for Oral Leukoplakia: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Joud Taha, Sajesh K Veettil, Swagatika Panda, Divya Gopinath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe correlation between human papillomavirus (HPV) infection and cervical neoplasia has been well documented. Nonetheless, its correlation with the progression of oral cancer and oral leukoplakia (OL) remains contentious. We aimed to conduct a comprehensive meta-analysis to examine the most recent evidence on the association between HPV infection and OL.MethodsThe protocol was registered in the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocol, INPLASY (INPLASY202540033) and complied with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses, PRISMA 2020 requirements. A systematic literature review was performed using the electronic databases PubMed, Scopus and Wiley Library without any date or time restrictions to identify observational studies that documented any association between HPV and OL. A pooled odds ratio (OR) estimate and its associated 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated utilising the random effects model. The I2 statistic and Cochran's Q test were used to evaluate heterogeneity among studies. Sensitivity analysis and credibility ceiling tests were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings.ResultsThe database search retrieved 1716 articles, out of which 32 articles were selected for final analysis, meeting the inclusion criteria. The overall pooled OR and the corresponding 95% CI, estimated from a total of 1236 cases and 2916 controls in a random-effects model, demonstrated a positive association between HPV detection in OL (OR = 3.16; 95% CI, 2.06-4.95). OL was more strongly associated with high-risk HPV than low-risk HPV (OR = 5.42; 95% CI, 2.52-11.69 and OR = 1.90; 95% CI, 1.10-3.29, respectively). The sensitivity analysis and credibility ceiling test validated the robustness of the finding.ConclusionOral HPV infection, especially with high-risk HPV, is significantly associated with OL. Further prospective trials are encouraged to validate these results and elucidate the exact role of HPV in OL.Key words: Oral leukoplakia, HPV, OPMD, High risk HPV, Premalignant

Introduction

Oral leukoplakia (OL), an oral potentially malignant disorder (OPMD) of the oral cavity, is a white plaque or patch that emerges in the oral cavity.1 According to widely accepted international standards, OL is a white plaque of questionable risk having excluded (other) known diseases or disorders that carry no increased risk of cancer.2 The pooled estimate of OL worldwide is reported to be between 1.49% and 2.6%.1,2 Moreover, it shows a higher prevalence in males, but no variation is found between older and younger age groups.3 A multitude of risk factors are considered as the aetiology of OL, tobacco consumption and betel nut chewing being the most prevalent.1 One controversial factor is human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, as there are conflicting results regarding its role in the development of OL.4

HPV, which belongs to the alpha-papillomavirus family, consists of small double-stranded DNA viruses that exhibit a tropism for the epithelial tissues of the genital and upper respiratory tracts and the skin. There are several HPV subtypes, and they are classified as low-risk (LR) and high-risk (HR). LR HPVs, such as HPV-6 and HPV-11, in the oral cavity are associated with benign papilloma. On the other hand, HR HPVs, like HPV-16 and HPV-18, are implicated in the development of cervical intraepithelial lesions, which can undergo malignant transformation.5 A wide range of HPV DNA levels is detected in the oral cavity, with study values ranging from 0% to 81.1%, depending on the methods and the number of individuals studied.6 This variation is attributed to the sampling methods, the sampling site/location and the HPV detection method.5 In a previous review of observational studies comprising a total of approximately 4000 subjects, the prevalence of HPV in the oral cavity is reported to be 4.5%, where HPV 16 accounted for 1.3% of the cases.7 Among 1688 healthy male participants from Brazil, Mexico and the United States, linear array polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis of oral rinse samples revealed HPV prevalence of 4%, encompassing multiple HR subtypes (16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58 and 59).8 HPV has several host-related risk factors, which include age, gender, habits (i.e. sexual behaviour and smoking) and oral health. A cross-sectional study in the United States of 3196 participants showed several trends in HPV prevalence in the oral cavity.9 Males had a higher risk of HPV oral infection, particularly men aged between 51 and 60 years and the risk of HPV infection was higher in those with more sexual partners.9 According to a recent meta-analysis from Iran, the prevalence of HPV is over three times higher in individuals with oral lesions (27.1%) compared to healthy controls (8.2%).10

The detection of HPV employs a range of methods, which fall into three main categories: serological, histological and molecular biology techniques, all of which are under continuous development.11 Molecular biological techniques are being increasingly used for HPV detection, utilising HPV nucleic acid molecules, such as DNA and RNA, for detection. Immunohistochemistry, Southern blotting, electron microscopy, western blotting, in situ hybridisation (ISH), PCR and microarray technology all fall under this category of techniques for HPV detection. ISH primarily pairs probes with DNA or RNA of the biopsy specimen. ISH offers high sensitivity and specificity, but genotyping errors are a possibility. Essentially, PCR amplifies specific DNA parts to detect HPV. Although it has high sensitivity, the possibility of cross-contamination may lead to several problems, including false positives. Microarray technology detects HPV DNA though nucleic acid molecular hybridisation pairing. In contrast, gene chip technology can detect and analyse multiple specimens, offering several advantages, including high sensitivity and rapid speed.11,12

Although HPV has been thought to play a role in the development of OL, the supporting data is not yet definitive. According to a study performed in Brazil on 32 individuals with OL, 22 showed HPV positive results, which is around 69% of the sample size.13 On the other hand, a study conducted in the United States on 118 individuals with OL yielded only five positive results, representing a rate of 4.2% among the sample studied.14 Thus, there is a significant variability in the prevalence of HPV among these studies reported on OL. In the past two decades, numerous studies have indicated a higher prevalence of HPV in patients with OL compared to disease-free controls. The literature on this topic is expanding rapidly; however, the current evidence is still inconclusive. Therefore, to assess and analyse the most recent evidence on the relationship between OL and HPV, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis. This paper aims to determine the prevalence of HPV in OL lesions compared to healthy controls, to evaluate whether it can be considered a risk factor for OL.

Materials and methods

The review was first registered in the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocol (Registration number INPLASY202540033) and adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses).15 A systematic literature review was conducted using electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus and Wiley Library, with no date or period restrictions to identify the literature published till March 2025. A comprehensive literature search was performed using the different search terms for ‘Human Papillomavirus’ and ‘oral leukoplakia’. The Boolean operators AND and OR were used to combine the mentioned keywords including ‘Human papillomavirus’, ‘Human Papillomaviruses’, ‘Papillomaviridae’, ‘HPV’, ‘DNA Probes, HPV’, ‘Oral Leukoplakia’, ‘Oral Leukoplakias’, ‘Oral Leukokeratosis’, ‘Oral Leukokeratoses’, ‘Oral Premalignant Lesion’, ‘Oral Potentially Malignant Lesion’, and, ‘Oral Potentially Malignant Disorder’. The detailed search strategy is provided as Supplementary Table 1. The research question formulated is as follows: ‘Do patients with oral leukoplakia have a higher rate of HPV infection than normal oral mucosal controls?’ Accordingly, the population, exposure, comparator and outcome (PECO) framework is framed as:

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This meta-analysis included case-control observational studies that assessed the prevalence of HPV in patients with OL specimens in comparison to controls using markers specific for HPV, regardless of the detection method.

Case reports and case series, as well as systematic reviews with or without meta-analysis, and conference abstracts, were excluded from further investigation. Any research paper not available in the English language was excluded from the review. Studies in which the marker p16 was assessed through immunohistochemistry were excluded, as it is not a proven alternative for HPV detection in lesions of the oral and maxillofacial region, except in oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma.13

Studies selection and data extraction

The screening was conducted independently by two observers (J.T. and D.G.) for the titles and abstracts of the studies. If any disagreements occurred during study selection or data extraction, they were resolved through discussion between the two observers. All duplicates were removed, and any eligible research papers were subsequently retrieved manually for the extraction of the following information: the author and year of publication, the location and centre of the study and the habits described (i.e. tobacco smoking/chewing and alcohol consumption), the sample type, the site, the method of HPV detection, the total number of cases for the leukoplakia group, the positive cases in the leukoplakia group, the total number of healthy samples for the control group, the positive cases in the control group and sub group HPV data if available. The extracted data were recorded using Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA).

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

We included studies that reported any measure of association between HPV and OL or OPMDs, with subgroup data on OL, or that reported proportions of HPV-positive samples in OL cases and controls, to calculate estimates of ORs and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Subsequently, a pooled OR estimate and its corresponding 95% CI were computed using the random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird method).16 The I2 statistic and Cochran's Q were employed to assess heterogeneity among studies.12 Visual inspection of funnel plots was employed to investigate potential publication bias. Sensitivity analysis was performed after removing the studies that contributed to a small study effect. A subgroup meta-analysis was conducted using a random-effects model to investigate the association between HPV and OL by HPV subtypes, geographic area and detection methods. All the data analysed by STATA version 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA).

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale.17 The NOS comprises three components: (1) selection of research groups (0-4), (2) comparability of the groups (0-2) and (3) ascertainment of exposure (0-3). A score of 7 or above indicated a study of good quality.

Results

General characteristics of the included studies

One thousand seven hundred sixteen articles were identified from the databases, of which 91 were duplicates and therefore excluded. The remaining 1625 records were screened based on their title and abstract, and 1474 were excluded as not being relevant to the research question. 151 reports were sought for retrieval, out of which six articles were not retrieved. The 145 reports which were assessed for eligibility were excluded for the following reasons: 41 were excluded due to no control group, 30 were excluded as they did not specify leukoplakia values, 27 were excluded due to being reviews, seven had only detected markers, four were not in the English language, two were book chapters and two for other reasons. Thirty-two studies were selected for inclusion in the systematic review and meta-analysis. The PRISMA flow chart illustrating the selection of studies is shown in Figure 1.

Out of the selected 32 articles, 14 studies were conducted in the European sub-continent: four in Italy,18, 19, 20, 21 one each in Austria,22 Serbia,23 Republic of Macedonia,24 Greece,25 Croatia,26 Norway,27 Hungary,28 Spain,29 Denmark30 and in Germany.31 Regarding the Asian subcontinent, 13 articles were included: nine in India,32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40 two in Iran,41,42 one in China43 and one in Ukraine.44 The increased number of articles identified from India could be a direct reflection of the extensive tobacco/betel quid chewing habit associated with the development of leukoplakia.45 In addition, five articles were identified from the American subcontinent, primarily South America: two from Brazil,46,47 one from Argentina,48 and two from the United States.49,50

Association between HPV and OL

To perform a meta-analysis on the association between HPV and OL we pooled data from 32 studies that operationally defined the presence of HPV in terms of the detection of the virus in the specimens. As shown in Figure 2, the overall pooled OR and the corresponding 95% CI estimated in a random effects model show a positive association between HPV detection in OL (OR =3.16; 95% CI, 2.06-4.95), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 67.2%, P value ≤ .00001). A funnel plot for investigating publication bias is presented as Supplementary Figure 1. Visual inspection of the funnel plot suggests an evident publication bias. The credibility ceiling test yielded an OR of 1.553 (95% CI, 1.13-2.14), underscoring the significance of the finding. We further explored the effect of small studies. The p-value was .002, which demonstrated the impact of small studies on the results. We performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding studies with small sample sizes, and the OR remained significant (OR = 2.17; 95% CI, 1.46-3.23). This is illustrated in Figure 3.

Association between HR HPV and OL

For defining the association between HR HPV and OL, we pooled data from studies that have reported the presence of all types of HR HPV groups in OL. A total of 16 studies have reported the prevalence of HR HPV in their samples compared to controls. As shown in Figure 4, the overall pooled OR and the corresponding 95% CI estimated in a random-effects model indicate a positive association between HR HPV detection in OL (OR = 5.42; 95% CI, 2.52-11.69), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 68.6%). A funnel plot for investigating publication bias is presented in the Supplementary Figure 2. No evidence of publication bias was detected.

Forest plot showing the risk association of high-risk HPV with OL.

Association between LR group HPV and OL

To define the association between LR HPV and OL, we pooled data from eight studies that have reported the presence of LR HPV groups in OL. As shown in Figure 5, the overall pooled OR and the corresponding 95% CI estimated in a random-effects model indicate a positive association between HPV detection in OL (OR = 1.90; 95% CI, 1.10-3.29), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). A funnel plot for investigating publication bias is presented in Supplementary Figure 3. No evidence of publication bias was detected.

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

Subgroup meta-analysis was performed based on studies from Europe and Asia, revealing a stronger association between HPV and oral lesions in European populations (OR = 3.42; 95% CI, 1.62-7.43) compared to Asian populations (OR = 1.82; 95% CI, 1.02-3.24) (Supplementary Figures 4 and 5). Sensitivity analysis performed after removing small-sized studies to assess the robustness of the results revealed that OR remained significant (OR = 2.17; 95% CI, 1.46-3.23) (Figure 3). When the studies were stratified and pooled based on the most specific detection method, PCR, the results were largely consistent with the primary analysis (OR = 3.45; 95% CI, 2.02-5.91) (Supplementary Figure 6).

Discussion

Our systematic review and meta-analysis provide a comprehensive and up-to-date assessment of the association between HPV and OL, suggesting a significant association between HPV infection and an increased risk of developing OL. The consolidated data from 32 studies demonstrated a consistent association between HPV and OL, as shown by both the random effects model and the credibility ceiling test, which provides a conservative interpretation of the current observational evidence.51 Thus, this analysis presents robust data underscoring the significance of HPV infection in OL.

Despite general agreement that some OL carry HPV, there are striking differences among previously published reviews regarding how prevalent HPV-positive OL truly is. For example, Radzki et al found an overall HPV prevalence of only 6.7% in OL, whereas de la Cour et al reported a much higher pooled prevalence (20% of leukoplakia).52 One of the earliest meta-analyses focusing on the comparative risk of the presence of HPV in OL when compared to standard healthy controls was conducted by Syrjänen et al in 2011, demonstrating a strong association between HPV and OPMDs, including leukoplakia (pooled OR = 4 for OL vs. normal mucosa).53 Another meta-analysis of nine studies that exclusively explored the association of HPV 16 with OL reported an OR of 3.59.54 These reports indicate that HPV infection is strongly associated with OL than healthy oral tissue, a trend that our findings support. Our findings also help to reconcile the discrepancies by considering the methodological and demographic factors at play. Radzki et al restricted their review to recent studies (2015-2021) and reported a surprisingly low pooled prevalence. In contrast, de la Cour et al55 included a broader spectrum of OPMDs and found higher HPV rates in OL. Further, these studies focused on pooling the prevalence data and did not examine the controls, which is crucial from an epidemiological perspective. Thus, the current study builds on these works by expanding the geographic and temporal inclusion criteria beyond prior reviews. We incorporated data from a broader range of countries and did not limit by publication year, capturing older studies that a prior six-year window review may have missed. Furthermore, unlike de la Cour et al who combined all OPMDs, we focused exclusively on OL, heeding the suggestion that OL and other OPMDs should not be treated as one lesion.

Another key highlight of our current analysis is its attention to HPV genotypic risk categories. Previous reviews primarily emphasised HR genotypes (especially HPV16) given their known oncogenic potential.52 While de la Cour et al identified HPV 16 as the predominant type in HPV-positive OPMDs (48%), LR genotypes were not analysed in detail.55 Radzki et al explicitly advocated for including LR HPV in future OL studies, and we have addressed this gap by evaluating both HR and LR HPV prevalence in OL. Our analysis separately quantified the contribution of LR genotypes, revealing that although HR HPV infection has a strong association with OL, a measurable proportion of lesions carry only LR types.52 By specifically reporting on LR HPV, our study adds a virological perspective that was underrepresented in earlier reviews. This is important because LR HPV types, while not classically associated with malignant transformation, could contribute to benign proliferative features or act as co-factors in a subset of leukoplakia. Thus, our work uniquely highlights that HPV in OL may not be limited to the HR strains. Furthermore, any studies that relied solely on surrogate markers (such as p16 immunohistochemistry) for HPV detection were excluded, which further ensures that ‘HPV-positive’ truly means direct detection of viral DNA/RNA in the lesion. Such exclusion is crucial because p16 overexpression, often used as an indirect marker of HPV, can be elevated in dysplastic oral lesions due to non-HPV mechanisms, potentially confounding the results. By including studies that have utilised PCR or equivalent direct assays for HPV DNA, we enhanced the specificity of our analysis compared to studies that might have conflated p16 status with HPV infection. We observed that numerous primary studies had small sample sizes that could affect their results. By clearly addressing these concerns in our analysis, we bolster confidence that our aggregated results are not disproportionately influenced by small-sized studies.

Several factors, such as geographic and demographic differences, study design, sample characteristics and HPV detection methods, are likely to contribute to heterogeneity in epidemiological observational studies. Epidemiological patterns of HPV in the oral cavity vary by area and population,56,57 as seen in our subgroup analysis, which revealed that HPV in OL was more prevalent in European studies (pooled OR ∼3.4) compared to Asian studies (OR ∼1.8). This could reflect underlying differences in lifestyle risk factors, genetic susceptibility, or background prevalence of oral HPV in the community. For instance, regions with prevalent tobacco chewing or smoking (such as South Asia) may have many OL cases unrelated to HPV,50 potentially diluting the observed association in those populations. In contrast, European cohorts, which often include idiopathic OL cases, might show a stronger relative contribution of HPV. Some reports indicate that HR HPV is detected in OL patients from specific areas, such as parts of Europe or South America.52 In contrast, studies in other regions, including Northern Europe,53 have found little to no HPV in OL, underscoring the true geographic variation.

Although it has been suggested by sporadic case series58, 59, 60 that HPV may have different affinities towards OL with dysplasia and OL without dysplasia, de la Cour et al61 have evidenced the independent association of HPV with epithelial dysplasia. The prevalence of HPV in severely dysplastic leukoplakia and carcinoma in situ was reported to be 26.2%.62 However, there was no definite association of HPV with proliferative verrucous leukoplakia, which has the highest risk of malignant transformation,63 although some reports suggest that proliferative verrucous leukoplakia may harbour HPV more frequently.64 Inconsistent case definitions and dysplasia grading across studies may contribute to variability. The majority of studies18, 19, 20, 21,23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30,33, 34, 35,38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43,46,48, 49, 50 included in our analysis used PCR-based assays, which are highly sensitive for viral DNA, thus minimising the heterogeneity. A minority of studies32,44,47 employed immunohistochemistry for HPV antigens or ISH, which are less sensitive. Differences in study inclusion criteria and design can lead to variability. Smaller studies focused on HR subsets may report higher HPV rates simply due to selection bias or chance variation. Study designs might yield different apparent prevalence depending on the selection of controls. In some studies, ‘normal’ oral mucosa samples were taken as controls from the same patients or from different populations altogether, affecting the baseline HPV detection rate. Additionally, variation in the inclusion of clinically diagnosed and histopathologically diagnosed OL in studies can contribute to the heterogeneity. Even among PCR-based studies, differences in primer sets, targeted HPV regions and the spectrum of HPV types tested could lead to disparate results.65 Standardising sensitive and specific detection methods in future studies would help reduce this variability. Other factors, such as the nature of the sample (biopsy tissue/scrape cytology/oral rinse) and the timing of sample collection, can also play a role in detection differences.65 The considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 67% in our analysis) likely reflects a combination of these issues. We attempted to address some of this by subgroup analyses, such as by region, and by restricting to PCR-based studies, but variability still remains, which may be ascribed to the aforementioned reasons.

It is still unclear whether HPV in OL has any association with dysplasia status, suggesting that HPV infection may not be strongly correlated with the development of microscopic dysplastic changes and subsequent. The involvement of HPV in oral carcinogenesis appears to be limited due to the low positive rate of E6/E7 in OSCCs. HPV may be acting early in the course of lesion development (or infecting more benign, hyperplastic lesions), rather than driving the progression to dysplasia. This could explain why established dysplastic OL, which often arise from heavy tobacco or alcohol exposure, might not commonly harbour HPV. It is also possible that some HPV-positive leukoplakia represent a distinct clinicopathologic subset where viral-induced changes (e.g. koilocytosis) can be present even in the absence of classical epithelial dysplasia.66 Thus, it might be suggested that HPV-positive OL might follow a slightly different biological pathway than HPV-negative OL. Nevertheless, the studies that have explored the prevalence of HPV did not investigate the fate of these lesions prospectively; it remains uncertain whether HPV-positive leukoplakia are more or less likely to undergo malignant transformation. Wu et al demonstrated that cases that presented with HPV 16 in the initial analysis of OL, when followed up prospectively for a period of 78 months, were HPV-negative, suggesting that HPV 16 may not be implicated in the carcinogenesis of OL.67 However, the study utilised p16INK4A for the detection of HPV. Long-term follow-up studies with standardised methods are needed to determine if the presence of HR HPV in a leukoplakia confers additional cancer risk or if, conversely, some HPV-positive lesions are less inherently dysplastic and possibly less prone to progression. In the meantime, we may conclude that data on the association between HPV status and histological severity in OL is inconclusive, and decisions about management (e.g. biopsy, surveillance or excision) should continue to be guided by traditional risk factors and histopathology rather than HPV presence.

We acknowledge that, despite the strengths of our meta-analysis, certain limitations remain. First, the inherent weaknesses of the included studies (mostly cross-sectional or case-control designs) limit any causal inferences. While we demonstrate an association between HPV and OL, we cannot confirm that HPV infection precedes and causes the development of leukoplakia; the relationship could be incidental or bidirectional. Second, the detection of HPV DNA does not equate to active infection. Many included studies did not assess viral gene expression or oncoprotein activity; thus, some HPV-positive cases might harbour latent virus that is not actually driving epithelial changes. Future research would benefit from incorporating markers of active infection, such as E6/E7 mRNA or p16INK4a co-expression, to distinguish the HPV-driven lesions. Third, although we tried to minimise heterogeneity by utilising random effects methods, the variability remained high, and our pooled estimates should be viewed as approximations. There may be unpublished data or smaller regional studies that we could not include, which could introduce publication bias if predominantly HPV-positive results have been reported in the literature. Geographical imbalances in study populations with overrepresentation of certain regions and underrepresentation of others could limit the generalisability of findings. Furthermore, a lack of mention of leukoplakia subtypes and association with dysplasia status in the majority of studies restricts the ability to assess specific trends or outcomes. Additionally, our subgroup analyses by region or method, while informative, are limited by the available data within each subset and should be interpreted hypothesis-generatively. Finally, our focus was deliberately on the presence of HPV DNA in OL lesions, rather than on downstream clinical outcomes.

In summary, this study offers a comprehensive understanding of the current evidence on the HPV-OL association. This meta-analysis strengthens the understanding of HPV's role in OL by addressing key limitations of prior studies. It expands geographic and temporal inclusion criteria, focuses specifically on OL rather than grouping all OPMDs, and analyses both HR and LR HPV genotypes. Methodological rigor is enhanced by using only direct HPV detection methods (PCR-based) and conducting subgroup analyses to address heterogeneity. By systematically resolving inconsistencies in previous research, our analysis provides more reliable evidence on HPV's association with OL and establishes a foundation for future standardised investigations. However, variations in detection methods, geographic imbalances and a lack of age-stratified data may affect the comparability and generalisability of findings. We confirm that a meaningful minority of OLs worldwide contain HPV, often HR variants, reinforcing the notion that HPV may play a role in the etiopathogenesis of a subset of these premalignant lesions. At the same time, the majority of leukoplakia are HPV-negative and likely arise from classical carcinogenic exposures, highlighting that OL is a heterogeneous condition with multifactorial origins. Our work highlights that when studying OPMD, it is essential to consider differences in populations, methodologies and lesion characteristics to avoid oversimplified conclusions. For researchers, our findings emphasise the need for standardised protocols in future studies, including consistent diagnostic criteria for OL and uniform HPV detection techniques, to reduce the heterogeneity. Prospective longitudinal studies are particularly encouraged to determine the malignant transformation or recurrence rate of HPV-positive OL lesions. Such data would directly inform clinical management.

Conclusion

The current meta-analysis highlights that oral HPV infection, especially with HR HPV is significantly associated with OL. These findings contribute to a more refined understanding of HPV's association with OL and lay the groundwork for future studies to further elucidate the role of HPV in OPMDs.
