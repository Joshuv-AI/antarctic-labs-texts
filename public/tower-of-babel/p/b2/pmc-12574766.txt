Predictors of treatment response in patients with depressive disorder
Authors: Anwesha Mondal, Manish Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Depression is a mood disorder that causes a persistent feeling of sadness and loss of interest, negatively affects how an individual is, and can lead to a variety of emotional and physical problems. The primary treatment modality used in depression is a combination of pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy. Predictors of response to treatment in depressed patients are found to be a significant issue in their recovery, mainly the sociodemographic, clinical, and psychological variables.Aim:This study aims to find out the role of psychological variables in the prediction of the treatment outcome of depressed patients.Materials and Methods:60 depressed patients (moderate and severe level) having the first episode were selected as per ICD-10 DCR criteria through purposive sampling and inclusion criteria.Results:Cognitive variables like attention have been found to have a positive role in the treatment recovery of depressed patients. No other cognitive variable, coping strategies, or personality factors have been found to have any predictive role in the treatment response of depression.Conclusion:More researches need to be conducted to help fully elucidate the relationship between cognitive variables, coping strategies, and personality factors and their predictive role in treatment outcomes of depression.

Depressive disorder is a common and severe psychiatric illness that negatively affects how one feels, the way one thinks, and how one behaves. Depression is a significant public health concern worldwide and has been ranked as one of the illnesses having the greatest burden on individuals, families, and society.[12] The prevalence of this disorder varies by country, but rates are typically between 4.2% and 17%.[3]

Depression is among the leading causes of global disability,[4] and although the understanding of the disorder is growing, treatment outcomes at times remain unsatisfactory. Medications, especially antidepressants, are essential to treat depressive disorder along with psychotherapy. Long-term maintenance treatment may be suggested to decrease the risk of future episodes for certain people at high risk. Research suggests that only 50% of patients with depression respond to psychotherapy and pharmacological treatment, and relapse is common.[5] Therefore, there is interest in elucidating factors that help predict clinical response.

Different studies investigate depressive patients of different ages with different sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, course, and medication schemes with differing methodologies about how these variables affect the treatment outcome.[6] Previous studies have suggested some factors that are found to be associated with a generally positive response to treatment of depression, like fewer previous episodes of depression,[7] shorter episodes,[8] younger age, and the relative absence of delusions,[9] melancholic features,[6] early onset of improvement,[8] better global and family functioning.[9] However, these predictors are only partially definitive.[9]

Cognitive impairment is a crucial feature of depression, which often persists beyond remission; thus, baseline cognitive functioning can predict treatment outcomes in individuals with depression.[8] An association between poorer executive function and attention performance was found with poor overall treatment response in a sample of 268 depressed patients.[6] In a sample of 72 depressed patients, poorer attention and spatial working memory were associated with poorer response at 4 weeks of pharmacotherapy.[5] Mood and affective symptoms on their own often fail to justify the impairment associated with depression.[9] In a study with depressed patients, it was found that 6 months after baseline, neurocognitive functions like executive functions, attention, and processing speed were strongly and significantly associated with functioning,[5] which indicates that these functions not only influence current functioning, quality of life but can also act as predictors of treatment response. Another study also indicated that cognitive (mainly executive) functions evaluated at admission[58] predicted outcomes 4 months later in young patients with depression, both related to the severity of depression and work and social function.

Personality patterns may also play a role in treatment outcomes. Controversy exists about whether measure of personality patterns taken during the acute phase of depression are only state markers of the severity of the disorder. However, the presence of such patterns leads to a poor acute response to antidepressants[8] as well as a poor long-term prognosis.[9] Thus, personality patterns have emerged as the strongest identified predictors of response to treatment of depression.[7] Studies showed that the personality trait of high neuroticism in association with a slow onset of response to treatment of an episode of depression and a brief duration of treatment of the index episode predicted an increased chance of recurrence of chronic depression in a large proportion of patients with a lower rate of recovery.[7] Researchers reviewed several longitudinal studies showing neuroticism to be a predictor of both subsequent diagnoses and chronicity of major depression.[10] Another study found that introversion and neuroticism were related to poor outcomes, whereas openness and agreeableness were related to good outcomes in treatment response to depression.[10]

Clinical factors predict differential response to treatments to a limited extent. In this context, there has been increased interest in elucidating factors that help predict clinical response in depression, including cognitive factors, hormonal measures, and neural markers. Thus, this research aimed to assess baseline cognitive functioning along with personality and sociodemographic factors in predicting treatment outcomes in patients with depression.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

Selection and description of participants

The current research was a prospective study based on pre-post design and was conducted in the outpatient department of the Institute of Psychiatry, Kolkata. The sample size was 60, and purposive sampling has been used as a sampling technique.

Outpatients with a primary diagnosis of depression according to the diagnostic criteria of ICD-10 receiving treatment for the first time, with an age range of 18–45 years of both genders, providing consent to participate in the research and obtaining a score of 19 and above on Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) (moderate and severe level) has been included in the current research.

Any patient with the presence of major physical illness or head injury, substance abuse, psychosis, intellectual disability, or past history of treatment has been excluded.

Tools used

Sociodemographic data and clinical details

BDI[11]: The tool has been used to measure the severity of depression. It is a 21-item inventory where a score of 0–3 is assigned to each statement; the minimum score is 0, the maximum score is 63, and the total score is obtained by summing up the score obtained in each item. The score is then classified into mild, moderate, and severe according to the range in which the score falls. Internal consistency is highly satisfactory at 0.90, and the test-retest reliability ranged from 0.73 to 0.96.

Digit Symbol Substitution Test[12]:

This test has been used to assess visuomotor coordination and mental speed. The test consists of numbers 1–9 randomly arranged in 4 rows of 25 squares each, and each number has to be substituted by a number symbol. The time to complete the test forms, as well as the score and errors, are noted. This test has a high test-retest reliability, which ranges from 0.80 to 0.94, and the construct validity ranges from 0.34 to 0.47.

Comprehensive Trail Making Test (CTMT)[13]:

This test has been used to assess executive functioning and cognitive flexibility. The test consists of five trials where numbers are randomly mentioned in circles, and a line is to be drawn from numbers 1 to 5. The score for each trial is the number of seconds required to complete the trial. The internal consistency coefficient of this test is 0.92.

Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT)[12]:

This test has been used to assess verbal learning and memory. This test comprises two lists of 15 words (List A and List B) for five successive trials where recall and delayed recall are noted. The number of words correctly recalled is noted, forming the memory score. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of this test is r = 0.80, indicating good internal consistency of the items.

Digit Vigilance Test[12]:

This test has been used to assess sustained attention. This test consists of numbers 1 to 9 randomly ordered and placed in rows, and the target digits, that is, 6 and 9, need to be found among others. The time taken to complete it and the error score are noted. High test-retest reliability, r = 0.91, has been found for this test.

Brief Resilient Coping Scale (BRCS)[14]:

This scale has been used to assess the coping style. It is a 4-item measure with a rating of score 1 to 5; it does not describe me at all to describe me very well. The summation of the score gives the composite score divided into low, medium, and high resilient copers. Cronbach’s alpha reliability was found to be 0.71, and predictive validity was found to be 0.86 for this test.

Big Five Inventory-10 (BFI-10)[15]:

This scale has been used to gain an understanding of the personality pattern. This inventory consists of ten questions on a 5-point scale, ranging from “disagree strongly” to “agree strongly.” Scoring is done by adding the scores in the five domains. The test-retest reliability across 6 weeks was 0.72–0.75. Convergent Validity with NEO-PI is 0.67.

Procedure

The patients attending the outpatient psychiatry department and diagnosed with depressive disorder according to the International Classification of Diseases—10[16] by the psychiatry department for the first time have been selected following the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and those who are willing to participate in the study. After obtaining informed consent, sociodemographic variables and the selected patients’ clinical details were noted. Following this, the BDI was administered to the selected patients; those having a score of 19 and above were considered for further assessment with the mentioned psychological tests. After the post-administration of the tests, the patients were subjected to 3 months of pharmacological and psychotherapeutic intervention. After 3 months, the patients were assessed again with the BDI.

Statistics

Appropriate statistical computation (mean and SD for continuous variables, frequency and percentage for discrete variables, Spearman’s correlation, one-way analysis of variance, and linear regression analysis) were applied to analyze the data using SPSS 20.

DISCUSSION

Investigation of cognitive symptoms of depression was, for a long time, only secondary to mood symptoms. However, cognitive impairments like concentration problems, memory problems, negative automatic thoughts, dysfunctional attitudes, and maladaptive schemata, spanning from depression symptoms, constitute an essential part of the treatment response to depression. It is crucial to study and evaluate these cognitive symptoms, which are present with mood symptoms, and their role in the functional recovery of depressive symptoms.[1718]

Contrary to the positive evidence of cognitive symptoms playing predictive value in treatment response, the current research results have been found to be the opposite in most of the cognitive variables. Regarding the cognitive variables, executive functions do not have any predictive value in any change in the treatment response of depressive symptoms [Table 2]. This finding is supported by previous studies, which showed no evidence of an association (total n = 306).[1819] A more extended treatment trial[20] examined the relationship between executive function performance and response to 24 months of treatment (n = 91), pharmacology or psychodynamic psychotherapy, or a combination of both, which have found no association.

Other cognitive variables, such as mental speed, have also not been found to be predictive in treatment response [Table 3]. This finding has been supported by previous studies which showed no association between treatment and psychomotor speed (n = 320).[2021]

Cognitive variables like verbal learning and memory have been found to have no predictive value in treatment response to depressive symptoms, as supported by previous studies where no relationship between verbal learning and memory performance and treatment-related outcomes[2223] has been found [Table 3].

The above findings may be explained by the presence of depressive patients who have reported fewer cognitive deficits. Having such patients in a study may dilute findings, potentially to the point of a genuine association not being demonstrated.[24] Another reason may be that researchers using a more extensive battery of cognitive tasks may be more likely to show an association with response simply because they have used multiple tests, and an association with one of these may be a feature of multiple comparisons.[25]

Cognitive variables like attention are a predictor in the treatment response of patients suffering from depression, suggesting that better attention can predict good treatment response [Tables 3 and 4]. Previous studies have supported this finding.[26] This can be explained by the fact that depressed individuals make fewer errors of commission and can recruit brain areas important for cognitive control and error monitoring, including the anterior cingulate cortex (error monitoring), the left ventromedial PFC (decision-making and emotional regulation), and the right ventrolateral Pre Frontal Cortex (PFC) (motor inhibition) potentially influences attention task performance leading to better treatment response.[27] Structural and plasticity constraints exist on how much change can be elicited in treatment, and different networks are related to symptom severity and improvement.[28]

Resilient coping has also been found to have no predictive role in the treatment response of depressive patients [Table 5]. Previous studies have supported these findings.[29] It can be explained in the way that coping strategies, such as problem-solving, are used to find ways to change stressful situations; however, the use of active coping may not be effective and even unproductive in situations that are not amendable to change or are perceived as controllable by the patients; it leads them to the belief that they can never recover from their symptoms.

In terms of personality traits, no predictive role has been found in the treatment response of depressive patients [Table 6]. Previous studies suggest that changes in depressive symptoms are not necessarily accompanied by any personality traits.[30] Different models have emphasized the role of personality traits in the development of depressive disorder; however, their role in recovery from depression remains unclear. One explanation may be that personality trait mediates the effect of treatment on depression. This effect is not always confounded by the depressive state, as changes in personality traits may be independent of any treatment response to depression.

Limitations

The current research has limitations, such as a small sample size. The effect of psychotherapy and pharmacological intervention on treatment recovery has not been considered.

CONCLUSION

Depression profoundly impacts complex neuro-cognitive processes which play a role in interaction with the environment and also influences to what extent patients are capable of responding to psychotherapy and recovery. Hence, the role of cognitive variables and personality factors requires focus as key predictor variables in the treatment recovery of depression. The findings from the present research do indicate that certain aspects of cognitive functioning, like attention, may help predict treatment response in depression and reflect the need for explicitly targeting cognitive functioning during treatment, as doing so may result in improved treatment outcomes.

Future research can be conducted on male and female patients as a comparative study. The sample can be further subdivided based on the group of antidepressant medications chosen for the pharmacological treatment of depression. The non-significant cognitive variables might be confounded by sample characteristics or limited test scope, which recommends the need for future studies with a more diverse depression population and comprehensive cognitive assessments to delineate these relationships.
