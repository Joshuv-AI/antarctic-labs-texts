Pulmonary Veno‐Occlusive Disease: A Focused Clinicopathologic Series
Authors: Yub Raj Sedhai, Zein Kattih, Christopher A. Thomas, Junad M. Chowdhury, Anju Singhal, Alan S. Nyquist, Jared D. Wilkinson, Haresh Mani, Vikramjit Khangoora, Christopher S. King, Oksana A. Shlobin, Steven D. Nathan, Shambhu Aryal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary veno‐occlusive disease (PVOD) is a rare and aggressive subtype of pulmonary arterial hypertension characterized by fibroproliferative obstruction of post‐capillary pulmonary venules leading to increased pulmonary vascular resistance and progressive right ventricular failure. Heritable forms are linked to eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 alpha kinase 4 (EIF2AK4) mutations, while acquired cases have been associated with environmental exposures, including organic solvents and chemotherapeutic agents like mitomycin‐C. PVOD is classified under Group 1 pulmonary hypertension in the current World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension classification; however, pulmonary vasodilator therapy has limited efficacy due to the frequent occurrence of acute pulmonary edema. 1 Lung transplantation remains the only definitive and effective therapy for PVOD, and in its absence, the prognosis is uniformly poor. Given the therapeutic and prognostic implications, establishing an accurate diagnosis and early referral for lung transplantation evaluation is imperative. In this single transplant center case series of seven cases, we highlight the clinical presentation and the importance of early recognition and referral to specialized centers for optimal management.

Introduction

Pulmonary veno‐occlusive disease (PVOD) is a rare form of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) that primarily involves post‐capillary pulmonary venules [1, 2, 3]. PVOD characteristically presents with pre‐capillary pulmonary hypertension and has been described as a PAH mimic or PAH like disorder [3]. The estimated incidence of PVOD ranges from 0.1 to 0.5 cases per million population, while the prevalence is approximately 1–2 cases per million but the actual burden might be higher, given the likelihood of underdiagnosis [3]. Recognizing PVOD early in the clinical course is crucial because patients often exhibit limited responsiveness to PAH‐targeted treatments and experience worse transplant‐free survival than other PAH subcategories [4]. In this case series of 7 patients, we describe the clinical presentations, hemodynamic profiles, and characteristic radiological and pathological features of PVOD. We also discuss the clinical outcomes at our Advanced Lung Disease and Transplant program, underscoring the importance of early referrals for optimal management. Additionally, we provide an updated narrative review of the contemporary literature to contextualize our findings within the existing knowledge landscape.

Materials and Methods

We performed a retrospective chart review of all patients evaluated by the Advanced Lung Disease and Lung Transplant Program at Inova Fairfax Hospital from 2012 to 2025. Comprehensive clinical data were available in the electronic health record after its systemwide adoption in 2012, and data preceding 2012 were limited. Thus, although lung transplants have been performed at the institution since 1991, data from 1991 to 2012 could not be incorporated. We identified seven patients diagnosed with PVOD. Data on demographics, clinical presentation, hemodynamic parameters, imaging findings, response to pulmonary vasodilator therapies, and histopathologic features when available, were abstracted from the medical record.

Results

Synopsis of Clinical Presentation

A concise synopsis of each case is outlined below.

Patient 1 initially presented at the age of 18 with symptoms of exertional dyspnea and hypoxemia that were out of proportion to findings of mild precapillary PH on right heart catheterization (RHC). High resolution CT scan of the chest (HRCT) revealed ground glass opacity (GGO). He was placed on sildenafil without worsening of symptoms. PH progressed over time, as evidenced by a rising pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR). Sotatercept was added to the medical regimen at a later stage. Genetic testing revealed two pathologic variants of EIF2AK4. He continues to be medically stable with low risk Reveal score and echocardiographic features.

Patient 2 presented with exertional dyspnea and was diagnosed with severe precapillary PH on (RHC). Initial CT chest revealed GGO, mediastinal lymphadenopathy, and interlobular septal thickening. But developed clinical worsening and worsening of ground glass opacities on CT chest after therapy with tadalafil, macitentan, and intravenous treprostinil was instituted. She ultimately underwent bilateral lung transplantation, and pathologic review of the explanted lungs confirmed PVOD.

Patient 3 presented with persistent dyspnea after a COVID‐19 infection and was diagnosed with severe precapillary pulmonary hypertension on RHC. His symptoms worsened with macitentan, tadalafil, and subcutaneous treprostinil. Initial CT chest showed GGO. The patient presented to the hospital with a COVID‐19 reinfection associated with cardiogenic shock and ultimately underwent bilateral lung transplantation. Pathologic review of the explanted lungs revealed PVOD.

Patient 4 had limited systemic sclerosis (SSc) diagnosed 18 years prior to presentation when she was diagnosed with severe precapillary PH on RHC treated with intravenous Treprostinil. CT chest revealed GGO, mediastinal lymphadenopathy, and interlobular septal thickening. She was initially treated with IV Treprostinil; however, at a dose of 33 ng/Kg/min she developed worsening hypoxia prompting eventual weaning and discontinuation of therapy. She later presented to our center with cardiogenic shock and severe hypoxemic respiratory failure due to co‐infection with. Despite VA‐ECMO support, clinical syndrome progressed and she was deemed medically unsuitable for lung transplantation. She subsequently succumbed but an autopsy was not performed.

Patient 5 was referred for evaluation of interstitial lung disease. CT chest showed GGO, lymphadenopathy and interlobular septal thickening. Wedge biopsy revealed nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP)along with extensive veno‐occlusive and pulmonary arterial hypertensive changes. She was trialed on sildenafil and intravenous treprostinil but worsened on therapy. She later presented with worsening hypoxemic respiratory failure but had concurrent invasive aspergillosis with a cavitary lesion and was deemed not to be a viable transplant candidate. She ultimately died, and an autopsy confirmed PVOD.

Patient 6 presented after an episode of syncope. CT chest revealed GGO, interlobular septal thickening, and centrilobular nodularity. RHC revealed pre‐capillary PH. The patient was initiated on macitentan and tadalafil. However, had a paradoxical response evident as increased oxygen requirement and radiological worsening with progression of GGO prompting discontinuation of therapy. He was briefly supported with inhaled nitric while requiring oxygen by heated high flow nasal canula. Given this paradoxical response and the clinical context, a diagnosis of PVOD was favored. The patient later developed cardiogenic shock culminating in cardiac arrest, after which the family elected to pursue compassionate withdrawal of care. Autopsy was not performed.

Patient 7 had precapillary PH when initially evaluated with RHC in 2012. Initial HRCT showed mediastinal adenopathy, and GGO. He was vasodilator responsive and was initially treated with Amlodipine. PH therapy was later changed to tadalafil, and inhaled treprostinil. He initially responded to PH therapy, however, PH continued to progress despite therapy. IV treprostinil was added to the medical regimen later however, he developed clinical worsening with the development of worsening hypoxia and incremental oxygen requirement. He subsequently underwent lung transplantation, and histopathologic evaluation of the explanted lung was consistent with PVOD.

Clinicopathologic Heterogeneity and Disease Evolution

The cases in this series illustrate the marked clinicopathologic and hemodynamic heterogeneity of PVOD. Patients 2, 3, 5, and 6 demonstrated an aggressive phenotype or advanced stage with severe pre‐capillary pulmonary hypertension, progressive hypoxemia, and paradoxical worsening following pulmonary vasodilator therapy, often accompanied by interval progression of ground‐glass opacities and septal thickening suggestive of pulmonary edema on imaging. The rationale for relative tolerance of pulmonary vasodilator therapy in some patients with PVOD likely reflects the heterogeneous extent and distribution of venous and capillary involvement across the disease spectrum [2, 6]. In contrast, Patients 1 and 7 initially tolerated vasodilator therapy despite progressive pulmonary vascular disease. Patient 1 remained clinically stable for a prolonged period despite rising pulmonary vascular resistance and genetically confirmed EIF2AK4‐associated PVOD. Patient 4 represented an intermediate phenotype, initially tolerating prostacyclin therapy before later developing worsening hypoxemia at higher doses. Disease evolution was similarly variable, ranging from slowly progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling over years to rapid decompensation with cardiogenic shock and refractory hypoxemic respiratory failure. This variability in therapeutic tolerance may reflect differing degrees of venous remodeling, capillary involvement, and right ventricular adaptation across the PVOD spectrum. Collectively, these observations from our case series suggest the presence of distinct PVOD phenotypes characterized by variable rates of disease progression, differing severity of hemodynamic impairment, and heterogeneous tolerance to pulmonary vasodilator therapy.

Imaging and Computed Tomography

High‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest is a pivotal diagnostic tool for identifying findings that may substantially increase the pre‐test probability of PVOD. Classic radiographic features that reflect postcapillary venous involvement include mediastinal lymphadenopathy, centrilobular ground glass opacities, and smooth interlobular septal thickening. In our cohort, mediastinal lymph node enlargement was present in three patients, while all seven demonstrated centrilobular ground‐glass opacities and interlobular septal thickening. Lung Transplantation and Clinical Course

Bilateral lung transplantation was performed in three patients. The immediate postoperative course in all transplanted patients was complicated by primary graft dysfunction requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation and subsequent tracheostomy. Patient 3 experienced ventricular tachycardia during spontaneous breathing trial likely precipitated by adverse heartlung interaction in the setting of underlying right ventricular dysfunction. It culminated into cardiogenic shock and required VA‐ECMO support. Patient 7 developed acute tubular necrosis requiring continuous renal replacement therapy in the immediate postoperative period and subsequently was dependent on long‐term hemodialysis. Discussion

Epidemiology

PVOD is a rare and fatal form of PAH characterized by progressive occlusion of the postcapillary pulmonary venules due to intimal fibrosis. In most cases, it results in severe precapillary PH and progressive right ventricular failure. The 7th World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension categorized PVOD (now termed PAH with venous/capillary involvement) under Group 1 (subgroup 1.5) of the clinical PH classification [7]. However, several case series suggest that 5%–25% of patients initially diagnosed with PAH may have underlying features of PVOD, highlighting the likelihood of under‐recognition and misclassification [8].

Etiology

PVOD has a genetic association with increased incidence among and shows co‐occurrence of siblings and described familial inheritance. Hadinnapola et al. reviewed genomic sequencing data from 864 patients with idiopathic or heritable PAH and identified 130 individuals with bone morphogenic protein receptor 2 (BMPR2) mutations, all of whom had PAH rather than PVOD or pulmonary capillary hemangiomatosis (PCH) [9]. In the same cohort, 19 individuals carrying EIF2AK4 mutations were clinically classified as having PAH at presentation, with no initial radiographic or clinical features suggestive of PVOD or PCH. This supports the concept that diagnostic features may evolve over the course of time, contributing to the challenge of recognizing PVOD early. A French study examining the genealogy of 13 families with PVOD found biallelic EIF2AK4 mutations in all affected families and demonstrated an autosomal recessive pattern of inheritance [10]. EIF2AK4 codes for the GCN2 kinase protein, a central component of the integrated stress‐response pathway. GCN2 modulates protein translation and facilitates adaptive mechanisms in the setting of metabolic, inflammatory, or environmental stress [11]. Recessive pathogenic variants impair GCN2 activity, leading to dysregulated endothelial stress signaling, unusual cellular proliferation, and ultimately the characteristic pulmonary venous and capillary remodeling observed in PVOD [11, 12]. Heritable PVOD due to EIF2AK4 mutations generally manifests at a younger age, whereas sporadic forms of the disease among those without identifiable EIF2AK4 variants present later in life with a median reported age of 60 years [9, 10, 13]. EIF2AK4 mutation was identified in one patient among our cohort of seven.

Anorexigens like fenfluramine and multiple organic solvents, including benzyl sulfonate and trichloroethylene have been reported in association with PVOD. Trichloroethylene is used in metal cleaning and industrial degreasing. It has been reported to increase endothelial permeability, inducing pulmonary vascular injury, providing a mechanistic basis for its observed link to PVOD [14]. Patients with PVOD have been observed to have greater cumulative tobacco exposure than those with PAH, raising the biological plausibility that chronic tobacco‐related endothelial injury may contribute to the development of PVOD [15].

In a study from the French Pulmonary Hypertension Network, alkylating agents represented the principal drug class associated with PVOD, with cyclophosphamide and mitomycin C being the most frequently implicated [16]. The study further demonstrated that cyclophosphamide induced in vitro lung endothelial toxicity, accompanied by vascular remodeling and septal thickening [16]. Mitomycin C has a well‐established association with the development of PVOD in both human and rat models. Perros et al. further demonstrated that mitomycin C exposure led to decreased EIF2AK4 expression and reduced levels of its encoded protein, GCN2, in rat models, with an inverse dose–response relationship [17]. Patients treated with mitomycin C in combination with 5‐fluorouracil for anal squamous cell carcinoma have been reported to develop PVOD, often with a poor prognosis despite discontinuation of chemotherapy or initiation of pulmonary vasodilator therapy [18]. Cisplatin, bleomycin, and carmustine also have a reported association with PVOD [16]. PVOD has also been reported as a potential complication following hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) for hematological malignancies. Several mechanisms have been proposed, including graft‐versus‐host disease and endothelial injury from opportunistic infections, both of which may contribute to pulmonary vascular damage and the subsequent development of PVOD [19].

Among connective tissue diseases, SSc has the strongest association with PVOD [20]. There is an increasing body of histopathological and radiological evidence indicating that its true prevalence is higher than reported [20, 21, 22]. In a study by Gupta et al., out of 18 transplanted patients with SSc related PH and interstitial lung disease (SSc‐PH‐ILD), nine had 1 + PVOD and six had 2 + PVOD changes (where absence of a PVOD pattern on histology was classified as grade 0, mild changes as grade 1+, and moderate‐to‐severe changes as grade 2+) [23]. Although demographics did not differ, patients with limited SSc had a roughly tenfold higher risk of having a greater PVOD grade compared with those with diffuse disease (95% CI 0.77–133.4, p = 0.07) [23]. PVOD has additionally been described in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, sarcoidosis, and pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis [20, 24, 25].

Diagnosis and Diagnostic Modalities

Pathology

The hallmark histopathological features of PVOD include concentric narrowing and occlusion of small pulmonary venules, most prominently involving the pre‐septal venules. Morphometric studies have demonstrated that PVOD represents a pan‐pulmonary vasculopathy characterized by prominent septal vein and venular intimal/adventitial thickening with capillary congestion and proliferation, alongside variable degrees of pulmonary arterial remodeling, including marked medial thickening in small arteries measuring < 300 μm in external diameter [26, 27]. Nossent et al. demonstrated that PVOD is a pan‐vascular remodeling disorder with substantial pulmonary arterial involvement in addition to venous and capillary pathology, with EIF2AK4 mutation carriers exhibiting more pronounced venous muscularization and distinctive arterial intimal fibrosis. [28] Additional characteristic findings include alveolar congestion and hemorrhage resulting from capillary rupture, with accumulation of hemosiderin‐laden macrophages within the alveolar spaces. Because these features may be subtle on routine hematoxylin and eosin staining, elastin stains are particularly helpful in confirming the diagnosis of PVOD, as pulmonary veins demonstrate a single elastin lamina, whereas pulmonary arteries characteristically exhibit two elastin layers. PCH shares several histopathologic features with PVOD, including the presence of hemosiderin‐laden macrophages, thickening of pulmonary arteries, and capillary proliferation. However, features favoring PCH over PVOD include markedly widened alveolar septa containing multilayered capillary proliferations, which may coalesce to form sheet‐like patterns or discrete nodular lesions. PCH and PVOD have overlapping clinical presentations and may be pathologically indistinguishable particularly in the presence of EIF2AK4 mutations; thus, they are classified under a unified category as PAH with pulmonary capillary and/or venous involvement (Group 1.5). Histological evaluation of lung tissue specimen remains the definitive diagnostic method; however, lung biopsy is often not feasible due to poor anesthesia tolerance in patients with PH and the high risk of bleeding due to elevated pulmonary arterial pressures. Consequently, diagnosis relies on clinical risk factors, genetic testing, characteristic radiologic features, and the development of pulmonary edema with PAH‐specific therapy. In our series, five of the seven patients developed pulmonary edema in response to pulmonary vasodilator therapy.

Echocardiography

Echocardiography does not provide specific features that distinguish PVOD from other forms of PH, but it can help estimate the pre‐test probability of PH through structural and functional assessment [29]. TR velocity can be used to estimate RVSP, though reliable measurements are not always obtainable [30]. The 2025 American Society of Echocardiography guidelines recognize these constraints and emphasize that echocardiography should be used mainly for screening, while confirmatory diagnosis must be established by RHC [31]. Advanced PH may show right atrial and right ventricular (RV) dilation, RV hypertrophy, and interventricular septal flattening. In addition, left atrial enlargement, left ventricular dysfunction, or diastolic dysfunction suggests the presence group 2 component rather than PAH or PVOD alone.

Pulmonary Function Tests

Spirometry in PVOD may show mild restrictive or obstructive changes, though normal forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1,) ratio of FEV1, to forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC), and lung volumes can also be observed. Pulmonary function abnormalities typically appear in more advanced disease. A markedly reduced diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) is the most characteristic finding, reflecting reduced capillary blood flow and impaired diffusion from interstitial edema [3]. In a comparative study involving 24 patients with histologic evidence of PVOD, and 24 randomly selected idiopathic, familial, and anorexigen associated PH, diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide corrected for alveolar volume (DLCO,/VA) was found markedly lower in PVOD compared to PAH. A DLCO, < 55% was associated with 64.3% sensitivity, and high specificity (89.5%) for identifying PVOD [4].

Six‐Minute Walk Test

PVOD patients have more pronounced resting hypoxia than patients with PAH, with Montani et al. reporting significantly lower PaO2 values in PVOD (61.3 ± 17.3 vs. 75.4 ± 3.8 mmHg, respectively) [4]. This likely reflects pulmonary edema, occult alveolar hemorrhage, and fibrotic vascular involvement leading to impaired diffusion [4]. While some studies note lower 6MWDs in PVOD, Montani et al. found similar distances between PVOD and PAH [4, 32].

Radio‐Diagnosis and Imaging

HRCT is central to raising the pre‐test probability of PVOD. Typical HRCT features include mediastinal lymph node enlargement, centrilobular ground‐glass opacities, and smooth interlobular septal thickening (Figure 1 Panel G and H) [32, 33]. This triad is more prominent in PVOD than in PAH, and the presence of at least two of these features provides a sensitivity of 75% and specificity of 84.6% for PVOD detection in the context of precapillary PH [4]. Pleural effusions occur at similar rates in advanced PAH and PVOD, whereas pericardial effusions are more frequently observed in PAH [4, 33]. In dual energy computed tomography (DECT) study with 51 PAH and 12 PVOD/PCH patients, number of patients with perfusion defect or number of segments with abnormal perfusion did not differ between two groups. [34] While DECT does not reveal pathognomonic signs of PVOD, presence of perfusion defects may serve as a useful diagnostic clue in presence of other radiological signs and clinical suspicion [34].

Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing

CPET provides useful diagnostic information in suspected PH or PVOD. In a study by Pérez‐Olivares et al. [35] PVOD patients showed greater functional impairment than PAH controls, with lower peak oxygen uptake (peak V̇O2), reduced oxygen pulse, earlier ventilatory threshold, and more pronounced ventilatory inefficiency—in the form of higher ratio of minute ventilation/carbon dioxide production (V̇E/V̇CO2) and slope of minute ventilation to carbon dioxide production (V̇E/V̇CO2 slope) [36]. In a study with 28 patients with diagnosed PVOD and 51 PAH invasive CPET further improved diagnostic accuracy [35]. Specifically, a composite model incorporating predicted V̇O2, V̇E/V̇CO2, PETCO2 slope, and PVR yielded excellent discrimination for PVOD (AUC 0.97), although these metrics did not correlate with hemodynamic severity [35].

RHC

RHC is the gold standard for diagnosing and hemodynamic characterization of PH [7]. The hemodynamic profile of PVOD is indistinguishable from PAH, with both demonstrating precapillary PH [7]. Although PVOD primarily affects the small post‐capillary venules, the PCWP typically remains normal (≤ 15 mmHg) because it reflects pressures in the larger pulmonary veins and left atrium rather than the distal venous circulation, as illustrated in Figure 3 [3, 37]. A proposed theoretical method to differentiate PVOD from PAH involves partitioning pulmonary vascular resistance into arterial and venous components by analyzing the pulmonary artery pressure decay curve after balloon occlusion to estimate pulmonary capillary pressures [38, 39, 40]. However, these techniques were described more than three decades ago and require further validation in contemporary cohorts. Also, it may have limited clinical utility given wider availability of HRCT and [41].

Pulmonary Vasodilator Responsiveness Testing

Vasoreactivity testing is recommended in the evaluation of idiopathic, hereditary, and drug‐induced PAH to identify potential candidates for calcium channel blocker (CCB) therapy [7]. Although current guidelines do not address its role in diagnosing PVOD, up to 12.2% of PVOD patients may exhibit a positive vasoreactivity response; however, this does not predict CCB responsiveness or the risk of pulmonary edema. Development of pulmonary edema after initiation of vasodilator therapy despite a normal PAWP is highly suggestive of PVOD and may result in severe hypoxia or death [37, 42, 43]. Severe acute pulmonary edema following intravenous epoprostenol has also been reported in patients with pulmonary hypertension associated with pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis, likely related to pulmonary venous involvement by Langerhans cells, and can be considered a physiological mimic of PVOD [44]. Notably, Montani et al. reported no pulmonary edema during short duration reactivity testing with inhaled nitric oxide, possibly due to limited exposure time or the need for more potent vasodilators to provoke edema [4]. In their series, pulmonary edema typically occurred 24 h to 9 days after therapy initiation [4]. Therefore, vasoreactivity testing is not indicated in suspected PVOD, although pulmonary edema following vasodilator therapy may offer a diagnostic clue. Additional studies are required to better define the role of acute vasodilator testing. Pulmonary edema or worsening of radiological findings after initiation of PAH specific therapy was seen in five among seven patients in our study cohort.

Clinical Diagnosis of PVOD

The diagnosis of PVOD is based on a combination of clinical, radiological, functional, and genetic findings, as well as the development of pulmonary edema with pulmonary vasodilator therapy. The presence of at least two characteristic HRCT findings among mediastinal lymphadenopathy, centrilobular ground‐glass opacities, and smooth interlobular septal thickening together along with marked resting hypoxemia (PaO2 < 70 mmHg), reduced DLCO (< 55% predicted), occult alveolar hemorrhage, impaired exercise capacity, or biallelic EIF2AK4 mutations or known exposures can strongly support the diagnosis [45]. Genetic testing may be warranted in patients who meet clinical criteria and in at‐risk family members [13]. Histopathological confirmation is typically limited to explanted or post‐mortem lung tissue, as biopsy carries a substantial risk of life‐threatening bleeding in PVOD [3].

Prognosis and Treatment

PVOD is a rapidly progressive disease with markedly worse outcomes than other forms of PAH. Early referral to a center with PH expertise is crucial for multidisciplinary management, consideration for clinical trials, and lung transplant evaluation. Reported 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year survival rates in contemporary cohorts are 86%, 50%, and 27%, respectively, with a median survival of only 36 months from diagnosis [46]. An older series from nearly two decades ago reports a median time from diagnosis to death or transplant of 11.8 months in PVOD versus 42.3 months in PAH, with time from symptom onset to death/transplant of 24.4 months versus 57.9 months, respectively [4]. Boucly et al. evaluated multiple PH risk‐stratification tools including REVEAL, COMPERA, French PH Registry, and the ESC/ERS three‐ and four‐strata systems and found all capable of predicting prognosis in PVOD [46]. The ESC/ERS four‐strata model was reported to have the best discrimination for high‐risk patients at baseline and follow‐up [46]. However, c‐statistics ranged from 0.64 to 0.74, highlighting that risk scores should be applied cautiously and interpreted within the broader clinical context.

Supportive Care

Supportive care for PVOD includes oxygen therapy, diuretics for RV failure and pulmonary edema, immunizations, and cardiopulmonary rehabilitation. Preventing infection is critical; vaccination against SARS‐CoV‐2, influenza, and Streptococcus pneumoniae is recommended [7]. It is noteworthy that 3 of our 7 cases had concurrent infections that heralded or worsened their disease (2 viral, 1 fungal). Routine anticoagulation is not advised unless there is another clear indication. For patients who are not transplant candidates, early palliative care involvement can assist with symptom management, goals‐of‐care planning, and making decisions pertaining to end‐of‐life care. Additional supportive care, including nutritional support and mental health services to compliment a holistic management approach.

Effect of PAH‐Specific Therapy in PVOD

Current PAH pharmacotherapy targets four main pathways: the nitric oxide and prostacyclin pathways, endothelin receptor antagonism, and modulation of transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) signaling mediated via BMPR2. However, their role in PVOD is limited given the pathophysiology and propensity for precipitating pulmonary edema. For this reason, PVOD patients have been excluded from major randomized clinical trials. Evidence attesting to potential benefit in select cases is confined to anecdotal case reports and small case series [37, 47]. We conducted a literature search in MEDLINE via PubMed, Embase, and Scopus and identified five case series and retrospective studies reporting the effects of pulmonary vasodilator therapy in PVOD. Previously reported series and retrospective studies, excluding individual case reports and systematic reviews are chronologically summarized in e‐supplement Table D. Ogawa et al. conducted a systematic review of 20 studies involving 64 patients with PVOD treated with intravenous prostacyclin and endothelin receptor antagonists. Although 6MWT distance and hemodynamics improved, pulmonary edema developed in nearly 30 patients [48]. PAH‐specific vasodilators increase pulmonary capillary blood flow, but in PVOD, fixed obstruction of the post‐capillary venules prevents adequate outflow, causing a rapid rise in capillary pressure and pulmonary edema. The likelihood of pulmonary edema appears to depend on the vasodilatory response and the severity of venular fibrosis [4]. Given the potential for hemodynamic and functional improvement in select patients, PAH‐specific therapy should be individualized on a case‐by‐case basis at experienced pulmonary hypertension centers. Sotatercept is a first‐in‐class fusion protein that functions as a ligand trap for selected members of the TGF‐β superfamily. By binding circulating activins and growth differentiation factors (GDFs), it helps rebalance pro‐proliferative and anti‐proliferative signaling in pulmonary vascular cells [49, 50]. Further sotatercept does not increase cardiac output, but reduces pulmonary vascular resistance, thereby lowering mean pulmonary arterial pressure. PVOD was excluded from all four major clinical trials PULSAR, STELLAR, ZENITH, and HYPERION [49, 50, 51, 52], and consequently, data on the use of sotatercept in PVOD remain extremely limited. However, given its potential to reverse pulmonary vascular remodeling, sotatercept may represent a potential therapeutic avenue. Herein, our case series patient‐1 has been tolerating sotatercept for the last one and a half years without development of pulmonary edema or clinical deterioration. Although existing data are limited, sotatercept may represent a potential medical therapeutic option for a carefully selected subset of patients with PVOD, potentially serving as a bridge to delay lung transplantation. The long‐term effects of PAH‐specific therapy in PVOD remain uncertain, as prospective randomized controlled trials are challenging to conduct given the rarity of the disease.

Immunosuppressive Therapy

Cytotoxic injury, immune‐complex deposition, and inflammation are implicated in PVOD, thus CTD‐associated PVOD may respond to immunosuppression [53]. Small case series report clinical and hemodynamic stabilization with mycophenolate and corticosteroids in select patients [18, 54]. Imatinib has shown benefit in several reports, with improvements in functional class, hemodynamics, and survival in PVOD and PCH cohorts. However, evidence remains limited to anecdotal limited to case reports and small case series [55, 56, 57, 58].

Lung Transplantation

PVOD is a rapidly progressive disease, lung transplantation remains the only definitive, potentially life‐saving treatment modality. Wille et al. studied transplant outcomes in 49 patients with PVOD and 647 with PAH. Transplant rates were similar between groups (50.0% vs. 47.6%, p = 0.60); however, at 6 months, pre‐transplant mortality was significantly higher in the PVOD group (22.6% vs. 11%) [59]. Waitlist mortality is high in contemporary studies, underscoring the importance of close monitoring while awaiting transplantation [59, 60]. Recurrence of PVOD in transplanted lungs has been reported [28, 61]. Preoperative optimization and meticulous perioperative management are critical, particularly in patients with severe hypoxemia or right heart dysfunction. Despite the severity of PVOD, post‐transplant outcomes are generally favorable, with contemporary series reporting 1‐year survival of 81%–95% and 3–5‐year survival of 58%–84%, which is comparable to outcomes in patients transplanted for idiopathic PAH. [60, 62, 63] Therefore, early referral to a lung transplant center is crucial for appropriate candidates with suspected PVOD.

Conclusion

PVOD is a progressive and often fatal disease that necessitates prompt evaluation for early diagnosis. Exposure history, associated conditions, characteristic imaging findings, and disproportionate hypoxemia should heighten clinical suspicion. EIF2AK4 genetic testing can help to confirm hereditary cases and identify at‐risk relatives. Treatment options remain limited, as PAH‐specific therapies carry a significant risk of pulmonary edema and should be used with caution under the auspices of specialized PH centers with transplant expertise. Lung transplantation is the only definitive therapy, and early diagnosis and referral to a transplant center are therefore critical.
