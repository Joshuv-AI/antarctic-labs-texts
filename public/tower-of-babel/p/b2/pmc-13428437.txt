Cognitive functioning, verbal memory performance, and ADHD-related symptoms in late-preterm children
Authors: Elias Nasrallah, Jamil Andrawes, Huzam Joubran, Mervatte Shukha, Nael Elias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreterm infants have a higher risk for low intelligence quotient (IQ) levels, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and verbal memory problems compared to full term infants. However, still there is limited evidence regarding the late preterm group, which accounts for the largest group of prematurely born children.Objectives/aimsTo compare IQ levels, ADHD symptoms, and verbal memory performance between children born late preterm and full-term.MethodsA retrospective cohort study with prospective follow-up assessments, included 100 children who are 8–11 years old, 69 were born full term and 31 were born in the late preterm period, each child underwent evaluation by a specialized clinical psychologist, which included an IQ test, assessment for symptoms of ADHD and auditory memory problems.ResultsThere was no significant difference between the two groups in general IQ scores, including verbal and performance scores (p = 0.13, 0.91, and 0.14 respectively). Introduction

Newborn infants are considered term when born between 37 + 0 and 42 + 6 weeks of gestation, the World Health Organization (WHO) defines preterm birth as any birth before 37 completed weeks of gestation [1]. Nearly three-fourths of all preterm births are late preterm infants (LPI), born between 34 and 36 + 6 weeks’ gestation, making this population a significant public health concern [2].

Previous studies focused more on extremely preterm (< 28 weeks) or very preterm (28 to < 32 weeks) children, despite the fact that children born late preterm account for the largest group of all children born preterm [3–5]. Over the past decade, growing attention has been directed toward both the short- and long-term outcomes of late preterm infants. Since LPI are born with anthropometric measurements (weight, head circumference, and length) similar to those of term infants, some doctors still treat them as mature babies (those born prematurely) and believe they are not at increased risk for mortality and morbidity, including neurodevelopmental disease [6]. However, findings remain inconsistent. Some studies suggest that LPI are at increased risk for developmental, metabolic, neurologic, and respiratory complications, as well as higher mortality and overall morbidity. In contrast, other studies report outcomes comparable to those of term infants, with substantially fewer complications than those observed in earlier preterm groups [6–8]. Cognitive and behavioral outcomes are among the most important long-term neurodevelopmental concerns in children born late preterm, as they may significantly affect academic achievement, executive functioning, and daily functioning during school age. Previous studies also have reported controversial findings regarding the association between late preterm birth and lower cognitive performance, attention difficulties, and behavioral problems, including ADHD-related symptoms [5, 8–11]. Verbal memory performance in this population has been less extensively studied, and available data remain limited and inconsistent.

In this study, we aimed to compare IQ levels, ADHD symptoms, and verbal memory performance in children born late preterm with those of full-term peers at 8–11 years of age. Identifying the most common disorders in this population may help guide resource allocation, enhance antenatal counseling, and reduce delays in diagnosis, thereby enabling earlier and more effective interventions.

Study population and methods

Study population

Our study included children who were born between 2005 and 2008 at the St. Vincent French Hospital of Nazareth. From this population, we invited participants using a random sampling approach in a 1:3 ratio. Specifically, two groups were constructed (full-term and late preterm) and participants in each group were randomly selected from the eligible population. Their age during the study was 8 to 11 years. The study population was divided into two groups: the first group included term children who were born between 37+ 0 and 42+ 6, the second group included late-preterm children who were born between 34+ 0 and 36+ 6 weeks.

Exclusion criteria

Children excluded from the study if any of the following are reported: Intellectual disability not attributable to prematurity; birth before 34 weeks’ gestation; chronic illness or congenital anomaly that may affect the results of the administered assessments; parental refusal to participate in the study.

Methods

Initially we reviewed the birth files for both groups, and information was collected from the files of the hospital database.

Later, we asked the parents to fill out a Sociodemographic questionnaire. Parental academic degree was defined as completion of ≥ 12 years of schooling followed by attainment of a higher education (college or university) degree; low family income was defined as a monthly household income of < 9,000 New Israeli Shekels (approximately $2,400 USD); lactation was considered positive if the infant received breastfeeding for ≥ 3 months, either exclusively or predominantly breastfed.

In addition, Parents and teachers completed the Vanderbilt Assessment Scale for the assessment of ADHD. The Vanderbilt Assessment Scale divided into various subsections, including symptoms of ADHD; symptoms of oppositional defiant/conduct disorder; and symptoms of anxiety/depression. These subsections are scored on a scale of zero to three: 0 = never, 1 = occasionally, 2 = often, 3 = very often; the National Institute for Children’s Health Quality Vanderbilt Assessment Scales identify ADHD in children (aged 5–12) by requiring 6 + symptoms (scored “often” or “very often”) in either the Inattentive or Hyperactive/Impulsive categories, plus functional impairment [12–14].

We invited the children to perform a set of tests conducted by a specialized developmental psychologist, which included:

Cognitive assessment: Cognitive assessment was performed using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children–Revised 1995 version (WISC-R95), which includes verbal and performance domains [15]. The administered subtests included: Information, assessing general knowledge; Similarities, assessing verbal abstraction and reasoning; Arithmetic, assessing arithmetic reasoning and working memory; Vocabulary, assessing word knowledge and verbal concept formation; Picture Completion, assessing visual perception and attention to detail; Picture Arrangement, assessing sequential reasoning and social comprehension; Block Design, assessing nonverbal reasoning, spatial visualization, and problem-solving; and Coding, evaluate how quickly and accurately a person can process information [1, 2]. Subtest results are presented as standardized scaled scores (mean 10, standard deviation 3). In addition, Verbal IQ, Performance IQ, and Full-Scale IQ (FSIQ) scores (mean 100, SD 15) were used for analysis.

The administered subtests included: Information, assessing general knowledge; Similarities, assessing verbal abstraction and reasoning; Arithmetic, assessing arithmetic reasoning and working memory; Vocabulary, assessing word knowledge and verbal concept formation; Picture Completion, assessing visual perception and attention to detail; Picture Arrangement, assessing sequential reasoning and social comprehension; Block Design, assessing nonverbal reasoning, spatial visualization, and problem-solving; and Coding, evaluate how quickly and accurately a person can process information [1, 2]. Subtest results are presented as standardized scaled scores (mean 10, standard deviation 3). In addition, Verbal IQ, Performance IQ, and Full-Scale IQ (FSIQ) scores (mean 100, SD 15) were used for analysis.

Verbal memory: Assessed using the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT), a standardized neuropsychological measure that evaluates verbal learning. For each participant, raw scores were converted to standardized deviation scores relative to age-matched normative values, with higher scores indicating better performance [16]. Standardized deviation scores for learning and recall trials were analyzed.

For each participant, raw scores were converted to standardized deviation scores relative to age-matched normative values, with higher scores indicating better performance [16]. Standardized deviation scores for learning and recall trials were analyzed.

All neuropsychological assessments were conducted using standardized, validated versions of the instruments and were administered by a specialized clinical psychologist. Cognitive assessments were administered in the participant’s primary language. Given that the majority of the cohort consisted of Arabic-speaking children from Nazareth and surrounding areas, validated Arabic versions of the assessment tools were primarily used; for Hebrew-speaking participants, the corresponding Hebrew versions were administered.

Informed consent was obtained from all parents of children included in the study.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., New York, USA). Demographic characteristics were summarized using standard descriptive statistics. Differences in the distribution of categorical variables between the two study groups (LPI and term infants) were assessed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared between groups using the independent samples t-test when data were normally distributed, and the Mann–Whitney U test when normality assumptions were not met. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associations between continuous cognitive variables and study group were estimated using binary logistic regression analysis. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study included 100 children: 69 born full term and 31 born late preterm. Discussion

In the present study, we assessed the IQ levels, ADHD symptoms, and verbal memory performance in 8–11 years old children born late preterm, comparing them with children born term. Our results showed that late preterm children have no significant difference in IQ levels with almost similar distribution of IQ Scores (Fig. 1), ADHD symptoms, or verbal memory performance compared with children born at full term.

Distribution of IQ scores in term and preterm children

Previous studies that tested preterm infants born before 34 weeks showed lower cognitive function and higher risk for ADHD [8, 17–19], still the group of LPI have not been studied enough and the results were controversial. A recent meta-analysis by Pettinger et al. demonstrated that children born preterm 32–38 weeks gestation (particularly those born moderately preterm) have an increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders compared with term-born peers, including cerebral palsy, autism spectrum disorder, global developmental delay, cognitive impairment, social difficulties, and the need for special educational support. However, these associations were less pronounced among LPI’s [8]. Marjanneke et al. conducted a systemic review of 28 studies on school outcomes, cognitive functioning, behavior problems, and psychiatric disorders. Their findings demonstrated that moderate and LPI’s, both children and adults, had more school-related difficulties, lower cognitive functioning, increased behavioral problems, and a higher prevalence of psychiatric disorders compared with full-term peers [9]. Additional systemic reviews by Chan et al. and Martinez-Nadal also showed similar results [5, 10]. Bogičević et al. and Talge et al. studies showed lower cognitive function at 6 years for moderate-LPI and higher risk for attention problems [7, 20].

Romeo et al. study results support ours, showing that low risk LPT children had similar longitudinal cognitive development at 12- and 18-months corrected age, and a normal full IQ score at 5 years [11, 21]. In addition, Gurka et al. reported that LPI born otherwise healthy seem to have no real burdens regarding cognition, achievement, behavior, and socioemotional development throughout childhood (from age 4 to 15 years) [22]. Harris et al. reported that at 19 years of age, LPI have similar rates of learning disabilities and ADHD as infants born term [6].

Verbal memory in preterm infants and children is considered a significant area of concern, as research indicates that these individuals often exhibit deficits compared to their term-born peers. Previous studies show that preterm children, particularly those born with very low birth weight (VLBW), perform worse on verbal memory tasks, including narrative memory and sentence repetition, even after interventions like computerized working memory training, which can yield some improvements [23]. Furthermore, by early adolescence, preterm individuals struggle with complex verbal information, demonstrating lower performance on learning and memory tests that require higher verbal complexity [24]. Additionally, a study found that very preterm children had significantly lower verbal ability and spatial working memory scores at age 11, suggesting persistent cognitive challenges [25]. While some studies indicate potential for “catch-up” in verbal skills, the overall evidence points to enduring difficulties in verbal memory associated with preterm birth [25]. To our knowledge, our study is among the first studies to specifically examine verbal memory in late preterm infants (LPI). Our findings suggest similar verbal memory performance in this group, highlighting the importance of distinguishing LPI from children born at earlier gestational ages (< 34 weeks).

It is presumed that the increased risk of neurodevelopmental impairment is related to medical complications of prematurity that adversely affect brain development. LPI’s generally do not experience the full spectrum of complications seen in infants born before 34 weeks’ gestation; however, they exhibit higher rates of short-term morbidity [26–28], and increased healthcare utilization during the first 12 months of life compared with term infants [29].

With these conflicting data from previous studies and our study’s relatively small sample size, it is difficult to draw definitive conclusions, emphasizing that LPI group should be further studied. We are planning to further assess the children in our cohort at 15–17 years.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although we aimed to include a well-defined cohort, the sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect small but clinically meaningful differences between late preterm and term-born children. Second, the study population was derived from a specific geographic and healthcare and consisted predominantly of participants from the Arab minority population, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations. Third, outcomes were assessed at a single time point (8–11 years of age), which may not reflect developmental trajectories over time or capture differences that emerge later in adolescence. Fourth, the late preterm group (34–36 weeks) is inherently heterogeneous, and voluntary participation may have introduced selection bias, as families who agreed to participate in neurodevelopmental assessments may differ from those who declined, potentially limiting the ability to fully capture differences within this subgroup. Lastly, a formal performance validity measure was not included in the neuropsychological assessment protocol. Although all evaluations were conducted by experienced professionals according to standardized procedures, the absence of an objective performance validity assessment represents a limitation and may affect the interpretation of cognitive test results.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that late preterm children may have comparable IQ levels, ADHD symptoms, and verbal memory performance to those born at full term. Further studies are needed to confirm these findings.
