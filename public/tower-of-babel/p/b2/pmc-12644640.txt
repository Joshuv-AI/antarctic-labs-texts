The effects of low-yield nicotine cigarette smoke inhalation on orthodontic tooth movement
Authors: Burak Gulnar, Ahsen Gulnar, Alperen Kamiloglu, Peter Petocz, Ersan Karadeniz, Carmen Gonzales Karadeniz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the effects of low-nicotine cigarette smoke (CS) inhalation on orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) in rats.MethodsForty 4-week-old male Wistar rats, were randomly divided into four groups three experimental and one control. Group 1 (control group) had dental impressions taken at week 9. Group 2 OTM group received a continuous mesially directed force applied to the maxillary first molar at week 9 for 4 weeks. Group 3 CS group rats had 9 weeks of CS inhalation; Group 4 CS + OTM group, 9 weeks of CS inhalation and 4 weeks of orthodontic force application. Rats in groups 3 (CS) and 4 (CS + OTM) were exposed to 10 cigarettes for 8 minutes, 3 times per day for 9 weeks. All groups had polyvinyl siloxane impressions taken at week 9 and at the end of the experimental period (week 13). At week 13, all rats were sacrificed under general anesthesia, and their maxillae were dissected and scanned using a TRIOS 2014-1 intraoral scanner. The tooth movements were measured using digital models.ResultsA slight increase in tooth movement rate was observed in smokers. However, this difference was not statistically significant.ConclusionsCS inhalation did not increase the rate of OTM in rats.Keywords: Cigarette, Tooth movement, Rats

INTRODUCTION

Tobacco remains the leading cause of preventable death, claiming 8.7 million lives annually and incurring over US$1.4 trillion in healthcare and lost productivity costs each year.1-3 Within the oral cavity, tobacco adversely impacts oral health, leading to conditions such as oral cancer, other potentially malignant disorders4 (e.g., oral leukoplakia and erythroplakia), oral candidiasis,5 periodontal disease, tooth loss, increased gingival recession, implant failure, dental caries, discoloration of teeth and dental restorations, coated tongue (black hairy tongue), nicotinic stomatitis, and impaired wound healing after dental procedures like tooth extractions.6 Research indicates that heavy smokers (more than 10 cigarettes per day) experience significantly higher failure rates of orthodontic mini screws in the initial 4 months post-insertion compared to light smokers (10 or fewer cigarettes per day) or non-smokers.6

The influence of smoking on orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) remains a topic of debate. Some studies have suggested that rats exposed to nicotine exhibit accelerated OTM,7 while others have shown a slowdown in this process. Previous research has been limited, often examining only one chemical in smoke, such as nicotine injected intraperitoneally, and frequently using high doses. One study administered nicotine and tar to the drinking water. Ideally, cigarette smoke (CS) should be tested in its entirety as it is produced and experienced by smokers.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of conventional low-yield CS inhalation on OTM in rats. The orthodontic mesial tooth movement of the rat’ maxillary first molar has been most frequently used to study the mechanisms of tooth movement and the response of periodontal tissues.8,9 We hypothesized that CS affects orthodontically induced tooth movement. In this study, we evaluated the effect of smoking on dental movement. Whether dental movement is fast or slow does not change the fact that smoking has harmful health effects. An increased amount of dental movement occurs with increased bone resorption, which does not imply a benefit in terms of speeding up treatment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The present study followed the guidelines of the Animal Research Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE).10 The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki (2008 version). The study procedures were approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Commission of Karadeniz Technical University Surgical Research and Practice Center University (53488718/198, 18/04/2016).

Animals

Forty male Wistar rats (4 weeks old, 200–250 g) were used. The animals were housed in plastic cages, had access to food and water ad libitum, and were maintained on a 12-hour light-dark cycle. The temperature was between 22–23°C. Prior to the experimental procedures, all the animals were allowed to acclimatize to the animal facility environment for 5 days.

A sample of 10 animals per group was selected based on previous studies on OTM published by our research group.

Experimental design

The rats were randomly assigned to one control and three experimental groups: Group 1, control group (n = 10) rats were not exposed to CS at any time during the experimental period; only a maxillary dental impression was taken on the 9th week; Group 2, OTM group (n = 10), a continuous closed coil spring (50 g) was applied to the maxillary molar for 4 weeks; Group 3, CS group rats had 9 weeks of CS inhalation; Group 4, CS + OTM group, 9 weeks of CS inhalation, and 4 weeks of OTM.

Rats in groups 3 (CS) and 4 (CS + OTM) were exposed to 10 cigarettes for 8 minutes, 3 times per day for 9 weeks. All groups had polyvinyl siloxane impressions taken at week 9 and the end of the experimental period (week 13). The rats received blended food following the application of closed-coil springs. This ensured proper feeding with orthodontic appliances and minimized the risk of breakage (Figure 1).

Cigarette smoke chamber

The CS chamber consisted of a 55 × 35 × 30 cm3 clear acrylic box, an air-pump device, and air holes. We fabricated a similar chamber as previously described by César-Neto et al.11,12 and Nociti Júnior et al.13 A pump machine was built to suck the cigarettes simulating a “puff” that was inhaled by the rats. Ten commercially available cigarettes, each containing 0.7 mg nicotine, 9 mg tar, and 10 mg carbon monoxide, were placed in a holder, and a lighter was applied to each cigarette. Rats were placed in an acrylic chamber while the smoke of 10 was pumped into the chamber until the cigarettes were completely consumed and all that remained was the cigarette butts.11,13,14 Smoker rats inhaled CS drawn through at burning cigarette’ filter and exhaled through the mouthpiece of the cigarette into the acrylic chamber (Figure 2).

Smoker rats inhaled cigarette smoke which was drawn through the burning cigarette’s filter and exited through the mouthpiece of the cigarette into the acrylic chamber.

Orthodontic tooth movement

The appliance was placed under anesthesia using an intramuscular injection of xylazine at a dose of 10 mg/kg and ketamine at a doe of 80 mg/kg body weight for groups 2 (OTM) and 4 (OTM + CS) at week 9. A mesially directed force was applied, as previously described, utilizing a 50-gram NiTi closed-coil spring (American Orthodontics, Sheboygan, WI, USA) extending from the maxillary left molar to the left central incisor from weeks 9th to the 13th week (Figure 3A).

The orthodontic appliances were not reactivated at any time during the experimental period, but the rats and their appliances were checked daily. Rats were fed a crushed food to avoid appliance dislodgement and facilitate their feeding. At week 13 all rats were sacrificed under general anaesthesia, their maxillae were dissected and scanned with TRIOS 2014-1 intraoral scanner (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark). The coil springs were not removed from the maxillae to avoid any distortion in the amount of tooth movement achieved until the end of the experiments. Tooth movement was measured on digital models by using Maestro 3D software (Age Solutions S.R.L., Pisa, Italy). For this purpose, the change in the distance between the mesio-buccal cusp tip of upper first molar and the mesio-buccal cusp tip of upper second molar was quantified. Palatally, the change in the distance between the mesio-palatal cusp tip of upper first molar to mesio-palatal cusp tip of upper third molars was also determined. The total amount of tooth movement was calculated by taking the average of the buccal and palatal measurements at T0 (initial) and T1 (after 4 weeks of orthodontic force application).

Additionally, the change in distance between the mesiopalatal cusp tip of the upper first molar and the mesiopalatal cusp tip of the upper third molar was measured palatally.

The total amount of tooth movement was determined by calculating the average of the buccal and palatal measurements at initial (T0) and after 4 weeks of orthodontic force application (T1) (Figure 3B and 3C). Landmarks on the occlusal surface of the palatal cusps of the third molar were easily identifiable and therefore considered reliable reference points for analysis, in contrast to the mesiobuccal cusps of the second molar, which exhibited significant wear.

All measurements were performed by the same investigator who was blinded to the rats’ identification groups to avoid selection and reporting biases. Each measurement was repeated thrice and the mean value was used as the final measurement. To test measurement reproducibility, measurements were performed 10 times for each randomly selected group. The standard error was limited to less than 1%.

Statistical analysis

To determine the statistical significance of differences among groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was carried out using the group as a fixed factor, and post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni method. Differences were considered statistically significant at < 0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The sample size was determined based on previous studies.15,16 In this study, using an ANOVA experimental design with a power analysis value of 0.80, an effect size of 0.6, and a Type I error rate of 0.05, a minimum of nine samples per group and a total of at least 36 samples were required. We examined the sample sizes used in similar studies in the existing literature. Thus, we aimed to establish an appropriate sample size for our study to enhance statistical power and ensure the validity of the results.

The number of cigarettes (10 cigarettes) and duration of CS inhalation by the rats were determined in previous studies.11,13,17,18 Nevertheless, two rats demonstrated greater sensitivity to such volumes of smoke and died before completing the experimental period.

DISCUSSION

In this in vivo study, we aimed to examine the impact of low-nicotine (0.7 mg) CS inhalation on OTM in rats, which correlates with approximately 10 cigarettes per day in humans. The rats were subjected to a specific smoking regimen: inhalation CS three times daily for 10 minover 63 days. The rate of tooth movement in the OTM group was similar to that reported in previous study19 that assessed the effect of force magnitude on tooth movement in rat molars. Conversely, a slight increase was observed in the rate of tooth movement in the cigarette smoking group compared to the non-smoking group; however, this increase did not reach statistical significance.

These findings are consistent with those of previous studies.18,20-22 Ferreira et al.18 reported similar experimental conditions, administering 10 cigarettes (0.8 mg nicotine, 10 mg tar, and 10 mg carbon monoxide) for 8 minutes, three times a day for 60 days, and found no significant differences in OTM between smoking and non-smoking groups. Additionally, Araujo et al.20 conducted a study in which subcutaneous administration of nicotine (1 mg/kg) 1 month before the application of orthodontic force showed no statistically significant difference in tooth movement rate when a 25 g-force closed coil spring was applied to the rat’s maxillary molar for 28 days compared to the control group (saline + OTM). Furthermore Nagaie et al.21 and Matoba et al.22 utilized the Waldo method, where an elastic power chain was applied between the first and second molars of rats for 10 and 14 days, respectively, and found that tooth movement was retarded due to diminished bone resorption from osteoclastogenesis inhibition following the oral administration of water containing tobacco smoke components isolated from the mainstream smoke of 20 cigarettes (1.2 mg nicotine and 14 mg tar).

However, the influence of smoking on OTM remains controversial, with some studies suggesting increased tooth movement.23-26 Jiang et al.23 conducted a study on mice, applying a 30–35 g NiTi closed-coil spring between the incisor and left maxillary molar for 14 days, while exposing the mice to CS from two cigarettes (each containing 1.1 mg nicotine and 13 mg tar) three days prior to orthodontic treatment for 14 consecutive days. In this scenario, CS was derived from the burning cone through cigarette paper, simulating passive smoking.

In contrast, our study design involved the release of mainstream smoke at the mouth end of at cigarette using the chamber-activated puffing system. The selected dose (0.7 mg nicotine) three times a day would correspond to a daily consumption of 10 cigarettes per day in humans based on previous studies.12,27 The dose was selected following the global trend of reducing the nicotine content of cigarettes, as doses of nicotine around 0.5 mg per cigarette may provide enough nicotine for taste and sensory stimulation and should be adequate to prevent or limit the development of addiction in most young people.28

Using different force magnitudes and daily injections of nicotine at similar concentrations, we found that nicotine accelerated OTM in a dose-dependent manner in rats. Sodagar et al.24 applied a 60 g mesial force and administered daily injections of 0.5, 0.75, and 1 mg/kg nicotine for 14 days. They found that the greatest amount of tooth movement occurred with the highest nicotine dose (0.93 mg/kg). Bakathir et al.25 confirmed these findings histologically following daily intraperitoneal injection of nicotine (0.37, 0.57, and 0.93 mg/kg) for 28 days. Nicotine injections were started 14 days before 30 g-continuous force application and continued for an additional 2 weeks. Similar results were reported by Kirschneck et al.29 following the subcutaneous administration of nicotine (1.89 mg/kg) 10 days prior to orthodontic loading (125 g) for 14 and 28 days. In a mouse model, in vivo exposure to CS promoted tooth movement and enhanced osteoclast numbers in a 30–35 g NiTi-closed coil spring for 14 days. Each cigarette contained 1.1 mg of nicotine and 13 mg of tar.30 However, when nicotine was injected at a higher dose (2 mg/kg) for 3, 7, 14, and 21 days, Shintcovsk et al.8 found that nicotine decreased the number of osteoclasts during orthodontic movement with a 30 g coil spring inserted between the maxillary first molar and central incisors in rats.

Differences in experimental design, such as nicotine concentration and duration of nicotine, tar, and carbon monoxide administration, may have influenced these contradictory results. For instance, in vivo nicotine injection is not an ideal model for exploring the influence of smoking on alveolar bone resorption. Animal models that utilize inhalation as the route of administering CS have exceptional face validity because they best stimulate the unique pharmacokinetic characteristics (i.e., rate of absorption and brain delivery) that are associated with smoking.31 In addition, different methods have been used to quantify the rate of tooth movement, for example, using digital calipers directly on dental plaster models20 or on dissected jaws,18 sagittal22,30 and occlusal21 views of micro-computed tomography.

The mechanisms by which smoking affects the bones are not fully understood. Contradictory observations have been made; for example, no difference in the number20 or a lower number of osteoclasts8 has been reported. However, a higher frequency of osteoclasts has also been reported.25 Rats exposed to CS showed an upregulation of the receptor activator of the nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL)/osteoprotegerin (OPG) ratio compared to rats without exposure.32 In addition, two studies comparing smokers and non-smokers who were susceptible to periodontitis showed that smokers had lower OPG levels without a statistical difference in RANKL levels.33,34 Henemyre et al.35 observed that nicotine stimulated osteoclast-like cell differentiation in cell cultures derived from pigs. Saldanha et al.36 demonstrated that the administration of nicotine to jawbone defects in dogs changes the density of newly formed bone tissue due to the inhibition of revascularization. Henemyre et al.35 found that nicotine increases bone resorption. This has led to the idea that smoking could enhance OTM. In contrast, Saldanha et al.36 showed that nicotine decreased blood circulation. However, These two studies reported contradictory results. In this context, we aimed to evaluate which of these findings is more accurate. In our study, smoking did not significantly increase tooth movement.

Limitations in our study

It is not possible with the current experimental design to consider all smoker population variations that we may encounter in the orthodontic clinic; for example, patients with different durations of smoking habits (years vs. months), number of cigarettes smoked per day, cigarette content (nicotine, tar, and carbon monoxide), individual smoking patterns, ethnic differences, and rates of absorption that cannot be replicated in an animal model.

Future research should elucidate whether a longer period or the use of different smoking devices could lead to different results. Finally, the results of the present investigation cannot be directly extrapolated to clinical situations because of the significant anatomical and metabolic differences between rats and humans. Therefore, direct comparisons of our findings with hose of human studies should be performed with caution.

CONCLUSIONS

In this experimental design, CS inhalation did not significantly increase the rate of OTM in rats. However, CS did not accelerate tooth movement. However, this does not change the fact that smoking is harmful to the health of patients undergoing orthodontic treatment.
