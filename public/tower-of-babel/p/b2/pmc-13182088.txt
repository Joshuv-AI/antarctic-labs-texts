Feed-forward neural networks for genomic selection: insights into varying levels of dominance heritability
Authors: Hamid Sahebalam, Mohsen Gholizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenomic selection enables the prediction of genetic and breeding values from high-dimensional marker data, improving selection efficiency in breeding programs. Feed-forward neural networks (FFNN) are a type of deep learning model that approximates complex non-linear relationships between markers and traits through interconnected layers of neurons. The purpose of this study was evaluating the predictive performance of FFNN relative to parametric methods (GBLUP, BayesB, BayesL) and other machine learning (ML) approaches, including kernel-based (Support Vector Machine, SVM) and tree-based ensemble methods (Random Forest, RF), across traits with varying levels of narrow-sense (additive) heritability (= 0.05, 0.20, 0.50) and dominance heritability ( = 0.00, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20). Prediction accuracy was evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean squared error (MSE), while model stability was assessed via the coefficient of variation across 100 Monte Carlo replicates. Additionally, Cohen’s d effect size was used to assess the magnitude of differences among methods. The results indicate that in the absence of dominance, parametric methods showed higher PCC, but this advantage disappeared in the presence of dominance, such that in traits with low to medium additive heritability, ML methods showed higher PCC. Among ML methods, FFNN, RF and SVM reported higher PCC at low, medium and high additive heritabilities, respectively, in the absence of dominance. Also, method FFNN was able to perform better in the presence of dominance, especially when the additive heritability was high. An increase in dominance heritability at medium to high levels of additive heritability led to a decrease in PCC in all methods, which was not observed in low additive heritability. The FFNN method showed the highest MAE and MSE in the absence of dominance, which with increasing dominance heritability, this method was able to have the best performance among all methods. The highest stability of estimates was observed in traits with high additive heritability across 100 Monte Carlo replicates. Dominance variance substantially influenced predictive patterns, underscoring the importance of accounting for non-additive genetic effects in model selection. These findings highlight the effectiveness of FFNN for accurate and robust genomic prediction across complex genetic architectures and suggest that incorporating dominance heritability can optimize model performance in breeding programs.Keywords: FFNN, SVM, RF, Dominance heritability, Prediction accuracy, Model stability

Introduction

Traditional selection, such as the best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) that estimates traditional breeding values ​​using pedigree information, did not sufficiently improve the genetic progress desired by breeders. Therefore, a new molecular breeding technique was proposed by Meuwissen et al. (2001) [1] as genomic selection (GS). GS is a genetic evaluation strategy that uses high-density single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers throughout the genome to obtain genomic estimated breeding values ​​(GEBVs) of candidate individuals that are genotyped but lack phenotypes, which can better explain genetic variation between generations, improve the prediction accuracy of breeding value, shorten generation intervals, and accelerate genetic progress.

According to the principles of quantitative genetics, GS models generally decompose the phenotype into a genetic component (often captured by linear additive models) and a residual term. Partitioning the genetic variance of a trait into additive, dominance, and epistatic components provides valuable insights for prediction and selection, even if these statistical components do not fully represent the underlying biological mechanisms of gene action [2]. Accurate estimation of additive, dominance, and epistatic variances is essential for the prediction of breeding values and for guiding selection strategies. However, in traditional genetic evaluation models, non-additive effects are often ignored, primarily due to limitations such as insufficiently informative pedigrees and the computational complexity involved [3]. Importantly, dominance variance should not be overlooked, since additive variance captures only part of the biological dominance effects expressed by genes [4].

Various statistical models have been developed for GS, including frequentist methods such as Ridge Regression [5], least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) [6], elastic net [7], BLUP-based algorithms such as ridge regression BLUP (RRBLUP) [8] or the genomic BLUP (GBLUP) [9], and Bayesian-based algorithms such as BayesA [1], BayesB [1], BayesCπ [10] and Bayesian LASSO (BayesL) [11]. However, in most studies, there was no much variation in the predictive accuracy of different statistical models [12–14]. Furthermore, statistical models usually have strong assumptions and perform linear regression analysis. For example, the RRBLUP model assumes that all marker effects are normally distributed with a small (non-zero) variance and predicts phenotypes from a linear function of genotypic markers [15]. As a result, GS models based on traditional statistical methods not only have to face statistical challenges related to high-dimensional marker data, but also have difficulty in capturing complex relationships within genotypes (e.g., multicollinearity among markers), and between genotypes and phenotypes (e.g., genotype by-environment-by-trait interaction) [16].

In recent years, advances in machine learning (ML) algorithms, together with the rapid growth in computational power, have attracted considerable attention across scientific disciplines. ML approaches are particularly valued for their flexibility and ability to uncover latent structures within large, noisy, and complex datasets, including image-based information [17], vast heterogeneous records [18], and rapidly expanding digital data generated by fields such as computer vision, natural language processing (NLP), the internet of things (IoT), and modern hardware technologies [19]. In the field of genomics, the advent of high-throughput sequencing has led to the generation of massive and complex omics datasets that are often heterogeneous and redundant [20]. Consequently, the integration of ML methods has emerged as a promising strategy to strengthen GS and extend its applications in both animal and plant breeding.

One important branch of ML is deep learning, which relies on multi-layer neural network architectures, including feed-forward neural networks (multi-layer perceptrons, MLPs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and convolutional neural networks (CNNs). For a description on each type of network, its assumptions and input features see Goodfellow et al. (2016) [21] and Pérez-Enciso and Zingaretti, (2019) [22]. These architectures have also been applied in GS. For instance, Abdollahi-Arpanahi et al., (2020) [23] compared the performance of several methods, including GBLUP, BayesB, gradient boosting (GB), random forest (RF), MLP, and CNN, on both real and simulated datasets. They found that deep learning methods perform well with sufficiently large sample sizes, but their performance is often inconsistent.

Neural networks are composed of layers of interconnected neurons, where the output of each neuron is computed as the weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias term, followed by an activation function [24]. In the context of GS, each input neuron (marker) connects to all neurons in the first hidden layer, which in turn connects to the next hidden layer, continuing until the output layer that predicts the phenotype. Recent advances in neural network architectures and computational resources have enabled deep learning approaches with multiple hidden layers, capturing subtle interactions between markers [25]. Early applications of ML and neural networks in GS were demonstrated by Gianola et al. (2006, 2011) [26, 27], Gonzalez-Camacho et al. (2012, 2016) [28, 29], Perez-Rodriguez et al. (2012) [30], Ornella et al. (2014) [31], and Gonzalez-Recio et al. (2014) [32].

A Feed-Forward neural network (FFNN), representing one of the simplest forms of artificial neural architectures, is widely applied due to its straightforward training process and robust performance across diverse prediction tasks. Unlike models that assume explicit relationships among predictors, FFNN can operate effectively without predefined assumptions regarding input variables. The architecture is organized so that information propagates in a single direction, from the input layer, through one or more hidden layers, and finally to the output layer. Within this structure, there are no lateral connections between neurons of the same layer and no feedback loops from higher to lower layers. Instead, neurons in layer connect exclusively to neurons in the subsequent layer , with each connection assigned an independent weight [33].

Despite the extensive body of literature comparing ML and parametric methods for GS, several aspects remain insufficiently explored, particularly in the presence of non-additive genetic effects. Most existing studies primarily focus on additive genetic architectures, report prediction accuracy based on a single metric, and emphasize average performance without addressing the stability of predictions across repeated evaluations. Moreover, dominance effects are often either ignored or incorporated in a limited subset of models, and distinctions between the prediction of genomic breeding values and total genomic genetic values are rarely examined in a unified framework. In this study, we address these gaps by explicitly modeling additive and dominance effects across both parametric and ML methods, systematically evaluating their performance across varying combinations of additive and dominance heritability. In addition to conventional accuracy metrics, we assess prediction stability across extensive Monte Carlo replicates and evaluate practical significance using effect size measures, thereby providing a more comprehensive and robust assessment of model performance under diverse genetic architectures.

The objective of this study was to evaluate and compare the predictive performance and stability of a deep learning algorithm, specifically, a Feed-Forward neural network (FFNN), with other ML models, including a tree-based ensemble method (random forest, RF) and a kernel-based method (support vector machine, SVM), as well as three widely used parametric methods (namely, GBLUP, BayesB, and BayesL). These models were tested across a series of simulated scenarios varying in narrow-sense (additive, ​) and dominance (​) heritabilities. The performance or prediction accuracy was quantified using the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean squared error (MSE). In addition, prediction stability was evaluated based on the coefficient of variation (CV) of PCC over 100 Monte Carlo replicates.

Materials and methods

Data simulation

The population was simulated using the xbreed package [34] in the R software environment. To generate linkage disequilibrium (LD) and achieve mutation-drift equilibrium, a historical population was created by applying mutation and genetic drift over 500 generations of random mating, with an effective population size of 300 individuals (150 males and 150 females). Offspring were produced through random union of paternal and maternal gametes, maintaining a constant population size with equal numbers of males and females across generations. The final generation of this historical population (500th generation) served as the founding generation of the recent population. From this founding generation, 200 individuals (100 males and 100 females) were randomly selected as founders of the recent population, establishing a base generation of 200 individuals. Each female produced five offspring, increasing the population size to 500 in the first generation. Subsequently, 400 individuals (200 males and 200 females) were randomly selected from the first generation as parents of the second generation, expanding the population to 1000 individuals. This breeding scheme was continued for three generations, with parents of each generation selected from the previous generation. Phenotypic and genotypic data were simulated for all individuals in the second generation to serve as the reference population. The third generation was considered the target population, for which only genomic data were simulated, and the genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) and genomic estimated genetic values (GEGVs) were predicted. Tree-based ensemble methods

Random forest (RF)

The random forests algorithm is an ensemble algorithm that is built from the aggregation of decision trees (classification or regression). Each decision tree is grown using a bootstrap sample of training data containing genotypic and phenotypic information. The algorithm randomly (without replacement) selects a subset of predictor variables (features) (mtry parameter) as candidates for splitting the tree, thus looking for a predictor that can best partition the response variable in the training data set. In other words, this is done in successive splits until it eventually reaches nodes where there is maximum uniformity (animals with phenotypic information accumulate with similar genotypes for different SNPs in a node). The optimal predictor becomes the first node of the tree and splits the data. The algorithm continues to randomly select a subset of predictors at each node and then partitions the data until a complete tree is grown. Many trees are grown to build a forest (ntree parameter). The importance values ​​of the predictors are averaged across the trees and analyzed to ultimately determine those that best explain the variation in the response variable.

Hyperparameter optimization for machine learning models

For the SVM model, hyperparameter selection was performed using a hold-out validation strategy based on an independent target population. Specifically, several candidate values of the cost parameter C (0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0) were evaluated. For each candidate value, the SVM model with a Gaussian RBF kernel and an ε-insensitive loss function was trained on the reference population and subsequently used to predict GEBVs for the target population. Prediction accuracy was quantified using the PCC between true and predicted values. The value C = 1, which yielded the highest prediction accuracy, was therefore selected and used for all subsequent analyses. The same tuning procedure was applied independently across simulation scenarios.

For the Random Forest (RF) model, hyperparameter optimization for mtry included six candidate values: three based on the default value suggested in the literature (half, equal, and twice [72, 79]), and three additional values (1000, 1500, and 2000) to further explore the parameter space and ensure robust selection. Additionally, four candidate values for the ntree (500, 1000, 1500, 2000) and four values for the nodesize (5, 10, 15, 20) were tested. For each combination of these parameters, the RF model was trained on the reference population and used to predict GEBVs for the target population. Prediction accuracy was assessed using the PCC, and the combination yielding the highest PCC was selected for all subsequent analyses (mtry = 2000, ntree = 1000 and nodesize = 10). This procedure was applied independently across all simulation scenarios.

For the FFNN model, a two hidden layer architecture was used, with two neurons in each hidden layer. To select the optimal number of training epochs, five candidate values (150, 250, 350, 450, and 550) were evaluated. For each candidate, the network was trained on the reference population and used to predict GEBVs for the target population. Prediction accuracy was assessed using the PCC, and the number of epochs yielding the highest PCC (350) was selected for all subsequent analyses. All other hyperparameters were set to the default values of the implemented package.

Implementation of statistical approaches

The analyses of the evaluated methods were performed within the R software environment. Parametric approaches, including GBLUP, BayesB, and BayesL, were implemented using the BGLR package [46], while SVM was fitted using the e1071 package [47], RF using randomForest [48], and FFNN using the brnn package [49]. To ensure the stability and reliability of the estimates, each simulated scenario was replicated 100 times. The mean and standard deviation across replicates were reported for comparative purposes. Genotypic quality control was applied based on minor allele frequency (MAF), whereby markers with MAF < 0.05 were excluded from the genotypic matrix in each replication prior to marker effect estimation.

For the GBLUP, BayesB, and BayesL methods, a MCMC sampling scheme was employed to draw samples from the posterior distributions of the model parameters. The chain was run for 10,000 cycles, with the first 1,000 cycles discarded as burn-in. Marker effect estimates were obtained by averaging the samples from the remaining 9,000 cycles.

Model evaluation, performance metrics, stability of predictions, practical significance

The models were evaluated by the goodness of fit, based on the coefficient of determination (R2), which represents the proportion of variance in the dependent variable predicted by the statistical model. R2 was calculated as the square of the Pearson's correlation coefficient between the true and predicted values for the reference dataset.

Performance metrics such as Pearson's correlation coefficient (PCC), mean absolute error (MAE) and mean square error (MSE) were considered to assess the prediction accuracy.

Model performance was determined using descriptive statistics in boxplot format (such as minimum, maximum, median, mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation (CV)) for the PCC output, while only the mean and standard deviation were reported for the outputs of the MAE and MSE, all derived from 100 Monte Carlo simulation runs, enabling a robust characterization of both central tendency and variability in the results.

Results

Quality control and linkage disequilibrium (LD)

Based on the Monte Carlo simulation, the genotypic data in both the reference and target populations had dimensions of 1000 × 20,000. Out of the 20 K markers after quality control, 16,368 markers were fitted as predictors on the observations (phenotypes of simulated traits) by different statistical models on average in 100 replicates of Monte Carlo simulation.

The statistic, which is considered as a measure of the level of LD for adjacent positions (markers) in the genome, was estimated to be around 0.20 on average over 100 Monte Carlo replicates.

Discussion

Quality control and linkage disequilibrium (LD)

In this study, quality control (QC) of genotypic data was performed exclusively based on a minor allele frequency (MAF) threshold, where SNPs with MAF < 0.05 were excluded from further analysis. This commonly adopted threshold is effective in eliminating low-frequency variants that contribute little information and may introduce noise into genomic prediction models, particularly in small to moderate sample sizes [56, 57]. Other commonly used QC filters, such as per-marker or per-individual missingness rates, genotyping call rates, and deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE), were not applied in the current analysis. However, it is important to note that the genotypic data in this study were artificially simulated under idealized conditions, free from genotyping errors, missing data, or population structure artifacts. As such, applying additional QC filters beyond MAF would be redundant and may unnecessarily reduce the number of informative markers without improving the reliability of downstream analyses. Simulation under these ideal conditions is a widely accepted strategy in genomic prediction studies, allowing the isolation of specific effects (such as heritability level, dominance architecture, or statistical model performance) without the confounding influence of technical noise or biological irregularities [38, 58, 59]. Therefore, the restriction of QC to MAF-based filtering is scientifically justified within the design of this Monte Carlo simulation framework, ensuring that comparisons among statistical methods are conducted under controlled and unbiased conditions. Future work may benefit from incorporating realistic genotyping errors, population structure, or missing data patterns to evaluate model robustness under more complex genomic scenarios.

In this study, the average pairwise LD between adjacent markers, measured by the squared correlation coefficient , was estimated to be approximately 0.20 across 100 Monte Carlo replicates. This level of LD reflects an appropriate genetic correlation structure consistent with empirical observations in outbred populations such as humans and livestock, particularly under realistic marker densities [1, 60, 61]. This magnitude of LD strikes a critical balance between information redundancy and genomic coverage. Specifically, it is sufficiently high to ensure that markers effectively capture the phenotypic variance attributable to causal variants, thereby enhancing the power and accuracy of genomic prediction models. Simultaneously, it remains low enough to mitigate severe multicollinearity issues that could compromise model stability and interpretability [62]. Furthermore, this level of LD facilitates the construction of genomic relationship matrices (GRM) that reliably model the additive genetic relationships among individuals. This is essential for improving the predictive accuracy of genomic selection methods such as GBLUP and Bayesian variable selection approaches [38]. The consistency of LD estimates across multiple simulation replicates further confirms the robustness of the simulation framework and lends credibility to the estimated genomic parameters. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the simulation design employed in this study successfully reproduces biologically realistic LD patterns, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the results to practical breeding and genetic research applications. This underscores the relevance of evaluating genomic prediction models under plausible genetic architectures.

Goodness of fit

Although the primary objective of our study was to evaluate the predictive performance of different models using PCC, MAE, and MSE, we also examined the coefficient of determination (R2) in the reference population as a measure of model goodness of fit. Interestingly, some models showed relatively low R2 values across various genetic architectures (particularly under certain levels of additive and dominance heritabilities) yet demonstrated high predictive performance. This apparent discrepancy highlights a critical distinction between goodness of fit and predictive ability. A model may fit the training data poorly (low R2) but still generalize well to unseen data if it captures the underlying genetic signal relevant to prediction. Conversely, models with high R2 may be prone to overfitting and perform poorly on independent samples. Similar findings have been reported in previous genomic prediction studies (e.g., [24, 63]), where R2 was not always indicative of true predictive utility.

The analysis of model goodness of fit showed that increasing dominance heritability at all three levels of additive heritability led to higher R2 values across all parametric and ML models. This indicates the positive effect of total genetic variance (or broad-sense heritability) on model fit. Sahebalam et al. (2024b) [64], in a simulation study, reported that increasing heritability improved model goodness of fit and that the model fitted on the data required a smaller regularization parameter to penalize regression coefficients. Parametric models such as GBLUP, BayesB, and BayesL consistently provided higher R2 values across different genetic architectures, especially under conditions dominated by additive effects. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that parametric models, due to their linear structure and assumption of normally distributed effects, are more suitable for modeling additive genetic variance [65, 66]. On the other hand, some ML models such as FFNN showed better R2 values than other ML methods under purely additive scenarios. However, the goodness of fit of this model remained lower than that of parametric models. It is noteworthy that under the same genetic architecture, despite higher R2 values than RF and SVM, the FFNN model showed lower prediction accuracy (based on PCC) at moderate and high levels of additive heritability. Furthermore, in the additive plus dominance deviation model, the RF method achieved higher R2 values than other ML models across all levels of additive and dominance heritabilities. This advantage may be attributed to the ability of RF to capture complex patterns and non-additive interactions, especially when dominance variance accounts for a substantial portion of the genetic variability. Although the FFNN model demonstrated relatively high prediction accuracy (as indicated by PCCGEGV), it yielded the lowest R2 values in certain scenarios. This inconsistency may stem from model overfitting, heightened sensitivity to noise or outliers, or a structural mismatch between the model and the underlying genetic variance components. Meanwhile, although ML models showed lower R2 values compared to parametric models in all scenarios, they achieved higher PCCGEGV under the additive plus dominance deviation model across all levels of additive and dominance heritabilities. This is likely due to the ability of such models to learn complex nonlinear relationships that improve prediction accuracy, even if they are not fully aligned with the variance structure of the training data. Therefore, in genomic prediction, high prediction accuracy does not always imply a good model fit, particularly in high-dimensional data settings where overfitting and limited generalizability may occur. This apparent contradiction has also been reported in previous studies [63, 67].

In purely additive genetic architectures, the lower R2 values observed for the FFNN compared to parametric models can be explained by differences in model structure and variance representation. Parametric models are explicitly formulated to capture linear additive genetic variance and therefore achieve a more efficient decomposition of phenotypic variance within the reference population. In contrast, FFNN possess greater structural flexibility and allocate part of their modeling capacity to representing potential nonlinear patterns, even when such patterns are weak or absent. As a result, a smaller proportion of the phenotypic variance is explained in a strictly additive manner, leading to reduced goodness-of-fit. Nevertheless, FFNN consistently showed higher R2 values than other machine learning methods such as RF and SVM. This suggests that FFNNs are better suited to approximate dominant linear additive effects within high-dimensional genomic data, whereas tree-based and kernel-based models may be less efficient at explaining variance when the underlying genetic architecture is predominantly additive. Under additive plus dominance deviation genetic architectures, parametric models consistently exhibited higher R2 values than ML approaches within the reference population. This is expected, as these models explicitly incorporate dominance effects through predefined genomic relationship matrices, enabling an efficient decomposition of additive and dominance variance components. Among ML methods, the FFNN generally achieved higher R2 values than SVM across most dominance scenarios. This advantage can be attributed to the layered structure of FFNN, which allows for flexible combinations of marker effects and facilitates the implicit representation of dominance-related nonlinearities. However, the FFNN model showed lower R2 values compared to RF under dominance architectures. RF inherently capture complex conditional and interaction effects through hierarchical splits, making them particularly effective at modeling dominance deviations without requiring explicit parameterization. In contrast, FFNN may require more complex architectures or extensive tuning to fully represent dominance variance, leading to comparatively reduced goodness of fit under these scenarios.

The observed discrepancy between R2 and PCC values arises because R2 was calculated as the goodness-of-fit between predicted and observed values within the reference population, whereas PCC was calculated using an independent target population to assess predictive accuracy. Consequently, FFNN may show higher R2 yet lower PCC compared to RF and SVM at moderate and high levels of additive heritability. This distinction reflects standard practices in genomic prediction studies and highlights the importance of evaluating prediction performance on independent data.

Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) and Cohen's d effect size

As expected, the PCC of all the methods studied increased with increasing heritability in the purely additive model, which is consistent with previous findings [68–70]. High heritability indicates a high contribution of genetic effects to phenotypic variance, which reduces the role of environmental factors and consequently reduces the error variance, which in turn increases prediction accuracy [71].

In purely additive scenarios, parametric approaches such as GBLUP, BayesB and BayesL exhibited superior predictive performance, reflected by higher PCCGEBV compared to ML algorithms. This is likely because parametric models are specifically designed to capture linear additive genetic effects, which align closely with the underlying genetic architecture in these settings. Their underlying assumptions allow for efficient estimation of genetic values and contribute to greater stability across Monte Carlo replicates. In contrast, ML methods, such as RF, SVM, and FFNN, are tailored to detect complex, nonlinear, and non-additive interactions, which are minimal or absent under strictly additive conditions. As a result, these models may be prone to overfitting or show higher variability when applied to simpler genetic architectures, ultimately reducing their predictive accuracy. Therefore, the relative advantage of parametric models in additive scenarios underscores the importance of aligning model complexity with the genetic architecture of the trait under investigation. Among ML models, the highest PCCGEBV was shown by FFNN model at low heritability level, RF at medium heritability, and SVM at high heritability.

Sahebalam et al. (2019) [72] reported, based on simulated data, that parametric methods (BayesA and BayesRR) performed better than ML methods such as (RF and SVM). Also, the SVM method recorded higher PCCGEBV than RF.

In contrast to the findings of the present study, Naderi et al. (2016) [73] reported that the RF algorithm outperformed GBLUP in terms of predictive performance. They found that RF outperformed GBLUP in predicting binary disease traits under simulated conditions with varying heritabilities, disease incidences, and genomic architectures, particularly at high marker densities, although RF showed greater variability in performance. This discrepancy may be largely explained by the fundamental difference in the nature of the target traits; while they focused on binary disease traits, the present study evaluated continuous traits. Such differences in trait type can substantially influence the relative performance of statistical and ML methods, due to variations in the underlying data distribution, signal-to-noise ratio, and the suitability of specific algorithms for classification versus regression tasks. Ogutu et al. (2011) [74] demonstrated that stochastic Gradient Boosting and SVM outperform RF in prediction genomic breeding value, particularly due to their ability to model complex genetic interactions such as epistasis. However, RF was useful for SNP ranking, and SVM required intensive computation for hyperparameter tuning. These findings confirm the superiority of gradient boosting and SVM methods in genomic prediction tasks. Liang et al. (2021) [75] compared the performance of Adaboost.RT, SVR, KRR, and RF against the conventional GBLUP method for predicting breeding values of growth traits (carcass weight, live weight, and eye muscle area) in Chinese Simmental cattle. Using a dataset of 1217 young bulls with 671,990 SNPs, they found that all evaluated ML methods outperformed GBLUP. This corresponds to improvements of 12.8%, 14.9%, 5.4%, and 14.4% over GBLUP for SVR, KRR, RF, and Adaboost.RT, respectively, indicating the superior predictive performance of ML algorithms in this context.

Additionally, in the additive plus dominance deviation model, increasing dominance heritability (​) was associated with reduced prediction accuracy (PCCGEGV) at moderate and high levels of additive heritability (e.g., and ), despite using a genomic prediction model that included both additive and dominance effects to capture the total genetic variance. This reduction in prediction performance may be due to the model's limitations in fully accounting for complex non-additive genetic architectures or higher-order interactions beyond dominance, which can reduce the accuracy of genetic value estimates. Conversely, in conditions of low additive heritability (), increasing dominance heritability improved PCCGEGV, likely due to an increase in the proportion of genetic variance exploitable in the model by accounting for dominance effects. These findings are consistent with previous studies that emphasize the importance of modeling non-additive effects, especially dominance, to improve genomic prediction accuracy [39, 76, 77].

Sahebalam et al. (2025) [78] in a simulation study with 6 chromosomes, heritability of 0.4 and different values of dominance variance showed that the conventional GBLUP method had higher PCCGEBV and PCCGEGV than the SVM model in all different scenarios of dominance variance. They also reported that with increasing dominance variance, the PCCGEBV of both GBLUP and SVM methods increased. The first and second parts of their results were consistent with the results of the present study when the additive heritability was high and when the additive heritability was low and medium, respectively.

In another study, using a simulated trait with 20 QTLs, it was shown that dominance and epistasis gene activity reduced the prediction accuracy of genomic evaluation methods. They also reported that the low PCCGEGV under non-additive effects could be due to the fact that dominance and epistatic variance are nested within the additive variance. Therefore, the contribution of additive variance to total genetic variance decreases while error variance remains constant [79]. In addition, researchers reported that the PCCGEGV of both parametric and ML methods decreased when gene action was more complex [80].

Another key finding of this study was that, with increasing dominance heritability, the prediction accuracy of ML models surpassed that of parametric models such as GBLUP and Bayesian methods. However, when dominance heritability was zero, parametric methods significantly outperformed ML models. These results suggest that ML models may better capture complex and non-additive genetic architectures, whereas parametric models perform more effectively when the genetic architecture is predominantly additive. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that ML approaches can effectively model complex genetic interactions and epistasis, thereby improving prediction accuracy for traits influenced by non-additive effects [81–83]. Conversely, parametric models like GBLUP have consistently been shown to perform well within classical genomic selection frameworks, especially for traits with predominantly additive genetic architectures, supporting the observed advantage of these models in the absence of dominance heritability [1, 9].

Abdollahi-Arpanahi et al. (2020) [23] compared the performance of ensemble learning methods (RF and GB) and deep learning algorithms (MLP and CNN) with traditional models (GBLUP and BayesB) for predicting quantitative traits in Holstein cattle. Both simulated datasets with varying genetic architectures and real data from 11,790 bulls were utilized. The results indicated that traditional models performed better under purely additive genetic effects, whereas ML methods, particularly GB, outperformed conventional approaches when non-additive effects and a small number of QTNs were involved. This study highlights the importance of genetic architecture and the number of influential loci in the predictive accuracy of models.

Gianola et al. (2011) [27] evaluated the predictive performance of Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) for three quantitative traits, such as milk, fat, and protein yield, in Jersey dairy cows. Using a dataset of 297 cows and 35,798 SNPs, the study found that BNNs with at least two neurons in the hidden layer exhibited superior performance. Bayesian regularization effectively reduced the number of weights, mitigating overfitting to some extent. However, overfitting remained an issue in the training set, as indicated by high correlations between observed and predicted values (0.90–0.95) during training, which declined substantially in the testing set.

In another study, a multilayer perceptron neural network was developed to predict performance and fertility traits in 92 Holstein heifers. The optimized model, consisting of three layers and 11 neurons in the hidden layer, achieved high coefficients of determination for traits such as fat percentage, protein percentage, cheese merit, and fertility rates, demonstrating the strong predictive capability of the neural network for complex phenotypic traits [84].

The lower performance of FFNN under purely additive genetic architectures can be explained by the interaction between model complexity and the underlying genetic signal. In scenarios with low additive heritability, where the signal-to-noise ratio is reduced, FFNN exhibited superior performance compared to other ML approaches, likely due to its ability to capture weak, complex, or non-linear patterns that are not efficiently modeled by simpler approaches. However, as additive heritability increased, this advantage gradually diminished. Under high additive heritability, the genetic architecture becomes predominantly linear, allowing parametric methods and other ML models to more effectively exploit additive relationships. In such cases, the high flexibility of FFNN may result in overparameterization without providing additional predictive benefit, ultimately leading to reduced prediction accuracy. These findings suggest that the relative performance of FFNN is highly dependent on the complexity of the underlying genetic architecture, with non-linear models offering advantages primarily in low-heritability or more complex scenarios. This observation is consistent with previous genomic prediction studies indicating that deep learning models do not consistently outperform simpler models unless substantial non-additive or complex genetic effects are present.

In this study, we used effect size to determine the distance between the prediction accuracy through PCC. Effect size is used as a numerical scale of relationship or difference between variables, while the comparison of the p-value with the probability of type I error (α) is a criterion for confirming or rejecting the null hypothesis, and the largeness or smallness of the probability value does not play a role in the hypothesis test. The p-value is dependent on the sample size, while the effect size is independent of it, so the effect size can be considered a more appropriate tool in the analysis and interpretation of statistical tests and understanding the strength of the difference between two groups. In previous studies, Cohen's d effect size was used to assess the gap between the predictive accuracy of different statistical models in genomic evaluation [14, 64].

Performance stability

In order to evaluate the stability of prediction accuracy across simulation replicates, we computed the CV of the PCC for each method. The CV serves as a normalized measure of relative variability, independent of the scale of the values. In our analysis, methods with CV values below 15% were interpreted as producing stable and consistent predictions, indicating that 100 Monte Carlo replicates were sufficient. This threshold is in line with recommendations from previous methodological literature, where CVs under 15% are commonly accepted as reflecting adequate reliability of estimates [85].

As observed in the results, increasing additive heritability led to a decrease in the CV across all studied methods, indicating improved stability in prediction accuracy. This suggests that stronger genetic signals reduce variability among Monte Carlo replicates by facilitating clearer pattern recognition by models. Therefore, higher heritability yields more reliable and consistent prediction estimates. These findings are consistent with previous research on genomic prediction and statistical modeling [59], and align with recent studies highlighting the effects of heritability and sample size on prediction accuracy in livestock populations [86].

Across all examined levels of additive heritability (low: 0.05; medium: 0.2; high: 0.5), increasing dominance heritability was associated with elevated CV for the PCCGEGV. This pattern held consistently across all prediction methods evaluated, including both parametric and machine learning approaches. These findings suggest that the presence of dominance effects, which involve complex nonlinear interactions and can account for a substantial portion of the total genetic variance, may undermine the stability of predictive models in Monte Carlo replicates. Notably, despite accurate modeling of dominance variance, increases in dominance heritability still resulted in higher CVs and reduced estimate stability, reflecting the intrinsic complexity and nonlinear nature of these effects [77, 87].

It is noteworthy that when additive heritability was high (0.5) and dominance heritability was also substantial (0.2), all methods demonstrated CVs below 15%, indicating stable and reliable predictions. In contrast, at low (0.05) and medium (0.2) levels of additive heritability with the same dominance heritability (0.2), CVs increased to approximately 24% and 20%, respectively. This pattern suggests that under conditions of weak additive genetic signals, dominance heritability constitutes a larger fraction of total genetic variance, thereby increasing variability in model performance. These results underscore the critical role of additive heritability in stabilizing genetic predictions in the presence of dominance variance. Similar observations have been reported by Toro and Varona (2010) [88], who demonstrated that increased dominance variance can decrease prediction reliability unless properly modeled. Additionally, recent findings by Endelman (2023) [89] confirm that incorporating directional dominance effects into multi-environment genomic prediction models improves both accuracy and prediction stability.

At low additive heritability (0.05), increasing dominance heritability did not significantly affect the CV of the PCC; however, the mere presence of dominance effects elevated CV across all methods. This indicates that when additive heritability is very low, dominance acts as a disruptive and uncertainty-enhancing factor, as models struggle to accurately capture these non-additive effects. Therefore, even without increased dominance variance, its presence as a complex non-additive factor contributes to instability in prediction accuracy. These findings align with previous reports emphasizing that dominance effects can reduce the stability of prediction models, particularly under low additive heritability conditions [76, 87].

Mean absolute error (MAE) and mean square error (MSE)

Prediction error analysis (MAE and MSE) across different levels of additive and dominance heritabilities highlights the significant impact of genetic architecture on the performance of both parametric and ML models. In the purely additive model, increasing additive heritability was associated with elevated MAE and MSE values, consistent with previous findings [63, 83]. This pattern may reflect increased unpredictability due to greater genetic variance or overfitting in certain models, especially when models fail to effectively exploit complex additive genetic information. Adding dominance effects to the model, despite increasing the total genetic variance, did not consistently improve prediction error. The observed decrease in MAE and concurrent increase in MSE with rising dominance heritability in some scenarios suggests that modeling non-additive effects (particularly in the absence of epistatic interactions) may weaken prediction accuracy. This is in line with previous studies [77, 87], and may be due to increased model complexity, unstable estimates, or large variance in dominance effect estimation. On the other hand, ML models such as FFNN and SVM outperformed parametric methods under low additive heritability, likely due to their ability to capture complex and nonlinear patterns, especially when additive genetic variance is limited and dominance or other non-additive components become more prominent [29, 33]. However, at higher levels of additive heritability, parametric models like GBLUP and BayesB exhibited more stable and competitive performance, possibly due to their linear structure aligning well with the additive nature of traits in such scenarios [65]. Srivastava et al. (2021) [90] evaluated the performance of RF, XGB, and SVM in predicting four traits in Hanwoo cattle. Their results showed that XGB achieved higher PCCs than GBLUP for carcass weight and marbling score, but lower PCCs for backfat thickness and eye muscle area. Additionally, GBLUP exhibited the lowest MSE across all traits. Despite the competitive performance of XGB and SVM, the authors concluded that ML methods did not offer a significant advantage over GBLUP.

These results emphasize the importance of aligning model structure with the type and magnitude of genetic variance. They demonstrate that increasing model complexity without regard to the underlying genetic architecture does not necessarily lead to improved predictive performance.

Conclusion

Feed-forward neural networks (FFNN) demonstrated robust strong predictive performance in non-additive genetic architectures, often outperforming other ML approaches such as SVM and RF, especially in traits with high heritability. While parametric methods excelled at higher heritability, FFNN maintained lower mean absolute and squared errors, capturing complex, non-linear marker–phenotype relationships. These results highlight FFNN as a versatile and reliable tool for genomic prediction, improving accuracy and stability in breeding programs even when traits exhibit low heritability and dominance effects.
