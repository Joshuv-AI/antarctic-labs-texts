R-5780, a SagA-engineered Lactococcus lactis, is a safe oral synthetic-biology microbial therapy that potentiates PD-1 blockade
Authors: Miaki Fukuhara, Bikash Sahay, Gary R Fanger, Christian Furlan Freguia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) have transformed cancer therapy, yet response rates remain limited across solid tumors. Modulation of the gut microbiome has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance immunotherapy efficacy. R-5780 is an engineered Lactococcus lactis strain expressing the peptidoglycan hydrolase Secreted Antigen A (SagA), which generates muramyl dipeptide (MDP), a natural ligand of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain 2 (NOD2) receptor involved in innate immune activation and antigen-presenting cell maturation. Therapeutic potential of R-5780 was assessed in vivo murine models. Using the CT26 murine colorectal carcinoma model, oral administration of R-5780 with anti-PD-1 significantly inhibited tumor growth in both prophylactic and therapeutic settings compared to ICI therapy alone. Mechanistic studies revealed that R-5780 enhanced dendritic-cell activation and promoted a pro-inflammatory tumor microenvironment characterized by elevated interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β). Multiplex cytokine profiling and flow cytometry further showed that R-5780 reduced CD8+ T-cell exhaustion, with decreased expression of PD-1, LAG-3, and TIM-3 on intratumoral T cells. In murine toxicology studies, R-5780 was well tolerated, with no adverse events or systemic inflammation observed following oral administration. Together, these results identify R-5780 as a safe and potent NOD2 activator that synergizes with PD-1 blockade to enhance antitumor immunity and support its advancement toward clinical evaluation.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00262-026-04480-2.Keywords: PD-1 blockade, Gut microbiome, Live biotherapeutic products, NOD2 signaling, T-cell exhaustion, Preclinical toxicology

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade have been revolutionary in oncology, highlighting the ability to harness the immune system to fight cancer [1]. Combination strategies pairing PD-1 inhibitors with other checkpoint targets, such as CTLA-4, or with conventional therapies such as radiation, continue to be a major focus of clinical development [2] [3]. Despite these advances, ICIs alone achieve meaningful responses in only ~ 30% of patients due to factors such as terminal T-cell exhaustion, poor T-cell infiltration, and additional mechanisms of immune resistance [4].

Over the past several years, the gut microbiome has emerged as a critical determinant of cancer immunotherapy efficacy, capable of modulating inflammation, immune activation, and tumor metabolism [5, 6]. Specific bacterial taxa have been shown to influence the anti-cancer response by modulating immune responses, producing key metabolites, altering the tumor microenvironment, and directly impacting the effectiveness of therapies [7]. These findings have catalyzed efforts to leverage the microbiome as a strategy to improve clinical outcomes with ICIs.

Among the microbial pathways implicated in immunotherapy responsiveness, bacterial peptidoglycan remodeling has recently gained attention as a potent regulator of host immunity. We previously demonstrated that the Enterococcus-secreted Antigen A (SagA), a conserved NlpC/p60 peptidoglycan hydrolase, generates immunostimulatory peptidoglycan fragments, or muropeptides, leading to enhanced efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors through Nod2-dependent signaling [6, 8, 9]. Muropeptides are known to activate the cytosolic receptor NOD2, driving NF-κB and MAPK signaling, pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and dendritic cell maturation with upregulation of CD80 and CD86 [10]. In mouse tumor models, oral administration of Enterococcus. Faecium (E. Faecium), which endogenously expresses SagA, or SagA-expressing engineered probiotics, enhanced anti-PD-L1 therapy, leading to increased intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration and heightened antitumor activity in a NOD2-dependent manner [10].

Building on this mechanistic framework, we further demonstrated that SagA-mediated enhancement of checkpoint blockade is portable and does not require native Enterococcus species, which are unsuitable for therapeutic use due to vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) concerns. Heterologous expression of SagA in the food-grade probiotic Lactococcus lactis (called R-5780) was sufficient to reproduce the antitumor effects observed with E. faecium. Only strains expressing catalytically active, secreted SagA improved anti-PD-L1 efficacy, underscoring the requirement for NlpC/p60 hydrolase activity and extracellular generation of NOD2-stimulatory muropeptides.

These findings established SagA as a novel immunomodulatory, anti-cancer potentiator effector and validated R-5780 as a viable live biotherapeutic for mucosal delivery of peptidoglycan-derived immune signals. The present work details the translational studies supporting advancement of R-5780 toward human clinical evaluation.

Results

Synergistic effects of R-5780 and checkpoint inhibitors as cancer treatment

We have previously shown that Lactococcus lactis engineered to express the peptidoglycan hydrolase SagA (R-5780) significantly enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy, and the observed activity was driven by SagA [10].

To enable clinical translation of this therapeutic approach, Investigational New Drug (IND)-enabling studies were required to define appropriate dosing, identify pharmacodynamic biomarkers, and further elucidate the mechanism of action. Accordingly, a new set of studies was conducted to characterize the antitumor effects of R-5780 in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors and to gain deeper insight into the SagA-mediated immunomodulatory pathway. For these studies, a Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compatible manufacturing process was established to produce R-5780 as a freeze-dried bacterial formulation suitable for oral administration, designed to mimic the intended clinical route of administration and using material representative of clinical lots. This formulation ensured stability, dosing precision, and reproducible delivery of viable bacteria.

We employed the CT26 subcutaneous colon carcinoma murine model, which is only partially responsive to PD-1 blockade. To confirm the synergy between the clinical formulation of R-5780 and immune checkpoint inhibition, mice were administered R-5780 1E9 Colony Forming Unit (CFU)/dose by oral administration twice weekly beginning one week prior to tumor inoculation and continuing throughout the study. Anti-PD-1 was administered intraperitoneally (I.P.) at a dose of 10 mg/kg twice weekly for a total of three injections, starting when tumors reached approximately 50 mm3. As expected, PD-1 blockade alone produced only modest tumor control, consistent with previous reports that CT26 tumors respond poorly to PD-1 monotherapy [11]. R-5780 monotherapy did not reduce tumor growth; however, co-administration of R-5780 with PD-1 blockade significantly suppressed tumor progression compared with either treatment alone (Fig. 1a). By the end of the study, Day 28, PD-1 treated mice showed tumor volume of ~ 900 mm3, while mice treated with R-5780 in combination with anti-PD-1 showed a significant reduction in tumor volume (180 mm3, p < 0.05, Fig. 1b). In contrast, the wild-type Lactococcus lactis strain failed to enhance PD-1 efficacy, confirming that SagA expression is required for the observed therapeutic effect.

The next set of studies was designed to understand the minimal effective dose (MED) and inform the doses to be tested in human trials. A dose–response study was conducted using escalating oral doses (1E6 to 1E9 CFU) of R-5780 in combination with anti-PD-1 in the CT26 model (Fig. 1c). Tumor growth inhibition was observed across all doses compared with anti-PD-1 monotherapy, with maximal tumor growth suppression achieved at 1E8 and 1E9 CFU. Lower doses (1E6 and 1E7 CFU) produced only partial tumor control, indicating a dose–response effect for mediated immune activation. The anti-PD-1 + R-5780 (1E8 and 1E9 CFU) groups maintained significantly smaller tumor volumes throughout the study and at Day 28 (p < 0.05 vs. anti-PD-1 alone). These results suggest 1E8 CFU as the minimal effective dose in this murine model.

Because earlier preclinical experiments involved dosing R-5780 prior to tumor implantation, a scenario not reflective of potential clinical use; we next evaluated administration of R-5780 after tumor formation. R-5780 was administered therapeutically once tumors reached approximately 50 mm3, together with PD-1 blockade. In this study, R-5780 was dosed twice weekly for the duration of the experiment, and anti-PD-1 antibody was administered intraperitoneally three times in total (10 mg/kg, I.P). Tumor volumes at Day 28 showed comparable inhibition between preventative and therapeutic regimens (Fig. 1d), indicating that R-5780 remains effective even when delivered after tumor establishment.

Together, these findings demonstrate that R-5780 exerts robust antitumor activity across a defined therapeutic window, with 1E8 CFU representing the minimum effective dose (MED) for combination efficacy. Moreover, the comparable responses observed under both preventative and therapeutic dosing regimens confirm that R-5780 remains effective when administered after tumor establishment, supporting its translational for clinical use.

R-5780 modulates T-cell exhaustion and cytokine profiles in the tumor microenvironment

To further investigate the mechanism of action (MOA) underlying R-5780-mediated enhancement of PD-1 blockade and to identify pharmacodynamic biomarkers that could inform clinical endpoint selection. To this end, we conducted mechanistic analyses including immunophenotyping of intratumoral lymphocytes and multiplex cytokine profiling of tumor tissues (Fig. 2). We first examined classical markers of T-cell exhaustion, including PD-1, LAG-3, and TIM-3, which are upregulated on chronically stimulated or functionally impaired CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment. These markers were selected because their expression reflects the extent of inhibitory pressure limiting cytotoxic T-cell function and responsiveness to checkpoint therapy [12, 13]. Flow cytometric analysis of the tumor microenvironment revealed that R-5780 monotherapy alone reduced the frequency of the exhaustion markers PD-1 + , LAG-3 + , and TIM-3 + on CD8 + T cells compared to Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) controls (p < 0.05), indicating that SagA-dependent NOD2 activation can relieve inhibitory signaling and sustain T-cell functionality. The reduction in T-cell exhaustion markers was also evident when R-5780 was used in combination with anti-PD-1 treatment (Fig. 2a). Collectively, these data suggest that R-5780 alleviates immune suppression and releases the brakes on effector T cells, thereby potentiating PD-1 blockade.

Cytokine analysis of tumors further supports these findings. Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) levels were elevated in the PD-1/R-5780 combination group, consistent with enhanced local immune activation, whereas Interleukin-6 (IL-6) was mostly increased in tumors from R-5780-treated mice (Fig. 2b). IL-6 is a multifunctional cytokine that is widely recognized for its involvement in immune activation and inflammatory responses, including recruitment and activation of immune cells at sites of insult [27, 28]. Although IL-6 is often described in the context of pro-inflammatory signaling, its upregulation in this setting likely reflects localized immune priming and activation of antigen-presenting cells within the tumor microenvironment rather than evidence of chronic or systemic inflammation. [14]. Anti-inflammatory mediators, including Interleukin-27 (IL-27) and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), remained largely unchanged across treatment groups. Collectively, these results indicate that R-5780 promotes a locally activated yet balanced tumor microenvironment that supports T-cell function and potentiates PD-1 blockade without inducing systemic inflammation.

Together, these findings suggest that R-5780 synergizes with PD-1 blockade by fostering a locally pro-inflammatory environment, reinvigorating exhausted CD8 + T cells, and enabling sustained antitumor effector responses.

R-5780 has a safe and tolerable profile in mice

Safety is a critical parameter in the transition of any live biotherapeutic product from preclinical to clinical development. Before advancing R-5780 toward human evaluation, it was therefore essential to establish a comprehensive non-clinical safety and toxicology profile to better define tolerability of R-5780. First, during the murine cancer studies, mice were followed for any sign of macroscopic toxicity. Cageside macroscopic evaluation of mice receiving R-5780 with and without Anti-PD-1 therapy showed no effect on animal posture, fur condition, or any other behavior indicative of toxicities. In addition, no loss of weight was recorded for any animals (Fig. 3c) throughout the studies, suggesting that oral administration of R-5780 alone or in combination with ICI therapy does not lead to unforeseen macroscopic toxicity in mice.

As part of the IND package, formal toxicology assessments were initiated to meet regulatory expectations and ensure clinical readiness. Two safety toxicology studies were conducted, a non-Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) single dose, and a GLP 28-day repeat-dose assessment. In the single-dose study, mixed-sex C57BL/6 J mice received a single oral dose of R-5780 of 1E10 CFU, followed by longitudinal assessment of clinical parameters and systemic immune activation for 1 week. In this study, no significant changes in body weight were observed between R-5780-treated and vehicle groups, indicating good overall tolerability (Fig. 3b). Comprehensive serum biochemistry and hematology panels revealed no treatment-related abnormalities in hepatic or renal function markers: aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), urea, total protein, albumin, globulin, and phosphorus. or hematologic parameters: white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), red cell distribution width (RDW), and platelet count (PLT) (Fig. 3d–e). Systemic blood cytokine levels, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), remained comparable between groups (Fig. 3f). Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) analysis of fecal DNA showed that R-5780 transiently passed through the gastrointestinal tract, peaking at 6 h post-administration and returning to baseline by 24 h, confirming its non-colonizing behavior (Fig. 3a). Notably, real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) performed on blood samples collected 24 h and 7 days after dose resulted in no detectable R-5780 DNA, further confirming drug confinement to the gastrointestinal compartment (Supplementary Fig. 1).

R-5780 is well tolerated and does not exacerbate intestinal inflammation in DSS-induced colitis

We additionally performed a 28-day repeat-dose GLP toxicity study in which mice received oral R-5780 daily for 28 days at dose of 1E9/dose and 1E8 CFU/dose. Also, in this study, no significant differences were observed in body weight, cytokines, serum biochemistry, hematology, or qPCR clearance compared with vehicle controls (Supplementary Fig. 2a–e). Together, these results demonstrate that R-5780 is well tolerated following both single and repeated dosing, clears transiently from the gastrointestinal tract, and does not induce systemic toxicity or inflammation.

Given the association of immune checkpoint inhibitors with immune-mediated colitis, and the local gastrointestinal activity of orally delivered R-5780, it was important to confirm that repeated administration does not induce or exacerbate intestinal inflammation. In the 28-day GLP toxicology studies, no gastrointestinal adverse findings were observed, and histopathological evaluation showed normal mucosal architecture, supporting good local tolerability. Next, we evaluated the effects of R-5780 under inflammatory conditions, using the dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)–induced colitis model, a well-established system for inducing mucosal irritation, hence mimicking immune-mediated exacerbation of gut injury. In this model, mice received an oral R-5780 (or vehicle) three days prior to colitis induction and continuing throughout the DSS exposure and post-DSS recovery period, dosed three times per week at therapeutic levels. Colitis was induced by administration of 2.5% DSS in drinking water for five days, followed by a return to normal water. Administration of R-5780 did not exacerbate colitis and instead improved colonic inflammation, as demonstrated by histopathological analysis (Fig. 4). Collectively, these studies establish a favorable safety profile for R-5780, demonstrating that oral administration is well tolerated and is compatible with therapeutic use in immune checkpoint inhibitor-based treatment settings.

Discussion

The gut microbiome is increasingly recognized as a key regulator of antitumor immunity and a determinant of response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) [6, 9]. Specific commensal bacteria, including Enterococcus faecium, have been shown to enhance T-cell-mediated tumor control by augmenting antigen presentation and promoting a pro-inflammatory tumor microenvironment [10]. This mechanism is further supported by work showing that synthetic NOD2 agonists can recapitulate microbiome-driven enhancement of checkpoint therapy. Griffin et al. developed N-arylpyrazole desmuramyl dipeptide analogs that phenocopied MDP, with their lead compound (TT030) enhancing anti-PD-L1 efficacy in a strictly NOD2-dependent manner. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of treated tumors revealed expansion of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and enrichment of NF-κB and IL-1β signaling programs, mirroring the inflammatory remodeling observed with SagA-mediated peptidoglycan processing. These findings underscore NOD2 as a central, druggable link between microbiome signals and improved checkpoint responsiveness [15]. Our findings extend this body of work by demonstrating that microbial activation of the receptor NOD2 through SagA-derived muramyl dipeptide (MDP) can modulate the tumor microenvironment and enhance the efficacy of PD-1 blockade in vivo.

R-5780, an engineered Lactococcus lactis strain recombinantly expressing SagA from a genome integrated expression cassette, exhibited a favorable in vivo safety profile characterized by transient gastrointestinal transit, absence of systemic dissemination, and lack of hematologic or biochemical abnormalities. This non-colonizing, non-pathogenic behavior satisfies the regulatory criteria for live biotherapeutic products and reinforces the suitability of L. lactis as a chassis for mucosal immunomodulation [16, 17]. The absence of systemic cytokine release further confirms that its immune activity is localized, distinguishing it from conventional adjuvants that frequently induce systemic inflammation and toxicity [18].

In the CT26 colorectal carcinoma model, which is moderately responsive to PD-1 blockade [19, 20], R-5780 synergized with anti-PD-1 therapy to significantly inhibit tumor growth. This synergy required SagA expression, consistent with prior reports that bacterial-derived MDP acts as a potent NOD2 agonist promoting dendritic cell (DC) maturation and T-cell priming [10]. Mechanistically, R-5780 shows reduction markers of T-cell exhaustion as shown by decreased expression of PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3 on CD8+ T cells suggesting R-5780 preserves effector function within the tumor microenvironment. While R-5780 downregulates immune checkpoint constraints and promotes a permissive immune environment, it does not elicit antitumor efficacy as a monotherapy, consistent with a mechanism that requires tumor antigen presentation to initiate effective immune-mediated tumor clearance. It should be noted that although PD-1 flow cytometry measurements in anti-PD-1-treated mice may be influenced by antibody-mediated epitope masking, the observation that R-5780 alone reduces PD-1 expression supports a bona fide biological effect of R-5780 on T-cell exhaustion.

These results are consistent with studies showing that microbial NOD2 signaling enhances cross-presentation and supports CD8+ T-cell activation via MyD88-dependent cytokine networks [21, 22]. Beyond DC activation, NOD2 engagement likely modulates type I interferon and pathways that support T-cell recruitment and cytotoxic activity [23]. The convergence of these innate pathways could explain the broad immune activation observed with R-5780, positioning NOD2 as a key node linking microbial sensing to adaptive immune potentiation. Importantly, despite this localized immune activation, R-5780 demonstrated an excellent safety and tolerability profile in all preclinical studies. Oral administration of R-5780 did not elicit systemic inflammation, as serum cytokines remained at baseline and no treatment-related abnormalities were observed in hematology, serum biochemistry, or body weight. qPCR analyses confirmed that R-5780 behaves as a non-colonizing organism, transiently transiting the gastrointestinal tract without evidence of systemic dissemination. Repeat-dose toxicology studies further showed that daily oral administration for 28 days did not induce hepatic, renal, or hematologic toxicity, nor did it exacerbate inflammation in the gastrointestinal mucosa, including under DSS-challenged conditions. Collectively, these findings indicate that R-5780 delivers targeted mucosal NOD2 stimulation sufficient to potentiate antitumor immunity while avoiding intestinal as well as systemic immune activation or toxicity. This favorable safety profile supports the translational potential of R-5780 as an orally delivered microbial adjuvant to enhance checkpoint blockade in cancer patients.

Cytokine profiling further supported this pro-inflammatory. Tumors from R-5780-treated mice showed elevated IL-6 and IL-1β, cytokines associated with local immune activation and antigen-presenting-cell recruitment. Although IL-6 has dual roles in tumor biology, recent studies demonstrate that localized IL-6 signaling can support antitumor immunity by promoting T-cell priming, survival, and tumor infiltration rather than chronic inflammatory signaling [27, 28]. Furthermore, blockade of IL-6 signaling has been shown to impair vaccine-mediated antitumor efficacy and reduce intratumoral T-cell accumulation. [24, 27, 28]. Systemic IL-6 and TNF-α levels remained unchanged, underscoring the safety of localized mucosal stimulation. These findings indicate that R-5780 orchestrates a tumor-confined, balanced immune activation conducive to PD-1 mediated T-cell reinvigoration.

From a translational perspective, R-5780’s oral delivery offers significant advantages. Live microbial therapeutics that mediate targeted activation of innate immune sensors without long-term colonization minimize risks of dysbiosis and horizontal gene transfer. Oral administration may also enable flexible combinations with checkpoint inhibitors or other immunotherapies, enhancing systemic immunity through gut–tumor immune crosstalk. Clinical observations support this concept: antibiotic use that disrupts gut microbes correlates with reduced ICI efficacy [25], while fecal microbiota transplantation from responder patients can restore checkpoint sensitivity [26]. R-5780 therefore represents a rationally designed, defined alternative to empiric microbial therapies.

An important translational milestone for R-5780 is its progression into first-in-human testing. A Phase I clinical trial (NCT06398418) is currently evaluating R-5780 in patients with advanced solid tumors previously treated with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. This study will assess safety, tolerability, and exploratory clinical activity and immune biomarkers, including fecal and peripheral cytokine profiles, NOD2-responsive gene expression, and T-cell activation signatures. The trial builds directly on the preclinical foundation presented here, leveraging R-5780’s favorable safety, transient GI clearance, and localized immune activation, to define dose levels and immune readouts for combination therapy. The dose levels selected for the first-in-human Phase 1 study of R-5780 DP were based on an integrated assessment of the available preclinical pharmacology, pharmacokinetic, biodistribution, and toxicology data. Dose selection was informed by the no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) established in the repeat-dose toxicology study, the absence of detectable systemic exposure, the observed gastrointestinal clearance profile, and the pharmacologically active dose range identified in murine efficacy studies. The clinical dose range was then selected using body-weight-based conversion from mouse to human, with the starting dose chosen to provide an appropriate safety margin relative to the toxicology NOAEL while enabling evaluation of doses expected to span the projected pharmacologically active range.

Importantly, R-5780 is not being developed as a stand-alone oncology treatment but as an oral targeted immunopotentiator intended to enhance checkpoint efficacy and expand the fraction of responding patients. The ongoing clinical study incorporates longitudinal biomarker analysis to correlate R-5780 exposure with microbial muropeptide production, systemic immune modulation, and changes in T-cell phenotype. Correlative analyses will be critical for linking mechanistic findings to clinical outcomes and for establishing translational biomarkers of response.

In conclusion, this study establishes R-5780 as a safe, microbiome-derived immunomodulator that leverages NOD2 signaling to reshape the tumor microenvironment, alleviate T-cell exhaustion and potentiate PD-1 checkpoint blockade. Together these findings provide a foundation for developing next generation immunopotentiators that combine precision engineering, mucosal safety, and clinical scalability to enhance cancer immunotherapy outcomes.

Materials and methods

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) Detection of R-5780

For single-dose pharmacokinetic and toxicology studies, fecal pellets and whole blood were collected from C57BL/6 mice at predefined time points following oral administration of R-5780. Fresh fecal pellets were collected directly from individual animals into sterile, pre-weighed tubes and immediately frozen on dry ice, then stored at − 80 °C until processing. Whole blood was collected via terminal cardiac puncture or submandibular bleed into Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-coated tubes and stored on ice prior to DNA extraction.

Total DNA was extracted from fecal samples using a bead-beating-based microbial DNA extraction kit QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen,) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including an initial mechanical lysis step to ensure efficient disruption of Gram-positive bacteria. Briefly, ~ 50–100 mg of fecal material was resuspended in lysis buffer, subjected to bead beating, and processed through spin columns to obtain purified DNA. For blood samples, genomic DNA was isolated using QIAamp Fast DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen) extraction kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentrations were measured by spectrophotometry, and all samples were diluted in nuclease-free water to a standardized working concentration for qPCR.

R-5780 levels were quantified using a strain-specific TaqMan assay targeting a unique SagA-expressing Lactococcus lactis locus. Primers and probe were designed to selectively amplify R-5780 without cross-reactivity to murine genomic DNA or commensal microbiota. qPCR reactions were performed on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using TaqMan chemistry. The following qPCR primers were used to quantify R-5780 F 5′-TCCTGAGCAGTCAAATCCAC-3′ R 5′-CAACACCAACACCAACGC-3′.

In vivo study design

All in vivo experiments were conducted using the CT26 murine colon carcinoma model in BALB/c female mice. This syngeneic model was selected due to its low-to-moderate response to PD-1 checkpoint blockade. Animals were randomized into treatment groups prior to study initiation. In therapeutics efficacy studies, randomization was performed such that mean tumor volumes were comparable between groups at treatment initiation, all tumor measurements within studies were collected by the same investigation using caliper to minimizes variability; however, different investigator performed measurements across independent studies. Tumor measurements were calculated by measuring shortest tumor diameter (width) and longest tumor diameter length implementing the volume Tumor volume (mm3) = (W2 × L)/2. Mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories and housed in individually ventilated cages under High-Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA)-filtered, specific pathogen-free conditions. Animals were maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle and provided food and water ad libitum. All animal care and procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). The CT26 murine colon carcinoma cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and was confirmed to be negative for mycoplasma prior to use.

R-5780 drug substance (DS) was manufactured at Rise Therapeutics. Lot Development-5780-039-DS was used for all studies. R-5780 DS was stored at − 20 °C and thawed at room temperature for two hours prior to reconstitution in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

The CT26 cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI) 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Fisher Scientific). Cells between passages 3 and 4 were used for all studies. A total of 300,000 CT26 cells were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of each mouse.

Anti-PD-1 antibody (CD279; clone RMP1-14, BioXCell) was administered 10 mg/kg, I.P, three times total beginning when tumors reached ~ 50 mm3.

Organ harvesting and dissociation

Tumor and spleen tissues were harvested and rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual debris and blood. Tumors were dissociated and stained for flow cytometry on the day of harvest. Tumor samples were minced into < 1 mm fragments using a scalpel and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in an enzymatic digestion solution containing Collagenase/Hyaluronidase, DNase I, and RPMI with orbital shaking. Digested tumors were quenched with RPMI, pelleted, and washed before trypan blue counting and downstream flow cytometry staining.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions from spleen, tumor, and blood were prepared as described. Half a million cells were plated per sample and stained with viability dye and Fc block for 20 min, followed by surface and intracellular antibody staining. Intracellular staining was performed using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm™ Fixation/Permeabilization Kit. Samples were acquired on an Attune NxT flow cytometer, and data were analyzed using FlowJo and GraphPad Prism.

Multiple cytokine ELISA

Tumors were dissociated into single-cell suspensions and normalized to 1 × 106 cells per sample prior to lysis. Cells were lysed in Cell Extraction Buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and incubated on ice for 30 min with intermittent mixing. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation and buffer exchanged into nuclease-free water using the Compat-Able™ Protein Assay Preparation Reagent Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cytokine concentrations were quantified using a LEGENDplex™ bead-based multiplex immunoassay (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal volumes of lysate derived from 1 × 106 cells were incubated with capture beads, followed by detection antibodies and streptavidin–phycoerythrin (SA-PE). Beads were acquired on a flow cytometer and analyzed using LEGENDplex™ software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 11.0.0). Data are presented as mean +−standard deviation. Time-course measurements, including tumor growth and body weight, were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Comparison amount three or more groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Pair wise comparison between two groups was performed suing an unpaired two-tailed Welch’s t test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

DSS acute colitis

Colitis was induced by placing 2.5% Dextran Sulfate Sodium Salt (DSS; MP Biomedicals, OH) in the drinking water for 5 days. On Day 5, DSS was removed and replaced with fresh water for the remainder of the study. R-5780 DS or vehicle control was administered beginning on Day—3, three times per week, and continued until study termination. Oral dosing was performed using a 22-gauge oral gavage needle attached to a 1 mL luer-lock syringe. Mice in the treatment group received 1.7E8 VCC in 200 μL per dose. Control mice received 200 μL of vehicle via the same route. At study termination, intestines were collected for histopathological evaluation. Tissues were arranged as Swiss rolls, fixed, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and examined by a board-certified veterinary pathologist. Histological scoring included assessment of epithelial damage, mucosal lesions, inflammatory cell infiltration, and submucosal lesions, as described below.
