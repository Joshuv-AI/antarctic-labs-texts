Defect-engineered nonstoichiometric perovskite hosting high-activity PdO sites for enhanced hydrocarbon oxidation
Authors: Jihang Yu, Zhi-Qiang Wang, Yongjun Jiang, Jiaorong Yan, Yanglong Guo, Yun Guo, Li Wang, Aiyong Wang, Sheng Dai, Feng Gao, Wangcheng Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSupported noble metal catalysts are central to industrial, environmental, and energy applications, yet precisely constructing active sites with optimized geometry remains a formidable challenge beyond conventional particle size control. Here, we introduce a perovskite-based surface defect engineering strategy that enables atomic-level regulation of noble metal active sites. By tailoring La stoichiometry in LaAlO3, we engineer three distinct surface morphologies (planar, stepped, and crater-like) that serve as templates for anchoring and replicating supported PdO nanoparticles. Among these, the step-rich PdO configuration on La0.9AlO3−δ exhibits the highest methane oxidation activity, with a linear correlation between Pd step-site density and catalytic performance. The enhancement arises from low-coordination Pd atoms at step sites, which form stable Pd–C(CH3) covalent bonds and facilitate C–H bond activation, the rate-determining step in methane oxidation. This work establishes a generalizable approach to precisely tailor noble metal active sites through perovskite surface engineering, providing a robust framework for the rational design of efficient and durable oxidation catalysts.Subject terms: Heterogeneous catalysis, Nanoparticles, Catalyst synthesis, Catalytic mechanisms

Introduction

Supported noble metal catalysts are central to industrial manufacturing, environmental protection, and energy conversion because of their unique surface chemistry and electronic properties1–3. Catalyst supports are generally classified as inert (e.g., SiO2, Al2O3) or redox-active (e.g., CeZrOx, Fe2O3, and perovskite oxides). While inert supports primarily provide mechanical stability and high surface area, redox-active supports can also participate directly in catalytic processes by storing and releasing lattice oxygen or by facilitating electron exchange with the metal or reactants4–8. For the noble metal active phase, particularly in structure-sensitive reactions, parameters such as particle size, oxidation state, surface orientation, defect structure, and metal–support interaction critically influence both catalytic performance and stability9–11. This complexity underscores the challenge and necessity of achieving rational catalyst design through precise control of metal–support interfaces.

Recent advances in wet-chemical synthesis have enabled the fabrication of metal nanoparticles with precisely controlled sizes, shapes, and surface orientations12,13. However, once these nanoparticles are supported on oxide substrates and the structure-stabilizing ligands are removed during thermal treatments, they often undergo severe structural destabilization. Metal–support interactions (MSI) offer a promising route to modulate active-site properties: they can regulate metal dispersion, suppress sintering, and tailor the electronic structure of noble metals to enhance both catalytic activity and durability9,14–16. Surface defect engineering of supports is a commonly employed strategy for modulating MSI, introducing controlled defects can strengthen metal–support interactions and create opportunities for tailoring the geometric and electronic structure of supported noble metals2,17–19. However, increasing surface defect density often comes at the cost of structural stability of the support.

Perovskite oxides (ABO3), which consist of a rigid three-dimensional network of corner-sharing BO6 octahedra, exhibit exceptional structural tolerance over a wide range of compositions and distortions owing to the Goldschmidt tolerance factor20,21. This inherent structural robustness makes them an ideal platform for precisely regulating the surface active sites of supported noble metals while maintaining support integrity. Previous studies have demonstrated that perovskite oxides can modulate the particle size, the electronic structure and redox behavior of the supported metals through tuning A- or B-site cation compositions or introducing lattice defects22–27. Whereas, these approaches often involve the simultaneous variation of multiple parameters, making precise single-variable control difficult and thereby preventing the decoupling of the individual contributions of each factor to the overall catalytic performance. More importantly, compared with macroscopic descriptors such as particle size and oxidation state, probing and deliberately tailoring the atomic-scale microstructure of supported noble metal particles remain far more challenging. These bottlenecks hinder the rational design of catalysts with predictable performance and obscure the establishment of unambiguous structure–activity relationships.

Here, we employ LaAlO3 perovskite as a support, PdO as the active phase, and low-temperature methane oxidation as the probe reaction to demonstrate that precise control of the support’s defect density enables atomic-level control over supported PdO nanoparticles. Both experimental and density functional theory (DFT) approaches are combined to elucidate this relationship. From a theoretical perspective, systematic tuning of A-site stoichiometry was used to introduce controlled defects, yielding over 800 distinct defect structures. These defects were found to serve as anchoring and restructuring centers for PdO nanoparticles. Guided by these predictions, we synthesized perovskite supports with well-defined defect types, which generate strong metal–support interactions that stabilize step-rich PdO configurations. This stabilization enables precise structural control of the supported PdO. Collectively, our results establish a surface-defect engineering strategy for perovskite oxides that allows atomic-level regulation of noble-metal active sites. This generalizable approach provides a new pathway for the rational design of highly efficient and durable catalysts for alkane oxidation and potentially a broad range of oxidation reactions.

Results and discussion

Morphology tailoring of PdO on La-deficient LaAlO3: DFT studies

A defect-engineering strategy was developed to control perovskite surface reconstructions and dictate the morphology of supported PdO clusters. To guide the subsequent catalyst synthesis, this strategy was first examined through DFT calculations. Previous studies have shown that in the LaAlO3 system (denoted La1.0Al), the most stable exposed surface is the LaAlO3(001) surface, with the LaO-terminated configuration exhibiting the highest stability (Supplementary Fig. 1)28.

In this work, we constructed La-deficient LaAlO3(001) surface models via theoretical calculations to generate La16Al20O54 (La0.8AlO3−δ, denoted La0.8Al) and La18Al20O57 (La0.9AlO3−δ, denoted La0.9Al) structures with controlled La deficiencies. Given the structural complexity of defective LaAlO3 surfaces, a genetic algorithm (GA)–assisted DFT approach was employed to identify thermodynamically favorable configurations. During the global structural optimization, formal oxidation states of +3, +3, and −2 were assigned to La, Al, and O, respectively. To improve computational efficiency, a La12Al12O36 slab was used as the substrate. For the La0.8Al model, an overlayer of La4Al8O18 was introduced to enable full surface relaxation, while the La0.9Al model employed a La6Al8O21 overlayer.

Based on the initial surface models, metastable and globally stable configurations of both La0.8Al and La0.9Al systems were explored using the Universal Structure Predictor: Evolutionary Xtallography (USPEX) code29–31. This search generated ~800 candidate structures, providing insight into the structural evolution of La-deficient LaAlO3 surfaces. The ten most stable configurations were subsequently refined through high-accuracy DFT calculations (Fig. 1a).

The stoichiometric LaAlO3(001) surface exhibits a smooth topology with stable LaO terminations. As the La content decreases from 1.0 to 0.8, progressive surface reconstructions emerge (Supplementary Figs. 2–3): the La0.9Al surface relaxes across two atomic layers, forming a stepped morphology, while the La0.8Al surface undergoes a deeper four-layer reconstruction that produces a pronounced crater-like topology. These results demonstrate that controlled La deficiency drives distinct surface reconstructions, which can then serve as structural templates for tailoring the morphology of supported metal nanoparticles during catalyst synthesis.

To verify this hypothesis, a three-layer PdO thin film was deposited on the LaxAl surfaces to emulate supported PdO clusters. The calculated results reveal that the PdO films adopt morphologies that closely mirror those of their underlying supports (Fig. 1b–d). On the stoichiometric La1.0Al surface, PdO retains a nearly flat and ordered configuration (denoted Pd/La1.0Al), exposing three-coordinated Pd (Pd3c) and O (O3c) sites. In contrast, PdO supported on La-deficient surfaces undergoes substantial reconstruction: on La0.9Al, PdO forms a stepped morphology exposing Pd2c, Pd3c, O2c, and O3c sites, whereas on La0.8Al, it develops a crater-like topology dominated by Pd2c, Pd3c, and O3c terminations.

These distinct coordination environments are expected to strongly influence the activity and selectivity of structure-sensitive alkane oxidation reactions. Furthermore, charge-density difference analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4) indicates progressively decreased electron transfer from the support to PdO with increasing La deficiency. In general, these findings demonstrate that defect engineering of La-deficient LaAlO3 surfaces provides a powerful strategy to tailor PdO cluster morphology, thereby establishing a robust theoretical foundation for the rational design of next-generation oxidation catalysts. In later sections, CH4 activation on these model surfaces will be further probed via DFT.

Defect-engineered Pd/LaxAl: experimental investigations

Guided by the theoretical predictions, a series of LaxAl perovskite materials with varying La deficiencies were synthesized by precisely controlling the La content. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Supplementary Fig. 5) confirm the formation of the perovskite phase for all compositions25,32. With increasing La deficiency, the diffraction peaks gradually decrease in intensity and shift toward lower 2θ angles, suggesting a reduction in crystallinity and lattice expansion associated with defect incorporation26. All samples exhibit comparable specific surface areas (12–15 m2·g−1, Supplementary Table 1), indicating that the introduction of La vacancies exerts minimal influence on the overall textural properties. To determine the distribution of La defects, ICP and XPS were used to analyze the bulk and surface La and Al contents of the LaxAl support (Supplementary Table 2). The ICP results reveal that the overall La/Al ratios are consistent with the theoretical values, while the surface La/Al ratios measured by XPS are lower, suggesting that La defects are mainly concentrated on the surface of LaxAl. CO2-TPD and NH3-TPD measurements (Supplementary Fig. 6) further verify invariant acid-base characteristics of these perovskites, despite the presence of surface defects. Together, these results confirm that the LaxAl perovskites maintain comparable physicochemical characteristics, thereby providing an ideal platform to precisely tailor the architecture of supported Pd species and to systematically isolate the influence of Pd surface structure on catalytic behavior.

The Pd/LaxAl catalysts were prepared by incipient wetness impregnation of the LaxAl supports with an aqueous Pd(NO3)2 solution to achieve a nominal Pd loading of 1.0 wt.%, followed by calcination in air. Under these conditions, Pd is present predominantly as PdO nanoparticles. Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis confirms an actual Pd loading of ~0.94 wt.% (Supplementary Table 1). Compared with the corresponding supports, the catalysts exhibit a slight reduction in specific surface area to ~10 m2·g−1 (Supplementary Table 1). Step-scan XRD patterns (Supplementary Fig. 7) reveal that all catalysts retained the characteristic diffraction features of the LaxAl supports, with no detectable PdO reflections, indicating that PdO species are highly dispersed on the support surface.

High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) was used to characterize the dispersion and particle size of PdO nanoparticles (Fig. 2a–c), complemented by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping for accurate particle-size evaluation33. The analyses reveal that PdO nanoparticles are uniformly distributed across all LaxAl supports, with sizes ranging from 4 to 12 nm and average diameters of 7.3, 7.1, and 7.0 nm for Pd/La1.0Al, Pd/La0.9Al, and Pd/La0.8Al, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8). The comparable particle sizes among these samples indicate that La stoichiometry exerts negligible influence on Pd nanoparticle growth under the applied synthesis conditions.

Aberration-corrected high-resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-STEM) provided direct visualization of the surface structures of PdO nanoparticles (Fig. 2d–f and Supplementary Figs. 9–11). In the Pd/La1.0Al and Pd/La0.9Al catalysts, most PdO particles displayed distinct lattice fringes corresponding to the PdO(101) and PdO(110) surfaces. PdO particles on La1.0Al exhibited well-defined surface morphologies, consistent with growth governed by the Wulff construction34,35. Those on La0.9Al showed 1–2 atomic-layer steps, indicative of slight structural distortion. In contrast, PdO particles on La0.8Al formed hemispherical aggregates with blurred lattice fringes, signifying reduced crystallinity.

These observations demonstrate that decreasing La stoichiometry progressively destabilizes the PdO lattice, thereby increasing surface disorder. Such disorder likely alters the distribution of edge and corner sites—structural motifs widely recognized as highly active for oxidation reactions. It is noteworthy that, although the experimentally observed PdO particles are significantly thicker than the three-layer PdO thin films modeled theoretically (Fig. 1b–d), the influence of support morphology remains remarkably consistent.

Following visualization of the PdO structural evolution, in situ CO-DRIFTS measurements were performed to obtain complementary insights into Pd surface structures (Fig. 2g–i). Prior to measurement, the catalysts were reduced in 5 vol.% H2/N2 at a mild reduction temperature of 150 °C to preserve the morphological differences upon conversion to metallic Pd. Three characteristic CO adsorption regions were identified: linear CO at 2100–2050 cm−1 corresponding to Pd corner sites, bridging CO at 2000–1950 cm−1 associated with step sites, and threefold-hollow CO at 1950–1800 cm−1 attributed to Pd(111) terraces36–38. Band integration (Supplementary Table 3) revealed that decreasing the La content from 1.0 to 0.8 led to a steady increase in linearly adsorbed CO and a decrease in threefold-hollow CO, indicating a higher proportion of corner sites and fewer extended Pd(111) terraces.

Under conventional particle-size effects, such trends are typically accompanied by a monotonic increase in bridging CO intensity due to a higher density of step edges39,40. Unexpectedly, the bridging CO band here followed a volcano-type trend, peaking for Pd/La0.9Al. This anomaly suggests that particle size alone cannot account for the observed Pd surface evolution. Instead, interactions between Pd species and the La-deficient perovskite support likely alter the electronic and geometric environments of surface Pd atoms, thereby regulating the balance among corner, step, and terrace sites. It should be noted, however, that the morphology of metallic Pd does not precisely replicate that of PdO, and thus the CO-DRIFTS results should be interpreted with this distinction in mind.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to determine the surface chemical state of Pd (Fig. 2j). The Pd 3d5/2 binding energy at 336.5–336.9 eV is characteristic of +2 oxidation state41,42. The absence of signals below 336.0 eV indicates that metallic Pd is not present, confirming that Pd exists predominantly as PdO. The Pd K-edge XANES spectra (Fig. 2k) further support this assignment, although the white-line intensity in all catalysts is slightly weaker than that of bulk PdO43,44. This attenuation is attributed to interfacial charge transfer between Pd and the LaxAl support, consistent with the theoretically predicted electronic redistribution at the Pd/LaxAl interface (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis (Fig. 2l) revealed distinct Pd–O and Pd–Pd scattering paths, characteristic of PdO. Fitting of the coordination numbers (Supplementary Figs. 12–13 and Supplementary Table 4) showed a gradual decrease in the first-shell Pd–O coordination number (CN) with increasing La deficiency (CN = 3.9, 3.7, and 3.6, respectively, for x = 1.0, 0.9, and 0.8). This trend indicates slight destabilization of Pd–O bonding as defects accumulate and implies a stronger interfacial interaction between PdO and the defective LaxAl surfaces.

Low-temperature methane combustion over Pd/LaxAl catalysts

Methane combustion over supported PdO is a structure-sensitive reaction, in which activity strongly depends on the particle size and surface orientations of PdO45,46. In the current work, the surface structure of PdO was tuned by varying the stoichiometry of the LaxAl supports, which is expected to directly influence the catalytic performance of Pd/ LaxAl catalysts. As shown in Fig. 3a, the light-off curves indeed reveal a pronounced dependence of catalytic activity on La stoichiometry. Among the three catalysts, Pd/La0.9Al exhibited the highest activity, achieving 90% CH4 conversion (T90) at 365 °C, followed by Pd/La1.0Al (T90 = 400 °C) and Pd/La0.8Al (T90 = 420 °C). Kinetic measurements (inset of Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 5) further confirmed this activity trend. The Pd/La0.9Al catalyst showed the lowest apparent activation energy (Ea = 66.9 kJ·mol−1), indicating that CH4 activation and oxidation are energetically most favorable47. Correspondingly, Pd/La0.9Al exhibited the highest reaction rates and turnover frequencies (TOFs) for methane combustion at 300 °C (Supplementary Table 5). The enhanced activity of Pd/La0.9Al persisted regardless of reductive pretreatment (5 vol.% H2/N2, 150 °C, 30 min) or variation of the CH4/O2 ratio (1 vol.% CH4, 2 vol.% O2; Supplementary Fig. 14). To contextualize its performance, the activity of Pd/La0.9Al was compared with recently reported Pd-based catalysts using T50 and TOF as key metrics (Supplementary Table 6). Although direct comparison is complicated by differences in reaction conditions and experimental setups, Pd/La0.9Al exhibits one of the most competitive catalytic activity among the evaluated catalysts.

The catalytic stability of the Pd/LaxAl catalysts, including durability in cyclic light-off tests and water tolerance, was systematically evaluated. During cyclic tests up to 500 °C (Supplementary Fig. 15a), Pd/La0.8Al and Pd/La1.0Al exhibited significant deactivation after the second cycle, with increases in T50 (the temperature for 50% CH4 conversion) of 33 °C and 23 °C, respectively, before stabilizing in the third cycle. In contrast, Pd/La0.9Al showed only a modest 6 °C increase in T50 during the second cycle, and no significant decrease in activity was observed over the subsequent 10-cycle test (Supplementary Fig. 15b), indicating substantially enhanced durability.

The water tolerance of the catalysts was assessed by introducing 5 vol.% H2O into the reaction stream (denoted Pd/LaxAl–W). Under these wet conditions (Supplementary Fig. 16), all catalysts experienced very clear H2O inhibition, reflected by ~50 °C increases in their T90 values. This behavior is consistent with the well-known sensitivity of surface PdO species to water vapor48–50.

Stability tests at 380 °C (Fig. 3b) revealed that CH4 conversion remained nearly constant for Pd/La0.9Al over the first 30 h, whereas Pd/La0.8Al and Pd/La1.0Al declined from 61% to 54%, 72% to 67%, respectively. Upon the addition of 5 vol.% H2O, CH4 conversion for all catalysts decreased by ~25%, which is attributed to H2O inhibition, and possible PdO deactivation, e.g., hydroxylation of the PdO surface48,50. After returning to dry conditions, Pd/La0.9Al rapidly recovered its initial conversion indicating H2O inhibition alone; in contrast, the CH4 conversion for Pd/La1.0Al and Pd/La0.8Al only restored to 45% and 60%, respectively, suggesting combined H2O inhibition and H2O-induced PdO deactivation. Over the total reaction time of 100 h, Pd/La0.9Al showed only a 5% loss in activity, confirming its high catalytic stability and enhanced resistance to H2O.

To further evaluate the thermal durability of the catalysts, the fresh Pd/LaxAl catalysts were thermally aged at 600 °C and 700 °C for 2 h (Supplementary Fig. 17). After aging at 600 °C, the T90 values of Pd/La0.8Al and Pd/La0.9Al increased by only 8 °C and 6 °C, respectively, indicating largely preserved catalytic performance. In contrast, Pd/La1.0Al showed a pronounced activity loss, with T90 increasing by 23 °C. When the aging temperature was raised to 700 °C, the T90 increases for Pd/La0.8Al, Pd/La0.9Al, and Pd/La1.0Al were 25, 18, and 45 °C, respectively, reflecting more severe deactivation. Notably, Pd/La0.9Al still exhibited the smallest T90 increase among the three catalysts at both aging temperatures. These findings confirm that Pd/La0.9Al exhibits superior thermal stability relative to the other two catalysts.

Structural characterization revealed that varying the La stoichiometry in LaxAl does not significantly affect the oxidation state or particle size of supported PdO, but it markedly alters the surface microstructure of PdO. The methane oxidation turnover frequencies (TOF) correlate strongly with the fraction of step sites as a function of La stoichiometry (Supplementary Fig. 18), both peaking for the Pd/La0.9Al catalyst. This trend indicates that a higher density of PdO step sites directly enhances methane combustion activity. Upon PdO reduction by H2 followed by CO titration, these step sites transform into metallic Pd sites that exhibit strong bridging CO adsorption (Fig. 2g–i).

To verify this relationship, all LaxAl supports were calcined at 1000 °C prior to Pd loading (denoted Pd/LaxAl(1000)). Interestingly, in this second catalyst series, Pd/La0.8Al(1000) — rather than Pd/La0.9Al(1000) — displayed the highest methane oxidation activity (Supplementary Fig. 19a). Subsequent CO-DRIFTS measurements after mild H2 reduction revealed that Pd/La0.8Al(1000) exhibited the strongest bridging CO adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 19b). Consolidating the results from both catalyst series yields a robust linear correlation between the step-site fraction and TOF (Fig. 3c), confirming that PdO step sites act as decisive structural descriptors governing methane combustion activity.

To evaluate the generality of this support-induced strategy, pre-synthesized Pd nanoparticles (~5 nm) prepared via wet-chemical route were loaded onto LaxAl (denoted as PdNPs/LaxAl). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 20, neither catalytic activity nor surface structure of these Pd nanoparticles was affected by La deficiency in the supports. This observation indicates that the defect-induced modulation of PdO surface structures primarily occurs during the nucleation and growth of Pd species on the LaxAl surface. Furthermore, Pd/LaxAl catalysts exhibited consistent activity trends in the oxidation of other alkanes, including ethane, propane, n-butane, and n-hexane, with Pd/La0.9Al consistently delivering the highest catalytic performance (Supplementary Fig. 21). This trend was further corroborated in propane oxidation over Pt/LaxAl catalysts prepared using the same strategy (Supplementary Fig. 22). These findings demonstrate that the proposed defect-engineering approach is broadly applicable across different noble metals and alkane substrates.

For the most active Pd/La0.9Al catalyst, in situ CO-DRIFTS was again performed following methane oxidation under both dry and wet conditions, and after subsequent mild H2/N2 reduction (Supplementary Fig. 23). The fraction of bridging CO remained nearly unchanged after 20 h of reaction at 500 °C in either atmosphere, demonstrating that PdO step sites remain structurally stable under the catalytic operating conditions. To further substantiate the durability of PdO step sites, CO-DRIFTS measurements were performed on fresh samples after thermal treatment at 600 °C and 700 °C for 2 h. The CO-DRIFTS spectra of Pd/La0.9Al after thermal treatment (Supplementary Fig. 24) show a clear decrease in μ2-CO bridging adsorption, accompanied by reduced linear CO adsorption and increased μ3-CO adsorption, features indicative of particle agglomeration. Consistently, TEM images of Pd/La0.9Al after calcination at 700 °C reveal PdO particle growth beyond 30 nm (Supplementary Fig. 25). Overall, these results demonstrate that the step sites remain structurally stable below 600 °C but undergo sintering and reconstruction at higher temperatures.

Reaction mechanism and the decisive role of PdO step sites in C–H activation

The activation of the first C–H bond is widely recognized as the rate-determining step in lean methane combustion over PdO, which proceeds via a Mars–van Krevelen (MvK) mechanism involving lattice oxygen participation51,52. To gain direct insight into this process, CH4 temperature-programmed surface reaction (CH4-TPSR) experiments (Fig. 3d) were performed. The profiles reveal methane consumption peaks at 262, 244, and 254 °C for Pd/La0.8Al, Pd/La0.9Al, and Pd/La1.0Al, respectively, accompanied by concurrent CO2 formation. This trend closely mirrors the activity order observed in the light-off curves (Fig. 3a).

The corresponding H2O and CO evolution signals (Supplementary Fig. 26) show that the H2O peak appears ~5 °C higher than CO2, indicating stronger H2O adsorption and higher desorption temperatures. This observation partly explains the inhibitory effect of water vapor during catalysis. Trace amounts of CO were detected for Pd/La0.8Al and Pd/La1.0Al, but were absent for Pd/La0.9Al, reflecting the better oxygen reactivity of the latter and its enhanced ability to sustain complete methane oxidation.

To further elucidate the mechanistic pathways of methane oxidation over Pd/LaxAl catalysts, density functional theory calculations were performed to investigate CH4 adsorption and activation on three-layer PdO thin films supported on LaxAl surfaces (x = 1.0, 0.9, and 0.8) (Supplementary Figs. 27a, 27d, and 27 g). Although the experimentally observed PdO particles are much thicker, we have demonstrated above that the support morphology effect applies consistently to both model and real systems. Additionally, the C−H bond distances of CH4 before and after C−H bond dissociation were also compared to validate the rationality of the calculated transition states (Supplementary Table 7).

The calculated CH4 adsorption energies were –0.30 eV, –0.36 eV, and –0.28 eV for Pd/La1.0Al, Pd/La0.9Al, and Pd/La0.8Al, respectively, indicating the strongest CH4 binding on the Pd/La0.9Al surface. This enhanced adsorption is primarily attributed to the exposure of low-coordination Pd2c and O2c sites. Subsequently, the activation of the first C–H bond in methane was examined (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 27). On Pd/La1.0Al, C–H cleavage requires overcoming an activation barrier of 0.79 eV (76.23 kJ·mol–1), with an exothermicity of 0.47 eV. On Pd/La0.8Al, the barrier increases to 1.12 eV (108.07 kJ·mol–1), with a reaction energy of –0.88 eV. The higher barrier on Pd/La0.8Al arises from its crater-like surface morphology, where elongated Pd–O distances are less favorable for C–H bond dissociation despite the presence of low-coordination Pd sites. Notably, for Pd/La0.9Al, the activation barrier is only 0.61 eV (58.86 kJ·mol–1), accompanied by an exothermicity of 0.94 eV, fully consistent with its superior experimental catalytic activity. The C–H bond activation energies obtained from DFT calculations are consistent in trend with the experimentally measured apparent activation energies (inset of Fig. 3a): 68.8, 66.9, and 72.8 kJ·mol–1 for Pd/La1.0Al, Pd/La0.9Al, and Pd/La0.8Al, respectively.

To uncover the electronic origins of these activity differences, the density of states (DOS) was calculated for key intermediates in the heterolytic CH4 dissociation transition state, namely, the Pd–CH3 and O–H species, on the Pd/LaxAl surfaces (Fig. 4b–c). The results reveal that the O–H species formed during CH4 activation exhibit strong covalent character, with energy levels at –8.19 eV, primarily associated with O2c sites on the Pd/La0.9Al surface. Furthermore, the Pd–C (CH3) bonds on Pd/La1.0Al and Pd/La0.8Al are predominantly ionic, whereas those on Pd/La0.9Al display significantly greater covalent character and occur at lower energy levels. This feature correlates with the presence of low-coordination Pd2c sites, which facilitate more effective orbital overlap with adsorbed CH4 fragments.

From the above discussion, we can clear conclude that CH4 activation on Pd/LaxAl surfaces is highly sensitive to the local coordination and electronic structure of PdO clusters, features that are dictated by La-deficiency-induced surface templating of the LaxAl substrate. In particular, the La0.9Al support can etch or template an optimal PdO structural motif. This motif is characterized by a high density of Pd2c/O2c site pairs and more suitable local Pd–O geometric parameters, which simultaneously enhance CH4 adsorption and markedly lower the first C–H bond cleavage barrier, leading to efficient methane conversion. This provides clear mechanistic validation of our strategy: by engineering La-deficient LaxAl surfaces with tailored morphologies, one can precisely construct high-activity PdO sites, thereby markedly enhancing hydrocarbon oxidation performance.

In summary, by combining the USPEX global optimization method with density functional theory (DFT), we precisely constructed well-defined surface defects (planar, stepped, and crater-like morphologies) on LaAlO3 supports through systematic tuning of La stoichiometry. These distinct defect structures act as structural templates, directing the anchoring and restructuring of supported PdO nanoparticles. Guided by theoretical predictions, perovskite materials with controlled defect types were successfully synthesized. The resulting defect sites generate strong metal–support interactions that stabilize step-rich PdO configurations, enabling precise structural control over PdO nanoparticles. Building on these insights, we propose a perovskite surface-defect engineering strategy for atomic-level regulation of noble-metal active sites. A quantitative linear correlation between step-site density and methane oxidation activity establishes the step site as a decisive structural descriptor. Moreover, DFT analysis reveals that the step-rich PdO/La0.9Al configuration promotes C–H bond activation through the formation of stronger covalent O–H and Pd–C bonds at low-coordination Pd2c and O2c sites, directly linking atomic-scale geometry to catalytic performance. Overall, this work provides a general framework for the rational design of supported noble-metal catalysts with enhanced activity and stability.

Methods

Catalyst preparation

Synthesis of LaxAlO3−δ

The perovskite LaxAlO3−δ (LaxAl) with different La stoichiometry (x = 1.0, 0.9 and 0.8) were synthesized by the citrate sol-gel method. 10, 9 and 8 mmol of La(NO3)3·6H2O (A.R., Adamas-beta), 0.01 mol of Al(NO3)3·9H2O (A.R., Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) and adequate amounts of citric acid (A.R., Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) were dissolved in 30 mL of deionized water, the molar ratio of citric acid to total metal cations was 1.1. Next, the solution was thoroughly stirred and evaporated at 80 °C to get a viscous hydrogel, which was subsequently dried at 110 °C overnight. The obtained spongiform solid was milled and then calcined at 800 °C in air for 3 h with a heating rate of 4 °C·min–1. The resulted samples were denoted as LaxAl. The actual La and Al contents of the LaxAl samples are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

In addition, a series of supports calcined at 1000 °C were also prepared for comparison, recorded as LaxAl(1000).

Synthesis of Pd/LaxAl and Pt/LaxAl

1 wt.% Pd was loaded onto the LaxAl supports by the incipient wetness method. Specifically, 0.067 g of Pd(NO3)2 aqueous solution (0.15 gPd·g–1, Heraeus) was first diluted into 2 mL deionized water to form a uniform solution. Then, the as-prepared solution was added into 1 g LaxAl support dropwise with stirring and ultrasonic treatment for 30 min to get a slurry. After standing at room temperature for 8 h, the slurry was transfer to an 80 °C oven to evaporate for 12 h, followed by calcinating in a muffle furnace at 500 °C for 3 h, with a ramp of 4 °C·min–1. The obtained samples were denoted as Pd/LaxAl. Likewise, Pd/LaxAl(1000) catalysts were synthesized using LaxAl(1000) as the support. The Pt/LaxAl catalysts were prepared using the same method, except that Pt(NO3)2 (0.149 gPt·g–1) aqueous solution was employed as the precursor.

Synthesis of Pd nanoparticles (PdNPs)

Pd nanoparticles with a size of ~5 nm were prepared using a colloidal chemical method reported in the literature53. Typically, 75 mg of Pd(acac)2 was mixed with 15 mL of oleylamine (OAm) in a three-necked flask under a nitrogen atmosphere, and the resulting solution was heated to 60 °C within 10 min. Subsequently, 300 mg of borane tributylamine (BTB) dissolved in 4 mL of OAm was rapidly injected into the above Pd–OAm solution. The temperature was then raised to 90 °C at a ramp rate of 3 °C·min–1 and maintained for 1 h. After the solution was cooled to room temperature, 30 mL of anhydrous ethanol was added, and the resulting suspension was separated by centrifugation (8000 rpm, 8 min). Finally, the obtained Pd NPs were redispersed in n-hexane and stored in a low-temperature environment.

Synthesis of PdNPs/LaxAl

Typically, 1 g of LaxAl support powder was dispersed in 20 mL of n-hexane under magnetic stirring. An appropriate amount of the Pd NP dispersion in n-hexane was then added dropwise to the above mixture, and stirring was continued for 8 h. The resulting solid was collected by centrifugation and dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 60 °C. The dried sample was subsequently heat-treated at 700 °C for 30 s to remove the organic ligands from the nanoparticle surface, followed by calcination at 500 °C for 2 h to obtain the final PdNPs/LaxAl catalyst.

Catalysts characterization

The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on a Bruker D8 Focus diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å) operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. The as-prepared supports were scanned within a region of 2θ from 10 to 80° (6 °/min) to analyse the phase structure. The catalysts were step scanned with the step size of 0.02°, for 1 s per step.

The actual La and Al contents of the LaxAl samples and Pd contents of the Pd/LaxAl catalysts were determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) (Agilent 725).

The specific surface areas of the catalysts were determined by nitrogen adsorption at 77.4 K using a NOVA 4200e instrument. Prior to each measurement, the samples were vacuumed at 80 °C for 10 h.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained on a JEOL 2100 F electron microscope operated at 200 kV. The sample powder was dispersed in ethanol and then dropped to carbon films coated on copper mesh grids and dried in calm air.

The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were obtained on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer under an ultrahigh vacuum (6.7 × 10−8 Pa), employing Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV) as excitation X-ray source. All binding energies (BE) were calibrated by the C 1 s peak level at 284.6 eV.

The dispersion of Pd was tested by CO pulse on the same instrument as the O2-TPD. 50 mg of catalyst was placed in a U-shaped quartz reactor and pretreated with 5 vol.% H2/Ar at 300 °C for 30 min. After cooling to 25 °C in He flow (50 mL·min–1), 1 vol.% CO/He (50 mL·min–1) was injected into the sample cell every two minutes until CO signal was kept constant.

CH4-TPSR experiment was carry out on the same instrument as the O2-TPD. The catalysts (50 mg) were pretreated in 3 vol.% O2/He (50 mL·min–1) at 400 °C for 40 min, and then cooled to room temperature and kept in the gas flow for 40 min. Finally, the catalyst was heated from 30 to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C·min–1 in a flow of 1 vol.% CH4/He (50 mL·min–1). The outlet gas was monitored by a mass spectrometer (HPR-20 QIC) and MS signals of CH4 (m/z = 15), CO2 (m/z = 44), H2O (m/z = 16), CO (m/z = 18) and H2 (m/z = 2) were detected.

Temperature-programmed desorption of CO2 (CO2-TPD) for the supports were also carried out on Micromeritics AutoChem II 2920. 100 mg of support was pretreated in He (40 mL·min–1) for 30 min at 400 °C, after the sample was cooled down to 30 °C, it was exposed in 10 vol.% CO2/He (40 mL·min–1) at room temperature until reach a saturation state and subsequently purged for 30 min with He. Finally, the sample was heated to 800 °C in He (40 mL·min–1) with a rate of 10 °C·min–1, the MS signal of CO2 (m/z = 44) was recorded.

Temperature-programmed desorption of NH3 (NH3-TPD) for the supports were carried out on a PX200 apparatus equipped with a (TCD). Firstly, 100 mg of the support was preheated at 450 °C in Ar flow (40 mL·min–1) for 1 h and cooled to 100 °C. Then, the samples were saturated with 5 vol.% NH3/Ar (40 mL·min–1) for 40 min and then purged with Ar (40 mL·min–1) for 30 min. Finally, the samples were heated from 100 to 600 °C at a rate of 10 °C·min–1 in Ar (40 mL·min–1). The amount of desorbed NH3 was determined by a TCD.

In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform (DRIFT) spectroscopy of CO adsorbed on the catalysts were recorded on a Nicolet Nexus 670 FT-IR spectrometer equipped with a liquid-nitrogen-cooled HgCdTe (MCT) detector. The samples were pressed into the IR cell with a BaF window. Before the CO adsorption, the sample was reduced in 5% H2/He at 150 °C for 30 min and then cooled to 30 °C in Ar. After the background spectra were recorded at 30 °C, Ar was replaced by the gas of 0.5% CO/Ar (50 mL·min–1), and the DRIFTS spectra of the adsorbed CO on the catalyst surface were collected by averaging 64 scans at a resolution of 8 cm−1.

X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements, including X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS), were performed at the Pd K-edge on beamline 14W1 of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility, China. The storage ring was operated at an energy of 8.0 GeV with an average electron current below 99 mA. The acquired EXAFS data were extracted and processed following standard procedures using the ATHENA module implemented in the FEFIT software package54.

Computational methods

We used the genetic algorithm optimization method embedded in the Universal Structure Predictor Evolutionary Xtallography (USPEX) code to search for stable catalysts structures, which has been successfully applied for predicting stable structures of bulk crystals, nanoclusters, surface reconstruction, and polymers29–31. As a global optimization method, the USPEX code uses four ways, namely, random, hereditary, mutation, and transmutation, to produce new structures, and the whole prediction usually requires hundreds or thousands of individual structure relaxations. In our calculations, mixed-metal oxide structures were modelled based on a slab surface model, with the entire surface layer taken into account in generating new structures.

In this work, all spin-polarized DFT calculations were carried out using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP 5.4.1)55. The projector augmented wave (PAW)56 method and the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)57,58 were applied for geometry optimization, electronic structure, and energy calculations. The kinetic energy cut-off was set to 400 eV, and the k-point mesh of 1 × 1 × 1 suggested by previous studies was used for Brillouin-zone integrations. The force threshold for structural optimizations was 0.05 eV/Å, and a large vacuum region of 15 Å was used to eliminate interactions between neighboring layers.

The transition states (TSs) of surface reactions were located using a constrained optimization scheme59,60 and were verified when (i) all forces on the relaxed atoms vanished and (ii) the total energy was a maximum along the reaction coordinate and a minimum with respect to all other degrees of freedom61. Activity test

The CH4 combustion was evaluated on a fixed-bed quartz reactor (i.d. = 6 mm). 0.1 g of catalyst (40-60 mesh) was mixed with 0.2 g of quartz sand to eliminate thermal effects. The gas mixture composed of 1 vol.% CH4, 20 vol.% O2, and 79 vol.% N2 (and 5 vol.% H2O if used) flowed through catalyst bed. The total gas flow rate was 50 mL·min–1, corresponding to a weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) of 30,000 mL·gcat–1·h–1. The activity was test in the temperature range of 150–600 °C at a heating rate of 4 °C·min–1. Finally, the CH4 in the reaction gases was monitored on-line by a gas chromatograph (Agilent 7890 A, USA) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID).

Similarly, the C3H8 combustion was performed in a U-shaped quartz tube (i.d. = 4 mm). 0.1 g of catalyst (40-60 mesh) was mixed with 0.2 g of quartz sand. The reaction mixture consisted of 0.2 vol.% C3H8, 2 vol.% O2 and 97.8 vol.% Ar with total flow rate of 50 mL·min−1, and the corresponding WHSV was 30,000 mL·gcat–1·h–1. The conversion of C3H8 was measured after the catalytic reaction by an on-line gas chromatograph (GC-2060) that was equipped with a FID. In addition, the catalytic oxidation of other volatile organic compounds (1000 ppm), including ethane, n-butane and n-hexane, was evaluated under the same reaction conditions using air as the balance gas. For the tests involving n-hexane, the liquid reactants were injected into a vaporization chamber via a syringe pump prior to entering the reactor.
