Non-antibiotic management of bacterial keratitis
Authors: Mizna Akbar, Christopher Phung, Alexis Monical, Mikhayla L. Armstrong, John C. Alverdy, Asim V. Farooq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial keratitis (BK) is a common subtype of infectious keratitis, the most common cause of corneal blindness globally. While antibiotics are the mainstay of treatment for BK, numerous nonantibiotic treatments and adjuncts have emerged in recent years. These include, but are not limited to, collagen cross-linking, phototherapy, immunomodulators, and various forms of transplantation. The purpose of this work is to provide a comprehensive summary of the current literature, highlight promising areas of progress, and identify gaps and areas for further work in the management of BK. With rising resistance to antibiotics, this review echoes and emphasizes the call to study and establish alternative and adjunctive strategies for BK treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Infectious keratitis is the most common cause of corneal blindness globally, with bacterial keratitis (BK) being the most common subtype in most regions.[1] BK can be caused by a wide range of pathogenic organisms that vary in prevalence by location and antibiotic resistance patterns.[2] A recent meta-analysis of over forty studies across over 20 countries found the most commonly cultured organisms to include Staphylococcus spp. (41.4%), Pseudomonas spp. (17.0%), Streptococcus spp. (13.1%), Corynebacterium spp.(6.6%), and Moraxella spp. (4.1%).[3] BK is generally treated with broad-spectrum topical antibiotics, with common agents including a aminoglycoside-cephalosporin or monotherapy with a fluoroquinolone.[4] BK, even when treated in a timely manner with antibiotics, can be complicated by corneal scarring, intraocular inflammation, scleral involvement, corneal ulceration, perforation, and melt that necessitates corneal transplantation and vision loss. Given the context of these complications and the rise in antibiotic resistance, the development of nonantibiotic treatments and adjuncts to prevent and treat BK is needed. This review outlines such treatments, presenting those that are relatively established, ranging to those in the early stages of development.

METHODS

A literature search through PubMed was conducted with the following key words: “bacterial keratitis,” “microbial keratitis,” “infectious keratitis,” “treatment of bacterial keratitis,” “treatment of microbial keratitis,” “treatment of infectious keratitis,” “complications of infectious keratitis,” “adjunct treatments for infectious keratitis,” and “non antibiotic treatment of infectious keratitis.”

RESULTS

Nonantibiotic treatments and/or adjuncts

Collagen cross-linking

Collagen cross-linking as a technique was first introduced for keratoconus by Seiler in 1996.[5] Currently, collagen cross-linking is used in ectasia postcorneal refractive surgery and to prevent keratoconus progression. Studies have begun to look at the potential for collagen cross-linking in infectious keratitis. Traditionally, collagen cross-linking is achieved by removing the central corneal epithelium and applying riboflavin for 30-min followed by cornea irradiation with ultraviolet A (UV-A) for an additional 30-min.[6] This is known as the Dresden protocol. There have been several modifications to the procedure since it was first described that have not been as widely adopted, but that remain promising areas for future research and clinical use.[6]

The goal of collagen cross-linking is to promote the formation of free oxygen radicals, resulting in stiffening and stabilization of the cornea, and an overall decrease in microbial invasion and replication. In this procedure, riboflavin is exposed to ultraviolet light (UV-A 365-370 nm), causing a photochemical reaction and production of free radicals. This byproduct induces collagen production and reduces microbial load.[78] In addition to the structural benefits of riboflavin, there is evidence in that riboflavin has anti-inflammatory effects and antinociceptive effects and sensitizes the pain-relieving effects of morphine, potentially decreasing the dosage of opioid treatment needed in extremely painful ulcerations and avoiding risk of anesthetic abuse.[9] Experimental models suggest corneal stiffening from collagen cross-linking may be counterproductive in infections that also require concurrent antibiotic treatment due to the reduced the permeability of the cornea to antimicrobial agents, although in practice the two treatment strategies are often successfully combined.[10]

Multi-drug resistant organisms highly associated with infectious keratitis have been shown to be susceptible to collagen cross-linking, including Staphylococcus aureus, Methicillin-resistant S. aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.[1112] Optimizing the exposure time and intensity of UV-A may lead to greater therapeutic success.[1314] A meta-analysis from 2016 reviewed 210 human eyes (collagen cross-linking, n = 175; control, n = 35) that underwent collagen cross-linking for infectious keratitis of multiple etiologies.[15] The majority of the collagen cross-linking was used in conjunction with antibiotic treatment (n = 159).[15] The population with BK showed 85.7% therapeutic success with a 14% complication rate, which was comparable to antibiotics alone at an 86% success rate.[15] One prospective, nonrandomized study analyzed the effect of collagen crosslinking in patients with BK (n = 16), the majority of which had ulceration (n = 13), that did not receive prior antibiotic exposure.[16] They found that the corneal ulcer was healed in every patient, and 2/16 patients required follow-up antibiotic therapy.[16] Collagen cross-linking is associated with complications including pain, epithelial defects, keratitis, corneal opacity, reduced visual acuity, and blurred vision.[17]

The prolonged time required for UV-A exposure is one downside involved in corneal cross-linking, in which patients are often uncomfortable due to their condition. A few novel approaches have been recently developed and have been tested largely in keratoconus treatment. A next step will be testing the potential role of these updated protocols in bacterial corneal ulcer therapy. Under one of these protocols, the accelerated protocol, the intensity setting of photoactivation are increased to reduce irradiation time to as low as 2-min.[618] Early research is looking promising for the accelerated protocol in cases with BK.[1920] Another protocol involves pulsed cross-linking, which has shown some promise in keratoconus treatment but has no current data on its potential in BK.[2122] A third alternative protocol, transepithelial cross-linking, involves cross-linking while maintaining the epithelial layer rather than removing it, such as through biochemical alteration of riboflavin or the creation of an epithelial flap.[623] This protocol also only has unclear results in keratoconus, although it is an important area for future research in both keratoconus and BK treatment.[24] At this time, conventional collagen cross-linking has relatively stronger evidence of successful treatment of both keratoconus and infectious keratitis, but transepithelial approaches remain important for future research.

Phototherapy

Various light-based therapies have been investigated for BK. Photodynamic therapy is an approach composed of three components: radiation, a photosensitizer, and oxygen.[25] Radiation of the photosensitizer leads to energy transfer to oxygen molecules and production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage bacterial membranes, proteins, and DNA with eventual cell death. Multiple photodynamic systems have been successfully tested, but the most well-studied uses Rose Bengal and green (518 nm) light.[262728] Rose Bengal has been shown to have antimicrobial activity against S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, and Acanthamoeba castellani as well as to have mild collagen cross-linking effect in in vitro studies.[282930] Several diverse cases of P. aeruginosa keratitis in patients treated with Rose Bengal photodynamic therapy as a last resort treatment have been described, with outcomes ranging from clinical resolution after 30 days to corneal scarring and partial corneal transplant.[3132] Rose Bengal has also been shown to have some efficacy in fungal and acanthamoeba keratitis, and a clinical trial is currently underway.[3334] Further modifications to photodynamic therapy have aimed to improve its efficacy. While inflammation and stromal melting have been hypothesized to facilitate sufficiently deep penetration of Rose Bengal, iontophoresis in ex vivo human corneas has been shown to improve diffusion depth to treat deeper infections.[3135] Platinum nanoparticles with catalase activity have been used to increase endogenous oxygen availability for optimal photodynamic therapy in oxygen-poor ulcers in vitro.[36] Delivery of photosensitizers has been combined with other molecules such as nitric oxide donors or dexamethasone for additional antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effects in a pre-clinical study.[37] Four hundred fifteen nm blue light has even been tested with no photosensitizer in an ex vivo rabbit and in vivo murine model, instead relying on endogenous porphyrins and flavins for ROS production, to treat keratitis, although toxicity remains a concern.[38]

Photothermal therapy is another strategy which utilizes the direct thermal energy of lasers to kill bacteria. Argon green laser (532 nm wavelength) treatment with fluorescein sodium as a chromophore can create local temperatures of 90°C and has been tested as an adjunct in patients with resistant bacterial, fungal, and viral keratitis.[3940] A small prospective study of patients with resistant, infected corneal ulcers saw complete healing of the epithelial defect after 1 month and better visual acuity results than controls treated with conventional antimicrobial agents.[39] A larger case series of 150 patients saw 82% achieve complete re-epithelialization with the laser as the sole adjunctive treatment and 100% when combined with other adjuncts.[40] A minority of patients experienced corneal microperforations and corneal thinning with descemetocele formation as adverse effects. To limit adverse effects, several other chromophores have been investigated. Fluorescein antibody complexes and ε-poly-l-lysine polydopamine have been tested in-vitro to target P. aeruginosa and methicillin-resistant S. aureus, respectively, limiting damage to uninfected tissue.[41] Further, pH-activatable organic nanoparticles and cyclic heating have enabled the use of lower temperatures, below 55°C, that still are effective in treating keratitis in vivo.[4243]

Several cases of phototherapeutic keratectomy (PTK) used to treat BK have also been documented. Often used to treat corneal erosions, dystrophies, and scars, PTK utilizes an excimer laser to superficially ablate the cornea.[44] In BK, this serves to both ablate infected tissue and kill bacteria. Although PTK has been tested as a primary treatment for BK, it more likely has a role as an adjunct to antibiotics where it can enhance the effective penetration of topical antibiotics.[4546] PTK use has been described alongside topical and systemic antibiotics in a case of acinetobacter and S. aureus keratitis.[47] It has also been used in several patients alongside antifungal and antiamoebic agents to successfully treat resistant cases of fungal and acanthamoeba keratitis, respectively.[484950] PTK does notably carry a risk of perforation and, as such is limited to superficial infections, requiring early intervention.[4445]

Immunomodulators

Beyond bacterial killing, attention has been directed toward downregulating the inflammatory response with the goal of limiting corneal tissue melt and scarring. Immune activation and inflammation are critical components of the host defense against BK, but prolonged or excessive immune activation can lead to collateral tissue damage, exacerbating vision loss.[51] New immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory treatments are being explored for infectious keratitis to balance pathogen clearance with minimizing tissue damage. For example, downregulation of the proinflammatory interleukin-1 (IL-1) and IL-17 cytokines significantly reduced neutrophil migration in murine models.[5253] These include designed antibodies and receptor antagonists, but also agents such as thrombomodulin and lithium.[545556] Combined with antibiotics, sufficient bacterial clearance can be achieved while dampening any overactive immune response to achieve improved outcomes.

Inhibition of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which degrade extracellular matrix components, can serve as antibiotic adjuncts to minimize collagen breakdown and to preserve basement membrane integrity.[5758] Tetracyclines are downregulators of MMPs, and two patient cases of adjunctive doxycycline drops have been reported in the treatment of P. aeruginosa keratitis.[596061]

Resolvins are endogenous components of the Specialized pro-resolving lipid mediator (SPM) pathway, which promote inflammation resolution.[62] Pre-clinical studies demonstrate that Resolvin D1 inhibits the production of tumor necrosis factor-α, IL-6, and IL-8 and decreases neutrophil infiltration, reduces corneal opacity and thickness, and promotes the anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage phenotype in S. aureus and P. aeruginosa keratitis.[63] Vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) is an upstream activator of the SPM pathway and has been shown to manage keratitis inflammation, upregulating not only resolvin D1 but also IL-10 and transforming growth factor-beta.[6465] Thymosin beta-4 similarly upregulates the SPM-associated enzyme 12-LOX when combined with ciprofloxacin in the eye, providing a similar benefit to resolvin D1 and VIP.[6667]

Another promising agent is glycyrrhizin, which decrease levels of extracellular high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1). HMGB1 is a late mediator of the inflammatory response, with in vitro models and in vivo murine models demonstrating a potential, direct therapeutic role as well as enhancement of tobramycin’s effectiveness.[68]

Inhibitors of IL-Iβ converting enzyme (ICE), also known as caspase-1 inhibitors, also show promise in BK treatment. IL-Iβ expression is a crucial component in Pseudomonas keratitis; caspase-1 inhibitors prevent the intracellular protease from cleaving the precursors of IL-Iβ and IL-18 into active cytokines.[69] In a murine model, improved clinical scores were seen with ICE inhibitor use with and without ciprofloxacin when compared to placebo. Treatment of mice with the ICE inhibitor also improved clinical scores after corneal infection with a clinical isolate, KEI-1025, and with a ciprofloxacin-resistant strain of P. aeruginosa.[53]

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are polypeptides between 10 and 60 amino acids long which are part of the innate immune system.[7071] Various endogenous AMPs are found in the human eye, including cathelicidins, defensins, and thymosins.[71] Several AMPs have already been approved by the Food and Drug Administration including gramicidin, daptomycin, and colistin, and further AMPs are being explored as a source of new antimicrobial agents.[72] Within ophthalmology, the application of AMPs has been limited to preclinical stages. AMPs with promising results include Shiva-11, Thiazomycin A, and Catoid.[73747576] Some AMPs have demonstrated synergy when used in conjunction with current antibiotics, such as OH-CATH30, a natural cathelicidin AMP from king cobra, and bovine lactoferricin.[777879] Cecropin A and melittin-derived AMPs demonstrated efficacy against P. aeruginosa keratitis,[80] RP444, derived from natural cecropins and magainins, and frog-derived Esc (1-21) may also play a role in the management of BK, with the latter also promoting corneal wound healing when molecularly modified.[8182] Other AMPs being studied include derivatives of CAP37, LyeTxI-b, derived from spider venom, and CaD23, derived from LL-37.[838485] While direct applications of AMPs to treat BK have been limited to preclinical stages, they have shown promise as a contact lens (CL) coating. Phase III clinical trials of Mel4-coated etafilcon A lenses demonstrated a reduction in corneal infiltrative events by 69%.[86]

Immunization is another strategy being investigated for BK. Inactivated P. aeruginosa-based vaccines have been successfully delivered nasally, orally, intraperitoneally, and topically and shown to protect against keratitis in animal models, with nasal or intraperitoneal administration appearing to be most effective.[87888990] Immunized animals have been shown to have reduced disease severity even when challenged by heterologous strains.[8890] Various antigens have also been used for immunization. Both a lipopolysaccharide flagella preparation and pili-based immunization reduced bacterial adhesion but only provided protection against homologous strains.[919293] Multifunctional antibodies with multiple targets may address this. Antibodies targeting both Psl, another P. aeruginosa exopolysaccharide, and the type 3 secretion protein PcrV can improve treatment outcomes.[94] Several antitoxoid vaccines have also been developed, including P. aeruginosa protease and elastase, S. pneumoniae pneumolysin, and S. aureus alpha toxin and Panton–Valentine leukocidin.[9596979899100101] Toxoid vaccines do not affect bacterial load, but target the bacterial toxins that are significant contributors to keratitis pathogenicity, intercellular junction disruption, cell membranes perforation, and proinflammatory cytokine secretion.[102] Future approaches might combine anti-toxoid vaccines with traditional antibiotics.[96]

Silver is another antimicrobial agent with emerging ophthalmic applications. Silver has recently seen prominent application in the setting of CL and CL cases thanks to their effectiveness against various species, including P. aeruginosa. The ProGuard™case is a silver-impregnated case with clinical trials finding that users of the ProGuardTM case had significantly less bacterial contamination than users of traditional cases.[103] Two other silver-impregnated CL cases are commercially available: the i-clean from Sauflon Pharmaceuticals (Sauflon Pharmaceuticals Ltd., London, UK) and nanocase (Marietta Vision, Marietta, GA).[104] Despite similar proposed methods of action, they demonstrated different efficacy profiles, with the i-clean being more effective against S. aureus whereas the nanocase was mildly effective against only S. maltophilia.[104] Preclinical studies on silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), which disrupt cell membrane and generate ROS, are also underway.[105] One notable study using a rabbit keratitis model found that eyedrop AgNPs exhibited greater bacterial killing and clinical efficacy than levofloxacin, and that loading of these AgNPs into CLs maintained their antimicrobial effectiveness while enabling more steady drug delivery, thereby counteracting tear dilution.[106]

Platelet-rich plasma

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has high concentrations of growth factors known to promote wound healing.[107] Although there is limited information regarding the use of PRP in infectious keratitis, it has been evaluated as a treatment for persistent epithelial defect (PED), an undesirable outcome after infectious keratitis, which can be difficult to treat. One study evaluated 28 patients with PED induced by postinfectious ulcerative conditions.[107] Eleven patients were treated with PRP, all of whom achieved complete reepithelialization with PRP alone. Seventeen of the patients were treated with autologous serum, five of whom failed to achieve complete reepithelialization and thus were not included in the comparison between the groups. The healing rates, defined as the size of the initial epithelial defect divided by the healing time, were compared and showed a significantly better healing rate in those treated with PRP.[107] Additional studies are needed to further explore PRP as a treatment.

Phosphate-based therapy

An increase in various organisms’ pathogenicity due to overexpression of virulence factors has been noted in settings of phosphate limitation, likely mediated by quorum sensing, a critical bacterial communication system.[108] P. aeruginosa has served as a model organism for much of the work on the role of phosphate therapy in decreasing virulence.[109110111] Changes have been observed in P. aeruginosa’s proteome in response to altering phosphate concentration.[108] A phosphate-containing polyethylene glycol polymer named Pi-PEG is nonmicrobicidal and has been observed to prevent transformation of P. aeruginosa to its tissue-destructive phenotype in intestinal epithelial cells and prevented anastomotic leak in a murine model.[112] Pi-PEG has also been observed to better suppress virulence pathways than supplying inorganic phosphate alone, likely explained by PEG’s interaction with epithelial membrane components that facilitates a greater degree of phosphate concentration.[112] Phosphate-based therapy holds potential to reduce organismal virulence, allowing for preservation of the host microbiome and the potential to attenuate antimicrobial-resistant organisms. Phosphate-based therapies may have applications in the management of BK keratitis, particularly where P. aeruginosa is the causative microbe.

Hyperbaric O2

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy, in which 100% oxygen is delivered in a pressurized chamber greater than atmospheric pressure, has known use in infections.[113] Typically, local hypoxia predisposes wounds to infection due to the decrease in neutrophil-mediated killing of bacteria by free radicals.[114] Hyperbaric oxygen therapy prevents this hypoxia, thus restoring this defense to kill some common bacteria. In addition, hyperbaric oxygen is bacteriostatic for Pseudomonas.[115] Two case reports have described its use as an adjunctive treatment for individuals with keratitis, one case with Pseudomonas keratitis associated with CL use and the other case with keratitis of undetermined cause.[116117] There have been many uses reported for the use of hyperbaric oxygen therapy in ophthalmic conditions as an adjunctive or salvage therapy. Two case reports have described its use as an adjunctive treatment for individuals with Pseudomonas keratitis associated with CL use.[116117] Both cases involved many confounding variables, such as concurrent treatment with various antibiotics, yet shed a light on the potential benefit of hyperbaric oxygen and the need for proper clinical studies. There are few reported ocular side effects, with myopia being the most common due to the direct toxic effect of oxygen on the lens. It is often reversible, but that is not always the case.[118]

Topical steroids

Steroids can be used as a tool to inhibit immune-mediated damage in a variety of disease states, and their use has historically been somewhat controversial in the treatment of infectious keratitis. The steroids for corneal ulcer trial (SCUT) was designed to address this uncertainty in 2012.[119] The trial found that steroids demonstrated some benefit for ulcers with certain characteristics, such as being centrally located, large, deep, or infected with bacterial organisms other than Nocardia.[22] A 12-month follow-up study of SCUT patients found that steroids may be associated with better clinical outcomes in non-Nocardia ulcers.[120] Steroids were associated with the same size scar in non-Nocardia ulcers but a larger scar size in Nocardia-positive ulcers.

With regards to concerns that steroids may delay epithelial defect healing, SCUT found that aside from a higher proportion of steroid arm patients having a PED at the 21-day mark, there was no observed difference in healing rates between study arms at 3 months.[119] There was notably no increased risk of perforation and requiring penetrating keratoplasty in the steroid arm, as previous observations had suggested.[119] Further clinical trials are needed to better establish the role of steroids in BK treatment.[121] Beyond optimizing the use of antibiotics to target causative pathogens, the future of BK treatment may involve adjunctive treatments that target the inflammatory response and modulate wound healing.

Amniotic membrane transplant

Amniotic membrane transplantation (AMT) has become more frequently used for ocular surface diseases, including infectious keratitis. The amniotic membrane, which is the innermost layer of the placenta, has been shown to exhibit antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and anti-angiogenic properties.[122123] These properties may be beneficial in the treatment of infectious keratitis as an adjuvant treatment. A systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical studies in the literature examined the effectiveness and safety of AMT as an adjuvant treatment for infectious keratitis.[124] In this study, which included 28 studies and 861 eyes, adjuvant AMT resulted in improved outcomes compared to standard antimicrobial treatment. Those who underwent adjuvant AMT had significantly shorter mean time to complete corneal healing and better uncorrected distance visual acuity at 1 month follow-up. There were no significant differences in the risk of adverse events when comparing the groups.[124]

The addition of topical corticosteroids to AMT as an adjuvant treatment has also been examined. In a prospective case series of twelve patients with severe BK, each patient was treated with immediate topical antibiotics followed by AMT at 48 h and topical steroid treatment at 72 h.[125] Ten patients received a second AMT due to epithelial healing not being achieved with only one AMT. All patients reported improvement in pain, with median pain at patient’s admission being an eight on the NRS-11 pain scale. On day three, after the first AMT procedure, median pain reported by patients was two. In addition to improved pain scores from all patients after AMT, epithelial healing was achieved by all patients after a mean of 25.5 days. No perforation or neovascularization was observed in any of the eyes.[125]

Corneal transplantation

Corneal transplantation, or keratoplasty, has been employed for corneal ulcers as rescue therapy when medical treatment has failed. Innovation in the field has transformed full-thickness, or penetrating, keratoplasty into partial-thickness (lamellar) procedures, including deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty.[126] In recent years, ophthalmologists have been moving toward lamellar keratoplasty for various indications.[127128] However, there is still a place for penetrating keratoplasty, and the surgical choice should consider severity, location, etiology, and patient characteristics when developing the surgical plan.

Penetrating keratoplasty has been described for bacterial corneal ulcers refractory to aggressive antibiotic therapy. Surgical debridement removes infected and inflammatory tissue, allowing the host to clear the remaining infection.[129] Penetrating keratoplasty may be preferred for deep, central ulcers, ulcers of larger size, and/or cases of descemetocele or perforation.[130] Some studies have shown the high therapeutic effect of penetrating keratoplasty in complicated infectious corneal ulcers; however, this procedure also has a high incidence of postoperative complications.[131] Reports are mostly retrospective studies with small sample sizes and a wide variety of ulcer etiologies. For example, in a study from Tabriz Nikookari Eye Hospital, 33 patients, 15 with BK, achieved 83% therapeutic success after 1 year of postsurgery follow-up.[132] Another retrospective study looking at 43 perforated infectious corneal ulcers, including 30 with bacterial infections, showed 97% therapeutic success, with 71% graft transparency at 2-year follow-up.[131] Similarly, a study of 24 patients, 5 with bacterial ulcers, showed an impressive 100% therapeutic success for bacterial etiologies without recurrence.[133] While these studies highlight the potential efficacy of penetrating transplant, each study also reported significant posttransplant complications, including new or recurrent ulcers, wound gape, graft rejection, endothelial cell loss, glaucoma, uveitis, cataract, phthisis bulbi, and others.[129131134135136137]

Lamellar keratoplasty has lower rates of immunologic rejection compared to penetrating keratoplasty.[138139] In a retrospective, comparative study between penetrating keratoplasty and deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty, the rate of infection recurrence was comparable (12% vs. 15.3%, respectively), whereas graft survival was higher in the lamellar approach (78.4% vs. 90%).[139] While there may be decreased risk of postoperative complications with lamellar versus penetrating keratoplasty, the lamellar techniques may be require a higher level of technical proficiency in some cases.[139140] With appropriate expertise, lamellar keratoplasty may be a viable option even for perforated bacterial corneal ulcers.[141] Many approaches to therapeutic lamellar keratoplasty in the context of bacterial corneal ulcers may be performed. Deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty aims to remove as much corneal stroma as possible, while leaving behind an intact Descemet’s membrane.[126139142] One of the most important complications of deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty remains perforation of the Descemet’s membrane.[143] Recently, a small study demonstrated the efficacy of lamellar keratoplasty utilizing acellular porcine corneal stroma in treating infectious corneal ulcers, providing a potential alternative to donor tissue in areas without access to corneal tissue banks.[144]

Conjunctival flaps, patch grafts, and tissue adhesives

In the absence of donor tissue, there exist several other surgical options for the treatment of bacterial corneal ulcers. Conjunctival flap surgery is performed by mobilizing and suturing conjunctival tissue to overlay the ulcer.[145] This technique is a simple and affordable way to stabilize the cornea, decrease inflammatory response, and provide pain relief.[145146] It can be effective, with some studies showing up to 94% therapeutic cure.[146] However, there can be a high rate of repeat surgeries and complications.[146147] If a full keratoplasty cannot be performed, an infectious perforated corneal ulcer can also be treated with a corneal patch graft in which only the ulcerated area is repaired.[148] This can be a good alternative technique in an urgent perforation. Another option for bacterial corneal ulcer management is tissue adhesives. These adhesives are synthetic or naturally occurring compounds that can be used in surgical wound closure, composed of either cyanoacrylate or fibrin glue.[149] Cyanoacrylate derivatives help rapidly seal corneal perforations < 3 mm in size and are useful in preventing an imminent perforation from occurring.[149] Fibrin glue is a biological adhesive that provides another easy way to seal off a corneal ulcer, and can be effective in defects >3 mm in size.[149] While this is a simple, affordable procedure to seal an infectious ulcer, they have no therapeutic application for the bacterial infection itself. These techniques should be used with caution as they may worsen inflammation, slow healing, and create new infection.[149150]

CONCLUSION

Many researchers are contributing to the establishment of new approaches to BK treatment, where options exist aside from traditional antibiotics.[20] Nonantibiotic treatments and adjuncts have emerged in recent years include collagen cross-linking, phototherapy, immunomodulators such as anti-MMPs, PRP, AMPs, hyperbaric oxygen, and more. Many of these are currently investigational or adjunctive treatments alongside antibiotic management. Compared to the other nonantibiotic adjuncts discussed, collagen cross-linking and photodynamic therapy have the strongest body of supportive clinical evidence. Transplantation continues to serve as an option in challenging cases of BK. This review elaborates on the evidence behind many of these emerging treatments and how they can complement or even replace existing treatments and reduce complications of BK. Treatments that modulate the immune response also may address the critical issue of corneal melt and perforation as a complication of BK even when broad-spectrum, powerful antibiotics are used. Finally, this review calls on researchers to further explore nonantibiotic approaches to infectious keratitis management and consider methods that attenuate the host-pathogen interaction.
