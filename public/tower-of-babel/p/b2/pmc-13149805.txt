Avoidance of rejuvenation: a stress test for evolutionary theories of aging
Authors: Samir I Aisin, Boris V Lidskii, Peter V Lidsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe biological feasibility of human rejuvenation remains a subject of intense debate, yet answering this question is critical for guiding research strategies. Should aging research focus only on reversing aging in older individuals, or pausing its progression at mid-ages, be more accessible? Here, we attempt to address this question with evolutionary biology. Rejuvenation occurs in a few species, and, paradoxically, is typically induced by stress but not used under optimal conditions. Using mathematical modeling of lifespan plasticity in eusocial insects that can rejuvenate, we show that avoidance of rejuvenation is poorly explained by classic theories of aging in their standard formulations, requiring substantial assumptions to fit them. By contrast, one of the programmed theories of aging–the pathogen control hypothesis–offers a consistent evolutionary framework for understanding avoidance of rejuvenation. Moreover, our model mathematically predicts the evolution of the genetic program executing lifespan termination – adaptive aging mechanisms.Subject terms: Developmental biology, Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Systems biology

Introduction

Physiological rejuvenation is one of the key objectives of biogerontological research. Recent advances in partial cellular reprogramming and regenerative biology have fueled optimism that physiological rejuvenation may be achievable. Yet whether rejuvenation is fundamentally possible remains deeply contested. Some authors consider systemic rejuvenation within the long-term reach1, while others claim it is theoretically impossible2. The debate is complicated by the absence of universally accepted definitions of “aging” and “biological age,” and by widely diverging opinions on aging’s primary causes and mechanisms3,4. However, understanding the feasibility of rejuvenation is critical for informing experimental research: should efforts focus on rejuvenating old organisms, or is slowing aging progression in middle-aged individuals a more accessible goal?

To evaluate the feasibility of physiological rejuvenation, we use evolutionary approaches. First, we assess the known examples of rejuvenation, analyze the ecological context that may trigger animals to rejuvenate, and use mathematical modeling to reconcile these observations with different evolutionary hypotheses. Our goal is to resolve a central paradox: if rejuvenation is mechanistically possible, why has evolution not widely adopted it as a strategy to extend lifespan and enhance fitness?

We use the following definitions. Living organisms possess an intrinsic characteristic, known as biological age, that gradually increases over time through a process called aging. Aging has a negative impact on an organism’s health, ultimately leading to its death. Changing the tempo of aging in response to the intrinsic or extrinsic stimuli is termed aging plasticity. Plasticity includes a faster increase in biological age, or accelerated aging, e.g., in response to inflammation5,6 or mating7, and, oppositely, slower or decelerated aging, e.g., in response to dietary restriction8. Rejuvenation is a specific type of aging plasticity manifested in decreasing biological age over time. Rejuvenation can be comprehensive, meaning that all physiological parameters are reverted, and the organism becomes indistinguishable from its younger self. Alternatively, rejuvenation can be partial, meaning that only some age-associated physiological parameters are reversed. Evidence for rejuvenation can be divided into two types: reverse development, when an animal metamorphoses into an earlier developmental stage, and adult rejuvenation, when the functions of an adult individual revert to a more youthful state. Rejuvenation should not be conflated with negative senescence, defined as a decrease in all-cause mortality with age upon sexual maturity9. In most cases, negative senescence is observed in organisms that grow after reaching sexual maturity. In these cases, the decrease in extrinsic hazard due to larger body size can offset the increasing intrinsic mortality caused by aging. Thus, negative senescence is an effect, similar to the decline in juvenile mortality that extends into the post-maturation period. Unlike rejuvenation, negative senescence does not require physiological reversal of age in cells and tissues.

Several examples of animal rejuvenation were described. Rejuvenation was reported to occur ubiquitously during development: germline cells acquire molecular signatures of aging similarly to somatic cells10; however, these signatures are erased in early embryogenesis, e.g., during gastrulation in mammals11. Thus, the data suggest that early rejuvenation is universal and possible; only the feasibility of late-stage rejuvenation remains in question.

The immortal jellyfish (Turritopsis dohrnii) is the most renowned example of reverse development in animals. The typical jellyfish lifecycle includes an attached polyp stage that later transforms into a free-swimming medusoid that reproduces and dies. It was shown that under starvation, suboptimal temperature, or chemical stress, medusoids can revert into polyps and escape death this way12. Recently, a similar ability to reverse development in response to stress was discovered in the ctenophore, Mnemiopsis leidyi13. Observations made in these very distant species indicate the conserved pattern: although rejuvenation is mechanistically feasible, paradoxically, animals resort to it only when under stress.

Rarely, reverse development can be found outside the context of stress. In some termite species (Archotermopsidae and Kalotermitidae families), later larvae can reverse develop into earlier larvae under optimal conditions during so-called regressive molts14,15.

Evidence for rejuvenation without metamorphosis is scarce. In vertebrates, the regeneration of limbs in axolotls is accompanied by decreased tissue biological age as judged by methylation clocks16. Additional evidence supporting the feasibility of tissue rejuvenation comes from laboratory experiments using Yamanaka factor-mediated partial reprogramming17,18 and perfusions with young plasma19 in mice and rats. These interventions result in the reversion of multiple physiological parameters to their more youthful states. Overall, these findings are consistent with the possibility of at least partial physiological rejuvenation in mammals, within the limits imposed by the current methodology.

While encouraging, these results pose a fundamental paradox: if rejuvenation is mechanistically possible, why did organisms not evolve to activate it whenever they reach old age, and instead prefer to die? Why did evolution not employ rejuvenation to increase older organisms’ fitness? Since evolution universally probes all available opportunities, the rarity of rejuvenation in nature must indicate its mechanistic impossibility. However, the abovementioned examples of cnidarians, ctenophores, and termites fail to fit this general model.

The paradoxical avoidance of physiologically available longer lifespan can be observed in species capable of switching to an increased longevity trajectory in response to environmental stimuli, such as caloric restriction8 or reproductive activation (see below). It remains unclear what constraints limit the evolutionary benefits of living long and favor shorter lifespans regardless of a longevity trajectory available. Resolving this contradiction is critical for instructing future research aimed at developing anti-aging and rejuvenating therapies. Specifically, solving this problem may help us test and refine existing theories of aging and assist in the search for a unifying paradigm currently missing from the field3,4.

The most striking examples of lifespan plasticity are found in eusocial animals. Eusociality is a colonial social structure characterized by the division of labor between breeder and non-breeder individuals. Different castes have different aging rates: breeders age more slowly than non-breeders in most species of eusocial insects and mole-rats (families Bathyergidae and Heterocephalidae). In mole-rats, a non-breeder can undergo reproductive activation and become a breeder. In this case, it ages at a slower pace20,21. Similar phenotypes are observed in some ant species: if the queen dies, several workers in the colony may undergo reproductive activation and become “gamergates,” pseudo-queens that can live approximately five times longer than unperturbed sterile workers. Gamergates can also revert to a worker caste and regain faster aging22,23. These essential findings imply that the same genetic information can result in different aging trajectories and the existence of a master regulator that can switch between them. However, the existence of such a master regulator does not prove rejuvenation. These phenotypes can be rationalized by pausing aging, a state in which biological age increments very slowly but does not reverse.

An example, mostly resembling physiological rejuvenation in adults, can be observed in honeybees (Apis mellifera). After leaving the pupae, the honeybee is committed to working as a nurse, cleaning the nest and feeding larvae and the queen. Nurses do not leave the colony. After some weeks, nurses transition to foraging and begin collecting nectar and pollen outside the hive. This job transition is associated with a dramatic shift in the honeybee physiology: the protein storage decreases, specifically the amounts of a regulatory and storage protein vitellogenin; the levels of juvenile hormone increase, inducing the immune cells (hemocytes) to die via pycnosis24. Foragers work for a few more weeks, deteriorate, and die because of extrinsic reasons or aging. However, if the number of nurses declines due to a predator attack or experimental manipulation, some foragers must return to the nursing job. In this case, they undergo a physiological rejuvenation: protein storage and vitellogenin levels increase, the number of hemocytes is restored, and the mortality decreases. Transcriptomics and methylomics profiles also revert to a more youthful, nursing stage25,26.

Like ants and mole rats, honeybees can also extend their lifespans through reproductive activation. Upon the queen’s death, some workers become laying workers, long-lived, fertile isoforms27.

Although it remains unclear whether honeybee rejuvenation is comprehensive or partial, it is evident that adult animals have some potential to reverse physiological decline and extend lifespan. Nevertheless, as in cases with jellyfish and ctenophores, honeybees and most other eusocial animals resort to rejuvenation or lifespan extension only under sub-optimal conditions, e.g., if queens or nurses die. But why don’t they utilize the existing capability to live longer if not under stress? One of the traditional takes is that foragers underinvest in their maintenance due to high extrinsic mortality. If an animal will not survive to a late age, underinvestment might be a good energy-saving strategy. However, regardless of high extrinsic mortality, aging was shown to account for about 7% of foragers’ deaths28. Hence, the switch to a more long-lived trajectory during the normal lifecycle must save a fraction of foragers and therefore be evolutionarily beneficial, as it would save energy and increase the hive’s fitness. Why aren’t the existing lifespan plasticity mechanisms utilized in these cases?

To answer this question, we use mathematical modeling to rationalize these observations in the context of different theories of aging. We limited our focus to eusocial communities not only because rejuvenation is often found in eusocial species, but also because the lack of replication in workers allows us to avoid modeling the exponential population growth that can complicate and confuse the study. Additionally, we assume that the colony’s high worker-production rate and ample resource base effectively decouple energy allocation from time: resources can be ‘borrowed’ across periods without constraint. As a result, the precise timing of energetic investment becomes irrelevant, making accounting for development timing and other scaling parameters29 redundant and allowing us to focus on modeling the colony’s steady state instead.

We examined several evolutionary models of aging – the non-programmed theories: (i) damage accumulation, stating that aging is an entropic process driven by accumulating chemical modifications30–34; (ii) antagonistic pleiotropy35 assuming that aging is a byproduct of the late detrimental effects of the genes with early beneficial effect; and (iii) disposable soma36 that explains aging as a result of overinvestment in reproduction at the expense of repair and part of the strategy of energy optimization. We found that these models cannot explain the avoidance of rejuvenation and longevity without introducing additional assumptions that would forbid lifespan plasticity where it does not happen, and allow it where it happens.

Then we considered the programmed models of aging that suggest the existence of an ecological optimum for lifespan. The evolutionary reasons determining this optimum vary depending on the theory. There are three main directions of thought: preventing overpopulation37,38, acceleration of evolution39–41, and pathogen control42–46. The first two classes of hypotheses do not seem relevant to the determination of eusocial worker lifespan: overpopulation can be prevented by reducing the production of new animals, while the speed of evolution is mainly affected by the lifespan of fertile queens, not sterile workers. However, we found that the pathogen control hypothesis fits the data well and reconciles it with evolutionary theory.

Hence, if the model allows the investment in maintenance to vary with time, young honeybees would underinvest in their maintenance to conserve energy when in large numbers. Later, the few individuals lucky enough to survive to older ages will allocate more resources to repairing damage and preventing deaths. According to this calculation, death by aging should never be observed in honeybees or any other species with existing mechanisms of aging plasticity. If energy is the currency that defines life history, and if a mechanism for prolonging lifespan exists, such a mechanism should be activated every time an individual reaches old age. This prediction contradicts the empirical data: aging is observed in honeybees28, while cases of increased investment in repair and longevity in older organisms have never been demonstrated to the best of our knowledge.

This disconnection indicates a conceptual gap between the classic evolutionary theories of aging and data on lifespan plasticity. Critically, our results demonstrate that not only rejuvenation (reached when M>D; biological age decreases) but also the pausing of aging (M=D) cannot fit these theories in their classic formulation. While physiological rejuvenation is a rare phenotype found only in a few species, pausing aging of different types is widespread, significantly expanding the ecological significance of our findings49. As such, a different theory is needed to explain phenomena.

Model III

Next, we tested whether evolutionary avoidance of prolonged lifespan and rejuvenation can be rationalized by a programmed aging model, specifically the pathogen control hypothesis. According to this model, aging evolved not as a side effect of underinvestment in repair but as an adaptive program that limits the transmission of chronic infectious diseases42.

To test this opportunity, we introduced a chronic pathogen that can spread between bees and makes foragers stop working. Such effects of the parasites on worker behaviors were described in ants50,51, honeybees52, and eusocial wasps53. Basically, parasites reprogram host behavior to maximize their spread. In two cases50,53, the unproductive infected workers did not live shorter than the unparasitized ones. Moreover, metabolic levels of infected ants were not different from those of healthy ones50, suggesting they consume comparable amounts of food. For simplicity of calculations, we modeled the epidemics only among foragers without taking nurses and queens into account. The number of healthy foragers susceptible to infection was designated as NS, while the number of infected ones – as NI. The number of foragers infected daily was NS·NI·β, where β was the pathogen transmission efficiency. We tested a range of β values, assuming that within the wide variety of pathogens in the environment, there can be found one or several agents with desired characteristics. No recovery from infection was assumed.

Therefore, limiting lifespan under the pathogen pressure may be an evolutionarily advantageous strategy. Moreover, even if we assume that damage D is not sufficient to mediate honeybee death at the optimal age of X even with M = 0, the model predicts that the negative value of M to become evolutionarily advantageous, ensuring the timely death of a forager. The negative values of M can be interpreted as active killing mechanisms that would increase damage and accelerate aging. Thus, the presence of chronic debilitating pathogens may favor the evolution of earlier death and even the emergence of self-damaging mechanisms for its realization. On the contrary, the activation of rejuvenation or pausing aging may pose epidemiological risks and must be avoided.

Discussion

Recent advances in cellular and tissue rejuvenation are remarkable and exciting. However, here we demonstrated that an optimistic interpretation of these experiments contradicts the classic theories of aging.

Selection shadow and damage accumulation consider aging as a detrimental effect that affects fitness too little to be removed by evolution. The so-called “buy now, pay later” models, antagonistic pleiotropy, and disposable soma view aging as an adverse side effect of some positive traits that improve reproduction in the youth35,54,55. The advantages an organism receives at the expense of aging could be increased fecundity (irrelevant for sterile workers in eusocial species) or energy saving (see Models I and II).

While these hypotheses are remarkably different, they converge on the point that aging is detrimental to fitness and predict that if an animal can rejuvenate or pause aging, utilizing this opportunity must be evolutionarily advantageous. Why, then, does the ability to reverse or pause aging not evolve ubiquitously? The classic answer would be that rejuvenation may be a complex or energetically expensive mechanism, and there is insufficient selective pressure to develop it, e.g., because cases where an old animal accesses a rich food source are too scarce. Indeed, if we accept the premise, we can explain the scarcity of rejuvenation.

However, eusocial insects pose a fundamental challenge to this logic. Workers in an eusocial community are analogous to the somatic tissue in a multicellular organism. Workers’ contribution to reproductive fitness is directly linked to the tasks they perform, rather than investment in childbirth, and as such, what can be the benefit acquired according to the “buy now, pay later” model? Additionally, members of the eusocial community have constant access to an abundant food source. Why doesn’t evolution favor long-lived workers? Is it impossible to make them more durable?

Observations suggest the opposite: aging is exceptionally plastic in eusocial animals. Queens in many species live 10 to 50 times longer than workers. While the rejuvenation exhibited in honeybees is remarkable, it remains unclear whether it is comprehensive or partial, and to what extent it increases the lifespan. Nevertheless, data from other eusocial insects indicate that workers’ lifespan can be extended enormously: the worker to gamergate transition in ants Harpegnathos saltator increases the lifespan ~4 times22. Tapeworm infection in ants, Temnothorax nylanderi, was estimated to increase lifespan more than ten times50. As the mechanisms underlying a longevity trajectory are already evolved, it remains unclear why they are used only under stress applied to the colony.

Let’s examine the remaining argument that extending workers’ lifespan is energetically disadvantageous. If a worker dies, the colony must produce a new worker to replace the old one. Could this be an energy-saving strategy? During development, a honeybee larva consumes ~160 mg of pollen, while the forager’s daily ration is ~4 mg56,57. Could the cost of the worker rejuvenation or pausing aging exceed 160 mg of pollen? It does not seem possible just due to the adult honeybee’s digestive capacity. The metabolic levels of the long-lived, parasitized ants do not indicate high energy costs associated with longevity50. Moreover, such high costs of rejuvenation contradict the laws of conservation, as replacing a proportion of damaged cells and proteins should always be cheaper than creating a new organism from scratch. Of course, the introduction of some non-additive factors that make rejuvenation unreasonably expensive can reconcile classic theories with the Model II. However, there are no empirical or theoretical indications that rejuvenation or other forms of lifespan elongation can be so costly. Other traditional arguments may include some fluctuations in levels of damage that may exceed the levels of repair, fundamental limits to lifespan, rare irreparable errors, slow timing of repair processes, etc. However, these attempts to explain the impossibility of longevity fail in the cases when longevity trajectories are already in place, are expected to be evolutionary beneficial, but for some reason, are not activated. This contradiction can perhaps be resolved by introducing some other hypothetical factors that prevent rejuvenation or pausing lifespan in cases where they are not found, and allow them when they are. However, given the large number of diverse examples of lifespan plasticity, we cannot easily hypothesize a single factor that could reconcile all the observed phenomena with classic theories.

Therefore, Model II identifies an inconsistency between the “buy now, pay later” framework in its classic formulation and the evidence for rejuvenation in nature.

It should be emphasized that our modeling concerned non-reproductive members of the eusocial colony. This social structure, in many instances, benefits the self-sacrifice of non-breeders58, who are functionally equivalent to somatic tissue and can be disposed of, much like skin or gut cells in the mammalian body. However, according to our calculations, even such dispensable animals must prefer a longer lifespan trajectory if it is mechanistically possible. The effect in solitary animals is expected to be even stronger.

Finally, with Model III, we found that the pathogen control hypothesis provides a good fit to the observed phenomena without introducing extreme assumptions. An epidemic of a chronic pathogen may impose selective pressure that favors shorter lifespans in foragers. As the probability of being infected increases with age, the death of aging may help remove older and potentially infected individuals. Pathogens that alter workers’ behavior, causing them to stop benefiting the hive and instead promote parasites’ reproduction, have been described across multiple species of eusocial insects50–53. Rejuvenation or pausing aging in such infected animals would not be evolutionary beneficial for the host, as it would exacerbate epidemics. Therefore, rejuvenation or pausing aging should be avoided unless necessary to overcome other environmental challenges, such as the death of queens, nurses, or suboptimal temperature.

Moreover, if biochemical damage received by an animal is insufficient to kill it by the optimal time, the model predicts the evolution of killer mechanisms that ensure optimal lifespan. In this case, the evolution of adaptive death is mathematically predicted by our model.

The pathogen control hypothesis posits aging as a programmed adaptation that evolved to control epidemics. Consistently, some chronic pathogens are expected to inhibit aging, thereby extending the host’s lifespan and infectious period. Such parasites of eusocial insects were mentioned above50,53. Other parasites have been shown to reduce mortality and prolong lifespan in solitary insects59, fish60, molluscs61, and even humans62. However, the extent of host lifespan extension is much smaller than the tapeworm-mediated lifespan extension in ants T. nylanderi50, and is apparently constrained by species-specific maximum lifespan. It is plausible that pathogens do not introduce entirely new host lifespan-prolonging functions, but rather hijack the intrinsic aging plasticity mechanisms. If this hypothesis is correct, the presence of such mechanisms in an animal may render it vulnerable to a potential breach in pathogen defense. Hence, aging plasticity mechanisms, including rejuvenation, should not evolve unless they provide substantial selective advantages as in eusocial species. Moreover, if such mechanisms eventually become redundant, they must be eliminated by evolution to remove a potential weakness in anti-pathogen defense. This logic may explain why rejuvenation is scarce, despite being mechanistically possible: pathogen control determines avoidance of rejuvenation at the level of an individual life-history and at the level of adaptive evolution.

The progress in developing rejuvenation therapies requires a solid theoretical basis. Here, we formally demonstrate that neutral aging (damage accumulation and selection shadow) and “buy now, pay later” (antagonistic pleiotropy and disposable soma) models in their classic formulations imply that rejuvenation is mechanistically impossible. Therefore, approaches striving to achieve rejuvenation based on these models contain internal contradictions. Alternative models, such as the pathogen control hypothesis42–44 or substantial modifications to the classic theories, are required to reconcile evolutionary theory with rejuvenation. Detailed investigations of natural examples of rejuvenation are essential not only to inform the field about its feasibility, but even more importantly, to advance and refine the evolutionary theory of aging.
