Focus on lipoprotein(a)
Authors: Marta Biolo, Camilla Portinari, Camilla Goffi, Riccardo Berti, Sandra Bertocco, Lorenzo Previato, Sabina Zambon, Paolo Simioni, Alberto Zambon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a causal and independent cardiovascular risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and calcific aortic valve stenosis, with elevated levels observed in approximately 20% of the general population. Plasma Lp(a) concentrations are predominantly determined by genetic factors, particularly polymorphisms of the LPA gene, which modulate apolipoprotein(a) [apo(a)] size and influence its hepatic synthesis. Structurally, Lp(a) consists of an LDL-like particle containing apolipoprotein B100, covalently bound to apo(a), conferring markedly enhanced atherogenic, pro-inflammatory, and prothrombotic properties compared with an LDL particle. In the absence of therapies specifically approved for Lp(a) reduction, current clinical management relies on an intensive approach to global cardiovascular risk reduction. ESC/EAS guidelines recommend aggressive lowering of LDL cholesterol, particularly through high-intensity statin therapy and, in patients at high or very high risk, the addition of ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibitors, in order to mitigate the residual risk associated with elevated Lp(a) levels. Lipoprotein apheresis represents the only intervention capable of producing a substantial and immediate reduction in Lp(a); however, its use is limited to selected patients due to its invasive nature and limited availability. In parallel, innovative therapies targeting LPA gene silencing—such as antisense oligonucleotides and small interfering RNA—as well as oral agents inhibiting Lp(a) assembly, are in advanced stages of development. These approaches have demonstrated Lp(a) reductions of up to 80–90%, offering concrete prospects for a causal therapeutic strategy, pending the results of cardiovascular outcome trials.Keywords: Lipoprotein(a), Cardiovascular disease, ASO, siRNA

Structure and atherogenicity of lipoprotein(a)

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD). The association between Lp(a) and major cardiovascular events has been demonstrated in numerous epidemiological, genetic, and Mendelian randomization studies.1

From an epidemiological perspective, approximately 20% of the global population—equivalent to around 1.5 billion individuals—exhibits Lp(a) concentrations considered elevated (≥100–125 nmol/L, corresponding to approximately 50 mg/dL).2 More than 90% of the variability in Lp(a) concentrations is genetically determined, with minimal influence from lifestyle factors.3 However, increased levels have been observed in chronic kidney disease, particularly in patients with nephrotic syndrome or undergoing dialysis, whereas severe liver disease is associated with reduced Lp(a) concentrations. Mean Lp(a) levels are higher in women than in men (approximately 5–10%) and tend to increase after menopause, while remaining relatively stable in males. Despite substantial differences in absolute Lp(a) concentrations among ethnic groups—higher in individuals of African ancestry and lower in European and East Asian populations—prospective studies have demonstrated that the association between elevated Lp(a) and ASCVD risk is largely consistent across ethnicities, supporting the use of shared clinical thresholds and the rationale for universal screening.3

Lp(a) is synthesized by hepatocytes and consists of an LDL-like particle containing a single molecule of apolipoprotein B100 (Apo B100), covalently bound to apolipoprotein(a) [apo(a)] via a single disulphide bond.4 The LPA gene, located on chromosome 6q27, is highly polymorphic and determines apo(a) size, defined by the number of kringle IV (KIV) domain repeats. These triple-loop protein domains, stabilized by disulphide bridges, account for more than 40% of the variability in Lp(a) plasma concentrations. An inverse relationship exists between apo(a) size and circulating Lp(a) levels: smaller apo(a) isoforms are associated with higher plasma Lp(a) concentrations. This phenomenon appears to reflect reduced efficiency of hepatic synthesis and secretion of larger apo(a) isoforms, which are more prone to intracellular degradation.5

Apo(a) exhibits marked structural homology with plasminogen, sharing kringle IV and V domains as well as an inactive protease domain. Unlike plasminogen, apo(a) contains ten kringle IV subtypes, with wide variability in the number of copies of the KIV-2 subtype (ranging from 2 to over 40), which accounts for the structural and functional heterogeneity of Lp(a).5 In addition to KIV-2 repeat number, specific single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the LPA gene (e.g. rs10455872, rs37982209), typically associated with smaller apo(a) isoforms, significantly modulate plasma Lp(a) concentrations and cardiovascular risk.5

Lp(a) exhibits an atherogenic potential approximately sixfold greater than that of LDL when normalized per individual lipoprotein particle, suggesting that the pathogenic mechanisms through which Lp(a) contributes to cardiovascular disease cannot be explained solely by its structural similarity to LDL.6

The pathogenic role of Lp(a) has traditionally been attributed to multiple pathophysiological mechanisms, including atherogenesis, thrombosis, endothelial dysfunction, valvular calcification, and modulation of the immune response. Lp(a) accumulates at sites of endothelial injury within the vascular wall and the aortic valve leaflets, where it undergoes oxidative modifications and transports large amounts of oxidized phospholipids (OxPL). These processes amplify local inflammation and promote the osteogenic differentiation of valvular interstitial cells.1,7

In parallel, the apo(a) component, which is structurally homologous to plasminogen, has been suggested to compete with plasminogen for fibrin binding and to be associated with increased expression of plasminogen activator inhibitor type 1 (PAI-1), resulting in impaired fibrinolysis. These antifibrinolytic effects represent a potential pathogenic link between atherosclerosis, calcific aortic valve stenosis, and thrombosis. Moreover, preclinical and experimental studies indicate tha Lp(a) may influence the expression of tissue factor and inhibiting the activity of its principal regulator, tissue factor pathway inhibitor, thereby contributing to an increased thrombotic risk.8

Oxidized phospholipids (OxPL) constitute a key determinant of Lp(a) pathogenicity. By binding to apo(a), OxPL enhance the pro-atherogenic, pro-inflammatory, and prothrombotic properties of Lp(a) through activation of endothelial cells, immune cells, and platelets. Levels of OxPL associated with Lp(a) correlate more strongly with cardiovascular risk than Lp(a) concentration alone or LDL cholesterol levels, suggesting that OxPL burden may represent a central functional determinant of Lp(a)-mediated atherothrombotic risk.9

Lipoprotein(a) in the most recent guidelines

The most recent European Society of Cardiology and European Atherosclerosis Society (ESC/EAS) guidelines on dyslipidaemias recommend measuring lipoprotein(a) at least once during a lifetime, recognizing concentrations ≥105 nmol/L (≈50 mg/dL) as a threshold associated with a clinically significant increase in cardiovascular risk. Lp(a) is now considered a true global risk multiplier, capable of substantially increasing the absolute risk of cardiovascular events, particularly in individuals with intermediate or high baseline cardiovascular risk. In this context, Lp(a) plays a dual role: on the one hand, as an independent prognostic biomarker enabling more accurate risk stratification; on the other, as a potential specific therapeutic target. This perspective is further reinforced by the development of novel therapies selectively targeting Lp(a) reduction, whose effects on cardiovascular outcomes are currently being evaluated in advanced-phase clinical trials.10

Elevated Lp(a) levels and their clinical significance

It is now well established that the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) increases in a continuous and proportional manner with rising Lp(a) levels.10

Although the inclusion of Lp(a) has not yet conclusively demonstrated to improve the predictive performance of currently recommended risk scores, such as SCORE2 and SCORE2-OP, emerging evidence suggests incremental value in specific clinical settings. In particular, an observational study involving 8720 individuals, designed to assess the contribution of Lp(a) to the prediction of first myocardial infarction beyond conventional risk factors, showed that Lp(a) concentrations >47 mg/dL enabled correct risk reclassification in 23% of first myocardial infarction events.11

Extensive epidemiological evidence derived from population-based cohorts in the United Kingdom, Denmark, and the United States indicates that, in terms of relative risk, elevated Lp(a) levels are strongly associated with the development of myocardial infarction and calcific aortic valve stenosis, and to a lesser—but still statistically significant—extent with peripheral arterial disease, heart failure, and ischaemic stroke.3

In particular, for myocardial infarction, aortic valve stenosis, and peripheral arterial disease, individuals with Lp(a) levels above the 95th percentile exhibit a relative risk up to 1.7–3-fold higher compared with those with lower concentrations. Elevated lipoprotein(a) levels are also associated with an increased risk of recurrent cardiovascular events, as demonstrated by numerous observational studies and prospective analyses.12

In a prospective cohort study conducted in a population without a prior history of coronary artery disease, who underwent serial coronary computed tomography angiography over a follow-up period of approximately 10 years, elevated Lp(a) levels were associated with a significant increase in per cent plaque volume, independently of other cardiovascular risk factors. Moreover, high Lp(a) concentrations correlated with the presence of non-calcified (low-density) plaques and with markers of pericoronary adipose tissue inflammation, suggesting a role for Lp(a) in promoting progression of coronary atherosclerotic burden towards a vulnerable plaque phenotype, potentially unstable over the long term.13

The association between elevated Lp(a) and ASCVD risk appears to be independent of other major cardiovascular risk factors, including LDL cholesterol (LDL-C), non-HDL cholesterol (non-HDL-C), apo B100, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), as well as independent of statin therapy use.14 This independence is consistent with the principles of Mendelian randomization, which support a causal role of Lp(a) in the development of cardiovascular disease.

Within this framework, the analysis by Ridker et al.15 evaluated the prognostic value of a single baseline measurement of Lp(a), integrated with hs-CRP and LDL-C, in a large prospective cohort of nearly 28 000 initially healthy US women followed for 30 years. Each biomarker was independently associated with the risk of a first major cardiovascular event, with hazard ratios (HRs) of 1.70 for hs-CRP, 1.36 for LDL-C, and 1.33 for Lp(a). However, the combination of all three biomarkers provided the greatest long-term prognostic discrimination. These findings support the inclusion of Lp(a) in extended risk stratification beyond traditional 10-year models and underscore the potential value of earlier and more integrated preventive strategies.

In this context, Lp(a) assumes a dual clinical significance: on the one hand, it represents an independent causal risk factor that should be interpreted within the framework of global cardiovascular risk to enable more accurate stratification beyond traditional risk factors; on the other hand, it emerges as a potential specific therapeutic target.

At present, in the absence of therapies specifically designed to selectively reduce Lp(a) levels, individuals with elevated concentrations are recommended to undergo early and intensive management of cardiovascular risk factors, tailored to absolute global cardiovascular risk and Lp(a) levels.3,10

A clinically relevant implication is that the pro-atherogenic impact of elevated Lp(a) levels can be partially mitigated through more aggressive reduction of the overall atherogenic burden, beginning with LDL-C. Observational data suggest that at very low LDL-C levels, the association between elevated Lp(a) and ASCVD risk is attenuated. In particular, a recent population-based study from the U.S. Family Heart Database involving more than 270 000 individuals with baseline ASCVD demonstrated that the use of highly effective lipid-lowering therapies was associated with a significant reduction in recurrent cardiovascular events even among individuals with markedly elevated Lp(a) levels (≥180 nmol/L), especially in patients receiving proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors (PCSK9i). The benefit associated with PCSK9i use appears to be primarily attributable to substantial LDL-C lowering, despite a modest but significant concomitant reduction in Lp(a). These findings support a strategy of intensive LDL-C control in patients with elevated Lp(a) as an effective approach to mitigate residual cardiovascular risk while awaiting the availability of specific Lp(a)-lowering therapies.16

An alternative and currently available option for the management of individuals with elevated Lp(a) levels and progressive ASCVD or recurrent cardiovascular events is lipoprotein apheresis, which will be briefly discussed in the following section.

Elevated Lp(a) levels: current and future therapeutic armamentarium

The management of elevated Lp(a) levels requires a comprehensive approach integrating accurate cardiovascular risk assessment, optimization of lifestyle measures, and aggressive control of modifiable risk factors, with particular emphasis on LDL cholesterol levels.17

In the context of primary prevention, Lp(a) measurement represents a valuable improvement to conventional ASCVD risk estimation and should be performed at least once in every adult individual,10 particularly in those with a family history of premature ASCVD or with elevated coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores.10,17

Lp(a) is currently recognized as a causal cardiovascular risk factor and as a true cardiovascular risk modifier or risk enhancer.10 In light of the anticipated results from ongoing clinical trials investigating agents capable of selectively reducing Lp(a) levels, it is increasingly evident that Lp(a) represents a clinically relevant therapeutic target (Figure 1).

The figure illustrates the structure of lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] and the current and emerging therapeutic strategies aimed at reducing its circulating levels. Currently available therapies (statins, niacin, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors and bempedoic acid) achieve only modest reductions in Lp(a), with limited efficacy and, in some cases, relevant tolerability issues. Emerging therapies based on targeted approaches to apolipoprotein(a) synthesis—such as antisense oligonucleotides, small interfering RNA (siRNA), and gene-editing techniques (e.g. CRISPR)—promise a more substantial and specific reduction of Lp(a). The ultimate therapeutic goal is the lowering of plasma Lp(a) concentrations and the expected significant reduction in cardiovascular risk.

In patients with established ASCVD, the presence of elevated Lp(a) underscores the need for aggressive reduction of residual cardiovascular risk within the framework of secondary prevention. Clinical management should incorporate a shared decision-making process, particularly with regard to cascade screening of first-degree relatives. Data from the UK Biobank cohort have demonstrated a strong familial association with elevated Lp(a) levels, identified in more than 40% of first-degree relatives and in approximately 30% of second-degree relatives.

To date, no pharmacological agent has been specifically approved for the reduction of Lp(a) concentrations. The effects of currently available lipid-lowering therapies on Lp(a) levels are variable and generally insufficient to achieve a clinically meaningful reduction in the cardiovascular risk associated with this lipoprotein.

Lipoprotein apheresis represents the only strategy currently available for achieving a substantial and immediate reduction in Lp(a), with post-procedural decreases up to 60–70%, and has been associated with a reduction in cardiovascular events, with reported reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events ranging from 53% to 90%.3,17 This intervention is reserved for selected patients at very high cardiovascular risk, and markedly elevated Lp(a) levels and progressive ASCVD. However, apheresis is an invasive, costly, and not widely accessible procedure, requiring repeated treatments and a considerable commitment from the patient.

Lipoprotein apheresis is the most effective intervention, achieving acute reductions of up to 70% and an average reduction of approximately 35%, supported by prospective longitudinal studies; however, it is an invasive procedure available only in a limited number of specialized centres. Among pharmacological therapies, nicotinic acid and PCSK9 inhibitors demonstrate clinically relevant reductions in Lp(a) (approximately 20–40% and 15–30%, respectively), as documented by randomized trials and meta-analyses. Ezetimibe and lomitapide produce modest reductions, whereas statins and bempedoic acid do not show significant effects on Lp(a) levels, as confirmed by large meta-analyses of randomized studies.

Niacin is one of the few traditional pharmacological agents capable of reducing Lp(a), with reductions of up to 20–30%. Nevertheless, cardiovascular outcome trials have failed to demonstrate a significant additional cardiovascular benefit, and the use of niacin is limited by relevant adverse effects, including flushing, hepatotoxicity, and worsening of glucose metabolism.3 For these reasons, niacin is no longer recommended for the treatment of elevated Lp(a).

Statins remain the cornerstone of cardiovascular prevention, but they have no significant effect on Lp(a) levels. Despite this, statins are a cornerstone in the treatment of patients with elevated Lp(a) because they substantially reduce LDL cholesterol and overall cardiovascular risk. In this context, the therapeutic goal is aggressive LDL cholesterol lowering to offset the additional risk conferred by elevated Lp(a). In the FOURIER and ODYSSEY OUTCOMES trials, which evaluated evolocumab and alirocumab, respectively, reductions in Lp(a) of approximately 20–30% were observed. Post hoc analyses suggested that patients with higher baseline Lp(a) levels derived greater benefit in terms of cardiovascular event reduction, indicating that Lp(a) lowering may independently contribute to the protective effects of PCSK9 inhibitors.3

The magnitude of Lp(a) reduction required to achieve a clinically meaningful benefit remains uncertain. Mendelian randomization studies suggest that a reduction in Lp(a) of approximately 66–100 mg/dL may be necessary to produce significant reduction in cardiovascular outcomes.18 In the interim, clinicians should focus on optimizing control of all other cardiovascular risk factors in patients with elevated Lp(a), particularly in those with established ASCVD.

Although therapies specifically designed to reduce lipoprotein(a) are not yet available in routine clinical practice, intensive research efforts are ongoing, and the results of the first phase III trials currently underway are expected in 2026 (Table 2). Antisense oligonucleotides, such as pelacarsen, are single-stranded nucleotide sequences conjugated with N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc), which facilitates their selective delivery to hepatocytes via asialoglycoprotein receptors. Once internalized, these agents bind to LPA messenger RNA, forming a duplex that recruits RNase H, leading to selective mRNA cleavage and inhibition of apo(a) protein synthesis.

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), including olpasiran, lepodisiran, and zerlasiran, operate through a complementary mechanism. These double-stranded molecules, also conjugated with GalNAc, undergo intracellular processing, after which the antisense strand is incorporated into the RNA-induced silencing complex. This complex guides the antisense strand to LPA mRNA, inducing its cleavage and degradation, thereby silencing apo(a) production.

Beyond gene-silencing strategies, muvalaplin introduces a third mechanism of action by interfering with Lp(a) particle assembly. As the first oral small-molecule inhibitor of Lp(a), muvalaplin prevents the binding of apo(a) to apoB100, thereby blocking formation of the mature Lp(a) complex.

These therapeutic approaches have demonstrated high efficacy, achieving reductions in circulating Lp(a) concentrations of approximately 80–90%, and in some dosing regimens near-complete suppression (Table 2).19 However, it remains to be determined whether these biochemical reductions will translate into clinically meaningful reductions in cardiovascular events, pending the results of large-scale cardiovascular outcome trials. Nonetheless, early-phase clinical studies have reported a favourable safety profile across all classes of these investigational agents, with mild injection-site reactions being the most frequently observed adverse events. Long-term exposure data are still required to more definitively establish the efficacy and safety profiles of these emerging therapies specifically targeting Lp(a) reduction.
