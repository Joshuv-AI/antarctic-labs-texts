COVID-19 morbidity and mortality in young people in selected countries of the Americas
Authors: Sonja Caffe, Emmanuel Gonzalez Bautista, Oscar San Roman-Orozco, Antonio Sanhueza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile COVID-19 primarily affected adults, the epidemiology among adolescents and young adults has been less explored. This study describes the burden, outcomes, and correlates of COVID-19 among people aged 10–24 years in selected countries in the Americas during the acute pandemic phase (2020–mid-2022).MethodsWe analyzed case-based surveillance data reported by 32 countries and territories to the Pan American Health Organization between January 2020 and July 2022. Confirmed and probable SARS-CoV-2 infections were included following WHO definitions. We described age, sex, and temporal distributions, estimated cumulative incidence, and assessed associations between recovery status, comorbidities, and treatment modalities using logistic regression models adjusted for age and sex.ResultsA total of 17,031,217 COVID-19 cases were reported among young people (72% aged 10–19; 28% aged 20–24). Females accounted for 53% of cases. The pooled cumulative incidence was 7.7 per 100 population aged 10–24, ranging from 0.1 in Cuba to 52.0 in Bonaire, Sint Eustatius, and Saba. Recovery was documented in 88.9% of adolescents and 87.0% of young adults. The odds of recovery were lower among those aged 20–24 years (aOR 0.84, 95% CI 0.83–0.84), those with ≥ 1 comorbidity (aOR 0.17, 95% CI 0.16–0.18), and those requiring ventilatory support (aOR 0.28, 95% CI 0.25–0.31). Females and hospitalized patients had slightly higher recovery odds. Cardiovascular, kidney, and diabetic comorbidities were significantly associated with increased mortality.ConclusionsYoung people in the Americas represented a substantial proportion of COVID-19 cases, though with low overall mortality. However, chronic conditions markedly increased the risk of poor outcomes. Strengthening surveillance, ensuring continuity of care for young people with chronic diseases, and tailoring risk communication are essential components of future pandemic preparedness and life-course health strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-026-02831-0.

Background

The first case of SARS-CoV-2 in the Americas was reported on January 21, 2020 [1]. By August 2022, the Region was among the most affected globally, accounting for 29% of the world’s confirmed COVID-19 cases and 44% of global deaths [2]. Because most reported COVID-19 cases and severe disease globally and in the Americas were in adults, in particular older adults, public health strategies, messaging, and research primarily focused on the adult population initially. Available data indicated that adolescents (aged 10–19) and young adults (aged 20–24) carried a lower burden of SARS-CoV-2 infection during the pandemic, and in case of infection, were less likely than older adults to be hospitalized, experience severe disease, or die [3]. However, this focus on older populations created a gap in the understanding and timely health systems response for adolescents and young adults in the Region.

A systematic review of publicly available data and papers with no country of origin restrictions conducted between May 12 and October 31, 2020, included 5,933 pediatric patients aged 10–14 years who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 through PCR detection of viral RNA, rapid antigen detection test, or positive IgG serology, found that half (50.3%) of the children were asymptomatic. The hospitalization and intensive care unit (ICU) admission rates were very low, respectively 0.52% and 0.05% [3]. In a study among 3,222 young adults aged 18–34 years hospitalized with SARS-CoV-2 infection between April 1 and June 30, 2020, in the United States, 21% required intensive care, 10% required mechanical ventilation, and 2.7% died [4]. In this study, obesity and hypertension were associated with a greater risk of death or mechanical ventilation [4]. Also, a study in Italy among 3836 pediatric patients younger than 18 years reported nearly thrice the odds of a severe disease presentation in pediatric patients with an existing comorbidity (i.e., respiratory, cardiovascular, neurologic, oncologic, diabetes, or immunodeficiency) than their counterparts (OR = 2.8, 95% CI 1.74–4.48) [5]. An analysis of COVID-19 cases in Ecuador concluded that the risk of infection was greater for adolescents compared with children under one year of age, but the risk of death was greater for the younger children, and a national study of hospitalized children with confirmed COVID-19 found that children younger than 2 years and those aged 12–19 years had greater risk of death compared to the age group 2–11 years [6, 7].

These studies indicate age variability in the risk of infection and death, and suggest that although overall risks for complications in young people with COVID-19 infection may be lower compared with older adults, specific subgroups of young people, particularly those with chronic conditions, may be at increased risk for complications, which has important implications for public health strategies and clinical management. Moreover, the frequent occurrence of asymptomatic infections and the possible underdiagnosis of cases in this age group contributed to a lower estimation of the actual disease burden in young people [8–11].

A better understanding of the burden, distribution, and risks of rapidly evolving infectious diseases such as COVID-19, can help inform and refine national and local responses to young people and generate lessons learned for the future. The acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic in the Americas provides a unique opportunity to address the gap in knowledge and extract lessons for strengthening regional surveillance and emergency response systems. Such information is particularly critical for adolescents and young people who may be overlooked in emergency preparedness and risk communication frameworks and strategies.

This analysis took advantage of the existence of a regional surveillance system maintained by PAHO in which countries and territories report probable and confirmed cases and deaths. The two main objectives of this analysis were to use data from selected countries and territories (1) To describe the COVID-19 reported cases and outcomes in young people in the Americas during the acute phase of the pandemic (2020-mid 2022) and to estimate the cumulative incidence; (2) To describe the correlates of the recovered/ not-recovered outcome from COVID-19 cases in terms of age, sex, comorbidities and treatment modalities. Finally, a secondary objective was to explore the correlates of COVID-related mortality using the available data reported by countries and territories in the Americas.

Methods

Study population

This is a secondary analysis of surveillance data. We used official COVID-19 data reported to PAHO/WHO by countries via the Incident Management System [12]. (See Suppl Material for details). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards established in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Ethical approval from an Institutional Review Board (IRB) or Ethics Committee was not required, as this study involved the secondary analysis of fully anonymized, de-identified surveillance data. No directly identifiable human data were accessed or analyzed.

Description of the age, sex, and period of the reported cases

In this analysis, we included data from the 32 countries and territories that reported at least 300 cases in the 10–24 age group from January 1, 2020, to July 27, 2022. The ≥ 300 case threshold was introduced as a pragmatic data quality and stability criterion which is close to 10% of the absolute numbers of people within the age groups of interest in very small member states. Bonaire, Sint Eustatius and Saba were counted as one. The list of countries and territories is provided in Table 1. Entries without age or sex specification were excluded from this analysis, as well as cases under age 10. We focused on adolescents and young adults (10–24 years), in alignment with life-course public health frameworks that treat this age span as a distinct demographic and epidemiological group. Children under 10 years of age differ in clinical presentation, risk profiles, and surveillance considerations which would require a dedicated analytic approach. Data containing null values, no values, or unknown values were coded as missing data. (Fig. 1. Flowchart of cases included for the descriptive results)

Analysis of the correlates of recovered/not recovered status and died/not died for available data

For this analysis, we used data available from cases reported with an outcome as per the Incident Management System. The list of the percentage of missing outcome data for each country is presented in Supplementary Table S3. The primary outcome was categorized as “recovered” versus “not recovered.” The secondary outcome was coded as “died” versus “not died”.

Independent variables included the treatment modalities: admission to the hospital, admission to the ICU, and use of external mechanical respiratory support. The cases with unknown treatment modalities were treated as missing and thus were excluded from the corresponding statistical model.

Comorbidities

We included cardiac disease, diabetes, and kidney disease, defined in the WHO case report form as part of case-based surveillance [14]. These conditions were selected based on their high prevalence and potential link to COVID-19 outcomes based on available studies, and data completeness [15–18].

Statistical analysis

We used counts and percentages to describe categorical variables. We used histograms to plot the temporal distribution of cases (Fig. 2) and proportions to describe the cases in the population aged 10–24, based on the total number of cases reported (Fig. S1). We also estimated the cumulative incidence for the 10–24 age group using the country-level population published by the UN Population Data Portal as denominator [19] (Fig. S2).

Chronological distribution of cases

We computed univariate logit models to estimate the age and sex differences in recovered cases. Tests for differences in Tables 2 and 3 correspond to t-tests. (Tables 2 and 3). We analyzed the association between the recovered/not-recovered outcome and treatment modalities, as well as heart disease, diabetes, and kidney disease, using age- and sex-adjusted logit models. We provided adjusted odds ratios (aORs) to measure the strength of the association between the independent variables and the outcome of recovered/not recovered. (Table 4). Finally, we tested the bivariate association between the ‘died’ vs. ‘not died’ outcome and comorbidities and treatment modalities. Binary logistic regression models with a logit link function were fitted using maximum likelihood estimation. Crude and adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Model assumptions included independence of observations and correct specification of the logit link function. The latter was tested using the Pregibon’s link test. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18 (StataCorp). 2023. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC.

Results

Description of the age, sex, and period of the reported cases

A total of 17,031,217 cases in the 10–24 age group were extracted from the database, with 72% corresponding to ages 10–19 and 28% to ages 20–24. For the younger age group, there were three peaks in the number of reported cases, lasting 2–3 months, in December 2020, April 2021, and August 2022, with the highest number of cases occurring in February 2022, which more than doubled the highest monthly number of cases reported previously during the study period. Discussion

Our study found that the waves of reported COVID-19 cases happened quarterly and presented 2–3 months earlier for the 20–24 age group compared with those aged 10–19 years. An exception to this was the remarkable case number for February 2022, which coincided with the circulation of the Omicron variant. Most of the reported cases were female. The cumulative incidence in this age group was not negligible, with 7.74 cases reported per 100 people aged 10–24, but reaching up to 52.0 in Bonaire, Saint Eustatius, and Saba Fig. S2. Those results might be correlated with the age composition of the countries or the health system’s capacity to respond to outbreaks. However, considering that these are real-world data gathered in the context of a pandemic, they must be interpreted with caution and bearing in mind the reporting difficulties.

Among the countries with reasonable data completeness, our results show that percentages of hospitalization and deaths due to COVID-19 were low in young people aged 10–24 years in the Americas. Recovery chances were higher among females and those admitted to hospitalization. Significantly lower chances of recovery were found for those aged 20–24 years, and as expected, for those with comorbidities (notably cardiovascular disease), and for those who required ventilatory support.

During the February 2022 peak, the wave in which Omicron began to circulate, high levels of transmission were observed in all age groups compared to previous variants [16]. Our results align with those reported by the CDC regarding the association between chronic diseases and COVID-19, which lists cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, and diabetes as comorbidities associated with a higher risk of severe COVID-19 infection [17, 18]. However, when compared to other age groups, they set the 18–39 age group as the reference group with a null risk ratio (i.e., they did not analyze the 10–19 and the 20–24 age groups separately) [20].

A strength of the COVID-19 response in the Americas is the existence of the SARInet network, established in 2014 to enhance surveillance and response to respiratory infections in the Americas. This network aims to share information, best practices, and lessons learned among the countries in the region regarding the surveillance and management of respiratory infections. The national influenza centers belonging to this network were trained in COVID-19 surveillance protocols. Nevertheless, during the pandemic, countries faced human resource and other challenges that negatively affected their capacity for timely and complete reporting, resulting in delays and incomplete country reports.

The role of hospital admission in the outcome of COVID cases among young people deserves special attention in the design of plans for pandemic preparedness and response. In our study, admission to the hospital was associated with higher odds of reporting a recovered status across all age groups, even after adjusting for age, sex, and comorbidities. Further research is needed to identify the most cost-effective interventions for managing COVID-19 cases that may be feasible outside the hospital in a community-based setting. In this vein, our results suggest that these interventions might be suitable for a fraction of medium-risk patients, whose case severity was high enough to warrant hospitalization but not high enough to require ICU care. Thus, future pandemic response strategies should rapidly state risk-stratification criteria for a more effective population health management [21–23].

Of particular concern is the finding of the elevated risk of severe illness and death of young people with specific chronic diseases. The information regarding their increased vulnerability would have been, and continues to be, critical for the young people themselves, their parents and caregivers, and healthcare providers. In this context, ensuring continuity of care for their chronic condition in the context of emergencies, additional measures to reduce the risk of infection, and prompt attention in case of infection are essential to reduce the risk of death.

The study also emphasizes the need for comprehensive policies that enhance the health system’s response capacity in outbreaks for all, while addressing the needs of specific groups across the life course. Risk communication strategies must consider the specific characteristics of various age groups, their needs, and preferences to increase the uptake of public health recommendations. Awareness, information, and communication efforts for young people will require differential strategies that align with their communication patterns and preferences.

Furthermore, our results underscore the urgent need for multisectoral strategies to reduce chronic diseases and risk factors in young people, and mitigate the risks and complications of comorbidities (e.g., cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, and diabetes) in this age group, which increase the risk of adverse outcomes in the context of public health emergencies, such as the COVID-19 pandemic.

While this study examines the acute pandemic phase (2020–mid-2022), it does not address other COVID-19 related issues affecting young people, such as post-acute COVID-19 syndrome or long covid, and multisystem Inflammatory Syndrome (MISC), but the findings hold direct relevance for current public health actions related to these sequelae. Emerging evidence indicates that adolescents and young adults experience persistent symptoms at clinically significant rates, including fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, and exercise intolerance, with potential impacts on educational attainment and workforce participation [24, 25].

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study on the dynamics of COVID-19 focusing on adolescents and young adults, with individual-level pooled data from the Americas region.

A strength of this study was the availability of real-time data from countries generated by a surveillance strategy that built on the existing surveillance and response system for respiratory infections in the Americas.

A significant limitation in this study was the high level of incompleteness of the data reported by countries. Using the available data, our study examined the cross-sectional association between comorbidities and COVID-19 outcomes in these age groups, drawing on data from 18 countries in the Americas. However, our results should be interpreted with caution, taking into consideration the high level of incomplete data, which excluded significant numbers of cases in the analysis of outcomes, and taking into consideration that the accuracy of the reported COVID-19 data is highly dependent on each country’s testing approaches and the capacity of the health information system to track and record the relevant data.

Country-specific adaptations of case definitions may have also affected the sensitivity and specificity of case detection, particularly across different age groups. For example, if a country uses a stricter case definition, it may report fewer mild cases, which can affect comparability with countries using broader definitions. Moreover, the variability in clinical presentation among younger individuals compared to older adults might result in an underestimation of cases in young people if the case definitions do not adequately capture the symptoms more common in this age group.

Other limitations include the selection bias introduced by the inclusion of only countries with a minimum level of complete data in the outcome analysis. This could lead to an unbalanced representation of the global situation, as countries with complete data often have more robust health systems and better reporting capabilities, which could result in either an underestimation or overestimation of specific regional effects or trends. For instance, countries with less developed health systems and incomplete data might have higher mortality rates and complications, which would not be reflected in our analysis.

Conclusions

This analysis confirmed that a significant number of COVID-19 cases occurred in young people (10–24 years) in the Americas during the acute phase of the pandemic. This study highlights the importance of strengthening data systems to generate complete and disaggregated data and utilize this data to inform public health actions.

The timely availability of reliable, disaggregated data is crucial for developing appropriate Public Health strategies to identify and reach vulnerable subgroups. Considering that in some scenarios the focus of the pandemic response was on the ageing population without addressing the risks and needs of young people, particularly those with certain chronic diseases, presents a missed opportunity to adequately respond to the needs of these groups with a higher risk of morbidity and mortality. Strategies for emergency preparedness should include efforts to address chronic disease in younger populations, as well as the early establishment of risk-stratification criteria.
