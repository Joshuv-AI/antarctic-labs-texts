Identification of factors associated with intraductal carcinoma of the prostate
Authors: Motoki Yamagishi, Casey M. Phan, Yoshihiro Nakagami, Akiko Tokunaga, So Murai, Toshiko Yamochi, Kazuhiko Oshinomi, Masakazu Nagata, Takashi Fukagai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProstate cancer is a common malignancy. Intraductal carcinoma of the prostate (IDCP) is associated with poor prognosis, but is underreported in certain geographic regions. The presence of IDCP is recently recognized as an independent prognosticatior of poor prognosis. We aim to identify factors associated with IDCP on radical prostatectomy specimens to aid in more accurate diagnosis of IDCP.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on specimens from Showa Medical University Hospital (Japan) and Queen’s Medical Center (Hawaii) from April 2020 to March 2024. Clinical data included age, PSA, and race; pathological data included GS, GG, and factors indicated the extent of cancer (EPE, RM, LVI, PNI, SVI). IDCP was diagnosed morphologically; equivocal lesions underwent basal cell IHC. Statistical analyses identified factors associated with IDCP.ResultsAmong 279 cases, IDCP was found in 70 (25.1%). The IDCP-positive group had higher PSA levels (14.2 vs. 10.7 ng/ml, p = 0.048). Univariate and Multivariate analyses identified GS (OR: 16.41, p < 0.001), EPE (OR: 2.36, p = 0.02), and PNI(OR: 2.57, p = 0.02) remained independently associated.ConclusionHigh grade (GS ≥ 8), EPE, and PNI serve as practical pathological triggers to scrutinize ducts and apply basal-cell IHC, which may reduce under-recognition of IDCP in RP specimens.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is one of the most prevalent malignancies, affecting 1,466,680 patients in the world per year [1, 2]. Intraductal carcinoma of the prostate (IDCP), characterized by the infiltration of prostate cancer cells into the normal glandular architecture of the prostate, has been reported [3–7]. IDCP is now recognized as a distinct pathological entity associated with aggressive disease biology and adverse clinical outcomes [7]. Historically, Rammy et al. first described IDCP [3], and Kovi conducted the initial clinical studies [4]. Subsequent research has indicated that the presence of IDCP in prostate cancer specimens is associated with poor prognosis in terms of Overall survival and clinical progression-free survival (PFS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) [7, 8]. Despite increasing recognition, IDCP remains a diagnostically challenging lesion. Previous surveys have demonstrated substantial interobserver and inter-institutional variability in diagnostic thresholds and reporting practices among pathologists [9, 10]. Recent advances in the molecular pathology of prostate cancer have highlighted diverse genomic alterations and signaling pathways associated with aggressive tumor phenotypes [11]. More recently, large-scale molecular analyses have demonstrated that intraductal carcinoma of the prostate represents a molecularly distinct subtype of prostate cancer, characterized by specific genomic alterations, transcriptomic features, and an altered tumor microenvironment [12].

Currently, International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) and Genitourinary Pathology Society (GUPS) acknowledge differing approaches to the handling of IDC-P, and this remains under active debate [9, 10, 13, 14].. Nonetheless, some reports suggest that the poor prognostic implications of IDCP should be reflected in the Gleason score [15–17]. It has also been reported that IDCP is not yet widely recognized in Japan and Ireland [18, 19], raising concerns about potential underdiagnosis and inappropriate management. IDCP has recently been recognized as an independent prognosticator of poor prognosis [20, 21].

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to investigate the clinicopathologic factors associated with IDCP, by analyzing radical prostatectomy specimens. Our goal was to identify key factors associated with IDCP, to increase recognition and reporting of this process.

Methods and Subjects

This study utilized radical prostatectomy specimens from patients who underwent surgery and diagnosis at Showa Medical University Hospital (SUH) in Japan between April 2020 and March 2024, as well as specimens diagnosed at The Queen’s Medical Center (QMC) in Hawaii. The presence or absence of intraductal carcinoma of the prostate (IDCP) was assessed in these specimens. In Japan, the evaluation was conducted by S.M. and T.Y. at SUH, while in Hawaii, M.Y. and C.P. at QMC were responsible for the assessments. IDCP was diagnosed per the criteria established by Guo and Epstein [7]. Equivocal lesions received basal-cell IHC. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using automated platforms according to the manufacturers’ protocols. At SUH, basal cell immunohistochemistry was performed using p63 (clone DAK-p63; Dako, Agilent Technologies) and high–molecular-weight cytokeratin (CK34βE12; clone 34βE12; Leica Biosystems). At QMC, a PIN4 multiplex stain (rabbit monoclonal antibody cocktail; p504S/AMACR, p63, and CK34βE12; Biocare Medical, Pacheco, CA) was performed on the Leica BOND-III automated stainer using Leica’s ChromoPlex dual-stain detection system (Fig. 1).

Clinical data were extracted from patient records, including age, PSA levels, and race, while postoperative pathology reports were reviewed to obtain information on postoperative Gleason score, Grade Group, tumor progression parameters, extraprostatic extension (EPE), resection margin status (RM), lymphovascular invasion (LVI), perineural invasion (PNI), and seminal vesicle invasion (SVI). As grading of IDCP remains debated, we report both Gleason Score (GS) and Grade Group (GG) and phrase conclusions independent of the schema. Pathologic staging followed the 8th edition American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Cancer Staging Manual. For grading purposes, if the tertiary pattern was pattern 5 and comprised ≥ 5% of the tumor, it was treated as the secondary pattern. Conversely, if the tertiary pattern was pattern 5 but comprised < 5% of the tumor, the final score consisted of the primary and secondary patterns with an appended minor (tertiary) pattern 5. In this study, tertiary pattern 5 was not incorporated into the Gleason Score (GS); analyses were performed with tertiary pattern 5 excluded. EPE was analyzed excluding SVI; microscopic bladder neck invasion was considered pT3a.

The patients were divided into two groups–those with IDCP and those without IDCP. Univariate analysis was conducted for each group. Factors associated with IDCP were determined by considering factors such as average age and high-risk characteristics, followed by univariate analysis. Based on the results of the univariate analysis, logistic regression analysis was performed to identify significant factors associated with IDCP.

For statistical analysis, JMP Pro 17 software was used. Univariate comparisons used Fisher’s exact and t-test for PSA. Multivariable associations were assessed using logistic regression. A significance level of P = 0.05 was adopted for statistical interpretation. This study was conducted with the approval of the Showa Medical University Research Ethics Review Board in Japan (2024-167-B) and the Research and Institutional Review Committee in the Queen’s Medical Center in Hawaii (RA-2024-206).

Results

This study included 178 radical prostatectomy cases diagnosed at SUH in Japan and 118 cases diagnosed at QMC in Hawaii. Among these, cases that had undergone preoperative hormone therapy or lacked available slides for evaluation were excluded—13 cases from SUH and 4 cases from QMC. The final study cohort comprised 279 cases, with 165 from SUH and 114 from QMC.

Among the included cases, 38 patients at SUH and 32 at QMC were diagnosed as IDCP positive (IDCP+), resulting in a total of 70 IDCP+ cases, while the remaining 209 cases were classified as IDCP negative (IDCP-). The mean age of the IDCP+ group was 67.7 years, whereas that of the IDCP- group was 68.9 years, with no statistically significant difference in age between the two groups (p = 0.15). Regarding PSA levels at the time of prostate biopsy, the IDCP+ group had a mean PSA of 14.2 ng/ml, compared to 10.7 ng/ml in the IDCP- group, showing a statistically significant difference (p = 0.048*). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of racial distribution.

Statistical comparisons of postoperative Gleason Scores and Grade Groups revealed significant differences between the IDCP + and IDCP- groups. Similarly, all evaluated parameters related to tumor progression showed statistically significant differences between the groups (Table 1).

Discussion

In this pathological analysis of IDCP, 279 cases were included, with IDCP positivity observed in 70 cases (25.1%). The PSA levels at the time of biopsy were significantly higher in the IDCP+ group compared to the IDCP- group. Furthermore, the IDCP-positive group exhibited higher grade tumors by Gleason score and Grade Group. Similarly, tumor progression was significantly more advanced in the IDCP-positive group. Multivariate analysis identified three significant associated factors for IDCP: Gleason score, extraprostatic extension (EPE), and perineural invasion (PNI).

Previous studies have shown that IDCP positivity rates vary depending on whether biopsy specimens or radical prostatectomy specimens are used [22, 23]. A review of previous reports indicates that IDCP positivity rates in biopsy specimens range from 2.3% to 8.6% [19, 24, 25], while those in radical prostatectomy specimens vary widely, from 0.002% to 43.2% [25–33]. Several studies have also reported differences in positivity rates based on malignancy grades, cases with distant metastases, cases of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), and high-risk prostate cancer undergoing radical prostatectomy [34–43]. Table 4 summarizes these reported rates under different conditions. The table suggests that IDCP positivity tends to be higher in cases with distant metastases and in those that progress to CRPC. Moreover, IDCP-positive cases have been reported to be more likely to develop distant metastases [44], suggesting that IDCP could serve as a high-risk factor for tumor progression. Recently, regional differences in the incidence of IDCP have been reported [45]; no significant difference was found between the Japanese and US cohorts. In our study, we reported a comparison between Asian and non-Asian groups. Because our regional cohorts differ from those in the prior study, we intend to conduct an additional study specifically to evaluate regional differences.

The potential for underreporting of IDCP in pathological evaluations may result in an inadequate interval of post-operative follow-up. In our study, the IDCP+ group had higher PSA levels, Gleason scores, Grade Groups, and more advanced tumor progression compared to the IDCP- group. This suggests that cases with high-risk pathological findings should be carefully examined for the presence of IDCP. Our findings indicate that GS, EPE, and PNI + are significantly associated with the presence of IDCP. Since these factors are already well-established as poor prognostic indicators in prostate cancer, the clinical significance of this association lies in its potential impact on pathological diagnosis. In patients who already exhibit established high-risk pathological features, identification of IDCP does not necessarily alter adjuvant treatment or surveillance strategies. Rather, its primary value in this setting lies in improving pathological recognition and reporting accuracy. In cases with high Gleason scores (≥ 8), extraprostatic extension, and perineural invasion, pathologists should maintain a high index of suspicion for IDCP to ensure accurate diagnosis. Additionally, given the underreporting of IDCP in certain geographic regions, our study underscores the need for heightened awareness and rigorous diagnostic evaluation in high-grade prostate cancer cases. Previous studies analyzing the pathological relative factors of IDCP in radical prostatectomy specimens from high-risk prostate cancer patients have identified EPE, SVI, and SM [8]. Our findings are consistent with these reports, indicating that cases with high Gleason scores and extraprostatic extension are at increased probability of demonstrating IDCP. Therefore, in cases with more aggressive tumor progression, the possibility of IDCP should be considered during pathological diagnosis.

However, our study has certain limitations. First, this study did not evaluate isolated IDCP in the absence of other high-risk pathological features. Although IDCP diagnoses were strictly conducted at each institution, inter-institutional diagnostic confirmation was not performed. Previous studies have reported discrepancies in IDCP diagnosis among pathologists [46, 47], suggesting that inter-institutional review could potentially alter diagnostic outcomes. Additionally, genetic risk factors could not be assessed due to differences in racial distribution across institutions. Some studies have indicated that genetic alterations in IDCP may differ between Asian and Caucasian populations [28, 48, 49]. For example, PTEN loss has been reported to increase tumor progression, potentially affecting relative factor assessment.

Moreover, there have been reports suggesting that the presence of IDCP in low-risk prostate cancer cases does not necessarily indicate poor prognosis [50], implying that IDCP may not always be associated with high-risk, aggressive tumor progression. However, even in isolated IDCP cases identified in biopsy specimens, 38% of patients were found to have pT3a disease upon radical prostatectomy [51], underscoring the potential prognostic impact of IDCP diagnosis in prostate cancer patients.

Conclusion

Our analysis identified GS, EPE and PNI + as factors associated with the presence of IDCP. For accurate pathological diagnosis of IDCP, careful attention should be given to high-grade prostate cancer cases that exhibit EPE and PNI.
