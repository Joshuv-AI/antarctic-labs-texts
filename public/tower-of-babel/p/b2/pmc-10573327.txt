Programmed Release METTL3-14 Inhibitor Microneedle Protects Myocardial Function by Reducing Drp1 m6A Modification-Mediated Mitochondrial Fission
Authors: Boyue Huang, Liu Xie, Ming Ke, Yonghong Fan, Ju Tan, Jianhua Ran, Chuhong Zhu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractM6A modification is an RNA-important processing event mediated by methyltransferases METTL3 and METTL14 and the demethylases. M6A dynamic changes after myocardial infarction (MI), involved in the massive loss of cardiomyocytes due to hypoxia, as well as the recruitment and activation of myofibroblasts. Balanced mitochondrial fusion and fission are essential to maintain intracardiac homeostasis and reduce poststress myocardial remodeling. Double-layer programmed drug release microneedle (DPDMN) breaks the limitations of existing therapeutic interventions in one period or one type of cells, and multitargeted cellular combination has more potential in MI therapy. By employing hypoxia-ischemic and TGF-β1-induced fibrosis cell models, we found that METTL3-14 inhibition effectively decreased cardiomyocyte death through the reduction of mitochondrial fragmentation and inhibiting myofibrillar transformation. DPDMN treatment of MI in rat models showed improved cardiac function and decreased infarct size and fibrosis level, demonstrating its superior effectiveness. The DPDMN delivers METTL3 inhibitor swiftly in the early phase to rescue dying cardiomyocytes and slowly in the late phase to achieve long-term suppression of fibroblast over proliferation, collagen synthesis, and deposition. RIP assay and mechanistic investigation confirmed that METTL3 inhibition reduced the translation efficiency of Drp1 mRNA by 5′UTR m6A modification, thus decreasing the Drp1 protein level and mitochondrial fragment after hypoxic-ischemic injury. This project investigated the efficacy of DPDMNs-loaded METTL3 inhibitor in MI treatment and the downstream signaling pathway proteins, providing an experimental foundation for the translation of the utility, safety, and versatility of microneedle drug delivery for MI into clinical applications.Keywords: microneedle, mitochondrial fission, Drp1, myocardial infarction, METTL3

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) is one of the most important causes of heart failure, leading to poor quality of life and hospitalization.1 After acute myocardial infarction, massive loss of cardiomyocytes and myocardial interstitial cells, proliferation and phenotypic transformation of cardiac fibroblasts (CF), and massive deposition of collagen lead to the development of myocardial fibrosis.2 N6-methyladenosine mRNA modifications (m6A) are the most common epigenetic modifications in RNAs3 and have an important regulatory role in myocardial injury and cardiac remodeling. M6A modification is mainly mediated by polyprotein complexes (writers), which consists of methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14),4 and the binding subunit Wilms tumor 1-associated protein (WTAP), of which METTL3 is catalytically active and METTL14 is responsible for substrate recognition5 and is removed by erasers, the fat-mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO), and a-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase alkB homologue 5 (ALKBH5).6 Elevated m6A levels were found in both mouse and human heart failure samples, and m6A methylation regulates the stability and translation efficiency of cardiomyocyte mRNAs, thereby regulating and influencing cardiomyocyte fate.7−9 Although the significance of m6A in myocardial remodeling has begun to be elucidated, additional exploration is required to determine which downstream genes play key roles in disease development and how they are regulated by METTL3.

Coordinated mitochondrial dynamics is essential for the bioenergetic function of mitochondria and is involved in the alteration of mitochondrial ultrastructure in response to stress.10 Fusion contributes to mitochondrial maintenance, while excessive fission leads to mitochondrial fragmentation and is associated with cardiomyocyte death in ischemia-reperfusion injury.11 Studies have confirmed the fragmentation of the mitochondrial network in heart failure.12 Fewer studies have been conducted to regulate mitochondrial dynamics through epigenetic modulation. Intervening in mitochondrial fission by regulating m6A modification of mRNA of genes related to mitochondrial dynamics may be effective in protecting myocardial function.

Systemic administration of drugs to modulate m6A levels may affect other tissues and organs. In situ and relatively noninvasive administration is more advantageous. Current studies have mainly used cellular-level knockdown knockout models or studied the phenotype of tissue-specific knockout mice,8,13,14 limiting the clinical applicability and advancement of RNA epigenetic modifications. Microneedles (MN) are advantageous because of high drug loading capacity, mild preparation conditions, and high biosafety.15 Conventional treatments can slow the progression of heart failure and reduce mortality,16 but relatively homogeneous, targeting only one pathological alteration. The development of platforms combining biomaterials and small molecules for the programmed release of drugs for different pathological processes is particularly promising in the treatment of MI.

In this study, we designed a double-layer programmed drug release microneedle (DPDMN) capable of delivering a drug in a predetermined order. We evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of DPDMNs using in vitro characterization and in vivo evaluation in a rat MI model. The DPDMNs showed good mechanical and drug release properties, which released the drug rapidly in the first stage to save the dying cardiomyocytes and slowly in the second stage to achieve long-term suppression of fibroblast over proliferation, collagen synthesis, and deposition. The data indicated that DPDMN has a promising therapeutic effect and application potential in the prevention of ventricular remodeling and improvement of cardiac function after myocardial infarction. In addition, MeRIP-seq data demonstrated that the translation of 5′UTR m6A-containing mRNA of Drp1 was reduced upon inhibition of METTL3, thus decreasing the degree of mitochondrial fission and ameliorating the energy metabolism disturbance. The METTL3-14 inhibitor SAH could effectively protect against mitochondrial injury. Our study investigated the practicality, safety, and versatility of microneedles for MI, which has good scientific significance and translational relevance.

Results and Discussion

METTL3-14 Inhibition Improved Cardiomyocyte Viability Mainly by Reducing Mitochondrial Fission

Studies have shown that m6A methylation regulates cardioprotective protein expression,17 impacting the fate of cardiomyocytes.7 The findings presented above suggest that m6A methylation plays an important role in myocardial injury and cardiac remodeling. The demethylase FTO was found to be reduced in expression in human infarct tissues versus heart failure mice, and myocardial tissue-specific overexpression of FTO improved postinfarction cardiac function.13 However, no efficient FTO agonist has been identified, limiting its further development in clinical translation. Therefore, we anchored to its functionally opposing and catalytically active methyltransferases METTL3 and METTL14. Silencing METTL3 in animals with pathological myocardial hypertrophy model maintains cardiac function, cardiac homeostasis, and inhibits further development of myocardial hypertrophy.18 In a mouse cardiac ischemia-reperfusion model, m6A methylation level was increased in cardiomyocytes, and silencing METTL3 enhanced autophagic flux in hypoxia/reoxygenation-treated cardiomyocytes, indicating that METTL3 was the negative regulator of autophagy.14 In conclusion, METTL3 and its mediated m6A may be potential therapeutic targets for the pathological myocardial remodeling.

We hypothesized that the inhibition of METTL3-14 could reduce cardiomyocyte death. S-Adenosyl-l-homocysteine (SAH) is an inhibitor of the METTL3-METTL14 complex with a half-effective inhibition amount of 0.9 μM,19 and has no effect on other m6A modification-related enzyme activities. We initially evaluated the toxicity of SAH on cardiomyocytes and found no cytotoxicity at concentrations below 27 μM (Figure S1A). We cultured primary rat cardiomyocytes as models to explore the therapeutic effect of SAH on myocardial ischemia and hypoxia. SAH significantly reduced the level of m6A modification in primary rat cardiomyocytes in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 1A). Due to the unavoidable reoxygenation at each opening when using a CO2 incubator and what we need more is a hypoxic-ischemic model rather than a hypoxia-reoxygenation model, we chose the cobalt chloride (CoCl2)-induced hypoxia model instead of a CO2 incubator, which could stabilize hypoxia-inducible factors 1α and 2α under normoxic conditions. The rat cardiomyocyte ischemia-hypoxia model was stimulated using 600 μM CoCl2 and replacing 10% FBS with 1% in complete medium. Consistent with literature reports, SAH suppressed the expression levels of METTL3-METTL14, but did not affect ALKBH5 (Figures 1A and S1B–E). CCK8 assay and live–dead cell staining were performed after 24 h treatment with SAH administration, the results showed that SAH significantly improved the reduction of cardiomyocyte activity and the number of deaths after ischemia and hypoxia, and the effect was dose-dependent (Figure 1B,C). Since SAH generates adenosine and l-cysteine under the action of SAH hydrolase, in order to rule out the effect of its metabolites, we separately treated the cells with the same concentration of adenosine, l-cysteine and the combination of the two drugs, and found that it did not improve the death of cardiomyocytes after hypoxia (Figure S1F). These results demonstrate that SAH can significantly reduce the level of m6A modification and inhibit the death of cardiomyocytes after ischemia and hypoxia. The effect is independent of its metabolites.

In the above ischemic-hypoxic cardiac myocyte model, we found no significant difference in mitochondrial number after ischemic-hypoxic treatment, but the integrity was severely compromised with punctate distribution of mitochondria. SAH administration increased mitochondrial DNA integrity to some extent (Figure 1E), as well as ATP content (Figure 1F). Normal cardiomyocytes displayed an interconnected network of long tubular mitochondria, which almost completely fragment into round-shaped mitochondria upon ischemia and hypoxia. CoCl2 exposure resulted in a drop in mitochondrial membrane potential, but a statistically significant decrease after treatment of SAH (Figure 1G,H). We also found that mitochondrial morphology was significantly altered, with mitochondria in cardiomyocytes showing fragmentation after hypoxia, which was reversed by METTL3 inhibition (Figure 1H,I). The fragmentation of mitochondria is most likely due to excessive accumulation of ROS in mitochondria. As expected, the mitochondrial ROS level in the ischemic-hypoxic group was significantly higher than that in the control group, and SAH administration reduces ROS production (Figure 1J).

m6A RNA Methylation in the Cardiomyocyte Differentiated of Human Pluripotent Stem Cell

Given the existing m6A sequencing data in mice and the persistent differences between mouse and human pathological mechanisms, we opted to utilize human-induced pluripotent stem-cell-derived cardiomyocytes as sequencing samples. The major regulators of m6A RNA methylation FTO, METTL3, and METTL14, are expressed in the cardiomyocytes differentiated from human pluripotent stem cell (iCMs). Next, we used a hypoxia model established by incubating cells with 600 μM CoCl2 for 24 h. We have also confirmed in the iCMs that SAH can reduce the cell death phenotype following hypoxia (Figure 2A). To investigate the underlying mechanisms for the role of SAH in hypoxia of iCMs, a transcriptome-wide analysis of m6A RNA modification was performed using m6A-specific methylated RNA immunoprecipitation. Differential gene analysis revealed changes in the expression of a large number of genes in iCMs following hypoxia (Figures 2B and S2A). 4840 transcripts were deregulated in their expression upon hypoxia (Figure 2C,D), whereas 1290 transcripts were deregulated between the hypoxia group and the 9 μM SAH administration group (Figures 2C and S2B). The m6A-seq data identified 5487 differentially methylated (m6A-modified) fragments in the hypoxia group compared with the control group and 3555 in the SAH group relative to the hypoxia group. The m6A peaks were strongly enriched near the 3′UTR, 5′UTR, and exons (Figures2E,F and S2C). Motif analysis of the peaks revealed the enriched conserved sequences in the control group to be UGGACGAG (Figure 2G), GAAGA in the hypoxia group, and CGGAA in the SAH group (Figure S2D).

Based on the MeRIP-seq results, m6A peak difference analysis revealed that the biological processes impacted by hypoxia were mainly focused on the apoptosis, cellular stimulation of DNA damage, and oxidation–reduction processes (Figures 2H and S2E). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that hypoxia mainly affects cell motility, growth, and energy metabolism (Figures 2I and S2F). Compared with the SAH-treated group, ATP binding, RNA binding, RNA transport, citrate cycle, and lysine degradation were altered (Figure S2G,H). The above results suggested that METTL3-14 complex inhibition may affect mitochondrial energy metabolism signaling pathways. We further described the alterations in signaling pathways following METTL3-14 complex inhibition in human cells, which presents a relatively advantageous approach for clinical translation. Understanding the m6A modifications in mRNAs of particular functional genes and the regulation of downstream signaling pathways by METTL3-14 is crucial to unraveling the molecular mechanisms governing myocardial remodeling.

Drp1 Is a Potential Target Gene Downstream of METTL3

Based on the previous MeRIP-seq results, we performed an intersection analysis of the differential peaks and differential transcripts in the control, hypoxia, and SAH groups after a two-by-two analysis. The four-quadrant diagram directly showed the relationship between m6A level and mRNA expression in the results of association analysis (Figure 3A). And a total of 45 candidate genes were identified (Figure 3B, Table S1). Combined with the previous GO and KEGG analysis, SAH mainly improved the mitochondrial function of cardiomyocytes; Drp1, one of the candidate genes, was significantly different among the three groups (Figure 3C), known as a key protein for mitochondrial fusion and fission. Therefore, the downstream target gene screening concentrated on genes relevant to mitochondrial dynamics.

Myocardial remodeling and chronic heart failure are associated with disturbed energy metabolism and mitochondrial dynamics imbalance.20 Changes in mitochondrial dynamics have a profound effect on mitochondrial metabolism, apoptosis, and autophagy as well as on the size and shape of mitochondria, which are associated with changes in the fate of various cell types. Altered mitochondrial dynamics are regulated by mitochondrial fusion proteins and mitochondrial splitting proteins. Mitofusins and OPA1 regulate fusion at the outer and inner mitochondrial membranes, respectively, while dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) and its mitochondrial receptor regulate mitochondrial fission.21 Drp1 receptors include FIS1 and mitochondrial fission factor (MFF), 49 and 51 kDa mitochondrial kinetic proteins (MID49 and MID51), whose relative contribution to Drp1-dependent fission is cell-type-specific.22 Manipulating mitochondrial dynamics can improve the cardiac function after MI and inhibit the progression of myocardial hypertrophy. Mitochondrial fragmentation was enhanced and inhibition of Drp1 reduced myocardial infarct size in myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury model.23 Drug- or gene-targeted inhibition of Drp1 in diabetic rat model of myocardial hypertrophy curbed the progression of myocardial hypertrophy.24 In addition, we selected TOMM20, a marker of the mitochondrial outer membrane as a negative control. TOMM20 is located in the outer mitochondrial membrane and identifies precursors with a presequence to facilitate protein import across the outer mitochondrial membrane.25

Consistent with sequencing results, the m6A levels increased upon hypoxia and decreased after SAH administration (Figure 3D). RT-PCR results showed that the expression levels of Drp1 and MFF were upregulated after hypoxia and downregulated after SAH administration, while the mRNA expression levels of OPA1 and TOMM20 were not significantly changed (Figure 3E). This was consistent with the results of RNA-seq and Input sequencing data. Moreover, the m6A modification peak of Drp1 was significantly decreased after hypoxia and recovered after SAH treatment. But no significant changes were observed in OPA1, MFF, and TOMM20 (Figure 3F).

M6A modification, which is mainly distributed in common with RRACH sequence, can affect cell function by affecting mRNA translation efficiency. The difference m6A peak of Drp1 is mainly concentrated in the 5′UTR and exon region but not 3′UTR, and the level decreased after hypoxia and increased following SAH administration (Figure 3G). There was no difference in m6A peak enrichment between TOMM20 and OPA1 among the three groups (Figure S3A,B). It has been reported that 5′UTR m6A can bypass the 5′cap-binding proteins to promote gene translation under stress, the translation efficiency of 5′UTR m6A-containing mRNAs is reduced in the depletion of METTL3, whereas mRNAs containing m6A elsewhere in the transcript does not exhibit this effect.26 This is consistent with our results: SAH reversed the elevated expression levels of the mitochondrial fusion proteins Drp1 in ischemic and hypoxic cardiomyocytes. We speculated that the translation efficiency of Drp1 mRNA decreased after SAH treatment, which mediated the decrease in protein level.

In order to verify the direct relationship between METTL3 and Drp1, RIP-RT-PCR was used for verification. The results showed that compared with IgG, METTL3 IP antibody could significantly enrich Drp1 mRNA. It is proved that METTL3 can bind Drp1 mRNA, and the two have a targeting relationship (Figure 3H). A similar phenomenon was not observed with METTL14 (Figure S3C). These results suggested that Drp1 mRNA was the target of the METTL3 modification but not of METTL14.

We also detected the expression levels of the above proteins, and SAH mediated the downregulation of Drp1 (Figure S3D) and phosphorylation at a serine 616 (S616) site (Figure S3E). The decreased phosphorylation of Drp1 enhances its dimerization and interaction with MFF at the mitochondria (Figure S3F). Consistent with previous sequencing results, there was no significant change in the OPA1 protein level (Figures 3I and S3G). These findings imply that SAH primarily decreases mitochondrial fission via lowering Drp1 expression and phosphorylation levels to enhance mitochondrial function and hence reduce cardiomyocytes after ischemic-hypoxic injury.

Less research has been done on m6A modification in relation to mitochondrial dynamics. Studies reported that METTL3 modification of PGC-1α mRNA mediated mRNA degradation, decreased protein expression levels, reduced ATP production and oxygen consumption rate (OCR), and thus enhanced oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL)-induced monocyte inflammatory response.27 It had also been reported that METTL3-mediated m6A modification may activate the mitochondrial fusion proteins MFN2 and OPA1 thereby protecting against chromium-induced mitochondrial autophagy.28 Our study found that SAH effectively inhibited mitochondrial fragmentation, enhanced mitochondrial membrane potential, and improved mitochondrial function in ischemic and hypoxic cardiomyocytes.

METTL3-14 Inhibitor Inhibits TGF-β1-Induced Proliferation and Transformation of Myofibroblasts

TGF-β1 activates cardiac fibroblasts, promoting their proliferation and the development of fibrosis. We cotreated rat cardiac fibroblasts with SAH and 20 ng/mL TGF-β1 and validated the effectiveness of SAH in inhibiting methyltransferases (Figures 4A and S4A–D). Then, we monitored the extent of proliferation using Ki67 immunofluorescence labeling after 24 h. SAH administration significantly decreased the ratio of Ki67-positive cells, and there was no difference between the blank control and negative control groups (Figure 4B,C), showing that SAH might inhibit TGF-β1 overactivation-induced cardiac fibroblasts and SAH had no impact on cardiac fibroblast proliferation in the healthy condition.

The transition of cardiac fibroblasts into myofibroblasts is regarded as a crucial step in the development of cardiac fibrosis and an important component of the profibrotic effects of TGF-β1. α-SMA plays an important role in the process of myocardial fibrosis and has been widely used to mark the activated state of fibroblasts differentiated into myofibroblasts. Moreover, TGF-β1 resulted in an increase in the expression of α-SMA and an irregular expansion of cell morphology. In the SAH-treated cells, the shape of cardiac fibroblasts was considerably less affected, while α-SMA expression was significantly reduced (Figure 4D–F). In addition, we also detected the expression levels of fibrosis-promoting and collagen-related factors. The expression of all of these factors was decreased in the SAH group compared with the control group, such as Fgf2, Fn1, Col3a1, and Col6a3 (Figure 4G–J). These results suggest that SAH can effectively inhibit the TGF-β1-induced myofibroblast transformation. The results of cell scratch test showed that TGF-β1 could significantly promote the migration of cardiac fibroblasts, and SAH treatment could effectively inhibit the migration-promoting effect induced by TGF-β1 (Figure S4E).

Additionally, Mitochondrial membrane potential was detected, and there was no significant difference between the TGF-β1 group and the treatment groups (Figure 4G). We also examined mitochondrial DNA quantity and integrity, and no significant variations were found (Figure S4H–I). Consistent with the results in cardiomyocytes, the expression of mitochondrial fission-related protein Drp1 and MFF were decreased in the SAH-administered group, but there was no significant change in p-Drp1 (S616) and the mitochondrial fusion-related protein OPA1 (Figures 4L and S4J–M).

Characterization of DPDMNs

Soluble microneedles avoid the need to be removed from the body again after application and have the advantages of high drug loading capacity, mild preparation conditions, and high biosafety.29 The fabrication process of the DPDMN is shown schematically in Figure 5A. We added a certain amount of polymer sodium hyaluronate solution containing SAH to the PDMS mold and made the solution spread on the tip part by vacuum or centrifugation. Next, we added the polylacticcoglycollic acid (PLGA) dissolved in 1,4-dioxane containing SAH, and following by vacuum or centrifugation to obtain the needle body part. Finally, the needles were freeze-dried and demolded to obtain the programmed release composite microneedle. As depicted in the scanning electron microscopy (SEM), the prototype DPDMN was 8 mm in diameter and contained 100 needle tips with base widths of 285 ± 6.88 μm and heights of 570 ± 3.25 μm (Figure 5B). Furthermore, the composition of DPDMN was confirmed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Figure 5C). By testing the mechanical properties of microneedles, it could be found that the DPDMN had good toughness, with a variation force of 0.4 N/needle, which meets the requirements for puncturing into myocardial tissues (Figure 5D).

(D) Compression force–displacement curves for DPDMNs with or without SAH. (E) Drug release curve of drug-loaded HA microneedles. (F) Drug release curve of drug-loaded PLGA microneedles. We measured the drug release ratio of SAH loaded with different materials and found that the drug release can be completed in around 15 min under HA load (Figure 5e) and in around 9 days under PLGA load (Figure 5F). Programmed release leads to a more efficient drug delivery as follows: 20.12% of the SAH was rapidly released within 15 min, followed by a gradual release (Figure 5G). The microneedle-loaded drug is almost completely released in about 8 days. In addition, DPDMNs had excellent drug loading capacity and could meet the drug release concentration requirements at all stages. After confirming the optimal dosage through our preliminary exploratory experiments, the entire microneedle was loaded with approximately 0.1 mM SAH.

Existing microneedles are mostly used for transdermal therapy, including the promotion of wound healing, insulin treatment for diabetes, etc. Several microneedle treatments on heart attack have also been reported, including AAV–vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF),30 a vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-loaded and near-infrared (NIR)-triggered self-unfolding graphene oxide (GO)-poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) MN patch,31 and double-layered adhesive microneedle bandages (DL-AMNBs) consisting of a bio functional MAP-based root and a regenerated silk fibroin (SF)-based tip.32 All of these patches improve cardiac function by promoting neovascularization, demonstrating the feasibility and great potential of MNs in the treatment of MI. Compared to the microneedle systems mentioned above, ours has the advantage of programmed release of drugs on pathological process treatment at different orthogonal stages of infarction including short-term reduction of myocardial cell loss and long-term inhibition of myofibroblast activation and proliferation. This intervention addresses two pathological processes, including cardiomyocyte death and fibroblast proliferation, as well as collagen deposition. Previous reports on microneedles mostly focused on single-release mechanisms. Conventional microneedles used for treating myocardial infarction are mainly aimed at improving cardiac function by promoting angiogenesis. Our microneedles specifically target cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts, which is also novel in terms of the mechanism of treatment. Furthermore, METTL3 is expressed in all tissues and organs throughout the body, and systemic drug administration may affect the function of other nonlesioned locations. The localized drug delivery of microneedles in the myocardial infarction area avoids the above-mentioned risks.

DPDMNs Ameliorate Cardiac Dysfunction in MI Rats

We established a model of acute myocardial infarction in SD rat hearts with left anterior descending coronary artery ligation surgery. To assess changes in cardiac function, we measured the ejection fraction (EF), fractional shortening (FS), LVIDs, and LVIDd by echocardiography in each group at 2 weeks after DPDMN application. MI rats treated with MN-SAH had increased EF and FS values and smaller LVIDs and LVIDd values relative to the MI group (Figure 6A). Compared with the sham group, the EF and FS values of MI-MN without SAH were not substantially improved 2 weeks after MI surgery, indicating that DPDMN material itself could not enhance cardiac function (Figure 6A). The H&E staining results revealed the morphology of the heart, and the myocardium in the sham group was structurally intact and generally uniform in shape, with neatly arranged cells. However, in the MI group, we observed a large amount of loose fibrous tissue and plasmocyte infiltration (Figure 6B). Relative to the experimental group, both inflammatory cell infiltration and fiber breaks were reduced in the MI+SAH group. Masson’s trichrome staining revealed the fibrotic morphology of the heart tissue. Compared to those in the MI group, the scar areas and infarct in the MI + SAH group were effectively controlled by SAH application (Figure 6C). Type I collagen, type III collagen, and α-SMA were indicated by immunohistochemical staining (Figure 6D). Compared with the MI group, the total collagen content in the infarction area of the MN group was decreased, but the α-SMA expression level was not significantly different.

Since heart failure is a progressive event, we also tested the cardiac ultrasound at 4 weeks after DPDMN application (Figure 7A,B) and collected the myocardial specimens of the rats at 4 weeks postoperatively to conduct histopathological and immunofluorescent staining analysis. Masson staining and magnified images demonstrated large areas of blue-stained fibrotic tissue and massive deposition of myocardial interstitial collagen in the MI group, which separated the myocardial bundles in the grid (Figure 7C). Compared to the MI groups, the MI + SAH group had smaller blue-stained areas and less staining, indicating a lower degree of myocardial fibrosis (Figure 7D,E). Samples from each group were subjected to qPCR and WB assays against collagen-related and fibrosis-promoting factors to measure the therapeutic impact of METTL3 inhibition on MI. The sham group was set to be the control. In the MI group, Col3a1, Fn1, Col6a1, and Col6a2 were highly transcribed, but it was relatively low in the MI + SAH group (Figure 7F). WB analysis (Figure 7G) showed that Collagen-I, Collagen- III, and α-SMA were significantly upregulated in the MI group and decreased in the MI + SAH group. In addition, the ratio of type I to III collagen (type I/type III) was lower than that in the MI + SAH group (Figure 7H). The same results were found in immunofluorescence staining of type I and III collagen (Figure 7I). As with the results 2 weeks after operation, these results also indicated a significant improvement in cardiac function and fibrosis levels in the rats 4 weeks after operation, suggesting the superior effect of DPDMN in slowing the progression of heart failure. Considering that the cycle of drug release by microneedle is less than 2 weeks and the improvement effect at 2 weeks is better than that at 4 weeks, it suggests an important role of early intervention to improve myocardial remodeling.

We similarly confirmed the expression levels of methyltransferases in the tissue of infarcted parts of rats in each group and found that the expression levels of METTL3-METTL14 were elevated in the MI group, whereas the expression levels decreased in the MI-SAH group to a level without significantly different from the sham group. There was no significant difference in the expression of the ALKBH5 (Figure 7G).

In addition, the von Willebrand factor (vWF) protein level was detected and labeled by immunofluorescence staining. There was no statistically significant difference between the MI and MI-SAH group, which demonstrated that it did not influence neoangiogenesis during the healing phase (Figures 7G and S5A). To validate the cardiac limits of SAH administration, HE staining was conducted in the livers and kidneys of all three groups of animals; no significant difference was seen between the MI and MI-SAH groups (Figure S5B).

To verify Drp1 as a METTL3 target, the expression of m6A level and mitochondrial dynamics-related genes in three groups of rats was analyzed (Figure 7J,K). The results showed that consistent with the cell model, the mRNA level of Drp1 was higher in MI rats and lower in MI-SAH groups (Figure 7L), while the level of the m6A modification level of Drp1 dramatically reduced in MI rats and rose after SAH treatment (Figure 7M). We conducted a RIP experiment on tissues from the infarcted area of rats 1 week after myocardial infarction when they were in the stage of fibroblast proliferation. The RIP experiment demonstrated once again that Drp1 was the target of METTL3 in vivo (Figure 7N).

While the data from Zhao et al.,33 suggest that METTL3 overexpression could improve cardiomyocyte proliferation and subsequently ameliorate MI, we think their conclusions are not entirely inconsistent with ours. Kun Zhao et al. focused more on the impact of m6A on the proliferative capacity of myocardial cells, and the time point of collecting tissue samples from rats is 1 week after MI. Our research focused on the regulation of METTL3 on cell death in myocardial cells after ischemia and hypoxia, and we examined tissue samples from the myocardium 4 weeks after MI. These lead to differences in our conclusions. There are a lot of studies that support our findings. Silencing METTL3 can reduce apoptosis in myocardial cells after hypoxia-reoxygenation,14 METTL3 overexpression promotes the deterioration of cardiac function, and METTL3 deficiency attenuates cardiac dysfunction and cardiac fibrosis after myocardial infarction.34 The phenomenon of reduced cell vitality and apoptosis after hypoxia is indeed in line with the loss of METTL3. However, there are some controversial conclusions regarding the pathological processes of cell proliferation and hypertrophy in myocardial cells. Contrary to Kun Zhao et al., studies have shown that transfection of small interfering RNA targeting METTL3 can promote CMs proliferation, while overexpression of METTL3 has the opposite effect.35 Moreover, METTL3 overexpression decreased cardiomyocyte proliferation and suppressed heart regeneration in postnatal mice.36 The above-mentioned studies highlight the different roles of METTL3 in different pathological processes and at different time points.

Conclusions

In summary, this project proposed to design a bilayer polymer composite microneedle with programmed release of the METTL3-14 inhibitor for the treatment of heart infarction. In the early stage, rapid release of SAH is proposed to rescue dying cardiomyocytes, and slow release is proposed in the later stage to achieve long-term inhibition of fibroblast over proliferation and collagen synthesis, secretion, and deposition. Regarding the molecular mechanism, we discovered that the Drp1 5′UTR m6A modification caused the protein level to decrease following METTL3 inhibition, thus reducing mitochondrial fragmentation after hypoxia-ischemia damage and mitigating mitochondrial metabolic disturbance. This work provides an experimental basis to support the translation of the practicality, safety, and versatility of MN drug delivery for infarct treatment through minimally invasive surgery and its clinical application, which is of good scientific and translational relevance.

Experimental Section

Model of Myocardial Infarction (MI)

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All experiments were performed with permission for animal experimentation from the Ethics Committee of the Third Military Medical University, China, and followed the guidelines from Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes.

Healthy male Sprague–Dawley rats were acclimated to Third Military Medical University, Chongqing, vivarium for at least 7 days before experiments. The SD rats aged 7–8 weeks were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (30 mg/kg) and then intubated into a rodent ventilator. The left side of the chest was opened through the fourth intercostal space, the incision was fixed with a chest expander, and the pericardium was opened to expose the heart. MI was induced by ligating the descending branch of the left coronary artery with a 6/0 nylon wire, and myocardial tissue at the ligature gradually turned white along with localized darkening of the left ventricle and diminished vascular pulsation to confirm myocardial ischemia.

Due to the fast-beating heart of rats, it is difficult to fix MN by applying pressure to the fingers. Therefore, we used 3D printing to construct a resin device for MN puncture. The internal and external diameters of the device were 9.2 and 10.3 mm, respectively. The height of the lumen is 2.2 mm. A 32.7 mm diameter hole was located in the center of the plate and is connected to a suction tube. We used a negative pressure instrument with a negative pressure of ∼8 kPa (∼60 mmHg) to enter suction on the target myocardial area, the DPDMNs are passively and completely inserted into the soft myocardium.30 After cessation of the suction supply, the DPDMNs are detached from the back plate. The suction intensity used is safe and did not cause myocardial injury.

For tissue collection, the mice were euthanized by excess carbon dioxide administration.

Echocardiographic Measurements

Echocardiographic measurements were performed after two and 4 weeks for each group. Rats were anesthetized by isoflurane followed by standard transthoracic echocardiography using an ultrasound imaging system (VINNO 6 LAB, VINNO, Suzhou, China) equipped with a 30 MHz phased-array transducer. Parasternal long-axis view of the left ventricle for assessment of left ventricular volume and function in rats. Ventricular parameters were analyzed including left ventricular EF, FS, LVIDd, and LVIDs.

Fabrication and Characterization of DPDMNs

DPDMNs were prepared with an air-permeable but water-impermeable mold (EFL-MMN-600). Briefly, the first layer of MNs was made of sodium hyaluronate, filtered in a vacuum (EFL-VDM-001) to form the top layer, which contained approximately 0.02 mM of SAH. The second layer consisted of polylactic glycolic acid (PLGA) dissolved in 1,4-dioxane and contained approximately 0.08 mM of SAH. Throughout the vacuuming operation, the microneedles must constantly monitor the suction level in order to maintain a vacuuming efficiency of −0.1 MPa for 10 min. After that, the drying bottle must be opened for the first time to determine the condition of the microneedle mold and the bubbles must be eliminated using a needle. The process was repeated until the mold was entirely filled. The mold was put in a 60 °C oven for 30 min, and PLGA-containing SAH solution was added. Before demolding, the microneedles must be stored in a dry, matte environment at 26 °C for at least 24 h to guarantee full solvent evaporation. The microneedles were placed in a Petri dish and wrapped in aluminum foil before storing them at 4 °C or in a dryer for later use. The DPDMN has excellent mechanical properties and can be successfully lanced into the myocardium.

Culture and Treatment of Cardiac Fibroblasts and Myocardial Cell

Primary culture cells were isolated from 0- to 1-day-old neonatal rats. The hearts were removed and rinsed with DMEM (11995040, Gibco). Then, the tissues were cut into 1 mm pieces and digested in digestive fluid (0.1% trypsin, 0.05% collagenase II). Tissue was digested in a water bath beaker on a magnetic stirrer at 37 °C for 7 min; after digestion, the supernatant was collected into a new centrifuge tube containing complete medium; the digestion process was repeated in the previous step until the heart tissue mass was not visible to the naked eye. The supernatant collected in the above steps was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and the cells were resuspended and filtered through a 200-mesh filter. Cells were centrifuged (1500 rpm, 5 min), resuspended, and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (HyClone) for 1.5 h. Fibroblasts preferentially adhered to the bottom of the culture plate, and the supernatant was filled with unadhered cardiomyocytes, which were transferred to a new cell plate. The purified CFs were further incubated in DMEM (10% FBS) at 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2 and 95% O2. Then, TGF-β1 (20 ng/mL; SinoBiological, China) was added into the plates for 24 h.

Cardiomyocytes differentiated from iPSCs: Cells were differentiated as previously described.37 Briefly, after human-induced Pluripotent Stem Cells (iPSCs) reached 85% confluence (day 0), the medium was changed to RPMI-1640 medium (11875, Life Technologies), 500 μg/mL O. sativa-derived recombinant human albumin (A0237, Sigma-Aldrich), 213 μg/mL l-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate (Sigma-Aldrich), and 6 μM CHIR99021 (Selleck). On day 2, CHIR99021 was removed and 2 μM Wnt-C59 (Selleck) was added. On day 4, Wnt-C59 was removed from the medium. The medium was changed every 48 h. Contractile cells could be observed from day 7.

Dot Blot

Total cellular RNA samples were extracted by the Total RNA Extraction Kit (Invitrogen). The samples were spotted in rows on the N+ nylon membranes (Merck Millipore, German) in a super clean bench. After UV cross-linking, the membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 2 h at room temperature, then washed twice with TBST solution, and incubated with N6-methyladenosine/m6A Rabbit mAb (Abclonal, China) overnight at 4 °C; then, the membrane was immersed in TTBS solution and washed three times at room temperature with shaking for 5 min each time. The washed membranes were incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature with shaking. After several washes of TBST, the membranes were detected with a DAB Peroxidase Substrate Kit, 3,3′-diaminobenzidine substrate kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, China).

Western Blot

Tissues or cells were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Thermal Scientific) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and protein phosphatase inhibitor (Solarbio). The extracted fragments were homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer and spun at 12,000g for 20 min at 4 °C. Total protein concentration was measured by BCA (Pierce) and resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). To detect our target proteins, we incubated the membranes (4 °C overnight) with the following primary antibodies. The antibodies used in the current study are shown in the Supporting Information. Then, PVDF membranes were incubated (1 h, room temperature) with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Goat antirabbit IgG, Scicrest). We visualized the blots using the ECL Plus Kit (Amersham Biosciences). Relative protein expression was quantified by the optical density.

Immunofluorescence

The rats were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital and then perfused with saline and fresh 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.01 M PBS (phosphate-buffered saline). The hearts were removed and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by sucrose gradient dehydration. The tissues were frozen and sectioned in the coronal plane at 10 μm for immunofluorescence detection (Olympus). Slices were washed with PBS for 5 min thrice. Then, the slices were washed in PBS-T (0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 0.5 h at 37 °C and blocked with 5% goat serum in 0.3% PBS-T overnight at 4 °C. Then, the slices were incubated overnight with primary antibody solution (Table S2). Slices were then washed in PBS thrice and incubated with mixtures of secondary antibodies (Table S2) for 1 h at room temperature.

H&E and Masson’s Trichrome Staining

Fixed rat hearts were rinsed with tap water for 1 h and then dehydrated using 70, 80, 95, and 100% alcohol for 1 h, respectively. Paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were cut into 5 μm thick sections by a paraffin slicer and dried until ready for use.

After dewaxing and rehydrating according to the standard procedure, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Finally, the sections were dehydrated transparently with alcohol and xylene and sealed with neutral resin. Sections were examined with light microscopy (Olympus, Japan) for histological analysis.

The slices were stained with Modified Masson’s Trichrome Stain Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Collagen fibers were stained blue, and myofibrils, cytoplasm, fibrin, keratin, and erythrocytes were red. The fibrillar collagen percentage was determined by measuring collagen fibers (blue) using Image-Pro Plus 6.0. Infarct size was compared by measuring the sum of epicardial and endocardial infarct circumferences divided by the percentage of the sum of total left ventricular epicardial and endocardial circumferences.38

Quantitative Reverse Transcription-PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA samples were extracted from cultured cells or tissues using a total RNA Extraction Kit (Invitrogen). For each sample,1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA with RTIII All-in-One Mix with dsDNase for qPCR (Bioman, Beijing, China), and then amplified with Amp SYBR Green qPCR Mix (None ROX) (Bioman, Beijing, China) using CFX connect real-time system (Biorad, Germany). Using the 2–ΔΔCT method to determine the relative expression. For qPCR, quantifications of transcripts were normalized to GAPDH. The sequences of primers are shown in the Supporting Information.

m6A MeRIP Sequencing

Data are given in the Supporting Information.

m6A MeRIP-qPCR Assay

For the MeRIP-qPCR assay, an Invitrogen Dynabeads antibody coupling kit (Invitrogen) and antibodies for m6A(MeRIP-qPCR) or METTL3 (RIP-qPCR) were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells or tissues were collected and lysed with the buffer in the kit, and then anti-m6A antibody and IgG (negative control) were used for immunoprecipitation for isolation of m6A methylation modification RNAs. Purified RNA of 300 ng was used for reverse transcription; qPCR was performed to evaluate the RNA levels.

Cell Cytotoxicity Assays

After treatment with different drugs, cells were added to the configured live/dead working solution, incubated for 30 min, protected from light, and photographed under a laser microscope (Olympus).

Cytotoxicity and cell viability were assessed with the CCK-8 assay kit (Biosharp, China). Briefly, about 5,000 cells were plated into 96-well per well and then treated with different drugs for 24 h. Next, we added a CCK-8 solution (10%) in the media for 1 h at 37 °C. ELISA (Bio-Tek, MQX200) was used to measure the absorbance at 450 nm.

Measurement of Mitochondrial ATP Content, mtDNA Copy Number, mtDNA Integrity, and ROS

ATP content was quantified with the ATP-Lite Assay Kit (Vigorous, China) following the manufacturer’s instruction and was normalized by the protein concentration. Genomic DNA and mitochondrial DNA were extracted using the genomic DNA miniprep kit (Zoman Biotech, China). mtDNA was quantified by the relative copy number ratio of mtDNA to nucleic DNA. The lipoprotein lipase (lpl) gene was used for determining the genomic DNA, 12S rRNA gene for the total mtDNA, and NADH dehydrogenase subunit 4 (Nd4) for integrated mtDNA.39 Primers for qPCR are shown in the Supporting Information.

MitoSOX was used to detect mitochondrial matrix superoxide, respectively. Cells were incubated in 20 μM MitoSOX for 10 min, washed three times with PBS 1×, and imaged immediately. The fluorescence intensities of MitoSOX were detected at 510 and 580 nm, respectively. The total average fluorescence intensity was quantified by using ImageJ.

Mitochondrial Inner Membrane Potential (ΔΨ) Detection

The membrane potential, ΔΨ, was measured by loading cells with 10 nM tetramethyl rhodamine methyl ester (TMRM; Invitrogen) for 30 min at 37 °C. Then, PBS was used to wash cells and the membrane potential was detected with confocal microscopy. TMRM excitation was performed at 543 nm, and emission was in the range of 560–660 nm.

Statistical Analyses

All data are presented as mean ± S.E.M. Data were analyzed using Student’s t tests (for two-grouped comparisons) or one-way ANOVA (for multiple-group comparisons). Significance was assigned at P < 0.05. N refers to the sample size of the experiment and statistics in all of the figures. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software unless otherwise mentioned.
