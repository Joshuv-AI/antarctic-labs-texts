Linear Focal Elastosis: A Peculiar Dermatosis
Authors: Adnan Ahmad, Fatmah Al‐Sughayer, Humoud Al‐Sabah, Atlal Al‐Lafi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLinear focal elastosis (LFE), also known as elastotic striae, is a rare cutaneous condition characterized by abnormal or increased deposition of elastic fibers in the dermis. It typically presents as asymptomatic, atrophic, or hyperpigmented linear bands, most commonly on the back. We report a case of LFE in a 15‐year‐old healthy male who presented with multiple asymptomatic, parallel hypopigmented linear bands with hyperpigmented borders on his back. A skin biopsy revealed characteristic thickened and fragmented elastic fibers in the reticular dermis, confirming the diagnosis. The pathogenesis of LFE remains unclear, but hypotheses suggest a role for mechanical stress, trauma, or growth spurts, particularly in adolescents. The condition is often misdiagnosed as striae distensae, linear morphea, or other elastotic disorders. Histopathological confirmation is essential to prevent unnecessary interventions. Increased awareness of LFE can aid in early recognition and differentiation from other dermatoses. Reassurance and patient education remain a cornerstone of management.Keywords: dermatology, elastotic striae, linear dermatosis, linear focal elastosis

Introduction

Linear focal elastosis (LFE) is a dermatological condition characterized by linear bands of increased elastic fibers in the dermis. It was originally described by Burket et al. [1] in 1989 as elastotic striae. LFE has no known risk factors or disease associations, and its exact pathogenesis remains unclear, though it is believed to result from abnormal elastic fiber deposition in response to mechanical stress, trauma, or unknown genetic factors [2]. It classically appears on the back but can rarely affect the face or extremities [3, 4]. We present a case of LFE in an adolescent male who exhibited characteristic clinical and histopathological features of LFE, contributing to the growing recognition of this rare entity.

Case History and Physical Examination

A 15‐year‐old previously healthy boy attended the dermatology clinic with his father due to an incidental finding of unusual, asymptomatic lines on his back. The patient does not remember when the lines first appeared, likely due to their location and the absence of symptoms such as itching or pain. He described the lines as pale in color with some darkening. This was the first time to complain of this condition, and he denied any history of trauma. No other skin lesions were present elsewhere on his body. The patient had no chronic illnesses, prior surgeries, or history of medication use, including over‐the‐counter drugs or herbal remedies. He also denied any known food or drug allergies. His family history was negative for malignancies or autoimmune disorders, and no family members had developed similar skin findings. Social history was unremarkable, and there was no history of psychiatric conditions.

Physical examination showed multiple parallel hypopigmented linear striae‐like bands with hyperpigmented borders on the patient's back. These lesions appeared atrophic and slightly depressed upon palpation (Figure 1).

Investigations and Diagnosis

A cutaneous punch biopsy was obtained from one of the linear bands under local anesthesia. Histopathological evaluation revealed a slightly acanthotic epidermis with elongated material deposits intersecting collagen fibers in the reticular dermis on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Figure 2). Orcein staining demonstrated abundant thickened and fragmented elastic fibers in the reticular dermis stained red, interwoven with dermal collagen fibers (Figure 3). Therefore, our final diagnosis was LFE based on clinical and histopathological findings with correlation.

Management and Follow‐Up

After confirming the diagnosis, both the patient and his father were reassured, as LFE is a benign condition that requires no treatment. However, if the condition pose a cosmetic concern, aesthetic therapeutic options may be considered and sought to address the patient's aesthetic needs.

Discussion

LFE remains a poorly understood dermatological condition, primarily due to its rarity and the limited number of cases reported in the literature (Table 1). Its clinical presentation, with asymptomatic, atrophic, and hyperpigmented linear bands, often leads to confusion with other dermatological disorders [5]. The differential diagnosis of LFE includes striae distensae, linear lichen planus, linear morphea, solar elastosis, pseudoxanthoma elasticum, mid‐dermal elastolysis, cutis laxa, and dermatofibrosis lenticularis disseminata with osteopoikilosis (Table 2). Several hypotheses have been proposed for the pathogenesis of LFE, including growth spurts in adolescents and mechanical stress or trauma [6]. Some researchers propose that LFE represents a reactive dermal process resulting from abnormal elastic fiber repair, even without obvious injury [7]. While clinical diagnosis is possible, histopathological examination is crucial for confirmation and ruling‐out other dermatoses [8]. The hallmark histological finding is a localized increase in elastic fibers within the reticular dermis, appearing thickened and fragmented on special stains such as Verhoeff‐van Gieson (VVG) and orcein [9]. Although LFE is not associated with mortality, its significance lies in its potential for misdiagnosis and unnecessary investigations or interventions. Given cosmetic concerns, some patients may seek aesthetic treatments. Although no standardized therapy exists, topical retinoids and platelet‐rich plasma (PRP) have been explored with variable outcomes [8, 9, 10]. Ultimately, reassurance and patient education remain the cornerstone of management, as LFE does not pose any systemic health risks.

Conclusion

LFE is often underrecognized due to its asymptomatic nature and resemblance to other atrophic dermatoses. Accurate diagnosis relies on histopathological examination, which reveals characteristic elastic fiber proliferation in the reticular dermis. Although the exact pathogenesis remains unclear, hypotheses suggest a role for mechanical stress, trauma, or genetic predisposition. While no specific treatment is necessary, patient education and reassurance are essential, particularly for those with cosmetic concerns.
