Malnutrition Is Common in Cyclic Vomiting Syndrome: A Prospective Study Using Validated Nutritional Assessment Tools
Authors: Thangam Venkatesan, Praveen S. Goday, Saranya Arumugam, Kebire Gofar, Mahima Vyas, Ana Petrova, Jimenez-Castillo Raul, Muhammad Ayyan, Sonali Srivastava, Kristen Heitman, Peter Madril, Kristin Goheen, Kristen M. Roberts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS) can impair nutritional status due to recurrent episodes of nausea and vomiting, yet systematic data are lacking. We aimed to evaluate nutritional status in adults with CVS using validated assessment tools.MethodsIn a prospective study of adults diagnosed with CVS based on Rome IV criteria, nutritional status was evaluated using the Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment Short Form (PG-SGA-SF) completed by participants, the PG-SGA completed by a Registered Dietitian Nutritionist (RDN) and the Malnutrition Screening Tool (MST).Key ResultsAmong 80 participants (mean age 39 ± 16 years) 77.5% (n = 62) were female, and 85% (n = 68) had moderate-to-severe CVS. Based on the PG-SGA-SF, 70% (n = 56) were at risk of malnutrition (42.5%, n = 34 severe; 27.5%, n = 22 moderate). More than half (41; 51%) were at risk for malnutrition (MST score ≥ 2). Despite this, 27% (n = 22) were obese, and 20% (n = 16) were overweight. Mean BMI did not differ between well-nourished and malnourished participants (28.4 ± 8.2 kg/m2 vs. 29.1 ± 7.7; p = 0.7). Of the 64 participants with a completed PG-SGA, 62.5% were classified as malnourished (28%, n = 18 severe; 34%, n = 22 moderate). The PG-SGA-SF demonstrated high sensitivity (100%) and specificity (71%) for detecting malnutrition with a positive predictive value of 85% and a negative predictive value of 100%.Conclusions and InferencesMost adults with CVS met criteria for malnutrition, indicating a need for referral to an RDN. BMI should not be used as a screening tool for malnutrition, and mechanisms underlying obesity in CVS warrant further investigation.

Introduction

Cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS) is a disorder of gut–brain interaction (DGBI) characterized by recurrent episodes of nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain [1]. It affects both children and adults, with an estimated prevalence of ~ 2% in the United States [2–4]. Despite increasing recognition, CVS remains a challenging illness associated with frequent emergency department visits, hospitalizations, and marked impairment in quality of life [5–7]. Episodes occur with variable frequency, ranging from several times per year to twice per month, and may last from hours to up to 10 days, with a mean duration of four days in adults [8, 9].

During acute episodes, most patients are unable to tolerate oral intake [10]. Some adopt compensatory behaviors such as drinking large amounts of water followed by self-induced emesis to “dilute the acid,” while others restrict intake altogether [10]. These behaviors, combined with recurrent vomiting and retching, may contribute to weight fluctuations, dental problems, and potential nutrient deficiencies [11]. Further, about 30% of participants with CVS have inter-episodic symptoms such as nausea, low-grade vomiting, and dyspepsia that may further limit dietary intake [8, 9]. However, participants are thought to regain weight between episodes, and prior studies indicate that the mean BMI in CVS is approximately 28 kg/m2, which may suggest weight cycling-associated metabolic adaptation is occurring in this population [9]. Medication side effects such as amitriptyline-associated weight gain and cannabis use for appetite stimulation may further complicate the nutrition picture [12]. Thus, many participants may present as overweight or obese by BMI yet remain at risk for malnutrition.

Most recently, avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) was noted to be present in over 30% of those with moderate-to-severe CVS and may be associated with symptoms and supported high rates of malnutrition [13]. Further investigation into the sensitivity and specificity of various malnutrition screening tools is warranted to inform GI clinics on appropriate screening tools for use in this patient population.

Comprehensive nutritional assessment in gastrointestinal disease requires integrated evaluation of nutritional intake, weight assessment, clinical status, food availability, food beliefs, and functional assessment by a Registered Dietitian Nutritionist (RDN). Yet such evaluations are rarely performed in GI practice due to the limited availability of RDNs in gastroenterology practices. The Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA) is a validated tool that combines patient-reported dietary intake and symptoms along with an RDN or clinician-led physical assessment of muscle and fat stores, fluid balance, and metabolic demand [14]. Its utility has been demonstrated in multiple chronic disease populations [14, 15].

Given these gaps in knowledge, the aim of this study was to determine the presence of malnutrition in participants with CVS during the inter-episodic phase using the PG-SGA. We also sought to assess the sensitivity and specificity of malnutrition screening tools for determining malnutrition status in CVS. Through the implementation of validated nutrition risk screeners and systematic characterization of malnutrition, this work will inform clinicians caring for CVS patients about key nutritional concerns, and appropriate use of nutrition screening tools in CVS. This provides a foundation for future dietary and behavioral interventions aimed at improving outcomes in CVS.

Methods

This study was performed at a tertiary referral center with a specialized CVS clinic. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (#2024H0003), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to study procedures. Participants received remuneration for their participation.

Study Population

All participants who were diagnosed with CVS based on Rome IV criteria were included in the study. Some participants had typical clinical features, but others had longer episodes that did not conform to the 7-day criteria as per Rome. A recent prospective study demonstrated that 13% had episodes lasting 7–10 days, 18% had episodes that last greater than 10 days, indicating that CVS episodes are not always consistently less than 7 days [16].

Inclusion criteria included the following: Age > 18 years, ability to read English and independently operate an iPad or home computer. Exclusion criteria included 1. Inability to voluntarily provide informed consent for the study (including vulnerable populations such as prisoners). 2. Other major GI problems such as inflammatory bowel disease, cirrhosis or medical problems like renal failure, cardiac failure, or liver cirrhosis.

Severity of CVS as mild or moderate/severe was defined based on current guidelines. This was defined based on both episode duration and frequency and duration and need for emergency department visits and/or hospitalization. Patients who had < 4 episodes a year, lasting < 2 days, with a quick recovery and without any ED visits or hospitalizations were classified as having mild CVS. Those with any of the following: 4 or more episodes a year, duration of episodes which last no more than 2 days and need for ED visits and/or hospitalizations were classified as having moderate-to-severe CVS [17].

Subject Recruitment

Potential participants with CVS were recruited through the CVS clinic and consecutively offered the opportunity to participate in the study. Potential participants were also contacted through an established CVS patient registry. An e-consent form was used to obtain informed consent. All questionnaires were completed using REDCap, a HIPAA-compliant online research platform that utilizes data encryption, safeguarding data from unauthorized access during transmission and storage, ensuring confidentiality and integrity of research data.

Clinical Data

All participants completed a standard CVS intake questionnaire that asked about disease characteristics, comorbidities, treatment, and response to treatment. This was reconciled with chart review and patient interview as needed if any discrepancies were noted.

Assessment Tools

Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA)

The PG-SGA is a validated, comprehensive, multipart assessment used to determine malnutrition risk (PG-SGA-Short Form [SF]) and severity of a malnutrition diagnosis [15]. This is considered a 4-1 tool that can be used for screening, assessment, triage, and monitoring of interventions. It consists of two sections: a self-report questionnaire (boxes 1–4, also referred to as the PG-SGA-SF) and a professional evaluation (worksheets 1–5); each box/worksheet is individually scored and then totaled. Scores can range from 0 to 39, with higher numbers indicating a greater risk of malnutrition. Specifically, a score of 0–1 indicates no nutritional intervention required, 2–3 suggests the need for patient education or symptom management, 4–8 indicates moderate malnutrition and warrants RDN-led intervention, and ≥ 9 indicates severe malnutrition and signifies a critical need for urgent RDN-led intervention.

In addition, the professional evaluation using the PG-SGA classifies participants as well-nourished (A), moderately or suspected malnourished (B), or severely malnourished (C) [15].This assessment was performed by a RDN and integrated five domains: recent weight change, dietary intake, nutrition-impact symptoms, functional capacity, and physical examination findings. Overall nutritional status was determined based on the cumulative severity across these domains. All assessments were performed during the inter-episodic phase of a CVS episode. Those deemed well-nourished scored < 7 points while those with moderate-to-severe malnutrition had ≥ 7 points.

Cannabis Use Disorder Identification Test (CUDIT)

Cannabis use was assessed using the CUDIT-R, a validated tool used to screen for cannabis misuse [18]. This questionnaire consists of 8 questions that are each scored from 0 to 4 based on the response. A score > 12 indicates possible cannabis use disorder, and a score of 8–12 indicates hazardous cannabis use.

Malnutrition Screening Tool (MST)

The MST is a simple 2-item validated screening tool with a score of ≥ 2 indicating individuals who are at risk of becoming malnourished and who may benefit from an RDN assessment [19].

Food Security Questionnaire

The food security questionnaire is a standard 6-item questionnaire with households with scores of 0–1 characterized as food secure, while scores of 2–4 indicate low food security, and scores of 5–6 represent very low food security [19]

Statistical Analysis

Demographics and clinical characteristics were summarized and differences between the two groups (well-nourished vs. malnourished) based on RDN assessment were determined using Chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, T test for continuous variables, and exact Wilcoxon test for count variables. Univariate logistic models were developed to test the associations between nutritional status and various demographic and clinical factors. Multivariable logistic models were then conducted. Variables added to the model were selected a priori and based on important clinic factors and univariate analyses. SAS Studio (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) was used for all analyses. Two-sided tests were conducted, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 80 participants participated in the study and demographics and clinical characteristics based on nutrition status of patients who completed the PG-SGA are shown in Table 1. Mean age was 39 ± 16 years (range: 18–73), 62 (77.5%) were female, and 64 (80%) participants were White with 8 (10%) participants being Black. Eighteen % (n = 14) of participants lived in households that were food insecure. Most participants (n = 68, 85%) had moderate-to-severe CVS and were overweight or obese (n = 38, 47.5%). Participants with malnutrition compared to those without malnutrition were more likely to be female (85% vs. 54%, p = 0.01), have moderate-to-severe CVS (95% vs. 71%, p = 0.003), have a greater number of ED visits per year (3 vs. 0.5, p = 0.02) and live in households with food insecurity (25% vs. 4%, p = 0.05). There was no difference in nutrition status based on cannabis use, hazardous use, or cannabis use disorder, BMI, or use of prophylactic agents for CVS (all p > 0.05). Risk Assessments

Malnutrition Risk Assessed by PG-SGA-SF and MST

Malnutrition risk was assessed by both the PG-SGA-SF and the MST. Of the 80 participants who completed the PG-SGA SF, 27.5% (n = 22) were at risk for moderate malnutrition, 42.5% (n = 34) were at risk for severe malnutrition, with a mean score of 7.9 ± 6.5 (0.0– 27.0). Problems with eating due to symptom burden in the inter-episodic phase were reported in 71% (n = 57) of participants. When analyzing the MST as a comparison tool to the PG-SGA-SF, fewer participants were identified at nutritional risk with 51% (n = 41) being at risk of malnutrition with a score of ≥ 2 on the MST.

Diagnostic Accuracy of the PG-SGA SF and MST

The PG-SGA-SF had a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 71% for detecting malnutrition with a positive predictive value of 85% and a negative predictive value of 100%. The MST had a sensitivity of 75%, a specificity of 70%, a positive predictive value of 81% and a negative predictive value of 61.5%

Nutritional Status Assessed by PG-SGA

Evaluation by the RDN showed that 63% (n = 40) were malnourished, with 34% (n = 22) being moderately malnourished and 28% (n = 18) being severely malnourished.

On univariate analysis, female gender, moderate-to-severe CVS, a higher number of comorbidities and symptoms during the inter-episodic phase, and a positive screen on the MST were significantly associated with malnutrition (Table 2). On multivariate analysis, only an increase in the number of symptoms that interfered with eating in the inter-episodic phase was associated with malnutrition (OR 14.5, CI 2.3–92.7, p = 0.004). Discussion

Our results show profound rates of malnutrition in adults with moderate-to-severe CVS (62%). While there has been limited data on the prevalence of malnutrition in other vomiting disorders, such as gastroparesis and functional dyspepsia, one study in 305 patients in a national gastroparesis registry, 64% reported caloric-deficient diets, though rates of malnutrition from this were not studied [11]. Another study of 182,580 patients with gastroparesis using the Nationwide Inpatient Sample showed that 15% were diagnosed with malnutrition and had a higher risk of mortality, sepsis, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism compared to those without malnutrition [20]. In another study comparing diabetic vs non-diabetic gastroparesis, malnutrition was observed in 23% of patients with DM and 18% of those with non-diabetic gastroparesis and was a predictor of higher mortality in those with diabetes [21]. Challenges to each of these studies include differences in the diagnostic tool that was used to diagnose malnutrition and/or unvalidated approaches for diagnosis. Therefore, our findings are the first to identify malnutrition prevalence in CVS using a validated approach to diagnosis which further underscores the importance of diagnosing and treating malnutrition in CVS in clinical practice.

The PG-SGA-SF is a sensitive screener in this population, although it may overestimate the severity of malnutrition. This is likely driven by the large burden of symptoms in patients with CVS, even during the inter-episodic phase, although the overall prevalence of malnutrition diagnosed by the PG-SGA-SF compared to the RDN assessment was comparable. The MST demonstrated lower sensitivity than the PG-SGA-SF (75% vs. 100%, respectively) and, despite being a brief two-item screening tool, may have limited suitability for patients with CVS, as symptomology likely contributes to malnutrition risk which is not a component of the MST. However, the PG-SGA-SF can be completed by patients and administered through the electronic medical record or other applications, which facilitates its integration into clinical practice and reduces the burden on clinicians [22].

The presence of malnutrition in CVS is almost certainly masked by patients who are largely overweight or obese in this population, leading to a lack of recognition or regular screening for malnutrition. Although BMI is not a diagnostic tool for malnutrition, it is commonly used by healthcare professionals as a screening tool. The mean BMI was not different between well-nourished and malnourished participants which further supports the exclusion of BMI as a malnutrition screening tool. In clinical practice, RDN consultation is uncommon and therefore, nutritional status may be overlooked. Improving nutritional risk screening practices in CVS clinics may improve the frequency of RDN consultations. Understanding barriers to RDN consultation should be the focus of future research protocols in CVS. Additionally, increased awareness and education about the assessment of nutrition are required for all providers in a clinical setting. It is important that the care of these patients, particularly with a high symptom burden during the inter-episodic phase, includes screening for malnutrition with the PG-SGA-SF and/or MST with prompt referral to an RDN with positive screens. These data underscore the need for systematic nutritional assessment and support specialized, multidisciplinary care models that include an RDN as a core member of tertiary GI clinics to improve patient outcomes.

Other notable findings in this study were that only the presence of inter-episodic symptoms was associated with malnutrition risk on multivariate analysis. The presence of inter-episodic symptoms is variable in patients—while some have no symptoms during baseline, up to 60% of patients can have inter-episodic chronic nausea and or dyspepsia [8, 10]. While PG-SGA assessments were performed during the inter-episodic phase after resolution of the acute vomiting phase, timing from the prior episode was not recorded as inter-episodic symptoms may persist independent of episode proximity. It is plausible that patients who have episodes but return to normal between episodes can compensate and remain well-nourished as opposed to those who have a high burden of inter-episodic symptoms that preclude them from resuming normal intake even when an acute CVS flare has resolved. Investigation into usual dietary intake around episodes would be useful to determine changes in dietary intake around CVS episodes. In the present study, patients with CVS had a high burden of inter-episodic symptoms which interfered with normal eating patterns and were associated with malnutrition. Some patients with GI disorders have been found to have fears eating due to provoking symptoms. Triggers for CVS typically include stress, and food is seldom a trigger [9]. However, patients sometimes might associate certain foods with their CVS episodes and develop avoidant restrictive behaviors, which may be maladaptive. We have published a paper on the prevalence of Avoidant Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID) in this cohort and its impact on nutrition [13].

One limitation of this study is that these participants were seen at a tertiary care referral clinic, and most (85%) have moderate-to-severe CVS and are not representative of the general community. These data are less likely to be true in mild CVS, but further evaluation is necessary to confirm this. Despite this limitation, the focus on moderate-to-severe CVS allows health care providers to understand prevalence of malnutrition and consider implementation of routine nutrition risk screening practices. Prompt recognition and appropriate triage and early intervention may improve overall patient care outcomes, but further research on the impact of nutrition intervention and/or outcomes associated with malnutrition in CVS is warranted. These data would support a comprehensive multicenter care model that incorporates an RDN and routine screening for malnutrition of all patients with CVS, especially in specialized clinics, given the very high rates of malnutrition in this patient cohort.

Key Points

Most adults with CVS meet the criteria for moderate-to-severe malnutrition, highlighting the need for routine screening and referral to a Registered Dietitian Nutritionist.
