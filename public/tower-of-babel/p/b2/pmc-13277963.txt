A Randomized Controlled Phase 3 Trial of Oral Prednisolone Administration Versus Local Triamcinolone Injection Therapy for Esophageal Stricture Prevention After Extensive Endoscopic Submucosal Dissection (JCOG1217)
Authors: Masaki Tanaka, Hiroyuki Ono, Kohei Takizawa, Shintaro Iwamoto, Yusuke Sano, Seiichiro Abe, Tomohiro Kadota, Takuya Wada, Akiyoshi Ishiyama, Hiroyoshi Nakanishi, Shinji Nagata, Haruhiko Fukuda, Manabu Muto, Tomonori Yano, the Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Study Group of the Japan Clinical Oncology Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesEndoscopic local triamcinolone injection is a standard therapy for esophageal stricture (ES) prevention after extensive endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD); however, oral prednisolone administration is a promising alternative. This trial aimed to investigate the superiority of oral prednisolone administration to local triamcinolone injection for ES prevention after extensive ESD.MethodsThis phase 3, open‐label, multicenter, randomized controlled trial enrolled patients with extensive superficial esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. The primary endpoint was stricture‐free survival (SFS). Secondary endpoints were the number of endoscopic balloon dilations (EBDs) for 12 weeks after ESD, dysphagia score ≤ 1 at 12 weeks after ESD, and adverse events (AEs).ResultsA total of 281 patients from 45 institutions were enrolled. The 12‐week SFS rates were 88.5% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 81.6–92.9) and 94.8% (95% CI: 89.4–97.5) in the triamcinolone and prednisolone arms, respectively (hazard ratio 0.672, 90% CI [0.361–1.250], p = 0.14). Thirty‐eight and 26 EBDs were performed for 14 and 10 patients, respectively, in the triamcinolone and prednisolone arms within 12 weeks after ESD. In the triamcinolone and prednisolone arms, 73.8% (95% CI: 65.7–80.8) and 80.7% (95% CI: 73.2–86.9) of patients, respectively, had dysphagia scores ≤ 1 at 12 weeks after ESD (p = 0.20). Grade 3/4 AEs occurred in 12 (8.7%) and 9 patients (6.7%) in the triamcinolone and prednisolone arms, respectively.ConclusionsWhile oral prednisolone administration and local triamcinolone injection demonstrated comparable outcomes, oral prednisolone did not demonstrate superiority for ES prevention after extensive esophageal ESD.

Introduction

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is a standard treatment for superficial esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) [1, 2, 3, 4]; however, treatments for extensive lesions involving over three‐fourths of the luminal circumference often cause severe esophageal strictures (ES) [5, 6, 7]. Patients with ES may develop dysphagia and need to undergo multiple therapies of endoscopic balloon dilation (EBD) for a long period [8]. Therefore, the development of prophylactic therapy for ES after extensive ESD is crucial.

Endoscopic local injection of triamcinolone acetonide was reported to be effective for preventing ES after extensive ESD [9, 10]. While local triamcinolone has been accepted as the community standard prophylactic therapy, some case reports have described serious procedure‐related complications, including esophageal delayed perforation and infections [11, 12]. Additionally, the technical difficulty of local injection may lead to differences in treatment quality associated with the skills of operators.

The efficacy of prophylactic oral prednisolone administration after extensive ESD has also been reported [13]. Oral prednisolone is considered the most promising alternative therapy for ES prevention because of its excellent results. However, studies have been limited by small sample sizes, retrospective designs, and insufficient information regarding corticosteroid‐related systemic adverse events (AEs) [14, 15].

Therefore, we considered that oral prednisolone may be a more effective and easier, although slightly more toxic, treatment than local triamcinolone injection for ES prevention. Because no reliable study has directly compared these treatments, we conducted this study to investigate whether oral prednisolone is superior to local triamcinolone injection for ES prevention after extensive ESD.

Methods

This randomized phase 3 trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Japanese Clinical Trials Act. The Certified Review Boards of National Cancer Center Hospital approved the study protocol. Forty‐five institutions of the Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Study Group (GIESG) of the Japan Clinical Oncology Group (JCOG) participated in this study. The study protocol was approved by each participating institute. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

After registration using a web‐based system, patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive local triamcinolone injection or oral prednisolone utilizing a minimization method with a random component that used institution, luminal circumference of the primary lesion (less than three‐fourth versus three‐fourth or more), and lesion size in the longitudinal axis (< 30 mm versus ≥ 30 mm) to ensure balance. Patients were followed up until 1 year after the ESD.

Participants

The major inclusion criteria were as follows: histologically proven ESCC including high grade intraepithelial neoplasia or basaloid cell carcinoma; primary tumor located in the thoracic esophagus; main tumor depth of invasion was diagnosed as the epithelium or mucosal [16]; lymph node and distant metastases in neck, chest and abdomen were not confirmed by computed tomography; the luminal circumference of the primary lesion was one‐half or more, but not entire circumference, as determined by endoscopy; major lesion length was ≤ 50 mm in the longitudinal axis; aged 20–85 years; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1; and dysphagia score (DS) of 0. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in the Appendix S1.

Study Endpoints

The primary endpoint was stricture‐free survival (SFS), which was defined as days from randomization to either the first event of ES defined as DS ≥ 2 with an inability to pass a standard (9.6–10.4 mm) endoscope through the stricture site or performing EBD for any reason, or death from any cause. It was censored at the last day without ES when the patient received additional therapy including esophagostomy, radiotherapy, or chemoradiotherapy according to the guidelines [17]. Secondary endpoints were the number of required EBDs performed within 12 weeks after ESD, proportion of patients with DS ≤ 1 at 12 weeks after ESD, AEs, and serious AEs such as grade 4 non‐hematological toxicity, death within 30 days from protocol treatment, or treatment‐related death according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.0. The DS was defined as previously published [18].

Procedures

ESD was indicated within 28 days after registration and was performed in an inpatient setting. After making a marker circumferential around the tumor and submucosal injection, a circumferential mucosal incision and submucosal dissection were performed with electrosurgical devices. All ESD and local triamcinolone injection procedures were performed by certified endoscopists or other investigators under the supervision of the certified endoscopists. The principal investigator certified the endoscopists who experienced ≥ 50 cases of gastric ESD or ≥ 20 cases of esophageal ESD as operators or supervisors of ESD.

Local Triamcinolone Injection

Patients in the triamcinolone arm received a single triamcinolone injection to the mucosal defect immediately after ESD. Triamcinolone acetonide was diluted 1:1 with saline to create a 5 mg/mL solution. The total amount of triamcinolone was 100 mg/10 mL and an injection of 0.5–1.0 mL was administered to each site using a through‐the‐scope needle (23–25 gauge), as recommended [14]; however, injections were not performed in areas where the muscle layer was exposed. A proton pump inhibitor (PPI) and potassium‐competitive acid blocker (P‐CAB) were administered for 8 weeks after ESD. The safety of ESD and that of local triamcinolone injection were evaluated on days 1 and 2. If perioperative complications were observed at the study sites, the endoscopic images and movies were reviewed in group conferences as quality control.

Oral Administration of Prednisolone

Patients in the prednisolone arm received treatment for 8 weeks. Treatment was initiated 3 days after ESD. Prednisolone started at 30 mg/day and was tapered by 5 mg every 2 weeks until reaching 20 mg/day; then, it was tapered by 5 mg each week until reaching 0 mg [13]. Prophylactic antibiotics (trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole combination), PPI and P‐CAB were also administered for 8 weeks after ESD.

Data Acquisition and Patients Follow‐Up

After ESD, the DS, AEs, and laboratory tests were evaluated at 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 weeks and 1 year. Follow‐up endoscopy was scheduled 12 weeks after ESD. Additional endoscopy was indicated within 7 days if the patient's DS was 2 or more. EBD was performed when the DS of the patients was ≥ 2 and a standard‐sized endoscope (diameter: 9.6–10.4 mm) could not pass through the ESD site. EBD performed within 12 weeks after ESD was regarded as the protocol treatment. Any use of additional steroid therapy, including a second local triamcinolone injection or an additional dose of oral prednisolone, was prohibited. Additional EBD was performed according to the criteria for the initial EBD.

Statistical Analyses

This trial was designed to confirm the superiority of oral prednisolone over local triamcinolone injection in terms of SFS for patients. Based on unpublished retrospective research by the GIESG of the JCOG, which showed a 12‐week SFS of 79% in those receiving local triamcinolone injection, we assumed that the SFS at 12 weeks would be 80% in the triamcinolone arm. In contrast, the expected 12‐week SFS in the prednisolone arm was set at 90% after considering a 10% difference as clinically meaningful based on consensus among investigators and balancing the potential systemic AEs and burden of a 2‐month oral steroid regimen. The planned sample size was 360 to detect a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.565 in SFS with one‐sided alpha of 5%, power of 80%, planned accrual period of 2.5 years and a follow‐up period of 1 year. However, the planned sample size was revised from 360 to 280 with a power of 70% because of poor accrual in March 2019.

The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate SFS, and Greenwood's formula was used to estimate the confidence interval (CI) for 12‐week SFS. The stratified log‐rank test was applied to compare both arms using the luminal circumference and longitudinal length of the lesion as strata. HRs and their CIs were estimated using a stratified Cox regression model. Predetermined subgroup analyses in terms of SFS were performed according to the luminal circumference of the lesion before treatment, luminal circumference of the lesion at ESD, longitudinal tumor diameter, estimated depth of tumor invasion, presence of muscular layer injury, tumor location, and type of electrosurgical devices. Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables were used to compare secondary endpoints between the arms.

The efficacy analysis was performed in the intention‐to‐treat population, and the safety analysis was done in the per‐protocol population. All statistical analyses were conducted at the JCOG Data Center. Patient demographics, ESD and local injection procedures, pathological findings of ESD, outcomes, and safety data were collected. SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), was used to perform all statistical analyses. The trial was registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network Center (UMIN000015064), as well as in the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCTs031180118).

Efficacy

The SFS at 12 weeks was 88.5% (95% CI: 81.6–92.9) and 94.8% (95% CI: 89.4–97.5) in the triamcinolone arm and prednisolone arm, respectively (HR 0.67, 90% CI [0.361–1.250], 95% CI [0.321–1.408]; one‐sided p = 0.1442). The superiority of the prednisolone arm to the triamcinolone arm in SFS was not shown (Figure 2).

Stricture‐free survival of both treatment arms. The Stricture‐free survival (SFS) at 12 weeks was 88.5% (95% CI: 81.6–92.9) and 94.8% (95% CI: 89.4–97.5) in the local triamcinolone arm and oral prednisolone arm, respectively (HR 0.67, 90% CI [0.361–1.250], 95% CI [0.321–1.408]; one‐sided p = 0.1442).

Significant differences in secondary endpoints were not observed between arms. Thirty‐eight and 26 EBDs were performed for 14 patients and 10 patients, respectively, in the triamcinolone and prednisolone arms, respectively, within 12 weeks after ESD. In the triamcinolone arm, a median of 2 (range: 1–7) EBDs per patient were performed; in the prednisolone arm, a median of 2.5 (range: 1–5) EBDs per patient were performed (Wilcoxon rank sum test two‐sided p‐value = 0.38). The proportions of patients with a dysphagia score of 0 or 1 at 12 weeks after ESD were 73.8% (95% CI: 65.7%–80.8%) and 80.7% (95% CI: 73.2%–86.9%) in the triamcinolone arm and prednisolone arm, respectively (Fisher's exact test two‐sided p value = 0.20).

In the subgroup analysis, the 12‐week SFS of the patients with mucosal defect comprising ≤ 3/4th of the lumen was 100.0% (95% CI: 100.0–100.0) and 97.1% (95% CI: 80.9–99.6) in the triamcinolone arm and prednisolone arm, respectively (HR 1, 95% CI: not estimable). And, the 12‐week SFS of the patients with mucosal defect comprising ≥ 3/4th to < 7/8th of the lumen was 96.0% (95% CI: 84.9–99.0) and 96.5% (95% CI: 86.7–99.1) in the triamcinolone arm and prednisolone arm, respectively (HR 1.229, 95% CI [0.275–5.492]) (Figure 3a). For patients with mucosal defects comprising ≥ 7/8th of the lumen circumference, the SFS at 12 weeks was 75.2% (95% CI: 58.8–85.8) and 92.5% (95% CI: 78.5–97.5) in the triamcinolone arm and prednisolone arm, respectively (HR 0.545, 95% CI [0.198–1.502]) (Figure 3b). For patients with mucosal defects comprising the entire lumen circumference, the SFS at 12 weeks was 50.0% (95% CI: 11.1–80.4) and 75.0% (95% CI: 12.8–96.1) in the triamcinolone arm and prednisolone arm, respectively (HR 0.360, 95% CI [0.040–3.223]) (Figure 3c). No noteworthy variables were observed in the subgroup analysis.

Safety

The proportions of randomized patients completing the protocol treatment were comparable between the two treatment arms [107 (76%) and 112 (80%) patients in the triamcinolone and prednisolone arms, respectively]. The main reasons for protocol treatment discontinuation were treatment failure attributable to noncurative resection [28 and 19 patients in the triamcinolone and prednisolone arms, respectively]. The proportions of patients who completed the scheduled steroid administration were 97.9% (138/141) and 88.6% (124/140) in the triamcinolone and prednisolone arms, respectively (Figure 1).

Safety profiles are listed in Table 4. Grade 3/4 AEs were observed in 8.7% (12/138) and 6.7% (9/134) in the triamcinolone and prednisolone arms, respectively. One serious AE (grade 4 hyperglycemia) was observed in the prednisolone arm. Esophageal perforation during ESD was seen in 1.4% (2/138) in the triamcinolone arm and 1.5% (2/134) in the prednisolone arm among the protocol population. Delayed esophageal perforation was observed in two patients in the triamcinolone arm. In one patient, mediastinitis caused by delayed perforation occurred 8 days after ESD and local injection. The patient recovered and was discharged approximately 1 month after conservative treatment comprising endoscopic drainage and administration of antibiotics. In another patient, mediastinitis caused by delayed perforation occurred 10 days after ESD and local injection. That patient underwent surgical intervention for infection control immediately after the diagnosis. Esophageal perforation caused by EBD was observed in one patient in the triamcinolone arm and two patients in the prednisolone arm; however, they were treated without surgery. No treatment‐related deaths occurred.

Discussion

This is the first phase 3, randomized, controlled trial comparing the efficacy and safety of local triamcinolone injection and oral prednisolone for the ES prevention after ESD, and it showed that both prophylactic treatments effectively prevented esophageal stricture after extensive ESD.

Several studies have shown the efficacy of prophylactic agents or devices, including polyglycolic acid sheets for ES [19, 20, 21, 22]. Among these prophylactic options, corticosteroids are the most used because of their favorable outcomes, availability, and low cost [23]. However, the best method of corticosteroid administration and the optimal dosage remained unclear because of the lack of comparisons of local triamcinolone injection and oral prednisolone in a multicenter setting. With local injection, administration comprises a single session immediately after ESD, which provides the advantage of additional treatment, including esophagectomy or chemoradiotherapy, after ESD for cases of noncurative resection.

Both treatment arms showed acceptable safety profiles in terms of AEs. Systematic AEs associated with corticosteroids, such as hypertension, hyperglycemia, and infectious disease, were observed more in the prednisolone arm; however, the incidence of grade 3/4 AEs was only 6.7%. Among the five patients in the oral prednisolone arm who discontinued the protocol treatment because of AEs, only two (one with severe diarrhea and the other with infectious disease) were thought to be associated with the systemic effects of corticosteroids. On the other hand, all five patients in the local injection arm discontinued the protocol treatment because of AEs related to esophageal perforations. Among them, two experienced a delayed perforation that was considered related to ESD, followed by local injection. Delayed perforation after esophageal ESD has been published previously [12, 24, 25]. Such cases were observed only in the local injection arm, possibly because an inappropriate injection procedure, such as direct injection into the muscle layer of the ESD ulcer, may lead to esophageal perforation [26]. Thus, caution is necessary to avoid perforation.

According to the subgroup analysis, oral prednisolone might have greater potential for lesions with mucosal defects comprising ≥ 7/8 of the lumen. Additionally, combination therapy comprising local triamcinolone and oral prednisolone may be more effective than local triamcinolone alone for such subjects [27]. In this study, the statistical power and sample size were amended from 80% to 70% and from 360 to 280, and these amendments cannot be ruled out as causes of the negative findings of the primary and subgroup analyses. Therefore, further studies are needed to clarify the use of oral prednisolone. For patients with whole‐circumference mucosal defects, neither oral prednisolone nor local triamcinolone was sufficiently effective. Previous studies including stent placement have reported; however, appropriate therapy for ES prevention after whole‐circumference mucosal defect has not been established [28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. Therefore, more intensive treatments should be investigated.

This study had several limitations. First, the number of stricture events was lower than expected in both arms. Approximately 25% of the patients had mucosal defects comprising less than three‐quarters of the lumen circumference. Consequently, overestimation of the SFS rate may have occurred in the present study despite even randomization of the patients. Second, because patient enrollment was completed several years ago, subsequent advances in ESD techniques and perioperative management may limit the applicability of these findings to current clinical practice. Third, the triamcinolone dose was fixed at 100 mg; however, a multi‐institutional survey showed that more than half of the included institutions adjusted the dose [33]. Appropriate dose adjustment should be investigated to maximize treatment effects and safety. Finally, the generalizability remains lacking because we enrolled only Japanese with ESCC.

While the therapeutic effect was comparable, oral prednisolone did not demonstrate superiority to the standard care of local triamcinolone injection in terms of ES prevention after esophageal ESD. However, oral prednisolone may be a potential alternative option to local triamcinolone injection for extensive lesions.
