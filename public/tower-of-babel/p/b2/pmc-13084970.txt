Anti-TIM-3 antibody TQB2618 in combination with penpulimab in relapsed or refractory classic Hodgkin lymphoma previously treated with PD-1/PD-L1 therapy: a multicenter, open-label, single-arm, phase Ib clinical trial
Authors: Huangming Hong, Zegeng Chen, Jie Cui, Qingyuan Zhang, Ping Li, Rong Tao, He Huang, Binghua Wang, Wuping Li, Hui Wu, Jin Wei, Liping Su, Zhihua Zhang, Mingxing Zhong, Huaqing Wang, Cong Wang, Hui Zhou, Jishi Wang, Min Dong, Tongyu Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors achieve high response rates in relapsed or refractory (r/r) classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL), but few treatment options are available for patients who experience failure after PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. T cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3, a key mediator of immune escape from PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition, is targeted by TQB2618, a humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody. This phase Ib study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of TQB2618 in combination with the anti-PD-1 antibody penpulimab in patients with r/r cHL.MethodsThis multicenter phase 1b study was conducted from June 2022 to September 2024 at 12 sites in China (NCT05400876). The study included a dose-escalation phase in which patients with r/r lymphoma received TQB2618 (600 or 1200 mg, every 3 weeks, Q3W) plus penpulimab (200 mg, Q3W) to evaluate dose-limiting toxicities and determine the recommended phase II dose (RP2D). The subsequent dose-expansion phase enrolled patients with r/r cHL previously treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors to assess the objective response rate (ORR).Results10 patients with r/r lymphoma were enrolled in the dose-escalation phase, and TQB2618 (600 mg, Q3W) plus penpulimab (200 mg, Q3W) was selected as the RP2D. In the dose-expansion phase, 18 additional patients with r/r cHL previously treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors were enrolled. Among the total 21 r/r cHL patients, the median age was 32 years (range, 22–65), and 12 (57%) were male. The ORR was 52%, including 1 complete response and 10 partial responses. Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) occurred in 18 patients (86%), with grade ≥3 TRAEs in 5 (24%). The most common TRAEs (≥20%) were platelet count decreased (24%), anemia (24%), and aspartate aminotransferase increased (24%). As of the data cut-off in December 2024, the median follow-up was 14.1 months, the median duration of response and the median overall survival had not yet been reached.ConclusionsTQB2618 in combination with penpulimab was well tolerated and demonstrated promising efficacy in patients with cHL who had failed prior anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy, supporting its potential as a therapeutic option for this difficult-to-treat population.Trial registration numberNCT05400876.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Lymphoma

Introduction

The majority of patients with classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) can be cured with frontline therapy; however, approximately 20% still develop primary refractory or experience relapse.1 cHL is characterized by rare malignant Hodgkin Reed-Sternberg cells within an abundant inflammatory microenvironment.2 Genomic studies have demonstrated near-universal copy gain of chromosome 9p24.1, leading to overexpression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 and enhanced PD-1 signaling.3 These features contribute to the marked sensitivity of cHL to PD-1 blockade and make it a compelling model for studying immune checkpoint-based combination strategies. Accordingly, PD-1 inhibitors have become an important therapeutic option, offering meaningful efficacy and favorable tolerability as monotherapy in the relapsed/refractory (r/r) patients.4 5 In addition, growing evidence from clinical trials suggests that incorporating immunotherapy into first-line treatment may improve clinical outcomes in advanced-stage patients.6,8 Although anti-PD-1 antibodies achieve relatively high objective response rates (ORR), complete remission (CR) rates remain modest. Even among responders, disease control is often temporary, with progression frequently occurring within 1–2 years.9 10 Emerging evidence indicates that anti-PD-1 rechallenge retains clinically meaningful activity in r/r cHL, including patients previously exposed or refractory to PD-1 inhibitors.10 11 Moreover, ongoing studies are evaluating combination strategies that pair PD-1 inhibitors with chemotherapy or targeted agents such as brentuximab vedotin.12,14

T cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3) was first described on interferon-γ-secreting T cells and has since been recognized as a critical immune-checkpoint molecule.15 16 Beyond immune cells, TIM-3 has also been reported on malignant cells such as myeloid leukemia, cutaneous melanoma, and colon carcinoma, among others.17 18 Multiple studies have demonstrated that tumors developing acquired resistance to PD-1 blockade exhibit compensatory upregulation of alternative inhibitory receptors, particularly TIM-3, on tumor-infiltrating T cells.19 20 Evidence from both preclinical models and clinical studies across diverse malignancies indicates that dual blockade of TIM-3 and PD-1/PD-L1 exerts antitumor activity and may help restore antitumor immunity in tumors resistant to PD-1 inhibition.21 22 However, combined PD-1 and TIM-3 inhibition has not been investigated in cHL.

TQB2618 is a humanized IgG4 antibody targeting TIM-3. A recent study indicated that incorporating TQB2618 into standard regimens may provide clinical benefit in the first-line treatment of recurrent or metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma.23 We hypothesized that TQB2618 in combination with the PD-1 inhibitor penpulimab might provide clinical activity in patients with PD-1-exposed cHL. Therefore, we conducted a phase Ib study to assess the safety and efficacy of TQB2618 plus penpulimab in patients with r/r cHL.

Methods

Participants

This study was a multicenter, open-label, phase Ib trial conducted from June 2022 to September 2024 at 12 sites in China. Patients were consecutively enrolled from participating centers based on routine clinical referral and eligibility screening. Eligible participants included patients with r/r lymphoma in the dose-escalation phase and those with confirmed r/r cHL who had received PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor therapy, enrolled in the dose-expansion phase. Additional criteria included measurable disease according to Lugano 2014, age 18–75 years, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 0–1, an expected survival ≥3 months, and adequate organ function. Women of childbearing potential were required to be non-pregnant, non-lactating, and have a negative pregnancy test.

Key exclusion criteria were the presence of another active malignancy within 3 years (except adequately treated basal cell carcinoma or cervical carcinoma in situ), unresolved toxicities from prior therapy (>Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade 1), recent major surgery or severe trauma (within 28 days), unhealed wounds or fractures, significant bleeding risk or bleeding events (≥CTCAE grade 3 within 4 weeks), or arterial/venous thrombosis within 6 months. Patients with psychiatric illness, severe uncontrolled comorbidities, central nervous system (CNS) lymphoma, hemophagocytic syndrome, prior allogeneic transplantation, or autologous transplantation/chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy within 3 months were excluded. Patients with any condition deemed by the investigator to compromise study participation were also ineligible. Details are provided in the trial protocol (online supplemental file 1).

Study design

The study consisted of a dose-escalation phase followed by dose expansion. During dose escalation, a traditional 3+3 design was applied to determine the recommended phase II dose (RP2D). Patients received TQB2618 at either 600 mg or 1200 mg every 3 weeks (Q3W) in combination with penpulimab 200 mg Q3W, and dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) were closely monitored. Based on dose-escalation findings, one dose level was selected for further investigation in the expansion phase, in which additional patients were enrolled to further evaluate the safety and efficacy of TQB2618 combined with penpulimab.

In both the dose-escalation and expansion cohorts, patients received TQB2618 in combination with penpulimab on a 21-day cycle. Therapy was continued in successive cycles until the occurrence of intolerable toxicity, withdrawal by the investigator or patient, disease progression, death, loss to follow-up, or study closure.

The primary objectives were to evaluate DLTs and determine the RP2D of TQB2618 in combination with penpulimab during the dose-escalation phase, and to assess the ORR based on the Lugano 2014 criteria during the dose-expansion phase. Secondary endpoints included the investigator-assessed complete response rate; the disease control rate (DCR), defined as the proportion of patients achieving CR, partial remission (PR), or stable disease (SD); the duration of response (DoR), defined as the time from the first documented CR or PR to disease progression or death; progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from randomization to progression or death; and overall survival (OS), defined as the time from randomization to death from any cause. Additional secondary endpoints involved the overall rates of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs).

Outcomes

DLTs were evaluated during the first treatment cycle and included both hematologic and non-hematologic events. Hematologic DLTs were defined as grade 3 febrile neutropenia, grade 3 thrombocytopenia with bleeding, or any grade ≥4 hematologic toxicity. Non-hematologic DLTs included grade 3 vomiting or diarrhea not resolving to grade ≤1 within 7 days despite standard management, grade 3 rash or pruritus not resolving to grade ≤1 within 14 days, and grade 3 infusion reactions not resolving to grade ≤1 within 6 hours after infusion discontinuation. Other grade ≥3 non-hematologic toxicities, excluding controllable symptoms such as fatigue, asthenia, or nausea, were also considered DLTs.

TEAEs were evaluated according to the CTCAE (V.5.0). Safety monitoring, including vital signs, laboratory tests, and 12-lead electrocardiograms, was conducted at baseline, on day 1 of each treatment cycle, and within 7 days after the last dose. AEs were coded by preferred term and summarized by the highest severity and causality. Safety data were reviewed across dose levels, with interim analyses performed before dose escalation and study expansion. TRAEs were determined by investigator assessment of possible treatment association.

Tumor response was assessed according to the Lugano 2014 criteria for lymphomas, with CT/MRI or PET/CT scans performed at baseline and at scheduled intervals. Imaging evaluations were conducted every two cycles (weeks 6, 12, 18, and 24) during the first 6 months, every four cycles (weeks 36 and 48) from 6 months to 1 year, and every eight cycles thereafter until disease progression, withdrawal, or study closure.

Statistical analysis

The dose-escalation phase followed a standard 3+3 design, with 3 patients initially treated at each dose level to evaluate safety. Based on tolerability and preliminary efficacy observed in this stage, an additional 18 patients were enrolled in the expansion cohort. Safety and efficacy were analyzed in r/r cHL patients using the full analysis set, which included all patients who received at least one dose of TQB2618 in combination with penpulimab. Response rates with 95% CIs were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method, and survival outcomes (PFS, OS, DoR) were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier approach. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (V.4.4.1).

Results

Patients

Between June 2022 and September 2023, 10 patients with r/r lymphoma were enrolled in the dose-escalation phase, including 3 (30%) with cHL, 6 (60%) with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and 1 (10%) with peripheral T-cell lymphoma. Among these patients, the median age was 53 years (range, 29–71), and all had an ECOG performance status of 0–1. The median number of prior lines of therapy was 3 (range, 2–11). In the dose-expansion phase, another 18 patients with r/r cHL were enrolled (figure 1). Among the total 21 r/r cHL patients, 12 (57%) were men, and the median age was 32 years (range, 22–65). 17 patients (81%) had advanced-stage disease at baseline. The median number of prior therapy lines was 4 (range, 1–8). All patients had received prior PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and experienced disease progression after an initial response. Four patients (19%) had been treated with brentuximab vedotin, and two (10%) had undergone autologous stem cell transplantation (table 1).

Safety

During the dose-escalation phase, no DLTs were observed among four patients receiving TQB2618 at 600 mg Q3W. At the 1200 mg Q3W dose level, one of the first three patients experienced a DLT (grade 4 thrombocytopenia), and no additional DLTs occurred among the subsequent three patients. Treatment-related grade ≥3 adverse events were slightly more frequent at 1200 mg, whereas no corresponding improvement in antitumor activity was observed at this higher dose level (online supplemental figure S1). Based on the overall safety profile and clinical activity observed in this phase, TQB2618 at 600 mg Q3W combined with penpulimab 200 mg Q3W was selected as the RP2D.

A total of 20/21 patients (95%) experienced TEAEs, and 10/21 (48%) had grade ≥3 events (table 2). Hematologic TEAEs were predominant, with lymphocyte count decreased (11/21, 52%), anemia (10/21, 48%), white blood cell count decreased (7/21, 33%), platelet count decreased (5/21, 24%), and neutrophil count decreased (4/21, 19%) being most common. Grade ≥3 hematologic TEAEs included lymphocyte count decreased (3/21, 14%), anemia (2/21, 10%), and white blood cell count decreased (2/21, 10%). Among nonhematologic TEAEs, hyperuricemia (7/21, 33%) and aspartate aminotransferase increased (6/21, 29%) were most frequent, followed by several events observed in 4/21 patients (19%), including diarrhea, upper respiratory tract infection, fever, gamma-glutamyl transferase increased, blood bilirubin increased, alanine aminotransferase increased, and hypertriglyceridemia. Grade ≥3 nonhematologic TEAEs were uncommon and included CD4 lymphocyte count decreased (2/21, 10%) and single cases (1/21, 5%) of fever, hypokalemia, lipase increased, decreased appetite, peripheral neuropathy, pneumonia, nephrotic syndrome, and immune-mediated enterocolitis. Dose-level TEAEs are summarized in online supplemental table S1. A higher incidence of hematologic TEAEs was observed in the 1200 mg cohort compared with the 600 mg cohort.

TRAEs occurred in 18/21 patients (86%), and 5/21 (24%) experienced grade≥3 events (table 2). Hematologic TRAEs were most common, including anemia (5/21, 24%), platelet count decreased (5/21, 24%), white blood cell count decreased (4/21, 19%), and neutrophil count decreased (4/21, 19%). Grade ≥3 hematologic TRAEs consisted of white blood cell count decreased (2/21, 10%), anemia (2/21, 10%), and lymphocyte count decreased (2/21, 10%). Among nonhematologic TRAEs, aspartate aminotransferase increased (5/21, 24%) was most frequent, followed by alanine aminotransferase increased (4/21, 19%), fever (3/21, 14%), fatigue (3/21, 14%), and hyperuricemia (3/21, 14%). Grade ≥3 nonhematologic TRAEs were uncommon and included single cases (1/21, 5%) of fever, nephrotic syndrome, immune-related peripheral neuropathy, pneumonia, and enterocolitis. Dose-level TRAEs are summarized in online supplemental table S2, with hematologic TRAEs also occurring more frequently in the 1200 mg cohort than in the 600 mg cohort.

Six patients (29%) experienced TEAEs that led to dose interruption, of which four (19%) were considered treatment-related, including pneumonia (10%), pancytopenia (5%), and blood myoglobin increased (5%). No dose reductions were implemented in any cohort during the study. Four patients (19%) experienced TEAEs that resulted in permanent treatment discontinuation, including local infection (5%), peripheral neuropathy (5%), COVID-19 (5%), and ileal perforation (5%). Among these events, peripheral neuropathy was deemed treatment-related. No treatment-related deaths occurred during the study.

Efficacy

A final data cut-off for efficacy analyses was performed in December 2024, with a median follow-up of 14.1 months. All 21 patients with r/r cHL were evaluable for efficacy analysis. Eleven patients (52%) achieved an overall response, including 10 PR (48%) and 1 CR (5%). An additional patients (14%) had SD, resulting in a DCR of 67% (14 patients). In addition, 8 of 11 responders (73%) maintained their responses (figure 2).

At the data cut-off, 10 patients had experienced disease progression and 2 had died. The estimated median PFS was 5.1 months (95% CI 1.4 to NR) (figure 3A), with 6-month and 12-month PFS rates of 45.9% (95% CI 23.9% to 65.5%) and 39.4% (95% CI 18.2% to 60.1%), respectively. The median OS had not been reached (figure 3B). The median time to response was 1.4 months (range, 1.2–8.7). The estimated median DoR had not been reached (figure 3C). No significant differences in PFS, OS, or DoR were observed between the 1200 mg and 600 mg cohorts (online supplemental figure S2).

Discussion

In this trial evaluating TQB2618 combined with penpulimab for r/r cHL, we observed marked antitumor activity in heavily pretreated patients with disease progression after prior PD-1 blockade. Responses were sustained, with the median DoR not yet reached. These findings, although requiring validation in larger studies, suggest that TIM-3 inhibition may contribute to the observed antitumor effects in patients previously treated with PD-1/PD-L1 therapy. Previous studies in solid tumors have shown that TIM-3 monotherapy has limited activity,24 25 and that dual TIM-3/PD-1 inhibition in patients with primary PD-1/PD-L1 resistance provides minimal benefit, with ORRs of approximately 10%.21 26 By contrast, our trial demonstrated that combined TIM-3/PD-1 blockade achieved substantial clinical efficacy in patients with PD-1/PD-L1-exposed r/r cHL. This discrepancy likely reflects the unique biology of cHL, characterized by a highly immunogenic tumor microenvironment, frequent 9p24.1 alterations resulting in abundant PD-L1/PD-L2 expression, and strong dependence on immune evasion pathways.27 In addition, previous studies have reported that TIM-3 is frequently expressed within the cHL tumor microenvironment, suggesting that TIM-3-mediated immune exhaustion may contribute to immune escape in this disease.28 Recent studies further suggest that the remarkable sensitivity of cHL to PD-1 blockade is associated with clonally diverse and expanding CD4+T cells, along with contributions from innate immune effectors such as activated NK cells.29 Taken together, these findings highlight cHL as an ideal disease model for exploring novel checkpoint inhibitor combinations.

The combination of TIM-3 inhibition with PD-1 blockade was generally well tolerated. In our cohort, most TRAEs were grade 1–2 laboratory abnormalities or cytopenias, grade ≥3 hematologic events occurred in fewer than 10% of patients, and non-hematologic toxicities were infrequent and manageable. Although cytopenias were relatively frequent at the laboratory level, they were mostly mild and likely reflected multiple contributing factors, including treatment-related effects, prior therapy-associated bone marrow vulnerability, and potential disease-related factors such as bone marrow involvement and tumor-associated inflammatory processes. Consistent with these findings, previous studies of cobolimab (TIM-3 inhibitor) plus dostarlimab (PD-1 inhibitor) in melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer reported grade ≥3 TRAEs in 11%–28% of patients, most of which were controllable, with no treatment-related deaths.21 26 Notably, two patients (~10%) in our study developed serious immune-related adverse events, including immune-mediated pneumonitis/enterocolitis and peripheral neuropathy. While these irAEs fall within the expected spectrum of PD-1-related toxicity,30 31 their occurrence highlights the need for close monitoring when dual checkpoint blockade is applied. Importantly, our favorable safety outcomes were observed despite nearly 80% of patients having received ≥3 prior lines of therapy, underscoring the feasibility of TIM-3/PD-1 co-inhibition in r/r cHL.

Previous studies have suggested that retreatment with PD-1 inhibitors may induce responses in a subset of patients who progress after prior PD-1 therapy, although the depth of response is often limited.10 11 Consistent with this observation, although the ORR observed in this study was encouraging, the CR rate remained modest. A similar pattern has been reported with other strategies investigated after progression on prior PD-1 therapy. For example, the LAG-3 inhibitor favezelimab combined with pembrolizumab achieved an ORR of 29% with a CR rate of 9% in patients who had failed prior PD-1 therapy,32 while HDAC inhibitor vorinostat combined with pembrolizumab yielded an ORR of 56% with a CR rate of 11% in PD-1 refractory cHL.33 Notably, JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib plus nivolumab produced an ORR of 53% with a CR rate of 32% in patients previously treated with checkpoint inhibitors,34 suggesting that incorporating agents with distinct immunomodulatory mechanisms may enhance response depth. Importantly, all patients in our cohort had experienced disease progression after an initial response to prior PD-1/PD-L1 therapy, consistent with secondary resistance. Such resistance may involve multiple adaptive immune escape mechanisms, including upregulation of alternative inhibitory pathways such as LAG-3 and TIM-3, immune microenvironment rewiring, impaired antigen presentation, and tumor-intrinsic alterations in interferon signaling.2835,37 Consequently, strategies focusing solely on immune checkpoint blockade may restore partial antitumor immunity, but may be insufficient to consistently produce deep or durable CRs. Future studies should therefore focus on mechanism-guided combination strategies that more comprehensively address immune resistance pathways while improving remission depth and preserving tolerability.

This study has several limitations. As a phase I trial, it was constrained by its open-label design, lack of randomization, and absence of a control arm. Moreover, the relatively small sample size and limited follow-up reduce the ability to fully characterize long-term safety and durability of response. In addition, the study did not include biomarker analyses, which limits mechanistic interpretation and the ability to identify predictive correlates of response. Future studies incorporating biomarker analyses including TIM-3 and PD-L1 expression profiling may help elucidate the biological basis of response to dual checkpoint inhibition and refine the identification of patients more likely to benefit from this strategy.

In summary, the combination of TQB2618 and penpulimab was well tolerated and showed meaningful activity in r/r cHL patients who had progressed on prior PD-1/PD-L1 therapy. These results suggest that dual TIM-3/PD-1 inhibition may provide a new therapeutic avenue for patients with PD-1-exposed cHL.
