New experimental insights into the biology of avian malaria parasites
Authors: Gediminas Valkiūnas, Tatjana A Iezhova, Carolina Romeiro Fernandes Chagas, Mélanie Tchoumbou, Tanja Himmel, Mary E Petrone, Mélanie Duc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite much research on avian malaria, the details of development of Plasmodium species in birds remain insufficiently investigated. This study aimed to gain new experimental information on avian malaria parasites regarding their host specificity, molecular characterization, persistence, and association with Matryoshka RNA viruses (MaRNAV), which are linked to haemosporidian infections.MethodsThree species of widespread avian Plasmodium—P. circumflexum pTURDUS1, P. vaughani pSYAT05, and P. relictum pCOLL7—were found in naturally infected wild birds of the Muscicapidae, Turdidae and Phylloscopidae, respectively. The parasites were transferred to non-infected Eurasian siskins Spinus spinus (Fringillidae) by inoculation of infected blood, and infections were monitored up to 185 days. Microscopy, histology, chromogenic in situ hybridization (CISH), PCR, RNA sequencing and bioinformatic methods were applied in this study.ResultsAll recipient birds were susceptible to these pathogens. Extensive histology and CISH testing did not detect any tissue stages in all birds, suggesting that long-lasting persistence is mainly due to erythrocytic merogony. However, in the case of pCOLL7, parasitaemia consisted almost exclusively of gametocytes, presenting an unusual mode of parasitaemia in avian malaria. Based on morphological and phylogenetic data, pCOLL7 was linked to P. relictum. Morphological characterization showed that taxonomic characters of blood stages of all three parasite species are relatively stable across host species, supporting the value of the morphological characters of blood stages in Plasmodium species identification in birds belonging to different families. Furthermore, this study detected and associated a Matryoshka RNA virus (MaRNAV-9) with avian Plasmodium parasites for the first time. This virus was found in association with the bird malaria parasite P. (Giovannolaia) circumflexum (pTURDUS1) widespread in Eurasia, which can be used as a model host-parasite system to better understand the biology of MaRNAVs and their relationship with haemosporidians.ConclusionsThis study provides new information to better understand diagnostics and persistence of avian malaria parasites in avian hosts. It also contributes to new knowledge about the diversity of MaRNAVs associated with Plasmodium pathogens, opening opportunities for detecting these viruses in association with malaria parasites.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05879-z.Keywords: Plasmodium, Bird malaria, Persistence, Matryoshka RNA viruses

Background

Avian malaria parasites of the genus Plasmodium (Plasmodiidae, Haemosporida) are cosmopolitan mosquito-transmitted pathogens that often are responsible for bird diseases, which can be severe in non-adapted host species [1]. Over 55 species of avian Plasmodium parasites were described morphologically and named [2], and 5323 unique genetic mitochondrial cytochrome b (cytb) lineages were reported and can be used for diagnostic purposes of avian malaria infections ([3], MalAvi database, accessed November 2025). Several recent reviews summarised findings on various aspects of taxonomy, genetics, distribution, transmission, ecology, and diagnostics of these blood parasites [1, 2, 4–7]. The number of publications on avian malaria has been increasing exponentially in the PubMed database during the past 25 years. However, most of these studies deal mainly with genetic lineage information detected in wildlife. The basic knowledge on subtleties of the parasites’ development and persistence in avian hosts remain insufficiently addressed or is fragmentary. For example, it is well known that malaria infections can persist in individual avian hosts after a single infection for many years [8, 9], but the mechanisms of persistence remain unclear. Tissue stages might be involved in the parasite survival in avian hosts [10], but malarial infections probably can also persist exclusively due to long-lasting erythrocytic merogony as in human malaria parasite Plasmodium malariae [7, 11–15]. Both these modes of long-lasting persistence probably occur in avian Plasmodium species, but the disease consequences might be different due to involvement of organ-damaging tissue stages or not. This issue remains unclear for most described avian Plasmodium species.

Experimental research is needed to better understand the biology of avian malaria parasites, but such studies remain exceptionally rare [1, 15], partly due to ethical limitation in conducting research with wild birds, which are the hosts of most described Plasmodium species. Thus, susceptible experimental hosts, which can be purchased commercially and are easy to maintain in captivity are needed. Traditionally, the domestic canaries Serinus canaria forma domestica were often used in avian malaria experimental research [16, 17]. However, this bird species was resistant to many recently tested Plasmodium isolates or developed only low-level transient infections, limiting its suitability for experimental research with some common malaria pathogens. Recent studies showed that the Eurasian siskin Spinus spinus was susceptible to experimental exposure of many avian malaria isolates, importantly often resulting in high and/or long-lasting parasitaemia [18]. This provides opportunities for targeted controlled laboratory observations and sampling of various data for genetic and parasitology research [14]. As it is the case with domestic canaries, Eurasian siskins are readily bred in captivity and can be purchased commercially, providing opportunities to solve permit issues by avoiding wild-caught birds in experimental investigations.

As a part of an ongoing study on the association of haemosporidian parasites with viral infections, experiments were performed aiming to test the susceptibility of Eurasian siskins to three isolates of Plasmodium species and to contribute to diagnostics of these infections. Traditional microscopic and histological observations were combined with chromogenic in situ hybridization (CISH) methods for detection of tissues stages of the parasites in naturally infected and experimentally exposed bird species. The parasite donor birds were also tested for haemosporidian parasite-associated Matryoshka RNA viruses (MaRNAV), which were recently discovered in closely related Leucocytozoon parasites in Oceania, America and in Europe [19–21] but remain undetected during avian Plasmodium infections (Supplementary Table 1).

Methods

Parasites

The experimental strains of Plasmodium (Haemamoeba) sp., Plasmodium (Giovannolaia) sp. and Plasmodium (Novyella) sp. were found and isolated from single naturally infected individuals of wood warbler Phylloscopus sibilatrix (parasitaemia intensity was 0.3%), the red-breasted flycatcher Ficedula parva (0.02%) and the common blackbird Turdus merula (0.6%), respectively. The parasite-donor birds were caught at Ventės Ragas Ornithological Station (55° 21' N, 21° 12' E) in May 2023. About 35 µl of whole blood was taken in heparinized microcapillaries by puncturing the brachial vein and stored in SET-buffer [22] for molecular analysis. For detection of MaRNAV, approximately the same amount of blood was stored in Trizol (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania) and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. A drop of freshly withdrawn blood was used to make three thin blood films, which were air-dried, fixed in absolute methanol, stained with Giemsa, and examined under light microscope, as described by Valkiūnas [9]. The parasites were identified to subgeneric level in the field using microscopic examination of blood films, while parasite species identification was done later in the laboratory using available keys [2] after the extensive microscopic examination of blood films prepared from wild donor birds and the exposed Eurasian siskins (see description below). Barcoding partial mitochondrial cytochrome b (cytb) sequences of these parasites were obtained in laboratory (see description below).

Microscopic examination

Several models of Olympus light microscopes were used for examination of blood films in the field and laboratory. The Olympus BX61 microscope equipped with an Olympus DP70 digital camera and the imaging software AnalySIS FIVE (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used for morphological research and preparation of parasite illustrations. Intensity of parasitaemia was determined as a percentage by counting of the number of parasites per 10,000 erythrocytes [9]. Voucher blood films from the wild-caught parasite donor bird species and the exposed Eurasian siskins with blood stages of Plasmodium relictum (lineage pCOLL7, 49878–49895 NS), P. circumflexum (pTURDUS1, 49896–49906 NS) and P. vaughani (pSYAT05, 49907–49918 NS) were deposited at the State Scientific Research Institute Nature Research Centre, Vilnius.

Experimental design

Eurasian siskins were purchased commercially in the Netherlands (Vogelhandel Van der Weegen, Steenbergen); they were uninfected as determined both by microscopic examination and PCR-based testing before the start of experiments. Each parasite strain was inoculated into three Eurasian siskins, as described by Valkiūnas et al. [14]. Briefly, approximately 50 µl of blood was taken from each donor bird and mixed with 12.5 µl sodium citrate and 62.5 µl of 0.9% saline solution. This mixture was divided into three equal portions and inoculated in pectoral muscle of three Eurasian siskins immediately after preparation of the mixture. Blood films were prepared and examined under microscope as described above. Each recipient bird was inoculated with infected blood containing zero microscope-visible erythrocytic meront of P. relictum (pCOLL7, only gametocytes were seen), 3.5 × 103 erythrocytic meronts of P. circumflexum (pTURDUS1) and 2.5 × 105 erythrocytic meronts of P. vaughani (pSYAT05). Six additional uninfected Eurasian siskins were maintained in the same vivarium room as negative controls to monitor the absence of inside-vivarium malaria transmission. All individuals were maintained in individual separate cages (90 × 50 × 90 cm) located in the same indoors vector-free licenced vivarium under controlled conditions (natural light–dark photoperiod and temperature of ~ 20 °C). They were offered water and a standard high-quality dry food (Deli Nature, BEDUCO NV, Belgium) ad libitum.

Infected bird groups were maintained 181–185 days post infection (dpi). The control group was maintained during the entire experiment. Blood from the recipient birds was taken every 4 days. The brachial vein was punctured with a sterile needle, and approximately 35 µl of blood was collected in heparinized microcapillaries. A small drop of blood was used to make two thin blood films, which were air-dried, fixed by dipping in absolute methanol, stained with Giemsa, and examined microscopically as described above. Remaining blood was fixed in SET buffer for molecular analysis; these samples were maintained at − 20 °C. Control birds were examined five times during this study (beginning, middle, and end of the experiment). Blood samples from experimental birds were collected in Trizol at the beginning of experiment (5 dpi), the middle of experiment (15–61 dpi) and the end of experiment (182, 183, 185 dpi). In all, 39 blood samples were collected for MaRNAV testing.

Wild birds serving as parasite donors were euthanized in the field while all recipient birds were euthanized at the end of the experiment, except for one bird that died at 57 dpi. From each bird, the brain, heart, kidney, liver, lungs, spleen, pectoral muscle, stomach and intestines were collected, fixed with 10% neutral formalin for 24 h, washed in distilled water and stored in 70% ethanol (up to one month) before being embedded in paraffin blocks (see description below). Additionally, a thin smear of bone marrow was prepared, fixed with absolute methanol, stained with 10% Giemsa, and examined as blood films [9].

Molecular and phylogenetic analyses

DNA was extracted following an ammonium acetate protocol from SET-buffer stored blood samples from all 12 individuals [23]. Extracted DNA was diluted to 25 ng/µl for the nested PCR. The primers HAEMNFI and HAEMNR3 were used in the first PCR with the extracted DNA as template, while the primers HAEMF and HAEMR2 were used for Plasmodium/Haemoproteus detection and the primers HAEMFL and HAEMR2L were used for Leucocytozoon detection with the PCR products from the first PCR as templates [22]. A second nested PCR was performed, using the primers Plas1F and HAEMNR3 followed by 3760 F and HAEMJR4 [24]. A positive control (already known Plasmodium positive sample) and a negative control (distilled water) were used to check the success of the PCRs. PCR profiles were according to the original protocols [23, 25]. Positive amplifications were checked on 2% agarose gels, prepared for sequencing, and sequenced with Sanger bi-directional sequencing with a Big Dye Terminator V3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit and ABI PRISM™ 3100 capillary sequencing robot (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA).

Chromatograms of sequences were viewed and analyzed using the software Geneious Prime 2023.2.1 (https://www.geneious.com). All sequences were BLASTed in MalAvi [3] and NCBI GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/) databases to identify the lineages. Detected parasite DNA sequences were deposited in GenBank (accessions PX880594-PX880604).

Twenty-three Plasmodium sequences, three of which were obtained in this study, and one Leucocytozoon sp. sequence (lSISKIN2, the outgroup) were used to construct a phylogeny in Geneious. The model GTR + I + G was suggested as the best fit model by the software jmodeltest-2.1.10 [26, 27] with the criteria AIC and AICc, and it was implemented with the plugin Mr.Bayes v2.2.4 [28]. Five million generations were calculated, sampled every 100th generation and 25% of the trees were discarded before the consensus tree assembly. The tree was finalized in CorelDraw 2019 (RRID:SCR_014235, https://www.coreldraw.com/en/).

Histology and chromogenic in situ hybridization

For histology, organs stored in 70% ethanol were dehydrated with 90% and 96% ethanol. Subsequently, the samples were cleared in xylene and embedded in paraffin wax. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks were cut with a microtome and 2 µm or 3 µm sections were prepared, stained with haematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and examined under microscope. Standard histological techniques were used [29].

Tissues were examined in the laboratory in Vilnius and Vienna applying slightly different cutting and staining regimes. Organs collected from the red-breasted flycatcher were examined in Vienna. For this purpose, three serial (consecutive) sections of each organ were prepared in a first round, one of which was subjected to haematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining, and the remaining to CISH. For in-depth examination of the organs, another 15 sections were prepared for CISH, with trimming 20 µm off the block after every section. Tissue sections were mounted on regular glass slides (for H&E-staining) or on SuperFrost Plus Adhesion slides (Epredia, Portsmouth, USA) (for CISH), and air-dried. In total, 18 sections of each organ were investigated (1 H&E, 17 CISH), resulting in a total number of 162 sections (9 H&E, 153 CISH) examined from this bird in Vienna. All other birds were examined at the P. B. Šivickis Laboratory of Parasitology in Vilnius. To ensure in-depth examination of the organs, three sets of sections of each organ were prepared; each set consisted of one H&E slide (containing two sections), followed by one CISH slide, and another H&E slide. Between sets of sections, 100 µm were trimmed in each organ. In all, 36 sections of each organ were examined (24 H&E and 12 CISH), resulting in a total number of 900 sections (600 H&E and 300 CISH) investigated from eleven birds.

To detect parasite blood and tissue stages, CISH was carried out following previously published protocols using a Plasmodium genus-specific probe (Plasmo18S, 5’-CTT AAA CTT CCT TGT GTT AGA CAC ACA AT-3’) targeting the parasites’ 18S ribosomal RNA [30–32]. In brief, sections were deparaffinized and treated with proteinase K (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) 3 μg/ml for 40 min at 37 °C. Thereafter, sections were rinsed in distilled water, dehydrated in 90% and 100% ethanol 5 min each and air-dried. Next, sections were covered with hybridization solution containing 1 ng/100 μl digoxigenin-labelled probe and incubated overnight in a humid chamber. On the next day, stringency washes were performed and the sections incubated with anti-digoxigenin-AP Fab fragments (Roche) at a concentration of 1:200 for 1 h. After a washing step, chromogenic detection with the substrates 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate (BCIP) and 4-nitro blue tetrazolium chloride (NBT) (Roche) was carried out to visualize the parasite-probe hybrids. Finally, chromogenic reaction was stopped with TE-buffer and the sections were counterstained with haematoxylin before covering with Aquatex (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and glass coverslips. For every CISH procedure a positive control slide containing lung tissue of a Plasmodium-infected bird tested previously was included. To rule out co-infection with Haemoproteus or Leucocytozoon, organs obtained from the red-breasted flycatcher were additionally tested using a Haemoproteus-specific (Haemo18S, 5'-GCT AAC CGT AGT TAT AGT CGC CAT CTC-3') and a Leucocytozoon-specific probe (Leuco18S, 5'-TAG GAC TCC CCA CTT GTC TTT TTC TTG A-3') [32] following the same protocol.

H&E-stained and CISH-treated tissue sections were examined with a brightfield microscope using 10×, 20×, 40×, and 100× objectives. Images of CISH-positive parasite blood stages were acquired at 1000× magnification using an Olympus BX61 (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) microscope equipped with an Olympus DP70 digital camera and the image analysis software cellSens Entry (Olympus).

RNA extraction, sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

RNA was first extracted from the three naturally infected donor birds using an Invitrogen PureLink RNA mini kit with PureLink DNase I on-column treatment (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania). cDNA was synthesised using SuperScript IV RT (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania) following the manufacturer’s protocols. The three RNA samples were sent for total RNA sequencing (library TruSeq stranded total RNA with Ribo-Zero Gold and Sequencing NovaSeqX 150 bp paired-end) to Macrogen Europe (Amsterdam, the Netherlands). For each sample, two files of about 1.5–1.86 GB were received and analysed bioinformatically to search for MaRNAV. The following analyses were performed using the public server Galaxy Europe (https://usegalaxy.eu, accessed February-November 2025): FastQC [33] for sample quality, Trimmomatic [34] for trimming the adapters, FastQC and MultiQC [35] to check the quality of the dataset after trimming, Trinity de novo assembly v.2.15.1 [36] to assemble the reads into transcripts and diamond BLASTx (e-value 0.001 threshold) [37] on the assembled transcripts using a local library composed of all deposited MaRNAV sequences in GenBank. Following Duc et al. [20], hit sequences from the diamond BLAST longer than 150 bp were subjected, one by one, to BLASTx and BLASTn in GenBank to confirm their match to only MaRNAV. The longest open reading frame (ORF) of the transcripts, determined with NCBI ORF finder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/), was then checked for protein sequence and functions using HHpred [38, 39] and Protein Homology/analogy Recognition Engine v2.0 (Phyre2) [40].

Upon analysis, only one donor bird had transcripts that shared sequence identity with a known MaRNAV. Following the same methodology, two blood samples from each recipient bird associated with that donor were processed. The two RNA extracts were then pooled per individual and sent for total RNA sequencing and the same bioinformatic analyses were conducted. Fragments of viral sequences (270–663 nt) were obtained.

Five different phylogenies were then inferred using IQ-Tree v.3.0.1 [41] to further confirm the relationship of the MaRNAV we identified to known MaRNAVs (Supplementary Table 1). All phylogenetic models were chosen by IQ-Tree using ModelFinder [42] and were run with 1000 bootstrap (ultrafast bootstrap UFBoot) [43] and SH-aLRT [44] replicates. The first phylogeny (Supplementary Fig. 1) was constructed using MaRNAV-1 to MaRNAV-7 RdRp nucleotide sequences (2112–3459 bp) with all found fragments (270–663 bp) and Wilkie narna-like virus 1 (MF176347) as outgroup (model: GTR + F + I + G4). The corresponding amino acid sequences of the longest ORF for each sequence were then retrieved through NCBI ORF finder and run for a second phylogeny (model: LG + G4) (Supplementary Fig. 2). All found nucleotide fragments were merged into one sequence and connected with N and run in a third phylogeny (Supplementary Fig. 3) using the same set of sequences as in the first phylogeny (model: GTR + F + I + G4). The fourth phylogeny (Fig. 5) was run with amino acid sequences of all MaRNAVs, the outgroup and the central fragment only (model: LG + G4). The last phylogeny (Supplementary Fig. 4) was calculated with the same subset as in the fourth, but all amino sequences were cut to the length of the central fragment (235 aa alignment) (model: LG + G4). Trees and illustration of the alignment and ORF were prepared in Inkscape.

The abundance of each positive transcript was checked in Galaxy Europe using the tool “align reads and estimate abundance” with RSEM [45] as the abundance estimation method and Bowtie 2 [46] for the alignment method.

Results

Phylogenetic analysis

PCR-based testing revealed that Plasmodium (Haemamoeba) sp. (genetic lineage pCOLL7), Plasmodium circumflexum (pTURDUS1) and Plasmodium vaughani (pSYAT05) were present in donor and recipient birds. Phylogenetic analysis placed the lineage pCOLL7 in the same clade comprising other lineages of Plasmodium relictum lineages (Fig. 1). Genetic similarity of the lineage pCOLL7 and other lineages ranged between 96.2% and 99.8% (Geneious Prime, Distance matrix and percentage identity, respectively). Based on the close phylogenetic relationships and morphological similarities of blood stages (see description below), the lineage pCOLL7 was attributed to the Plasmodium relictum group.

Morphological characterization of parasites

In accordance with the PCR-based testing, the blood stages of P. relictum (Fig. 2a–p), P. circumflexum (Fig. 3a–d), and P. vaughani (Fig. 3e–h) were seen in donor birds and recipient Eurasian siskins. A brief description of the blood stages of these parasites is given below. No other parasites were observed by microscopy, while the lineage lRECOB3 Leucocytozoon sp. was detected in one donor bird only by the nested PCR with the primers 3760F/HAEMJR4.

Variously shaped roundish, oval, and lobulated gametocytes were seen during P. relictum (pCOLL7) infection (Fig. 2a–l). These were typical for parasites of P. relictum group, which blood stages markedly influence infected erythrocytes due to the deformation and displacement of host cell nuclei. Roundish and oval gametocytes predominated. Pigment granules were scattered, and a nucleolus was often visible (Fig. 2a). A parasitaemia pattern unusual for avian malaria parasites was observed during pCOLL7 infection. Mainly, exclusively gametocytes (parasitaemia 0.3%) were seen in the naturally infected wood warbler after examination of entire blood films (Fig. 2a–h); erythrocytic meronts were not seen in the wood warbler. Morphology of gametocytes was indistinguishable in wood warbler (Fig. 2a–h) and exposed Eurasian siskins, suggesting absence of visible influence of host factors on the parasite morphology. As in the case of the parasite donor wood warbler, gametocytes readily predominated in all exposed Eurasian siskins (Fig. 2i–m). However, single erythrocytic meronts (Fig. 2n–p) were seen, showing that minimal erythrocytic merogony occurred rarely. In all, only 17 erythrocytic meronts were observed in three experimentally infected Eurasian siskins during entire period of observation. Single erythrocytic meronts were observed only at 17, 89, 93, and 129 dpi. The observed meronts were at different stages of maturation and shared typical features of P. relictum. These were roundish, contained predominantly grouped pigment granules; the parasites markedly deformed host cells and displaced their nuclei (Fig. 2n–p). Completely mature meronts (segments) were not seen, so the maximum number of merozoites developing in erythrocytic meronts remains unidentified in the pCOLL7 lineage.

Typical blood stages of P. circumflexum (pTURDUS1) were seen in the red-breasted flycatcher donor bird and the corresponding recipient siskins (Fig. 3a–d), with circumnuclear erythrocytic meronts (Fig. 3b, d) and halteridial gametocytes (Fig. 3a, c) present. Morphologically indistinguishable gametocytes and erythrocytic meronts developed in the parasite donor red-breasted flycatcher (Fig. 3a, b) and the experimentally exposed Eurasian siskins (Fig. 3c, d), suggesting absence of influence of host factors on the parasite morphology. In both species of avian hosts, mature gametocytes enclosed host cells with their ends; numerous scattered pigment granules were present (Fig. 3a, c). Mature erythrocytic meronts were circumnuclear, contained 18–22 merozoites and a prominent clamp of pigment granules (Fig. 3b, d). Parasitaemia consisted of gametocytes and erythrocytic meronts, which were seen simultaneously at all observation time points.

Typical blood stages of P. vaughani (pSYAT05) were seen in the donor blackbird and the corresponding recipient Eurasian siskins (Fig. 3e–h), with erythrocytic meronts smaller than the erythrocyte nuclei (Fig. 3f, h) and thin elongate gametocytes (Fig. 3e, g) present. Morphologically indistinguishable gametocytes and erythrocytic meronts developed in the parasite donor common blackbird (Fig. 3e, f) and the experimentally exposed Eurasian siskins (Fig. 3g, h), suggesting absence of visible influence of host factors on the parasite morphology. In both species of avian hosts, mature gametocytes were slender, elongated, and amoeboid in outline; they extended along nuclei of host cells and contained a few prominent scattered pigment granules (Fig. 3e, g). Mature erythrocytic meronts were smaller than the erythrocyte nuclei, contained 6–8 merozoites and a small clamp of pigment granules (Fig. 3f, h). Refractive globule was visible in meronts (Fig. 3f). Parasitaemia consisted of gametocytes and erythrocytic meronts, which both were seen permanently simultaneously.

Susceptibility, parasitaemia, prepatent period, and survival

All control birds remained uninfected and survived until the end of this study. All Eurasian siskins exposed to P. relictum (pCOLL7) were susceptible and parasitaemia developed. However, parasitaemia was low and varied between 0.003 and 0.02% in all birds during the entire study. The minimum prepatent period was 17 dpi in one bird, while in the other two birds, parasites were first recognised only on 69 and 97 dpi. Additionally, the parasitaemia was markedly transient in all exposed Eurasian siskins—days with the visible parasitaemia alternated with periods of 10–45 days during which the parasites were not seen in the peripheral circulation. All Eurasian siskins exposed to the lineage pCOLL7 survived until the end of the study, suggesting low virulence in this avian host.

All Eurasian siskins exposed to P. circumflexum (pTURDUS1) were susceptible, with prepatent periods of 8, 12, and 24 dpi. Parasitaemia was low; it varied between 0.007 and 0.04% in all birds during the entire study. Additionally, the parasitaemia was transient in all exposed Eurasian siskins—days with the visible low parasitaemia alternated with periods of 10–35 days during which parasites were not seen in the peripheral circulation. All birds exposed to the lineage pTURDUS1 survived until the end of this study, suggesting low virulence in this avian host.

All Eurasian siskins exposed to P. vaughani (pSYAT05) were susceptible, with prepatent periods of 21, 25, and 33 dpi. Parasitaemia was low; it varied between 0.003 and 0.4% in all birds until 41 dpi. Thereafter, parasitaemia increased in all birds; reaching maximum 18.5% in one bird at 93 dpi, and then gradually decreased, being between 0.2 and 4.4% in two of the birds at the end of the study. One bird died at 57 dpi, with parasitaemia of 2.2% during the last blood check, suggesting high virulence in this avian host.

Histological and chromogenic in situ hybridization investigation

Extensive histological and CISH investigation of naturally infected donor wild birds and the exposed Eurasian siskins did not reveal any tissue stages. However, CISH investigation revealed blood stages of malaria parasites in infected donor birds and recipients, supporting the validity of the used diagnostic test (Fig. 4a–i). CISH of organs of the red-breasted flycatcher using the Haemoproteus- and Leucocytozoon-specific probes turned out negative, ruling out co-infection of this bird with these parasites.

Blood stages were identified in red blood cells by chromogenic in situ hybridization (CISH) using a Plasmodium genus-specific probe (Plasmo18S). Matryoshka RNA viruses

Among the three donor birds, BLAST results showed no MaRNAV match in the wood warbler infected with P. relictum pCOLL7, nor in the blackbird infected with P. vaughani pSYAT05. In the breasted flycatcher infected with P. circumflexum pTURDUS1, four contigs shared sequenced identity with MaRNAV (Figs. 5, 6; Table 1). However, these transcripts were fragmented, and a complete segment could not be obtained for this new virus when compared to the previously identified and characterized sequences of MaRNAVs (Fig. 6). A fragment of 663 bp (220 amino acids) in length matched MaRNAV-2 RdRp with 72% identity by BLASTx (Table 1). Furthermore, the four fragments clustered with previously characterized MaRNAV sequences and were close to MaRNAV-7 and MaRNAV-2 in all phylogenies (Fig. 5; Supplementary Figs. 1–4); strong nodes support is considered when SH-aLRT ≥ 80% and UFBoot ≥ 95%. The abundance for all fragments was low (Table 2).

Discussion

Despite much research on avian malaria, the details of Plasmodium species development and persistence in avian hosts remain insufficiently investigated in most of the described Plasmodium species [1, 13]. Experimental research under controlled laboratory conditions is needed but remain rare with avian malaria parasites. Due to strict ethical requirements, the choice of experimental birds is limited to bird species which can be bred in captivity. Experimental observations show that domestic birds are resistant to many wildlife Plasmodium species or only low transient parasitaemia occurs [8, 9], limiting their suitability for studies of Plasmodium species parasitizing birds. Additionally, it is expensive and might be challenging to maintain large groups of poultry (domestic chicken, ducks, turkeys, pigeons) in academic research laboratories, precluding the use of poultry in experimental wildlife malaria species. Some small passerines are readily bred in captivity and can be purchased commercially, making them more convenient for experimental laboratory observations, particularly due to relatively low maintenance costs. However, in many instances, the problem of selective resistance to Plasmodium species remains. For example, the attempts to infect zebra finches Taeniopygia castanotis, budgerigar Melopsittacus undulatus, or common quails Coturnix coturnix with P. relictum and P. circumflexum—the most generalist avian malaria parasites—were unsuccessful or only low transient parasitaemia developed (G. Valkiūnas, personal observations). Available studies showed that two bird species—the domestic canary and the Eurasian siskin—are good experimental hosts for some malaria parasites [14, 18]. However, susceptibility even of these birds to different Plasmodium lineages and their different isolates might vary from high susceptibility to resistance, and it remains unknown for most of the described Plasmodium species [9, 18]. This calls for experimental studies aiming at further testing the susceptibility of these two avian hosts to common malaria parasite lineages. The need for such experiments is particularly timely in order to find convenient model host-parasite systems for investigating the relationships of haemosporidian parasites with their recently discovered viruses such as MaRNAVs. To address this need, this study explored host susceptibility and infection dynamics of three common Plasmodium lineages in Eurasian siskins.

The lineage pSYAT05 of P. vaughani is cosmopolitan ([25], MalAvi database, https://tavimalara.shinyapps.io/malavi_tables/, accessed November 2025). Eurasian siskins were susceptible and developed high parasitaemia in this study but different isolates of the same cytb lineage from the same avian host species (common blackbird) were resistant in several previous experimental observations [18]. In other words, the host-parasite relationship even on the level of same species of host and the same parasite lineage might not always be predictable. This issue remains insufficiently addressed in avian malariology and calls for further delicate experimental investigations.

The lineage pCOLL7 was formerly reported mainly in naturally infected African birds ([47], MalAvi database https://tavimalara.shinyapps.io/malavi_tables/, accessed November 2025). In this study, it was found in a wood warbler, a species wintering in Africa. It is possible that transmission occurred in Africa. Species identification of the lineage pCOLL7 was done for the first time. Morphological (see description above) and phylogenetic data (Fig. 1) showed that this lineage belongs to P. relictum group. Eurasian siskins were susceptible, but only low, transient parasitaemia developed. This might preclude the collection of templates for genetic and other parasitology studies using this host-parasite association. Consequently, this bird species hardly can be recommended for experimental research with the lineage pCOLL7.

Importantly, this study recognised a formerly unknown mode of avian Plasmodium parasitaemia. Mainly, the parasitaemia of the lineage pCOLL7 consisted of almost exclusively gametocytes in the naturally infected and experimental birds. Such parasitaemia provides opportunities for transmission by vectors but is unusual for P. relictum and other investigated avian malaria infections, during which parasitaemia usually contains both gametocytes and erythrocytic meronts present in parallel [1, 8, 16]. This finding calls for research aiming at a better understanding of the mechanism of gametocyte existence during malaria [48]. Because tissue stages were not found in any of the tested birds, it is possible that this parasite lineage survives in its avian hosts mainly due to minor erythrocytic merogony, as likely is the case in P. relictum (pSGS1) infection, which appears to be maintained by blood stages persisting in the circulation of blood-inoculated domestic canaries [13].

It is worth mentioning that the parasitaemia of exclusively gametocytes is expected in wildlife malaria parasites, but was not reported before in avian Plasmodium species. For example, a similar mode of only gametocytes’ occurrence in the peripheral circulation was described in the lizard malaria parasite Plasmodium (Billbraya) australis. It was shown that the persistence of erythrocytic meronts and gametocytes is separated in time [49]. Primary parasitaemia was represented mainly of erythrocytic meronts for approximately a month. Then, the meronts disappeared, and parasitaemia was comprised of only gametocytes, which also predominated during relapses. Delicate experimental investigation of life cycles of avian Plasmodium species is needed for better understanding some neglected modes of their existence in vertebrates [7]. The malaria parasite lineage pCOLL7 can be used for this purpose; however, susceptible experimental avian host still need to be identified.

The lineage pTURDUS1 is a generalist avian malaria parasite, which is widespread in Eurasia ([3], MalAvi database, https://tavimalara.shinyapps.io/malavi_tables/, accessed November 2025). Two experimental studies tested the susceptibility of Eurasian siskins to the lineage pTURDUS1 of P. circumflexum [18], with similar results on susceptibility and minimal prepatent period after blood-induced infections. However, the maximum parasitaemia of this lineage isolate from Eurasian wren Troglodydes troglodytes was higher (up to 30%) as compared to the same lineage isolated from the red-breasted flycatcher in this study [18]. The reports of marked variation in maximum parasitaemia call for studies aiming at better understanding of virulence of the same parasite lineages isolated from different avian hosts. Such differences may reflect intra-lineage genetic variation, including SNPs not captured by the standard barcoding region defining parasite lineages. Application of high-throughput sequencing can identify such cases, but this methodology has not yet been widely used in virulence studies during avian malaria [50].

Negative results for search of tissue stages both in the naturally infected parasite donor birds and the experimentally exposed Eurasian siskins indicate that long-lasting persistence of avian malaria pathogens in vertebrates might be entirely due to erythrocytic merogony. This mode of persistence is probably evolutionary advantageable due to minimizing of internal organ damages by tissue stages (exo-erythrocytic meronts). A similar mode of persistence was recently reported during blood-induced infections of P. relictum (pSGS1), P. (Novyella) delichoni (pCOLL6), and P. (Novyella) homonucleophilum (pSW2) [12–14]. Further research is needed to clarify the location and role of exo-erythrocytic development in the life cycle of avian malaria parasites, since development in tissues is obligatory for species of Haemosporida parasites [1, 9]. Experimental research is needed, particularly using sporozoite-induced infections, which might initiate development of hypnozoite-like stages, as is the case in Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale infections in humans [11], but remains unknown during avian malaria. Research on sporozoite-induced avian malaria infections remains rare due to logistic difficulties, particularly related to unknown vectors for most described avian Plasmodium species.

Across all three experiments, malaria parasites belonging to different subgenera retained their species-diagnostic characters in hosts belonging to different families (Figs. 2 and 3), indicating that the major morphological characters, which are used in taxonomy are relatively stable. Blood stages of P. relictum pCOLL7 looked similar in birds of the Phylloscopidae and Fringillidae, those of P. circumflexum pTURDUS1 appeared similar in Muscicapidae and Fringillidae, and those of P. vaughani (pSYAT05) were similar in Turdidae and Fringillidae. In other words, it is possible to report Plasmodium species morphological characters of blood stages in different avian hosts in accordance with detected genetic lineages. This finding supports the use of rapidly accumulating genetic lineage data on avian malaria parasites for taxonomic purposes, at least for parasites infecting passerine hosts.

It is worth mentioning that limited available data shows that virus infections might influence pathogenicity of protists by increasing (hypervirulence) or decreasing (hypovirulence) disease severity in vertebrates [51, 52]. This is particularly well illustrated in two examples, where viral infection can arrest parasite growth (GLV virus infection in Gardia species) or increase the virulence of the pathogen (Leishmania sp.) [51]. However, poor knowledge exists about associations and relationships between haemosporidians and viruses. If hypovirulence occurs in malaria pathogens infected with recently discovered MaRNAV and other viruses of the family Narnaviridae, and the parasite becomes less virulent for vertebrates, this information could be applicable to the development of novel strategies of anti-malaria prevention in the future [19, 53, 54].

This study is the first experimental observation with birds infected with haemosporidians associated with MaRNAV. The biology of MaRNAV remains unclear, and its unravelling needs application of experimental observations for a better understanding of the mechanisms of its persistence and transmission. It was speculated that these viruses might use haemosporidian parasites as vectors to survive and infect new blood-sucking insects and birds [21]. Model organisms are needed for such research, but remain unidentified, as most MaRNAV are associated with Leucocytozoon spp. (Supplementary Table 1), for which vectors species are mostly unknown. This is the first record of MaRNAV associated with an avian Plasmodium species and the first association reported for any non-vivax Plasmodium species. Importantly, even if the lineage lRECOB3 (Leucocytozoon sp.) was caught by one of the two nested PCR, no Haemoproteus or Leucocytozoon parasites were detected in the bird infected with MaRNAV-9 by either the microscopic examination of the blood smears, or CISH, thereby supporting that the virus was associated with Plasmodium infection. The RdRp and hypothetical protein sequences of the new virus were fragmented, but this did not impede the assessment of the virus relationship with the previously characterized MaRNAV. All fragments matching a part of the RdRp sequence of MaRNAV clustered more closely with MaRNAV-7 and MaRNAV-2 (Fig. 5, Supplementary Figs. 1–4), both formerly found in birds infected with Leucocytozoon spp. [19, 20], than with the RdRp sequence of the MaRNAV-1, found in humans infected with P. vivax [19]. It is also interesting to note that MaRNAV-7 was found in an African-European migratory bird [20] and MaRNAV-2 was found in birds from Oceania [19], while MaRNAV-9 was found in an Asian-European migratory bird, the red-breasted flycatcher [55]. These viruses were associated with haemosporidians of Old World birds.

The low parasitaemia intensity of P. circumflexum (0.02%) in the donor, coupled with the low abundance of the fragments in the total number of reads of the sample (less than 0.001%; Table 2) could explain the fragmentation of the viral sequences. In comparison, MaRNAV-7, which was found in a migratory bird infected with Leucocytozoon spp. (0.05% parasitaemia), Haemoproteus sp. (< 0.0001%, only one gametocyte seen), and Plasmodium sp. (< 0.0001%, only three trophozoites seen), had an abundance of 0.02% for its RdRp sequence among the total number of reads [20]. These low parasitaemia and low viral abundance could also explain the absence of the virus in the recipients of the experiment. The virus was present in the donor, but possibly at a too low levels to be transmitted or detected during the RNA analysis of samples from recipients. It is also unclear, if the virus can be transferred via infected blood inoculation or requires obligatory involvement of insect-vectors, as suggested by Esperanza et al. [21]. In future research, birds with higher parasitaemia should be preferred for selection in similar experimental infections. However, this might be challenging in wildlife where the great majority of avian malaria infections are low (parasitaemia < 1%) [1, 9]. Further studies are needed for better understanding of MaRNAV’ biology during avian haemosporidiosis, particularly due to increasing number of reports about new MaRNAVs in birds infected with haemosporidian parasites [56].

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the widespread Plasmodium lineages pCOLL7, pSYAT05 and pTURDUS1 can be experimentally accessed by exposure to Eurasian siskins. This bird species is a suitable host for detailed in vivo experimental observations with the lineages pSYAT05 and pTURDUS1, but not with the lineage pCOLL7. Tissue stages (exo-erythrocytic meronts) were not detected after blood-induced infections, suggesting long-time persistence of these parasite lineages in the experimental hosts mainly due to low blood merogony, which remains an underestimated mode of Plasmodium species persistence in avian hosts. On the other hand, P. relictum pCOLL7 parasitaemia was found to comprise almost exclusively gametocytes, calling for studies to better understand mechanisms of persistence of malaria parasites. Morphological characterization showed that the morphology of blood stages of all three lineages is relatively stable in birds belonging to different families, supporting the value of the traditional morphological characters in parasite species identification. The first Matryoshka RNA virus (MaRNAV-9) associated with avian Plasmodium parasites was discovered. This virus was found in association with the bird malaria parasite P. (Giovannolaia) circumflexum (pTURDUS1) widespread in Eurasia, which can be used as a model host-parasite system to better understand the biology of MaRNAVs and their relationship with haemosporidians. This study contributes to the better understanding of the diversity of MaRNAVs associated with Plasmodium pathogens, and the obtained genetic information can be used for improvement of the virus diagnostics.
