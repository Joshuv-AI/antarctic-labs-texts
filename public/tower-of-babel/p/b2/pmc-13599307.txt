When PSMA is not enough: the diagnostic blind spots of PSMA PET/CT and the added value of [¹⁸F]FDG in high-risk prostate cancer: dual-tracer cohort study
Authors: Efrah Ahmed Ibrahim, Eray Alper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePSMA PET/CT has transformed prostate cancer management, yet its diagnostic utility is not absolute. It fails to detect PSMA-suppressed disease variants, including treatment-induced neuroendocrine prostate cancer (t-NEPC) and dedifferentiated tumors, and is inherently blind to second primary malignancies (SPMs). This study evaluated the additional diagnostic yield and direct clinical impact of incorporating [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT into a systematic, indication-driven dual-tracer protocol in high-risk prostate cancer patients.MethodsIn this retrospective, STROBE-compliant, single-center cohort study, 82 patients with histologically confirmed prostate cancer who underwent both [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 and [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT were analyzed. Clinical indications encompassed suspected SPM, suspected dedifferentiation or t-NEPC, initial staging of very high-risk disease, pre-[¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-PSMA therapy evaluation, and equivocal PSMA findings. The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients in whom [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT provided additional diagnostic information. Secondary endpoints included concordance analysis and the rate of management changes.ResultsMedian age was 72 years (IQR 65–75). [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 demonstrated superior prostate cancer detection compared to [¹⁸F]FDG (70.7% vs. 25.6%; McNemar’s p < 0.0001). However, [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT provided clinically critical additional information and directly altered management in 51.2% of patients (95% CI: 40.6–61.7%). The most impactful contributions were histopathologically confirmed SPM detection (30.5%, predominantly lung and colorectal adenocarcinomas) and identification of PSMA-negative/FDG-positive discordant disease signaling dedifferentiation. No significant correlation was found between PSMA and FDG SUVmax values (Spearman rho = 0.149, p = 0.422), underscoring their biologically distinct and complementary targets.Conclusion[¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT is not a redundant adjunct to PSMA imaging; it fills a critical diagnostic blind spot. When applied through an indication-driven framework, dual-tracer imaging directly reshapes clinical decision-making in more than half of selected high-risk prostate cancer patients, enabling detection of occult malignancies and guiding pivotal treatment decisions that PSMA PET/CT alone cannot support.Keywords: Prostate cancer, Dual-tracer imaging, Dedifferentiation, Neuroendocrine prostate cancer, Treatment-induced NEPC

Introduction

PSMA positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) has fundamentally reshaped prostate cancer management. By targeting the transmembrane glycoprotein overexpressed on the surface of most prostate adenocarcinoma cells, [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT delivers superior sensitivity and specificity compared to conventional imaging across the disease spectrum from initial staging and biochemical recurrence detection to patient selection for radioligand therapy [1–5]. Its adoption into international clinical guidelines has been rapid and well-justified.

Yet PSMA PET/CT has a blind spot, and it is clinically consequential. As prostate cancer evolves under the selective pressure of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) and radiotherapy, a subset of tumors undergoes neuroendocrine transdifferentiation, downregulating androgen receptor signaling and, with it, PSMA expression [6–10]. The result is a PSMA-silent but metabolically aggressive phenotype that eludes PSMA-based imaging entirely. Furthermore, PSMA PET/CT is inherently unable to detect synchronous or metachronous second primary malignancies (SPMs), an increasingly recognized clinical entity in a population of predominantly older men with significant environmental exposure histories [11].

The biological rationale for these limitations is well established. Treatment-induced neuroendocrine prostate cancer (t-NEPC) arises through ADT-mediated androgen receptor suppression and epigenetic reprogramming via CREB1/EZH2-mediated REST downregulation [7], as well as radiation-induced neuroendocrine differentiation through CREB activation and ATF2 cytoplasmic sequestration [12]. These mechanisms converge on a common phenotype: high glycolytic metabolism, suppressed PSMA expression, and a dismal prognosis with a median survival of only 7 months from transformation diagnosis [13].

Second primary malignancies represent an equally important but underappreciated dimension of PSMA’s limitations. Population-based registry data demonstrate that prostate cancer survivors face an elevated risk of metachronous malignancies, particularly lung and colon adenocarcinomas, driven by shared environmental carcinogens and field cancerization effects [14, 15]. PSMA PET/CT, however precise it characterizes prostatic disease, is entirely blind to these oncological events unfolding in parallel.

[¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT, which exploits increased glycolytic metabolism characteristic of aggressive and dedifferentiated tumors as well as most non-prostatic malignancies, is uniquely positioned to fill this diagnostic gap [16]. Prior studies predominantly from East Asian and North American mCRPC cohorts have demonstrated that FDG-positive/PSMA-negative discordant lesions occur in 21–25% of advanced disease cases, correlate with high Gleason scores, and predict poor prognosis [17–19]. One of the recent systematic review confirmed [¹⁸F]FDG’s role in identifying inter-lesional heterogeneity that may preclude patients from [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-PSMA therapy [20].

Despite these insights, data on dual-tracer imaging across a broader clinical spectrum beyond mCRPC, beyond East Asia remain sparse. Critically, the direct, quantified impact on clinical management decisions across multiple real-world indications remains incompletely characterized.

The present study was designed to help address precisely this gap. We report the additional diagnostic yield and direct clinical management impact of an indication-driven dual-tracer [¹⁸F]FDG and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT protocol in a real-world high-risk prostate cancer cohort from a tertiary center. We hypothesized that [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT would provide critical complementary information, detecting what PSMA cannot see and directly alter clinical decision-making in a substantial proportion of carefully selected patients.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective, single-center observational study conducted at a tertiary academic referral center. The study was performed in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments and reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [21]. The Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis (Approval Number: 2026/154/6 − 2).

Patient population

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of all patients with histologically confirmed prostate cancer who underwent both [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 and [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT between January 2023 and June 2025. During this period, approximately 2,450 PSMA PET/CT scans were performed at our institution; of these, dual-tracer imaging with [¹⁸F]FDG was clinically indicated in 145 patients. Of these 145 patients, among 145 patients initially identified as having a clinical indication for dual-tracer imaging, 48 were excluded before formal eligibility assessment because they did not meet the predefined screening requirements, including incomplete dual-tracer imaging, unavailable clinical records or follow-up, duplicate examinations, or imaging performed outside the accepted interval. Therefore, 97 patients were assessed for eligibility, of whom 15 were further excluded, resulting in a final analytic cohort of 82 patients.

The remaining 97 patients were formally assessed for eligibility; 15 were subsequently excluded due to incomplete clinical or pathological data, prior diagnosis of another primary malignancy antedating the prostate cancer diagnosis, or receipt of systemic treatment between the two scans, yielding a final highly selected cohort of 82 patients.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) histologically confirmed prostate adenocarcinoma; (2) completion of both scans within 90 days with no change in systemic therapy between scans (median interscan interval: 33 days, IQR 14–90 days); and (3) availability of complete clinical, pathological, and follow-up data.

Clinical indications for dual-tracer imaging

The clinical indications for ordering complementary [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT were predefined and included: (1) suspicion of a second primary malignancy based on clinical presentation or conventional imaging; (2) initial staging of very high-risk or locally advanced disease; (3) pre-treatment evaluation before [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-PSMA radioligand therapy; (4) suspicion of tumor dedifferentiation or t-NEPC in patients on long-term ADT or maximum androgen blockade; and (5) discordance between PSMA PET/CT findings and clinical or biochemical data. Dual-tracer imaging was specifically prioritized for patients with prolonged ADT exposure (> 5 years in 14.6% of the cohort) and for those with prior radiotherapy (48.8%), given established mechanisms of therapy-induced neuroendocrine differentiation [12, 13].

Imaging protocol

All PET/CT examinations were performed using a dedicated PET/CT system (Discovery IQ, GE Healthcare, USA) equipped with bismuth germanate (BGO) crystal detectors. This system does not incorporate time-of-flight (TOF) capability; image sensitivity is achieved through the use of thick (30 mm) BGO crystals with high stopping power for 511 keV photons. PET data were reconstructed using a block-sequential regularized expectation maximization (BSREM) algorithm (Q.Clear, GE Healthcare), with a penalization factor (β) typically ranging between 350 and 450 according to institutional protocol.

For [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT, patients received an intravenous injection at a dose of 1.8–2.2 MBq/kg (0.05–0.06 mCi/kg), followed by image acquisition after an uptake period of (60 ± 10) minutes.

For [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT, patients fasted for at least 6 h before tracer administration, and blood glucose levels were confirmed to be < 150 mg/dL before injection[¹⁸F]FDG was administered intravenously at a dose of 3.7–5.5 MBq/kg (0.10–0.15 mCi/kg), with PET/CT acquisition performed after an uptake period of (60 ± 10) minutes.

All acquisitions were performed from the vertex to the mid-thigh. A low-dose, non-contrast-enhanced CT scan was obtained for attenuation correction and anatomical localization using standard parameters (120 kVp, automated current modulation, slice thickness 3.75–5 mm). PET emission data were acquired in three-dimensional mode with a fixed acquisition time of 2 min per bed position.

Image analysis

All PET/CT images were retrospectively evaluated by two experienced nuclear medicine physicians with access to clinical histories. Scans were evaluated at two separate time points to minimize recall bias; discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Focal uptake above background not attributable to physiological distribution was considered pathological. SUVmax was recorded for the primary prostate lesion (or prostate bed) and the most avid metastatic lesion. Concordance was categorized per patient as: PSMA+/FDG+ (concordant positive), PSMA+/FDG- (discordant, PSMA-dominant), PSMA-/FDG+ (discordant, FDG-dominant), or PSMA-/FDG- (concordant negative). Inter-rater reliability assessed in 20% of randomly selected cases (n = 16) yielded excellent agreement for PSMA prostate uptake (kappa = 0.86), FDG prostate uptake (kappa = 1.00), and overall (kappa = 0.94).

Histopathological confirmation and endpoint definitions

All suspected SPMs, neuroendocrine transformations, or PSMA-/FDG+ discordant findings were mandated to undergo histopathological confirmation via biopsy. The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients in whom [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT provided additional diagnostic information not apparent on [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT. Secondary endpoints included: correlation between PSMA and FDG SUVmax values, and the proportion of patients experiencing a direct change in clinical management (change in treatment modality, additional imaging, biopsy ordering or cancellation, or MDT discussion initiation).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.11) with the SciPy Stats and Statsmodels libraries. Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) or means with standard deviations. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. McNemar’s test compared paired detection rates. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (rho) assessed SUVmax relationships. Fisher’s exact test evaluated the smoking-SPM association. Confidence intervals were calculated using the Wilson score interval. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All figures were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0).

Results

Patient characteristics

Of the 2,450 PSMA PET/CT scans performed during the study period, dual-tracer imaging was ordered for 145 patients based on specific clinical indications; 82 met all eligibility criteria and comprised the final cohort (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 72 years (IQR 65–75), and the median PSA at the time of imaging was 9.84 ng/mL (IQR 3.50-20.15). The cohort represented a high-risk oncological population: 47.6% were ISUP Grade Group 4–5 (Gleason 8–10), 48.8% had received prior radiotherapy, and 59.8% were on ADT. The most frequent indication was suspected SPM (61.0%), followed by suspected dedifferentiation or t-NEPC (13.4%) and initial staging of very high-risk disease (11.0%).

Detection rates and additional diagnostic yield

[⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 demonstrated significantly superior prostate cancer detection compared to [¹⁸F]FDG on a per-patient basis (70.7% [58/82] vs. 25.6% [21/82]; McNemar’s p < 0.0001). Across the full cohort (n = 82), the distribution of tracer concordance patterns was as follows: 38 patients (46.3%) were PSMA+/FDG- (PSMA-dominant), 19 (23.2%) were PSMA+/FDG+ (concordant positive), 2 (2.4%) were PSMA-/FDG+ (FDG-dominant, signaling potential dedifferentiation), 22 (26.8%) were PSMA-/FDG- (concordant negative), and 1 (1.2%) was indeterminate (Fig. 2). The odds ratio for discordant pairs was 19.0, strongly confirming PSMA dominance for primary prostate lesion detection.

(B) Stacked bar chart of concordance patterns stratified by clinical indication

Critically, [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT provided additional diagnostic information altering clinical management in 42 patients (51.2%, 95% CI: 40.6–61.7%). This additional yield was primarily driven by SPM detection (30.5%) and identification of discordant PSMA-/FDG+ metastatic lesions (2.4%) (Fig. 3B). The data underscore a fundamental principle: PSMA and FDG do not compete; they operate on orthogonal biological axes. The remaining 51 patients were excluded from this specific quantitative analysis because they lacked measurable focal uptake above background on at least one of the two tracers (e.g., concordant negative scans or absolute single-tracer dominance) or they underwent biopsy, but the results were not significant (mostly benign), precluding a paired continuous variable comparison.

Second primary malignancy spectrum

Among the 25 patients with histopathologically confirmed SPMs (30.5%, 95% CI: 21.6–41.1%), a total of 30 individual malignancies were identified (5 patients had more than two malignancies concurrently), mirroring global adenocarcinoma predominance: lung adenocarcinoma (n = 8, 26.7%), colorectal adenocarcinoma (n = 7, 23.3%), bladder urothelial carcinoma (n = 5, 16.7%), gastric adenocarcinoma (n = 3, 10.0%), and renal cell carcinoma (n = 1, 3.3%). Other malignancies included hepatocellular carcinoma (n = 1, 3.3%), synovial sarcoma (n = 1, 3.3%), and other types including squamous cell carcinomas (n = 4, 13.3%), consistent with the biological rarity of radiation-induced sarcomas, which typically require latency periods exceeding 5 years [22]. Synchronous SPM presentation occurred in 44% of cases (n = 11), with 56% detected metachronously (n = 14), aligning with Portuguese registry data [15].

Treatment-induced dedifferentiation: clinical phenotypes and outcomes

Among 11 patients imaged for suspected dedifferentiation, 1 (9.1%) demonstrated true PSMA-/FDG+ discordance (Fig. 4) (Patient 58), while another (Patient 49) showed concordant positivity at metastatic sites despite absent prostate uptake. Both had received prolonged ADT, with or without prior radiotherapy, consistent with established t-NEPC mechanisms [20]. Clinical outcomes were strikingly aggressive: one patient died within one week of imaging; another demonstrated no metabolic response to chemotherapy at six months, trajectories consistent with the published median t-NEPC survival of only 7 months [13, 23].

Follow-up [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT demonstrated no significant PSMA uptake, raising suspicion for phenotypic transformation. Subsequent [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT revealed FDG-avid disease in the prostate gland and a focus of increased FDG uptake in the left ischium. Repeat prostate biopsy confirmed adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation, consistent with treatment-induced neuroendocrine prostate cancer (t-NEPC) following prolonged hormonal therapy

In stark contrast, Patient 58, a 64-year-old treatment-naive patient with de novo neuroendocrine prostate cancer (small cell subtype), demonstrated no PSMA uptake but intense, widespread FDG hypermetabolism involving the primary tumor, pelvic lymph nodes, and skeleton at baseline. Following platinum-based chemotherapy, interval [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT at 3 and 6 months demonstrated near-complete morphologic and metabolic response with sclerotic transformation of osseous sites and no evidence of metabolically active disease (Fig. 5). This case illustrates the differential chemosensitivity of de novo versus treatment-induced neuroendocrine disease [24] and demonstrates [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT as the only modality capable of detecting, staging, and monitoring therapeutic response in this PSMA-silent phenotype.

SUVmax correlation analysis

In patients with measurable paired lesions available for quantitative comparison (n = 31), median SUVmax was significantly higher for PSMA than for FDG (10.2 vs. 5.6; Wilcoxon W = 99.5, p = 0.006). Scatter plot analysis revealed no significant linear correlation between the two tracers (Spearman rho = 0.149, p = 0.422) (Fig. 6A). PSMA uptake exceeded FDG uptake in 71.0% of paired lesions by waterfall analysis (Fig. 6B). The absence of any meaningful correlation between PSMA and FDG SUVmax values is not a statistical artifact; it is a biological statement: these tracers measure different cellular processes, and their respective signals are independent(Fig. 7 shows an example when the biopsy result didn’t totally reflect the imaging). This independence is precisely what makes dual-tracer imaging diagnostically powerful. The remaining 51 patients were excluded from this paired analysis because they lacked measurable focal uptake on at least one tracer: 22 had concordant PSMA−/FDG− negativity, 17 had FDG avidity attributable entirely to non-prostatic findings (confirmed SPMs) without a measurable prostatic PSMA focus, and 12 had equivocal uptake below the institutional threshold for reliable SUVmax quantification.

Clinical impact and management changes

[¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT directly altered clinical management in 42 of 82 patients (51.2%, 95% CI: 40.6–61.7%), as detailed in Table 2. The most frequent interventions were biopsy ordering (18.3%, n = 15), to confirm suspected SPMs or neuroendocrine transformation, and fundamental change in treatment modality (17.1%, n = 14), including withholding [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-PSMA therapy in favor of systemic chemotherapy due to FDG-dominant discordant disease. In addition, dual-tracer imaging helped avoid unnecessary biopsies in certain cases (Fig. 8), while supporting timely biopsy decisions in others (Fig. 9). No adverse events related to the dual-tracer imaging protocol were observed.

Discussion

This real-world, indication-driven cohort study answers a question the field has long sidestepped: in carefully selected high-risk prostate cancer patients, how often does [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT change what clinicians do? The answer is unambiguous in a substantial proportion of patients (51.2%). This is not a marginal diagnostic increment; it is a clinically decisive contribution.

Our central finding challenges an implicit assumption embedded in current prostate cancer imaging paradigms: that once PSMA PET/CT has been performed, the diagnostic picture is complete. It is not. PSMA imaging operates on a single biological axis, receptor-mediated uptake, and is systematically blind to dedifferentiated disease variants and concurrent malignancies. [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT, targeting glycolytic flux, operates on an orthogonal axis. These tracers are not competitors for the same diagnostic territory; they are complementary maps of distinct oncological landscapes. Beyond staging and recurrence, the diagnostic utility of PSMA PET/CT is rapidly expanding into the primary diagnosis setting. Recent evidence highlights the emergent role of PSMA PET/CT in the diagnosis of clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa) by guiding targeted biopsies, demonstrating high sensitivity for primary prostatic lesions and potentially reducing the need for repeated systematic biopsies [25].

Furthermore, the interpretation of PSMA PET/CT must account for established diagnostic pitfalls. False-positive PSMA uptake can occur in benign inflammatory conditions such as prostatitis and granulomatous disease, as well as in various non-prostatic malignancies (e.g., renal cell carcinoma, thyroid cancer) due to PSMA expression in tumor neovasculature [26]. Conversely, false-negative results are a recognized limitation in small-volume disease and, critically, in tumors undergoing neuroendocrine dedifferentiation where PSMA expression is suppressed [27].

Additionally, specific histological variants present unique imaging challenges; for instance, PSMA PET/CT has demonstrated low accuracy in the diagnosis and clinical staging of prostatic ductal adenocarcinoma, further emphasizing the need for complementary diagnostic approaches in atypical phenotypes [27].

The second primary malignancy finding: beyond a diagnostic curiosity

The 30.5% SPM detection rate in our cohort substantially exceeds that reported in unselected prostate cancer populations, where the 19-year cumulative SPM incidence ranges from 8.4% to 14.8% [14]. This disparity reflects deliberate patient selection: our cohort was enriched for suspected SPM (61.0% of indications) and represented a high-burden oncological population, not a population-level screening exercise. Our findings align with Swiss registry data showing standardized incidence ratios of 1.19 for lung and 1.73 for colon cancers in prostate cancer survivors [14], and with Portuguese data demonstrating temporal risk escalation beyond 4 years post-diagnosis [15].

The predominance of adenocarcinomas (lung, colorectal, gastric) over sarcomas in our SPM spectrum is biologically coherent (see Figs. 10, 11, and 12). Shared environmental carcinogens and field cancerization effects explain this distribution far more parsimoniously than radiation-induced mesenchymal transformation, which requires latency periods typically exceeding 5 years and occurs at only 1.8-fold excess compared to surgery patients [22]. In populations with significant tobacco exposure [28], [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT simultaneously characterizes prostatic disease and surveys for synchronous oncological events, a diagnostic efficiency unavailable from any single-tracer approach.

The dedifferentiation finding: a window that PSMA cannot open

The clinical trajectories of dedifferentiated disease in our cohort illustrate what is at stake when this diagnostic window is missed. The rapid mortality of t-NEPC patients underscores the urgency of early detection. At the same time, the complete metabolic response observed in the treatment-naive de novo neuroendocrine case (Patient 58) demonstrates that [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT can do something PSMA PET/CT fundamentally cannot: detect the disease, stage it accurately, and monitor treatment response longitudinally, all using the same tracer.

This survival disparity between t-NEPC and de novo neuroendocrine carcinoma reflects distinct mechanistic underpinnings. t-NEPC accumulates resistance mechanisms through RB1/TP53 loss and cross-resistance to ADT, docetaxel, and radiotherapy [12], while de novo neuroendocrine tumors may retain greater platinum sensitivity [12]. Identifying which biological reality a patient faces requires [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT as part of the diagnostic workup, not as an afterthought [29].

The SUVmax independence: a biological, not statistical, statement

The absence of any significant correlation between PSMA and FDG SUVmax values (Spearman rho = 0.149) is mechanistically explanatory. PSMA expression is driven by androgen receptor signaling and differentiation status; FDG avidity reflects glycolytic demand driven by hypoxia, proliferation rate, and metabolic reprogramming. These biological determinants are largely independent, and our data reflect this independence directly. High PSMA uptake does not predict high or low FDG avidity, and clinical decisions based solely on PSMA quantification will systematically mischaracterize a subset of patients whose disease has evolved beyond the prostatic adenocarcinoma phenotype.

Guideline integration and expanded indications

Toward a shared accountability model for dual-tracer imaging

Resource-intensive imaging must be applied judiciously. We propose a Shared Accountability Model that distributes responsibility across the clinical team: referring clinicians must identify high-risk phenotypes (rising PSA with PSMA-negative imaging, prolonged ADT, prior radiotherapy, suspicious conventional imaging) and provide comprehensive clinical context; nuclear medicine physicians serve as informed gatekeepers, ensuring [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT is ordered sequentially after PSMA imaging when findings are suspicious or discordant [30, 33]; and healthcare systems must support this through integrated clinical decision support tools and optimized scheduling protocols to minimize interscan intervals. This framework ensures that dual-tracer imaging is reserved for cases where it provides a clear, measurable diagnostic benefit.

Expanding the role of PSMA PET/CT: targeted biopsy guidance

Beyond its established role in staging and recurrence detection, PSMA PET/CT is increasingly recognized as a valuable tool in the primary diagnostic pathway for clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa). Recent evidence demonstrates that PSMA-targeted biopsy guidance improves the detection of clinically significant disease while reducing the sampling of indolent tumors, thereby refining the diagnostic yield of prostate biopsy in the era of active surveillance [25]. This emerging application underscores the expanding clinical utility of PSMA PET/CT beyond its traditional oncological staging role and reinforces the importance of understanding its inherent limitations—including the diagnostic blind spots addressed by dual-tracer imaging in the present study.

False-positive and false-negative pitfalls of PSMA PET/CT

The diagnostic accuracy of PSMA PET/CT, while superior to conventional imaging, is not absolute. False-positive PSMA uptake has been documented in benign prostatic conditions, most notably granulomatous prostatitis and chronic inflammation, which can mimic malignancy on PSMA imaging and lead to unnecessary biopsies or treatment escalation [26]. Sympathetic ganglia represent another well-recognized source of false-positive findings in the retroperitoneal and pelvic regions. Additionally, certain non-prostatic malignancies (renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, thyroid carcinoma) may demonstrate PSMA uptake on their neovasculature, potentially confounding the interpretation of metastatic disease extent. On the false-negative side, low-volume biochemical recurrence below the spatial resolution threshold of current PET systems, post-treatment fibrosis masking residual viable disease, and pharmacological modulation of PSMA expression by androgen receptor pathway inhibitors all contribute to potential underestimation of disease burden. These pitfalls further justify the complementary role of [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT in selected clinical scenarios where PSMA findings are equivocal or discordant with clinical presentation.

Prostatic ductal carcinoma: a histological subtype with attenuated PSMA expression

Prostatic ductal adenocarcinoma represents an aggressive histological variant that poses particular challenges for PSMA-based imaging. Pepe et al. demonstrated that PSMA PET/CT exhibits reduced accuracy in diagnosing and staging ductal prostate cancer compared to conventional acinar adenocarcinoma, attributable to the attenuated PSMA expression characteristic of this subtype [27]. This finding has important implications for both staging accuracy and eligibility assessment for [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-PSMA radioligand therapy, as ductal variants may be systematically understaged by PSMA imaging alone. In the context of dual-tracer imaging, [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT may provide superior sensitivity for detecting ductal carcinoma given its typically high glycolytic activity, further supporting the complementary role of metabolic imaging in histologically heterogeneous prostate cancer. Representative cases illustrating the spectrum of dual-tracer findings, including concordant and discordant uptake patterns, second primary malignancy detection, and exclusion of malignancy, are presented in Figs. 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The retrospective, single-center design introduces inherent selection bias; results should not be extrapolated to unselected prostate cancer populations. The sample size, while robust for a dual-tracer cohort, limits statistical power in subgroup analyses. Follow-up duration was relatively short for some patients, precluding comprehensive long-term survival analysis by imaging phenotype. The interscan interval exceeded 26 days in some patients owing to scheduling constraints at our institution; any patient who received systemic treatment during this period was excluded. Finally, while histopathological confirmation was mandated for all SPMs and suspected neuroendocrine transformation, biopsy of every discordant metastatic lesion was neither ethically nor practically feasible. This reflects the real-world operational constraints inherent to a high-volume public university hospital, where appointment availability for PET/CT is limited by scanner capacity, radiopharmaceutical production schedules (particularly for [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 requiring on-site generator availability), and competing clinical priorities; the interval was dictated by scheduling logistics rather than study design.

Conclusion

PSMA PET/CT is transformative, but it has a blind spot. When PSMA is not enough, [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT is not merely a supplementary tool; it is a clinically decisive one. In this real-world, indication-driven cohort, the addition of [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT directly and measurably altered management in more than half of carefully selected high-risk prostate cancer patients. It detected second primary malignancies invisible to PSMA, identified dedifferentiated disease variants with urgent therapeutic implications, and provided a biologically orthogonal assessment that no other clinically available single-tracer approach can replicate.

The field needs to move beyond the question of whether [¹⁸F]FDG adds value, it does, unequivocally, in the right patients. The more clinically productive question is: which patients, at which decision points, benefit most from crossing the PSMA threshold into dual-tracer territory? The indication-driven framework validated by this study offers a principled answer. When applied within a multidisciplinary shared accountability model, dual-tracer [¹⁸F]FDG and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT is not an imaging indulgence; it is a precision diagnostic imperative.
