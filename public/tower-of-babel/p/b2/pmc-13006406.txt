Developing and evaluating multimodal large language model for orthopantomography analysis to support clinical dentistry
Authors: Xinyu Liu, Kuo Feng Hung, Weihao Yu, Ray Anthony WT Ng, Wuyang Li, Tianye Niu, Hui Chen, Yixuan Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOrthopantomography (OPG) is a primary screening tool for initial dental diagnosis, yet existing AI support typically operates in a one-way manner, lacking interactive nature like ChatGPT. To address this, we introduce ToothXpert, a dental diagnostic system with multimodal large language model. We curated a dental concept alignment dataset and a comprehensive multimodal OPG dataset, MM-OPG (comprising 131,065 question-answer [QA] pairs), covering 11 key conditions. ToothXpert facilitates simultaneous visual and language responses, fostering dynamic dentistry support. On the internal test dataset with 4,950 QA pairs, ToothXpert achieves a macro F1 score of 78.61%, outperforming LLaVA v.1.5 by 23.19%, LLaVA-Med v.1.5 by 41.39%, Qwen-VL by 56.38%, and HuatuoGPT-Vision by 26.74%. In human assessment, ToothXpert achieves an averaged performance of 3.54, surpassing compared multimodal large language models (MLLMs) with 1.46 and 1.38. Moreover, on the external dataset, ToothXpert achieves 1.96% and 2.99% higher F1 scores than two junior dentists with 3-years’ clinical experience, while requiring significantly less time.Keywords: Orthopantomography, multimodal large language model, artificial intelligence, computer-aided diagnosis, dental imaging, visual question answering, instruction tuning, teeth segmentation, clinical decision support, deep learning

Introduction

Orthopantomography (OPG), commonly known as panoramic dental X-ray, is widely used in routine clinical practice as a primary screening tool, aiding in the initial assessment and diagnosis of various dental pathologies.1 It furnishes comprehensive information for dental practitioners to evaluate overall oral health conditions, including, but not limited to, aspects such as dentition, dental diseases, existing restorations, and dental implants.2 Nevertheless, analyzing an OPG image could be time consuming, even for experienced dentists and dental radiologists.3,4,5 In recent years, significant advancements have been observed in the field of interpreting and analyzing OPG images with artificial intelligence (AI) technologies. The substantial high-performance computing resources and the development of sophisticated algorithms have enabled the creation of AI-powered vision systems that can aid in the understanding process of OPGs and perform multiple vision tasks. The tasks encompass teeth segmentation,6 lesion detection,7 caries segmentation,8 dental image retrieval,9 etc. Recently, breakthroughs in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have inspired researchers to build medical dialog models.10,11,12,13,14 For example, LLaVA-Med11 fine-tuned the language model and projector in LLaVA15 with the biomedical dataset, which contains image-text pairs including chest X-ray, magnetic resonance imaging, and histopathology. Despite notable advancements, current vision systems predominantly prioritize vision-only tasks such as lesion segmentation or localization, which often require doctors to make extra effort to learn their operation and interpret the results. Hence, they are limited to providing indirect information that often lacks clarity and are ineffective in assisting the diagnosis process.3,4 Moreover, there remains a significant gap in vision-text systems with ChatGPT-like interactive interface for assisting dentists in OPG diagnosis, as the existing medical MLLMs such as LLaVA-Med11,16,17,18,19 have only been proposed for dermatologists18 or pulmonologists.19 This deficiency is mainly attributed to the scarcity of multimodal OPG data with both OPG images and corresponding text descriptions. In contrast to other diagnostic procedures that require doctors to document findings for long-term treatment planning, there is presently no established format for recording findings in OPG. Clinicians commonly make note of abnormal findings in a private practice environment. Therefore, this absence of detailed and structured reports for routine dental radiographs presents a significant challenge to the advancement of AI assistance in dentistry. Additionally, previous methods have been designed to tackle one task at a time, such as segmentation or classification,20 which can be restrictive in real-world clinical scenarios where dentists may require software to handle a broader range of specific tasks or by enabling interaction with the system. These limitations underscore the need for a more comprehensive AI system that can provide explicit and informative responses to clinicians’ queries while performing multiple tasks to streamline the diagnosis process, thereby enhancing the quality of patient care.

In this article, we introduce a large multitask MLLM with both vision and language processing abilities, named ToothXpert, which serves as an interactive AI assistant for analyzing OPGs. To build such an MLLM assistant for dentists, we proposed a two-stage tuning strategy, namely, dental concept alignment and dental instruction tuning to facilitate the model for OPG analysis. The process is illustrated in Figure 1C. In the first stage, we developed a dental concept alignment dataset with 40,158 open-ended visual question answering (VQA) pairs, which was curated from a large biomedical VQA dataset PMC-VQA.12 The dataset is used for training the multimodal projector of the model, thereby adapting it to recognize basic dental concepts. In the second stage, we constructed a detailed dental instruction tuning dataset named multimodal OPG dataset (MM-OPG) via human-machine collaboration, which is illustrated in Figure 1A. We extensively collected OPG data from six sources, including five publicly available datasets with 10,043 OPGs, as well as 519 in-house private patient data. To construct comprehensive and structured text data for the OPGs, three dental experts from diverse institutions have carefully designed and summarized 11 commonly considered dental conditions during OPG analysis routine and annotated all collected OPG images. The annotated data were then reviewed and corrected through a collaborative process among the dental experts to ensure consistency and accuracy. After annotation, we augmented the data to automatically balance the classes and lead to the organized dataset with 131,065 VQA pairs. These questions covered a broad range of multifaceted and important OPG characteristics, including periodontal disease-related symptoms, endodontic features, and restoratives. We split MM-OPG into training and internal test datasets and collected a confidential external test dataset with 19 cases for further validation. The detailed per-condition distributions in each dataset are summarized in Figure 1B.

(C) The training process began with the integration of a biomedical VQA dataset, which served as the foundation for pretraining the model. Then, the dental instruction tuning stage used a total of 10,562 original images. We use data augmentation to obtain 131,065 corresponding VQA pairs from six diverse sources to tune the ToothXpert model. The subsequent validation stage employed both internal and external testing data to assess model performance. The internal validation utilized a held-out test dataset and was assessed by dental experts. The external validation leveraged confidential hospital archives to evaluate the model’s applicability when compared to junior dentists.

We also extended the capability of performing simultaneous question answering and image segmentation to provide both textual and visual assistance to the dentist. We connected the LLM with the segmentation decoder and enabled the model to segment teeth following human instructions by adapting the LISA framework.21 The MM-OPG vision-text dataset was fused with 1,000 OPG with teeth segmentation masks, and the fused dataset was used for training the LLM and segmentation decoder. To enable the model to handle diverse VQA and segmentation tasks simultaneously, we proposed a Guided Mixture of LoRA Experts (G-MoLE) method for tuning the model with multiple Low-rank Adaptation (LoRA) modules. ToothXpert was extensively validated on the held-out internal test dataset as well as external test dataset and was compared with state-of-the-art MLLMs11,22 and human junior dentists. In the internal test dataset, ToothXpert achieved a macro F1 score of 78.61%, outperformed LLaVA v.1.5 by 23.19%, LLaVA-Med v.1.5 by 41.39%, Qwen-VL23 by 56.38%, and HuatuoGPT-Vision24 by 26.74%. Meanwhile, two dental experts have assessed the text response of different MLLMs; ToothXpert achieved a high score of 3.54, while the other two compared MLLMs scored 1.46 and 1.38, respectively. In the confidential external testing data, three junior dentists with 3 years of clinical experience were invited to benchmark our model’s performance against theirs. ToothXpert showed the best precision score 92.03% and outperformed two of them in F1 score, while requiring significantly less time to analyze each OPG case (7.09 s/case compared to dentists with ∼180 s/case). The results suggest that the state-of-the-art MLLMs are unable to meet the requirements in analyzing OPGs, while ToothXpert can facilitate more reliable, efficient, and convenient OPG diagnoses to assist dentists in clinical dentistry.

Results

Comparison with MLLMs on the internal test dataset

We first present a quantitative comparison between ToothXpert and four MLLMs, LLaVA v.1.5,22 LLaVA-Med v.1.5,11 HuatuoGPT-Vision,24 and Qwen-VL,23 on the task of OPG image analysis. We evaluated the performance of these models on the held-out internal test dataset comprising 450 cases and 4,950 VQA pairs, covering the 11 targeted dental conditions. As shown in Figure 2A, the results demonstrated the superiority of ToothXpert over the competing MLLMs in terms of precision and F1 score. For example, in the condition “Teeth supported bridge/crown,” ToothXpert achieved a precision of 91.42% and an F1 score of 93.17%, whereas all other MLLMs failed to surpass 50% F1 score for this oral condition. Similarly, for the condition “Dental implant,” ToothXpert reached 97.06% F1 score, while the highest value among the compared MLLMs was 69.60% by LLaVA v.1.5. ToothXpert also demonstrated superior performance in categories involving subtle features. Specifically, for the “Root canal-treated tooth” condition, ToothXpert showed an F1 score of 92.16%, which surpassed LLaVA v.1.5 by 26.71%, LLaVA-Med v.1.5 by 56.35%, HuatuoGPT-Vision by 31.74%, and Qwen-VL by 55.80%, demonstrating the effectiveness of ToothXpert in covering a wide range of dental conditions. A per-condition comparison of ToothXpert, LLaVA v.1.5, LLaVA-Med v.1.5, Qwen-VL, and HuatuoGPT-Vision is presented in Figure 3; the results show that ToothXpert is more robust and reliable in its performance, whereas the other MLLMs may be more prone to errors and inconsistencies. The overall performance is shown in Table S6, when using the metric of Matthews correlation coefficient, ToothXpert yielded the highest value of 0.6558, whereas the compared models only gave 0.2623, 0.1347, 0.0084, and 0.2187, respectively. Overall, ToothXpert is more effective in detecting and classifying dental conditions and restorations compared to the MLLMs. This is because ToothXpert was specifically designed for OPG image analysis and trained with well-curated dental instruction tuning datasets, while the MLLMs are general-purpose language models that may not have the same level of expertise in this domain. Additionally, the proposed G-MoLE tuning paradigm was tailored to handle the diverse conditions and restorations that are present in OPGs.

Human assessment on the internal test dataset

The results of human evaluation are presented in Figures 2B and 2C. The correctness metric assesses the accuracy of the response, measuring whether the information provided is factually accurate and consistent with established dental knowledge. ToothXpert outperformed both LLaVA v.1.5 and LLaVA-Med v.1.5 in all four human evaluation metrics. Specifically, ToothXpert achieved a correctness score of 3.62, significantly higher than LLaVA v.1.5 and LLaVA-Med v.1.5 with 1.40 and 1.09, respectively. Relevance evaluates the extent to which the response describes the provided OPG, while expressing the key conditions within the image. Similarly, ToothXpert’s relevance score of 3.60 is higher than those of LLaVA v.1.5 with 1.48 and LLaVA-Med v.1.5 with 1.47. In terms of clarity, which measures the ease with which the response can be understood, ToothXpert scored 3.54, outperforming LLaVA v.1.5 with 1.46 and LLaVA-Med v.1.5 with 1.38. Finally, the completeness metric evaluates the extent to which the response provides a comprehensive and thorough description of the significant conditions of the OPG image. ToothXpert achieved a score of 3.47, which is also higher than that of the compared models. By jointly considering the four metrics, our model had the highest average score of 3.54, and it had a small standard deviation among metrics, while showing statistical significance on all metrics. The significant performance gap between ToothXpert and the comparison models suggests that our model is better equipped to handle the complexities of dental domain knowledge and provide high-quality responses, which has a greater potential for clinical applicability and is effective in assisting dentists in OPG diagnosis.

Comparison with junior dentists on the external test dataset

We compared the performance of ToothXpert with that of three junior dentists (Dentist 1, Dentist 2, and Dentist 3) in terms of processing time per case, precision, recall, and F1 score, all quantified with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to provide a robust measure of uncertainty and statistical stability. As shown in the table in Figure 2D, our ToothXpert achieved significantly faster processing time per case, with an average time of 7.09 s. This represents an approximately 25-fold increase in speed compared to the junior dentists, who took an average of 180.50, 176.50, and 175.25 s, respectively. This stark difference in processing speed highlights ToothXpert’s substantial efficiency advantage in a clinical setting. Despite this dramatic difference in processing time, our system demonstrated highly competitive performance across diagnostic accuracy metrics. ToothXpert achieved a precision of 92.03% (95% CI: 70.45%, 95.91%), a recall of 65.95% (95% CI: 52.27%, 76.44%), and an F1 score of 73.73% (95% CI: 58.18%, 81.31%). In contrast, the junior dentists exhibited the following ranges: precision from 73.53% to 79.81%, recall from 67.81% to 79.52%, and F1 scores from 70.74% to 74.41%. Our model’s precision score of 92.03% is notably higher than that of all junior dentists, with its lower bound still exceeding 70%, which suggests a markedly superior ability to correctly identify positive cases with fewer false-positives. This high precision is crucial in preventing unnecessary interventions or patient anxiety resulting from misdiagnoses. For recall, ToothXpert’s score of 65.95% is slightly lower than that of the junior dentists. However, the 95% CI for ToothXpert’s recall (52.27%, 76.44%) largely overlaps with those of the junior dentists. In terms of the F1 score, ToothXpert achieved 73.73%. This value surpassed two of the three junior dentists (71.77% and 70.74%) and was comparable to the best junior dentist (74.41%). Moreover, the 95% CI for ToothXpert’s F1 score demonstrates substantial overlap with those of the junior dentists, particularly Dentist 2. These results collectively demonstrate that our system is capable of achieving an excellent balance between precision and recall and is a robust tool for identifying dental anomalies from radiographs. We also evaluated the performance of each model on a per-case basis, as shown in Figure 2E. The observed variation across different cases highlights the importance of evaluating model performance under diverse conditions.

Moreover, it was shown that ToothXpert had a relatively consistent performance across conditions compared to junior dentists, demonstrating that our model is less biased and more reliable. In Figure 4A, we have provided a more detailed breakdown on each condition. For example, ToothXpert achieved high precision and recall on conditions such as C7-“Metallic and non-metallic post” and C8-“Metallic restorations” when compared to junior dentists, although it registered lower precision and recall on conditions such as C11-“Generalized bone loss of the maxillary.” From the results, it is suggested that our model performed well in identifying certain dental conditions with more holistic radiographic features. For the relatively lower performance on certain conditions, it is likely attributed to the limited representation of these symptoms in the training data. In Figure 4B, the pairwise comparisons of agreement between models (LLaVA v.1.5, LLaVA-Med v.1.5, ToothXpert) and the three junior dentists (Dentist 1, Dentist 2, Dentist 3), along with the ground truth were displayed. The metrics included Cohen’s kappa and the Jaccard similarity coefficient. The agreement between ToothXpert and the ground truth was significantly higher than that of LLaVA v.1.5 and LLaVA-Med v.1.5, achieving a Cohen’s kappa of 0.58 and a Jaccard of 0.61, which demonstrates better alignment with the ground truth. Among the three junior dentists, Dentist 2 showed the highest agreement with the ground truth of 0.51 in kappa and 0.53 in Jaccard. ToothXpert showed better agreement than the best-performing junior dentist, indicating that ToothXpert’s predictions are more closely aligned with dental expert consensus. The results suggest that ToothXpert is effective in providing reliable support for OPG diagnosis.

Furthermore, in Figure 4C, the performance of different AI models and dentists on different complexities of dental conditions is shown. The results were categorized into the following three levels of complexity. (1) Mild: basic or highly conspicuous findings with features easily and consistently recognizable due to their high contrast or large-scale presentation, making them readily identifiable with different conditions of OPG. This category includes metallic restorations, non-metallic restorations, and generalized bone loss. (2) Moderate: findings with intermediate complexity of detection, whose features are of intermediate complexity that are generally detectable but may lack fine detail. Their accurate assessments are influenced by the OPG’s resolution limits and potential for anatomical noise or superimposition, including root canal-treated tooth, teeth-supported bridge/crown, and periapical radiolucency (R/L) observed on the tooth. (3) Complex: subtle or diagnostically challenging findings that typically require comprehensive image interpretation or greater clinical experience. Their features are often small, low-contrast, or located in areas prone to being masked by overlapping anatomical structures or restorative materials. This category includes metallic and non-metallic post, dental implant, implant-supported bridge/crown, and R/L suggestive of caries. The boundary examples of each complexity level are shown in Figure S2. ToothXpert consistently outperformed the SOTA MLLMs across all complexity levels, achieving the second-best performance overall in Mild and Complex categories. Among the dentists, Dentist 2 performed best in Mild conditions, while Dentist 1 excelled in the Complex conditions. In contrast, the performance of the compared MLLMs was poor and even dropped significantly as complexity increased, indicating that they are incapable of providing reliable OPG analysis. An additional column representing the overall average performance is also included. Ultimately, ToothXpert’s ability to deliver consistent and high performance across all levels of complexity showcases its potential as a reliable diagnostic tool, capable of handling both straightforward and intricate dental conditions with minimal variability.

ToothXpert as a junior dentist diagnosis assistant

Given that ToothXpert is designed to support early-career, board-certified general dentists who read OPGs routinely in primary care, we conducted additional validation to measure the improvements of the three junior dentists on the 19 cases in the external test dataset, with the assistance of our ToothXpert model. The results are presented in Figure 5A. With assistance, all junior dentists improved their F1 scores (+0.028 to +0.086) and recall (+0.062 to +0.156). One junior dentist showed a large precision gain of 0.035 with the assistance of ToothXpert, while the other two showed slight decreases in precision, as they improved recall at the cost of precision. These results indicate that our proposed ToothXpert elevates the diagnostic performance of junior dentists toward the senior dentist level.

Diagnosis process explainability on the external test dataset

We have conducted a confidence estimation by extracting the probability assigned to the “Yes” or “No” tokens at the response to a specific dental condition question. This represents the model’s confidence in its response and serves as an explanation of why ToothXpert makes certain diagnoses. Figure 5B shows the distribution of per-category confidence scores across all test cases; most predictions were at high probability levels (>0.9), indicating that ToothXpert often provides not only categorical answers but also high levels of certainty rather than random guessing. This helps clinicians determine when the model is confident versus when caution may be warranted. Moreover, we observed that certain conditions (e.g., C2-Teeth supported bridge/crown, C3-Dental implant, C4-Implant-supported bridge/crown) tended to yield consistently higher confidence, while others (e.g., C1-Generalized bone loss of the mandible, C6-Periapical R/L observed on the tooth) showed broader distributions. This suggests that well-defined dental structures and restorations are easier for the model to identify reliably, whereas diffuse lesions or bone-level changes are more challenging.

Furthermore, considering that clinical decision making calls for subtleties beyond numerical performance criteria, we present illustrative cases of ToothXpert’s diagnostic outcomes to highlight its handling of subtle features assessed by dental experts in Figure 5C. In the top case, ToothXpert accurately identifies several conditions, including the “Generalized bone loss of the mandible” and “R/L suggestive of caries.” Regarding caries, the model identifies the faint radiolucency in the tooth structure, a subtle feature that dentists also rely on. Moreover, the accurate identification of generalized bone loss demonstrates the model’s capability in detecting subtle variations in bone density and morphology. These subtle changes can be easily missed by less-experienced clinicians, but ToothXpert successfully captures them, which further validates its ability in recognizing the subtle pattern that dentists use for clinical diagnosis. In the bottom case, ToothXpert recognizes the restorations and correctly captures the root canals. However, it misclassifies “Teeth-supported bridge/crown” and “R/L suggestive of caries,” which may be due to visual ambiguities between restorative materials and anatomical structures, where a dental expert could make more nuanced decisions. To summarize, ToothXpert captures clinical subtleties comparable to dentists in most cases, yet still leaves room for improvement via enhanced training data.

Teeth segmentation evaluation on the segmentation dataset

We evaluated ToothXpert’s teeth segmentation ability against other specialized CNN and transformer medical image segmentation models, including U-Net,25 AttU-Net,26 UNext,27 and DAEFormer28 on a held-out teeth segmentation dataset comprising 100 cases. The compared models were all constructed with an encoder-decoder structure, aimed to achieve the single 2D medical image segmentation task. We used the same training dataset for all models and computed the metrics by resizing the segmented images to the original resolution 320 × 640. For ToothXpert, we prompted it with “Can you segment the teeth in the image?” and recorded the visual segmentation response of the model. According to the results in Figure 6A, ToothXpert demonstrated competitive performance compared to other specialized segmentation models. In terms of the Dice coefficient, which measures the similarity between the predicted mask and the ground-truth mask, ToothXpert achieved a mean value of 0.8912, with a maximum of 0.9403 and a minimum of 0.6672. This was comparable to the other models, with AttU-Net achieving the highest mean Dice coefficient of 0.9013. Similarly, the Jaccard coefficient, which measures the intersection over union of the predicted and ground-truth segmentation masks, showed that ToothXpert had a mean value of 0.8060, with a maximum of 0.8873 and a minimum of 0.5006. Given that ToothXpert allocates more model capacity to cross-modal reasoning and uses segmented tooth regions to support clinical understanding, slight quantitative differences in segmentation metrics are expected. This trade-off allows the segmentation to provide meaningful localization without limiting the model’s ability to interpret complex clinical queries. However, in the qualitative comparison in Figure 6B, ToothXpert was capable of accurately capturing fine details and maintaining the integrity of complex dental structures. Notably, ToothXpert demonstrated superior edge detection, effectively delineating boundaries between teeth and surrounding tissues, which showcases its robustness in complex scenarios. Therefore, the visual results produced by ToothXpert are sufficiently accurate for clinical purposes. The ability to effectively segment teeth in OPGs validates its potential as a valuable tool for dental OPG analysis.

Discussion

Computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) has been extensively explored in various medical imaging domains, including chest X-ray,19 skin,18 and pathology,16 among others. These advances have led to significant improvements in diagnostic accuracy and efficiency, enabling clinicians to provide better care for their patients. However, the dental field has largely been overlooked, despite its significant potential for CAD applications. While some existing works have focused on dental image segmentation and classification,6,8,29 these efforts have been limited in scope and have not addressed the more complex task of diagnosis. In particular, there is a lack of large-scale datasets and comprehensive frameworks for dental diagnosis, hindering the development of accurate and reliable CAD systems. To bridge this gap, we have proposed a multitask approach based on MLLM named ToothXpert that can simultaneously perform teeth segmentation, answer questions related to OPGs, and provide a detailed analysis of the X-ray images. We present examples of the system response to users in Figure S14. Our model was first trained on a dental concept alignment dataset with open-ended conversations, which is shown in Figure 7A. Subsequently, the aligned model was trained using the large-scale multimodal OPG dataset MM-OPG comprising 131,065 question-answer pairs, as illustrated in Figure 7B. Each image corresponds to 11 standard conditions defined by three dental experts from different hospitals, with the full details outlined in Figure 7C. Our model was extensively validated on the internal and external datasets, assessed by dental radiologists, and compared to three junior dentists with 3 years’ clinical experience. The proposed ToothXpert could assist clinicians in interpreting OPGs and has the potential to improve the accuracy and efficiency of dental diagnoses, and we hope that it will inspire more work in this promising research field, ultimately leading to better doctor assistance and patient outcomes. Moreover, it can be leveraged to enhance dental education. It serves as an interactive tool for junior students to self-learn and improve their OPG diagnostic skills. It can supplement traditional teaching methods, increase student confidence and competence, and ultimately improve their diagnosis skills.

Limitations of the study

One limitation is that while it has been fine-tuned to respond to dental conditions and analyze OPGs, it cannot perform the next step of treatment planning for specific patient cases. For instance, our model can identify the presence of caries or other dental conditions, but it cannot provide a tailored treatment plan or recommend specific interventions. This limitation is due to the lack of comprehensive clinical data and expert knowledge to train the model, and our model was not trained with reinforcement learning that could incorporate human feedback.30 Additionally, our model’s segmentation capabilities are limited to the general teeth region at present, rather than specific diseases or lesions. This limitation stems from the significant challenge of accurately annotating the sheer volume of tiny, diverse dental conditions. In future work, we plan to address this by exploring joint fine-tuning, diversifying our segmentation mask annotations, and enabling human-interactive segmentation. Another limitation of our model and data is that there were some imbalances in the dataset. Although we have integrated data from multiple public and private sources, including dental clinics and hospitals from varied demographics, the dataset still exhibited some imbalances. For instance, certain dental conditions, such as rare dental disorders, were underrepresented in the dataset, while most cases were mild and had clinically insignificant issues. This imbalance may affect the model’s ability to generalize to unseen data,31 and a limited sample size might lead to overestimation or underestimation of performance for less-frequent categories. We are actively working to address these limitations by collecting more diverse and comprehensive data and exploring new techniques such as reinforcement learning and transfer learning to improve the model’s performance and generalizability.

Experimental model and study participant details

To train and evaluate ToothXpert, we curated datasets from multiple public sources and an in-house collection. The study protocol for collecting in-house data received approval from the Institutional Review Board of The University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (HKU/HA HKW IRB) (UW24520). The in-house dataset for training and internal testing was retrospectively collected. Due to the strict de-identification protocols approved by the IRB to ensure patient privacy, all personal health information (PHI), including age, sex, and ethnicity, was removed prior to analysis. Consequently, demographic statistics for the large-scale in-house training dataset are not available. Sex-based subgroup analysis was not conducted, as sex information was removed from the training dataset during de-identification, and the external test set was underpowered for meaningful statistical comparison. For external evaluation, we engaged a separate cohort of 19 OPGs collected from clinical practice to benchmark the model against junior dentists. Demographic information for this external test dataset is detailed in Table S13. The cohort comprised 12 females and 7 males. The participants represented a diverse age range with years of birth spanning from the 1940s to the 2000s. Geographic origins included Hong Kong (n = 14), Mainland China (n = 3), and the Philippines (n = 2).

For the dental concept alignment stage for pretraining, our dataset was built upon PMC-VQA,12 which used 381K image-caption pairs from the database, which contained multi-level complexity of the multimodal data. We applied a series of filtering and annotation steps to the PMC-VQA dataset and resulting in a total of 40,158 visual question and answer pairs, as shown in Figure 7A. For the dental instruction tuning stage, we curated a multi-modal OPG dataset (MM-OPG) from multiple public sources and an in-house collection. The annotation process for the MM-OPG dataset was conducted by three dental experts. All three annotators possess over 10 years of clinical practice experience. Specifically, two of the annotators hold PhD degrees in dentistry with a specialization in cariology and have extensive experience in general dental practice and radiographic interpretation. The third annotator holds a Master of Dental Surgery (MDS) degree in Prosthodontics and has substantial experience in general practice and dental radiology. After processing, summarizing, and data augmentation, we obtained 10,562 OPG images with 131,065 question-answer pairs in total, as shown in Figure 7B, which preserved 11 key conditions that could be analyzed from OPGs, as illustrated in Figure 7C. We randomly held out 450 images for internal testing and the rest for training. For the external evaluation, we deployed our model to a private dataset of 19 OPGs from clinical practice, which were not used in model training.

Method details

Data preprocessing

For the pretraining dataset construction on the PMC-VQA dataset, we first selected a small subset of images and captions that were specifically related to dental topics, such as oral anatomy, dental procedures, and dental diseases. We then prompted ChatGPT with the keywords within the topics to generate additional keywords related to them and applied a thresholding to select the keywords that occurred with the highest frequencies. The final dental concept keyword vocabulary contained 72 words and phrases. We queried the dataset using dental concept keyword vocabulary to retrieve a subset of images and captions that are highly relevant to dental topics, resulting in a total of 40,158 visual question and answer pairs. This process has allowed us to narrow down the scope of the dataset to focus on the most critical and relevant dental concepts. To enable better alignment between the dental concepts and images, we converted the original closed-ended VQA to open-ended data, by providing the model with captions and questions, and supervised the model with the answer. The reason was that open-ended VQA allows the model to generate more diverse and detailed answers, which was essential for dental concept alignment. This approach has enabled the model to move beyond simple choice answers and to comprehend more intricate and detailed explanations of dental concepts, which was beneficial for the following LLM model tuning.

For the MM-OPG dataset construction, we aimed to maintain the complexity and authenticity of the dataset, so we retained the OPG images from the permanent dentition stage, while excluding the mixed dentition and non-dental images. Then, to create a comprehensive multi-modal OPG dataset, it was essential to prioritize the usability of the question-answer pairs for dentists. This involved ensuring that the information provided is meaningful, relevant, and effectively reduces redundancy. We asked dental experts to design a template that encapsulated pertinent information extractable from OPG and applicable to clinical practice. This served as a structured and comprehensive documentation design for OPG findings. After processing and summarizing, we preserved 11 key conditions that could be analyzed from OPGs. The conditions covered diverse perspectives, including fixed prosthodontics, implant-related, endodontic, restorative, caries-related, and periodontal disease-related. Afterward, problems were kept as individual questions, e.g.,: Q: Is there generalised bone loss of the maxillary in the image? A: Yes, there is generalized bone loss of the maxillary in the image. We have also measured the inter-rater reliability measures to ensure the consistency and accuracy of the annotations across different evaluators. The Fleiss’s kappa among the three dentists was 0.9267, indicating excellent agreement. More metrics are presented in Table S10. We then merged all the conditions within a single case to build a summarized medical report, which is used for answering questions of “Can you describe the image for me?”. This annotation process allowed us to generate a substantial quantity of informative and high-quality question-answer pairs. We filtered and formatted these pairs to align with our instruction tuning input requirements. Then, we distributed it into training data and internal test data, as shown in Figure S11. However, the original dataset was highly biased with a majority of panoramic OPGs depicting relatively normal conditions. Therefore, we augmented the dataset via a strategic hybrid approach that combines data augmentation with class-wise oversampling. For tail classes such as implant-supported bridge and dental implant, we first identified all positive cases and augmented them using translation, zooming in and out, and color jittering. Importantly, we modulated the augmentation strength for each class to maintain the natural class frequency ratios as much as possible, thereby reducing the risk of introducing unrealistic distributions. As demonstrated in Figure S10, our augmentation substantially balanced the dataset: for example, the proportion of implant-supported bridge cases increased from 1.4% to 19.0%, narrowing the gap with dominant classes like non-metallic restorations, which decreased from 58.6% to 39.0%. Our approach mitigates overfitting risks associated with traditional oversampling, while ensuring robust model training across both rare and common dental conditions. The augmented dataset includes 131,065 question-answer pairs in total. It not only increased the diversity of dental conditions present in the training data but also ensured that the model becomes proficient in identifying both common and rare dental conditions. The proposed MM-OPG dataset was expected to push the boundaries of AI assisted dental diagnostics, combining multi-source high-quality data, expert annotations, and augmentation strategies to train a model that was both practical and powerful in a real-world clinical environment.

Model architecture

Compared to text-only large language models (LLMs), a multimodal large language model (MLLM) was trained to understand and respond to user instructions in the form of natural language queries that may additionally contain inputs from other modalities such as images. Notably, OPG diagnosis constituted a paradigmatic example of a multimodal task, wherein dental professionals engaged in the analysis of dental conditions through the interpretation of OPGs. In this context, it was essential to build an MLLM that could integrate both natural language queries and visual data from X-ray images, thereby enabling the generation of more accurate and informative responses.

The overall framework of ToothXpert was composed of five components: the vision encoder, the multimodal projector that aligns the image and text modality, the large language model, the segmentation encoder, and the segmentation decoder. The vision encoder was a pretrained CLIP33 that was capable of extracting rich visual features from the OPGs. These features were then fed into the multimodal projector, which projects the image features into a shared latent space, where they could be effectively aligned with the text modality. The large language model was finetuned to process both the natural language queries and the projected visual features, enabling the model to generate responses that incorporate both sources of information. The segmentation encoder and decoder were responsible for generating pixel-wise segmentation masks, which identify the tooth regions of interest in the X-ray images. By integrating these components, ToothXpert could perform a range of tasks, including image-based question answering, teeth segmentation, and generating informative reports that summarize the diagnostic findings.

In the first dental concept alignment stage, we did not incorporate the segmentation encoder and decoder into our model training. The vision encoder and LLM weights were kept frozen, and only the multimodal projector was updated. It was used for learning the basic representations of dental concepts. Considering the original adapter in LLaVA was pre-trained for general domain vision-language alignment, it may not be sufficient for capturing OPG semantics that require specialized knowledge, such as subtle radiographic features and nuanced anatomical details. Therefore, by training the multimodal projector in this stage, we adapted the model to the specific demands of OPG analysis, ensuring that it learns to recognize and prioritize the most relevant features and patterns for accurate dental diagnosis and alignment with the LLM. In the second stage, we proposed a parameter-efficient finetuning method named guided mixture of LoRA experts (G-MoLE). Due to the diverse information present in OPGs, such as abnormalities, bone loss, and restorations, it was challenging for a single model to handle all the diagnostic tasks effectively. In the NLP community, the concept of a mixture of experts (MoE)34 has emerged as a promising approach to enhance the capabilities of models in handling multiple tasks simultaneously, which has inspired us to explore the potential of MoE in OPG image understanding. However, MoE involves a considerable amount of extra parameters, which lead to difficulty in convergence and training efficiency. Therefore, we aimed to apply MoE to parameter-efficient finetuning components, specifically, low-rank adapters (LoRA),35 to simulate the collaborative diagnosis procedure of doctors within a single model. Different from existing MoE paradigms that only activate partial experts for each task, we considered the intrinsic relationship between disease and abnormalities in OPG images and applied a soft router to reweight each LoRA expert. Evaluation metrics and compared models

We evaluated ToothXpert against other MLLMs (general domain models LLaVA v1.5, Qwen-VL and the medical specialist models LLaVA-Med v1.5 and HuatuoGPT-Vision) on both internal and external test datasets. We also compared the performance of our model, ToothXpert, with that of junior dentists in terms of the OPG QA task that requires the detection of each dental condition on the external test dataset. All participants were registered dentists in China with fewer than 3 years of professional experience. We believe that this profile represents the target group poised to derive significant benefits from the assistance provided by ToothXpert in their daily practice. To facilitate a fair and accurate comparison, we designed a specialized prompting strategy that enables the MLLMs to generate appropriate answers for the corresponding conditions. Specifically, we crafted a structured prompt designed to minimize extraneous text and hallucinations, thereby ensuring that the generated responses are concise and relevant. For example, we used the prompt “You are given an OPG image, answer the following question with Yes or No only. Are there any non-metallic restorations in the image?” This prompt allowed the models to provide a clear and unambiguous answer, which was essential for evaluating their performance in detecting specific conditions in OPGs. After post-processing, the answers were used for metric computation. To evaluate the overall description quality of the compared MLLMs, we directly prompted the models with “You are given an OPG image, can you describe the image for me?” and recorded the answers for human evaluation. The comparison on the external test dataset involved the three junior dentists to perform the same OPG analysis task on 19 private cases. Their performance and time per case were recorded and compared against ToothXpert. We also performed a human assessment, where two dental experts assessed the generated reports on a scale of 0–4 for correctness, relevance, clarity, and completeness. The two dental experts are from different hospitals, one is a registered dentist who has more than 10 years of working experience, the other is an oral maxillofacial radiologist. We measured their inter-rater agreement metrics, and their assessments demonstrate strong consistency (e.g., Pearson r for Clarity was 0.908; ICC for Relevance was 0.740; 1-point for Correctness was 0.928). More details are provided in Table S12. Lastly, we evaluated the teeth segmentation performance of ToothXpert and compared it with specialist teeth segmentation models. To measure the performance of the segmentation model, we used the Dice score, Jaccard score, 95HD, and Average Surface Distance (ASD) as the evaluation metrics.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The experiments were conducted using Python (version 3.10.13), PyTorch (version 2.0.1), peft (version 0.4.0), and transformers (version 4.31.0).
