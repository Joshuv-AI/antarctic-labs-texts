A complete blood count-based machine learning model for rapid differentiation of aplastic anemia, immune thrombocytopenia, and myelodysplastic syndromes in routine clinical practice
Authors: Xiaohan Wang, Jian Huang, Jingwen Xi, Huan Liu, Yu Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate differentiation of common hematologic disorders remains challenging in routine clinical practice and often requires invasive diagnostic procedures. Although complete blood count (CBC) testing is widely available, its diagnostic value for early disease triage has not been fully understood.MethodsRetrospectively, among 165,181 routine blood test records collected between October 2011 and June 2025, 4056 samples with confirmed diagnoses were included for model development and validation after exclusion of cases lacking definitive diagnostic information. Patients were classified into aplastic anemia (AA), immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), and other hematologic conditions. Machine learning models were developed using routinely available CBC parameters. Model performance was assessed using one-vs-rest receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, area under the curve (AUC), and class-specific precision, recall, and F1-scores. Model interpretability was evaluated using Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsBaseline demographic and hematologic parameters differed significantly among diagnostic groups (all P < 0.001). Among the evaluated models, LightGBM demonstrated robust overall performance, achieving one-vs-rest AUCs of 0.920 for AA, 0.970 for ITP, 0.788 for MDS, and 0.870 for other conditions, with an overall accuracy of 0.82. While AA and ITP were identified with favorable precision and recall, MDS showed lower recall, reflecting substantial overlap in routine laboratory features. SHAP analysis identified platelet count, red blood cell count, and white blood cell count as the most influential predictors.ConclusionsA machine learning model based on routinely available CBC parameters can support non-invasive differentiation of common hematologic disorders. This approach may serve as a practical screening and triage tool at the outpatient or pre-bone marrow stage, helping optimize the use of invasive diagnostic procedures.

Introduction

Hematologic disorders such as aplastic anemia (AA), immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) are common causes of cytopenia in clinical practice [1,2]. These conditions differ markedly in pathogenesis, treatment strategies, and prognosis, making accurate and timely diagnosis essential for appropriate patient management [3]. However, their initial clinical presentations often overlap, particularly at early stages, posing challenges for differential diagnosis.

The diagnosis of many hematologic disorders relies on invasive procedures, including bone marrow aspiration and biopsy. Although these methods remain the diagnostic gold standard, they are associated with patient discomfort, procedural risks, and limited accessibility in some clinical settings [4]. In routine practice, complete blood count (CBC) testing is usually the first laboratory evaluation performed in patients with suspected hematologic abnormalities [[5], [6], [7]]. CBC parameters reflect underlying hematopoietic activity and are readily available, inexpensive, and repeatable [8,9]. Nevertheless, the diagnostic value of CBC data alone is often underestimated and underutilized. Previous studies have shown that individual CBC parameters, such as platelet count, hemoglobin level, and mean corpuscular volume, are associated with specific hematologic diseases [10,11]. However, reliance on single laboratory indicators is insufficient for accurate disease differentiation due to substantial overlap between conditions. As a result, clinicians often proceed directly to invasive diagnostic procedures, even in cases where non-invasive data may already contain useful diagnostic signals.

Machine learning techniques are capable of modeling complex, nonlinear relationships among multiple variables and have demonstrated promising performance in disease classification and risk stratification tasks [12,13]. Several studies have explored machine learning approaches for hematologic disease diagnosis, including models based on laboratory data and clinical features [14,15]. However, many existing studies focus on limited disease categories, small sample sizes, or single-center datasets, which may restrict their generalizability. Moreover, the interpretability of machine learning models remains a key concern for clinical adoption.

In this study, we aimed to develop and evaluate a machine learning model based solely on routinely available CBC parameters for the non-invasive differentiation of common hematologic disorders. Using a large real-world dataset, we compared the performance of multiple machine learning models and assessed their diagnostic accuracy using standard classification metrics. In addition, we employed explainable machine learning techniques to identify key laboratory features contributing to model predictions. Our findings may provide evidence for the clinical utility of routine blood count data as a supportive screening tool in the differential diagnosis of hematologic disorders.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective study was conducted using routine CBC data collected from patients who underwent outpatient blood testing between October 2011 and June 2025 at the Institute of Hematology & Blood Diseases Hospital (Tinajin). All laboratory records were obtained from the hospital laboratory information system. Demographic information and laboratory data, including CBC parameters, were collected at the time of initial diagnosis. Blood samples were processed using EDTA-K2 anticoagulated tubes and analyzed on a Sysmex XN9000 automated hematology analyzer. The study was performed in accordance with institutional regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki. As this was a retrospective analysis of de-identified data, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Study population and case selection

A total of 165,181 blood routine test records were initially screened. Samples without definitive diagnostic information were excluded. After data cleaning and matching with diagnostic records, 4056 samples with confirmed diagnoses were identified at the sample level. Samples with missing laboratory features required for model construction were further excluded, resulting in a final dataset of 3826 samples used for machine learning analysis. Patients were categorized into four diagnostic groups based on clinical diagnoses recorded in the medical system: AA, ITP, and MDS, and other hematologic conditions. When multiple blood test results were available for the same patient, each sample was treated independently to reflect real-world clinical testing scenarios.

Feature selection and data preprocessing

Routinely available CBC parameters were selected as candidate features for model development. These included age, sex, white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), platelet count (PLT), neutrophil percentage (NEUT%), monocyte percentage (MONO%), reticulocyte percentage (RET%), and immature granulocyte percentage (IG%). Sex was encoded as a binary variable. Continuous variables were used without transformation. Samples with missing values in any of the selected features were excluded prior to model training. Because routine complete blood count parameters are generally well standardized and missing values were relatively uncommon in the dataset, a complete-case analysis approach was adopted to ensure data quality and avoid potential bias introduced by imputation methods.

Model development and validation

The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and an independent test set (30%) using stratified sampling to preserve the distribution of diagnostic categories. The independent test set was used to evaluate model performance and reduce the risk of overfitting. In addition, only routinely available CBC parameters were included as model features to avoid excessive model complexity. Hyperparameters for the machine learning models were kept at standard settings, which helps prevent overfitting when applied to structured clinical datasets.

Three machine learning models were developed for comparison: logistic regression, random forest, and LightGBM. Logistic regression was used as a baseline model because of its simplicity and interpretability. Random forest and LightGBM are tree-based ensemble learning algorithms that combine multiple decision trees to capture complex nonlinear relationships between variables. LightGBM (Light Gradient Boosting Machine) is a gradient boosting framework that sequentially builds decision trees and optimizes prediction errors during model training. Compared with traditional boosting methods, LightGBM is computationally efficient and performs well on structured clinical datasets.

Model performance evaluation

Diagnostic performance was assessed using one-vs-rest receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). In addition to ROC analysis, several complementary classification metrics were used to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of model performance, including class-specific precision, recall, and F1-scores. Overall accuracy, macro-averaged, and weighted-averaged metrics were also calculated. Furthermore, confusion matrices were used to evaluate the distribution of correct and incorrect classifications across disease categories. These complementary metrics help provide a more detailed assessment of model performance in multiclass classification tasks beyond ROC analysis alone.

Model interpretability

To improve model transparency and interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were applied to the LightGBM model. SHAP is an explainable machine learning approach based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory, which quantifies the contribution of each feature to an individual prediction. By calculating SHAP values, the relative importance and direction of influence of each CBC parameter on model predictions can be visualized, thereby helping clinicians better understand how the model uses laboratory features to differentiate between hematologic diseases.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR). Differences among diagnostic groups were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and model development were performed using Python (version 3.9), with scikit-learn and LightGBM libraries.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that machine learning models built on routinely available CBC parameters can provide clinically meaningful support for the non-invasive differentiation of common hematologic disorders. Using a large real-world dataset, the LightGBM model showed strong discriminative performance for AA and ITP, while highlighting the inherent challenges associated with identifying MDS using peripheral blood data alone.

In routine clinical practice, patients presenting with abnormal CBC results often require further diagnostic workup to distinguish among AA, ITP, MDS, and other benign or reactive conditions [16,17]. Definitive diagnosis, particularly for MDS, commonly relies on bone marrow examination, which is invasive and may not be immediately accessible in outpatient or primary care settings [[18], [19], [20]]. In our study, the present model is designed to function as a triage tool at the outpatient or pre-bone marrow stage, rather than as a definitive diagnostic method. By leveraging data already generated in routine testing, the model may assist clinicians in identifying patients who warrant early hematology referral or prompt bone marrow evaluation, while reducing unnecessary invasive procedures in low-risk cases. To our knowledge, this is among the first large-scale real-world studies using routine CBC parameters and clinically confirmed diagnoses to simultaneously differentiate AA, ITP, and MDS within a single multiclass framework.

Previous studies have investigated CBC-based machine learning approaches for the detection of MDS and related cytopenic disorders, with reported AUC values generally ranging from 0.75 to 0.85 depending on cohort characteristics and model design [21,22]. Although ROC analysis is widely used for evaluating classification models, additional metrics such as precision, recall, and F1-score provide complementary information on class-specific performance, particularly in multiclass clinical classification problems where class imbalance may exist. Consistent with these reports [23,24], our model achieved acceptable overall discrimination for MDS. However, in the present study, LightGBM demonstrated particularly strong performance for AA and ITP, with one-vs-rest AUC values of 0.92 and 0.97, respectively. Our finding is clinically plausible, as AA and ITP are typically associated with more distinct hematologic patterns, such as severe pancytopenia or isolated thrombocytopenia. In contrast, although the model achieved high precision for MDS (0.82), recall remained low (0.14). This pattern likely reflects the marked clinical and biological heterogeneity of MDS, especially in early or low-risk cases, where CBC abnormalities may overlap substantially with those observed in AA or other cytopenic conditions. Similar challenges have been noted in prior CBC-based models [25], underscoring the intrinsic limitations of peripheral blood parameters for the sensitive detection of early-stage MDS.

Model interpretability analysis using SHAP identified platelet count, red blood cell count, and white blood cell count as the most influential predictors [26]. By quantifying the contribution of each feature to model predictions, SHAP provides an interpretable framework that helps clinicians understand how different hematologic parameters influence the diagnostic classification. This interpretability is particularly important for translating machine learning models into real-world clinical decision support. These features align closely with established clinical reasoning, where cytopenia patterns form the basis of initial differential diagnosis [22]. The use of interpretable models may therefore facilitate clinician understanding of model outputs and support integration into real-world diagnostic workflows. The low recall observed for MDS likely reflects the intrinsic heterogeneity of this disease entity and the substantial overlap in peripheral blood features between early or low-risk MDS and other cytopenic conditions such as AA or reactive cytopenias. This finding is clinically plausible and consistent with prior reports, and it highlights the inherent limitation of relying solely on routine CBC parameters for sensitive detection of early-stage MDS. Importantly, our model is therefore not intended as a rule-out tool for MDS, but rather as a triage support system to prioritize further diagnostic workup in patients with atypical or high-risk laboratory patterns.

From a clinical perspective, it is important to emphasize that the present model is not intended to replace bone marrow examination or other definitive diagnostic procedures [27,28]. Rather, it may serve as a screening or decision-support tool that highlights abnormal hematologic patterns using routinely available data. Once the model is developed, predictions can be generated almost instantly using routinely available CBC data, and the computational time for each prediction is minimal. Because the model relies solely on laboratory parameters that are already generated in routine clinical testing, no additional laboratory assays or costs are required. Therefore, this approach may help optimize the use of invasive diagnostic procedures and potentially reduce unnecessary healthcare expenditures. This approach may be particularly valuable in primary care or resource-limited settings, where access to specialized testing is limited. By evaluating multiple common hematologic disorders simultaneously in a large real-world cohort, the present study reflects routine clinical scenarios more closely than disease-specific screening models. In machine learning studies using structured clinical data, the number of training samples should generally exceed the number of input variables to ensure stable model performance. In the present study, 14 CBC-related variables were included, while the final dataset contained 3826 samples, indicating that the available sample size was adequate for model development.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design may limit generalizability. Future studies involving multi-center cohorts and external validation in independent populations will be necessary to confirm the robustness and generalizability of the model. In addition, incorporating data from different healthcare settings and laboratory platforms may further improve the applicability of the model in real-world clinical practice. Second, only routine CBC parameters were included; incorporation of additional clinical variables, advanced cell population data, or molecular markers may improve discrimination, particularly for MDS. Third, model calibration was not assessed and should be addressed in future studies prior to clinical implementation. To reduce the risk of overfitting, the dataset was divided into independent training and test sets, and model performance was evaluated only on the test dataset. Nevertheless, external validation in independent cohorts will be necessary to further confirm model robustness. In addition, samples with missing laboratory parameters were excluded during preprocessing. Although this approach helps ensure data completeness for model training, it may introduce potential selection bias.

Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths, including the use of a large real-world dataset with clinically assigned diagnoses and reliance on widely available laboratory parameters. Importantly, the combination of machine learning with model interpretability enhances clinical relevance and transparency.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that machine learning models based on routinely available complete blood count parameters can support the non-invasive differentiation of common hematologic disorders in real-world clinical settings. The proposed approach showed robust performance for aplastic anemia and immune thrombocytopenia, while highlighting the inherent challenges of identifying myelodysplastic syndromes using routine laboratory data alone. By combining large-scale real-world data with interpretable machine learning techniques, our work underscores the diagnostic value of standard blood count parameters beyond their conventional use. Although further external validation and integration of additional clinical information are warranted, these findings suggest that routine blood tests may serve as a practical screening tool to assist clinical decision-making and optimize the use of invasive diagnostic procedures.
