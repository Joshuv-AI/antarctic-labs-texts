Characterization of JIA subtypes, clinical features, treatment patterns, and early outcomes in Palestinian children: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Fawzy M Abunejma, Wasef Alhroub, Ahmad Fasfoos, Hala Imwas, Mina Hassan, Mohammad Ghawadreh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJuvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is the most common chronic rheumatologic disease of childhood and includes a heterogeneous group of subtypes with varying clinical presentations, laboratory profiles, and outcomes. Data from the Middle East, including Palestine, remain limited. This study aimed to compare clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, and short-term outcomes between oligoarticular and non-oligoarticular JIA and to identify factors associated with disease subtype and six-month remission or relapse.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at pediatric rheumatology centers in Hebron, Palestine, including children diagnosed with JIA between January 2019 and August 2025. ILAR criteria were used for subtype classification. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and treatment data were extracted from medical records. The primary outcome was remission or relapse at six months based on Wallace criteria. Statistical analyses included group comparisons and multivariable logistic regression to identify variables associated with subtype and relapse.ResultsA total of 171 children were included. Persistent oligoarticular JIA was the most common subtype (38.0%), followed by polyarticular RF-negative (PRF-) (22.8%) and enthesitis-related arthritis (ERA) (14.6%). Systemic JIA patients tended to have a younger age at onset and demonstrated a higher inflammatory burden. Knee arthritis was the most frequent joint involvement (63.7%), and extra-articular manifestations, particularly oral ulcers, rash, and uveitis, differed across subtypes. NSAIDs were used in 62.0% of patients, methotrexate in 79.5%, and biologics in 22.2%. After six months, 53.8% achieved remission while 46.2% relapsed, with systemic JIA and PRF− showing the highest relapse rates. Multivariable analysis showed that younger age, knee involvement, lower ESR, and six-month remission were independently associated with the oligoarticular subtype. Predictors of relapse included PRF− subtype, systemic JIA, and family history of rheumatologic disease.ConclusionThis study provides the first detailed characterization of JIA in Palestinian children, highlighting clinically meaningful differences between subtypes and identifying factors associated with remission and relapse. Systemic and PRF- JIA were linked to higher inflammatory activity and poorer short-term outcomes. These findings may support improved risk stratification and therapeutic planning and demonstrate the importance of long-term prospective studies in the region.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Juvenile idiopathic arthritis, JIA, Oligoarticular, Systemic JIA, Polyarticular, Remission, Relapse

Introduction

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is a chronic inflammatory joint disease of unknown cause that begins before the age of 16 years. Its reported incidence ranges from approximately 1.6 to 23 cases per 100,000 children, while prevalence estimates vary widely, from about 3.8 to 400 cases per 100,000 children [1]. JIA may be associated with a range of complications, including growth retardation, osteoporosis secondary to treatment or disease activity, and life-threatening macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) with multi-organ involvement [2]. Furthermore, establishing a diagnosis requires careful exclusion of alternative causes of pediatric arthritis, given the presence of several potential mimickers [3].

JIA encompasses a heterogeneous group of disorders that share the common feature of arthritis. Each subtype differs in terms of presentation, progression, and outcome. The ILAR classification criteria divide JIA into seven subtypes. Oligoarticular JIA, the most common subtype, is further categorized into persistent and extended forms based on joint involvement after six months; polyarticular RF-positive JIA (PRF+); polyarticular RF-negative JIA (PRF-); juvenile psoriatic arthritis (JPsA); enthesitis-related arthritis (ERA); systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis; and undifferentiated arthritis [4].

Epidemiologic reports show wide variation in JIA subtypes rates across different regions. In Jordan, a single-center analysis of 210 patients found oligoarticular JIA to be the most common subtype (57.7%) with uveitis occurring in 14.2% of cases [5]. Additionally, a study from Saudi Arabia identified systemic JIA as the most common subtype (36.5%),in contrast to the patterns observed in Oman and Jordan, and reported uveitis in 8.5% of patients [6]. Other studies conducted in Lebanon and within the Pediatric Rheumatology Arab Group (PRAG) multicenter cohort, reported a diverse JIA subtype pattern, with oligoarticular and PRF- frequently reported, systemic JIA less common in several populations, variable ANA positivity, and generally low rates of uveitis. The PRAG study further documented patterns of cumulative disease damage and long-term outcomes across Arab countries [7, 8]. These findings highlight important regional differences in subtype distribution and burden, underscoring the need for epidemiologic data in other under-studied populations such as in Palestine.

The management of JIA has increasingly shifted toward early, aggressive treatment strategies to induce rapid remission and reduce the risk of long-term joint damage. Evidence from early-intervention trials demonstrates that initiating methotrexate and biologic therapy early can achieve clinically inactive disease within the first six months of treatment [9]. Nonetheless, sustaining remission remains difficult. A randomized clinical trial demonstrated high flare rates when methotrexate was withdrawn at 6 months, suggesting that initial remission often requires continued therapy [10]. Additional observational data suggest that the sequence in which medications are tapered has a meaningful impact on flare-free survival, with different outcomes depending on whether methotrexate or biologics are discontinued first [11].

This study aimed to compare the clinical features, laboratory findings, and treatment outcomes between oligoarticular and non-oligoarticular subtypes of JIA in Palestinian children, and to identify factors associated with disease subtype and six-month remission or relapse.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This research was conducted as a retrospective cohort study in Hebron, Palestine. Medical records from pediatric rheumatology services, including Palestine Red Crescent Society (PRCS) Hospital and affiliated outpatient clinics, were reviewed to identify all children diagnosed with JIA. The study covered the period from January 2019 to August 2025, depending on the availability and completeness of clinical documentation. The study aimed to compare clinical characteristics and treatment outcomes between oligoarticular JIA and non-oligoarticular JIA subtypes. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethical Committee of Hebron University, and permission to access patient medical records was granted by PRCS Hospital. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. As this is a retrospective analysis with anonymized patient and family data, informed consent was not required.

Study population

A total of 171 patients diagnosed with JIA were included in this study, all meeting the International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) classification criteria [12]. The date of diagnosis was defined as the first recorded visit in which a pediatric rheumatologist assigned a JIA diagnosis using the appropriate ICD-10 code. Each diagnosis was subsequently categorized into its specific JIA subtype based on the ILAR criteria. Throughout the study period, diagnostic classifications were reviewed and updated when necessary to ensure accuracy. Only patients younger than 16 years at the time of diagnosis and who had a confirmed ILAR-based JIA classification were included in the analysis.

Eligible participants were younger than 16 years at diagnosis and had a confirmed clinical and laboratory assessment consistent with ILAR categories. Patients were required to have at least six months of follow-up to allow proper outcome evaluation. Individuals were excluded if they were older than 16 years of age at diagnosis, had incomplete baseline data, had alternative rheumatologic diagnoses, or were lost to follow-up prior to the six-month assessment. Patients were categorized into two main groups: those with oligoarticular JIA (persistent or extended) and those with non-oligoarticular JIA, which included systemic JIA, PRF + and PRF- subtypes, ERA, JPsA, and undifferentiated JIA, reflecting cases that did not meet criteria for a single ILAR category.

Data collection

Data were extracted retrospectively from both electronic and paper-based medical records using a standardized and structured data collection form. Information obtained included demographic characteristics such as age at diagnosis and sex, along with clinical variables including age at symptom onset, number and distribution of affected joints, and extra-articular features such as uveitis, rash, oral ulcers, abdominal pain, and family history of rheumatologic diseases. Laboratory data collected at baseline included complete blood count (CBC), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), ANA, RF, anti-CCP antibodies, and HLA-B27 status when available. Treatment-related variables included the use of NSAIDs, oral or intravenous corticosteroids, intra-articular steroid injections, conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (cDMARDs) (primarily methotrexate), and biologic therapies such as anti-TNF and anti-IL-6 agents.

In patients who otherwise fulfilled the clinical features of polyarticular JIA, a single positive RF test was accepted as part of the inclusion criteria, as local practice involves performing RF testing only once when polyarticular involvement is suspected. However, a solitary positive RF result did not exclude patients whose overall presentation was consistent with an oligoarticular pattern.

Outcomes were evaluated using the Wallace criteria for clinically inactive disease, which consider the following: absence of active arthritis in any joint, no systemic features such as fever, rash, serositis, splenomegaly, or lymphadenopathy attributable to arthritis, no active uveitis, normal ESR and/or CRP values (if both tests were performed), and the lowest possible score on the physician’s global assessment of disease activity. Clinical remission while on medication was defined as maintaining inactive disease for six consecutive months [13, 14]. In the context of this retrospective study, remission reflects short-term clinical inactivity rather than sustained or long-term disease control.

The primary outcome measured was disease status after six months of treatment, classified as either remission or relapse according to clinical evaluations and laboratory markers of inflammation, reflecting short-term treatment response.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25). Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges depending on distribution, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between the oligoarticular and non-oligoarticular groups were assessed using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. To identify independent predictors of oligoarticular JIA as well as predictors of relapse at six months, two multivariable logistic regression models were constructed. Variables with a p-value less than 0.1 in univariable analysis were included in the multivariable models. Results were reported using adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and statistical significance was defined as a p-value below 0.05.

Results

In our cohort of 171 patients, persistent oligoarticular was the most common subtype, accounting for 38.0% of cases. This was followed by PRF- (22.8%) and ERA (14.6%). Systemic JIA represented 9.9% of patients, while PRF + was less frequent (5.3%). Undifferentiated JIA was observed in 10 patients (5.8%). Extended oligoarticular was rare, occurring in 5 patients (2.9%), and juvenile psoriatic arthritis was very uncommon, affecting only 1 patient (0.6%) (Fig. 1).

Joint distribution patterns varied across the different groups. Knee arthritis was the most common overall (63.7%), affecting almost all extended oligoarticular JIA and JPsA patients and most persistent oligoarticular and PRF- cases. Ankle arthritis was frequently observed in extended oligoarticular, PRF-, systemic JIA, and ERA (about two-thirds in each), but hip involvement was more prevalent in systemic and extended oligoarticular. The small joints in the hands and feet were usually affected in the polyarticular forms and were also noted in other types. TMJ involvement was uncommon overall (5.8%) but were observed more often in extended oligoarticular and PRF-.

Extra-articular manifestations showed variability. Approximately one-third of patients demonstrated abnormal imaging findings, predominantly among those with undifferentiated JIA, PRF-, and oligoarticular JIA. Oral ulcers were more frequently seen in ERA and PRF-, and skin rashes were more commonly observed in systemic JIA (64.7%) and were also noted in the single JPsA case. Uveitis happened in 5.3% of the group, mainly in persistent oligoarticular, PRF-, and ERA. Abdominal pain and a family history of rheumatologic disease were reported more frequently in ERA and PRF- than in other groups. In general, the table shows clear patterns of clinical and lab differences between the ILAR categories in this JIA group. Three patients had concomitant familial Mediterranean fever (FMF), and all of them met the criteria for ERA.

Table 1B showed the lab results for the JIA patients. Blood test results and inflammation levels also appeared to differ among the groups. Systemic JIA was characterized by more pronounced anemia and inflammation, with the lowest hemoglobin (10.2 g/dL) and the highest ESR (64.8 mm/h) and CRP (64.6 mg/L), plus markedly elevated platelet counts (370.9 × 10⁹/L). On the other hand, ERA patients had the highest hemoglobin (12.9 g/dL) and lower inflammation levels. Platelet counts were usually normal to high across all groups, but the single JPsA patient was observed to have very high platelets. The serologic profiles showed that RF + was found in a few undifferentiated cases (6.4%). ANA was most often positive in systemic JIA (52.9%), then PRF+ (22.2%), and less so in other groups. HLA-B27 and anti-CCP testing was not performed in all patients, so the results show number positive/number tested. HLA-B27 was more frequently detected in ERA (3/8, 37.5%) but also in some persistent oligoarticular, PRF-, and undifferentiated JIA. Anti-CCP antibodies were mostly in PRF+ patients (4/6, 66.7%) and sometimes in persistent oligoarticular JIA and ERA.

Table 2 showed that 62.0% of the patients were given NSAIDs. This was more frequently observed in those with ERA (72.0%), undifferentiated JIA (70.0%), and persistent oligoarticular (66.2%), but was less frequently observed in those with PRF+ (only 22.2%). Oral corticosteroids were given to 18.7% of the patients, mostly those with systemic JIA (64.7%) and PRF- (30.8%). Getting IV steroids was rare (only 2 out of 171, and both had systemic JIA). Steroid injections into the joint were mainly for persistent oligoarticular (20.0%), for a total of 9.4% of all patients. Most patients (82.5%) received cDMARDs, with methotrexate being the primary therapeutic agent (79.5%). It was frequently used in extended oligoarticular, PRF+, and JPsA (all at or above 88.9%). A smaller group of patients (22.2%) were prescribed biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs), mostly those with ERA (44.0%), PRF- (35.9%), and systemic JIA (35.3%). Anti-TNF therapy was used in 18.1% of patients, frequently those with ERA (44.0%) and PRF- (30.8%). Anti-IL-6 agents were given to 4.1%, mainly for systemic JIA (23.5%). A mix of anti-TNF plus methotrexate was observed in 12.9% of the patients, especially for PRF-) and ERA (20.0%).

After six months of follow-up, 53.8% (92/171) of patients met criteria for remission, and 46.2% (79/171) had active disease. Remission was most frequent in persistent oligoarticular (69.2% vs. 30.8% active disease), extended oligoarticular, and undifferentiated (each 60% remission, 40% active disease). In ERA, remission and active disease were observed at similar proportions (52.0% vs. 48.0%). In contrast, systemic JIA showed a lower frequency of remission, with only 23.5% in remission and 76.5% with active disease. PRF- also showed a higher frequency of active disease (38.5% remission vs. 61.5% active disease). The single JPsA case achieved remission (Fig. 2).

Clinical status after 6 months across JIA subtypes

In multivariable analysis, younger age, knee involvement, lower ESR, and remission at 6 months were independently associated with having oligoarticular JIA. Each additional year of age was associated with decreased odds of oligoarthritis by about 10% (OR 0.899; 95% CI 0.82–0.985; p = 0.022). The presence of knee arthritis was associated with approximately tripled odds of oligoarthritis (OR 3.108; 1.373–7.032; p = 0.006). Higher ESR was correlated with slightly reduced odds (OR 0.971 per mm/h; 0.952–0.991; p = 0.004). Being in remission after 6 months was associated with an oligoarticular subtype (OR 2.813; 1.298–6.099; p = 0.009). Sex, other joint sites, CRP, skin rash, uveitis, and family history were not statistically significant (Table 3).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive characterization of JIA subtypes, clinical features, and outcomes in a Palestinian pediatric population, filling a critical data gap for an underrepresented region in global JIA epidemiology. While the subtype distribution in our cohort (38% oligoarticular) matches regional patterns in the Middle East and Africa, long-term evidence suggests these classifications are often a “moving target”. Data from the Nordic JIA cohort indicates that applying ILAR criteria years after diagnosis results in the reclassification of 10–30% of patients. Changes are frequently driven by updated information regarding heredity, such as a family history of psoriasis, which can shift a patient from the oligoarticular or undifferentiated category to psoriatic arthritis. While categories like systemic JIA and PRF+ tend to remain more stable over time, the shifting nature of other subtypes underscores the need for continuous reassessment rather than relying solely on a baseline diagnosis [15].

Systemic JIA represented 9.9% of our cohort and was associated with the highest inflammatory burden and the poorest six-month remission rate (23.5%). This outcome reflects the historically aggressive nature of systemic JIA, which in the pre-biologic era often led to “burned-out” disease characterized by life-long disability, stunted growth, and multiple surgeries. However, long-term studies highlight a dramatic shift in management over the last two decades. The introduction of targeted biologic therapies, such as IL-1 and IL-6 inhibitors, has revolutionized the prognosis for systemic JIA. In our Palestinian cohort, the relatively modest biologic exposure (22.2%) compared to modern Western registries may explain why remission remains elusive for many systemic JIA patients. Transitioning to a “treat-to-target” approach with earlier biologic initiation could potentially align our local outcomes with the improved trajectories seen in modern long-term cohorts [16].

The large-joint predominance observed, particularly the frequency of knee and ankle arthritis, aligns with international findings that certain joints are prone to persistent inflammation. In contrast, hip and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) involvement in our study was associated with more aggressive disease courses and a lower likelihood of early remission. These clinical features serve as early markers for high-risk patients who may require a more rapid escalation to systemic DMARDs or biologics to prevent permanent joint damage, a concern that remains relevant even 18 years post-onset as long-term remission off-medication is only achieved by approximately 33% of patients in advanced cohorts [16].

Extra-articular features in our cohort showed subtype-specific trends similar to previous reports. Skin rash was most commonly observed in systemic JIA, aligning with the classical systemic manifestations (fever, rash) described in systemic-onset patients [5]. Uveitis, although relatively infrequent (5.3%), is clustered among persistent oligoarticular, PRF-, and ERA subtypes, which is consistent with the higher risk of uveitis in early-onset, ANA-positive, oligoarticular disease [17]. Oral ulcers and abdominal pain were more frequent in ERA and PRF- patients, which aligns with literature noting higher rates of mucocutaneous and systemic complaints in ERA and polyarticular phenotypes, often with positive family history, suggesting a potential genetic predisposition [5]. These features were included descriptively as markers of systemic or mucocutaneous involvement rather than as defining characteristics of JIA subtypes. Overall, these results highlight the importance of subtype-specific extra-articular surveillance to inform treatment decisions and reduce the risk of long-term complications. The lower uveitis rate in our cohort may reflect variations in referrals, screening, or disease distribution. Nonetheless, any occurrence of uveitis underscores the importance of regular ophthalmologic monitoring, particularly in high-risk subtypes. These results highlight the importance of subtype-specific extra-articular surveillance to inform treatment decisions and reduce the risk of long-term complications.

One finding that warrants particular consideration is the relatively higher proportion of females with ERA in our cohort, which contrasts with the well-documented male predominance reported in the literature. Most published cohorts consistently show ERA to be more common in males; for instance, a Taiwanese pediatric JIA study reported that 86% of ERA patients were male (male-to-female ratio ≈ 6:1) [18], and broader reviews report male to female ratios of approximately 1.4:1 to 9:1 across different populations [19]. This may be due to the small sample size, potential referral or classification biases, or regional genetic and environmental factors. These findings warrant cautious interpretation and highlight the need for larger, multicenter studies in underrepresented populations.

Methotrexate (MTX) remains the cornerstone of therapy in our cohort, being used in 79.5% of patients. Although clinical practice often emphasizes the strategy of MTX withdrawal, available evidence indicates that the risk of disease flare is more strongly influenced by patient-related factors than by the discontinuation method itself. In a study involving 107 patients with JIA who achieved inactive disease on MTX monotherapy, disease flares occurred in 55% of cases following MTX discontinuation. Multivariate analysis identified younger age at symptom onset as an independent predictor of flare, with a more than twofold increased risk (OR = 2.2). Additionally, ANA positivity was associated with a doubled risk of flare compared to ANA-negative patients. Furthermore, higher baseline inflammatory activity, reflected by elevated ESR and CRP levels at the initiation of MTX therapy, was significantly correlated with an increased likelihood of future disease flares [20].

The predictors identified in our cohort align well with published literature, reinforcing their validity. The association of younger age at onset, knee involvement, and lower baseline ESR with oligoarticular classification reflects observations from Nordic and international cohorts suggesting that early-onset, low-inflammatory, large-joint disease tends to remain limited [17]. The strong link between early remission (at 6 months) and the oligoarticular subtype is consistent with evidence that early disease control is associated with better long-term outcomes. For relapse, the higher risk in PRF- and systemic JIA, and the association with family history, are in agreement with studies showing subtype and genetic predisposition are important correlates of chronicity and flare risk [21]. These predictors may help clinicians stratify patients at diagnosis into risk categories, potentially guiding early, more aggressive therapy and more intensive monitoring in high-risk groups, while allowing cautious tapering in lower-risk oligoarticular cases.

Systemic JIA showed the most severe inflammatory profile, with anemia, high ESR and CRP, and reactive thrombocytosis. The high prevalence of anemia is consistent with previous cohorts [22], and appears mediated by hepcidin-25-induced iron restriction [23], with hemoglobin inversely correlated with ESR [24]. Inflammatory markers also correlated with the systemic immune-inflammation index, a predictor of disease severity [25].

Serologic markers showed subtype-specific patterns in our cohort. ANA was most frequent in systemic JIA and PRF+, linked to disease activity and uveitis risk [26]. RF and anti-CCP were mainly seen in PRF+, associated with more aggressive and erosive disease [27, 28]. HLA-B27 was most common in ERA, correlating with higher disease activity and prolonged treatment [29, 30].

This study provides the first comprehensive characterization of JIA subtypes, clinical features, treatment patterns, and six-month outcomes in a Palestinian pediatric population. As such, it fills a critical data gap for an underrepresented region in global JIA epidemiology. The findings, including subtype distribution, joint involvement patterns, extra-articular manifestations, and predictors of remission/relapse, offer valuable insights for clinicians in Palestine and similar settings. Importantly, by linking clinical features (e.g., subtype, joint involvement, lab markers) with short-term outcomes, our results may help guide early risk stratification, therapeutic decision-making, and monitoring strategies tailored to local patient profiles.

Future research should include prospective, multicenter studies with extended follow-up (into adolescence and adulthood) to assess long-term outcomes such as growth, joint damage, functional status, quality of life, and extra-articular comorbidities. Incorporating standardized and comprehensive outcome measures, including validated composite disease activity and damage indices and patient/parent-reported outcome measures (e.g., functional ability, health-related quality of life), would allow better comparability with international cohorts. Given the limited use of biologic therapies in our cohort compared with many Western centers, future studies might explore barriers to biologic access, adherence patterns, and long-term safety/efficacy in our population. Additionally, given the role of genetics and environment in JIA pathogenesis, regional research could investigate genetic, epigenetic, and environmental risk factors specific to Palestine and MENA populations to explain subtype distributions and prognostic differences. In light of data showing growth impairment and reduced adult height in some JIA patients, future longitudinal studies should monitor growth and bone health over time.

Limitations

Our study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective study design introduces the potential for bias. In addition, health system–related factors in Palestine—including variability in access to pediatric rheumatology services, referral pathways, and availability of biologic therapies—may have influenced both the observed distribution of rheumatological subtypes and treatment outcomes. These factors could contribute to delayed diagnosis, differences in disease severity at presentation, and more conservative treatment escalation, particularly in severe subtypes. As a result, the observed subtype distribution and remission rates may not fully reflect those of broader pediatric JIA populations, especially in settings with earlier referral and wider biologic access. Second, while 171 patients is a relatively large sample for a single-center pediatric rheumatology service in Palestine, some subgroups (e.g., psoriatic JIA, extended oligoarticular) remain small, which limits the statistical power to detect associations or less common outcomes and limits generalizability. Third, follow-up was limited to six months for the primary outcome (remission vs. relapse), which may not capture long-term disease course, late flares, structural damage, growth impairment, or extra-articular complications that often emerge over years. In addition, HLA-B27 and anti-CCP testing was not performed uniformly across all groups. Finally, the absence of some advanced outcome measures (e.g., a composite disease activity index with parent/patient global assessment, imaging-based joint damage scoring, and standardized functional/quality-of-life assessments) limits comparison with other prospective cohorts or the evaluation of long-term functional outcomes.

Conclusion

Our retrospective cohort highlights persistent oligoarticular disease as the most common subtype, with systemic and polyarticular RF-negative variants being associated with higher inflammatory burden and worse short-term outcomes. Joint involvement, extra-articular manifestations, and serologic markers varied by subtype and were associated with remission and relapse risk. Although nearly half of the cohort relapsed within six months, the identified predictors (younger age at onset, knee involvement, lower ESR, subtype, and family history) may help stratify patients for treatment intensity and follow-up frequency. This study represents a significant contribution to regional JIA data and underscores the need for prospective, long-term research to better define disease course, optimize management, and improve outcomes in Palestinian and MENA children with JIA.
