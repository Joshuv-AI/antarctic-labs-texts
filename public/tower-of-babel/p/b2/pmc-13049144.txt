Turning precision medicine into reality with statistics and data-driven decision making across pharmaceutical development
Authors: Guillaume Desachy, Karl Köchert, Nicole Krämer, René Kubiak, Biomarkers European Special Interest Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ten years after the launch of the Precision Medicine Initiative, its broader impact remains uncertain beyond select genetic and oncological cases. Here, we share our perspective as pharmaceutical statisticians and propose strategic shifts to realize its full potential.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Drug development, Clinical trial design, Biomarkers

Introduction

Precision medicine has emerged as a transformative approach in drug development, offering the promise of tailored therapies that address the unique needs of specific patient populations. While the concept is compelling, the practical implementation of precision medicine remains challenging.

Hence, we need to ask a rather provocative question: Has Precision Medicine lived up to its expectations—or does it only work in “clear-cut” cases, such as rare genetic disorders and specific oncological conditions with single causal somatic DNA alterations?

Here, we share our industry perspective as statisticians and data scientists working in precision medicine. Jointly speaking, the authors of this article have been working in biomarker-driven drug development for the last decade and longer. More recently, starting around 2022, we have started to systematically exchange on this very matter across companies and academic institutions, foremost within the European Special Interest Group for Biomarkers, supported by Statisticians in the Pharmaceutical Industry (PSI) and the European Federation of Statisticians in the Pharmaceutical Industry (EFSPI).

In this group, one theme came up repeatedly. Precision medicine methods and trial designs were rarely applied. Transitions from all-comers to biomarker-defined subgroups with better safety and efficacy were uncommon in drug development. That this was a shared observation came as a surprise to us, individually thinking that specific circumstances and chance had prevented successful precision medicine programs only in our own institutions. Hence, we decided to corroborate or falsify this notion by reviewing recent New Drug Approvals (NDAs) from the FDA that were labeled as precision medicine drugs by the Personalized Medicine Coalition in their Annual Reports 20201 and 20222. In brief, taking these two annual reports as samples, the corresponding FDA label was retrieved for each of the listed NDAs3. Subsequently, we read and analyzed the publications reporting the results for the trials listed in the respective FDA label to i) develop a general understanding of the clinical development programs and ii) focus specifically on the implementation of biomarker and precision medicine aspects.

In addition, we screened a recent sample from available Complete Response Letters4 by the FDA for compounds that were developed with a specific biomarker strategy but ultimately were not approved. We complemented this by discussing published examples that had been developed at our own companies. Whilst this was not a formal literature review, we think our initial notion was clearly substantiated. Henceforth, we outline what we believe must be done to turn the promise of precision medicine into reality—even beyond the “clear-cut” cases, where there is a very complex underlying disease biology. Our main motivation is our firm belief in the promise of precision medicine and that, eventually, we will be able to make it a reality for all types of diseases.

With this comment, we want to trigger much needed discussions of viable precision medicine strategies in drug development and to provide a clear path forward grounded in collaborative, data-driven solutions.

Why and how we conducted the review

The review was initiated as part of a pre-competitive, collaborative effort among biomarker statisticians and data scientists from leading pharmaceutical companies (Bayer, Boehringer Ingelheim, Pierre Fabre and Sanofi). Choosing from all FDA-approved NDAs that were labeled as “precision medicine” compounds by the Precision Medicine Coalition yearly progress reports in 2020 and 2022, we scrutinized the associated FDA labels and development trial publications of over 30 recent NDAs. These were selected to representatively encompass diverse modalities such as gene therapies, mutation-targeted therapies, exon skipping techniques, and treatments for ultra-rare hereditary genetic disorders. This was complemented by screening development trial publications of recent NDAs (March to August 2023) that were not approved by the FDA according to FDA’s Complete Response Letters and which had a precision medicine strategy built into their development program.

Our objective was to identify common success factors, challenges, and opportunities for improvement in the clinical development of precision medicine therapies.

Key findings: success in “clear-cut” cases

One of the most striking findings from the review was that most successful precision medicine NDAs involved “clear-cut” cases. These programs shared several common characteristics.

Predefined Biomarkers: Essentially, all successful precision medicine drug development programs started with a well-defined biomarker, being the drug target or a genetic alteration that was directly linked to the drug’s mechanism of action. For example, therapies targeting folate receptor α (FRα) overexpression5, B-cell associated auto-AQP4-IgG antibodies6 or KRAS G12C mutations7 benefited from a clear understanding of the biomarker’s—likely causal—role in disease pathology.

Limited Exploratory Biomarker Development: Virtually none of the programs involved exploratory biomarker discovery or complex signature detection that ultimately impacted the drug development program to change from “all-comers” to a “precision-population”. Instead, in ultimately successful programs the biomarker was often predefined and used primarily for patient selection and optimization of trial inclusion and exclusion criteria. However, whilst a biomarker-based, approved NDA certainly is proof of the biomarker-efficacy relationship, this does not rule out that a broad biomarker discovery approach could have identified even more specific patient populations with far better benefit/risk-ratios.

Therapeutic Focus: Most successful NDAs were in the fields of oncology or rare diseases, where the biology is often well-understood, and patient populations are more homogenous.

These findings suggest that precision medicine has been most successful in cases where the path was already well-defined. However, this raises an important question: How can we make precision medicine a reality in indications with even more complex underlying biology and heterogeneity of the patient population, such as psychiatry8?

Is precision medicine a fluke?

Our answer is a resounding no. While the reviewed NDAs highlight the importance of starting with a well-defined biomarker, they also reveal significant opportunities to expand the scope of precision medicine. To achieve this, we must adopt better strategies. Much of the current discourse focuses on research, translational medicine or biomarkers/technology to address the inherent complexities of precision medicine.

Our comment highlights the critical, and often underappreciated, role of statistical thinking and data-driven decision making in realizing these advances at scale. The proposed strategies focus on the clinical drug development process in the pharmaceutical industry, up to market approval. With necessary adaptations, these strategies are also applicable to post-approval research, including academic-sponsored trials.

The role of statistics and data-driven decision making in precision medicine

Statistics and decision making are essential for enabling the broader success of precision medicine. We have identified the following strategies and factors as particularly impactful:

Apply enrichment strategies consistently, starting in early phases

Enrichment designs, which focus on recruiting patients most likely to benefit from the therapy, can improve trial efficiency and increase the likelihood of success. For example, targeting high-biomarker expressors in early-phase studies can de-risk later phases. A common challenge is that in early phases, there is limited knowledge on optimal biomarker cut-offs. This can be overcome with trial designs that adapt the biomarker cut-off and hence the degree of enrichment during the trial conduct9.

Invest in robust biomarker subgroup identification and cut-off determination

Identifying biomarker-based subgroups and determining biomarker cut-offs are critical. We acknowledge that this is a challenging task, especially when patient numbers are low. We still recommend starting early by identifying potentially predictive biomarkers (see e.g.10.) and optimizing cut-offs once more information is available. Bayesian methods and adaptive trial designs can help optimize cut-offs and guide decision-making.

Use more adaptive trial designs

Adaptive designs, in early-phase as well as in late-phase trials, allow for mid-trial modifications based on data accumulated, enabling more flexible and efficient exploration of biomarker-defined subgroups. This is particularly relevant if the subgroup is identified in a data-driven way during the trial, potentially using machine learning or other advanced methods. Solutions to seamlessly include data-driven subgroup identification into the trial design exist11 and we need to adopt them systematically. Conducting a systematic search of patient subgroups that is embedded in the clinical trial design will empower key decision-makers within an organization to choose between pursuing a precision medicine development approach or an all-comer development strategy.

Integrate biomarker strategy into program strategy

We have lost count of how often we have heard the statement that biomarker analyses are solely exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Without a clear goal on how the biomarkers impact the overall strategy, precision medicine is likely to fail. Biomarker statisticians should be involved from the outset to ensure that biomarker strategies are seamlessly integrated into the overall development plan. This includes providing risk assessments of complete program strategies for the entire program to evaluate trade-offs and risks. We recommend assessing different strategies for enrichment designs, subgroup identification and adaptive designs. Tools like probability of success12 and extensive simulations add substantial value in this context.

Integrate scientific and commercial considerations

Organizations should integrate precision medicine approaches from the earliest stages of drug development, combining both scientific and commercial considerations. This includes identifying disease endotypes through molecular characterization to enable precise patient stratification and inform adequately powered sub-population studies. As emphasized by Jenkins et al.13, biomarker studies require prospective planning with meaningful effect size specifications, even in exploratory phases, while gathering prior knowledge on variability sources and maintaining high data quality standards. Following established statistical frameworks, teams should ensure robust methodologies support their precision medicine strategies throughout the development continuum, enabling more targeted therapy development while optimizing resource allocation and enhancing clinical and commercial success probability.

Leverage large datasets using AI/ML

While exploratory biomarker discovery was rarely presented in the reviewed NDAs, large datasets (e.g., OMICS) collected in phase III/IV or sourced from external databases can be used for back-translation and identifying new biomarkers for future programs. Given the complexity and dimensionality of such data sets and the underlying disease biology, best practices and standardized application of Artificial Intelligence / Machine Learning (AI/ML) approaches should be employed to derive robust and actionable insights (see, e.g.14,15).

What are the barriers keeping us from implementing these changes?

Precision medicine, despite its tremendous promise, requires additional investment to overcome challenges where critical decisions may depend on limited data or biomarkers that ultimately prove inadequate for their intended purpose. In fact, there is a broad range of potential hurdles that, if not accounted for by adequate contingency plans, may lead to late-stage regulatory rejection—a very costly failure for both patients in need for new treatments and sponsors.

Common barriers include the lack of experience and confidence with innovative approaches, a reluctance to pre-specify biomarker strategies and a pressure for speed that can discourage upfront investment. Adaptive designs are often perceived as too complex from both an operational as well as a design perspective while for AI/ML, limited access to data and the evolving regulatory landscape can make integration challenging. These barriers can create an environment where new ideas remain theoretical rather than operational.

We are confident that these risks can be managed. With proactive planning, quantitative decision-making, and a stronger focus on early stages of drug development coupled with tight cross-functional alignment, precision medicine can deliver on its potential without becoming a gamble.

Conclusion

Precision medicine is not a fluke, and its broader success requires a shift in how drug development is approached. We strongly advocate that precision medicine becomes the default approach across all development stages. By leveraging the power of statistics and data-driven decision making, smarter trials can be designed, biomarker strategies can be optimized early in research and development, and henceforth development programs can be de-risked. This implies that more resources should be allocated to translational medicine activities and early-phase studies, including larger patient cohorts and more strategic biomarker assessments. We believe that this initial investment will substantially reduce the risk of late-phase failures.

These tools are essential for moving beyond “clear-cut” cases and unlocking the full potential of precision medicine to deliver transformative therapies to patients in all indications.
