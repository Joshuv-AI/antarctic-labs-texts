Hmox1 expression indicates distinct injury phenotypes of proximal tubule cells in sepsis-associated acute kidney injury
Authors: Yifei He, Weiru Zhang, Zhilan Li, Chenxi Liu, Yi Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SAKI) is the most common organ dysfunction in sepsis and a significant contributor to high mortality rates. Despite its clinical importance, the underlying pathogenesis of SAKI remains unclear, and effective therapies are currently lacking. This study aims to identify specific biomarkers for SAKI diagnosis by integrating bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing data from renal tissues in the SAKI model. We analyzed changes in mRNA/protein expression and cellular localization in kidney tissues affected by SAKI and confirmed that Hmox1 has potential as an indicator of proximal tubule (PT) injury in SAKI. Further validation across mouse models, and patient samples confirmed the role of Hmox1 in SAKI. Notably, we observed that Hmox1 high- and low-expressing PT cell subsets exhibit distinct phenotypes in SAKI. The Hmox1hi PT cells are the principal cells undergoing injury and metabolic alterations, while Hmox1low PT cells exert a major pro-inflammatory role through interactions with macrophages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-05764-w.

Introduction

Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SAKI) is a leading cause of acute kidney injury(AKI), accounting for over 50% of AKI cases in intensive care units (ICUs)1,2. Patients with SAKI often experience prolonged ICU and hospital stays, an elevated risk of chronic kidney disease, and increased mortality2–4. Early prevention of SAKI remains challenging due to difficulties in pinpointing the onset of injury during sepsis5, and no specific, effective treatments currently exist3. Therefore, the identification of novel biomarkers for early SAKI diagnosis, along with research into key genes and underlying mechanisms, is essential for providing optimal treatment and minimizing kidney damage.

Current diagnostic methods for AKI primarily rely on changes in serum creatinine and urinary output6, which have notable limitations for early detection and do not specifically indicate tubular function or injury. Significant tubular damage often occurs before serum creatinine levels rise, leading to delayed diagnoses and potentially worsening patient outcomes7. Additionally, urine output is influenced by diuretics, fluid resuscitation, and renal replacement therapy, diminishing its diagnostic value8. Previous studies indicate that proximal tubule (PT) cells are the primary effector cell population affected in AKI9–11. Although early biomarkers of kidney injury, including kidney injury molecule-1 (Kim1) and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (Ngal), have been widely studied3,12, their clinical utility remains constrained by distinct limitations. While Kim1 is markedly upregulated in PT cells during injury, its utility as a biomarker for AKI has shown inconsistent results across studies13, including limited prognostic discrimination in patients14. Conversely, Ngal exhibits limited specificity due to its predominant expression in distal nephron segments following injury15. Thus, gaining deeper insights into specific pathogenic cells and genes in SAKI is crucial for achieving early and preciseAKI detection and damage localization. In our study, we integrated the bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing analysis to investigate the potential common hub genes and biomarkers of SAKI and possible regulatory mechanisms in SAKI, and to validate them using animal models and clinical patients. Our research aims to identify reliable molecular markers and also help to understand the pathophysiological mechanisms in SAKI.

Methods

Data acquisition and quality control for bulk RNA-seq

Bulk RNA-seq data of mice kidneys induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) were retrieved from the GEO database, and three datasets that met the criteria were selected: GSE179554, GSE224127, and GSE186822. For GSE179554, we extracted kidney data from mice in the sham group and the 12-h post-CLP group, with 4 samples in each group. For GSE224127, kidney data were obtained from the sham group and the severe CLP 12-h group, with 5 samples per group. GSE186822 includes kidney data from 2 sham samples and 3 samples collected 6 h after CLP. Before further analysis, to reduce the impact of outliers, only genes expressed in more than half of the samples were retained, and data normalization was performed using the limma R package (version 3.58.1)16.

scRNA-seq analysis

The scRNA-seq dataset used in this study was derived from a previously published source17. The sham group consisted of both kidneys collected from a single mouse, whereas the CLP group included kidneys from three different mice subjected to CLP treatment. The relevant quality control and analysis methods refer to previously published articles by our research group17,18. In brief, scRNA-seq data were analyzed using the Seurat R package (version 5.2.1). High-quality cells with nCount_RNA > 500, nFeature_RNA > 250, log10GenesPerUMI > 0.8, and mitoRatio < 0.2 were retained for downstream analysis. Following normalization and principal component analysis (PCA), batch effects were corrected using the Harmony R package (version 1.2.3). After quality control, dimensionality reduction, and batch effect correction, cell types were identified using representative marker genes from published studies19.

DEGs identification

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for bulk RNA-seq data were identified using the limma R package (version 3.58.1)16 and visualized as a volcano plot using ggplot2 R package (version 3.5.1)20. The DEGs thresholds were set to |log2Foldchange|> 1 and adj. p < 0.05. Common DEGs across the three datasets were identified using the VennDiagram R package (version 1.7.3)21.

For scRNA-seq, DEGs between two groups of samples or two clusters of cells were identified using the FindMarkers function in the Seurat R package (version 5.2.1)22, with the same thresholds applied |log2Foldchange|> 1 and adj. p < 0.05. The identification of common DEGs between bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq, as well as the enrichment analysis, followed the previously described methods.

Hub genes identification

The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of DEGs was established using the STRING database23 and visualized with Cytoscape software (version 3.10.2)24, applying a cut-off of a combined score > 0.7 for interactions. The key module within the PPI network was identified using the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) plug-in in Cytoscape, which clusters the network based on topology to highlight densely connected areas. Subsequently, the maximal clique centrality (MCC) algorithm of CytoHubba25 was used to identify hub DEGs within the PPI network.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of the common DEGs was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.10.1)26. The Venn diagram and enrichment analysis were visualized through the online database bioinformatics27.

Pseudotime analysis

To observe the differentiation trajectory of PT cells in the CLP group of mice, pseudotime analysis and visualization were performed using the monocle R-package (version 2.24.1)28,29.

Functional enrichment analysis of PT-cell clusters

Functional enrichment analysis of the Hmox1hi or Hmox1low PT-cell clusters was conducted using the irGSEA R-package (version 3.2.5)30. Enrichment scores for various pathways within each cell cluster were derived through the AUCell, UCell, singscore, and ssgsea algorithms, and the number of upregulated or downregulated pathways in each cell cluster was visualized using a bar plot.

Cell-to-cell communication analysis

The CellChat R-package (version 1.6.1)29 was utilized to investigate cell communication between Hmox1hi/Hmox1low PT cells and immune cells during SAKI. The number of interactions and interaction weights/strengths were visualized using circle plots.

Animal experimentation

Male C57BL/6 mice aged 8–10 weeks, weighing 25 g ± 2 g, were procured from SLAC Laboratories (Changsha, China). They were housed under specific-pathogen-free conditions at the Xiangya Medical College of Central South University, with ad libitum access to food and water. The authors complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. All animal care and experimental procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Central South University (approval number: CSU-202201–0111), and all experiments were performed following relevant guidelines and regulations.

Establishment of SAKI models

The CLP mouse models were established following a standard procedure31. In brief, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and an approximately 1 cm incision was made along the linea alba. Following exposure of the cecum, it was ligated at its midpoint, punctured, and an appropriate number of contents was expelled. The cecum was then carefully repositioned in the abdominal cavity, and the incision was closed in layers. Mice in the sham group underwent the same surgical procedures except for CLP. The Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced mouse model was established via a single intraperitoneal injection of LPS (10 mg/kg body weight; E. coli O111: B4, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), while control mice received an equivalent volume of sterile saline. Subsequently, mice were euthanized under anesthesia induced by sodium pentobarbital 12/24 h post-CLP surgery or LPS injection, and kidney tissues were harvested for subsequent analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was conducted to analyze mRNA expression levels in mouse kidney tissues. Total RNA was extracted using AG RNAex Pro Reagent (Accurate Biology, China), followed by cDNA synthesis using the Evo M-MLV RT kit (Accurate Biology, China). The qPCR analysis was performed with a qPCR Kit (Accurate Biology, China). The expression levels of target genes were normalized to the reference gene β-actin (Mouse ACTB Endogenous Reference Genes Primers, Sangon Biotech, China). Laboratory examination

Serum creatinine and blood urea levels were measured using an automated biochemical analysis instrument (AU5400, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at the Department of Laboratory Medicine, Xiangya Hospital of Central South University.

Human kidney samples

Kidney tissue sections from patients were provided by Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. Relevant enrollment criteria and details of patients’ clinical data are available in the published literature of our group32. All experiments were conducted under the supervision of the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (approval number: 2022020606), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

Multiplex immunofluorescence was performed on the kidney sections of mice or patients with sepsis using the TSA Fluorescence Double Staining Kit (Abiowell, China). Briefly, the sections (4 µm) were dewaxed, rehydrated, and microwaved in EDTA for 15 min. After quenching endogenous peroxidase activity and blocking proteins, one antigen was detected in each round, which included primary and secondary antibody incubation followed by tyramine signal amplification (TSA) visualization. After the final round of staining, the sections were incubated with DAPI (Servicebio, China) for 10 min. Images were captured using a Leica microscope (Germany). The following antibodies were used: anti-Hmox1 (1:200, 10701–1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-Aqp1 (1:500, 20333–1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-Aqp2 (1:500, 29386–1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-Kim1 (1:200, 30948–1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-F4/80 (1:300, 27044–1-AP, Proteintech, China), and anti-Ki67 (1:200, 27309–1-AP, Proteintech, China).

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were subjected to rigorous statistical analysis using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 software. Results were presented as mean values with standard deviation (SD) error bars. Comparisons between two or more groups were performed using T-tests or One-way ANOVA, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. To assess the correlation between two variables, the normality of the data was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For variables following a normal distribution, Pearson correlation coefficients were used for correlation analysis. For non-normally distributed variables, the nonparametric Spearman correlation was applied.

SAKI predominantly affects the PT cells

Bulk transcriptional profiling has successfully characterized kidney injury, though it represents an average transcriptional profile across various cell types. To gain a deeper understanding of the cellular landscape and cell type-specific gene expression changes in kidneys from CLP-treated mice, we analyzed the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset from a previous study by Privratsky JR17. After stringent quality control filtering, we identified eight distinct cell clusters based on well-established cell markers from published studies19 (Fig. 2a,b). Among these, PT cells were further subdivided into PT-S1, PT-S2, and PT-S3 clusters, corresponding to proximal tubular segments S1–S3 (Fig. 2c). Next, we focused on the changes in the relative ratios of different cell types in sham and CLP mice. The results revealed a significant reduction in PT cells during sepsis (Fig. 2d), indicating PT cells as the central injury target in AKI. Preliminary exploration of the function of Hmox1 in PT cells during SAKI

To gain a deeper understanding of the functional roles of Hmox1 in PT cells during SAKI, we categorized the PT cells in the CLP samples into high-expression and low-expression subsets based on Hmox1 expression levels (Fig. 5a). The top 20% of Hmox1-expressing PT cells were identified as the high-expression subset (Hmox1hi PT cells), while the remaining PT cells constituted the low-expression subset (Hmox1low PT cells). Volcano plot analysis was employed to visualize the DEGs between Hmox1hi and Hmox1low PT cells (Fig. 5b). Subsequently, we conducted enrichment analysis to explore the correlation between Hmox1 expression levels and functional changes in PT cells. GO and KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the upregulated DEGs in Hmox1hi PT cells were positively associated with oxidative phosphorylation, TNF signaling pathways, apoptosis, and ferroptosis. Previous studies have reported that Hmox1 is a potent regulator of oxidative stress in AKI. In contrast, the downregulated DEGs were primarily enriched in pathways related to the membrane structure of PT cells, such as the brush border membrane and apical part of the cell (Fig. 5c–f). Subsequently, to identify potential cell–cell interactions between the two PT subsets and immune cells, we performed ligand-receptor analysis using CellChat. Hmox1hi and Hmox1low PT cells show different phenotypes in SAKI

The proliferative and repair capacity of PT cells following injury is a critical determinant of AKI outcomes33,34. To investigate whether the PT cells with different levels of Hmox1 expression can influence cell proliferation, we performed immunofluorescent staining for Ki67 (a proliferation marker) in SAKI model kidneys. Discussion

SAKI is a prevalent complication in septic patients, contributing to high rates of morbidity and mortality2. Despite its severity, theunderlying pathogenesis remains poorly understood, and current treatment options are limited in effectiveness. Therefore, identifying novel biomarkers for early detection and therapeutic intervention is crucial to improving the clinical management of SAKI.

PT cells are particularly susceptible to acute injury and account for the majority of cellular damage and death following SAKI10,11. Although the pathophysiology of sepsis-induced proximal tubular injury is not yet fully understood, it is thought to involve inflammatory responses, autophagy, apoptosis, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction35–38. In line with prior studies, we observed a significant reduction in PT cell proportion and downregulation of the PT-specific marker Aqp1 in SAKI, further supporting the role of PT cells as early responders in septic kidney injury. Traditional biomarkers like serum creatinine, cystatin C, and urinary output lack the sensitivity and specificity required for detecting PT injury accurately7.

To address this, we conducted an integrated analysis combining bulk and single-cell RNA-seq data to identify specific markers for PT cell injury in SAKI. Our results identified a notable upregulation of Hmox1 in the CLP-induced SAKI model, specifically within PT cells. Trajectory analysis indicated a trend of PT cell differentiation toward increased Hmox1 expression during sepsis. Consistent with bioinformatics findings, kidney samples from septic mice and patients exhibited substantial Hmox1 upregulation in PT cells. This marker’s specificity is highlighted by the differential release of biomarkers from tubular segments: Kim1 from PT cells and Ngal from distal cells10. Our scRNA-seq data further supported that Hmox1 expression in PT cells occurred notably earlier and was more robust than that of Kim1. Moreover, the localization of Hmox1 was more specific compared to Ngal. Previous research has shown that Kim1 begins to be upregulated 6 h after injury, with its peak expression occurring 24 h post-injury33,39. In addition, Hmox1 displayed a broader cellular distribution than Kim1. It was induced not only in Kim1-positive injured cells but also in Kim1-negative cell populations. Kim1 shows predominant S3 segment expression after injury13, while Hmox1’s pan-PT induction suggests superior sensitivity for detecting early tubular injury.

Hmox1 was reported as early as 2000 to be upregulated in the renal tissue of AKI model mice40,41; however, its specific molecular functions have only begun to be elucidated in recent years. Hmox1 has been identified as an inducible enzyme that is upregulated under cellular stress, exhibiting potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic properties42. It plays a crucial role as a rapid protective response in sepsis-related multi-organ injury43. Research has shown that the beneficial effects of Hmox1 induction are mediated by the breakdown of toxic heme into cytoprotective substances such as ferrous iron, biliverdin, and carbon monoxide42. These effects have been documented in various animal models of AKI, where increases in heme levels and Hmox1 expression correlate with elevated inflammatory markers in ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) mouse models44. In vivo studies demonstrated that Hmox1 deficiency in myeloid cells resulted in heightened histological injury, inflammation, and oxidative stress after renal I/R injury45. Conversely, in the CLP model, mesenchymal stem cells overexpressing Hmox1 alleviated renal injury and improved survival rates46, establishing Hmox1’s protective role against AKI.

In this study, through integrated analysis of high-throughput data and experimental validation, we found that in SAKI, Hmox1hi PT cells as well as Hmox1low PT cells, exhibited distinct phenotypes. Hmox1hi PT cells were found to be closely involved in cellular stress pathways and structural damage, while Hmox1low PT cells displayed pro-inflammatory traits. Specifically, Hmox1hi PT cells showed co-expression of the injury marker Kim1 and lacked normal PT cell functional and polarity markers (such as Aqp1). In contrast, Hmox1low PT cells exhibited extensive interactions with immune cells, particularly with an increased accumulation of macrophages near the Hmox1-low expression areas in the kidneys of CLP mice. Interestingly, while Hmox1 is widely acknowledged for its protective role in sepsis47, our study revealed a positive correlation between its expression level and the degree of kidney injury. This finding prompts the need for further experiments to ascertain whether this reflects a feedback self-regulatory mechanism by which PT cells respond to injury. Additionally, it is noteworthy that, although previous studies on AKI have indicated that injury and inflammation often occur simultaneously44, our study suggests that in SAKI, the PT cells exhibiting structural damage and those contributing to a pro-inflammatory response belong to two distinct subsets, and that the significant difference between them is the level of Hmox1 expression, which warrants more in-depth investigation in future studies.

Our study has some limitations. Since we primarily focused on the expression and distribution of Hmox1 in the kidneys during sepsis, we did not assess Hmox1 expression in other organs affected by sepsis. Therefore, the potential of plasma or urinary Hmox1 as an indicator of PT cell injury in SAKI requires further investigation. Additionally, more experimental research is needed to elucidate the mechanism behind the rapid and significant increase in Hmox1 expression following SAKI. Finally, given the distinct phenotypic differences between Hmox1low and Hmox1hi PT cells, our study suggests that Hmox1 induction may play a critical role in the response of PT cells to SAKI. However, the exact mechanisms by which Hmox1 mediates these phenotypic changes and its effects on macrophages require clarification through gene editing or inhibitor experiments, which will be the focus of our future research.

Conclusion

Our data identified hub genes and related pathways involved in SAKI through a combination of bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq analysis. We found that PT cells were the primary renal parenchymal cells affected in SAKI, with Hmox1 being significantly upregulated, predominantly in PT cells. Therefore, Hmox1 may serve as an early and sensitive marker for PT cell injury following SAKI. Validation across mouse models, human samples, and databases suggests that Hmox1 has strong potential as a biomarker for the early diagnosis of SAKI, as well as a crucial molecule modulating the early response of PT cells to injury.
