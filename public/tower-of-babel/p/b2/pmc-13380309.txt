Hierarchical Multi-Omics Trajectory Prediction for fecal microbiota transplantation: a novel machine learning framework for small-sample longitudinal multi-omics integration
Authors: Zhou Yi-Hui, Sun George
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has emerged as a highly effective treatment for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection and is being actively investigated for numerous other conditions. While multi-omics studies have revealed dynamic changes in microbial communities and host metabolism following FMT, existing approaches are primarily descriptive and lack the ability to model individual patient trajectories or identify early biomarkers of treatment response. Small-sample, multi-omics, longitudinal prediction presents unique computational challenges: high dimensionality (), multi-omics integration, temporal dynamics, and interpretability. Here, we present Hierarchical Multi-Omics Trajectory Prediction (HMOTP), a purpose-built machine learning framework that addresses these challenges through hierarchical feature construction, multilevel attention mechanisms, and patient-specific trajectory prediction. We evaluated HMOTP on 15 patients with recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection who underwent FMT, with lipidomics and metagenomics profiling at four timepoints spanning 6 months. Notably, naively concatenating multi-omics features degraded Random Forest performance ( to accuracy), whereas HMOTP’s hierarchical integration benefited from the additional omics layer, demonstrating that its advantage stems from structure, not from access to more data. Through hierarchical interpretability, HMOTP identified key biomarkers and revealed cross-omics associations between host lipid metabolism and microbial energy pathways, demonstrating utility for longitudinal modeling and biological discovery in FMT response. HMOTP provides a generalizable, principled framework for personalized medicine applications across small-sample multi-omics problems. Source code and a demo dataset are publicly available.Keywords: multi-omics integration, small-sample learning, hierarchical attention, trajectory prediction, fecal microbiota transplantation, longitudinal machine learning

Introduction

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has emerged as a highly effective treatment for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (rCDI), with cure rates exceeding 90% in randomized trials [1, 2]. Beyond rCDI, FMT is being actively investigated for a wide range of conditions including inflammatory bowel disease, metabolic disorders, and even cancer immunotherapy [3–6]. The therapeutic efficacy of FMT is thought to be mediated through complex interactions between the transplanted microbiota and host metabolism, yet the precise mechanisms remain incompletely understood [7].

Multi-omics approaches combining metagenomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics have been increasingly applied to study FMT, revealing dynamic changes in microbial communities and host metabolic profiles [8–10]. However, most studies have focused on descriptive analyses comparing pre- and post-FMT states, with limited ability to predict individual patient trajectories or identify early biomarkers of treatment response. While prediction models for FMT outcomes have been developed [11], these typically rely on clinical variables and do not leverage the rich multi-omics data now routinely collected in FMT studies. Machine learning approaches have shown promise for microbiome-based disease prediction [12, 13], yet have rarely been extended to longitudinal, multi-omics FMT data.

Integrating multi-omics data in small-sample settings poses substantial challenges, where the number of features far exceeds the number of samples (, where denotes the number of features and the number of samples), a common scenario in precision medicine applications like FMT where patient cohorts are often limited. Traditional dimensionality reduction methods such as principal component analysis lose biological interpretability [14], while simple feature concatenation fails to capture hierarchical biological relationships within and across omics layers. Existing multi-omics integration methods like MOFA [15] perform unsupervised factor discovery rather than supervised prediction; mixOmics [16] provides supervised integration but does not model longitudinal trajectories; and sparse canonical correlation analysis identifies correlated structure across omics layers [17] but is not designed for longitudinal outcome prediction. Longitudinal data require specialized modeling to capture temporal dynamics and patient-specific trajectories [18, 19], while black-box models provide limited biological interpretability [20].

Recent deep learning approaches to multi-omics integration (e.g. graph attention networks, transformer-based classifiers) have shown promise but typically require larger training sets and are not designed for longitudinal, patient-level prediction in the small-sample regime (). What is lacking is a supervised, interpretable method that integrates omics through structured attention, rather than naive concatenation or large-scale pretraining, and that models patient-specific trajectories in this regime.

To address these challenges, we developed Hierarchical Multi-Omics Trajectory Prediction (HMOTP), a purpose-built machine learning framework for multi-omics, longitudinal prediction. HMOTP integrates omics layers through multilevel within- and cross-omics attention [21], models patient-specific temporal trajectories via transfer learning [22], and optionally incorporates domain-knowledge feature aggregation. Advantages of HMOTP include (i) interpretable cross-omics attention over biologically meaningful features, rather than black-box latent factors; (ii) multi-head attention that learns importance at the individual-feature and cross-omics levels (with optional class-level aggregation); (iii) trajectory prediction that exploits shared structure across patients. Each of these components builds on established techniques (hierarchical representations, attention mechanisms, patient embeddings); the contribution of HMOTP lies in their principled integration under the specific constraints of small-sample, longitudinal, multi-omics prediction, a setting where no existing tool provides supervised, interpretable, trajectory-capable integration.

We applied HMOTP to a cohort of 15 patients with rCDI who underwent FMT [23], with comprehensive lipidomics (397 features) and metagenomics (10 634 pathways) profiling at four timepoints spanning 6 months. This application demonstrates the framework’s utility for personalized FMT monitoring while revealing biological insights through hierarchical interpretability, including cross-omics associations between host lipid metabolism and microbial metabolic pathways. Although demonstrated here on FMT, the framework is applicable to a broad range of multi-omics precision-medicine problems.

Methods

Study population and data collection

We analyzed data from 15 patients with rCDI who underwent FMT at the University of North Carolina. Fecal samples were collected at four timepoints: pre-FMT ( samples), 2 weeks post-FMT (), 2 months post-FMT (), and 6 months post-FMT (), resulting in 45 total samples. All samples were collected under approved institutional review board protocols (UNC IRB #16-2283). Patient metadata included patient ID, timepoint, age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). Patient IDs were used for patient-level cross-validation to prevent data leakage, ensuring that all samples from a given patient were assigned to the same fold.

Lipidomics profiling

Lipid profiling was performed using liquid chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry-collision induced dissociation-mass spectrometry (LC-IMS-CID-MS) as previously described [24]. This platform enables confident identification of over 850 unique lipid species across 26 lipid classes through 4D matching of retention time, collision cross-section [25], fragmentation pattern, and precursor mass. For this study, we identified 397 unique lipids across 22 lipid classes, including acylcarnitines, sphingolipids, glycerophospholipids, and glycerolipids. Lipid abundances were normalized for sample weight and total ion count, then log2-transformed and scaled to 110 for analysis.

Metagenomics profiling

Metagenomic sequencing was performed on fecal DNA extracts. Raw sequencing reads were processed using KneadData (v0.12.0) [26] to remove human-derived sequences, then profiled using MetaPhlAn (v4.1.1) [27] for taxonomic abundance and HUMAnN (v3.9) [28] for functional pathway abundance. We analyzed 10 634 metabolic pathways with counts normalized to counts per million. The metagenomics and multi-omics data were generated and deposited by McMillan et al. [29]; raw sequencing data are available in the Sequence Read Archive under BioProject ID PRJNA1055134, and targeted metabolomics in MassIVE under MSV000093844.

Hierarchical Multi-Omics Trajectory Prediction architecture

HMOTP consists of four main components: (i) hierarchical feature construction using domain knowledge, (ii) multilevel attention for multi-omics integration, (iii) patient-specific trajectory prediction (Fig. 1), and (iv) hierarchical interpretability.

Hierarchical feature construction

Traditional dimensionality reduction methods such as principal component analysis lose biological interpretability. HMOTP instead uses domain knowledge to create hierarchical feature representations at multiple biological levels (Fig. 1). At Level 1, we use raw features: the individual lipid species and the individual metabolic pathways. At Level 2, we aggregate features using biological knowledge: lipid abundances are summed within lipid classes (e.g. Acylcarnitines, Sphingolipids, Phosphatidylcholines), and pathway abundances are summed within pathway categories (e.g. Carbohydrate metabolism, Amino acid biosynthesis). This hierarchical structure reduces dimensionality while preserving biological meaning, enabling interpretability at multiple levels.

Feature selection and ensemble approach

To further reduce overfitting in the high-dimensional setting, we applied feature selection using univariate F-statistic (f_classif) to select the top features [31] from the combined lipid and pathway feature space. This ensemble approach improves stability and generalization, which is particularly important in small-sample settings.

Evaluation methodology

We used LOPO-CV to evaluate model performance. This strategy ensures that all samples from a given patient are in the same fold, preventing data leakage and providing realistic performance estimates for new patients. Patients contributed only a single pre-FMT sample in the integrated dataset and were excluded from fold-wise evaluation because patient-level metrics require multiple held-out samples per fold. For each fold, we held out one patient for testing and trained on the remaining patients. Feature selection was performed only on the training data to prevent data leakage.

We compared HMOTP against baseline methods in two configurations to ensure a fair comparison:

All experiments were run with fixed random seed (42) for reproducibility. Results are reported as mean standard deviation across folds.

Biomarker discovery and cross-omics analysis

Biomarker importance was calculated by aggregating attention weights from the ensemble models. For each model in the ensemble, we extracted attention weights from the multi-head attention mechanism and converted them to feature importance scores. The final importance for each feature was the mean importance across the three ensemble models.

Cross-omics correlations were calculated using Spearman correlation (nonparametric) between lipid species and pathway abundances across all samples. We identified strong correlations with and after multiple testing correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure [34].

Implementation

HMOTP was implemented in Python 3.11 using PyTorch 1.10+ [35] for neural network components, scikit-learn [36] for preprocessing and baseline methods, and standard scientific Python libraries (Pandas, NumPy) for data manipulation. All experiments were conducted on a standard workstation; see the Data Availability section for code access and reproduction instructions.

Results

HMOTP architecture and methodological innovation

HMOTP addresses the curse of dimensionality in small-sample multi-omics problems through hierarchical feature construction that preserves biological interpretability. The framework operates at three levels: Level 1 (raw features: 397 lipids and 10 634 pathways), Level 2 (aggregated features: 22 lipid classes and seven pathway categories), and cross-level interactions that combine these representations. At Level 2, lipid abundances are summed within 22 classes defined by the study lipid-class key; pathway abundances are summed within seven functional categories assigned by keyword rules on HUMAnN pathway names (Biosynthesis, Degradation, Fermentation, Energy Metabolism, Amino Acid Metabolism, Lipid Metabolism, and Other). This structure compresses the feature space (397 lipids 22 classes; 10 634 pathways 7 categories) while retaining biologically meaningful groupings, enabling interpretability at multiple scales.

The multilevel attention mechanism learns relative importance within and across hierarchy levels. At Level 1, attention integrates individual lipids with individual pathways; at Level 2, it integrates lipid-class summaries with pathway-category summaries; cross-level attention then combines the Level 1 and Level 2 representations. Timepoint-specific modulation allows the model to weight features differently across sampling times, supporting temporal modeling in the longitudinal cohort.

Patient-specific trajectory prediction supports personalized inference despite the limited cohort size. A shared neural network produces integrated representations from the hierarchical features; patient-specific embeddings and timepoint information are combined to predict pre versus post-FMT status. Information is shared across patients within the cohort through parameter sharing, enabling stable learning in the small-sample setting.

Trajectory predictions demonstrate temporal integration

HMOTP’s trajectory prediction component integrates timepoint information to model longitudinal dynamics, enabling time-dependent predictions that capture temporal progression patterns. Using LOPO-CV, we generated trajectory predictions showing how predicted probabilities of post-FMT status evolve across timepoints (Fig. 3).

The population trajectory showed a consistent temporal progression from pre-FMT (mean probability = 0.497 0.002) to 6 months post-FMT (mean probability = 0.573 0.003), demonstrating the model’s ability to capture time-dependent patterns. All patient trajectories showed positive slopes (mean = 0.0047 probability units per week, range: 0.0031–0.0063), indicating that the model successfully integrates timepoint information to provide time-dependent predictions. The trajectory direction analysis (Panel B) shows that all patients transition from lower initial probabilities (pre-FMT) to higher final probabilities (post-FMT), demonstrating that the trajectory prediction component successfully captures temporal dynamics across the longitudinal study period. We note that the observed temporal separation is partly attributable to the pre/post-FMT label structure, which is inherently aligned with the sampling timeline. The contribution of the trajectory component beyond label recovery is reflected in the patient-specific slope variation (range: 0.0031–0.0063), which captures individualized rates of biological change rather than a uniform group-level shift, enabling personalized time-dependent predictions that distinguish HMOTP from models that treat timepoints as exchangeable.

These results demonstrate that HMOTP successfully integrates timepoint information, validating the trajectory prediction component of the framework. The consistent temporal progression and positive trajectory slopes indicate that the model captures robust, time-dependent patterns that reflect the biological dynamics of FMT response over the longitudinal study period. To confirm that performance reflects genuine use of temporal information rather than omics-only pre/post separation, we conducted a timepoint-blinded sensitivity analysis in which the timepoint input was set to zero at test time. This yielded chance-level balanced accuracy (), confirming that the trajectory component is essential to the reported results and that the model does not achieve separation from omics features alone. Combined with the hierarchical interpretability and cross-omics integration (Sections 3.5 and 3.6), HMOTP provides both predictive capability and interpretable, hypothesis-generating insights into FMT response.

Cross-omics integration reveals mechanistic associations

HMOTP’s hierarchical multi-omics integration enabled the discovery of functionally coherent associations between host lipid metabolism and microbial metabolic pathways, suggesting previously unrecognized mechanisms underlying FMT efficacy. Through comprehensive correlation analysis across all lipid species and microbial pathways, we identified 40 868 significant associations (Spearman , FDR-corrected ), with associations involving top-predictive biomarkers among the strongest candidates for follow-up.

The strongest cross-omics association identified was between phosphatidylcholine PC(32:1) and fatty acid -oxidation pathways (FAO-PWY, , ), suggesting coupling consistent with a link between host membrane lipid composition and microbial energy metabolism. This association is consistent with the hypothesis that successful FMT restores a metabolic state where host-derived fatty acids serve as substrates for microbial -oxidation, while microbial metabolic activities reciprocally influence host membrane lipid remodeling. The correlation between PC(32:1) and heterolactic fermentation (P122-PWY, ) further supports this interpretation, indicating coordinated host–microbiome metabolic coupling.

A notable finding was the association between acylcarnitine AC(12:0) and methylglyoxal degradation (METHGLYUT-PWY, , ). Methylglyoxal is a toxic byproduct of glycolysis that accumulates during metabolic stress and can cause cellular damage through protein glycation. This association is consistent with the possibility that successful FMT enhances microbial capacity to detoxify host-derived metabolic byproducts, representing a potential mechanism by which the microbiome may protect host cells from metabolic stress. The parallel association between AC(12:0) and purine nucleotide biosynthesis (DENOVOPURINE2-PWY, ) is similarly consistent with restoration of nucleotide metabolism, which plays a critical role in cellular repair and regeneration.

The top-predictive biomarker AC(12:1) showed strong positive correlations with multiple energy metabolism pathways, including the TCA cycle (REDCITCYC, ), pyrimidine biosynthesis (PWY-7184, ), and heterolactic fermentation (P122-PWY, ), consistent with its role as a marker of restored mitochondrial function. Notably, AC(12:1) exhibited a strong negative correlation with UNMAPPED pathways (, ), indicating that successful FMT is associated with a reduction in uncharacterized or potentially pathogenic microbial functions. This finding raises the hypothesis that FMT efficacy is partially mediated by suppression of cryptic or deleterious microbial activities, rather than solely through promotion of beneficial functions.

A distinct pattern emerged for ceramide species, particularly Cer(t18:0/16:0)a, which showed strong negative correlations with multiple energy metabolism pathways, including protocatechuate degradation (PROTOCATECHUATE-ORTHO-CLEAVAGE-PWY, ), heterolactic fermentation (P122-PWY, ), and fatty acid -oxidation (FAO-PWY, ). Ceramides are bioactive sphingolipids involved in apoptosis, inflammation, and insulin resistance, and their negative association with microbial energy metabolism is consistent metabolic dysregulation that is ameliorated by successful FMT.

These cross-omics associations reveal a candidate systems-level metabolic network where host lipid metabolism and microbial energy metabolism are tightly coupled, with successful FMT potentially restoring this coordination. The hierarchical integration approach of HMOTP was essential for identifying these relationships, as they span multiple biological scales and would be missed by single-omics or nonhierarchical approaches. We emphasize that these associations are correlational and exploratory; experimental validation is required to establish causal relationships.

Discussion

HMOTP addresses a specific and underserved gap in multi-omics methodology: supervised, interpretable, trajectory-capable integration in the small-sample longitudinal setting. By combining hierarchical feature construction, multilevel attention, and patient-specific trajectory modeling, HMOTP outperformed both single-omics and multi-omics baselines across all reported metrics under rigorous LOPO-CV.

The individual components of HMOTP (hierarchical feature representations, attention mechanisms, and patient embeddings) each build on established techniques. The contribution lies in their principled integration: supervised classification, biological interpretability, and longitudinal trajectory modeling under the constraint, a combination no existing tool provides in the small-sample longitudinal setting.

The degradation of Random Forest under naive multi-omics concatenation confirms that the benefit of additional omics data depends entirely on how it is integrated; structure matters more than volume.

Beyond FMT, HMOTP is applicable to any longitudinal multi-omics problem where domain knowledge supports a feature hierarchy. The architecture accommodates additional omics layers through the attention mechanism and additional timepoints through the trajectory component, positioning it as a practical framework for precision medicine applications in immunology, oncology, and microbiome intervention studies.

Several considerations should be noted when applying HMOTP: (i) biomarker identities depend on the chosen feature hierarchy and ensemble , and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating; (ii) the framework requires domain knowledge to define feature hierarchies and is most beneficial when such biological structure exists.

HMOTP demonstrates that principled hierarchical integration of multi-omics data can deliver both strong predictive performance and interpretable biological insights in small longitudinal cohorts, a setting where existing tools fall short. As multi-omics profiling becomes routine in clinical studies, frameworks that operate effectively under sample size constraints will be essential for translating molecular data into personalized medicine applications.
