Health literacy in adolescents with chronic disease during transition
Authors: Noriko Ozawa, Taiga Shibayama, Noriko Hiraga, Kayuri Furuya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe transition from pediatric to adult-oriented healthcare is a major challenge, and adequate health literacy is essential for achieving a smooth transition. However, knowledge is scant regarding adolescents’ health literacy during this period, and research that includes both adolescents and their parents remains limited.ObjectiveThis study clarified the health literacy of adolescents with chronic illnesses and their parents and examined differences between adolescents’ self-assessments and parents’ evaluations.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted with 111 adolescent–parent dyads from two hospitals in Japan. The Japanese version of the 14-item Health Literacy Scale (HLS-14) assessed functional, communicative, and critical literacy. Parents also evaluated their adolescents’ literacy. Descriptive statistics, Pearson’s correlations, paired t-tests, and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were used.FindingsAdolescents showed moderate literacy levels across domains (functional: M=21.18/25; communicative: M=14.6/25; critical: M=13.1/20). Functional literacy developed independently, while communicative and critical literacy were strongly correlated. Parents demonstrated lower literacy than general adult norms, particularly in communicative and critical domains. Agreement between parent ratings and adolescents’ self-reports was fair (ICC=0.35–0.42), indicating limited correspondence.ConclusionDomain-specific strategies are needed to foster adolescent health literacy. Communicative and critical literacy require intentional educational support beyond basic reading and writing. Given parents’ relatively low literacy, transition programs should incorporate parent-focused resources. Improving health literacy in both adolescents and their parents may support a smoother transition to adult-oriented care and promote independent disease management; however, further research is needed to better understand and support this process.Keywords: Health literacy, Adolescents, Chronic Illness, Transition to adult care, Parent-child relationship

Background

Advances in health care science have enabled numerous children with chronic illnesses to survive into adulthood. Consequently, the transition from pediatric to adult-oriented healthcare has become a critical issue in contemporary health care practice.1 Despite various system-level initiatives, this transition remains complex and is not always implemented smoothly.2 Notably, several adolescents and their parents prefer the familiarity of pediatric care,3, 4 and some adolescents fail to adapt sufficiently to adult services and subsequently return to pediatric care after transfer.5, 6

Supporting adolescents’ autonomy during this transition period requires strengthening their ability to understand and manage their own conditions, as well as their capacity to communicate effectively with healthcare providers.7, 8, 9 These abilities can be conceptualized as “health literacy,” which reflects the developmental acquisition of competencies to understand and utilize health-related information.

Health literacy is defined as “the ability to access, understand, and use health information to make informed decisions.”10, 11 Following Nutbeam’s12 framework, the present study conceptualized health literacy across three domains: functional literacy (basic reading and comprehension of health information), communicative literacy (skills for extracting information from social interactions and applying it to changing circumstances), and critical literacy (capacity for critical analysis and autonomous application of health information to decision-making).

In adults, limited health literacy is associated with adverse health outcomes.13 Further, recent evidence from adult populations suggests that inadequate health literacy is associated with increased emergency department revisits.14 These findings support the importance of examining health literacy in the context of the transition from pediatric to adult-oriented care. However, research focusing on adolescents with chronic illnesses remains scarce, and the actual level of health literacy during this critical transition period lacks clarity.

Additionally, parental involvement plays a substantial role in supporting adolescents during healthcare transition. Parents are greatly involved in managing medical appointments, fostering autonomy, and preparing their children for the adult healthcare system.15, 16 Parental health literacy also influences adolescents’ health outcomes.17 Nevertheless, research remains limited regarding the health literacy of parents whose children are undergoing the transition to adult care and the relationship between parent and child health literacy.18, 19

Furthermore, previous research suggests that even when parents recognize the importance of transition preparation, they may not adequately encourage adolescents’ independent behaviors, such as attending health care appointments alone.20 Additionally, overprotective parenting may result in the underestimation of adolescents’ abilities.21 If discrepancies exist between adolescents’ self-reported health literacy and parents’ perceptions of their adolescents’ health literacy, understanding these differences may be important for supporting adolescents during the transition to adult-oriented care; however, this has not been fully examined.

Considering the aforementioned gaps in research, the present study aimed to

1.describe the health literacy of adolescents with chronic illnesses who are transitioning to adult care, as well as the health literacy of their parents;

describe the health literacy of adolescents with chronic illnesses who are transitioning to adult care, as well as the health literacy of their parents;

2.examine the associations between adolescent and parent health literacy; and

examine the associations between adolescent and parent health literacy; and

3.assess the level of agreement between adolescents’ self-reported health literacy and parents’ perceptions of their adolescents’ health literacy.

assess the level of agreement between adolescents’ self-reported health literacy and parents’ perceptions of their adolescents’ health literacy.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional survey was conducted using structured, self-administered questionnaires.

Participants

Adolescents with chronic illnesses and their parents were recruited at two hospitals in Japan. Adolescents satisfying the following criteria were recruited: (1) age 13–19 years (corresponding to junior high school age through young adulthood in Japan); (2) confirmed diagnosis of diabetes, congenital or acquired cardiovascular disease, or hematological malignancy requiring ongoing medical management; and (3) current enrollment in pediatric outpatient services at participating institutions. Exclusion criteria included cases wherein the attending physician judged that the adolescent could not understand a self-administered questionnaire because of reading or writing difficulties or developmental disabilities, as well as cases wherein the adolescent was in the terminal stage of illness. This age range was selected because transitional preparation generally begins in early adolescence, namely at approximately 13 years of age.1, 22, 23 The disease categories were selected because of their prevalence and the need for structured transition support.

Data collection

Between September 2020 and September 2021, physicians identified eligible adolescents and their parents during routine outpatient visits and provided initial study information. Research staff subsequently offered detailed verbal and written explanations of study procedures, voluntary participation, and data confidentiality. Only parent–adolescent dyads wherein both the adolescent and the parent provided informed consent were included. Dyads were enrolled consecutively when both parties agreed to participate. Following informed consent, parent–adolescent pairs independently completed paper-based questionnaires in private waiting areas to minimize response bias. Completed forms were placed in secure collection boxes; those unable to complete the survey onsite were permitted to mail it back within two weeks. When questionnaires were completed at home, separate questionnaire packets were distributed to parents and adolescents. Participants were instructed to complete the questionnaires independently and not view each other’s responses. Additionally, separate return envelopes were provided for each participant.

Measures

Background characteristics

Adolescents reported their age, and sex. Parents reported their age, sex, child’s diagnosis, and years since diagnosis.

Health literacy

Health literacy was assessed using the Japanese version of the 14-item Health Literacy Scale (HLS-14),24, 25 a validated instrument comprising three theoretically derived subscales: functional health literacy (five items), communicative health literacy (five items), and critical health literacy (four items).

Adolescents completed the HLS-14 to self-assess their own health literacy. Parents also completed the HLS-14 to self-assess their own health literacy. Parents also used the HLS-14 items to report their perceptions of their child’s health literacy. All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always), with higher scores indicating greater health literacy.

Previous validation studies in Japanese adult populations have demonstrated acceptable reliability and construct validity of the HLS-14.25 The total scale has exhibited satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=.81), with acceptable reliability for each subscale (functional health literacy, α=.77; communicative health literacy, α=.80; critical health literacy, α=.65). Construct validity has been supported by a three-factor structure corresponding to functional, communicative, and critical health literacy, as well as by associations with health-related behaviors and self-rated health. The items assess skills such as reading prescriptions, obtaining disease-related information, and critically evaluating treatment-related information.

However, the psychometric properties of the HLS-14 have not been formally established in adolescent populations.

Ethical considerations

Institutional approval was obtained from the ethics committees of the participating hospitals. This study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Informed consent was implied when respondents marked “I agree” on the survey form.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power 3.1 based on two primary analyses. For paired-sample t-tests comparing adolescent and parent ratings, 88 adolescent–parent dyads were required to detect a small-to-medium effect (Cohen’s d=.30) with 80% power and two-tailed α= .05. For correlation analyses examining parent–child associations, 123 dyads were needed to detect r = .25 given the same parameters. Our final sample of 111 dyads exceeded the requirement for the primary comparison (paired t-tests) and provided 80% power to detect correlations of r ≥ .27. While slightly underpowered for detecting r = .25, this sample size aligns with similar studies in pediatric transition research and represents a substantial proportion (69%) of eligible families at the participating centers.

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies) characterized participant demographics and health literacy scores. After confirming normality assumptions (Shapiro–Wilk tests, all p > .05), parametric tests were used. Pearson correlation coefficients examined associations between (1) adolescent self-reported health literacy domains, (2) parent self-reported health literacy domains, (3) parent-rated adolescent health literacy domains, and (4) cross-rater parent–child dyadic relationships. Paired-sample t-tests evaluated concordance between adolescent self-reports and parent ratings of adolescent health literacy within each dyad. To assess agreement, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated (ICC; two-way mixed effects, absolute agreement). Statistical significance was set at two-tailed p < .05. Given the exploratory nature of correlation analyses across multiple domains, uncorrected p-values are reported and patterns highlighted that were robust to Bonferroni correction (α=.05/12 =.004) where applicable.

Results

Among returned surveys, 32 were excluded for the following reasons: missing data from either parent or child (n = 18), partially completed questionnaires with > 20% missing items (n = 11), or invalid responses indicating poor comprehension, such as questionnaires wherein all items were answered identically (n = 3). The final analytic sample comprised 111 complete parent–child dyads (69.4% of eligible families). Demographic characteristics of non-responders were not available, precluding comparisons between responders and non-responders.

Adolescent participants (mean age=15.3 years; 55.9% female, n = 62) represented diverse chronic conditions: diabetes (n = 16, 14.4%), hematological malignancies or disorders (n = 64, 57.7%), and cardiovascular diseases (n = 31, 27.9%). All diabetes cases were type I. Most cancer-related cases were malignant tumors, with a few instances of aplastic anemia and hemophilia. Cardiovascular conditions were predominantly congenital; cases of Kawasaki disease and arrhythmia were rare.

Agreement analyses

Notably, ICC (two-way mixed effects, absolute agreement) were computed to assess concordance between adolescent self-reports and parent ratings (Table 6). Results indicated fair agreement across all domains: functional literacy (ICC=.416, 95% CI [.329,.615], p < .001), communicative literacy (ICC=.360, 95% CI [.186,.511], p < .001), critical literacy (ICC=0.352, 95% CI [.177,.504], p < .001), and total health literacy (ICC=0.412, 95% CI [.277,.578], p < .001). While all ICCs were statistically significant, the modest magnitude (0.35–0.42) suggests only fair absolute agreement, indicating that parent and adolescent ratings capture partially overlapping but not identical constructs.

Discussion

This study examined health literacy among adolescents with chronic illnesses and their parents during the critical transition period from pediatric to adult-oriented healthcare. The findings provide valuable insights into adolescents’ self-reported health literacy, parents’ self-reported health literacy, and parents’ perceptions of their adolescents’ health literacy, as well as the level of agreement between adolescents’ self-reports and parents’ perceptions.

First, the adolescents in this study demonstrated health literacy levels comparable to those reported in prior studies of adult populations using the HLS-14. As the HLS-14 does not provide established cut-off values, this interpretation is grounded in descriptive comparisons with previously reported score distributions rather than absolute classifications.25 These findings suggest that repeated exposure to health care environments, treatment-related experiences, and self-management demands during childhood and adolescence may contribute to higher health literacy levels prior to the transition to adult care.

However, the developmental trajectory of health literacy appears heterogeneous. The clear dissociation observed between functional literacy and higher-order communicative and critical literacy indicates that basic reading and comprehension skills alone do not naturally translate into the ability to interact effectively with healthcare professionals or to evaluate and apply health information reflectively. In interpreting these findings, distinguishing health literacy from self-efficacy is important, as these are conceptually distinct constructs. Health literacy refers to the ability to access, understand, evaluate, and apply health information, whereas self-efficacy reflects individuals’ confidence in their ability to perform specific behaviors. Although the HLS-14 is designed to assess health literacy, its self-reported format may partially capture perceived ability, which overlaps with self-efficacy. This distinction between basic and higher-order health literacy skills, as well as the potential overlap with self-efficacy in self-reported measures, aligns with Nutbeam’s12 conceptualization and has been confirmed in prior research.13

Furthermore, the relatively low internal consistency observed for adolescents’ total scores may reflect developmental variability in how self-reported health literacy items are understood and interpreted. This may also be partly attributable to the fact that the HLS-14 was developed and validated in adult populations and may not adequately capture developmental differences in adolescents’ health literacy. Ultimately, these higher-level competencies may require structured educational support, explicit opportunities for independent communication, and approaches that promote shared decision-making, rather than emerging solely from accumulated experience.4, 5

Second, the fair level of agreement between adolescents’ self-assessments and parents’ perceptions offers potential implications for transition planning; however, this interpretation must be approached with caution. Given the relatively low internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) of the HLS-14 observed in this study, it is possible that this fair level of agreement was partially due to chance or measurement error. Tentatively, while parents generally recognized their adolescents’ capacities, they tended to underestimate functional literacy, suggesting that reading and understanding information may be more visible to adolescents themselves than to observers who are focused on outcomes such as adherence or observable self-management behaviors. These findings imply that parental perceptions might be influenced not only by adolescents’ demonstrated competencies but also by parental fears, uncertainty, or expectations of vulnerability. Such perceptions can result in protective behaviors that inadvertently restrict adolescents’ autonomy at a time when the development of independent communication and decision-making is crucial.20 Nevertheless, drawing deeply nuanced conclusions regarding these dynamics may be premature given the reliability limitations of the current measure. Therefore, future research is needed to rigorously examine these associations once health literacy tools have been specifically developed and validated for adolescent populations.

Third, the relatively low levels of communicative and critical health literacy among parents highlight an important but often overlooked need in transition care. Although parents are tasked with navigating complex information, coordinating healthcare, and facilitating their child’s autonomy, they do so under considerable emotional, temporal, and informational burdens. Caring for a chronically ill child can limit the time, cognitive resources, and emotional availability required to critically evaluate healthcare information or to engage in collaborative decision-making with healthcare providers.26 In this context, deficiencies in parental health literacy may reflect not only individual knowledge gaps but also systemic constraints, such as limited consultation time, insufficient psychosocial support, or healthcare practices that reinforce hierarchical communication.27 Therefore, interventions aimed at transition readiness must extend beyond adolescents to include parents, ensuring that they possess not only information but also the time, confidence, and emotional stability to understand and use it effectively. These findings warrant replication in diverse populations and healthcare settings to determine generalizability and robustness. Future longitudinal studies are needed to examine how health literacy changes across adolescence and identify interventions that effectively support adolescents’ health literacy during the transition to adult-oriented care.

Taken together, these findings underscore the need for a dual focus in transition support: (1) Adolescents should be provided with intentional, developmentally tailored opportunities to engage independently in clinical communication, critical appraisal of health information, and autonomous decision-making. Such opportunities may encompass structured independent consultations, shared decision-making exercises, guided communication training, or targeted education programs that promote analytical skills. (2) Parents should receive both educational and psychosocial support to help them recognize their child’s emerging competencies, reduce anxiety related to relinquishing control, and actively participate in building their child’s autonomy. Viewing parents as collaborative partners rather than obstacles may facilitate a shift from protective overinvolvement to trust-based support, ultimately benefiting adolescents’ development. In this sense, transition support can be conceptualized not merely as an individual developmental process but as a relational, family-centered endeavor wherein health literacy is co-constructed through interactions among adolescents, parents, and healthcare providers.15, 28

Limitations

This study has several methodological limitations that require consideration. First, recruitment from only two hospitals may limit generalizability to adolescents receiving care in other healthcare systems with different transition protocols. Our sample’s disease composition (predominance of hematological conditions) may not reflect the broader population of chronically ill youth. Second, individuals who were unable to complete the questionnaire due to difficulty understanding or responding to the survey items were excluded, and questionnaires containing missing or invalid responses were not included in the analysis. As a result, responses from participants with lower health literacy may not have been adequately captured, and the reported health literacy levels among adolescents and parents may be somewhat overestimated. In addition, the HLS-14 has not been validated for adolescent populations, and the relatively low internal consistency observed among adolescents in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.44) may reflect limitations in applying an adult-developed instrument to this developmental stage. Further research is needed to establish the validity and reliability of age-appropriate health literacy measures for adolescents. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference to be made regarding the directionality of parent–child health literacy relationships and cannot capture how these associations evolve across the transition period. Longitudinal studies tracking health literacy trajectories from early adolescence through successful transition are needed to identify critical developmental windows for intervention. Finally, our sample included a range of chronic conditions; disease-specific factors may influence health literacy differently. Future research should examine such variations in greater depth.

Implications for practice and research

The findings have preliminary implications. They suggest potential strategies for healthcare systems preparing adolescents for adult care. First, transition programs may benefit from incorporating developmentally staged interventions that explicitly target communicative and critical literacy skills—such as independent healthcare communication, shared decision-making exercises, and health information appraisal training—rather than assuming that these skills naturally emerge from functional literacy.

Second, although parents generally demonstrate awareness of their adolescents’ capabilities, family-centered transition preparation may consider leveraging parental insights while simultaneously supporting parental health literacy and psychological readiness through psychoeducational interventions that may help reduce caregiver burden and enhance confidence in facilitating adolescent autonomy.

Third, routine assessment of health literacy in adolescents and parents may help identify dyads who could benefit from additional support; however, the feasibility and effectiveness of such approaches warrant further empirical evaluation. Transition programs may also consider including parent-focused components, offering education and psychosocial support to mitigate caregiving stress and enhance parents’ ability to guide their children toward independence.

Considering the time-intensive nature of transition care and the need for healthcare providers to prioritize feasible strategies, these implications should be interpreted cautiously. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to examine the relationship between health literacy and transition outcomes and identify which interventions most effectively support adolescents and families during the transition to adult-oriented care.

Conclusion

This study provides novel evidence that health literacy development in chronically ill adolescents during transition to adulthood requires domain-specific attention, as functional reading skills develop independently from higher-order communicative and critical competencies essential for autonomous healthcare navigation. While adolescents demonstrated adequate baseline literacy levels, the independence of functional from advanced domains underscores the need for targeted interventions addressing interactive and analytical skills beyond basic comprehension. Parents’ accurate perceptions of their adolescents’ capabilities position families as valuable partners in transition preparation, although parental health literacy support should be integrated into family-centered interventions to optimize dyadic functioning. Strengthening communicative and critical health literacy through structured healthcare engagement opportunities may enhance successful transition outcomes and long-term health autonomy for young adults with chronic conditions. Parents’ literacy was lower than population averages, likely reflecting the burden of caregiving. Despite parental concerns, their evaluations of adolescents’ literacy levels largely aligned with adolescents’ self-reports, offering a foundation for joint efforts to foster independence. Strengthening communicative and critical literacy through healthcare provider support may enhance successful transitions to adult care.
