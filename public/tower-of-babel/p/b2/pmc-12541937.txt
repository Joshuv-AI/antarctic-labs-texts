Association between long-term exposure to air pollutant mixture and the risk of Alzheimer’s disease among older people: a prospective cohort study in China
Authors: Hao Fu, Tao Zhang, Xue Gu, Fudong Li, Mingjuan Jin, Fan He, Fangrong Fei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlzheimer’s disease (AD) has garnered significant attention in recent years. However, evidence of the association between air pollutant mixture and AD from prospective studies has not been systematically explored. We aimed to examine the associations of combined exposure to nitrogen dioxide (NO2), sulfur dioxide (SO2), ozone (O3), and particulate matter (PM) with diameters of ≤ 2.5 μm (PM2.5) or ≤ 10 μm (PM10) with AD, and to further investigate the potential modifiable effects of lifestyle factors and air pollution.MethodsOur study was based on the Zhejiang Province Major Public Health Surveillance (ZJMPHS) Program. The concentrations of five air pollutants were estimated via the empirical Bayesian kriging (EBK) spatial interpolation method, and we applied a weighted joint air pollution score (JAPS) to evaluate their combined effects. AD was ascertained according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders and Statistics (DSM-IV) and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Disorders Association-Alzheimer’s Criteria (NINCDS-ADRDA). The Cox proportional hazards models were used to explore the associations between air pollution and AD.ResultsA total of 10,055 participants aged ≥ 60 years were included in the analysis. During a follow-up period of 34,764 person-years, 879 AD cases were identified. SO2, PM2.5, and PM10 were positively associated with AD, with HRs of 1.11 (95% CI, 1.09, 1.14), 1.03 (95% CI, 1.02, 1.05) and 1.03 (95% CI, 1.02, 1.04), respectively. High JAPS (based on the median) was related to higher risk of AD (HR = 2.16, 95% CI: 1.87, 2.51). The extremely high JAPS (based on the third percentile) significantly increased the risk of AD (HR = 2.31, 95% CI: 2.01, 2.65). Significant (or borderline) interactions between JAPS and exercise (p = 0.009) and living alone (p = 0.058) were found, which both amplify the incidence density of AD in extremely high JAPS.ConclusionsThis study suggested that high concentration of air pollutant mixture could increase the risk of AD. Moreover, it is important to weigh the impact of exercise under extremely high air pollution and pay attention to those who live alone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24837-z.Keywords: Air pollution, Alzheimer’s disease, Combined effect, Cohort study

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), which is characterized by an array of progressively worsening neurodegenerative manifestations (e.g., episodic memory deterioration, successive cognitive impairment and behavioral changes) [1], has been confirmed to pose a substantial health burden among the aging population. AD is the predominant form of dementia, and more than 50 million people were affected worldwide in 2020, which is predicted to more than triple by 2050 [2]. In China, the prevalence of AD is 3.21% among people aged 65 years or older [3]. Given the aging population and the lack of a cure for AD, there is a growing interest in pinpointing modifiable environmental risk factors for research [4].

Air pollution has been identified as a notable environmental risk factor for AD [5, 6]. Understanding the role of environmental factors in AD is crucial for reducing the burden of disease. Although increasing evidence has established a link with AD, some studies indicate a positive association [1, 7, 8], whereas others suggest the opposite [9, 10], most of the studies above focused only on a single air pollutant. Notably, the air pollution that individuals encounter in real life is a mixture of multiple components that coexist and can interact in a complex way, taking them together into consideration should be factual in reflecting the effects of air pollution [11]. However, the combined effects of these constituents have not been systematically examined. In addition, most studies above that focus on the Chinese population are cross-sectional, leading to a dearth of compelling longitudinal evidence.

In this study, we used data from a large-scale prospective cohort in eastern of China to investigate the associations between air pollutant mixture (including nitrogen dioxide (NO2), sulfur dioxide (SO2), ozone (O3), particulate matter (PM) with a diameter of ≤ 2.5 μm (PM2.5), and ≤ 10 μm (PM10)) and the risk of AD. Additionally, analyses stratified by lifestyle factors were conducted to examine whether lifestyle factors modified the effects of air pollution on AD.

Methods

Study design and participants

The Zhejiang Province Major Public Health Surveillance (ZJMPHS) Program is a large population-based prospective cohort study conducted by the Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control since 2014 and aimed at monitoring health conditions and their determinants among older people. Details of the study design have been described previously [12–14]. Briefly, we randomly selected 11 counties (Northern: Binjiang District, Nanxun District, Haiyan County and Tongxiang County), Southern: Yuhuan County and Jingning County, Middle: Yiwu city and Yuecheng District, Eastern: Haishu District and Putuo District, and Western: Changshan County) from 90 counties according to the distribution of Zhejiang Province (Supplementary material Figure S1).

A sample of 14,881 participants aged over 60 years, after being interviewed by local community general practitioners (GPs) in the community hospital or at home, participated in the baseline survey from 2014 to 2019. The process of subject selection was shown in Supplementary material Figure S2. We excluded 52 participants whose cognitive information was missing at baseline and 2449 participants whose cognitive function disorders were present at baseline. We further excluded participants with no follow-up records (n = 1145), those with dementia due to other causes (n = 253), those who lost at least one air pollution data (n = 580), and those who lost at least one covariate (n = 347). Finally, a total of 10,055 eligible participants were included in our analysis. All participants provided informed written consent before data collection.

Follow-up and assessment of outcomes

We conducted an annual follow-up until the date of diagnosis of AD, death, loss to follow-up, or December 31st 2021, whichever came first. First, we used the Chinese version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) to assess the cognitive function of each participant. The cut-off points for a cognitive impairment diagnosis in each group were less than or equal to 17 for the illiterate group, less than or equal to 20 for the primary school group and less than or equal to 24 for the middle school and above groups. Second, those with cognitive impairment were further diagnosed with AD and other types of dementia by two neurologists according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders and Statistics (DSM-IV) and National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Disorders Association-Alzheimer’s Criteria (NINCDS-ADRDA).

Estimation of air pollutants and joint air pollution scores

Zhejiang Province, China, has a total of 166 air quality monitoring stations evenly distributed across all counties and districts. Daily concentrations of air pollutants, including NO2 (µg/m3), SO2 (µg/m3), O3 (µg/m3), PM2.5 (µg/m3) and PM10 (µg/m3), were measured in the counties and districts where participants resided. First, we obtained these data of atmospheric environmental monitoring of 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, and 2019 from Department of Ecology and Environment of Zhejiang Province. Then, based on the pollutant concentrations detected by each monitoring station, we used the empirical Bayesian kriging (EBK) spatial interpolation to achieve higher spatial resolution of pollutant exposure levels. This allowed us to estimate more precise exposure levels for individuals. The performance of the interpolation was also validated by using Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV). The detailed information about this method and results were displayed in Supplementary material Text S1. For five air pollutant concentrations, the validation results suggested that the interpolation accuracy was high and the fitting performance is satisfactory. The annual average concentrations of air pollutants within the same county were assigned to participants who lived in the same place. Finally, we average five years of these air pollutants from 2014 through 2019 to determine baseline exposure levels for analysis.

To comprehensively estimate the associations between joint exposure to various air pollutants and AD, we established a joint air pollution score (JAPS) [15]. This method has been utilized in many studies and shown that this combined index is more accurate in reflecting the exposure levels, which could make epidemiological findings easier to be interpreted. Covariate measurement

Demographic information and lifestyle data that could distort the relationships between air pollution and the risk of AD were collected by trained healthcare staff under strict quality control via standardized questionnaires. In our study, we included age (60-, 65-, 75-, ≥85), sex (male and female), education (illiterate, primary school, middle school, high school and above), marital status (married, and unmarried/widowed/divorced), family income (RMB per year, < 10,000, 10,000-, 20,000-, 50,000-, and ≥100,000), working status (never worked, working and retired), body mass index (BMI) [normal (18.50–23.99 kg/m2), underweight (< 18.50 kg/m2), and overweight/obesity (≥ 24.0 kg/m2)], hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, smoking (never, current and past), alcohol drinking (never, current and past), exercise (taking part in physical exercise in your spare time) (no or yes), and living alone (no one lives with you over 1 year) (no or yes).

Statistical analysis

We present continuous variables as the means standard deviations (SDs) or medians (interquartile ranges (IQRs)) and categorical variables as the frequencies (percentages). The Schoenfeld residuals were used to verify the proportional hazards assumption. Cox proportional hazard models were applied to investigate the associations between exposure to air pollutant mixture and AD, providing hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The models were adjusted, in part or in full, for age, sex, education, working status, marital status, family income, BMI, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes, as well as smoking, alcohol consumption, exercise, and living alone. The analysis of the time-exposure relationship between JAPS and AD were also included to further explore the temporal relationship between JAPS and the risk of AD, including cumulative exposure concentrations over five years, with weighted adjustments for each exposure year (weights of 0.5 for 2014 and 2015, 0.3 for 2016 and 2017, 0.2 for 2018 and 2019), as well as setting the exposure window of JAPS into early stage (2014–2015), middle stage (2016–2017), and late stage (2018–2019). Additionally, we added the analysis of cumulative hazard AD based on different quartile of JAPS using Kaplan–Meier method.

For the stratified analysis, we first analyzed lifestyles (smoking, alcohol consumption, exercise and living alone) and observed the effects of JAPS on AD in each stratum. We subsequently used multiplicative interaction terms to examine whether lifestyle factors modified the effects of air pollution on AD. We also performed subgroup analyses by age (< 65, ≥65) and sex (male and female) to control for the impact of confounders.

For sensitivity analysis, we validated the robustness of the results via the following methods: (1) using only average air pollution concentrations reported in 2015, (2) excluding participants diagnosed with AD for less than 2 years, and (3) selecting five counties (one county from each of the five regions of Zhejiang Province) with a population of more than 1000 people (Northern: Haiyan County, Jiaxing City, Eastern: Haishu County, Ningbo City, Southern: Yuhuan County, Taizhou City, Middle: Yuecheng District, Shaoxing City, Western: Changshan County, Quzhou City) for analysis.

All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed via R software (version 4.2.1).

Results

The baseline characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 1. A total of 10,055 participants were involved in our study. During the 34,764 person-year follow-up period (average 3.46 years per person), 879 (8.74%) AD cases were identified, with an incidence density of 25.28 per 1,000 person-years. Compared with non-AD participants, those diagnosed with AD tended to be older, female, have poor education, be unmarried/widowed/divorced, have lower family income, be retired, be underweight, be more likely to have hypertension, be less likely to smoke, drink alcohol, engage in less exercise, and be more likely to live alone. In addition, the median (interquartile range, IQR) estimates of NO2, SO2, O3, PM2.5, and PM10 were 31.17 (8.55), 13.14 (4.39), 94.60 (12.75), 40.84 (5.30) and 66.24 (12.40) µg/m3, respectively. In general, both NO2 and SO2 were highly correlated with PM, and PM2.5 and PM10 were also highly correlated, whereas weak correlations were observed between O3 and other air pollutants, as well as between NO2 and SO2 (Table 2). The proportional hazards assumption results all indicated that the hypotheses were satisfied (all P > 0.05) (Supplementary material Figure S3).

The associations between each air pollutant and AD in the single-exposure models are shown in Table 3. In Model 2, increased per 1 µg/m3 in SO2, PM2.5, and PM10 were associated with HRs of 1.11 (95% CI, 1.09, 1.14), 1.03 (95% CI, 1.02, 1.05) and 1.03 (95% CI, 1.02, 1.04) for AD. Taking JAPS into analysis (Table 4), we found that the HR for JAPS per 1 µg/m3 was 1.01 (95% CI, 1.01, 1.02) in the fully adjusted model. When the JAPS score was divided into low and high groups on the basis of the median (139.79 µg/m3), the high group was positively related to the risk of AD (HR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.87, 2.51) in Model 2. When JAPS was divided into low and extremely high groups on the basis of the third percentile (Q75) (154.99 µg/m3), the risk of AD significantly increased in the extremely high group (HR = 2.31, 95% CI: 2.01, 2.65). From the analysis of the time-exposure relationship between JAPS and AD, we found that participants with higher cumulative JAPS exposure had a significant greater risk of developing AD compared to those in the low group. Early exposure to higher concentrations of JAPS, especially for the first two years, was strongly associated with an increased risk of AD development (Supplementary material Table S2). As for cumulative AD hazard, regardless of JAPS level based on different quartile, we observed a significant increase in cumulative hazard at two years of follow-up. Notably, the cumulative hazard in the high and extremely high JAPS group was approaching three-fold higher than that in the low JAPS group (Supplementary material Figure S4).

Stratified analyses, as displayed in Table 5, revealed that extremely high JAPS was related to an increased risk of AD compared with the low group and was consistent across all lifestyle factor strata. Next, we generated interaction models to evaluate whether there were interactions between lifestyle factors and JAPS. Using multiplicative interaction models, we identified significant interactions between JAPS and exercise (p = 0.009) and a borderline significant interaction between JAPS and living alone (p = 0.058). When we evaluated the continuous trait of incidence density of AD, something interesting was found. First, among those who exercise, 49.69 per 1,000 person years with extremely high JAPS compared to 12.92 per 1,000 person years with low JAPS (difference = 36.77 per 1,000 person years); in those who do not exercise, the corresponding values are 37.32 and 22.42 per 1,000 person years (difference = 14.90 per 1,000 person years); While participants who live alone with extremely high JAPS, 100.58 per 1,000 person years compared to 35.50 per 1,000 person years with low JAPS (difference = 65.08 per 1,000 person years); in participants who do not live alone, the corresponding values are 32.51 and 18.85 per 1,000 person years (difference = 13.66 per 1,000 person years). This finding seemed to suggest that exercise and living alone amplified the harmful effects on AD associated with extremely high JAPS (Fig. 1).

In other subgroup analyses (Supplementary material Table S3), high and extremely high JAPS increased the risk of AD regardless of age and sex. Discussion

To our knowledge, this prospective cohort study based on the ZJMPHS Program represents the first attempt to investigate the effects of a mixture of air pollutants on AD in China. Our findings revealed that a significantly increased risk of AD was associated with long-term exposure to high/extremely high JAPS. Furthermore, exercise and living alone could amplify the harmful effects on AD patients when air pollution is extremely high.

In our study, we found that SO2 could significantly increase the risk of AD. Although the epidemiological evidence on SO2 and elevated risk of AD remains limited, existing studies have implied that exposure to high concentrations of SO2 not only causes adverse respiratory responses [16, 17] but is also strongly linked to neurological dysfunction [18, 19]. Previous studies have shown that the expression of markers associated with neurodegenerative disease pathology increases with PM, which plays a pivotal role in the causation of AD [20, 21]. Our study revealed that long-term exposure to PM2.5 and PM10 increased the risk of AD in older adults. A study utilizing the Medicare Chronic Conditions Warehouse for Older Americans reported a 9% increase in AD with long-term exposure to PM2.5 in America [22]. Another cohort reported that the rate ratio (RR) was 2.14 (95% CI, 1.00, 4.56) in people exposed to ≥ 64.5 μg/m3 PM2.5 versus those exposed to < 63.5 μg/m3 PM2.5 [23]. There are few studies on PM10 and AD. A cross-sectional study revealed an increased OR (3.00, 95% CI: 1.22, 7.41) for PM10 and AD [24], whereas another prospective study from the UK biobank revealed that chronic exposure to PM10 was not associated with the rate of AD, which was inconsistent with the proposed hypothesis that PM10 may increase the risk of AD [25]. A potential explanation for this disparity was that the median PM10 concentration of 66.24 μg/m3 in our study significantly exceeded the median concentration of 15 μg/m3 observed in the previous study, thereby amplifying the impact of PM10 on AD.

A meta-analysis indicated that both NO2 (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.89, 1.13) and O3 (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.68, 1.57) were not significantly associated with AD at 10 μg/m3 increments [26]. Our study was in line with the above findings. However, the conclusions of studies on the associations between NO2, O3 and AD are not unified. A meta-analysis including six studies indicated that higher NO2 could increase the risk of AD (OR = 1.18, 95% CI 1.10, 1.28) [23], whereas another study indicated that higher concentrations of O3 had an adverse effect on AD [27]. These differences may be due to the measurement of air pollutant concentrations via different methods and inconsistencies in the time and year in which the concentrations were obtained. Notably, NO2 and O3 can be transferred from the respiratory system to the brain via the bloodstream while inhaled, resulting in the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress and inflammation, leading to degeneration of hippocampal neurons and AD-related pathological conditions [28–30]; therefore, we still need to pay attention to the potential pathological threat of NO2 and O3 in AD.

Studies on the combined effects of air pollutants and AD are rare. Chen et al. reported that the highest quartile of the air pollution score was related to a higher risk of AD (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.14, 1.43), which they combined PM2.5, PM2.5–10, PM10, NO2, and NOx derived from the UK Biobank database [31]. Fu and colleagues reported significant associations between combined air pollution effects and AD incidence (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.09, 1.61) [26], which is consistent with our findings. Notably, the effect size we obtained is significantly larger than that of the aforementioned study, likely because the median concentration of JAPS in our study reached 139.61 µg/m3, especially at extremely high concentrations with HR = 2.30, which further underscores the harmful effects of high concentrations of air pollution.

We found a multiplicative interaction between exercise and JAPS, as well as a borderline interaction between living alone and JAPS. Furthermore, in terms of incidence density, we found that exercise and living alone could amplify the harmful effects of extremely high air pollution on AD. Research has revealed that air pollution may affect physical activity (PA) behavior and may negate some or all the benefits associated with PA, especially in highly polluted locations [32–34]. However, some studies have shown that the benefits of PA remain in people irrespective of exposure to PM2.5 with dementia or AD [35, 36]; these differences may be attributed to our comprehensive consideration of the combined effects of mixed air pollutants. Additionally, we defined exercise merely as a “yes-or-no” variable, without considering the intensity of the PA, so the results serve more as an indication. Long-term exposure to multiple air pollutants is associated with an increased risk of depression and anxiety [37]. People who live alone usually experience heightened feelings of loneliness and social isolation, which have been proven to be risk factors for AD [38, 39]. This makes it easy to understand how the harmful effects of high air pollution concentrations, when combined with the negative impacts of living alone, can significantly increase the risk of developing AD.

Our study has several strengths, including a prospective design and a large sample size with a long-term follow-up period in China. First, we applied the novel approach of considering the combined effects of mixed air pollutants. Some limitations also exist that need further elaboration. First, we included only participants who resided in Zhejiang Province, and the generalizability of our findings should be cautious. Second, we measured air pollutants only on the basis of residential information recorded at baseline and did not consider the impact of changes in residence on variations in air pollution concentrations. However, our study population was elderly, and their residences were relatively stable during the follow-up period; thus, the influence of residential mobility was deemed minimal. Third, the composition of air pollution is complex, and we did not consider the effects of other components, such as nitrogen oxides and carbon monoxide, on AD. However, the air pollutants we included represented relatively major air pollutants. Fourth, the creation of the weighted JAPS presupposes a linear correlation between each air pollutant and AD. Although the relationship may be nonlinear, it is impossible to create a simple weighted score by examining the nonlinear relationship between them.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study establishes a clear positive association between air pollutant mixture and the risk of AD. Moreover, it is important to weigh the impact of exercise under extremely high air pollution and pay attention to those who live alone. These findings, derived from a Chinese cohort, contribute valuable insights and underscore the critical need for concerted efforts by both policy makers and individuals aiming at enhancing the prevention of AD among elderly people.
