Wild, scenic, and toxic: Recent degradation of an iconic Arctic watershed with permafrost thaw
Authors: Patrick F. Sullivan, Roman J. Dial, David J. Cooper, Charles Diamond, Christopher J. Tino, Daniel D. Gregory, Russell E. Wong, Timothy W. Lyons
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe Salmon River is among the most remote and protected watersheds in North America. Its pristine wilderness was featured in the opening chapter of John McPhee’s nonfiction classic, “Coming into the Country”. In 2019, the gin-clear waters of the Salmon turned orange and have remained turbid and discolored since. The sudden degradation of the Salmon and many other formerly clear streams in the region is thought to reflect sulfide mineral weathering following permafrost thaw. Here, we show the Salmon River mainstem and most of its tributaries have metal concentrations deemed toxic to aquatic life. Our findings might help explain a recent crash in chum salmon returns, which are an important commercial and subsistence resource in an economically challenged region.

In the opening chapter of the nonfiction classic “Coming into the Country”, John McPhee chronicled his 1975 reconnaissance of the Salmon River in northwest Alaska (Fig. 1). McPhee and his companions descended the Salmon in canoes and kayaks to assess its suitability for Wild and Scenic status – a designation providing rivers of “outstandingly remarkable value” with long-term federal protection. They found abundant Arctic grayling (Thymallus arcticus) and Dolly Varden (Salvelinus malma), a rich run of chum salmon (Oncorhynchus keta), and “the clearest, purest water I have ever seen flowing over rocks,” which allowed them to “see down 15 feet in pools” (1). It was a quintessential Alaskan wilderness trip, made complete with outstanding scenery and benign bear encounters, that cemented the Salmon’s Wild and Scenic status. The entire Salmon River watershed received further protection during the establishment of Kobuk Valley National Park as part of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act in 1980.

The Salmon River has changed. Routine National Park Service (NPS) monitoring (2, 3) and local bush pilot observations (4) suggest the Salmon was running clear through the fall of 2018 (Fig. 2). During the summer of 2019, the clear waters of the Salmon turned distinctly orange and have remained discolored and turbid since. The river runs clear for ~20 km from its mountain headwaters to Kanaktok Creek, which is the first of many affected tributaries. The subsequent ~100 river kilometers from Kanaktok to the confluence of the Salmon with the Kobuk River are characterized by a mix of clear and visibly affected tributaries. Embedded within a ~700,000 ha national park without roads or maintained trails, the Salmon River remains wild, but its scenic attributes are diminished, and the productivity of the ecosystem that it supports is in question.

The Salmon River is not alone. A recent inventory in Alaska’s Brooks Range identified 75 streams that have recently turned orange and turbid (5). This sudden widespread degradation of formerly pristine Arctic streams is hypothesized to reflect exposure of sulfide minerals in underlying bedrock to weathering following permafrost thaw (6). When dissolved oxygen in groundwater interacts with newly exposed sulfide minerals, such as the iron-sulfide mineral pyrite (FeS2), it can produce sulfuric acid, which leaches metals from the parent material. Pyrite contains trace metals substituted into its lattice (e.g., Ni and Cu) and within inclusions (e.g., Zn and Cd), which can lead to release of a great diversity and abundance of metals (7, 8). Pyrite is also frequently associated with other sulfide minerals, such as arsenopyrite, chalcopyrite, galena, and sphalerite, which contain abundant As, Cu, Pb, Zn, and Cd. Groundwater flow and surface seeps from sites of sulfide oxidation (SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2) can transport a suite of potentially toxic metals to stream ecosystems in a process analogous to acid mine drainage. During transport in both ground and stream water, the pH is progressively buffered by carbonate-dominated parent material (9) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), promoting the transition from clear acidic water with high dissolved metal concentrations to turbid circumneutral water with abundant Fe and Al oxyhydroxide particulates and other metals in solution.

While many Arctic streams have recently been impacted, we have a limited understanding of the metals involved, their concentrations, and their potential impacts on aquatic organisms. Here, we report metal concentrations from the major tributaries (SI Appendix, Figs S3–S12) and main stem of the Salmon River, from its headwaters to its mouth, on three sampling dates between August of 2022 and August of 2023. We then compared our measurements with predisturbance water chemistry data from routine NPS monitoring (3) and with chronic (4-d) exposure thresholds for toxicity to aquatic life established by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) for the State of Alaska (10). Our goal was to assess whether metal concentrations exceeded EPA thresholds and, if so, where (e.g., tributaries versus mainstem), when (high flow versus median flow), and what the consequences might be for an aquatic ecosystem that historically supported a robust chum salmon run of commercial and subsistence value.

Results

The Salmon River watershed (174,434 ha) has neither a gaging station to measure river discharge nor a weir or sonar to quantify salmon returns. However, three streams in the broader region are continuously monitored for discharge and vary in catchment area: Dahl Creek (2,823 ha), the Wulik River (182,594 ha), and the Kobuk River near Kiana, Alaska (2,455,309 ha) (SI Appendix, Table S1 and Fig. S13). We used discharge estimates from these gaged streams during our sampling periods and for the same dates over the past 30 y (1994–2023) to infer whether our Salmon River samples from August of 2022, July of 2023, and August of 2023 were collected during low, near median, or high discharge. Our August 2022 and August 2023 samples were likely collected during near median discharge for the Salmon River, while our July 2023 samples were collected during high flow, when discharges in Dahl Creek, the Wulik River, and the Kobuk River were close to the 90th, 80th, and 95th percentiles, respectively.

Dissolved SO42−and the molar ratio of SO42− to Ca in stream water are indicators of sulfide mineral oxidation within the watershed (11). SO42−: Ca is especially useful because it helps to distinguish between SO42− that is released from gypsum or anhydrite dissolution, in which SO42−and Ca are released in a 1:1 molar ratio, versus sulfide mineral oxidation, which produces SO42− without releasing Ca (12). Dissolved SO42− and SO42−: Ca were much higher in the Salmon River main stem (Fig. 3) and in most major tributaries (Table 1) than values observed at the Salmon River mouth in August of 2013 and in the unaffected Salmon River headwaters during 2023. Only the low elevation tributaries (Kitlik and “Paluqtaq”) had low SO42− concentration and low SO42−: Ca, suggesting more limited influence of sulfide mineral oxidation in their watersheds.

Stream water pH was generally circumneutral to basic at the mouths of the tributaries and along the main stem of the Salmon (Table 1), reflecting the abundance of carbonates within the watershed and their strong pH buffering capacity (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The two exceptions were “Savilhaq” and “Kannuyaq”, which drain small watersheds in the upper river and have an abundance of known acid seeps (sites of sulfide mineral oxidation) close to their confluence with the Salmon. Both tributaries had pH values less than 5.0, and Savilhaq had a pH of just 3.9 when sampled under what was probably near median flow in August of 2023.

The EPA has established threshold concentrations for potential toxic effects on aquatic life for Al, As, Cd, Cu, Fe, Ni, Pb, Se, and Zn in Alaska (10). Arsenic, Pb, and Se were below their respective chronic (4-d) thresholds for toxicity to aquatic life in all Salmon River tributary and main stem samples. However, Al, Fe, Cd, Cu, Ni, and Zn concentrations exceeded the EPA chronic aquatic toxicity thresholds in at least some of our samples. We refer to metal concentrations in unfiltered water samples as “total recoverable metals”, as they include both dissolved and particulate metals. Meanwhile, we refer to metal concentrations in filtered water samples as “dissolved metals” (0.45 µm filter). Total recoverable metals tended to be slightly greater during high than near-median discharge, while dissolved metals were slightly greater during near-median than during high discharge.

Total recoverable Al exceeded the EPA chronic toxicity threshold in all major tributaries sampled, except for Sheep, “Noname,” and Paluqtaq Creeks (Table 2). In the Salmon River, total recoverable Al exceeded the EPA chronic toxicity threshold from Savilhaq to Paluqtaq, reaching a peak immediately above the confluence of Sheep Creek with the Salmon, where total recoverable Al was 4.8x greater than the chronic toxicity threshold on July 19, 2023 (Fig. 3). Total recoverable Al was significantly lower at tributary and main stem water sampling locations where invertebrates were found beneath rocks in the streambed than at water sampling locations where no invertebrates were found during July of 2023 (T = 2.38, P = 0.03, SI Appendix, Fig. S14).

Because much of the total Fe load is in particulate form, which imparts the diagnostic orange color of affected waters, the difference between total recoverable Fe and dissolved Fe concentrations was closely correlated with field measurements of turbidity made in tributary and Salmon River samples during July of 2023 (n = 29, r = 0.95, P < 0.01). In August of 2022, when discharge was likely near the long-term median, total recoverable Fe exceeded the EPA chronic toxicity threshold of 1.0 ppm in the most visibly affected tributaries sampled (Kanaktok, Isuqtuq, and Anaktok). In July of 2023, during high discharge, total recoverable Fe exceeded EPA chronic toxicity thresholds in all but the relatively clear tributaries (Noname and Kitlik). In the Salmon River, total recoverable Fe followed a pattern nearly identical to that of total recoverable Al (n = 14, r = 0.99, P < 0.01), also peaking immediately above Sheep Creek at 4.1× greater than the EPA threshold on July 19, 2023. Total recoverable Fe concentrations remained above the EPA chronic toxicity threshold from below Savilhaq to the final ~10 river kilometers, where total recoverable Fe declined to just below the toxicity threshold. Total recoverable Fe was significantly lower at water sampling locations where invertebrates were found beneath rocks in the streambed than at July 2023 sampling locations where no invertebrates were found (T = 3.52, P < 0.01).

The EPA does not provide a chronic toxicity threshold for dissolved Fe in fresh waters of Alaska. However, the government of British Columbia, Canada, has a published threshold of 0.35 ppm (13). Dissolved Fe consistently exceeded the 0.35 ppm threshold in Kanaktok, Savilhaq, and Paluqtaq but not in the Salmon River. In July of 2023, the percentage of dissolved Fe averaged 25% of total Fe in eight tributaries of the Salmon but varied from less than 1% in Nikok (pH = 7.9) to 80% in Savilhaq (pH = 4.7). The percentage of dissolved Fe in the Salmon River (n = 10 sampling locations) was 30 to 45% in the clear headwaters above Kanaktok but declined to <10% with inflow from affected tributaries. In the Salmon River’s final ~10 km, percent dissolved Fe increased slightly to 10% upstream of Paluqtaq and to 13% near its confluence with the Kobuk.

The toxicity to aquatic life of Cd, Cu, Ni, and Zn is dependent upon water hardness (10, 14) because Ca and Mg compete with other metals for binding sites in aquatic organisms, such as the gills of freshwater fish (15), thereby reducing bioavailability. Dissolved Cd concentrations exceeded the EPA chronic toxicity threshold in all major tributaries of the Salmon, except for Sheep and Noname Creeks during high discharge in July of 2023 (Table 1). During closer-to-median discharge in August of 2023, dissolved Cd exceeded the EPA concentration threshold in all tributaries except for Sheep, Noname, and Paluqtaq. Savilhaq, with its small (12.9 ha) watershed, had dissolved Cd concentrations that were approximately an order of magnitude higher than in other tributaries. Dissolved Cd concentration in the Salmon mainstem exceeded the EPA chronic toxicity threshold from below Kanaktok to the Kobuk River, reaching a peak between Savilhaq and Kannuyaq, where the Cd concentration was 5.6× greater than the EPA threshold on August 10, 2023 (Fig. 3). While there was a tendency toward lower Cd concentrations at water sampling locations where invertebrates were found beneath rocks in the streambed, the difference was not statistically significant (T = 1.63, P = 0.12).

Dissolved Cu concentrations exceeded the EPA chronic toxicity threshold primarily in the small Savilhaq and Kannuyaq (5.7 ha) watersheds, where concentrations were approximately 50× greater than in the larger tributaries. Kanaktok, when it was sampled in August of 2023, was the only large tributary with a dissolved Cu concentration greater than the EPA chronic toxicity threshold. The Salmon River remained below the EPA chronic toxicity threshold for dissolved Cu from the headwaters to the mouth during July and August of 2023.

Dissolved Ni concentrations exceeded the EPA chronic toxicity threshold in Kanaktok and Savilhaq under both high (July 2023) and near-median discharge (August 2023). Kannuyaq was only sampled in August of 2023, when it exceeded the chronic toxicity threshold for Ni. Nikok exceeded the chronic toxicity threshold for Ni during near median discharge in August of 2023 but not during high discharge in July of 2023. The Salmon River was below the EPA chronic toxicity threshold for Ni throughout its course.

Dissolved Zn concentrations were measured only for the July 2023 samples. At that time, only Savilhaq and Kitlik exceeded the chronic toxicity threshold. Zinc toxicity is strongly inversely proportional to hardness, and Kitlik, a clear tributary, had very low hardness on all sampling occasions. Kitlik exceeded the EPA threshold for Zn toxicity with a concentration of only 62 ppb because its hardness was only 38 ppm. In contrast, Kanaktok had a Zn concentration of 285 ppb that did not exceed the chronic toxicity threshold because it had much greater hardness (561 ppm). The Salmon River varied in hardness from ~100 to ~400 ppm in July of 2023 and remained below the EPA chronic toxicity threshold for Zn throughout its course.

Discussion

Nine of ten major tributaries of the Salmon River that we sampled had toxic concentrations of at least one metal on at least one of our three sampling dates. Moreover, eight of the ten major tributaries had elevated SO42− and elevated SO42−: Ca, consistent with sulfide mineral weathering within their watersheds. Even the clear tributaries (Sheep and Kitlik) had at least one metal that exceeded EPA concentration thresholds for toxicity to aquatic life on at least one sampling date. Visible impairment was a function of turbidity, which was correlated with particulate Fe and Al concentrations. Turbid tributaries were consistently toxic, but water clarity was not a good predictor of metal concentrations. For example, Kitlik had clear water (July 2023 FNU = 2.2), but exceeded thresholds for total recoverable Al, dissolved Cd, and dissolved Zn. We hypothesize that tributaries like Kitlik and Nikok, which had elevated metal concentrations, but did not have orange turbid water, were also affected by sulfide mineral weathering. In these cases, the metal sources may have been far upstream, allowing particulate Fe to settle to the streambed while dissolved metals and smaller Al particulates remained within the water column (16). The locations of known upland acid seeps (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) are generally consistent with the hypothesis that orange, turbid stream water is more common closer to known metal sources. While Cu, Ni, and Zn toxicity was identified in some tributaries of the Salmon, the metals exceeding EPA thresholds in the Salmon River were total recoverable Al, total recoverable Fe, and dissolved Cd. All other metals remained below EPA chronic aquatic toxicity thresholds in the Salmon River from its headwaters to its mouth.

Aluminum is abundant in the Earth’s crust, but it lacks a known biological function (17). It is highly soluble at low pH, including sites of sulfide oxidation, but relatively insoluble in water at circumneutral pH (16–18), where it exists primarily as Al hydroxide (18). Aluminum also forms inorganic complexes with phosphate that are so effective in scavenging dissolved P that they are used for industrial water treatment and to remove excess P from aquatic ecosystems (19). Phosphorus is often a limiting nutrient for primary production in Arctic freshwater ecosystems (20). Thus, scavenging dissolved P from the water column by Al hydroxides may have a strong impact on the base of the aquatic food web. Aquatic invertebrates are generally thought to be relatively insensitive to Al (18). Fish eggs are also relatively insensitive to Al (21); however, fish gills can be highly sensitive, with toxicity derived from ion-regulatory effects and/or respiratory impairment from accumulation of precipitated Al on the gills (22). Ion-regulatory effects of dissolved Al should be more pronounced at low pH, while respiratory impairment from precipitated Al is expected to dominate at circumneutral and basic pH (23). There is some evidence that fish may be able to acclimate to high concentrations of Al, albeit with substantial metabolic costs (24). While Al can bioaccumulate in tissues of aquatic organisms, there is little evidence of Al toxicity to fish when exposed through diet (25).

Iron is also abundant in the Earth’s crust, and it can be an important limiting element for aquatic primary production, especially in marine ecosystems. Iron in freshwater ecosystems exists primarily as either Fe(II) (ferrous) or Fe(III) (ferric), depending upon pH and redox potential. Under anoxic conditions at circumneutral pH, Fe(II) is the dominant species. Meanwhile, under higher oxygen concentration and low pH, Fe(III) dominates. As water flows downslope from a site of sulfide oxidation, the pH increases and Fe(III) precipitates as Fe-oxyhydroxide(s), creating orange turbid waters. The effects of Fe on aquatic ecosystems operate through physical, chemical, and biological pathways. Turbidity associated with Fe(III) precipitates reduces light penetration and visibility within the water column, potentially limiting primary productivity, foraging by aquatic organisms, and predation of fish by birds and mammals. Some of the Fe precipitates settle on the streambed, where they can limit benthic primary and secondary productivity (26). Fe(III) precipitates have also been shown to limit algal production indirectly by complexing with P (27). The most important mechanism by which Fe impacts fish is through accumulation of Fe(III) precipitates on gill surfaces, where they limit oxygen diffusion, may cause fusion of gill laminae (28), and can induce potentially lethal respiratory distress (29). In addition to Fe(III) precipitates settling on the streambed and impacting the benthos, we also observed extensive development of what appear to be Fe-stimulated microbial mats (30), which can dramatically alter the benthic environment and have a large effect on habitat quality for aquatic invertebrates and salmon spawning success.

Acid rock drainage is not the only mechanism by which large quantities of Fe can be delivered from terrestrial to aquatic ecosystems. Provided that Fe is present within the sediments or underlying bedrock of wetland ecosystems, development of anoxic soil conditions can promote microbe-mediated reduction of relatively immobile Fe(III) to the more soluble and mobile Fe(II). Fe(II) can then be delivered to aquatic ecosystems in anoxic groundwater flow and reoxidize once exposed to the atmosphere. This process has recently been observed along gradients of permafrost thaw in wetlands of northern Sweden (31) and Siberia (32). The Paluqtaq watershed is dominated by wetlands, and the stream is interrupted by numerous beaver dams, which inundate the surrounding landscape with water, further increasing the potential for anoxic soil conditions. Microbe-mediated Fe(III) reduction under anoxic conditions may be largely responsible for the heavy Fe load observed in Paluqtaq Creek in the lower Salmon watershed, especially given the very low SO42− concentrations, very low SO42−: Ca, and the generally low metal concentrations observed in July and August of 2023. The 1975 Salmon River reconnaissance found that Paluqtaq carried “reddish, murky colored water” (1), further indicating that its degradation is not a result of recently enhanced sulfide mineral oxidation with permafrost thaw. However, the very low SO42−concentrations likely reflect its reduction to H2S and gaseous efflux under anoxic soil conditions, which means that we cannot completely rule out the possibility of some background level of sulfide mineral weathering in the Paluqtaq watershed.

Cadmium is rare in aquatic ecosystems and highly toxic to aquatic organisms (33). However, its concentration surpassed the EPA chronic toxicity threshold in most tributaries and in the Salmon River from the first major tributary (Kanaktok) to the Kobuk River. Cadmium is present almost exclusively in dissolved form, with exposure occurring directly through water and/or diet among aquatic organisms. Aquatic invertebrates are considered more prone to Cd exposure through diet, while fish are highly sensitive to Cd accumulation on gill surfaces (34). There is limited evidence of toxicity from diet-borne Cd among fish, although Cd has been shown to accumulate in the kidneys (35), perhaps in nonbioactive form (34). Consumption of fish tissues with high Cd concentrations may lead to oxidative stress, DNA damage, and impairment of DNA repair among mammals (36). The most important source of Cd toxicity among fish is the accumulation of water-borne Cd on gill surfaces, where Cd competes with calcium for binding sites and can lead to acute Ca deficiency and death (37). Cadmium exposure can also have neurotoxic effects on fish, leading to erratic and/or maladaptive behaviors that ultimately lead to mortality (38). Pacific salmon and Dolly Varden are among—if not the most—sensitive species to Cd exposure (34).

The Salmon River is not the only designated Wild and Scenic River in Alaska’s Brooks Range to be affected by recent metal loading. The Alatna and John Rivers, both tributaries of the Koyukuk River, have several affected tributaries. Kevuk Creek (a tributary of the John River, SI Appendix, Fig. S15) and the Kutuk River (a tributary of the Alatna, SI Appendix, Fig. S16) were among the first streams in the Brooks Range to show visible signs of impairment (orange and turbid). The Kutuk is heavily impacted and showed signs of degradation as early as August of 2006.

Observations of a headwater tributary in the nearby Akillik watershed revealed strong declines in periphyton biomass, macroinvertebrate density, and a complete loss of juvenile fish when comparing data from before and after degradation (5). Similarly, our observations of invertebrate presence/absence in tributaries and the main stem of the Salmon River showed that stream reaches where invertebrates were found beneath rocks in the streambed had significantly lower total recoverable Fe and Al concentrations.

The Salmon River has almost certainly provided historically important spawning habitat for chum salmon returning to Kotzebue Sound and the Kobuk River watershed (1). Between 1962 and 2023, the annual Kotzebue Sound commercial salmon harvest averaged 231,196 fish—the vast majority of which were chum—distributed across an annual average of 105 permits (39). The record harvest of 695,153 fish in 2018 was valued at more than $2.25 M, highlighting the importance of the harvest as a renewable revenue source in an economically challenged region. Salmon returns to Kotzebue Sound also represent a valuable subsistence resource that is third in importance to residents of northwest Alaska, behind caribou and sheefish (40). While subsistence salmon harvest estimates are not as accurate as commercial harvest data, annual subsistence harvest has been estimated at 60,000 or more chum salmon in the region (41).

Chum salmon use freshwater habitats during their first fall, winter, and spring, as eggs, embryos, alevin, and fry. Upon emergence from the gravel in the spring, salmon fry migrate to the ocean where they spend several years before returning to spawn in freshwater streams, generally at an age of 4 to 5 y in Kotzebue Sound (42). The most likely impact of stream degradation on chum salmon productivity is thus on spawning and the survival of eggs, alevin, and fry (collectively referred to as “spawning success”). Although a few nearby streams, such as Tukpahlearik and Nakolikurok Creeks, turned orange and turbid as early as 2008, widespread stream degradation in northwest Alaska was first observed during the summers of 2019 and 2020 (5). If the recent proliferation of degraded streams has impacted chum salmon spawning success and subsequent returns in the region, then the first signs of reduced salmon returns would be expected among returning 4-y-olds in 2023 and among returning 4- and 5-y-olds in 2024. This prediction closely matches observed returns. The commercial harvest in 2023 (141,781 salmon) was slightly below the long-term mean, while the total harvest in 2024 (5,392 salmon) and the harvest per permit (225 salmon) were the lowest since the State of Alaska began managing the Kotzebue Sound fishery in 1962 (39) (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). It may never be possible to conclusively link recent stream degradation to chum salmon productivity in Kotzebue Sound because of the lack of historical escapement data for watersheds like the Salmon River. Moreover, it is possible that chum salmon might shift their use of spawning habitat to other streams in the region that have not been affected, provided there is enough high-quality underutilized habitat available. Nevertheless, the numerous mechanisms by which salmon spawning success could be affected by recent widespread metal loading of streams in Arctic Alaska are clear and beg for immediate baseline studies in streams that are at risk but have not yet been affected.

The Salmon River watershed spans a steep bioclimatic gradient near the southern limits of the Arctic in a region characterized by dramatic recent changes in climate, flora, and fauna (43, 44). Our observations point to the potential for widespread future stream degradation with permafrost thaw further north and elsewhere at high latitudes. Watersheds with unweathered permafrost-protected sulfide minerals in upland areas are expected to be most strongly impacted. Further, if permafrost thaw promotes activation of microbial communities in anoxic soils of lowlands with an abundance of Fe(III), then more widespread fluxes of Fe to aquatic ecosystems are anticipated. While the effects of metal loading on the aquatic ecosystems of headwater streams and tributaries are expected to be strongly negative, uncertainty remains regarding the downstream effects in larger rivers and nearshore marine environments.

Materials and Methods

The Salmon River watershed is extremely remote because of its location and its inaccessibility. There are no reliable locations to land a bush plane within the watershed. Access is generally gained either by helicopter, if permitted by the National Park Service (NPS), or on foot from well outside of the watershed. In August of 2022 and July of 2023, we accessed the headwaters of the Salmon River on foot. In August of 2023, we took advantage of an NPS-permitted helicopter working in the broader area. In all three instances, we descended the river from its headwaters to its mouth using small (< 4 kg) inflatable packrafts (Alpacka Raft, Mancos, CO).

Water samples were collected from the tributaries and main stem of the Salmon River during mid-August of 2022, when discharge was likely near the long-term median (SI Appendix, Table S1), during late July of 2023, when discharge was high, and finally during mid-August of 2023, when discharge was again likely near the long-term median. In July of 2023, field measurements of pH, specific conductivity, dissolved oxygen, and turbidity (FNU) were made at each water sampling location using a YSI ProDSS multiparameter meter. The YSI ProDSS sensors were calibrated in the laboratory prior to departure for the field. In August of 2023, measurements of pH and specific conductivity were made at each sampling location using a Thermo Orion A325 with electrodes for pH (Thermo Scientific 9106BNWP) and electrical conductivity (Thermo Scientific 013010MD). The Thermo Orion sensors were calibrated each morning in the field. Water samples collected in August of 2022 were unfiltered and acidified to 1% nitric acid upon return to the laboratory approximately one week after collection. In July of 2023, both filtered and unfiltered samples were collected. Filtration was performed at the time of collection using 0.45 µm disc filters. Both filtered and unfiltered samples were acidified to 1% nitric acid at the time of collection. In August of 2023, only filtered water samples were collected. Filtration was performed at the time of collection using a 0.45 µm syringe filter, and samples were immediately acidified to 2% nitric acid.

Following academic and regulatory conventions, metal concentrations measured in samples that were passed through a 0.45 µm filter and subsequently acidified are referred to as “dissolved” metals, while metal concentrations in samples that were acidified but not filtered are referred to “total recoverable” metals. Acidification of unfiltered water samples is not expected to liberate metals from all particulates (e.g., clay minerals, silicates, etc.), but it is expected to release metals from the most bioavailable particulates, such as Fe and Al oxyhydroxides.

We analyzed water samples collected from the Salmon River and its tributaries during each of our three sampling periods for Al, As, Ba, Ca, Cd, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, K, Mg, Mn, Mo, Na, Ni, Pb, S, Se, Sr, U, V, and Zn (45). Samples were analyzed at the University of California Riverside (UCR) using standard methods via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP–MS). An Agilent 7,900, single quadrupole, ICP–MS was used, fitted with an Agilent ISIS integrated sample introduction system. Samples were run at 10x dilution in 2% nitric acid, and instrument outputs were calibrated using matrix-matched, multielement standards, freshly prepared in-house. All solutions were prepared using trace-metal grade nitric acid and 18.2 MΩ water. Various QA/QC protocols were used, including periodic analysis of standard reference material NIST1640a throughout the runs and online introduction of multielement internal standards. Within run accuracy was generally better than ± 5% for all elements. Long-term studies of the UCR ICP–MS indicate accuracy of better than ± 5% for major elements and 10% for trace elements. Within a single run, repeated analyses of the same solutions have a very high precision, generally below 1% and often orders of magnitude lower. Recovery of all measured elements in the standard reference material NIST1640a was within these tolerances. SO42− concentrations were estimated from ICP–MS measurements of bulk S, using SO42− standards in the run, and assuming that all S was present as SO42−.

All data processing was carried out in R (46) and all figures were prepared using the ggplot2 package (47) in R. Thresholds for chronic (4-d mean) toxicity of each metal to freshwater aquatic life were either taken from or calculated following United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) guidelines for the State of Alaska under the Clean Water Act (10). The chronic toxicity thresholds for As, Cd, Cu, Ni, Pb, and Zn (ppb) are dependent upon hardness (ppm), which was calculated for each water sample as 2.497 * Ca (ppm) + 4.118 * Mg (ppm). The chronic toxicity threshold for Al is dependent upon both hardness and pH. The size of Salmon River tributary watersheds was estimated using the coordinates for “pour points” at the mouths of the tributaries, a 5 m resolution interferometric synthetic aperture radar digital elevation model (48), and the whitebox geospatial package (49) in R.

One potential limitation of metal toxicity assessments based on EPA thresholds for Alaska is that they do not incorporate the potential for dissolved organic matter (DOM) to mitigate effects of high metal concentrations on aquatic organisms. DOM can complex with many metals, thereby reducing toxicity to aquatic organisms (50). DOM is typically assessed through measurements of its close correlate, dissolved organic carbon (DOC). We did not measure DOC concentration in our water samples. However, DOC has been measured repeatedly in the lower Salmon River near the mouth, where chum salmon are likely to spawn. These DOC measurements, made both before and after degradation of the Salmon River watershed, have all been approximately 1 mg C/L (3). To assess the potential for DOM to mitigate the toxic effects of Cd on salmonoids, we conducted an experiment using the Biotic Ligand Model (BLM, version 3.41.2.45), which is commonly used to estimate bioavailability of metals and the accumulation of metals on gill surfaces (51). The BLM includes a module for Cd toxicity to rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss), making it a useful proxy for salmonids in the Salmon River. We ran the rainbow trout Cd model using our water chemistry data from the Salmon River mouth in July and August of 2023 with DOC concentration set at 0.1, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0, and 5.0 mg C/L. We then examined the impacts of varying DOC concentration on model estimates of organic-bound Cd and the burden of Cd on gill surfaces (SI Appendix, Table S2). We estimated that DOC is 10% humic acid, as suggested in the BLM manual. We did not measure Cl− or alkalinity in our water samples but instead estimated those values as 0.485 mg/L and 67.0 mg/L, respectively, based on previous measurements at the mouth of the Salmon River (3). Varying DOC concentrations over this range had a negligible impact on the gill load of Cd, suggesting that DOM has very limited potential to mitigate the effects of high Cd concentrations on salmonids in the lower Salmon River watershed. Despite the strong increase in organically bound Cd with increasing DOC concentration, free Cd and gill-bound Cd remained relatively constant, suggesting that organically bound Cd was drawn from other sources, such as inorganically bound Cd.

To assess aquatic invertebrate presence as a function of metal concentrations, we performed surveys at all water chemistry sampling locations in tributaries (n = 19) and the main stem of the Salmon River (n = 7) during July of 2023. To limit variation in rock surface area across sampling locations, we surveyed between 10 and 20 submerged river rocks at each sampling location, depending on rock size. We then used t tests to compare log-transformed metal concentrations (total Fe, total Al, and dissolved Cd) where aquatic invertebrates were present versus absent.

The Salmon River has five named and numerous unnamed tributaries. To ease comparison among the ten major tributaries that we sampled, we applied temporary names from the local Iñupiaq language that describe the current state of four additional tributaries (52, 53), leaving one major tributary unnamed. We refer to the tributary 27.1 river km below our uppermost sampling location as Isuqtuq, which means turbid, murky, or muddy water because it is the most turbid of the Salmon River tributaries. The tributary at river km 32.1 had very high metal concentrations when sampled in both July and August of 2023. We refer to it as Savilhaq, which means iron, steel or any kind of metal. We sampled a small tributary at river km 36.1 on just one occasion in August of 2023. We refer to it as Kannuyaq, which is the word for copper because it had the highest observed Cu concentration. Finally, we named the low elevation tributary at river km 112 Paluqtaq, which is the name for beaver because of the many beaver dams along its course.
