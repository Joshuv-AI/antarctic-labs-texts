Coronary Flow Reserve Measured by Myocardial Perfusion Imaging in Patients Following Percutaneous Coronary Intervention
Authors: Jingjing Liu, Jian Zhang, Wenhua Lin, Yuming Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDespite achieving complete revascularization, patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) may experience adverse outcomes, potentially attributable to coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD). Coronary flow reserve (CFR), which integrates both epicardial and microvascular coronary function, and a reduced CFR following complete revascularization indicates the presence of CMD.AimsThis study aimed to measure CFR in patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for complete revascularization using myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) and analyzed the prevalence of CMD and its correlation factors.MethodsThe clinical data of patients admitted to TEDA International Cardiovascular Hospital between March 2020 and March 2022 were collected. CFR was assessed using MPI. Coronary angiography confirmed successful PCI‐defined revascularization. CMD was defined as CFR < 2.0 in any of the three coronary arteries. Logistic regression model was established to analyze the factors associated with CMD.ResultsA total of 301 patients were included. Among the enrolled patients, the incidence of CMD was 43.9% (132/301). Univariate analysis identified clinical presentation, prior myocardial infarction, Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hyperlipidemia, the use of angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blocker (ACEI/ARB) and the number of stents implanted as associated with CMD. Multivariate analysis identified acute myocardial infarction (AMI) (OR = 4.187 95% CI:1.291−13.586), T2DM (OR = 1.878 95% CI: 1.120−3.149) and hyperlipidemia (OR = 1.970 95% CI:1.177−3.299) as independent predictors of CMD.ConclusionCMD is prevalent following PCI, particularly in patients presenting with AMI or having comorbidities like T2DM and hyperlipidemia. Post‐PCI CFR assessment is crucial, and strategies to prevent CMD in the peri‐procedural period warrant consideration.Keywords: coronary flow reserve (CFR), coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD), myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI), post‐percutaneous coronary intervention (Post‐PCI)

Background

Coronary artery stenting is increasingly performed for the management of coronary artery disease (CAD), as it effectively restores patency and blood flow of occluded vessels. Even though percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is effective, patients may still experience adverse outcomes after intervention, such as persistent and unrelieved angina attacks, leading to recurrent hospitalizations and therefore higher treatment costs. Angina occurs or recurs in 20%−40% of patients after PCI [1]. The mechanisms underlying the occurrence or recurrence of angina following PCI remain unclear. The presence of coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) could be a potential cause of the recurrence of angina after PCI in addition to coronary spasm [2, 3, 4].

CMD is the manifestation of functional and structural abnormalities in the small blood vessels of the heart and can present independently or in combination with pathologies in the epicardium, coronary artery or myocardium [5]. Coronary flow reserve (CFR) refers to the capacity of coronary arteries to increase blood circulation when there is an increased demand for myocardial oxygen or under the influence of pharmacological stimulation and is crucial for assessing cardiovascular circulatory function. Despite successful stent implantation returning epicardial vessels to their normal anatomy, abnormal CFR may persist or worsen. In such cases, a decrease in the CFR often indicates CMD, which increases the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, including major adverse cardiac events, heart failure, and mortality [6, 7]. Thus, assessment of CFR following PCI is important.

Myocardial blood flow (MBF) can be measured using positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT)‐based myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI), and MBF at rest and under stress conditions are used to determine the myocardial flow reserve (MFR). PET is considered the gold standard for noninvasive measurement of MFR [8]. Despite its superior accuracy and comprehensive cardiac imaging capacity, PET/CT is used less frequently than single‐photon emission computed tomography (SPECT). The use of cadmium zinc telluride (CZT) as a semiconductor detector in SPECT enables rapid dynamic tomography, owing to its exceptional sensitivity and precise spatial and temporal resolution. Numerous studies have demonstrated the technical feasibility of employing CZT‐SPECT MPI with 99mTc radiopharmaceuticals for the clinical assessment of MBF and MFR, yielding outcomes comparable to those of PET [8, 9, 10]. CFR with a threshold of 2.0 by CZT‐SPECT strongly correlates with invasive fractional flow reserve (FFR) in patients with coronary stenosis and uesd for the assessment of CMD [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. The objective of our study was to evaluate the CFR in patients who underwent PCI using CZT‐SPECT and to determine the prevalence and correlation factors of CMD.

Methods

Study Population

We conducted a single‐center retrospective study and included 935 patients admitted to the TEDA International Cardiovascular Hospital between March 2020 and March 2022 who underwent successful coronary angiography (CAG) and MPI. The research ethics committee of TEDA International Cardiovascular Hospital approved the study protocol. Figure 1 illustrates the flow chart of the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged between 18 and 85 years; (2) successful CAG and rest‐stress MPI data; and (3) underwent PCI and confirmed by quantitative coronary angiography (QCA) to have residual coronary stenosis < 50%. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a history of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), cardiomyopathy, severe valvular heart disease, severe pulmonary disease, or malignant tumors; (2) perioperative complications, left main coronary artery stenosis, significant myocardial bridging, coronary artery spasm, or abnormal origin as identified by coronary angiography; (3) patients with evidence of persistent ischemia on MPI (reversible perfusion defects ≥ 10%) were excluded to minimize confounding by incomplete revascularization and significant residual myocardial ischemia; (4) incomplete clinical, CAG, and MPI data.

Study flowchart. PCI, percutaneous coronary intervention; CAG, coronary angiography; MPI, myocardial perfusion imaging.

Clinical Presentation Classification

Patients were categorized by initial diagnosis before PCI: (1) Unstable angina (UA): Rest angina (> 20 min), new‐onset severe angina, or accelerating angina. (2) Acute myocardial infarction (AMI): AMI per Fourth Universal Definition. (3) Stable angina (SA): Effort‐induced symptoms without recent deterioration.

Data Collection

We collected demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, height, weight, smoking status, and clinical data such as admission diagnosis and history of hypertension (HBP), Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hyperlipidemia, heart failure, arrhythmias, myocardial infarction, and PCI/CABG. Additionally, we obtained routine blood examination results, blood biochemical indices, and the parameters of PCI (including total contrast volume used), echocardiography, and MPI results. All clinical data were sourced from the hospital's electronic medical records system.

CAG and MPI

The results of the CAG were assessed by two interventional cardiologists using QCA. CAG was performed using multiple angiographic projections, including a combination of left anterior oblique (LAO) 45° and cranial (CRA) 20°, right anterior oblique (RAO) 30° and CRA 20°, LAO 45° and caudal (CAU) 35°, and RAO 30° and CAU 20°. On the other hand, for the right coronary artery CAG examination, performing it from at least two projections is recommended: LAO 45° and CRA 20°. Successful revascularization after PCI was defined by QCA (residual stenosis < 50%), TIMI flow grade III, and the absence of complications such as branch involvement, dissection or perforation. Intravascular imaging (intravascular ultrasound/optical coherence tomography) and functional (FFR) examinations was not routinely performed due to the retrospective design.

CZT‐SPECT's dynamic tomography allows quantification of MBF and CFR with accuracy comparable to PET, as validated against invasive coronary angiography in studies of microvascular dysfunction [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. The imaging system employed a CZT detector equipped with 19 pinhole collimators (NM530c, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA). 99mTc‐methoxy isobutyl isonitrile (MIBI) was supplied by Beijing Senke Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., or Atomic Hi‐Tech Tianjin Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd, and then administered. We utilized a 1‐day protocol for stress and rest MPI. Imaging at rest involved administering the imaging agent at rest while simultaneously performing dynamic gated myocardial perfusion tomography at rest. Stress imaging involves administering adenosine injection solution through a venous access port and injecting the imaging agent during the process. At the peak of stress, stress dynamics‐gated myocardial perfusion tomography is initiated. After image acquisition, three‐dimensional reconstruction is performed to obtain cross‐sectional images of the left ventricle in short‐axis, horizontal long‐axis, and vertical long‐axis orientations. Left ventricular global CFR (LVG‐CFR) was calculated as the mean CFR value across the entire left ventricular myocardium. LVG‐CFR provides an integrated measure of overall coronary microcirculatory. Please refer to the references for detailed methods and precautions before and after the procedure [14].

Definition of CMD

The CFR is the ratio of MBF during stress to rest and serves as an effective indicator for the identification of CMD. The CFR represents the comprehensive measurement of epicardial and coronary microvascular blood flow in the coronary arteries. At the vascular level, CMD was defined as CFR < 2.0 in any coronary territory despite successful epicardial revascularization by successful PCI (residual stenosis < 50% by QCA, TIMI flow grade III and absence of procedure‐related complications such as branch involvement, dissection, or perforation). At the patient level, a patient was classified as having CMD if CFR was < 2.0 in any coronary territory despite successful PCI. To distinguish chronic CMD from acute PCI‐related microvascular injury, patients with peri‐procedural myocardial infarction (PMI), defined as post‐PCI troponin elevation > 5 times the 99th percentile upper reference limit (URL), were excluded based on laboratory records.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables with normal distribution are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) and compared between groups using independent samples t‐tests. Non‐normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared between groups using the non‐parametric Mann−Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages (%) and compared between groups using the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. CFR values were analyzed separately for PCI‐treated vessels (stent‐implanted segments) and non‐PCI‐treated vessels (vessels without intervention or with < 50% stenosis). For comparisons between PCI‐treated vessels and non‐PCI‐treated vessels, the non‐parametric Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was applied. Furthermore, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyzes were performed to identify the factors correlated with CMD. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate logistic regression model. In this study, statistical significance was defined as a p value less than 0.05 (two‐tailed).

Results

Baseline Data Analysis

A total of 301 patients met the inclusion and were therefore included in the final statistical analysis. Among the enrolled patients, 59.8% were male, with an average age of 60.57 ± 10.03 years. The median time interval (non‐normal distribution) for SPECT after CAG was 1.03 (0.74−1.32) days. Clinical presentations at admission were SA in 10.6%, UA in 78.1%, and AMI in 11.3% of patients. We included patients with complete revascularization after successful PCI and obtained CFR values for all three coronary arteries by CZT‐SPECT. Patients were classified as having CMD if their CFR was less than 2.0 in any coronary territory. We divided the patients into two groups: the CMD and Non‐CMD groups, based on their CFR values. Significant differences between the CMD and Non‐CMD groups were observed for clinical presentation, comorbidities (prior MI, hyperlipidemia, T2DM), number of stents implanted, triglyceride levels, ACEI/ARB use, and CFR values of the three coronary arteries and the global left ventricle. Univariate Analysis of Factors Associated With CMD

In the univariable logistic regression analysis, several clinical and procedural factors showed associations with CMD. Notably, patients presenting with AMI had a significantly higher odds of CMD compared to those with SA (OR = 7.200; 95% CI: 2.425–21.379). Among comorbidities, prior MI demonstrated a significant association with CMD (OR = 2.037; 95% CI: 1.218–3.408). T2DM (OR = 1.852; 95% CI: 1.157–2.965) and hyperlipidemia (OR = 2.014; 95% CI: 1.253–3.238) were positively correlated with CMD. In terms of medical therapy, ACEI/ARB were associated with a reduced likelihood of CMD (OR = 0.602; 95% CI: 0.366–0.989). Additionally, the implantation of ≥ 2 stents versus 1 stent approached significance (OR = 1.677; 95% CI: 1.061−2.653). UA showed a borderline significant association with CMD (OR = 2.301; 95% CI: 0.993–5.333). Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) showed a trend toward an inverse association with CMD (OR = 0.969; 95% CI: 0.938−1.002). Other variables such as age, body mass index (BMI), sex, atrial fibrillation (AF), HBP, smoking status, and use of beta‐blockers did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05). These findings suggest that clinical presentation, prior MI, metabolic comorbidities, certain medications and procedural factors may independently influence CMD risk, warranting further multivariate adjustment. Vessel‐Specific CFR Analysis

To address the impact of PCI on microvascular function, we compared CFR values between PCI‐treated vessels (stented segments) and non‐PCI vessels (vessels without intervention or with < 50% stenosis). In the CMD group (n = 132), PCI‐treated vessels exhibited significantly lower CFR than non‐PCI vessels (1.51 [1.18−1.90] vs. 1.63 [1.29−1.99]; p < 0.001). Notably, 80.3% (106/132) of non‐PCI vessels in CMD patients still demonstrated CFR < 2.0, indicating systemic microvascular dysfunction. Discussion

PCI effectively restores blood flow in occluded or stenotic epicardial coronary arteries, thus restoring myocardial perfusion and improving patients' cardiac function and prognoses. However, despite complete revascularization, patients may still experience recurrent angina and deterioration of cardiac function, leading to rehospitalization and adverse outcomes [15, 16]. PCI primarily addresses epicardial coronary vessel blood flow but does not directly ameliorate microvascular pathology. The coronary microcirculation plays a crucial role in the coronary circulation, encompassing all vessels with a diameter less than 500 μm, including precapillary vessels (100–500 μm), intramural arteries (< 100 μm), and capillaries [5, 7]. These vessels play crucial roles in delivering oxygenated blood to the heart muscle and removing waste products. The complex microvasculature network ensures that the myocardium receives an adequate supply of nutrients and oxygen to support its continuous pumping action. Any disruption or dysfunction in the microvasculature can lead to impaired myocardial perfusion, potentially resulting in conditions such as ischaemia or even myocardial infarction [5, 17].

Our study found a high prevalence 43.9% (132/301) of CMD following successful PCI. CMD is a predictive factor for adverse outcomes in post‐PCI patients and is implicated in ischaemia with non‐obstructive CAD (INOCA), AMI, angina after PCI, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, Takotsubo syndrome, and complications following heart transplantation [7]. Patients diagnosed with INOCA often experience a decline in their overall well‐being and are likely to experience ongoing cardiac issues, which require frequent hospital stays and increase healthcare costs [18, 19]. The assessment of CMD following PCI is crucial for determining the effectiveness of treatment and managing long‐term outcomes. As a noninvasive modality for CMD evaluation, MPI is increasingly performed because of its ability to provide detailed information about MBF and identify areas of ischaemia or scar tissue. Furthermore, the use of MPI allows healthcare providers to tailor care plans for each patient on the basis of their specific cardiac function and perfusion status. By closely monitoring changes in myocardial perfusion over time, clinicians can intervene early to prevent further deterioration and improve outcomes for individuals with CMD following PCI.

Various underlying factors, such as impaired endothelial function, heightened oxidative stress, diminished nitric oxide release, and reduced shear stress in the distal segment of the stenosed vessel, may contribute to CMD following successful PCI [20]. These factors have the potential to negatively affect microvascular function. Furthermore, the presence of pre‐existing masked CMD may also contribute to the manifestation of anginal symptoms and/or myocardial ischaemia following successful PCI. Hasdemir et al. [21] demonstrated that CMD after PCI causes significant differences in microvascular perfusion parameters compared with CMD in patients with non‐obstructive CAD, which is attributable to variations in their underlying pathologies and contributing factors.

Our study identified hyperlipidemia and T2DM as factors correlated with CMD following PCI. Vasomotor function of the coronary microvasculature is regulated by the activity of smooth muscle in arterioles and endothelial cells. Endothelial damage and subsequent dysfunction, which often occur alongside high levels of blood sugar and lipids, worsen. The development of endothelial dysfunction in individuals with T2DM involves the creation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), the activation of protein kinase C (PKC), and the disruption of the polyol pathway [22]. In individuals with hyperlipidemia, LDL‐C levels are inversely correlated with the CFR [23]. CMD is associated with hypercholesterolemia, which may be partly due to the increased production of reactive oxygen species, including oxidized low‐density lipoprotein [20]. In animal models, vascular endothelial dysfunction resulting from hyperlipidemia and hyperglycemia disrupts vascular tone balance, leading to cumulative effects over time that significantly contribute to CMD [24]. These factors can manifest before the development of macroscopic vascular diseases. Effective management of hyperlipidemia and blood glucose levels following PCI can reduce the incidence and progression of CMD [25, 26].

AMI is a relevant factor associated with CMD. Approximately 50% of patients with MI develop CMD following PCI despite advancements in PCI technology [27]. Notably, patients presenting with AMI had the highest CMD prevalence (70.6%), even after excluding PMI, highlights the profound impact of the acute ischemic insult on the microvasculature. Microvascular damage, such as swelling and bursting of endothelial cells, blockage of tiny blood vessels and bleeding within the heart muscle lead to inadequate restoration of blood flow in the small blood vessels of the heart [28]. During the perioperative period of interventional surgery for patients with AMI, targeted myocardial protection measures (such as reducing the symptom‐onset‐to‐balloon time, using pharmacological agents, and employing advanced procedural techniques) can effectively decrease the incidence of CMD [6, 29]. Antiplatelet agents, beta‐blockers, calcium channel blockers, adenosine, and statin drugs can improve coronary microcirculation after PCI for patients with AMI [30]. Moreover, the application of thrombolytic drugs during PCI can improve coronary microcirculation [31]. Some studies have demonstrated that the presence of CMD following PCI may result in an increased the area of infarcted myocardium, the development of ventricular arrhythmias, early onset congestive heart failure, and adverse left ventricular remodeling [32, 33]. Therefore, early assessment and management of CMD in AMI patients who have undergone PCI are critical.

Our vessel‐level analysis revealed two key findings: First, PCI‐treated vessels exhibited significantly lower CFR than non‐PCI vessels within CMD patients, implicating local procedural factors (e.g., distal embolization, inflammation at the stented site) in exacerbating microvascular injury [24]. Second, 80.3% of non‐PCI vessels in CMD patients also demonstrated CFR < 2.0, strongly indicating underlying systemic microvascular dysfunction, likely driven by comorbidities like T2DM and hyperlipidemia [17, 19]. This underscores that post‐PCI CMD often represents the confluence of local PCI‐related injury superimposed on a background of diffuse microvascular disease. Consequently, mitigation strategies should target both procedural optimization and aggressive systemic risk factor control.

Notably, traditional risk factors such as sex were not independently associated with CMD in our adjusted analysis. This may be attributed to several factors: First, the dominant effects of AMI and metabolic comorbidities (e.g., T2DM and hyperlipidemia) potentially overshadowed subtler associations. Second, our cohort comprised Post‐PCI patients with successful revascularization, a population where acute procedural impacts may transiently modulate vascular responses. Finally, CMD pathophysiology Post‐PCI appears more directly driven by microvascular damage during acute events and endothelial dysfunction from metabolic stressors than sex‐specific hormonal pathways [7, 19]. Although beta‐blockers and ACEI/ARB have been proposed as potential therapies for CMD [17, 27], their use were not independently associated with reduced CMD risk in our final multivariate model. While ACEI/ARB use showed a potential association with reduced CMD risk in unadjusted analysis, this effect was attenuated and non‐significant after adjustment for covariates including AMI presentation and comorbidities. This suggests that the observed univariate association may have been confounded by underlying patient characteristics driving both ACEI/ARB prescription and CMD risk, rather than indicating a direct protective effect of these medications in this specific post‐PCI setting.

Our study presents several limitations. Firstly, the single‐center retrospective design limits generalizability and may introduce selection bias, particularly regarding the exclusion of patients with incomplete data. Future prospective studies with consecutive enrollment are warranted. Secondly, early CFR assessment (median 1.03 days after PCI) may capture both chronic CMD and transient microvascular injury. Although our trial design minimized the effect of PCI, our incidence of CMD may have been overestimated. Longer‐term follow‐up studies are needed to assess the evolution and persistence of microvascular dysfunction. Thirdly, the patients included in our study had residual coronary stenosis of less than 50% after PCI, yet we were unable to measure the FFR of the residual stenosis, and the lack of intravascular imaging (e.g., stent expansion, edge dissection) may impact the assessment of revascularization quality. Therefore, the decrease in CFR in our study cannot be ruled out as being attributed to residual stenosis. In addition, CFR was assessed non‐invasively via CZT‐SPECT rather than invasively. While SPECT‐based CFR correlates well with catheter‐derived measures, future studies should include simultaneous invasive CFR measurements to confirm our findings. Notably, our findings might not be applicable to patients with multivessel CAD, as our study specifically focused on those with single‐vessel CAD.

Conclusions

CMD is prevalent following PCI, particularly in patients presenting with AMI or having comorbidities like T2DM and hyperlipidemia. Post‐PCI CFR assessment is crucial, and strategies to prevent CMD in the peri‐procedural period warrant consideration.
