Efficacy of novel SARS-CoV2 vaccines in preventing SARS- CoV- 2 infection: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zigale Hibstu Teffera, Wubetu Yihunie Belay, Bantayehu Addis Tegegne, Habtamu Belew, Adane Adugna, Yasabe Laykun, Samrawit Tefera, Yalew Muche, Abateneh Melkamu, Gashaw Azanaw Amare, Desalegn Abebaw, Yibeltal Akelew, Mohammed Jemal, Mamaru Getinet, Enyew Fenta Mengist, Temesgen Baylie, Aysheshim Belaineh Haimanot, Tiruzer Hibistu, Kassaye Enchalew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEfficacious SARS-CoV-2 vaccines are urgently required to prevent the spread of the emerging and re-emerging SARS-CoV-2.ObjectiveAim to assess the efficacy of novel SRAS-CoV-2 vaccines in preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection.MethodsAll randomized placebo-controlled clinical trials eligible for this review were included. Scopus, PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar were searched. The risk of bias in the included studies was assessed using the modified Cochrane risk of bias tool 2 for RCTs. Result synthesis was performed using STATA software version 17.4. Forest plots, heterogeneity tests, meta-regression, sensitivity analysis, and publication bias were used to present the results.ResultEighteen studies comprising 186,657 participants who took the full dose of SARS-CoV-2 in an RCT were included. Of the participants, 110,768 (59.3%) were males and 111,619 (59.8%) were treatment groups. A total of 5665 (3.0%) participants were infected by SARS-CoV-2. Among the treatment groups, 3140 (2.8%) and 2525 (3.4%) from the placebo group were infected by SARS-CoV-2. The most efficient SARS-CoV-2 vaccine was BNT162B2, with 100% efficacy, whereas mRNA-1273 was the least efficient vaccine, with 36.8% efficacy. The overall efficacy of novel SRAS-CoV-2 vaccines was 70.5% (95% confidence interval (CI), 53.5 to 79.7). The relative risk of being infected was 84% lower in the treatment group compared to the placebo group. The novel SRAS-CoV-2 vaccines are efficient enough to protect 771 people out of 1000 from being infected by SARS-CoV-2.DiscussionNovel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines are efficacious inn preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection. However, their efficacy varies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11717-5.

Introduction

SARS-CoV-2 has caused significant distractions in ordinary life, public health, and the global economy [1, 2]. Vaccination is the only approach to combating future global pandemics. It is one of the most significant methods to prevent and control SARS-CoV-2 infection, its spread, and transmission [3] and protect affected persons from catastrophic complications [4]. Remarkably, SARS-CoV-2 vaccination lowered the number of new SARS-CoV-2 infections, with the greatest impact observed against symptomatic and high viral load infections, with a discernible difference from mild infections [5, 6]. However, prevention of SARS-CoV-2 infection is very challenging due to increased SARS-CoV-2 mutations resulting in the emergence of new variants [7].

The efficacy of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines is affected by different factors, like [8], type of vaccine [9], number of doses and duration [10], continuous adaptation of SARS-CoV-2 transmission and immune evasion, evolution and mutation patterns of SARS-CoV-2 [11], and route of administration [12]. For instance, administration of vaccines through intramuscular and subcutaneous routes fails to induce a strong mucosal immune response in the respiratory tract, where SARS-CoV-2 replication and shedding occur. Locally administered SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, like intranasal administration, have merits over intramuscularly and subcutaneously administered vaccines in preventing infection and transmission [12]. It is also challenged by organ failure and tissue damage complications following novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccination [13], emergence and selection of novel variants of SARS-CoV-2 [14], waning of antibodies or T cells in individuals following infection or vaccination, and a variety of characteristics, such as unique disease conditions, age, gender, and pre-existing immunity [15]. The efficacy of currently available novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines in preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection is confronted by their inability to reduce the spread of the virus completely and equally among all disease severity spectrums. Additionally, vaccine efficacy diminishes over time but can be enhanced with a booster dose. Boosters effectively restore vaccine potency and substantially reduce the incidence of new SARS-CoV-2 infections [6–17].

Evaluating the efficacy of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines is challenging because of the evolving basic understanding of the virus

Many vaccines are currently in clinical trials to test how efficacious they are [18]. There will likely be no one-winner vaccine. Multiple platforms and technologies can have different strengths and be important in different epidemiological scenarios. To comprehend the generalizability and therapeutic importance of efficacy results for a SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, they must be rigorously evaluated with scientific methods [19]. This review comprises 186,657 RCT participants from 18 included studies and will try to fill important data, knowledge, and attitude gaps on the efficacy of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines in preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection. This review aims to assess the efficacy of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines in preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection. In the meantime, it will address answerable questions on the efficacy of different types of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines. The review questions of this review and the outcomes of the report issue important information to review users such as patients, health professionals, policymakers, researchers, and all other stakeholders. The objective of this review is to assess the efficacy of SRAS-CoV-2 vaccines in preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

The review includes all original randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in any country within the past five years, written in English, and involving intramuscular administration of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines.

Exclusion criteria

All studies that underwent any type of review, literature scans, conference papers, studies that are not RCTs, books, and book chapters are excluded.

Framework of the study

The Population, Intervention, Comparator, and Outcome (PICO) framework tool was used to formulate RCT research questions and guide systematic reviews. The PICO criteria were used to select the studies to be included in the review, which involved screening studies based on predefined eligibility criteria related to PICO. The PICO for each synthesis was determined at the protocol stage regarding the specific question each synthesis aims to answer, how the synthesis will be structured and the specification of planned comparisons. The PICO criteria for each synthesis and definition are the following:

Population (P)

All individuals who were polymerase chain reaction (PCR) confirmed SARS-CoV-2 negative and free from chronic or unknown diseases.

Intervention (I)

Novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines.

Comparator (C)

All individuals who were PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 negative, free from chronic or unknown diseases, and received a placebo.

Outcome

Efficacy of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines.

The planned analysis methods were forest plot (subgroup analysis and cumulative meta-regression), heterogeneity test, meta-regression (sensitivity test), and publication bias. Either fixed-effect or random-effect models were the proposed statistical models for result synthesis.

Information source

Scopus, PubMed, Medline, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and the search engine Google Scholar were information sources. The last date to search for all literature is May 1, 2024.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was implemented to identify relevant studies from electronic databases and Google Scholar. A combination of controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text terms related to the population, intervention, comparator, and outcomes were employed. The full search strategy for all of the databases was: (((((((“SARS-CoV-2 vaccine efficacy” [Title]) OR (efficacy [Title])) AND (SARS-CoV-2[Title]))) OR (“Sever Acute Respiratory Syndrome-2” [Title])))) OR (COVID-19))))) OR (Coronavirus [Title])))))) AND (Vaccine [Title]))))))). Filters were applied to abstracts, RCTs, and published articles, and in the last five years, limits were applied to vaccines, human vaccines, immunotherapeutics, and the English language.

Selection process

The initial screening of search results involved evaluating titles and abstracts to find relevant studies. Eligible studies for full-text assessment were identified using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The screened studies were organized and managed using an Excel spreadsheet, and pertinent data was extracted from the included research. Quality assurance tests were used to ensure consistency and correctness in the screening and selection process. The methods used to decide whether a study met the inclusion criteria of the review were merging search results from different sources using reference management software, removing duplicate records of the same report, examining titles and abstracts to remove irrelevant reports, retrieving the full text of potentially relevant reports, linking together multiple reports of the same study.

Four independent reviewers screened and retrieved all titles and abstracts to remove extraneous reports. Each reviewer used the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to determine whether the study should be further evaluated. Full-text assessments were conducted on studies that were identified as possibly eligible during the title and abstract screening processes. To determine final eligibility, the reviewers independently reviewed the full-text reports against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements over eligibility for inclusion were settled through discussion. When there was a debate about interpretation, one reviewer arbitrated. There was no disagreement on the arbitration judgment. There was no implementation of automated searching and screening. EndNote 20.1, an automated reference management application, was used for organizing. Data collection process

A standardized Excel spreadsheet was used to record all essential information from each included study. The data items extracted were study characteristics (author, study location, publication year, study design, total sample size, sample size for each group per study, total infected and non-infected participants per study in each group), participant demographics (sex, age, location, the total number of participants and per sex), intervention details (total number of the treatment group and placebo group, treatment to placebo ratio, dose and dosage interval, mean/median follow-up period), outcome measures (vaccine efficacy, confidence intervals (CIs)), effect sizes (pooled odds ratio, mean, and median follow-up), and measures of variability (standard deviation, standardized mean difference, standard error, pooled standard deviation and error). The accuracy of the extracted data was verified by cross-checking against the sources, like study publications and supplementary materials. Quality checks were performed to ensure the avoidance of data entry errors or discrepancies. The extracted data were organized in a structured Excel spreadsheet to facilitate analysis. The data were labeled appropriately and consistently to avoid confusion during subsequent synthesis. Four reviewers performed data collection and extracted data from each report independently. There was no discrepancy during data collection and extraction. Reviewers worked independently during the data collection process to minimize bias and errors. Each reviewer extracted data from the reports based on predefined criteria without consulting other reviewers initially. Reviewers run to confirm an incomplete or unclear report by referring to supplementary materials, study protocols, or trial registries for additional information and consulting secondary sources or previous related publications for supplementary data. No data screening and extraction software platforms were used.

Data items

Specific data items were extracted from each included study to facilitate qualitative and quantitative synthesis and statistical analysis. The comprehensive list of data items extracted for this work includes study identification (author(s) name(s), publication year, study country, study title, journal or source, DOI or PMID), study design and methodology (study design, sample size, randomization method, blinding, allocation concealment, follow-up duration), participant characteristics (age, gender, residence) intervention details (intervention description, dosage, duration, mode of administration), comparator details (comparator description, dosage), duration), outcome measures, time points of outcome assessment, outcome measurement tools/methods), effect sizes (mean/median, odds ratio (OR)), measures of variability (standard deviation (SD), standard error (SE), confidence intervals (CI), P-values mean difference, standardized mean difference (SMD)), study results (overall vaccine efficacy), treatment effects and estimates), funding source and conflicts of interest, risk of bias assessments and, additional information (study location) and any other relevant study-specific details. The data were sought for the primary outcome of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccine efficacy in preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection. The SARS-CoV-2 vaccine efficacy is all about a measure of how much the risk of getting disease is lowered in a clinical trial [20]. All results at reported time points and analyses were sought for meta-analysis. Methods for All results at the reported time points and analyses were sought for meta-analysis. Methods for deciding which results to collect for meta-analysis are predefined in inclusion criteria, framework of the study, relevance, consistency across studies, and consultation with experts. In addition to outcomes, various other variables were sought for data collection to contextualize, assess study quality, and explore sources of heterogeneity. Missing or unclear information was handled through imputation, sensitivity analysis, and publication bias. Missing or unclear information about the following variables was considered and handled along with definitions (Supplementary file 1). Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the impact of missing data on study outcomes and conclusions. There was no missing or unclear information due to publication bias.

Study risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias assessment was undergone in and between studies to ensure the reliability and validity of the review. It was implemented by selecting a tool that can assess the risk of bias, defining bias domains, evaluating each study independently, recording justifications, resolving discrepancies, and summarizing findings (aggregating the risk of bias assessments for all included studies and presenting the results in a structured format such as a risk of bias table).

The Cochrane risk of bias (RoB-2) tool was used to assess the risk of bias in the included studies. Four independent reviewers assessed each study to ensure objectivity and reduce the risk of bias in the assessment process. After their independent assessments, they compared their evaluations. Discrepancies between them were resolved through discussion. No automation tools were used to manage the risk of bias assessment. The final risk of bias judgment was recorded for each study and categorized as ‘low risk’, ‘high risk’, or ‘unclear risk’ depending on the tool and criteria used.

Effect measures

Effect measures were calculated to quantify the magnitude and direction of the intervention across multiple studies. They were used to synthesize findings and estimate an overall treatment effect. The effect measures like mean difference, standardized mean difference, standard error, standard deviation, relative risk (RR), pooled standard deviation, pooled standard error, overall efficacy in percent, and CI were computed. Moreover, considerations for effect measures (interpretability, study heterogeneity, study design (RR for RCTs), reporting, subgroup analyses, and sensitivity and subgroup analyses) were sought.

Synthesis methods

The whole process passed to decide which studies were eligible for synthesis is described in the eligibility section. Handling of missing summary statistics, data conversions for meta-analysis, data screening, and quality control, addressing outliers and skewed data, sensitivity analyses, and documentation and transparency were the data synthesis methods employed to ensure the data used for presentation and synthesis are accurate, consistent, standardized, and compatible across studies. Tabulation and visualization methods were employed to effectively summarize and communicate the results of individual studies and syntheses in meta-analysis, which made interpretation and decision-making easier. Forest plots, funnel plots, Galbraith plots, and subgroup analyses were used to visually display the results of individual studies and synthesis.

The methods used for synthesizing results in meta-analysis, along with rationales for their choice:

Random-Effects Model

Assumption

Assumes that included studies estimate different treatment effects due to both random error and true heterogeneity.

Rationale

Random-effect models are suitable when significant heterogeneity is expected among studies, allowing for estimation of both within-study and between-study variability.

Method

Incorporates an estimate of between-study variance in addition to within-study variance, resulting in wider confidence intervals.

Advantages

It accounts for heterogeneity and provides a more conservative estimate of uncertainty.

Identification of statistical heterogeneity was performed by Cochran’s Q Test, I2 Statistic, and visual inspection of whether the CIs for each effect overlapped (forest plots, funnel plots, and Galbraith plots) (https://training.cochrane.org/handbook/current/chapter-10#section-10-10-4-1). Stata software package version 17.4 was applied for conducting the meta-analysis because of familiarity with the software and its compatibility with data formats and analysis requirements. The methods used for exploring heterogeneity were the forest plot (subgroup analysis, cumulative meta-analysis), heterogeneity test (Galbraith plot, sensitivity analyses), meta-regression, publication bias assessment, and quality assessment. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the synthesized results.

Reporting bias assessment

The risk of bias due to missing results in a synthesis arising from reporting biases (publication bias or selective outcome reporting) was assessed to ensure the validity and reliability of meta-analytic results. Methods like funnel plot asymmetry, publication bias tests, and sensitivity analyses (exclusion of small studies, trim-and-fill method, and file drawer analysis) were employed to assess reporting bias.

Certainty assessment (certainty of evidence or confidence in the estimates)

The certainty of the evidence for the outcome synthesized was assessed by applying GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation). The GRADE was employed to evaluate evidence on study limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias in the included studies and to draw conclusions. It rates assessments with high, moderate, low, or very low certainty, provides a systematic approach for rating the overall confidence in the evidence, and helps to provide recommendations [21] (www.gradeworkinggroup.org).

Results

Results of the Search and Selection Process

Search Strategy

A systematic search was conducted across Scopus, PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar by using predefined search terms and Boolean operators. The search was conducted based on inclusion criteria and the framework of this review. It was limited to studies published in English between January 1, 2019, and May 1, 2024. The initial search identified a total of 263 records from the predefined electronic databases and Google Scholar. No additional records were identified through other sources. After removing 99 duplicate records, title, and abstract screening were performed for 164 records. Of the 164 records, 106 were excluded based on relevance to the research question, predefined inclusion criteria, and the framework of this review. Following title and abstract screening, 58 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 18 studies were included in this review. The whole process of record/evidence searching, starting from evidence identification to inclusion, is shown in Fig. 1.

Studies that might appear to meet the inclusion criteria but were excluded and why they were excluded are presented in Supplementary File 2.

Risk of bias in included studies

The risk of bias in included studies was systematically assessed by using standardized tools and processes to ensure reliable and valid conclusions that ultimately inform better evidence-based decisions. Multiple reviewers and use of consensus methods were employed to mitigate individual biases. Assessing the RoB in the included studies was carried out by using the Cochrane RoB-2 assessment tool for RCTs, review process methods (number of reviewers, independent assessment, and consensus process), and steps in the risk of bias assessment methods (training and calibration, independent review, documentation, categorization, discussion, and final documentation). The assessments of RoB for each included study were presented by summarizing the judgments made using the Cochrane RoB-2 assessment tool for RCTs for each study (Table 2). The RoB assessments for each included study were performed to provide a transparent evaluation of the potential biases that could affect the review’s findings. The overall effects of this review were used to interpret the strength and reliability of the evidence synthesized. The assessment of RoB in the included studies is documented and presented by using the Cochrane RoB tool in supplementary file 4.

Interpretation

The results of the individual studies show the magnitude and direction of the efficacy of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines in preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection. The overall Hedges’s g effect size is 0.48% at a 95% CI. All of them are nearly similar and have positive effects on preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection. For example, Creech CB et al. reported that the prominent difference between treatment groups administered with the mRNA-1273 vaccine is half a standard deviation compared to the placebo group, while Anderson EJ et al. reported the least difference between treatment groups administered with the novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, mRNA-1273, is 46% a standard deviation compared to the placebo group. f FINLAY-FR-2 was the most efficacious novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, with 100% efficacy, Hedges’s g effect size of 0.48 (0.40–0.57), and a weight of 1.30% among 1,131 (6201 +/- 4485) treatment group participants, as reported by Ehsan Mostafavi. While mRA-1273 was the least efficacious novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, with 36.8% efficacy, Hedges’s g effect size was 0.46 (0.37–0.56) and a weight of 1.0% among 2,594 (6201 +/- 4485) treatment group participants, as reported by Anderson EJ et al. Random sequence generation

All studies reported random sequence generation and random allocation of participants transparently, except Anderson EJ et al. (2022), who did not report random sequence generation of participants transparently.

Blinding

All studies were double-blinded. However, one study was observer-blinded and placebo-controlled.

Incomplete outcome data

No study with inadequate handling of missing data.

Selective reporting

No evidence of selective reporting was found, as all studies reported on pre-specified outcomes.

Other biases

Funding sources were disclosed in all studies, and there were no potential conflicts of interest.

Reporting biases

Reporting biases due to publication bias, selective outcome reporting bias, location bias, language bias, time-lag bias, and citation bias were acknowledged and addressed to provide more accurate and reliable estimates of treatment effects. These biases were addressed through comprehensive search strategies and an assessment of publication bias (funnel plots). The funnel plot was analyzed and interpreted to assess the publication bias and to visualize the distribution of the studies included in this review. Its visual inspection is symmetrical, indicating the absence of significant publication bias or small study effects. The pooled effect estimate is robust and reliable. Larger and more precise studies are located at the top and close to the pooled estimate, whereas smaller and less precise studies are scattered more widely at the bottom. Discussion

The overall efficacy of novel SRAS-CoV-2 vaccines was 70.5% (95% CI, 53.5 to 79.7) within a median of 99 days. The vaccinee group has an 84% lower relative risk of being infected by SRAS-CoV-2 as compared to the placebo group. The novel SRAS-CoV-2 vaccines are efficient enough to protect 771 people out of 1000 from being infected by SRAS-CoV-2. The synthesized results are consistent across the included studies.

At 95% CI, this review revealed a higher efficacy of SRAS-CoV-2 vaccines than reported by P. Tabarsi et al., 44.22% [22], F. Zhu et al., 28·2% [23],, Ehsan Mostafavi et al., 49.70% [24], Kremsner PG et al., 48.20% [25], Anderson EJ et al., 36.80% [26], Anderson EJ et al., 50.60% [26], and Chunhua Zhoua et al., 51.60% [27] within a median of 55, 161, 100, 180, 71, 68, and 180 days, respectively.

A consistent efficacy of novel SRAS-CoV-2 vaccines in this review at 95% CI was reported by Ehsan Mostafavi et al., (64.90% [28], Mino Mohraz et al., 67.93% [29], J. Sadoff et al., 66.90% [30], Lianpan Dai et al., 75.70% [31], K.J. Hager et al., 71.00% [32], G. Áñez et al., 79.50% [33], A. Khobragade et al., 66·6% [34], K. Hardt et al., 75·2% [35], R. Ella et al., 77.8% [36], L. Bravo et al., 78.7% [37], and M. D. Tanriover et al., 69.9 [38] within a median of 142, 83, 78, 179, 60, 58, 48, 56, 99, 82, and 90 days, respectively.

This review reported lower efficacy of novel SRAS-CoV-2 vaccines at 95% CI than reported by Polack FP, et al., 95% [39], Baden LR et al., 94.10% [4], Thomas SJ et al., 93.20% [40], Creech CB et al., 88.00% [41], P.T. Heath et al., 89.70% [42], Robert W. Frenck et al., 100.00% [43], Edson D. Moreira et al., 95.30% [44], L.M. Dunkle et al., 90.40% [45], and D. Y. Logunov et al., 91.60% [46] within a median of 60,60,212,180, 82, 180,180,180,180, and 48 days, respectively. This discrepancy might be due to variations in vaccine type, dose and number of dosages, sample size, route of administration, sample management, geographical locations, season, and duration of the study.

The quality and reliability of the evidence included were evaluated using the GRADE approach to provide a transparent and systematic assessment of the quality of the evidence by considering random sequence generation (selection bias) and allocation concealment (selection bias) of participants, blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias), blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias), incomplete outcome data (attrition bias), selective reporting (reporting bias), and other biases. By applying the GRDAE approach, evidence was judged to have high certainty. The methods used in the study are robust and well-defined. The sample sizes are adequate, and there is a low risk of bias in the studies. Limitations of the evidence included in this review are lack of long-term follow-up studies, a good quality adequate datum, standardized age classification, detailed analysis of the efficacy of novel SARS-CoV-2 per sex, ethnicity, location, and statistical significance determination (P-value). Importantly, association indicators like relative risk, absolute risk, and odds ratio are not included in most of the evidence. The follow-up period was not expressed uniformly. Some were expressed as mean and standard deviation, while others were expressed as median and interquartile range. Moreover, doses of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines were different and given for different durations. The units of measurement for the novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines were also different. The limitations of the review process were the enrollment of only participants who were PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 negative and had no chronic or underlying disease. That means the result of the outcome cannot be generalized to these segments of the population. Heterogeneity, publication bias, subgroup analysis, and sensitivity analysis were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings in and across the included studies. There was no significant variation in the results of the synthesis. Both random model and fixed model effects revealed similar results, which also supports the absence of inconsistency in and between the included studies. However, one of the included studies had an unclear risk of bias in the evidence regarding randomization of sequence and allocation concealment of participants. This situation is considered to have a low risk of bias based on Cochrane’s guidelines (https://training.cochrane.org/handbook/current/chapter-08).

The strengths of this review are its comprehensive search strategy, rigorous inclusion criteria, robust RoB, and certainty of evidence assessments. The whole process of the review was transparent. The result of this review will fill data and knowledge gaps and controversies on the efficacy of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, from which patients, healthcare workers, and policymakers can benefit.

Conclusion

The overall efficacy of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines in preventing novel SARS-CoV-2 infections was 70.5%. The rate of relative risk reduction from being infected by SARS-CoV-2 is 84%. Overall, the vaccines have a protection capacity of 771 people out of 1000 people from being infected by SARS-CoV-2. A significant difference in the efficacy of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines among the included studies was observed. Continuous and consistent work on the efficacy of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines is required to establish a transparent policy and avoid controversies over the choice of novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines in clinical practice. Provided, efficacious novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines are available for immediate clinical use. However, additional studies are required to avoid dilemmas on their efficacy, immunogenicity, and safety.

Future directions

The development of efficacious novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines needs to focus on a combination of therapies, medication synergy, interaction of innate immune cells with SARS-CoV-2, regulation of cell-mediated immunity upon SARS-CoV-2 infection, use of adjuvants to boost efficacy, and strategies to develop a novel therapeutic SARS-CoV-2 that can act as both a prophylactic and therapeutic vaccine. Questions on the need for all-ready SARS-CoV-2-infected individuals are unanswered. Clinical trials for the newly emerging novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines need to involve citizens who are suffering from abnormal immunity due to different factors and have chronic or underlying diseases to infer the efficacy of these vaccines for the whole population at large. Research on novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines needs to have comprehensive information, complete statistical analysis, and transparency. Reporting bias (selective reporting) must be avoided. Finally, researchers also need to focus on developing therapeutic novel SARS-CoV-2 vaccines that can both treat and prevent SARS-CoV-2 infection, in parallel to improving the efficacy of novel SARS-CoV-2 prophylactic vaccines that prevent SARS-CoV-2 infection.
