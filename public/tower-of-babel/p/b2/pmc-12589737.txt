Advancing malaria treatment with magnetic and mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine: a review
Authors: Tsegaye Fekadu Egza, Eyob Mulugeta Kebede, Ramesh Duraisamy Perumal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria remains a pressing global health concern, particularly in regions with rising cases of drug-resistant Plasmodium strains. Sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP), a widely used antimalarial drug combination, faces challenges such as reduced bioavailability, systemic toxicity, and diminished efficacy due to resistance. In response, nanotechnology offers innovative strategies to improve SP delivery and therapeutic outcomes. This review focuses on the potential of magnetic and mesoporous silica nanoparticles as advanced drug delivery platforms for SPs. Magnetic and mesoporous silica nanoparticles combine a magnetic core, enabling targeted delivery through external magnetic fields, with a mesoporous silica shell that allows high drug loading and controlled release. This review explores recent advancements in magnetic and mesoporous silica nanoparticles synthesis, surface functionalization, and drug encapsulation techniques, highlighting their ability to increase SP bioavailability, ensure sustained drug release, and minimize off-target effects. In vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated the improved pharmacokinetics, reduced toxicity, and enhanced antimalarial efficacy of SP-loaded magnetic and mesoporous silica nanoparticles, particularly against resistant Plasmodium strains. Additionally, the versatility of magnetic and mesoporous silica nanoparticles paves the way for the codelivery of synergistic drugs and the development of theranostic systems. While magnetic and mesoporous silica nanoparticles show immense promise, challenges such as scalability, biocompatibility, and regulatory hurdles must be addressed to facilitate clinical translation. By bridging nanotechnology with malaria treatment, this approach has the potential to revolutionize antimalarial therapy, providing a scalable and effective solution for resource-limited settings.Graphical abstract Keywords: Magnetic and mesoporous silica nanoparticles, Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP), Drug delivery, Malaria, Drug resistance, Controlled release

Introduction

Malaria: global burden and challenges

Malaria represents a significant health crisis worldwide, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. According to the World Health Organization’s World Malaria Report 2024, there were an estimated 263 million cases and 597,000 malaria-related deaths worldwide in 2023. This marks an increase of approximately 11 million cases compared to 2022, while the death toll remained nearly unchanged. Notably, about 95% of these deaths occurred in the WHO African Region, underscoring the urgent need for effective prevention and treatment strategies in these areas. Despite significant efforts in prevention, treatment, and control, malaria transmission remains persistent due to factors such as limited access to healthcare, inadequate diagnostic infrastructure, and vector resistance to insecticides [1]. Below are some of the highlights from the 2024 report.

Malaria remains a major global health challenge, particularly in the African region, which bears the brunt of the burden. In 2023, an estimated 263 million new malaria cases were reported in 83 countries, up from 252 million in 2022. This increase was primarily concentrated in five countries, four in Africa and Pakistan, driven by catastrophic weather events, population growth, and conflict or forced migration. Although the total number of malaria deaths remained stable, with 597,000 deaths in 2023 compared to 600,000 in 2022, children under five years old accounted for 73.7% of these deaths, equating to over 1,200 child fatalities each day, mainly in Africa. The WHO African Region was responsible for 94% of malaria cases and 95% of deaths globally, with more than half of all deaths occurring in four countries: Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Niger, and the United Republic of Tanzania [2].

Malaria responses worldwide have made significant progress in preventing illness and saving lives. Since 2000, an estimated 2.2 billion malaria cases and 12.7 million deaths have been averted. Seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) continues to scale up, reaching 53 million children at high risk of severe malaria in 2023. Two malaria vaccines are being rolled out, with 17 countries expected to introduce them by December 2024 through routine childhood immunization. Insecticide-treated net (ITN) use has remained steady, with around 52% of those at risk sleeping under them. However, efforts to distribute more effective nets to combat insecticide resistance have made notable progress. Intermittent Preventive Treatment in Pregnancy (IPTp) remained stable in 2023 at 44%, up from 42% in 2021, though many pregnant women still miss out on essential malaria prevention treatments. Among the numerous challenges facing malaria elimination, equitable access was a focus of this year’s report (Figs. 1 and 2).

The socioeconomic impacts of malaria are equally devastating. Endemic regions face reduced productivity, healthcare burdens, and barriers to development. Malaria-related absenteeism affects both school attendance and labor force participation, further leading to poverty [3]. Additionally, climate change has contributed to the expansion of mosquito habitats, resulting in the increased spread of malaria to previously unaffected regions. This global public health issue necessitates innovative approaches, particularly in drug discovery and delivery, to overcome the persistent burden of the disease [4].

While previous reviews have explored nanoparticle-based drug delivery for malaria treatment, most have provided broad overviews of various nanocarriers without focusing on MNPs and MSNs for SP delivery. This review addresses this gap by critically examining the role of MNPs and MSNs in improving SP’s pharmacokinetics and therapeutic efficacy. It provides an in-depth analysis of drug loading mechanisms, release profiles, and targeting strategies associated with MNPs and MSNs, highlighting their potential to overcome key limitations of conventional SP treatment. Furthermore, this review identifies existing research gaps, such as the need for more in vivo studies, long-term stability assessments, and translational research efforts, to facilitate the clinical application of MNPs and MSNs in malaria therapy. This review aims to provide a more precise direction for future research and contribute to advancing nanotechnology-driven malaria treatments by filling these gaps (Tables 1 and 2).

Drug resistance in malaria: limitations of current therapies

Drug resistance is a significant challenge for malaria control efforts. While ACTs (artemisinin-based combination therapies) have been the gold standard for malaria treatment, the rise of Plasmodium falciparum resistance to artemisinin has been reported in Southeast Asia, particularly in Cambodia, Thailand, and Vietnam. It poses a growing risk to other parts of the world. Resistance manifests as delayed parasite clearance, necessitating more extended treatment regimens and increasing the likelihood of treatment failure. This threatens to undermine the substantial gains made over decades in reducing malaria transmission rates [5].

In addition to artemisinin resistance, Plasmodium strains have also developed resistance to other drugs, such as chloroquine and sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP), limiting the number of therapeutic options available. The increasing spread of resistant strains necessitates urgent research into new treatments and innovative delivery methods to restore efficacy [6]. Drug resistance to several antimalarials is sometimes either due to changes in drug accumulation or efflux mechanisms (chloroquine, amodiaquine, quinine, halofantrine, mefloquine resistance) or due to decreased affinity of the drug target, which may result from point mutations in the respective genes that encode these targets (Pyrimethamine, cycloguanil, sulphonamide, atovaquone, artemisinin resistance) [7], shown in Fig. 3.

Current therapies also face limitations regarding accessibility, affordability, and side effects. The global shortage of drugs such as artesunate, combined with the high costs associated with second-line treatments, makes it difficult for low-income countries to provide adequate care to their populations [8].

Nanotechnology in medicine: opportunities for malaria treatment

Nanoparticles (NPs) are materials with sizes ranging between 1 and 100 nm. Sometimes, their size may even range up to several hundred nanometers. In recent years, NPs have been widely used for drug delivery applications. NPs as drug carriers offer several advantages over their bulk counterpart, such as variability in size and shape, formation of stable interactions with ligands, high carrier capacity, and convenience of binding of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs. These characteristics render NPs favorable platforms for target-specific and controlled delivery of micro and macromolecules for various diseases. Due to their small size (< 100 nm), they can escape clearance from the reticuloendothelial system. NPs made of organic materials, such as polymers, carry the advantage of biocompatibility bio-imitability; lipidic NPs formed of long-chain fatty acids can simultaneously incorporate lipophilic and hydrophilic drugs (Figs. 4, 5, and 6).

On the other hand, inorganic-based NPs are also being studied due to their facile structure modification stimulus/stimuli-sensitive drug release. Silica NPs have been extensively studied for biomedical applications due to their higher thermal stability, biocompatibility, ease of surface functionalization, and low production cost. Also, amorphous silica and silicates are generally considered safe substances, as defined by the USA Food and Drug Administration. Ample literature demonstrating the versatility of silica as NPs is available, which ranges from the delivery of drug molecules to protein, peptides, DNA and RNA strands, and even diagnostic agents [9].

Porous materials can be classified based on the pore size of the NPs. They can be categorized into microporous, mesoporous, and macroporous, with pore sizes of less than 2 nm, between 2 and 50 nm, and greater than 50 nm, respectively. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSN) have gained increased scientific interest in recent years. MSN is a promising class of mesoporous materials with a submicron-sized structure carrying silica adducts with numerous pores that can load large quantities of drugs or other payloads. Mobil Research and Development Corporation first developed the mesoscopic material in 1992. They designed silica NPs named Mobile Crystalline Material (MCM-41) with uniform pores and hexagonal morphology. They were initially used as molecular sieves for the adsorption of gases and column chromatographic separations. Numerous other MCM types of MSN are being developed by varying the reaction conditions and starting precursors to form MCM-48 (cubic arrangement) and MCM-50 (lamella-like structure). Amongst these MSNs, MCM-41 is widely explored for drug delivery applications. Other advantages of MSN include uniform and tuneable pore size and morphology, facile surface functionalization, and the internal surface with organic and inorganic functional groups. MSN has an external surface of negative charge due to surface silanol groups, which confers hydrophilicity to the particles. This attribute of MSN is favorable for providing colloidal stability through repulsive surface charges [10]. Additionally, the pores can be quickly capped, thus allowing more control of the drug release kinetics. Researchers have successfully utilized these nanocarriers for loading a variety of cargo ranging from synthetic drugs to macromolecules such as protein, ribonucleic acid (RNA), deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), and phytoconstituents [10].

Nanotechnology holds great promise for improving malaria treatment by overcoming the limitations of conventional drug delivery systems. NPs can enhance the pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of antimalarial drugs, allowing for more efficient targeting of the parasite while minimizing off-target effects. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) and magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) have emerged as two promising platforms for advancing malaria therapy [12].

MSNs are nanoparticles with a high surface area and tunable pore structure, making them ideal candidates for loading and delivering hydrophobic antimalarial agents. By functionalizing the surface of MSNs, researchers can tailor the nanoparticles for targeted drug delivery to specific tissues or cells, such as the liver or red blood cells, where the malaria parasite resides and reproduces. This targeted approach reduces the potential for side effects and enhances drug efficacy. Recent advancements in MSN synthesis, including green chemistry approaches, have made them even more attractive for low-resource settings [13].

Magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) offer unique advantages because of their ability to be directed via an external magnetic field. This feature enables the precise localization of drug-loaded nanoparticles at the site of infection, such as the liver or spleen, where malaria parasites first mature. In addition to drug delivery, MNPs can be combined with hyperthermia, where localized heat is applied to enhance drug efficacy through synergistic mechanisms. Applying MNPs in drug delivery systems could substantially improve therapeutic outcomes for malaria treatment [14].

Significant progress has been made in the synthesis of magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) in the past decade. The emphasis has been on creating different techniques capable of generating MNPs with certain attributes such as size, shape, magnetic properties, and stability. Compatibility with living organisms is a very important issue that needs to be addressed especially in the case of their use in biological applications. A wide range of synthesis methods were developed over time. These synthesis methods can be divided into physical, chemical, and biological processes (Fig. 7) [15].

Nanotechnology also allows for combination therapies, which are essential in the battle against drug resistance. By encapsulating multiple antimalarial drugs or using nanoparticles to co-deliver complementary agents, such as artemisinin and adoxine-pyrimethamine, resistance mechanisms can be circumvented, and the efficacy of the treatment can be enhanced. Delivering multiple drugs in a single formulation can also simplify treatment regimens, potentially improving patient compliance [16].

Overview of sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) therapy

Mechanism of action of the SP

Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) is a well-known combination therapy used to treat malaria, particularly infections caused by Plasmodium falciparum. The mechanism of action of SP is primarily based on the inhibition of folate biosynthesis in the malaria parasite (Tables 3 and 4).

Sulphadoxine inhibits dihydropteroate synthetase, an enzyme involved in synthesizing dihydropteroic acid, a precursor in the folate pathway. Folate is essential for nucleotide synthesis and cell division in Plasmodium. Sulfadoxine blocks folate production by inhibiting dihydropteroate synthetase, impeding the parasite’s ability to replicate [18].

Pyrimethamine, the second component of the combination, inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), another enzyme involved in folate metabolism. This enzyme catalyzes dihydrofolate’s reduction to tetrahydrofolate, an essential form for purine and pyrimidine synthesis in the parasite. The dual action of Sulfadoxine and Pyrimethamine effectively impairs the folate pathway, leading to parasite death [19]. The effectiveness of SP is thus attributed to its dual blockade of two distinct enzymes in the folate biosynthesis pathway, which is critical for the survival of Plasmodium species during their blood stage of infection.

However, the efficacy of SP in treating malaria is becoming compromised due to the development of drug resistance, particularly in regions with high malaria transmission rates, such as sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia. Mutations in the genes encoding dihydropteroate synthetase and DHFR have reduced Plasmodium falciparum’s susceptibility to SP, rendering the drug less effective and necessitating the development of alternative therapies [22].

Recent experimental data, comparative studies, and clinical progress on SP-loaded MNPs and MSNs

Research into the use of magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) and mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) for delivering antimalarial drugs like sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) is still in its early stages. While specific studies on SP-loaded MNPs and MSNs are limited, related research provides valuable insights into their potential application.

For instance, a study by De Oliveira et al. (2024) detailed the synthesis of magnetic mesoporous silica (magMCM) nanoparticles loaded with chloroquine (CQ), an antimalarial drug. The magMCM nanoparticles demonstrated a high surface area and maintained the hexagonal structure characteristic of MCM-41 silica. Encapsulation of CQ resulted in rapid drug release and improved solubility compared to the non-encapsulated drug. Biocompatibility assessments using MRC5 non-tumoral lung fibroblasts indicated that the magMCM nanoparticles were non-toxic, suggesting their suitability as drug carriers [23]

Another study focused on developing magnetic mesoporous silica nanoparticles to deliver chemotherapeutic drugs and hyperthermia potentially. The synthesized nanoparticles exhibited low cytotoxicity and efficient cell uptake. They provided sustained drug release in acidic conditions and could generate heat upon exposure to an alternating magnetic field, indicating their potential for combined chemotherapy and hyperthermia treatments [24].

While these studies did not involve SP specifically, they demonstrate the versatility of MNPs and MSNs in drug delivery applications. The findings suggest that these nanoparticles could potentially enhance the delivery and efficacy of SP by improving drug solubility, enabling controlled release, and allowing targeted delivery through magnetic guidance. However, further experimental research is necessary to evaluate the encapsulation efficiency, release kinetics, and therapeutic outcomes of SP-loaded MNPs and MSNs (Table 5 and 6).

Therapeutic limitations: resistance and bioavailability challenges

One of the most significant challenges in the use of SPs is the emergence of drug resistance, particularly in Plasmodium falciparum. Resistance to SP develops through mutations in the target enzymes dihydropteroate synthetase and dihydrofolate reductase, which reduce the affinity of these enzymes for Sulfadoxine and Pyrimethamine, respectively. These mutations have become widespread in malaria-endemic regions, particularly Southeast Asia, where SP resistance is alarmingly high [25].

Resistance to SP has been particularly concerning because it is one of the most affordable and widely accessible antimalarial drugs. The emergence of resistant strains has led to the failure of SP-based treatments, which has significant implications for malaria control programs. In many cases, SP is used in combination therapies, such as SP + artesunate (SP + AS), for intermittent preventive treatment (IPT) during pregnancy or for the treatment of uncomplicated malaria [26]. However, resistance to SP in these combination regimens compromises their effectiveness, leading to the need for new drugs and alternative strategies to combat resistance.

In addition to resistance, the bioavailability of SP also presents challenges. SP is known to have relatively low solubility in aqueous solutions, which can limit its absorption in the gastrointestinal tract. As a result, therapeutic levels of the drug may not be achieved in the bloodstream, and its pharmacokinetics are less than ideal. This is especially problematic in individuals with poor gastrointestinal absorption or in children who may not absorb the drug effectively [27, 28]. The long half-life of SP also contributes to variable drug concentrations in the body, which can lead to suboptimal therapeutic effects and potentially allow for the emergence of resistant strains. Furthermore, inadequate patient adherence to treatment regimens, often due to the need for multiple doses, can exacerbate these issues, leading to incomplete drug courses and contributing to resistance development [29].

Importance of improving SP delivery

To overcome the therapeutic limitations of SP, particularly the challenges associated with resistance and bioavailability, improving the drug’s delivery is paramount. Nanotechnology offers a promising solution by enhancing poorly soluble drugs such as SP’s solubility, stability, and bioavailability. Nanoparticles, such as mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) and magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs), have shown potential in enhancing the pharmacokinetics of antimalarial drugs, including SP. These nanoparticles can encapsulate SP, protect it from degradation, and improve its solubility, allowing for more efficient absorption and sustained release in the body [30].

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles, for example, offer large surface areas and tunable pore sizes that can accommodate higher drug loads, providing controlled and prolonged drug release over time. This extended-release can reduce the need for frequent dosing and improve patient adherence to treatment, which is a key factor in preventing the development of drug resistance. Magnetic nanoparticles further enhance drug delivery by allowing for the targeted accumulation of SP at the site of infection via an external magnetic field, which could improve treatment efficacy and minimize off-target effects [31].

Incorporating nanotechnology into SP delivery systems could also help mitigate the effects of drug resistance. By using nanoparticles to deliver SP, it may be possible to bypass some of the resistance mechanisms employed by Plasmodium parasites. For example, drug-loaded nanoparticles can help concentrate the drug at the site of infection, ensuring that higher concentrations of the drug are delivered directly to the parasite, even in the presence of resistant strains. Moreover, nanoparticle-based formulations could be combined with other antimalarial agents to enhance the overall therapeutic effect and reduce the likelihood of resistance [32].

Improving SP delivery systems holds significant promise for enhancing the treatment of malaria and could be a crucial step in combating the growing issue of drug resistance. By improving the solubility, bioavailability, and targeted delivery of SP, these novel drug delivery systems could lead to more effective malaria treatments and ultimately contribute to the global fight against malaria (Figs. 8, 9 and 10).

Magnetic and mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MNPs and MSNs): design and properties

Structure and synthesis of MNPs and MSNs

Magnetic core: composition and synthesis (e.g., iron oxide nanoparticles)

Magnetic nanoparticles play a pivotal role in the biomedical applications of MNPs and MSNs because of their potential for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), hyperthermia, and targeted drug delivery. The most commonly used magnetic material in MNPs and MSNs is iron oxide, particularly magnetite (Fe₃O₄) and maghemite (γ-Fe₂O₃). These materials are preferred because of their superparamagnetism, meaning their magnetization is only present in an external magnetic field, ensuring minimal toxicity and no permanent magnetization once the field is removed (Fig. 11).

Mesoporous Silica Shell: Fabrication and functionalization techniques

A more advanced method to enhance the properties of magnetic cores involves core–shell structures, where the magnetic nanoparticle core (Fe₃O₄ or γ-Fe₂O₃) is coated with biocompatible materials such as gold or polymers to improve colloidal stability, minimize agglomeration, and enhance the therapeutic efficiency of the nanoparticles. This improves pharmacokinetics by extending circulation times and reducing nonspecific cellular uptake [34, 35].

Coprecipitation is a widely used synthesis method for these magnetic cores, where iron salts are mixed with a base, forming iron oxide nanoparticles. This method offers good control over particle size and magnetic properties. Other advanced methods, such as microemulsion and reverse micelle techniques, also allow for uniform nanoparticle production and better control over particle size distribution, which is critical for drug delivery applications. Magnetic cores can be further functionalized by coating them with materials such as polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) or polyethylene glycol (PEG) to prevent rapid clearance from the bloodstream and reduce immune system recognition [35, 36].

Mesoporous silica shell: fabrication and functionalization techniques

The mesoporous silica shell is essential for encapsulating therapeutic agents such as drugs, proteins, and nucleic acids. This shell is synthesized primarily via the sol–gel method, where a silica precursor such as tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) undergoes hydrolysis and condensation in the presence of surfactants to form a mesoporous structure. The most commonly used surfactant is cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), which helps to form mesopores with a defined structure [38]. Recent studies have also incorporated functionalized surfactants to control the size and morphology of mesopores, offering greater control over drug loading and release rates [39].

The mesoporous structure is characterized by a high surface area (up to 800 m2/g) and tunable pore size (2–10 nm). Modifying the pore size enables MNPs and MSNs to encapsulate a wide range of drug types, from small molecules to larger biomolecules such as proteins and nucleic acids [29]. Pore morphology, such as uniformity and pore volume, can also be adjusted by altering the synthesis conditions, such as temperature, pH, and precursor concentration, which can enhance the loading efficiency and release kinetics.

After synthesis, the mesoporous silica shell can be functionalized with various chemical groups for specific applications. For example, amine groups (–NH₂) can be introduced via 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane (APTES), enabling the binding of negatively charged molecules such as certain drugs or bioactive compounds. The functionalization of the mesoporous shell with targeting ligands, such as antibodies, peptides, or aptamers, enhances the selectivity of MNPs and MSNs for targeting specific cells or tissues, such as malaria-infected cells, providing better therapeutic outcomes [40, 41].

Key properties of MNPs and MSNs for drug delivery

High surface area and pore volume

MNPs and MSNs are renowned for their high surface area, ranging from 500 to 800 m2/g, making them ideal for drug encapsulation and sustained release applications. This extensive surface area facilitates the adsorption of various therapeutic agents, leading to high drug-loading capacity. Recent advancements have focused on optimizing the pore volume and size distribution, as these properties significantly influence the drug release rate. Studies have demonstrated that mesoporous silica particles with a high pore volume allow for a more significant amount of drug to be loaded and for more controlled, slower-release profiles, reducing the frequency of dosing and improving patient compliance [42].

Additionally, surface modifications such as PEGylation or functionalization with targeting ligands can improve drug loading efficiency by enhancing interactions between the drug and the silica surface. This increases the encapsulation efficiency and prolongs the drug’s therapeutic action by ensuring sustained and controlled release [43]. This feature is crucial for treating diseases such as malaria, where prolonged therapeutic action is often needed to prevent relapse and improve treatment outcomes.

Biocompatibility and biodegradability

Biocompatibility and biodegradability are fundamental for ensuring the safety and efficiency of MNPs and MSNs in therapeutic applications. Mesoporous silica is inherently biocompatible and does not elicit significant immune responses. In vivo studies have demonstrated that MNPs and MSNs are nontoxic and can circulate in the bloodstream for extended periods without causing adverse reactions such as cytotoxicity or hemolysis [44].

The biodegradability of mesoporous silica nanoparticles is also a significant advantage, as it ensures that the nanoparticles are safely eliminated from the body after their therapeutic task is complete. Studies have shown that silica nanoparticles undergo slow dissolution under physiological conditions, reducing the risk of long-term accumulation in tissues and organs [45]. This biodegradability is influenced by factors such as the size and surface characteristics of the nanoparticles. For example, smaller particles dissolve faster than larger particles, which can help tailor the drug release profile to the patient’s needs.

The biocompatibility of iron oxide nanoparticles is also well documented, with minimal toxicity observed at therapeutic doses. However, to prevent unwanted accumulation of iron oxide in the liver or spleen, surface modifications are employed to increase the nanoparticle circulation time and reduce nonspecific uptake by the reticuloendothelial system (RES) [46]. Functionalizing the nanoparticles with PEG or other biocompatible polymers enhances their stability and reduces the clearance rate, making them ideal for long-term treatments such as malaria therapy.

3Magnetic responsiveness for targeted delivery

One of the standout properties of MNPs and MSNs is their magnetic responsiveness, which enables them to be directed to specific tissues or organs via an external magnetic field. This capability is particularly valuable for diseases such as malaria, where selective targeting of infected red blood cells or the liver can significantly enhance therapeutic outcomes. Applying a magnetic field can effectively target the accumulation of MNPs and MSNs at the site of infection, reducing the drug’s off-target effects and minimizing side effects [47].

Magnetic targeting can be further optimized by adjusting the magnetic field strength, frequency, and duration to control the release of the drug. Using alternating magnetic fields (AMFs) allows for induced heating, which can trigger the release of encapsulated drugs in a controlled manner. This is particularly beneficial for hyperthermia-based therapy, where local heating destroys cancer cells or infected tissues, and the magnetic field guides the nanoparticles to the affected areas. This thermoresponsive release mechanism is under active investigation and has shown promise for enhancing the precision of drug delivery systems [48].

Moreover, magnetic nanoparticles also facilitate magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which can be used for real-time tracking of nanoparticle distribution in vivo, providing valuable insights into the targeting efficiency and therapeutic efficacy of MNPs and MSNs [49]. Combining the advantages of targeted drug delivery with MRI tracking makes MNPs and MSNs particularly appealing for malaria treatment, where monitoring the distribution and release of drugs is crucial for ensuring effective treatment.

Drug loading and controlled release mechanisms

Encapsulation strategies for SPs in MNPs and MSNs

Physical adsorption

Physical adsorption remains a widely utilized method for loading drugs such as SP into MNPs and MSNs because of its simplicity and efficiency. This method relies on weak intermolecular forces such as van der Waals interactions, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic forces. The high surface area of MNPs and MSNs enables a large quantity of SP to be adsorbed on their surface or within mesoporous channels without altering the inherent chemical structure of the drug. Multiple loading capacities can be achieved by modifying the mesoporous silica surface, such as through functionalization with hydrophobic or hydrophilic groups, optimizing the interaction between the SP and the nanoparticle surface [49]. However, physical adsorption typically results in rapid release of the drug, which may not be ideal for drugs requiring sustained release. The drug release rate can be tuned by controlling the pore size, surface charge, and hydrophobicity of MNPs and MSNs [50].

Covalent binding

Covalent binding, as opposed to physical adsorption, eniables a more controlled and prolonged release of SP, as the drug is chemically bonded to the MNPS AND MSNS surface. Standard methods include the use of silane coupling agents such as 3-aminopropyl trimethoxysilane (APTES), which introduces amine groups on the surface of the silica nanoparticles, providing reactive sites for covalent coupling with functional groups on the SP. This method enhances the loading efficiency and drug stability during transport, preventing premature release. Recent advancements have focused on designing specific pH-sensitive or enzyme-responsive linkers, ensuring the covalently bound drug is released only under the desired conditions [51]. This is especially important for targeted delivery to infected tissues or cells, as it minimizes side effects and maximizes therapeutic efficacy.

Controlled release kinetics

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) have emerged as highly effective carriers in controlled drug delivery systems due to their unique physicochemical properties, such as large surface area, tunable pore size, and ease of surface functionalization. MSNs are embedded within polymeric nanofibers to create a codelivery system for growth factors, enhancing osteogenesis through stimuli-responsive release and low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) [52]. The high porosity of MSNs allows for efficient loading of growth factors, while their functionalization enables the release to be triggered by external ultrasound stimulation. This approach ensures targeted delivery and sustains the release of the therapeutic agents, thereby optimizing bone regeneration. The synergistic effects of the ultrasound and the controlled release of growth factors from MSNs significantly enhance osteogenesis, demonstrating the potential of MSNs in regenerative medicine.

Further, it explores the role of MSNs in drug delivery by examining the physicochemical interactions and various drug-loading strategies [53]. The study emphasizes the versatility of MSNs in accommodating a wide range of drugs, including hydrophilic and hydrophobic molecules, through different loading methods such as adsorption, covalent bonding, and encapsulation. These strategies enable tailoring drug release profiles to meet specific therapeutic needs, such as sustained release or targeted delivery. MSNs can also be functionalized with targeting ligands to enhance their specificity toward particular tissues or cells, which is crucial in applications like cancer therapy. Moreover, the study highlights the potential of MSNs in achieving controlled and responsive drug release, where factors like pH or temperature can trigger the release of the drug, ensuring that therapeutic agents are delivered at the right site and time.

Diffusion-based mechanisms

The diffusion of SP through the mesoporous of MNPs and MSNs is a significant factor in determining the release profile. The diffusion rate depends on several factors, including the pore size, drug molecule size, and surface properties of the nanoparticles. The diffusion process can be mathematically modeled via Fick’s law of diffusion or Higuchi’s equation to predict the release rate. When the SP is physically adsorbed, the release tends to follow Fickian diffusion, where the drug molecule moves from an area of higher concentration (inside the nanoparticle) to an area of lower concentration (external environment), with the rate depending on the concentration gradient and the diffusion coefficient of the drug [54]. In cases where SP is covalently bound, the release is typically slower, as the chemical bond between the drug and the nanoparticle restricts it. The release rate can be controlled by altering the porosity of the mesoporous silica, for example, by incorporating mesoporous silica composites or by using surface modification techniques such as layer-by-layer assembly.

pH and enzyme-responsive release systems

As a novel strategy, pH-sensitive and enzyme-responsive release systems have been gaining traction for targeted drug delivery applications. The body’s pH varies across different tissues and compartments, offering a means to release SP at target sites selectively. For example, tumor and malaria-infected tissues typically present an acidic pH compared to normal tissues. SP can be preferentially released in such acidic environments by functionalizing MNPs and MSNs with pH-sensitive linkers or coatings [55]. Additionally, enzyme-responsive release is another promising strategy for malaria therapy. The use of proteases, lipases, and other enzymes found in infected cells (such as malaria-infected red blood cells) can catalyze the cleavage of specific linker bonds, leading to the controlled release of SP. The combination of both pH and enzyme-sensitive materials provides a highly tailored approach to optimize drug delivery to infection sites while minimizing systemic exposure [56].

Recent developments have also involved the use of polymers such as poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) or poly(N-vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP), which can undergo hydrolytic degradation under acidic conditions, facilitating SP release in low-pH environments associated with malaria-infected tissues [57].

Enhancing SP stability and bioavailability through MNPs and MSNs

Increasing stability

SP is unstable under environmental conditions such as light, temperature, and oxidative stress. Encapsulating SP in MNPs and MSNs offers protection from these external factors. The mesoporous silica shell acts as a protective barrier, shielding the drug from hydrolytic and oxidative degradation. This is particularly important in the case of SP, as its half-life can be significantly prolonged within the nanoparticles, ensuring sustained therapeutic concentrations over time. Furthermore, recent research has focused on functionalizing the silica surface with antioxidants, such as ascorbic acid, to provide additional protection to the encapsulated drug. This not only enhances the stability of SP but also extends its storage life, making MNPs and MSNs an ideal delivery system for oral or injectable formulations of the drug [58].

Enhancing bioavailability

The bioavailability of SP can be significantly increased through its encapsulation in MNPs and MSNs. As with many poorly water-soluble drugs, SP’s primary challenge is its low solubility in gastrointestinal fluids, leading to incomplete absorption and reduced therapeutic efficacy. Encapsulation in mesoporous silica nanoparticles helps overcome this issue by providing a larger surface area for dissolution, increasing the contact between the drug and the gastrointestinal lining [59]. In addition, MNPs and MSNs can be surface-modified with PEG or lipid-based coatings to improve their solubility and dispersibility in aqueous environments. Such modifications help the nanoparticles evade reticuloendothelial system (RES) uptake, leading to prolonged circulation time and increased bioavailability of SP at the target site.

The rate and extent of SP absorption are greatly enhanced by nanoparticle-based formulations, reducing the required dosing frequency and improving patient compliance. Moreover, the controlled release profile further ensures that therapeutic levels of SP are maintained for a prolonged period, minimizing fluctuations in drug concentration that can lead to therapeutic failure or resistance [60].

Applications of MNPs and MSNs in Malaria Treatment

In vitro studies

Evaluation of drug loading efficiency and release profiles

Multiple factors, including the nanoparticles’ size, surface charge, and pore structure, influence SP’s drug-loading efficiency in MNPs and MSNs. MNPs and MSNs are favored for their high surface area (up to 1000 m2/g) and well-ordered mesoporous structures, which enable high drug encapsulation capacities. The release kinetics of SP-loaded MNPs and MSNs are usually investigated under different pH conditions (e.g., pH 7.4 and 5.0) to simulate physiological and malaria-infected environments. At acidic pH values (which mimic the environment of infected tissues and the digestive vacuole of Plasmodium parasites), MNPs and MSNs can exhibit accelerated drug release due to the protonation of functional groups on their surface [61]. The cumulative release profiles of SP-loaded MNPs and MSNs typically demonstrate sustained release over 48–72 h, providing long-lasting antimalarial effects with reduced dosing frequency [62].

Various modifications to MNPs and MSNs have been explored to optimize drug release, including introducing surface coatings (e.g., PEGylation or polymers) to increase their stability and biocompatibility [63]. Drug retention studies using dynamic dialysis and UV‒Vis spectroscopy have also been conducted to determine how effectively MNPs and MSNs retain SP at neutral pH and release it under acidic conditions, mimicking the acidic environment in malaria-infected tissues [64]. This targeted delivery capability makes MNPs and MSNs promising carriers for resistant malaria strains that require high drug concentrations at the infection site.

Cytotoxicity and biocompatibility assessments on human and parasite cells

In vitro, cytotoxicity studies on human cell lines (e.g., HEK293and L929) and malaria-infected erythrocytes (such as Plasmodium falciparum) are crucial in determining the safety and efficacy of SP-loaded MNPs and MSNs. The MTT assay is frequently employed to evaluate cell viability post-treatment. The live/dead assay also provides insight into the percentage of viable cells after MNPS AND MSNS exposure. Low cytotoxicity and high biocompatibility are essential characteristics, and MNPs and MSNs often exhibit minimal cytotoxic effects compared with conventional drug formulations [65]. The hemolysis assay ensures that MNPs and MSNs do not induce significant red blood cell lysis, a critical concern when delivering antimalarial drugs, as malaria primarily targets red blood cells [66].

Moreover, drug uptake studies were conducted via flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy to evaluate the internalization of SP-loaded MNPs and MSNs by Plasmodium-infected erythrocytes. These studies revealed that MNPs and MSNs with surface functionalization (e.g., amine groups) are more readily taken up by parasite-infected red blood cells than unmodified nanoparticles are, suggesting that surface charge and functionalization play a significant role in parasite targeting and drug delivery efficiency [67].

In vivo studies

Biodistribution and pharmacokinetics of SP-loaded MNPs and MSNs

In vivo, biodistribution studies of SP-loaded MNPs and MSNs have been conducted in Plasmodium berghei-infected mice or humanized malaria models to evaluate how nanoparticles are distributed across various tissues and their targeting capability. Using fluorescence imaging or radiolabeled nanoparticles, researchers can track MNPs and MSNs in real time, measuring their uptake and accumulation in tissues such as the liver, spleen, and malaria-infected erythrocytes. Studies have demonstrated that magnetic MNPs and MSNs (incorporating iron oxide nanoparticles) can be directed to malaria-infected organs by applying an external magnetic field, which enhances therapeutic efficacy and reduces systemic toxicity [14]. The long circulation time of MNPs and MSNs, aided by PEGylation, helps prevent rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system (RES) and increases their bioavailability [68].

In animal models, pharmacokinetics are assessed by analyzing serum SP concentrations and determining the half-life, maximum drug concentration (C_max), and time to maximum concentration (T_max). Compared with free SP, SP-loaded MNPs and MSNs have been shown to have a prolonged half-life, ensuring sustained therapeutic action and reducing the frequency of drug administration [69]. Furthermore, biodistribution studies using radioisotopes have demonstrated that MNPs and MSNs accumulate predominantly in malaria-infected organs, supporting the targeted drug delivery approach.

Antimalarial efficacy in animal models

In Plasmodium berghei- and Plasmodium falciparum-infected mouse models, SP-loaded MNPs and MSNs have shown superior antimalarial efficacy compared with free SP. Studies have revealed that the encapsulation of SP in MNPs and MSNs significantly improves the parasitemia reduction and survival rate in treated animals. Histopathological analysis of tissue samples revealed less tissue damage in SP-loaded MNPS AND MSNS-treated animals than in those treated with free SP, highlighting the potential of MNPs and MSNs in minimizing drug-induced toxicity [70].

Moreover, the magnetic targeting of MNPs and MSNs enhances drug accumulation in infected organs, increasing drug concentrations at the site of infection and thereby improving therapeutic outcomes. This results in faster parasite clearance and longer survival rates in animal models [71]. In combination therapy, MNPs and MSNs can co-deliver SP and other antimalarial drugs (e.g., artemisinin derivatives), resulting in synergistic effects and enhanced antimalarial potency [72].

Roles of magnetic and mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MNPs and MSNs) in Diseases

Neurological disorders

Magnetic nanoparticles are extensively studied for their potential to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), a significant challenge in treating neurological disorders such as Alzheimer’’s and Parkinson’s. MSNs can encapsulate neuroprotective drugs, allowing for controlled release and targeting specific brain regions. These properties make MNPs and MSNs ideal for sustained treatment and improved therapeutic outcomes.

Cardiovascular diseases

MNPs and MSNs have been utilized in treating cardiovascular diseases by delivering anti-inflammatory drugs directly to vascular lesions, minimizing side effects, and improving the efficiency of drug therapy. Additionally, MNPs are widely used in MRI imaging for early diagnosis of cardiovascular conditions, helping visualize plaque and arterial damage.

Infectious diseases

For infectious diseases, MNPs functionalized with antimicrobial peptides or drugs have been shown to enhance the targeting and treatment of pathogens. MSNs encapsulate drugs and provide controlled release at infection sites, reducing systemic toxicity and improving therapeutic efficiency.

Importance of MNPs and MSNs over other composites in medicine

MNPs and MSNs offer advantages over traditional nanocomposites due to their dual functionality and biocompatibility. Combining magnetic properties and large surface areas with tunable pore sizes allows for efficient drug loading, sustained release, and enhanced targeting. The ability to functionalize both MNPs and MSNs with specific ligands means that these nanoparticles can be directed toward precise disease sites, minimizing side effects and improving therapeutic outcomes. Furthermore, their application in imaging and diagnostics provides a comprehensive approach to disease treatment and monitoring.

Recent advances in MNP and MSN-based nanocomposites

Recent advancements have highlighted the growing potential of MNPs and MSNs in theranostic applications. These include the combination of drug delivery and diagnostic capabilities in a single platform, enabling better disease treatment and monitoring. For example, developing MSN-MNP composites capable of simultaneously delivering drugs and enhancing MRI imaging has demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy to conventional drug delivery systems.

Overcoming SP resistance with MNPs and MSNs

Resistance to SP, particularly Plasmodium falciparum, is a growing challenge in malaria treatment. MNPs and MSNs offer a potential solution by providing controlled release and targeted delivery of SP, which may help overcome current therapies’ limitations. One of the key advantages of MNPs and MSNs is their ability to target infected cells directly, thereby reducing the exposure of healthy cells to high drug concentrations. This localized drug delivery minimizes the chances of developing drug-resistant strains [89].

Combining magnetic targeting and surface functionalization of MNPs and MSNs can improve their ability to overcome efflux-mediated resistance in malaria parasites. Some studies have shown that MNPs and MSNs can overcome P-glycoprotein-mediated drug efflux, a mechanism commonly responsible for drug resistance [90]. Moreover, by incorporating combination therapies, MNPs and MSNs can simultaneously deliver multiple antimalarial agents that target different stages of the Plasmodium lifecycle, thereby reducing the likelihood of resistance development.

Functionalization strategies (such as drug-resistant efflux pump inhibitors) have also been explored to increase drug retention inside malaria parasites. MNPs and MSNs can also be engineered to deliver multiple drugs sequentially or controlled, thus maximizing each drug’s efficacy while reducing the likelihood of resistance [91]. These multidrug delivery systems have shown promise in overcoming resistance and improving antimalarial treatment efficacy.

Challenges and future directions

Challenges in MNPS AND MSNS synthesis and scalability

The synthesis of mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MNPs and MSNs) is a complex and resource-intensive process. One of the significant challenges is the reproducibility of their structural properties across different batches. Achieving uniformity in particle size, pore size distribution, and surface functionality can be difficult, and minor deviations in the synthesis process can lead to significant variations in drug loading efficiency and release profiles [29]. Template-based synthesis methods, which are often employed for MNPS and MSNS production, are prone to variability because of reaction time, temperature, and precursor concentration.

Additionally, the scalability of MNPS and MSNS production for large-scale clinical applications remains an issue. Many traditional synthesis techniques, such as sol–gel or hydrothermal methods, are not easily scalable because of the procedures’ high cost, complexity, and time-consuming nature. These methods also result in low product yields at the industrial scale, which limits commercialization [92]. Researchers are exploring greener and more efficient synthesis pathways to address these issues, including microfluidic systems and continuous flow reactors. These could offer better control over the particle synthesis and enhance yield and reproducibility.

Biocompatibility and potential toxicity concerns

Although MNPs and MSNs have demonstrated outstanding potential in drug delivery and antimalarial therapies, their biocompatibility and toxicity remain significant concerns. Despite their relatively low cytotoxicity in vitro, the long-term in vivo effects of MNPs and MSNs are still under investigation. A critical problem is the accumulation of silica nanoparticles in various organs, such as the liver, kidneys, and spleen, leading to potential chronic toxicity. Silica-based nanoparticles can be challenging to fully degrade in the body, which may result in persistent tissue damage and inflammation over time [93].

Moreover, surface modifications that enhance drug delivery and biocompatibility, such as PEGylation or polymer coating, could mask the nanoparticles from the immune system but might also contribute to toxicological risks if the surface coatings degrade or elicit immune responses. Additionally, MNPS and MSNS interactions with proteins in the blood can influence their biological behavior, and any unintended protein adsorption could lead to the formation of a protein corona that alters the drug release characteristics and reduces the effectiveness of the treatment [94, 95]. Therefore, systematic toxicity assessments, including acute and chronic toxicity studies and biodegradability testing, are necessary to apply MNPs and MSNs in clinical settings safely.

Regulatory and ethical considerations for clinical translation

The regulatory approval of MNPs and MSNs for clinical use presents multiple challenges. Since MNPs and MSNs are classified as nanomaterials, their approval process falls under stringent regulations established by bodies such as the FDA or EMA. These regulations require comprehensive data on the nanoparticles’ physicochemical properties, biocompatibility, and toxicity. Furthermore, clinical trials involving MNPs and MSNs require detailed documentation on long-term safety, efficacy, and manufacturing processes.

Another critical factor is ethical concerns related to nanomaterials. Issues such as environmental impact, human exposure, and the potential for misuse need to be addressed, particularly in the context of clinical translation for diseases such as malaria, which predominantly affect low-resource settings. There is a growing emphasis on responsible nanotechnology, which advocates for sustainable and ethical research practices in nanomedicine [96]. Additionally, there are concerns about accessibility and affordability for malaria-endemic regions, where high-cost therapies might face barriers to adoption. These regulatory and ethical issues require global collaboration to ensure the safe and equitable distribution of MNPS AND MSNS-based therapies.

Future trends: codelivery systems, multifunctional nanoparticles, and theranostics

The future of MNPs and MSNs in malaria treatment lies in advancing multifunctional nanoparticles and codelivery systems that simultaneously address therapeutic and diagnostic needs. Developing codelivery systems will allow the simultaneous release of multiple drugs, such as antimalarial agents, immune modulators, or chemoprotective agents, enabling better synergistic effects and reducing resistance development. By using MNPs and MSNs as carriers for combination therapies, the possibility of enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of both conventional and novel antimalarial drugs has dramatically increased [97]. Additionally, multifunctional nanoparticles integrating drug delivery, imaging, and targeting capabilities are being explored to improve diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic precision. For example, MNPs and MSNs can be conjugated with imaging agents such as fluorescent dyes or magnetic nanoparticles, allowing real-time tracking of drug distribution and efficacy [98].

Another exciting development in the MNPS and MSNS field is the application of theranostics, which combines therapy and diagnostics into a single platform. Theranostic nanoparticles are designed to detect disease while simultaneously delivering targeted therapy. MNPs and MSNs can be engineered to deliver antimalarial drugs while monitoring disease progression by imaging techniques such as MRI or PET scans. The dual functionality of MNPs and MSNs for both treatment and diagnosis has the potential to revolutionize malaria management by enabling personalized and real-time monitoring of therapeutic outcomes [31]. Furthermore, nanocarriers can be designed to target specific Plasmodium stages (e.g., merozoites, schizonts) or infected erythrocytes, thus minimizing the risk of collateral damage to healthy cells and increasing treatment specificity.

In the long term, integrating MNPs and MSNs in multimodal therapies involving drug delivery, gene therapy, and immune modulation offers a promising approach for treating malaria and combating resistance. These innovations will lead to smarter, more efficient treatments that combine therapeutic efficacy with real-time diagnostics, ultimately improving patient outcomes and advancing the personalization of malaria treatment [99–101].

Conclusion

Using magnetic and mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MNPs and MSNs) represents a promising strategy for advancing malaria treatment, particularly for improving the efficacy of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) therapy. The unique properties of MNPs and MSNs, such as their high surface area, biocompatibility, biodegradability, and magnetic responsiveness, make them ideal candidates for drug delivery systems to overcome the limitations of conventional antimalarial therapies. Through targeted drug delivery, controlled release mechanisms, and the ability to increase drug stability, MNPs, and MSNs can address key challenges in SP therapy, including poor bioavailability and drug resistance. The encapsulation of SPs within these nanoparticles offers the potential for localized drug release, reducing the systemic side effects typically associated with free drug administration and increasing the therapeutic index.

Further research is needed to refine the synthesis and scaling of MNPs and MSNs for clinical application and to explore their long-term safety and toxicity profiles. Additionally, the integration of multifunctional nanoparticles capable of co-delivering multiple drugs or even immunomodulatory agents holds promise for enhancing the effectiveness of SP therapy. Combining MNPs and MSNs with advanced imaging and theranostic approaches could also improve patient monitoring, treatment customization, and early detection of malaria resistance to SP-based therapies.

However, the successful application of MNPs and MSNs in malaria treatment hinges on the collaboration of interdisciplinary research among nanotechnology, pharmacology, immunology, and infectious diseases. Integrating the expertise of material scientists, pharmacologists, and clinicians is crucial to developing safe, scalable, and effective MNPS AND MSNS-based therapies. A holistic approach to research and development will help overcome existing hurdles in drug delivery systems, optimize therapeutic regimens, and ultimately lead to the clinical translation of MNPs and MSNs for treating malaria.

By bridging the fields of nanotechnology and infectious disease therapy, MNPs and MSNs have the potential to revolutionize the treatment landscape for malaria, contributing significantly to global efforts to reduce the burden of malaria, particularly in endemic regions. The future of malaria treatment could include MNPs and MSNs at the forefront of personalized medicine, combination therapies, and novel treatment paradigms.
