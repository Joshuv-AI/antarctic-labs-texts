Therapeutic Opportunities for Macrophage Targeted Nanomedicine in Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Jiahui Luo, Yanjun Hou, Yang Lin, Xiong Zhang, Weikai Zhang, Kai Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes mellitus is a common metabolic disease affecting more than 500 million people worldwide. Macrophages, driving inflammation, immune dysregulation, and tissue injury, contribute to diabetes progression and diabetic complications. Nanomedicine offers unique advantages in drug delivery, tissue accumulation, and macrophage targeting, making it particularly suitable for modulating macrophage-mediated pathological processes in diabetes. In this review, we describe the role of macrophages in the pathological progression of diabetes mellitus and further summarize current strategies for using nanoparticles to target macrophages. We highlight the diagnostic and therapeutic potential of macrophage-targeted nanoparticles in diabetes and its related complications. Finally, we discuss key translational challenges, including biodistribution, off-target uptake, long-term immunological safety, toxicity, and clinical applicability. This review provides a distinct framework for understanding how macrophage-targeted nanomedicine can be developed to improve diabetes diagnosis, therapeutic modulation, and the management of diabetic complications.Keywords: nanomedicine, diabetes, chronic inflammation, macrophage, immune modulation, clinical treatment

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is one of the common metabolic disorders and affects over half a billion people worldwide.1 According to the International Diabetes Federation Diabetes Atlas 2025, approximately 589 million adults aged 20–79 years were living with diabetes worldwide in 2024, and this number is projected to increase to 853 million by 2050.2 Diabetes is characterized by elevated blood glucose levels and is mainly divided into two types based on its pathogenesis.3 Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is an endocrine disorder resulting from autoimmune destruction in which islet beta cells stop producing insulin.4 Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is characterized by insulin resistance-caused hyperglycemia and is associated with diet and environment.5 In the pancreatic islet, pro-inflammatory macrophages locally amplify the progression of insulitis.6 These macrophages secrete inflammatory cytokines to support autoimmune T cells and aggravate β cell destruction during T1D.7 In addition, macrophages play a predominant role in the low-grade, chronic inflammation and impaired would healing in T2D.8 The inflammatory cells infiltration in adipose tissue is consistently associated with increased body weight and insulin resistance.9 Beyond pancreatic islets and adipose tissue, macrophages also contribute to tissue injury and inflammatory remodeling in diabetes-associated complications, including diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, cardiomyopathy, periodontitis, and delayed wound healing.

At present, conventional therapies for DM typically include glucose monitoring, lifestyle intervention, glucose-lowering drugs, and insulin administration.10 However, tedious blood glucose monitoring procedures often lead to poor compliance and discomfort for patients. Moreover, the use of glucose-lowering drugs in adolescents with diabetes has raised safety concerns.11 More importantly, although glucose-lowering therapy and insulin replacement are essential for diabetes management, they do not directly address macrophage-driven inflammation, immune dysregulation, and tissue injury that contribute to diabetes progression and diabetic complications. Similarly, non-targeted anti-inflammatory approaches may cause broad immune suppression and systemic adverse effects, while failing to precisely modulate pathogenic macrophage subsets in specific diabetic tissues. Thus, there is still a need for novel disease-monitoring tools and improved therapeutic strategies that can target the inflammatory and immune mechanisms underlying diabetes progression.

Recently, nanotechnology has created new opportunities for disease diagnosis and therapy. Nanomedicine-based strategies have shown promising effects on the treatment of cancer and inflammatory diseases.12 Extensive studies have shown that the surface functionalized nanoparticles (NPs) can be used as multifunctional and highly selective nanoengineered systems based on their ability to load therapeutic agents, such as small-molecule drugs, peptides, and small interfering RNA (siRNA).13,14 In our pervious study, we found siRNA-loaded NPs efficiently targeted macrophages and could be applied to the treatment of inflammatory diseases.13 Additionally, macrophage targeted nano-strategies show improved ability to delivery imaging agents to inflammatory sites in the pancreatic islet, which helps diagnose the severity of the disease progression.15 More importantly, NPs show great advantages in improving the pharmacokinetic profile and chemical stability, and reducing the off-target and systemic adverse effects of the drugs.16,17 Several nanomedicines have been approved by FDA and used in preclinical studies for cancer therapy.18 These properties make nanomedicine particularly suitable for macrophage-targeted intervention, because macrophages are highly heterogeneous, tissue-specific, and dynamically polarized during diabetes progression.

In this review, the literature search was performed using PubMed as the primary database. The principal search term was “macrophage AND nanomedicine AND diabetes”. The articles and review articles relevant to macrophage, NP-based imaging, therapies, diabetes and diabetes -associated complications were screened. We summarized research progress in macrophage-targeted nanomedicine for diabetes over the past 10 years and discussed the major challenges before clinical translation.

Macrophage Pathophysiology in Diabetes

Immune cells are present in all organs under physiological conditions and contribute to the metabolic processes.19 Among them, macrophages occupy a high proportion and have critical functions on metabolic tissues. Macrophages represent around 30–50% of total immune cells in the white adipose tissue and up to 80–90% in the pancreatic islets.20,21 Thus, macrophages play a dominant role in the progression of diabetes. These immune events promote β-cell dysfunction and apoptosis, ultimately contributing to autoimmune β-cell destruction. (b) In type 2 diabetes (T2D), circulating macrophages can be recruited into the pancreas, where they produce inflammatory cytokines and amplify local inflammation. This inflammatory microenvironment induces β-cell stress and impairs β-cell function. (c) In adipose tissue, macrophage phenotype changes with metabolic status. The Metabolic Function of Macrophages in Adipose Tissue

Macrophages typically exhibit M1 (pro-inflammatory) classically activated phenotypes and M2 (anti-inflammatory) alternatively activated phenotypes.20 M1 macrophages may be polarized by bacterial stimuli such as lipopolysaccharide and type 1 immune cell signals such as interferon (IFN)-γ, and pro-inflammatory factors such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α).22,23 Type 2 immune factors such as interleukin-4 and interleukin-13 induce M2 macrophage subsets.24 These macrophages mainly secrete cytokines such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), which promote angiogenesis, tissue homeostasis and wound healing.22 In addition, macrophages can interact with autoimmune T cells by acting as antigen presenting cells and participate in the destruction of β cells.25

As the largest proportion of immune cells in adipose tissue, macrophages can recruit monocytes through the chemotactic monocyte chemotactic protein 1 (MCP-1)/C-C chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2) system.26 During obesity, macrophages increase in proportion and accumulate in large numbers in the crown-like structures surrounding dying fat cells.27 Studies have shown that adipose tissue macrophages undergo a transition from an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype to a proinflammatory M1 activated state during the progression of obesity.28,29 These macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory factors such as interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and TNF-α, which aggravate insulin resistance.30 However, other reports suggest that the M1/M2 subsets may not be applicable to in vivo studies due to the heterogeneity and plasticity of macrophages.31,32

Inflammatory responses in macrophages activate toll-like receptor signaling pathways, promote NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and trigger the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, all of which exacerbate the chronic inflammatory state in diabetes.33,34 The inflammasome assembles in response to pathogen infection or “danger” signals and promotes the maturation and release of several pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-18.35 NLRP3 inflammasome activation is associated with the pathogenesis of diabetes. Inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome has been shown to reduce the inflammatory state and improve insulin resistance in the progression of diabetes.36,37

The Role of Macrophages in Islet Inflammation

Islet macrophages are derived from embryonic yolk sac and bone marrow hematopoietic cells, and are involved in islet morphogenesis and β-cell expansion.38 During insulitis, macrophages from the peripheral blood are recruited into the pancreas, leading to β-cell secretory dysfunction and β-cell apoptosis through the production of excessive IL-1β.39 In the islet microenvironment, activated macrophages act as antigen-presenting cells by uptaking and processing β-cell-derived antigens to activate autoreactive T cells and induce β-cell dysfunction, thereby contributing to the onset of T1D.4 Islet inflammation also occurs during T2D, which is characterized by an increased number of proinflammatory macrophages in the islets.40 Unlike in T1D where both innate and adaptive immune cells are involved in islet inflammation, macrophages play a primary role in the occurrence of insulitis in T2D.41 Macrophages secrete inflammatory cytokines and chemokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and MCP-1, which contribute to β-cell dysfunction and the progression of T2D.42

Macrophage Targeted Strategies of Nanomedicine

Macrophage-targeted NPs come in different sizes and are usually spherical.43,44 Compared with neutral particles, charged NPs may have a better ability to bind and deliver drugs.45,46 NPs can vary significantly in origin and composition. In general, macrophage-targeting strategies in nanomedicine can be broadly classified into passive targeting and active targeting.47 Passive targeting mainly refers to nonspecific macrophage-associated uptake mediated by the physical properties of NPs, such as particle size, surface charge, morphology, and circulation behavior.48 Macrophages can uptake unmodified NPs of medium size (range from 10 to 300 nm), including some extracellular vectors and liposomes, and act at infected or inflammatory sites where mononuclear/macrophage cells are abundant.15,48 However, passive targeting should be interpreted with caution, because NPs uptake by macrophages does not necessarily indicate macrophage-specific targeting. In contrast, active targeting refers to intentionally designed nanoplatforms that enhance macrophage recognition or uptake through specific mechanisms, such as ligand-receptor interactions, macrophage-associated surface markers, macrophage-specific promoters, or other macrophage-selective delivery strategies.49 Some receptors expressed on the surface of macrophages play an important role in the process of active targeting, such as mannose-receptor and CD44.50–52 These natural or synthetic NPs can target macrophages by adding these macrophage-specific ligands to their surface.53 Modified nanomaterials bind to macrophage surface receptors and are internalized through receptor-mediated endocytosis. Thus, the drug loaded onto the NPs can exert intracellular effects by targeting macrophages.

Given the important role of macrophages in the pathogenesis of diabetes, macrophage-targeted therapy may have a promising prospect. However, it is relatively difficult for traditional drug to achieve targeted therapies. Moreover, current imaging methods for diabetes diagnosis cannot noninvasively assess the progression and severity of diabetes. Therefore, nanotherapeutic approaches targeting macrophages may offer a potential approach to these problems. Their surfaces can be engineered with macrophage-recognizing ligands, such as peptides, proteins, antibodies, nucleic acids, or receptor-binding molecules, allowing them to interact with specific receptors expressed on macrophages. By improving tissue delivery and regulating macrophage activation, recruitment, or polarization, macrophage-targeted nanomedicine may help monitor diabetes progression and modulate inflammatory responses involved in insulitis, insulin resistance, and diabetic complications.

NPs for Diagnostic Applications

Previous studies have shown that accurate diagnosis of diabetes and early blood glucose management are important for the treatment of the disease and can prevent or delay many diabetes-related complications.54 However, traditional diagnostic methods, including assessment of fasting blood glucose levels, glycated hemoglobin levels, and oral glucose tolerance tests, can be painful or inconvenient for patients and may not accurately reflect disease progression.55 In addition, hyperglycemia symptoms may appear many years after the onset of diabetes, making early intervention difficult. To address these challenges, a variety of nanotechnologies have been developed for the early diagnosis of diabetes and the non-invasive prediction of disease severity. Macrophage-Targeted Nanoplatforms and Non-Invasive Diagnostic Imaging of Diabetes

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

DM is often accompanied by pancreatic β-cell dysfunction and macrophage infiltration in pancreatic islet. MRI provides high spatial resolution and good soft tissue contrast, allowing visualization of macrophage accumulation at inflammatory sites within the islets.15 Superparamagnetic iron oxide NPs have been widely used as imaging contrast agents for MRI contrast agents due to their superior safety and high biocompatibility.64 These NPs can be taken up by macrophages and have been used in the diagnosis of cancer and cardiovascular diseases.18,65,66 Increasing evidence has also demonstrated the potential value of NPs in diabetes diagnosis. For example, MRI using monocrystalline iron oxide-47 magnetic NPs (MION-47) was applied to noninvasively visualize the islet inflammation and predict the onset of diabetes in non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice.56 This approach also helped identify complement receptor of the immunoglobulin superfamily (CRIg), which marks a macrophage subset associated with protection from diabetes. Ferucarbotran-containing iron NPs have also been used to assess islet graft tolerance and rejection.57 Imaging showed that iron particles were undetectable in the rejected islet tissue, and this approach did not significantly impair the ability of the transplanted islets to treat experimental diabetes. In addition, magnetic NP-enhanced-MRI technology can be applied to T1D patients, as macrophages in inflamed islet regions can phagocytose NPs.58 To visualize islet inflammation, NPs of ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide fermoxran-10 shows magnetic particles accumulating in the pancreas of a T1D patient by MRI.59 Another study reported an optimized non-invasive, three-dimensional, high-resolution MRI technique.60 Notably, significant differences in pancreatic NP accumulation were observed between diabetic and healthy subjects, and patients with T1D showed marked inter- and intra-pancreatic heterogeneity in signal intensity.

Other Imaging Tools

With the application of NPs in MRI, multimodal imaging strategies that combine MRI with other diagnostic tools have also been investigated. For example, long-circulating magneto-fluorescent NPs, composed of superparamagnetic iron oxide and an amino-crosslinked dextran coating conjugated with an Alexa Fluor 488 fluorescent probe, have been used to visualize the pancreas in T1D mouse models.61 These fluorescently labeled NPs were phagocytosed by macrophages, enabling real-time monitoring of islet inflammation. Biocompatible silica-coated magnetic NPs contain a near-infrared fluorescence dye within a silica shell and a magnetic core have also been developed. These near-infrared fluorescent magnetic NPs were used to track macrophage migration to inflammatory tissues.62 In addition, positron emission tomography (PET)/computer tomography (CT) strategies have been applied to visualize macrophages migration in vivo. The use of highly sensitive and stable radionuclide embedded gold NPs could be a nuclear biological imaging platform to visually track macrophage migration to the site of inflammatory lesions.63 With the continued development of imaging technology, more powerful NP-based imaging strategies are expected to improve the clinical diagnosis and monitoring of diabetes.

Taken together, current NP-based imaging strategies have shown promising potential for detecting macrophage-associated islet inflammation in diabetes. MRI-based iron oxide NPs remain the most widely investigated platform, whereas fluorescence/MRI and PET/CT-based systems may provide complementary advantages in sensitivity and macrophage tracking. However, most evidence remains preclinical, and many platforms depend on nonspecific macrophage phagocytosis rather than validated macrophage-specific targeting. Therefore, further studies are needed to compare the diagnostic sensitivity, tissue biodistribution, off-target uptake, long-term safety, and clinical feasibility of different imaging nanoplatforms.

Examples of Preclinical-Stage Macrophage-Targeted Nanomedicine for Diabetes Treatment

T1D

Traditional treatments for diabetes are usually based on injection of insulin.87 However, these approaches can be inconvenient and are associated with adverse effects, such as hypoglycaemia, and they may not maintain long-term glycemic stability in all patients.88 At present, nanomaterials synthesized from polymerized ursodeoxycholic acid have been developed as carriers for oral insulin administration.89 These NPs can protect insulin from degradation, enhance intestinal insulin absorption, and promote M2 macrophage polarization.89

Emerging studies have shown that nanotechnology can improve the efficiency, bioavailability, and safety of insulin replacement therapy. Considering the important role of macrophages in the pathogenesis of diabetes, macrophage-targeted nanomaterials may also provide therapeutic benefits by modulating insulitis and diabetes progression. For instance, oral administration of exenatide polylactide-co-glycoside NPs increased the stability of exenatide in the gastrointestinal tract, enhanced the absorption of exenatide, and reduced the glycaemic content of streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats.67 In vitro experiments showed that RAW 264.7 macrophages can effectively mediate the endocytosis and transport of exenatide polylactide-co-glycoside NPs through β-glucan receptor, suggesting that there may be a targeted absorption pathway in intestinal macrophages, which provides a potential strategy for oral drug delivery.67 Modulating inflammatory macrophage activity has also been considered an effective therapeutic approach. Macrophage-targeting NPs containing S100a9 siRNA mitigated streptozotocin-induced inflammation and calcification of atherosclerotic plaques in diabetic mice.68 In another study, liposomal NPs containing CCR2 siRNA targeted inflammatory monocytes/macrophages, and CCR2 downregulation inhibited macrophage accumulation at inflammatory sites. This treatment reduced the number of inflammatory monocytes in atherosclerotic plaques after islet transplantation in diabetic mice and prolonged normoglycemia.69 Furthermore, PEGylated bilirubin NPs can protect islet cells by removing scavenging reactive oxygen molecules and inhibiting the production of inflammatory factors secreted by activated macrophages, thus serving as an antioxidant and anti-inflammatory adjunct for islet xenotransplantatio.90

Taken together, macrophage-targeted nanotherapeutics for T1D have shown promising effects in improving oral drug delivery, reducing inflammatory macrophage activation, limiting macrophage recruitment, and protecting transplanted islets. However, the strength of evidence remains largely preclinical, with most studies performed in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rodents, NOD mice, or islet transplantation models. These models capture selected aspects of β-cell injury or autoimmune inflammation but do not fully recapitulate the complex and heterogeneous immune pathogenesis of human T1D. In addition, several platforms rely on macrophage uptake or macrophage modulation rather than rigorously validated macrophage-specific targeting. Long-term immunological safety is another important concern, because sustained suppression of macrophage recruitment or inflammatory signalling may impair host defense or tissue repair. Therefore, future studies should directly compare different nanoplatforms in terms of macrophage-targeting specificity, preservation of β-cell function, and safety before clinical translation.

T2D

During the onset of T2D, macrophage infiltration in adipose tissue is often accompanied by the production of large amounts of inflammatory cytokines, which aggravate insulin resistance. Therefore, manipulating macrophage function by suppressing inflammatory signaling pathways in macrophages has therapeutic potential for T2D. This can be achieved using small-molecule drugs, biomolecules, or gene-editing nanomaterials.

Small-Molecule Drugs

At present, numerous studies have shown the therapeutic effects of macrophage-targeted NPs in T2D. One study reported that nanoformulated copper/zinc superoxide dismutase reduced macrophage accumulation and inflammatory response in adipose tissue and promoted triglyceride metabolism during obesity.70 To target activated macrophages in adipose tissue, self-assembled hyaluronic acid NPs were developed to target CD44-positive macrophages and reduce the proportion of macrophages in adipose tissue, thereby suppressing inflammatory cytokine expression and NLRP3 inflammasome activation in obese mice.71 In addition, nanopolysaccharides based on biocompatible glucose polymers can effectively target adipose macrophages. Local peritoneal administration of NPs loaded with anti-inflammatory drugs mitigated inflammatory responses in the adipose tissue of obese mice.72 Another study reported that NPs containing the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) inhibitor, Curcusome, could target adipose tissue macrophages (ATMs) in ob/ob mice. These NPs inhibited NF-κB/RELA binding activity, reduced TNF-α release and increased IL-4 production, thereby improving symptoms of insulin resistance.73

siRNA, shRNA, and Plasmids

Recently, biomolecules-encapsulated lipid-polymer NPs have emerged as effective tools for regulating macrophage function by silencing or overexpressing target genes. For instance, glucan-encapsulated RNAi particles have been found to specifically deliver siRNA and subsequently silence gene expression in ATMs of obese mice.74 Chitosan nanomicelles linked to ATM and adipocyte ligands have been designed to deliver TNF-α/MCP-1 shRNA. Subcutaneous administration of these shRNA-loaded NPs reduced inflammatory cytokines levels and increased adiponectin levels, which increased glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in diabetic obese mice.75 Another study showed that mannose-ylated polyethylenimide NPs containing CD163 plasmids promoted M2 macrophage polarization levels in healthy patients.76

Gene Editing

Gene knockout using CRISPR-delivery particles in adipose tissue can target macrophages and exert therapeutic effects in metabolic diseases.91 Jun Wang et al developed optimized cationic lipid-assisted NPs (CLANs) that could deliver Cas9 mRNA and guide RNA into macrophages. CLANs carrying Cas9 mRNA and NLRP3 guide RNA disrupted NLRP3 expression and inhibited NLRP3 inflammasome activation in macrophages, thereby improving insulin sensitivity and reducing adipose tissue inflammation in high-fat diet (HFD)-treated mice.77 In addition, the same group designed NPs encapsulating CRISPR/Cas9 plasmids to achieve macrophage-specific Cas9 expression. Macrophage-specific promoter driven Cas9 expression plasmids (pM458 and pM330) were constructed and encapsulated in CLAN, Because Cas9 expression was driven by the CD68 promoter, CLANpM330/sgNtn1 enabled macrophage- or monocyte-specific knockout of Ntn1. Intravenously administration of CLANpM330/sgNtn1 disrupted the Ntn1 gene in macrophages and thereby showed therapeutic effect on T2D.78

Overall, macrophage-targeted nanotherapeutics for T2D include small-molecule delivery, RNA-based gene regulation, and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing. However, in some studies, NPs mainly function as drug carriers rather than rigorously validated macrophage-specific targeting systems, and their targeting specificity may remain limited even when administered locally. Although more advanced platforms, such as CLAN-based delivery systems, have shown improved macrophage-targeting potential, further validation in diverse animal models is still required. In addition, the durability of metabolic benefits, long-term safety, and compatibility with conventional glucose-lowering therapies remain to be fully investigated.

Diabetes Associated Complications

Diabetic Wounds

As one of the most common complications of T2D, impaired wound healing often leads to lower limb amputation and disability.92 During this process, macrophages are recruited to the wound site and participate in the healing process.93 Under normal conditions, macrophages transition from a pro-inflammatory phenotype to an anti-inflammatory and pro-repair phenotype, secreting cytokines such as (TGF-β and regulating the functions of keratinocytes and fibroblasts to promote tissue repair.94 However, this phenotypic transition is impaired in diabetic wounds, resulting in persistent inflammation and delayed wound healing. Given the plasticity of macrophages during impaired wound repair, nanomaterials have been developed to modulate macrophage function in T2D.

Macrophage-targeted nanomaterials can be designed to recognize the specific epitopes expressed on the surface of macrophages, such as macrophage mannose-receptors. Recently, KGM-modified SiO2 NPs have been found to promote M2 polarization by inducing mannose-receptors to aggregate on the cell surface.79 TGF-β and other cytokines produced by treated macrophages can promote fibroblast proliferation. In acute and chronic skin injury models, KGM-modified SiO2 NPs treatment significantly accelerated wound closure and collagen production, and reduced inflammatory responses and abnormal blood vessel formation in regenerated tissue.79 Mannose-decorated globular lysine dendrimers (MGLDs) can also target macrophages and reprogram them toward an M2 phenotype.80 These MGLDs are spherical particles with a positive surface charge and an average size of approximately 50–200 nm. MGLDs can reduce the inflammatory factor in macrophages and promote the expression of anti-inflammatory mediators such as TGF-β, and enhance the proliferation ability of fibroblasts, thereby accelerating wound repair in T2D mice.80 Impaired myeloid-to-fibroblast epithelioid cell conversion has also been reported in diabetic wounds. This defect can be improved using macrophage-targeted mannose-tagged NPs and miR-21-loaded lipid NPs.81 In addition, NPs encapsulating small molecules or siRNA to manipulate genes expression in macrophages have shown potential effect on the diabetic would repair. For example, macrophage-targeted NPs link glucan-carrying NPs loaded with GSK-J1, a known Jumonji domain-containing protein 3 (JMJD3) covalent inhibitor, specifically inhibited JMJD3 in macrophages and improved tissue repair in diabetic wounds.82 Moreover, lipidoid NPs loaded with siTNF-α reduced TNF-α expression in macrophages, inhibited macrophage infiltration at the wound sites, and accelerate tissue repair.83

Diabetic Nephropathy

Diabetic nephropathy can progress to end-stage renal failure.95 Because its early clinical manifestations are often subtle or asymptomatic, diabetic nephropathy is usually not diagnosed until the later stages of disease progression.96 Therefore, early diagnosis and timely treatment of DM are urgently needed. Low-density lipoprotein receptor-1 (LOX-1)-targeted iron oxide NPs have been developed for the detection of early diabetic nephropathy. Since early diabetic nephropathy is often accompanied by inflammation and activation of LOX-1, anti-LOX-1 ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (USPIOs) can detect LOX-1-rich inflammatory kidney injury in mouse models of early diabetic nephropathy.84 Renal T2-weighted MRI showed decreased signal intensity in the renal cortex 24 h after USPIO administration in mice with diabetic nephropathy, suggesting that this approach may be useful for monitoring early diabetic nephropathy.84

Diabetic Retinopathy

Neovascularization is a common characteristic of diabetic retinopathy. Recently, novel hybrid NPs have been used to selectively visualize hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha mRNA in microglia/macrophages in a mouse model of retinal neovascularization. Selective visualization of these microglia/macrophages may provide a powerful approach for predicting neovascularization and monitoring its progression at the molecular level.97 Another study reported dendrimer-triamcinolone acetonide conjugate could be selectively taken by activated microglia/macrophages and retinal pigment epithelial cells. This dendrimer-triamcinolone acetonide conjugate can significantly inhibit choroidal neovascularization (> 80% inhibition, > 50 times better than free drugs). It also suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine production, limited macrophage infiltration in pathological areas, and reduced choroidal and retinal inflammation.98

Periodontitis

Periodontitis is a common complication of diabetes and is often associated with an increased abundance of pathogenic bacteria under hyperglycemic conditions, leading to severe inflammatory responses in periodontal tissues.99 In addition, periodontitis is generally recognized as an important risk factor for diabetes because it can exacerbate insulin resistance.100 A Resveratrol-grafted mesoporous silica NPs drug carrier system provides effective and sustainable immune regulation for the treatment of diabetic periodontitis.85 Activation of sirtuin 1/adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase pathway and inhibition of NF-κB pathway promoted M2 macrophage polarization, alleviated insulin resistance symptoms and improved glucose metabolism.85

Diabetic Cardiomyopathy

Diabetic cardiomyopathy is a common cardiovascular complication in postmenopausal women with diabetes.101 Estrogen deficiency caused by oophorectomy has been shown to increase cardiac inflammation in diabetic mice, as manifested by excessive ratio of M1 to M2 macrophages.86 Abnormal increases in reactive oxygen species, apoptosis of cardiomyocyte, cardiac hypertrophy, and fibrosis were also observed in the hearts of ovariectomized diabetic mice.86 Gold NPs containing miR-155 antagonists delivered to macrophages and restored cardiac function in ovariectomized diabetic mice by increasing M2 polarization.86

Importantly, macrophage-targeted nanomedicine should not be interpreted as a uniform anti-inflammatory strategy across all diabetic complications. The desired macrophage response may differ by tissue and disease stage: pro-repair polarization may benefit diabetic wound healing, whereas excessive M2-like activation may contribute to fibrosis in kidney or cardiac complications. Similarly, macrophage suppression in periodontitis should be balanced against antimicrobial defense, and macrophage modulation in retinopathy should consider its potential influence on pathological angiogenesis. Thus, future platforms should be designed for tissue- and stage-specific macrophage reprogramming rather than broad macrophage inhibition or generalized M2 polarization (Figure 4).

Challenges and Perspectives

Although nanotechnology has shown many advantages in diabetes management, further preclinical studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of its clinical transformation. At present, some problems still need to be considered for the clinical transformation of nanomedicine. First, although many new nanodrugs targeting macrophages have been developed and shown to be effective in preclinical studies, their clinical trials have yet to be conducted. Further, there is a need not only to evaluate whether nanomaterials can improve glucose sensitivity in clinical patients, but also to monitor their long-term effectiveness in treating diabetes. Second, NPs targeting macrophages generally act by affecting macrophage activation and polarization, which has a short-term therapeutic effect in reducing inflammatory responses. However, it remains to be studied whether the immune deficiency will have side effects on the progression of other diseases. In addition, although developed nanomaterials are designed to target macrophages and act on a specific tissue, such as pancreas or adipose tissue, they will inevitably interact with other cells and act on other organs, which may lead to additional side effects. Finally, we currently know very little about the rational properties of NPs. Increased research on the size, charge and morphological composition of NPs will provide a more effective means for the development of nanomaterials targeting macrophages. At present, there are emerging studies in the field of nanotechnology, and the gene therapy through nanomaterials can provide new solutions for immune regulation in diabetes at the genetic level. In addition, nanotherapeutics may also be combined with the treatment of traditional medicines to develop more advanced combination platforms.

This review has several limitations. First, most available studies on macrophage-targeted nanomedicine in diabetes remain preclinical, and the lack of clinical evidence limits direct translation to patients. Second, the included nanoplatforms differ substantially in composition, targeting strategy, payload, disease model, and outcome assessment, making direct comparison difficult. Third, macrophage phenotypes are highly heterogeneous and tissue dependent. Therefore, simplified M1/M2-based interpretations in this review may not fully reflect the complex roles of macrophages in diabetes and its complications.

Conclusion

Macrophage-targeted nanomedicine provides a promising strategy for diabetes diagnosis, therapeutic modulation, and management of diabetic complications. This review summarizes recent advances in nanoplatforms designed to visualize macrophage-associated inflammation, improve drug delivery, regulate macrophage migration and polarization, and modulate tissue-specific immune responses in diabetes. Current evidence supports the potential of NP-based imaging for monitoring islet inflammation and of therapeutic nanoplatforms for reducing inflammatory macrophage activation, improving insulin sensitivity, protecting β cells, and promoting tissue repair. Nevertheless, most studies remain at the preclinical stage, and the strength of evidence varies among T1D, T2D, and different diabetic complications. Future research should develop more active macrophage-targeting strategies, validate disease-stage-specific macrophage function, and systematically evaluate biodistribution, off-target effects, long-term safety, toxicity, and compatibility with conventional glucose-lowering therapies. These efforts will be essential for translating macrophage-targeted nanomedicine from experimental models to clinical diabetes management.
