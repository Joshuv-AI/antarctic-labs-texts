Arthroscopic Treatment of Patellar Clunk Syndrome After Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Maxwell C Alley, W Doug Werry, Charles A Guerra, Hadley Winslow, Margaret Hedeman, Alan Shi, Brian T Samuelsen, Philip H Aurigemma, Mark J Lemos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatellar clunk syndrome is a complication of total knee arthroplasty. Surgical treatment with arthroscopic irrigation and debridement and lysis of adhesions is often required when conservative treatment fails. This study examines patient improvement following arthroscopic treatment of patellar clunk.MethodsThis single-surgeon case series uses a posterior-stabilized implant design. We performed a retrospective review of all patients from September 2013 to November 2020 treated for patellar clunk syndrome with arthroscopic debridement. Patient variables analyzed included knee flexion/extension angle and symptoms, including pain and mechanical symptoms. Analysis used paired t-tests with an alpha level of 0.05.ResultsFifty procedures were examined. Mean patient age was 61.4 years (standard deviation [SD] = 8.57 years), and 64% were female. Before arthroscopy, the mean maximum knee extension (KE) of the affected side was 0.85° (SD = 3°), and the mean maximum knee flexion (KF) was 120.5° (SD = 9.4°). At the last follow-up, the mean maximum KE was 0.36° (SD = 1.3°), and the mean maximum KF was 123.1° (SD = 9.4°). Paired t-tests comparing preoperative and postoperative KF and KE were P = .138 and P = .487, respectively. All patients reported less pain, and 90% (45/50) reported mechanical symptom improvement.ConclusionsArthroscopic treatment of symptomatic patellar clunk syndrome is safe and effective: almost all included patients experienced symptom relief, with a slight improvement in KF. This was a retrospective cohort study with a level of evidence of III.Keywords: Patellar clunk, Total knee arthroplasty, Arthroscopic irrigation, Debridement, Knee extension, Posterior-stabilized implant

Introduction

Patellar clunk syndrome can be a complication of total knee arthroplasty (TKA), where the growth of scar tissue on the posterior and superior aspect of the patella creates an impingement during the normal patellar arc of motion with knee flexion [1]. The result manifests in a variety of ways, ranging from catching sensations to grinding, popping, and pain, all of which can be limiting to patients [1,2]. A major contributor to patellar clunk development involves posterior-stabilized TKA implants [3,4]. Notable examples of prostheses with higher incidence rates include the Axiom PSK investigated by Anderson et al. [5] and the DePuy AMK Congruency TKA [6,7]. Additionally, a variety of other factors that contribute to the development of patellar clunk have been identified. These include anatomical variability, such as the Insall-Salvati Ratio (defined as the ratio of length between the patellar tendon and the patella itself) [[7], [8], [9]], joint line level changes relative to the hip and ankle [9,10], and patellar thickness [9,11,12]. Patellar clunk has also been linked to operative factors, including tibial tray position in the anterior-posterior plane [[8], [9], [10]].

With over 4.5 million TKA implants currently in use in the United States [13], and a patellar clunk incidence of up to 17% depending on prosthetic design [14], addressing adequate prevention, treatment, and resolution of this complication is essential for better patient outcomes. When posterior-stabilized prostheses are used, selecting components with a lower intercondylar box ratio has been shown to lessen the incidence of patellar clunk. Avoiding undersized patellar components can also be beneficial [15]. When patellar clunk does not resolve and is particularly painful for a patient, treatment options can initially include nonoperative management, such as physical therapy and NSAIDs if appropriate. However, these options have limited efficacy, and typically, surgical intervention is required [16].

Surgical treatment of symptomatic patellar clunk involves removal of the suprapatellar tissue, typically accomplished via arthroscopy or potentially patellar component revision [15,17,18]. While debridement has been proven to be successful at initially resolving symptoms, there is evidence for the potential of recurrence [[19], [20], [21]]. This study investigates the outcomes of patients with TKA who subsequently developed patellar clunk and underwent arthroscopic debridement at a single medical center. By investigating how well arthroscopy improves patient symptoms both in the short and long term, we hope to understand how effective this treatment modality is for patellar clunk. We hypothesize that arthroscopic debridement of patellar clunk syndrome improves patient outcomes regarding pain and mechanical symptoms, without a significant impact on knee flexion and extension.

Material and methods

This is a single-surgeon retrospective cohort study of patients treated with arthroscopic debridement. Patients who underwent arthroscopic debridement for patellar clunk from September 2013 to November 2020 were included in the study. For all patients, the implant used in the primary TKA was a posterior stabilized design (PFC Sigma, DePuy Synthes, Warsaw, IN, USA). In patients with symptoms indicative of patellar clunk syndrome, including pain, clunk, crepitus, popping, catching, and locking, nonoperative management was attempted first if symptoms were mild. If these conservative treatment measures failed (such as activity modification and physical therapy), patients were offered arthroscopic irrigation and debridement and lysis of adhesions.

Surgical technique

The senior author, a sports medicine fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeon, performed all arthroscopic debridement procedures. The arthroscope and instruments were introduced into the patellofemoral articulation using inferolateral and inferomedial portals (and if needed, superolateral and superomedial portals were utilized). The patellofemoral articulation was inspected with the knee in flexion and full extension. Consistently, a fibrous, hypertrophic nodule was identified on the undersurface of the extensor mechanism, at the junction of the proximal patellar pole and the quadriceps tendon. Hypertrophic tissue and synovium were thoroughly debrided from the undersurface of the extensor mechanism and the anterior surface of the distal femur using a motorized shaver and/or a bipolar radiofrequency probe. Additionally, similar debridement was performed to the medial and lateral gutters as well as the intercondylar notch. Throughout the procedure, care was taken to prevent damage to the arthroplasty components, and hemostasis was performed to prevent postoperative hemarthrosis. At the end of the procedure, the patellofemoral articulation was inspected with the knee in flexion for any remaining soft tissue impingement within the proximal flange of the femoral component.

All patients were treated with a postoperative rehabilitation protocol. This included weight-bearing as tolerated, full active knee range of motion exercises, and straight leg lifts. Specific values, such as pre- and post-arthroscopic debridement maximum knee flexion and extension (the maximum amount a patient can flex/extend their knee, respectively) were collected. Other values collected included intraoperative and postoperative complications and subjective patient pain reports.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software. Paired t-test was used to compare the variables of maximum knee flexion and knee extension preoperatively and postoperatively. Statistical significance was set at a P value of <0.05. Missing data were excluded from this analysis.

Results

This study examined 50 arthroscopic debridement procedures following TKA. During the same period (September 2013 to November 2020), 3378 TKAs were performed at the institution, suggesting that approximately 1.5% of TKAs that lead to symptomatic PCS required this surgical intervention. The mean age of patients at the time of the primary TKA was 61.4 years (range of 41 to 84 years old, standard deviation of 8.57 years). The majority of patients were female (based on binary assignment of male/female chosen by patient), with a proportion of 64% (32/50 patients). Indications for the primary TKA included osteoarthritis, osteonecrosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and failed prior knee arthroplasty (Fig. 1). Osteoarthritis accounted for most of the TKA indications (84%). The laterality of TKA/arthroscopic debridement was left in 52% of cases (26/50 patients). Mean time from TKA to arthroscopic debridement for patellar clunk was 31.8 months (range of 3.47 to 166.8 months, with a standard deviation of 32 months). This led to a mean age of 64 years at the time of arthroscopic debridement (range of 45.6 to 85.4 years, standard deviation of 8.2 years). These data are captured in Table 1.

Indications for initial total knee arthroplasty are reported above. Osteoarthritis was the most common indication, found in 84% (42 of 50) of patients. Rheumatoid arthritis (2 of 50), osteonecrosis (2 of 50), and failed previous total knee arthroplasty (1 of 50) were other indications. An indication could not be found in 3 of 50 patients upon chart review.

The study examined 50 arthroscopic debridements performed for patients who developed symptomatic patellar clunk syndrome (PCS) after TKA. While TKAs were performed by various surgeons at the institution, all debridements included in this study were performed by a single surgeon. Data and subsequent analysis were compiled and performed retrospectively.

Prior to arthroscopic debridement of patellar clunk, mean maximum knee extension of the affected side was 0.85° in patients (range of 0 to 15° with a standard deviation of 3°). Prearthroscopy mean maximum knee flexion of the affected side was 120.5° (range of 90° to 132° with a standard deviation of 9.4°). No patients experienced any reported intraoperative complications during patellar clunk arthroscopic debridement.

The mean time from arthroscopic debridement for patellar clunk to last in-person follow-up was 16.5 months (range of 0.37 to 92.3 months, standard deviation of 20.3 months). At the date of last follow-up, the mean maximum knee extension was 0.36° (range of 0 to 5°, with a standard deviation of 1.3°). The mean maximum knee flexion at last follow-up was 123.1° (range of 95° to 135°, standard deviation of 9.4°). Paired t-tests comparing preoperative and postoperative knee flexion and extension were not found to be significant (P = .138 and P = .487, respectively). These findings are displayed in Figure 2. Unmatched data, whether from missing preflexion/extension or postflexion/extension values, were omitted from this statistical analysis. Following arthroscopic debridement for patellar clunk, all patients included in this chart review reported an improvement in their pain. Mechanical symptom improvement was reported in 90% of patients (45/50). The remaining 10% of patients (5/50) were unspecified. Despite some relief, 10% of patients (5/50) still reported residual mechanical symptoms. Other lingering effects following arthroscopic debridement included neuropathic symptoms (tingling and numbness) with decreased sensation in the distribution of the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve for up to a year, which is common and rarely permanent. Therefore, this was noted in 4% of patients (2/50). Given that true complications are unexpected outcomes of surgical intervention, the overall complication rate in this study is considered 4% (2/50 patients with neuropathy), likely due to the index procedure.

Discussion

Upon analysis of arthroscopic debridement of 50 cases for patellar clunk syndrome performed by the senior author between September 2013 and November 2020, we found that all patients reported a reduction in knee pain, and the majority noted an improvement in mechanical symptoms (such as popping, clicking, etc.). This is the largest series to date. A similar rate of nearly universal pain reduction following arthroscopic treatment of patellar clunk has been reported in several other studies as well. In a prospective study examining 32 knees that initially underwent TKA with an IB-II prosthetic, Lucas et al. [17] found all but 1 patient reported pain reduction as well as improvement in functionality (via Knee Society Score). Another retrospective review of 15 cases of patellar clunk treated with arthroscopy also noted decreases in pain (from a mean score of 5/10 down to 1/10 by last follow-up) and crepitus symptoms (from a mean score 6/10 down to 1/10 by last follow-up) [22]. Further, an additional study of 12 patients who underwent arthroscopy for patellar clunk also reported relief of painful crepitus or clunk in all patients [23]. Based on these findings, it is reasonable to postulate that arthroscopic debridement of patellar clunk syndrome is a mechanism for pain relief and improved functionality. However, medical literature also suggests that symptom recurrence is possible, even after surgical revision of patellar clunk [[19], [20], [21]].

Regarding measurements of knee flexion and extension, similar to the results of this study, both Lucas et al. [17] and Dajani et al. [22] also reported minimal loss in knee flexion and extension postoperatively following arthroscopic treatment of patellar clunk. In contrast, the results of this study show a higher maximum knee flexion postoperatively than in these other 2 studies (mean of 123.1° vs 107.2 and 112, respectively) [17,22]. This finding is intriguing, as it has been noted that patients who achieve higher degrees of postoperative knee flexion in the first year after arthroplasty have been correlated to a higher incidence of patellar clunk [9,[24], [25], [26]]. With this in mind, further evaluation assessing the patellar clunk recurrence rates of patients within this study could be useful. Assessing whether increased postoperative knee flexion after arthroscopy for patellar clunk impacts the likelihood of recurrence, similar to what has been noted with initial TKA, may help illuminate the mechanism behind this phenomenon.

Overall, with a notable reduction in pain and mechanical symptoms across all patients, and in combination with no intraoperative complications, arthroscopic debridement appears to be an effective method for addressing bothersome patellar clunk symptoms. Based on the results of this study, the overall postoperative complications of arthroscopic debridement for patellar clunk (4%) appear similar to those reported for overall knee arthroscopy procedures (4.7%), such as meniscus repair or anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction [27]. These complications were minor and did not affect patients' overall satisfaction. Additionally, and most pertinently, all patients in this study have undergone prior TKA of their respective knees, a procedure known to have higher complication rates [28]. It is reasonable to suspect that undergoing this invasive procedure on the ipsilateral knee impacted complication rates.

While other studies have assessed patient outcomes following arthroscopic treatment of patellar clunk from TKA, this study is unique in the large case size it provides [17,22,23]. Furthermore, while other studies involving arthroscopic treatment of patellar clunk exist, they lack assessment of the specific parameters that this study addresses, including preoperative and postoperative knee flexion/extension and rates of persistent pain, crepitus, and other complications [12].

Limitations

In addition to this analysis being a retrospective chart review and the associated biases that come with this method of research, there are several notable limitations to this study. While all patients in this study were required to be 2 years postarthroscopy to ensure proper duration for negative outcomes to be reported, the timeliness of their actual follow-up exams was highly variable, evident by the “time to last follow-up” range of 0.37 to 92.3 months. Given this variability, it is highly possible that patient pain, mechanical symptoms reported, and maximum knee flexion/extension angles might have been reported differently had scores and evaluations been recorded at specific intervals, such as the 1-year mark used in the study by Lucas et al. [17] Moreover, institutional data on how many patients with symptomatic patellar clunk syndrome chose not to undergo arthroscopic treatment after failing conservative management were unavailable, and thus the focus of this study was on the clinical outcomes of patients who did undergo arthroscopic debridement for PCS. Additionally, the use of additional scoring systems, such as the Knee Society Score, which also assesses functionality and patient mobility, would have been a useful mechanism to further assess outcomes and has been used in other similar studies [12,17,22,23].

Conclusions

The objective of this retrospective cohort study was to examine patient outcomes following arthroscopic debridement of patellar clunk symptoms, specifically regarding knee pain, mechanical symptoms, and maximum knee flexion and extension. Overall, patients showed improvements in pain and mechanical symptoms, with no significant changes in maximum knee flexion or extension between preoperation and postoperation. While some mechanical symptoms and discomfort persisted postoperatively, the procedure was well-tolerated overall, with no intraoperative complications. Based on this evidence, we suggest that arthroscopic treatment of symptomatic patellar clunk is safe and effective.
