Hepatitis C self-testing and self-collection among people with experience in the Canadian carceral system: a qualitative study
Authors: Tamara Barnett, Kody Muncaster, Olivia Gemma, Chance Cordon, Andrew B. Mendlowitz, Guillaume Fontaine, Karen A. Campbell, Mia. J. Biondi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe World Health Organization (WHO) has identified hepatitis C virus (HCV) self-testing as a priority to achieve global HCV elimination goals. However, clinical utility and acceptability of HCV self-testing remain limited, especially among individuals with experience of incarceration, a population disproportionally impacted by HCV. This study aimed to explore the perspectives, preferences, and needs of individuals with experience in the Canadian correctional system in the context of HCV self-testing delivery.MethodsIndividuals with lived experience in the Canadian carceral system were recruited through a national prisoner advocacy organization. Semi-structured interviews were conducted to assess needs and identify preferences for HCV self-testing and self-collection. Data were analysed using an interpretive description methodology to generate clinically relevant insights to inform HCV self-testing rollout programs for people with incarceration experience.Result(s)Fifteen participants completed interviews between August and October of 2023. Analysis revealed four themes: (1) authentic representation as “words carry power”; (2) altruism triumphs in the face of stigma and uncertainty; (3) distrust of society, systems and even self, further shaped preferences for privacy, autonomy and control and this was paired with; (4) low-barrier and accessible options described as wanting the process to be “easy, easy, easy.” Participants were open to receiving HCV antibody test kits in many ways, with nearly half preferring immediately upon release. Themes informed expectations for promotional materials and that it should be a reflection of the population. Overall distrust of systems led to the desire for flexible options for receiving results, including phone, text, in-person, or email.Conclusion(s)Participants provided valuable insight into how a self-testing program may be shaped and delivered. This study contributes to the growing evidence base on the implementation of HCV self-testing, ultimately supporting efforts to increase testing rates and linkage to care among those who may benefit from it.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13198-6.

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) has called for the elimination of hepatitis C virus (HCV) as a public health threat by 2030 [1]. Historically, HCV care was complex with treatment provided by specialists only; however significant advances such as the availability of point-of-care testing, decentralization of care, and task shifting, has greatly improved access to testing and diagnosis [2]. Despite this progress, lower than ideal diagnosis rates persist, reflecting systemic barriers in reaching those with limited healthcare access, people who inject drugs (PWID), and individuals who are currently or formerly incarcerated [3]. People who are currently or formerly incarcerated are recognized by the WHO as a priority population for HCV elimination efforts [4], due to the high prevalence of HCV in correctional settings and the intersection between incarceration and the criminalization of drug use [5]. Individuals in carceral settings remain underdiagnosed and undertreated, presenting a significant global public health challenge [6]. In Canada, those incarcerated in federal facilities typically have access to HCV care and treatment. However, the provincial/regional landscape is quite different. Prevalence is not well understood and varies depending on the study, but a report released from the Public Health Agency of Canada in 2025, described a prevalence of 11% in federal correctional facilities and 14% in provincial facilities [7]. Furthermore, most facilities do not have programmatic approaches to testing, and even when someone is aware of their status, they may wait months for care and treatment. Expanding and scaling up testing strategies such as point-of-care and self-testing is essential to bridge the diagnostic gap by enabling faster diagnosis, treatment initiation and effective prevention [8].

The WHO’s Global Health Sector Strategy On, Respectively, HIV, Viral Hepatitis and Sexually Transmitted Infections for the Period of 2022–2030 [9] provides a framework to guide elimination efforts. It emphasizes decentralizing healthcare services (shifting healthcare delivery from central facilities to community-based settings), improving equity, and enhancing patient autonomy through differentiated testing approaches such as point-of-care testing and community-led models [10, 11]. Building on experience from other infections, the WHO issued a recommendation in 2021 identifying HCV self-testing as a priority to achieve global HCV elimination goals, basing this largely on evidence from HIV and COVID-19 self-testing rather than HCV-specific data [12]. HIV and COVID-19 self-testing have been shown to be accurate, feasible, and acceptable across diverse populations and settings, increasing testing access, uptake, and earlier linkage to care, which are outcomes that are equally critical for HCV elimination efforts [12, 13]. HCV self-testing, as part of decentralization and task-shifting to the patient, allows individuals to collect their own specimens, perform rapid diagnostic testing, and interpret their own results privately, addressing concerns with confidentiality and promoting greater autonomy in the testing process [12]. However, there is very limited empirical evidence on the use, acceptability, and implementation of HCV self-testing among people with incarceration experience, either in correctional settings or post-release [14, 15].

Multiple lower-barrier technologies are used to support decentralization. In 2017, Health Canada approved the first HCV antibody point-of-care test, which uses lateral flow technology to provide a visual result for HCV seropositivity and has demonstrated high sensitivity (98%) and specificity (100%) [16]. Point-of-care HCV antibody testing has had tremendous utility, helping to reach individuals who would not have been screened through conventional methods, with only a single drop of blood from a fingerprick [17]. However, a second test is required to differentiate exposure from infection. Dried blood spot (DBS) testing enables detection of HCV RNA to confirm active infection through collection of a small amount of capillary blood done by fingerprick onto filter paper [14, 18]. While the result is not given in real-time, it does avoid phlebotomy. DBS has been well received particularly among individuals with poor venous access and has been shown to be a low barrier, accessible option for HCV testing [19] with a sensitivity and specificity for HCV RNA detection at 97% and 100% [20]. Although these assays are typically delivered as provider- or peer-administered point-of-care tests rather than self-tests or self-collected specimens, they demonstrate the technical feasibility of simplified, minimally invasive testing platforms that could underpin HCV self-testing strategies in the future.

While HCV antibody self-testing with follow-up DBS self-collection has the potential to reach priority populations and increase testing rates, there is limited understanding and knowledge gap about optimal implementation strategies, needs, and preferences of priority populations including people with a history of incarceration [21]. At present, most studies evaluating point-of-care HCV self-testing were using saliva [22, 23], but these saliva based tests are not currently approved in Canada and many other countries, and there has been limited investigation on self-testing among those who have experience being incarcerated. This study aimed to explore the perspectives, preferences and needs of individuals with experience in the Canadian correctional system in the context of HCV self-testing delivery.

Methods

Study decisions were guided by Interpretive Description (ID) qualitative research design [24]. ID was suitable for this study because it aims to develop knowledge that can directly inform clinical practice. This qualitative study included a collaborative community consultation with Prisoners HIV/AIDS Support Action Network (PASAN), a community-based harm reduction, HIV, and HCV organization that provides support, education and advocacy for individuals who are currently or formerly incarcerated in Ontario, Canada. PASAN also works with organizations to reduce barriers that incarcerated, and formerly incarcerated people encounter when accessing housing, social services, and healthcare. The community consultation with PASAN involved collaborating on participant recruitment and providing ongoing input throughout the research process. Participants were recruited by PASAN team members prior to participants engaging with the study team. This could have occurred through any of PASAN’s in-person in-reach or outreach- based programing. PASAN recruited participants with the following inclusion criteria: (1) Incarcerated in a Canadian prison/jail (provincial or federal); (2) Speak English; (3) Over the age of 16; (4) Currently reside in Ontario. The exclusion criteria included: (1) Incarcerated in prison or jail outside of Canada; (2) Currently on HCV treatment. Participants received a $50 honorarium for their participation, and the interview occurred in a confidential office setting at PASAN. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by York University Institutional Review Board (certificate e2023-222). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study.

Given that the purpose of this research is to inform HCV self-testing rollout programs for people who have been incarcerated, it was essential to generate clinically relevant insights for implementation purposes. Interview guides (Supplemental 1) were developed to explore participants’ perspectives on HCV self-testing with questions relating to the following key ideas: preferences for receiving kits and timing, views on promotional materials and ordering platforms, feelings about reporting results and confirmatory testing, preferred follow-up contact, concerns about self-testing, and overall thoughts on self-testing for their population.

For this study, we operationally defined HCV self-testing as a testing approach in which individuals with incarceration experience would: (1) receive an HCV test kit either while in custody, at the point of release, or post-release in the community; (2) collect their own specimen (e.g. fingerprick capillary blood) using a simplified, pre-packaged kit; (3) perform a rapid antibody test themselves; and (4) interpret the result privately, with the option of subsequent self-collection of a confirmatory DBS HCV RNA test and linkage to care through health or community services [25].

Data was collected at PASAN through semi-structured interviews, which were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. When discussing the tests or platforms as noted in the guides, images or extensive description was used to describe the format. In alignment with ID methodology, data analysis involved finding the knowable, moving beyond the evident, engaging in interpretation, and making meaning of the data that informs practice [24]. The specific steps that guided this process included: (1) multiple readings of the transcripts, (2) creating narrative summaries of the transcripts, (3) noting insights through memos and diagrams, and (4) constantly comparing the concepts and data [26]. Analysis was conducted by researchers (TB, KM) who have qualitative research training and using Microsoft 365 applications. Findings were discussed and corroborated with the larger research team, which included clinicians and methodologists with respective expertise. This process conforms to the ‘thoughtful clinician’ test in ID that seeks to determine if findings resonate with clinicians and to interpret them to produce practical insights that inform clinical practice or policy [24].

Results

Between August and October of 2023, a total of 15 participants were enrolled in the study (11 men, 4 women). Interviews ranged from ~ 5–20 min, but most were between 10 and 20 min. Four key themes were developed from the data (Table 1), each reflecting complexity of HCV self-testing for people who have experienced incarceration in Ontario, Canada. These themes included: (1) Authentic representation because “words carry power,” which emphasizes the importance of being seen and how language shapes identity and influences perception; (2) Altruism triumphs even in the face of stigma and uncertainty which explores the tension between caring for others and navigating societal judgment; (3) Distrust of society, system and self, which focuses on deep skepticism toward institutions and internalized doubt; and, (4) Low-barrier and accessible means “Easy, easy, easy…,” which reflects the desire for simplicity amid persistent challenges and systemic barriers.

Authentic representation because “words carry power”

In response to viewing HCV self-testing promotion material, including a sample flyer (Fig. 1) and navigating a national comprehensive HIV self-testing website (readytoknow.ca), participants provided feedback on the content, design, and wording for advertising HCV self-testing kits within the community. All study participants felt that to engage and better reflect people who have been incarcerated the images on the flyer and website needed to be “hip and cool” and include authentic pictures of real people who had been incarcerated or pictures of prison art done by people with experience in corrections. Participants made comments, such as “that way I’ll know the advertisement is for me,” reflecting that participants need to be represented authentically. Generic or stock images were viewed as inauthentic and disconnected, and participants emphasized the need for poster advertisements to have relatable images that reflected their realities.

Participants noted that HCV self-testing advertisements should have just enough information without overwhelming the reader. Participants provided important insights into promotion materials including positive messaging to combat HCV stigma, a few statistics about HCV, and information about HCV transmission. The importance of the word, “free,” was emphasized by participants who encouraged including the cost of the test on materials as well as adding a quick response (QR) code for quick and easy access to additional information online. Participants thought the website should include information about HCV, such as the accuracy of the self-test kit, a list of resources, and treatment options. Treatment options need to include clinicians who provide healthcare for people with HCV who have been incarcerated. This is reflected when a participant speaks about HCV treatment: “A lot of folks know but they don’t know where to get treated, especially without being stigmatized. I think that’s an important thing.” This illustrates how access to stigma-free, supportive healthcare is essential for people with HCV who have been incarcerated.

Innovative suggestions for promoting HCV self-testing materials were offered from people who experienced incarceration. These suggestions included offering a multipack available through the website that contained fentanyl test strips, naloxone kits, and both HIV and HCV self-testing kits. In addition, participants suggested that people with lived and living experiences of HCV and incarceration are included in designing HCV self-testing websites. Slogans were suggested by participants to advertise HCV self-testing kits though flyers and websites (Table 2).

Participants’ suggestions for HCV self-testing advertising and awareness campaigns

The language used for HCV self-testing flyers and websites deeply mattered for participants, particularly as people who have been incarcerated and who may also have experience with addictions, shame, and systemic- and self-stigma. The notion of words carry power was introduced by one participant while explaining the importance of messages that are truthful, trustful, and effective:When you’re dealing with addicts, ex-cons, all of this stuff, you got to keep in mind that there’s [two] things that are extremely important. Your words. So, words carry power. Whatever’s said, whatever is being transmitted has to be, really has to be effective. The second thing is that talk is cheap. It’s the common thing with us, right?

When you’re dealing with addicts, ex-cons, all of this stuff, you got to keep in mind that there’s [two] things that are extremely important. Your words. So, words carry power. Whatever’s said, whatever is being transmitted has to be, really has to be effective. The second thing is that talk is cheap. It’s the common thing with us, right?

Thus, messaging and advertising must come across as empowering with recognition that words are relational and are not just for information exchange. HCV self-testing advertisements must ensure that the words used do not unintentionally reinforce stigma but instead foster dignity, engagement, and be relevant to the lived experience of people with a history of incarceration. Participants suggested that promotional materials be posted in high visibility public spaces that people with a history of incarceration frequent such as on buses and subways, at community service agencies that work with people that have a history of incarceration, and in newsletters/magazines that people with experience in correction read.

Altruism triumphs even in the face of stigma & uncertainty

Participants were not only motivated to self-test for their own health, but they also felt a sense of responsibility and desire to protect their families, partners, friends, and the community around them from unknowingly transmitting HCV. Many participants made comments, such as, “Don’t be shy to get tested. It’s for your own good. It’s for your own good and the people who are living around you” or “It makes sense to get tested and [ensure you do] not give it to others.” Altruism led to a strong preference for receiving HCV self-testing kits immediately upon release from correctional facilities, so that people who have been incarcerated could have the option to self-testing prior to going back into their communities.

Concern for the health of others despite the potential personal risk or discomfort also emerged in the qualitative data as many participants expressed conflicting emotion of wanting the autonomy to test for HCV independently but also desiring support or guidance from others. Despite this internal conflict and experiences of stigma, fear, judgement and uncertainty surrounding HCV testing, disclosure and treatment; altruistic motivation persisted guiding participants’ decisions to protect both themselves and those around them. One participant described the profound impact of stigma and negative healthcare experiences:A lot of people don’t really want to know the f**king answer to that test. I’ve had hep C for like, back then, I think it was like 15 years and because of the stigma and that, I went to a few places [and saw] the way I got treated. So, I just said, f**k it! You know what I mean? You’re asking people to go through that.

A lot of people don’t really want to know the f**king answer to that test. I’ve had hep C for like, back then, I think it was like 15 years and because of the stigma and that, I went to a few places [and saw] the way I got treated. So, I just said, f**k it! You know what I mean? You’re asking people to go through that.

This reflects the profound impact of stigma and negative healthcare experiences on willingness to keep engaging in a healthcare system after being treated poorly. It illustrates how past healthcare experiences and judgement can cause individuals to avoid accessing healthcare all together despite living with HCV for a long time. “You’re asking people to go through that,” also speaks to anticipated stigma and this further reinforces the need for non-judgmental and trauma informed approaches to HCV self-testing initiatives and “A lot of people who have hep C, they have lost a lot of self-esteem,” emphasising how stigma and feelings of shame can erode confidence and hinder engagement in care.

Distrust of society, system, & self

Distrust was prevalent in society, systems, and self. Beyond the stigma participants experienced from society, they also distrusted systems, such as healthcare organizations and even new technologies. Data reflected a justified distrust in the healthcare system due to negative experiences which motivated participants to want HCV self-testing.So, if you don’t trust the system. Look we don’t trust the system and if you have been living with hep C… You’re not sure or you think you are… you want to test yourself because you can’t trust people.

Even though participants distrusted healthcare organizations, they also struggled to trust themselves. One participant stated:Because there’s many things that could go wrong. I mean, I’m sure I would get directions, and they would be as clear as they could possibly be but the way I might read it might be different from the way it’s explained. I would probably be reading it over and over and over again and probably get nervous to do it the more I read it, right? Because…things of this importance are very delicate. You know, so…I think I’d probably want to go to my physician.

Overwhelmingly, participants expressed concerns about privacy and technology used in systems. For example, they were wary to submit a DBS for HCV RNA testing to confirm an active infection if HCV self-testing showed a positive antibody result. System distrust, likely stemming from their experiences with the carceral system, was a significant barrier:They’ll do a DNA sample on you especially in a prison system and they say, well, it gets deleted after so much time. Did it really get deleted? You know what they’re saying. And they can get a court order if they want to. You can fight it, but you’re going to lose, I think, in the end.

Distrust was not only about the technology, but also about the system and their potential of misusing blood samples in an unauthorized manner. The suspicion around data security and the accuracy of results further complicated this issue: “That’s just too sketchy for me. People crack up, people crack emails. You have to make sure the results are correct and that they are yours! You’ve got to actually see your file.” Participants expressed significant concerns about privacy, trust, and security of results related to HCV self-testing and follow up procedures involving DBS testing. Having autonomy in choosing their method of receiving results was one way to combat system distrust.

Low-barrier and accessible means “easy, easy, easy…”

Recognizing the stigma and barriers faced when accessing healthcare in traditional settings, data suggested the importance of offering accessible and non-stigmatizing testing options. Participants recognized the ease of self-testing and related this to their experiences of COVID-19 self-testing, “Yeah, I mean we do it for COVID it just makes sense…self-testing.” When asked about performing a fingerprick and collecting a drop of blood to complete the self-test, participants expressed confidence and willingness to engage in the process: “No, I think it actually simplifies it for folks because… a lot of folks’ veins are hard.” Participants further agreed that the HCV self-testing process must be low-barrier and accessible when speaking about challenges people have with phlebotomy making self-testing and DBS collection practical and easy. One participant stated:Make it easy so we can execute it. [So that] I don’t have to jump through rings and hoops to get it done, right? Especially if I want to get it done. So, you need to break it down as simple as possible.

Make it easy so we can execute it. [So that] I don’t have to jump through rings and hoops to get it done, right? Especially if I want to get it done. So, you need to break it down as simple as possible.

There was a strong preference to receive kits in low-barrier, accessible and easy places that participants frequented the most and felt comfortable. Established relationships such as community organization, harm reduction sites, supervised consumption sites, health centres, and pharmacies that provided support to people who have been incarcerated were also important. Another participant stated, “Let me say my pharmacist is a very nice guy. So, you know, so it would be easier with the pharmacist…” Probation offices could be another option for distributing HCV self-testing kits. Frequent interactions and an established relationship create an ideal opportunity for kit distribution:If probation officers can hand it out because they’ve got to see them every week anyways or every other week. That might be useful. Someone we see all the time …some person we see or like somewhere we have to go to…

If probation officers can hand it out because they’ve got to see them every week anyways or every other week. That might be useful. Someone we see all the time …some person we see or like somewhere we have to go to…

Participants suggested embedding HCV self-testing kits within existing support systems to increase accessibility and uptake, specifically for individuals who are navigating the justice system and disengaging with traditional healthcare. In addition, making HCV self-testing kits available in settings where access feels easy and routine.

The timing of receiving a self-test kit was highly individualized amongst participants. Most participants agreed that there is a window immediately after release when they may be more motivated to self-test, and providing an HCV self-test upon release was viewed as a low-barrier and accessible option that is easy. For example, one participant stated:If I walk out with this test kit, I might walk out and test myself right now because I’m out here right now… But if we walk out and think about testing it in two months down the road, I’m probably involved with a whole bunch of other stuff. So, you really want to utilize that window, that very little window.

This suggestion reflects a harm reduction approach where low-barrier and accessible means easy access to testing which can help reduce risk and promote autonomy. The entire process from obtaining an HCV self-testing kit to completing the test, reporting the result, collecting and submitting a DBS sample if the test is positive, and receiving the confirmatory result must be simple, efficient, and easy to navigate.

Discussion

Our study findings describe the perspectives, preferences, and needs in the context of HCV antibody self-testing HCV RNA DBS self-collection. We highlight several critical themes that span the self-testing and self-collection cascade of engagement, act of testing, obtaining results and reporting, and linkage to care. Overall participants were open to receiving self-testing kits through various mechanisms, with a key mechanism as receiving upon release. Clear self-testing instructions during self-testing was highlighted as a way to increase confidence. Distrust of systems led to the desire for flexible options for receiving results, including phone, text, in-person, or email.

In terms of advertising and engagement in self-testing, our findings suggest that HCV self-testing messaging, imagery, and rollout strategies must be tailored to people with incarceration experience, a priority population that has often been overlooked in self-testing research and program design. Promotional materials should incorporate prison-related imagery, depicting people with correctional experience, HCV statistics, and QR codes to link to more information. The WHO recommends using digital platforms to support ordering self-testing kits, voluntary reporting of results, and for facilitating linkage to care (WHO, 2024b), which is consistent with our study findings. However, this study revealed the importance of attention to accessible and non-stigmatizing language for people who have experienced incarceration. Furthermore, our findings suggest that the ideal time to self-test is when individuals feel ready, with kit distribution prioritized at the point of release from correctional facilities. Taken together, these findings suggest that HCV self-testing could be integrated into “release planning” and community re-entry supports as a concrete micro-elimination strategy, aligning with WHO calls for differentiated, people-centred models of HCV testing and care for justice-involved populations [27].

Altruistic motivation has also been found among people who inject drugs in other studies when exploring barriers and facilitators to HCV testing with participants reporting they tested out of concern for their significant others and the broader community [28]. In Seattle, individuals living with HCV who were actively injecting drugs expressed a desire to test to protect their relatives and the wider injecting community from HCV transmission [29]. Altruistic values may be leveraged to encourage HCV self-testing and could be embedded in self-testing promotion material and program rollout, appealing to the value of protecting friends, partners, families, and the overall community. Messaging that frames self-testing as an act of care for loved ones, rather than solely an individual responsibility, may be particularly resonant for people with incarceration experience who often occupy multiple caregiving roles despite structural marginalization.

While not explored extensively in this study, peer support is effective in engaging marginalized populations including people who use drugs and those with incarceration histories by reducing stigma and building trust [30]. Peers are often seen as credible sources of information and studies have shown that peer support and involvement increases linkage to care and can accelerate treatment initiation [31]. Within carceral and post-release contexts, peers with lived and living experience of incarceration and HCV may be uniquely positioned to “bridge” distrust in health systems and support interpretation of self-test results, facilitate self-collected DBS for HCV RNA, and navigation to treatment. Embedding self-testing within peer-supported models of care, rather than relying solely on anonymous digital platforms, may therefore be critical for ensuring both acceptability and effective linkage to care among people with incarceration experience. However, peer-supported models and digital care are not mutually exclusive, and our study findings suggest voluntary reporting of self-testing results must be via a secure, confidential, web-based portal. Digital reporting is both feasible and acceptable for people with a history of incarceration if care to address concerns is forefront in practices and policies.

This study highlights the importance of distributing self-testing kits through low-barrier, easily accessible locations such as pharmacies, probation offices, health centres, harm reduction sites, supervised consumption sites, and organizations supporting formerly incarcerated individuals. Trust, familiarity, and repeated contact in specific locations may increase HCV self-testing uptake. HCV self-testing kit distribution was also reported in a study in Vietnam with PWID and gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (gbMSM), describing privacy, convenience, and flexibility as key benefits of receiving a kit through community-based organizations [32].

Our findings demonstrate that almost all participants reported a strong willingness to perform a fingerprick while reporting no concerns with respect to pricking their own finger for both HCV self-testing and DBS self-collection following a positive self-test, highlighting acceptability and a clear preference for less invasive testing methods. Similarly, in a randomized trial, acceptance of the OraQuick fingerprick HCV antibody test was higher than that of traditional venipuncture, which was accepted by only 87% of participants [33]. Our study suggests that for HCV self-testing to be acceptable; the testing process needs to be “easy.” Acceptability of self-testing is further reinforced by the importance of a simple, easy, and user-friendly testing process. This is in line with studies using oral-based HCV self-tests which were found to be acceptable among people who use drugs although concerns exist about practical usability and linkage to care [22, 34].

Participants in our study described that when HCV antibody positive, performing a self-collected DBS was possible. However, autonomy and flexibility in returning DBS tests and receiving DBS test results are critical and could include phone calls, text messages, or be in-person. In Denmark, mail-based DBS HCV self-collection was shown to be feasible among PWID through an online harm reduction program that provides supplies to PWID in the mail, with participant receiving their results by peers via telephone calls [35]. However, self-testing and self-collection with positive results needs to be followed by linkage to appropriate post-test services.

A limitation of this study is that participants did not handle testing materials or navigate proposed online platforms. Future studies should utilize an end-user framework to validate both self-testing/self-collection and to better understand real-world acceptability. In addition, in this form of inquiry is that we cannot capture all possible experiences across a broad range of participants, and that those who chose to participate are a subset of the total population of people who may require HCV self-testing within the context of experience with the correctional system. While participants with experience being incarcerated were the focus of this study, it would be crucial for future studies to engage policy stakeholders who would be instrumental to implement and scale-up HCV self-testing and self-collection. Finally, we did not collect demographic information from participants. Therefore, transferability to other contexts may limited.

Conclusion

This study offers important insights into the perspectives, preferences and needs of HCV self-testing among individuals with lived experience in the Canadian carceral system, a priority population for HCV elimination efforts that is often underserved in traditional healthcare models. Findings indicate that HCV self-testing is well received and highlight key considerations for the design, implementation, and rollout of tailored self-testing programs. Iterations of self-testing rollout should consider incorporating such findings. Ultimately, this study contributes to the growing evidence base supporting HCV self-testing as a strategy to increase autonomy, improve testing uptake, and linkage to care among priority populations.
