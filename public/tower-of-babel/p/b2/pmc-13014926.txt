Mesoporous bioactive glass hybrid nanoparticles accelerate tendon-bone healing via synergistic angiogenesis and immunomodulation
Authors: Yongjun Chen, Haiyan Yao, Chen Li, Yun Zhou, Junlin Ren, Runhui Liu, Junchao Wei, Liang Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A critical clinical challenge for Rotator cuff tear (RCT) treatment is to enhance tendon-bone healing, which is greatly affected by insufficient vascularization, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress. In this work, a novel nanoparticle, MBG@SeHA/Sim, was designed to synergistically promote angiogenesis, modulate the immune response and promote bone regeneration, thereby accelerating tendon-bone healing. The comprehensive in vitro assays demonstrated that MBG@SeHA/Sim showed superior capabilities in promoting vascularization, mitigating inflammation, and stimulating bone regeneration. Besides, mechanistic studies suggested that the anti-inflammatory action of MBG@SeHA/Sim may involve the MAPK, NF-κB, and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways. In vivo, animal experiments demonstrated that MBG@SeHA/Sim can effectively promote tendon-bone healing. Overall, these findings demonstrated that MBG@SeHA/Sim effectively promotes tendon-bone healing through multiple functions and presents a viable approach to address the clinical challenge of suboptimal healing after rotator cuff repair surgery.

Introduction

Rotator cuff tear (RCT), a common shoulder disability, has become one of the most common tendon injury-related problems, meantime, its incidence rate increases greatly with population aging, and more than two-thirds of patients over the age of 70 with shoulder disorders suffer from rotator cuff tears [1,2]. Currently, the main method for RCT treatment is surgical intervention, such as rotator cuff repair (RCR) surgery. However, it is difficult to achieve successful tendon-to-bone healing after RCR surgery, which may be mainly hampered by the unfavorable biological microenvironment at the tendon-bone interface [[3], [4], [5]]. For example, the inherent hypovascularity of the tendon-bone interface critically limits the delivery of oxygen and nutrients, fundamentally impeding tissue regeneration and integration [[6], [7], [8]]. Besides, the early healing phase after RCR surgery is associated with sustained pro-inflammatory response and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, which disrupts the healing cascade and further leads to disorder of the collagen, suppresses the revascularization and contributes to tissue degradation [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Due to these unfavorable biological microenvironment, current clinical outcomes are unsatisfactory, including prolonged rehabilitation, high complication rates, and persistent postoperative pain and inflammation. Therefore, it is much necessary and important to establish an anti-inflammatory microenvironment and enhance vascularization after RCR surgery to promote tendon-bone healing [13,14].

While biological scaffolds for tendon-bone healing have advanced in replicating biomimetic mechanical and physiological properties, they often lack the bioactivity and therapeutic functions required for complete regeneration [[15], [16], [17]]. Current strategies for tendon-bone healing remain limited by impaired vascularization, dysregulated inflammation, and the poor bioactivity of conventional biomaterials. To address these limitations, multifunctional nanoparticles have emerged as a promising strategy, which can integrate controlled drug delivery, bioactive ion release, and enhanced biocompatibility. Mesoporous bioactive glass (MBG) is biodegradable and can release key ions such as silicon, calcium and phosphate, which are beneficial for bone regeneration and angiogenesis, thus showed excellent biological activity and has been widely used in the regeneration of soft and hard tissues. Furthermore, MBG possesses porous structure and large specific surface area, which makes it an effective drug carrier capable of achieving controlled release of ions and drugs [[18], [19], [20]]. Leveraging these characteristics, MBG is being increasingly explored for its application in tendon-bone integration [[21], [22], [23]]. Selenium (Se), an essential trace element, has emerged as a promising therapeutic agent due to its intrinsic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which are crucial for mitigating the early destructive phase of healing [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Studies have demonstrated that Se@SiO2 nanocomposites can enhance tendon-bone integration in animal models by scavenging ROS and reducing inflammatory cytokines [28]. Similarly, statins like Simvastatin (Sim) have shown potential beyond lipid-lowering, exhibiting pro-osteogenic and pro-angiogenic effects vital for interface regeneration [29,30]. For instance, localized delivery of Sim via gelatin hydrogel accelerated tendon-bone healing in a rabbit model by stimulating early-stage angiogenesis and osteogenesis [31]. While MBG is well-established for its excellent bioactivity, and drug-loading capacity, and both Se and Sim have been individually explored, a strategy that intelligently integrates all three components into a single, responsive system remains unexplored for tendon-bone healing. Therefore, an MBG-based nanoparticle functionalized with SeCy-crosslinked HA layer (SeHA) was engineered. Specifically, MBG was firstly treated with 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane (APTES), then grafted with oxidized hyaluronic acid (OHA), followed by loading with Sim, and then selenocystamine (SeCy) was used to crosslink the HA molecules to form MBG@SeHA/Sim (Scheme 1A), which works as a multifunctional nanoparticle to address the pathological microenvironment at the tendon-bone interface following rotator cuff repair. When functionalized with SeCy-crosslinked HA layer, the MBG serves as a bioactive carrier that gains ROS-responsive and antioxidant/immunomodulatory properties. This enables it to actively scavenge the excess ROS prevalent after surgery and mitigate the initial inflammatory phase. Critically, this ROS-triggered cleavage of the SeCy-crosslinked HA layer enables the intelligent release of the encapsulated Sim, which then acts as the primary driver for angiogenesis and osteogenesis (Scheme 1B). Material and methods

Preparation of MBG@SeHA

First, MBG was synthesized according to the previous method [32]. Briefly, 0.7 g of CTAB was dissolved in 33 mL of water, then 10 mL of ethyl acetate was added, followed by the addition of 7 mL of aqueous ammonia (3 mol/L) after 30 min. Then 15 min later, 3.6 mL of tetraethoxysilane, 0.36 mL of triethyl phosphate and 2.277 g of Ca(NO3)2.4H2O were sequentially added to the mixture at 30 min intervals. The solution was stirred vigorously for an additional 4 h, and the precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed three times with ethanol and water, and then dried at 60 °C for 24 h. The dried product was calcinated at 650 °C for 6 h to obtain MBG. Then, 0.3 g of MBG and 0.6 mL of APTES was added in 100 mL of ethanol, then the mixed solution was refluxed at 80 °C for 24 h. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed three times, and freeze-dried to obtain amino functioned MBG (MBG@NH2).

Next, 0.6 g of HA was dissolved in 40 mL of water, then 0.3192 g of NaIO4 was added and stirred for 24 h in the dark. Next, 0.45 mL of ethylene glycol was added to terminate the reaction, the solution was dialyzed to obtain the oxided HA(OHA). Then 0.3 g of MBG@NH2 was added in the OHA and stirred for 24 h. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed, and freeze-dried to obtain MBG@HA. Finally, 0.3 g of MBG@HA was dispersed in 10 mL of DMSO, then 0.195 g of SeCy dissolved in 70 mL of DMSO was added and stirred for 24 h. The SeCy functionalized MBG (MBG@SeHA) was collected by centrifugation, washed, and lyophilized.

Characterization

The morphology of nanoparticles was observed using scanning electron microscopy (TEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Talos F200X G2, USA). The surface composition of various samples was determined using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, ESCALAB 250, USA). The weight loss of different samples was measured with thermal gravimetric analyzer (TGA, Perkin Elmer, TGA 4000, USA). The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of different samples was measured via size and zeta potential analyzer (Brookhaven, 90 Plus PALS, USA).

Drug loading and release

30 mg of MBG@HA was dispersed in 20 mL of DMSO, and then 60 mg Sim was added and stirred for 30 min. Next, 19.5 mg of SeCy dissolved in 20 mL of DMSO was added to above solution and stirred for 24 h. Nanoparticles loaded with Sim (MBG@SeHA/Sim) was collected by centrifugation, washed, and lyophilized.

To measure the loading efficiency and drug content, Firstly, 6 mg of MBG@SeHA/Sim were dispersed in 2.5 mL ethanol and placed in a dialysis bag, then the dialysis bag was placed in 30 mL of ethanol containing H2O2 (10−3 mol/L). Subsequently, the absorbance of the supernatant at 238 nm was measured. The mass of Sim in the supernatant was calculated using the measured Sim standard curve, then loading efficiency (%) and drug content (%) were calculated using the following formula:Loadingefficiency(%)=massofdruginMBGinitialmassofdrug×100Drugcontent(%)=massofdruginMBGmassofMBG×100

The release of Sim from MBG or MBG@SeHA/Sim was determined by the dialysis method. Briefly, 3 mg of MBG/Sim or MBG@SeHA/Sim was dispersed in 2 mL of PBS containing 20% ethanol, and then loaded into a dialysis bag; the dialysis bag was placed in 30 mL of PBS containing 20% ethanol and incubated at 37 °C. At predetermined time intervals, 1.0 mL of the buffer solution was removed, and the absorbance was measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer at an excitation wavelength of 238 nm, then an equal volume of fresh medium was added to the system. To evaluate the ROS-responsive release of Sim, 1 mmol/L H2O2 was added to the buffer medium after the 5th hour. Subsequently, 1.0 mL of the buffer solution was used to measure the absorbance and calculate the release amount of Sim.

ROS scavenging of MBG@SeHA

The ROS scavenging performance of MBG@SeHA was assessed via the ABTS•+, DPPH•, and •OH scavenging experiments. As for the scavenging of ABTS•+, 10 μL of MBG, MBG@SeHA, and MBG@SeHA/Sim (with final concentrations of 125, 250, 500, and 1000 μg/mL) and 200 μL of ABTS•+ solution was added in to the 96-well plate. The plate was kept in dark for 6 min at 25 °C, then measured at 734 nm using a microplate reader. For the DPPH• scavenging assay, MBG, MBG@SeHA, and MBG@SeHA/Sim were dispersed in methanol. Subsequently, 80 μL of the nanoparticle solutions (with final concentrations of 125, 250, 500, and 1000 μg/mL) and 120 μL of DPPH• were added to a 96-well plate. Then the plate was kept in the dark for 30 min at 25 °C, and measured at 528 nm using a microplate reader. Furthermore, the •OH scavenging activities of nanoparticles were assessed using ESR analysis. •OH was trapped by the Fenton reaction (70 μL of 0.735 mM FeSO4 and 25 μL of 30 % H2O2), and DMPO was selected as the spin trapping agent. The ESR spectrum was recorded after •OH, DMPO, and nanoparticles sample (500 μg/mL) mixed for 100 s. The change of the relative peak intensity in the ESR spectra of the DMPO-•OH adducts indicated the •OH scavenging effect of nanoparticles.

The intracellular ROS scavenging properties of the nanoparticles were determined by DCFH-DA assays. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells was seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well, and then co-cultured with LPS (1 μg/mL), LPS (1 μg/mL) + MBG (200 μg/mL), LPS (1 μg/mL) + MBG@SeHA (200 μg/mL), LPS (1 μg/mL) + MBG@SeHA/Sim (200 μg/mL). After 12 h, cells were washed with PBS and cultured with DCFH-DA (10 μmol/L) at 37 °C for 30 min and then the intracellular ROS images were observed by fluorescence microscope (Leica, DMi8, Germany). For the flow cytometry analysis, RAW264.7 cells were treated according to the above method and a cell suspension (1 × 106 cell) was finally collected and analyzed with a flow cytometer (CytoFlex).

Cell culture and biocompatibility

In this study, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), murine macrophage cells (RAW 264.7 cells), and murine pre-osteoblastic cells (MC3T3-E1 cells) were utilized: HUVECs were obtained from the Cell Resource Center of Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. RAW 264.7 cells were purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. (China), and MC3T3-E1 cells were acquired from Haixing Biosciences (China). All cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2). HUVECs were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM). RAW264.7 cells were grown in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. MC3T3-E1 cells were maintained in α-Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

HUVECs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well and cultured with different concentrations (100, 200, 400, and 800 μg/mL) of MBG, MBG@SeHA, and MBG@SeHA/Sim nanoparticles. After 1, 3, and 5 days of incubation, cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Beyotime, C0037, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, 10 vol% CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h and then the optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured by a microplate reader (Bio-Rad 680, USA).

Based on the preliminary results, a concentration of 200 μg/mL was selected for subsequent experiments with MBG, MBG@SeHA, and MBG@SeHA/Sim nanoparticles. Cellular viability was further evaluated using the Calcein AM/PI cell viability/cytotoxicity assay kit (UElandy, L6037M, China). After treatment with 200 μg/mL nanoparticles for 24h, live and dead cells were identified by an inverted fluorescence microscopy (Leica, DMi8, Germany) based on Calcein-AM/PI staining (green fluorescence for viable cells, red fluorescence for dead cells).

Angiogenesis and migration properties assessment

Tubule-Forming Assay: Each well of a 24-well plate was pre-coated with Matrigel matrix (Corning, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow full polymerization. HUVECs pretreated with different nanoparticles were taken and inoculated on the surface of the Matrigel at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well, which was subsequently added with ECM medium containing different nanoparticles to continue the incubation for 6 h. At the end of the incubation, Calcein-AM (Uelandy, China) was used to stain the tubular structures for 15 min away from light, and the formation of tubular structures was observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica, DMi8, Germany) at an excitation wavelength of 490 nm. Three fields of view were randomly selected from each group for image acquisition, and the following parameters were quantitatively analyzed by ImageJ.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis (qRT-PCR): Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells using Trizol reagent according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined spectrophotometrically using a Nanodrop 2000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit. The expression levels of angiogenic factors (VEGF, Ang-1) were performed on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA) with SYBR Green master mix. All primer sequences were validated for amplification efficiency. Gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin as an internal control and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Each experiment was performed in triplicate with appropriate negative controls.

Transwell Assay: 500 μL of medium containing different nanoparticles was added to the lower chamber, while 200 μL of HUVECs suspension (1 × 105 cells/mL) was seeded into the upper chamber. After 24 h of incubation, the migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. The number of migrated cells was quantified by counting three random microscopic fields per well using ImageJ.

Scratch Wound Healing Assay: HUVECs were inoculated into 6-well plates, and when the cell fusion reached about 90%, a 200 μL pipette tip was used to scrape vertically along the longitudinal axis of the plate to form a homogeneous scratch. After washing with PBS three times to remove the detached cells, the width of the scratch was recorded under an inverted microscope at the baseline for 0 h. The width of the scratch was then changed to low-serum medium (2% FBS) containing different nanoparticles. Subsequently, the culture was changed to low-serum medium (2% FBS) containing different nanoparticles, and the healing of the scratches was observed and recorded again after 24 h in a humidified incubator.

Macrophage polarization experiments

RAW 264.7 were stimulated with LPS (1 μg/mL in DMEM, diluted from 1 mg/mL stock solution) for 24 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing different nanoparticles (MBG, MBG@SeHA, and MBG@SeHA/Sim) for an additional 24-h incubation period. Macrophage polarization was evaluated by flow cytometry, immunofluorescence staining, ELISA and qRT-PCR.

qRT-PCR: the expression levels of M1 polarization markers (TNF-α, IL-6) and M2 polarization markers (ARG-1, IL-10) was quantified.

Immunofluorescence Analysis: Cell fixation was performed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma, USA) in PBS for 10 min. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with immunofluorescence blocking buffer for 15 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing, cells were incubated with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h in the dark, then counterstained with 200 μL DAPI solution. Fluorescent images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss, LSM 800, Germany). RAW264.7 cells were stained with anti-TNF-α (M1 marker) and anti-Arg-1 (M2 marker) antibodies (1:200, both from Proteintech, China).

Flow cytometry: The collected cells were resuspended in PBS and stained in the dark for 30 min with PE-conjugated anti-CD86 (Elabscience, E-AB-F0994D, China) and APC-conjugated anti-CD163 (Elabscience, E-AB-F1295E, China). Unbound antibodies were removed by washing. Finally, the cells were resuspended in PBS and analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSMELODY™, USA). The polarization of RAW 264.7 cells was assessed using FlowJo software.

ELISA: After macrophage polarization assay, the concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and anti-inflammatory markers (IL-10) in cell culture supernatants were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Jianglai Biotechnology, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Osteogenic differentiation assay

MC3T3-E1 cells were inoculated in 6-well plates, and when the cell fusion reached 70%-80%, the medium was carefully removed and replaced with 2 mL of osteogenic medium containing different materials for osteogenic differentiation.

qRT-PCR: After 7 days of culture, the expression levels of the osteogenic genes Col I, OCN, OPN and RUNX2 were detected by qRT-PCR.

ALP staining and quantitative analysis of ALP activity: After 14 days of culture, the BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase staining kit (Beyotime, C3206, China) was used to stain, observe and take images, while the quantitative alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was measured using an alkaline phosphatase assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A059-1-1, China).

ARS staining and quantitative analysis: After 21 days of culture, the Congo red staining solution (Solarbio, G1452-100, China) was used to stain, observe and take images; then a 10% solution of hexadecyltrimethylammonium chloride (Solarbio, H8980, China) was used to dissolve the calcium nodules, and the absorbance of the solution at 562 nm was measured by microplate reader (Bio-Rad 680, USA).

Animal experiment

Animal models: All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanchang University (Approval No. NCULAE-20241111002). 48 male Sprague-Dawley rats (180–200 g) were randomly divided into four groups (n = 12 per group): control group (PBS treatment), MBG group, MBG@SeHA group, MBG@SeHA/Sim group. After anesthetizing rats with sodium pentobarbital, a skin incision was made over the anterolateral shoulder, and the trapezius muscle was dissected to expose the supraspinatus tendon. The tendon was then detached from the greater tuberosity of the humerus. A bone tunnel (0.5 mm diameter) was drilled into the humeral head, and the detached tendon was reattached to the greater tuberosity using 3-0 absorbable sutures through the tunnel. Subsequently, 50 μL of PBS, MBG, MBG@SeHA, and MBG@SeHA/Sim (nanoparticles dispersed in PBS, 10 mg/mL) was injected into the tendon-bone interface. Postoperative analgesia and penicillin injections were administered to alleviate pain and prevent infection. At 4 and 8 weeks after surgery, six rats from each group were sacrificed for tendon-bone healing assessment.

Micro-CT analysis: Samples were scanned using Micro-CT (SKYSCAN 1272, Bruker, Germany), and then CTAN software (Skyscan1172, Bruker; Gress) was used to reconstruct the data from the scans and further assess bone regeneration. The area above the greater tuberosity of the humerus connecting the supraspinatus was designated as the region of interest. Scanning conditions were 100 mA, 50 kV, and a voxel size of 50 μm.

Biomechanical test: Following the harvest of the supraspinatus-humerus complex, the cross-sectional area at the tendon-bone interface was measured. Biomechanical testing was performed using an electronic tensile testing machine (JHY-5000). The supraspinatus tendon was secured with the upper clamp, while the proximal humerus was fixed with the lower clamp. A preload of 5 N was applied, followed by uniaxial loading at a rate of 10 mm/min until failure. The ultimate failure load and ultimate tensile stress of the supraspinatus-humerus complex were recorded and calculated.

Histopathological observation: The supraspinatus-humeral complex specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and decalcified over a 2-month period. Following fixation, tissues underwent standard processing including graded dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning to obtain 5 μm-thick serial sections. The sections were characterized by HE, picrosirius red, Safranin O-fast green, immunohistochemical staining for collagen I, and immunofluorescence staining for VEGF, α-SMA, CD86 and CD163.

Tissue maturity evaluation: A modified maturity scoring system (Table S2) was used to systematically evaluate the supraspinatus-humeral complex, and the scoring indexes included key parameters such as tissue structure, cellular morphology and matrix composition.

Statistical analysis

Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were statistically analyzed using one-way ANOVA or Student's t-test. p-values <0.05 were determined to be statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of MBG@SeHA

The TEM images showed that MBG displayed a uniform spherical and a well-defined mesoporous structure, and the average diameter was about 75 nm (Fig. 1A). After HA modification, a thin coating was observed on the surface, and the element mapping images showed that nitrogen (N), silicon (Si), phosphorus (P), and calcium (Ca) uniformly distributed on the spherical structure of MBG@HA (Fig. S1), which indicated the successful modification of MBG with HA. After the HA on the surface of MBG was cross-linked by SeCy, the mesoporous channels of MBG become obscure. The element mapping images showed that silicon (Si), phosphorus (P), calcium (Ca), and Se are distributed in the spherical structure of MBG@SeHA (Fig. 1B), demonstrating the successful preparation of MBG@SeHA. The XPS survey spectrum of MBG@SeHA (Fig. 1C) shows the presence of C, O, N, Si, Ca, P, and Se elements. The change of N1s spectrum further demonstrating the successful prepared of MBG@SeHA (Fig. 1D). In the N1s spectrum of MBG@NH2, the peak at 399.8 eV was detected, which was attributed to nitrogen in -NH2 of APTES (Fig. 1E). As for MBG@HA, a new peak could be observed in 401.6 eV, which could be attributed to the nitrogen in amide group of HA (Fig. 1F). After SeCy was grafted to HA, another new peak appeared at 400.6 eV, which might be attributed to the nitrogen of the imine group (Fig. 1G). Furthermore, typical Se3d peaks were detected at 55.7 eV, which confirms the successful decoration of MBG with SeCy (Fig. 1H). The weight loss of MBG was 4.5%, which can be attributed to the evaporation of absorbed solvent. After amino functionalization, the weight loss of MBG@NH2 was 6.5%. As for MBG@HA, the weight loss was 12.1 %, indicating the successful modification of HA on the surface of MBG. After SeCy modification, the weight loss increased to 14.1 %, which may be attribute to the thermal decomposition of the organic molecules on the surface of MBG, further demonstrating MBG@SeHA was successful prepared step by step (Fig. 1I). The hydrodynamic diameter of MBG, MBG@NH2, MBG@HA, and MBG@SeHA were 73.2 ± 6.6 nm, 77.5 ± 11.8 nm, 131.9 ± 10.6 nm, and 124.2 ± 31.7 nm, respectively (Fig. 1J). The MBG@SeHA exhibits a broader hydrodynamic diameter distribution than that of MBG@HA, which might be due to the fact that Secy can cross-link HA on individual nanoparticles as well as the HA on the surfaces of different nanoparticles. The zeta potential of MBG and MBG@NH2 were −9.1 ± 0.5 mV and −3.9 ± 0.6 mV, respectively. When HA was grafted on the surface of MBG, the zeta potential of MBG@HA was −15.2 ± 0.5 mV, indicating the successful prepared of MBG@HA (Fig. 1K). After the SeCy modification, the potential of MBG@SeHA changed to −10.9 ± 0.4 mV. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption results revealed that all nanoparticles exhibited type IV isotherms with hysteresis loops, which can be attributed to the mesoporous framework of the materials (Fig. S2). The specific surface area and pore volume of the samples were calculated using the BET/BJH method. The results showed that after modification with HA and SeCy, both the specific surface area and pore volume decreased (Table S1). Drug loading, release and ROS scavenging

Sim is loaded in the MBG, and then the Se cross-linked HA was employed to encapsulate Sim to achieve ROS responsive release of Sim. The loading efficiencies of MBG and MBG@SeHA were about 12.5 % and 13.3 %, respectively (Fig. 2A), demonstrating that MBG@SeHA can improve the drug loading efficiency of Sim. The drug content of MBG and MBG@SeHA were 7.5 % and 8.0 %, respectively (Fig. 2B), indicating that SeCy-crosslinked HA layer improves the drug loading properties of MBG. In terms of release behavior, the cumulative release of Sim from MBG/Sim in PBS over 24 h was 20.7 %, whereas that from MBG@SeHA/Sim was 14.0 %, confirming that the SeCy-crosslinked HA layer can prevent drug leakage under physiological conditions. In addition, mathematical models of Sim release from MBG and MBG@SeHA were fitted, the results showed that the concentration of release of Sim before 10 h and the square root of time representing a linear function (Fig. 2), which demonstrated that the release model of Sim before 10 h is the Higuchi models. Furthermore, the release profile of Sim from MBG in an H2O2 microenvironment was nearly identical to that in PBS, suggesting that ROS has no effect on Sim release from MBG (Fig. 2C). However, when H2O2 was introduced at the 5th hour, MBG@SeHA/Sim exhibited a rapid release of 10% Sim within the subsequent hour, with a cumulative release of 22.9 % over 24 h, which significantly higher than its release in PBS (14.0 %), indicating the ROS-responsive release of Sim in MBG@SeHA/Sim.Fig. 2Drug loading, release, and ROS scavenging properties of MBG@SeHA. Excessive ROS may lead to tissue damage and inflammation, which is detrimental to the healing process of rotator cuff. Therefore, it is much important to eliminate excessive ROS, which can restore the regenerative microenvironment and be beneficial for tissue regeneration. MBG exhibited no ROS scavenging activity, and even at a concentration of 1000 μg/mL, the scavenging rates of ABTS•+ and DPPH• remained below 10% (Fig. 2D and E). When the concentrations of MBG@SeHA were 125, 250, 500, and 1000 μg/mL, the ABTS•+ scavenging rates were 25.8 ± 0.96 %, 36.9 ± 0.26 %, 56.2 ± 0.09 %, and 80.9 ± 1.84 %, respectively, indicating that MBG@SeHA possess excellent ROS scavenging activity. The DPPH• scavenging rates of MBG@SeHA in 125, 250, 500, and 1000 μg/mL were 27.2 ± 0.76 %, 46.9 ± 0.39 %, 58.5 ± 1.02 %, and 62.3 ± 0.16 %, respectively (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, after loading Sim, the reactive oxygen species scavenging activity of MBG@SeHA did not change significantly. Besides, the •OH peak detected by electron spin resonance (ESR) were significantly weakened after adding MBG@SeHA and MBG@SeHA/Sim, which further verify the antioxidation property of MBG@SeHA.

The intracellular antioxidant capacity of MBG@SeHA was detected by DCFH probes. The fluorescence image shows that after LPS treatment, there are a large number of green cells, indicating that the RAW264.7 cells were activated and produced a large amount of ROS within the cells. After MBG treatment, there was still large amount of green fluorescence, indicating that MBG was unable to scavenge ROS. After treatment with MBG@SeHA, there was almost no green fluorescence in RAW264.7 cells, indicating that MBG@SeHA effectively scavenged intracellular ROS (Fig. 2G). Meanwhile, the results of flow cytometry indicated that the proportion of ROS-positive cells after LPS treatment was approximately 34.8%. After MBG treatment, the proportion of ROS-positive cells was 27.5%, which did not changed significantly compared with LPS group. After treated with MBG@SeHA and MBG@SeHA/Sim, the proportion of ROS-positive cells reduced to 6.17% and 6.41%, respectively, indicating the excellent ROS scavenging activity of MBG@SeHA (Fig. 2H). The ROS scavenging activity of MBG@SeHA originates from the selenocystamine on the surface of MBG, which can react with ROS and achieve antioxidant effects in cells, thus alleviating oxidative stress and promoting tissue regeneration.

Besides, live/dead assay results demonstrated consistently low levels of dead cells across all experimental groups (Fig. S4). 200 μg/mL, which was demonstrated to be safe for both RAW 264.7 cells and MC3T3-E1 cells (Fig. S5), was systematically applied throughout all subsequent experimental procedures to ensure biological relevance and methodological consistency.

Immunoregulatory effects of MBG@SeHA/Sim in vitro

As with rotator cuff tears, inflammation usually accompanies RCR surgery, which prompts investigation into strategies for modulating this response to enhance tendon-to-bone healing. Here, to evaluate the regulatory effect of MBG@SeHA/Sim on macrophage polarization, LPS was applied to mimic inflammatory stimulation and then RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in media containing MBG, MBG@SeHA, MBG@SeHA/Sim, or DMEM alone. The qPCR analysis results revealed that MBG@SeHA/Sim treatment significantly downregulated the M1 macrophage marker (TNF-α and IL-6) while upregulating the M2 macrophage marker (Arg-1 and IL-10) (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, flow cytometry analysis further confirmed the macrophage polarization. Compared to the LPS group, the proportion of M1 macrophages (CD86+) significantly decreased in the MBG@SeHA/Sim group, while the proportion of M2 macrophages (CD163+) increased (Fig. 4E). Similarly, ELISA assays showed that the MBG@SeHA and MBG@SeHA/Sim groups exhibited significantly reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) compared to the LPS control group. Conversely, the levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10) were significantly increased in these groups. (Fig. 4F). The results indicated that MBG@SeHA promoted a shift in macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, suggesting its potential therapeutic value in mitigating inflammation-associated conditions. This effect is may due to the ROS scavenging and anti-inflammatory properties of selenium. Moreover, the addition of Sim provided an additional potent anti-inflammatory stimulus, explaining why MBG@SeHA/Sim induced a greater degree of M2 polarization than MBG@SeHA. Interestingly, an anti-inflammatory effect was also observed with MBG alone, which may be attributed to the release of bioactive ions such as silicon (Si) from MBG, known to contribute to immune modulation by promoting macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype [33,34]. Unlike amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) that primarily enhances osteogenesis and angiogenesis [35], MBG provides additional key benefits for tendon-bone healing. It functions as a stable, controled-release drug carrier while simultaneously delivering various bioactive ions, creating a more favorable microenvironment for repair.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Fig. S8) indicated that the biological processes affected by MBG@SeHA/Sim were associated with regulation and responses to external stimuli. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to explore potential inflammatory regulatory pathways. Notably, the MAPK signaling pathway and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway were significantly enriched (Fig. 5D).

These findings suggest that MBG@SeHA/Sim may exert its anti-inflammatory effects through modulation of these specific pathways. This inference is consistent with existing literature reporting that Se alleviates inflammation via MAPK and NF-kappa B pathways [[36], [37], [38]], and Sim enhances anti-inflammatory responses through the PI3K/Akt pathway [39,40]. Collectively, these findings indicated the involvement of MAPK, NF-kappa B, and PI3K/Akt signaling in the immunoregulatory mechanism of MBG@SeHA/Sim. However, the precise molecular mechanisms and the functional hierarchy of these signaling pathways require further validation through gain- or loss-of-function experiments using specific agonists or inhibitors, which represents a critical direction for our future research and also is the current limitations of our studies.

MBG@SeHA/Sim enhances tendon-bone healing

First, hepatic and renal function assessments, alongside histopathological examination (H&E staining) of major visceral organs, revealed no statistically significant differences among the experimental groups (Figs. S10 and S11). These findings collectively indicated the absence of significant in vivo toxicity induced by the administered nanoparticles in rats, demonstrated the biocompatibility of MBG@SeHA/Sim.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, Picrosirius red (PR) staining and immunohistochemical staining for Collagen I further exhibited tendon-bone interface repair and remodeling histologically. H&E staining revealed that at 4 weeks after surgery, the tendon-bone interface structure remained incomplete across all groups. By 8 weeks, however, the MBG@SeHA/Sim group exhibited significantly more organized tendon fiber attachment to bone than that of other groups (Fig. 7A), indicating enhanced tissue regeneration and guided positive remodeling of the interface. According to the Modified Tendon Maturity Score (Table S2) [41,42], the histological improvement was corroborated by significantly higher maturity scores in the MBG@SeHA/Sim group at both time points (Fig. 7D), confirming its superior ability to accelerate tissue maturation and remodeling. Biomechanical testing exhibited that the failure load and stress values increased within 8 weeks after surgery in all experimental groups. Compared with the control group, the failure load and stress values of the MBG, MBG@SeHA and MBG@SeHA/Sim groups were significantly higher (Fig. 8C–E). Notably, measurements of the supraspinatus-humerus complex revealed that the cross-sectional area of the MBG@SeHA/Sim group was significantly larger than that of the other groups (Fig. S13), further validating the potential of this material system in promoting tendon-bone interface tissue regeneration and functional reconstruction.

Furthermore, the immunoregulatory and pro-angiogenic effects were evaluated by immunofluorescence staining at the tendon-to-bone interface. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages (CD86+) and a concomitant shift towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (CD163+) in the MBG@SeHA and MBG@SeHA/Sim groups compared to the control and MBG groups at 4 weeks after surgery, indicating potent anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 9A and S14). The pro-angiogenic properties were evaluated by immunostaining for α-SMA (mature vessels) and VEGF (key angiogenic factor). Conclusion

In this study, novel nanoparticles loaded with Sim (MBG@SeHA/Sim) was prepared to enhance tendon-to-bone healing after RCR surgery. MBG@SeHA/Sim responds to the excessive ROS present in the post-surgical microenvironment to smart release of Sim, effectively promoting angiogenesis at the tendon-bone interface. Furthermore, MBG@SeHA/Sim modulates the inflammatory response by inducing a shift in macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype towards the pro-regenerative M2 phenotype. Besides, the anti-inflammatory effects of MBG@SeHA/Sim may mediate through the MAPK, NF-κB, and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways. Collectively, these mechanisms contribute to accelerated and superior tendon-to-bone healing, demonstrating the significant therapeutic potential of MBG@SeHA/Sim.
