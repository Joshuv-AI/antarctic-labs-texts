Serum procalcitonin as a predictor of intravenous immunoglobulin resistance and coronary artery lesions in Kawasaki disease
Authors: Jae Sung Son
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Procalcitonin (PCT) has emerged as a potential biomarker for predicting resistance to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) treatment and coronary artery abnormalities in Kawasaki disease (KD). This study aimed to evaluate the clinical utility of serum PCT in the management of KD.Materials and Methods:A retrospective analysis was performed on 170 patients with KD treated at a single center. Serum PCT levels were measured along with clinical and laboratory data. Patients were categorized as responders or nonresponders to IVIG treatment based on the resolution of fever following treatment. Logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis were used to assess the predictive value of serum PCT.Results:Serum PCT levels were significantly higher in nonresponders compared to responders (1.40 ng/mL versus 0.32 ng/mL, P < 0.001). Logistic regression analysis identified serum PCT as an independent predictor of IVIG resistance (odds ratio – 1.385; 95% confidence interval, 1.071–1.791; P = 0.013). A cutoff value of 1.80 ng/mL yielded a sensitivity of 71.4% and specificity of 86.6%, with an area under the curve of 0.838. Although elevated serum PCT levels were observed in patients with coronary artery abnormalities, it was not an independent predictor in logistic regression analysis.Conclusions:Serum PCT is a useful biomarker for predicting resistance to IVIG treatment in KD. However, its utility for predicting coronary artery lesions is limited. Further multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and standardize methodologies for clinical application.

INTRODUCTION

Kawasaki disease (KD), also known as mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome, is an acute febrile vasculitis that primarily affects children under 5 years of age.[1] It is the leading cause of acquired heart disease in this population, with the potential to result in severe coronary artery lesions (CALs) despite timely treatment.[12] The long-term prognosis of KD heavily depends on the severity of CALs, including coronary dilation, aneurysms, and intimal thickening, which can significantly impact cardiovascular health into adulthood. The global incidence of KD demonstrates significant geographic and ethnic variability. In western countries, the incidence ranges from 4 to 25 per 100,000 children under 5 years of age.[34] However, in Northeast Asia, the overall KD incidence is considerably higher, with Japan reporting rates of 319.6 per 100,000 during the 2015–2016 survey and South Korea at 202.2 per 100,000 during the 2015–2017 survey.[56] These data underscore the importance of regional studies to understand disease patterns and outcomes. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is the primary treatment for KD and is highly effective in reducing the risk of CALs.[78] Nevertheless, approximately 10%–20% of patients exhibit IVIG resistance, which significantly increases their risk of developing severe coronary complications.[191011] Recent studies have investigated novel parameters, encompassing inflammatory, immunological, cellular, proteomic, and genetic biomarkers, to enhance the prediction of IVIG resistance and the risk of CALs development in KD.[112] Given the lack of robust evidence linking clinical risk factors or biomarkers to CAL formation in KD children, identifying reliable biomarkers is crucial to improving prognosis prediction and ultimately enhancing the quality of life for these patients.[4] Among the biomarkers studied, procalcitonin (PCT), a pro-inflammatory marker, has shown potential as a predictor of IVIG resistance and CALs. However, previous studies have yielded conflicting results regarding its utility, with some identifying elevated PCT levels as a significant risk factor for IVIG resistance, while others found limited predictive value for CALs.[111314151617] However, the role of PCT as a reliable biomarker in KD remains unclear, necessitating a deeper investigation into its clinical utility. This study aims to evaluate the clinical utility of PCT in predicting IVIG resistance and CALs for KD patients. By analyzing data from retrospective cohorts and comparing findings across geographic regions, we seek to clarify the predictive role of PCT and its potential impact on clinical decision-making.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and population

This retrospective study reviewed the clinical records of KD patients treated at Konkuk University Medical Center in Korea between January 2009 and December 2018. The diagnosis of complete and incomplete KD relies on standards recommended by the American Heart Association scientific statement for diagnosis, treatment, and long-term management of KD.[1] All patients received standard treatment for KD, including a single infusion of high-dose IVIG at 2 g/kg over 10–12 h and oral aspirin at 30–50 mg/kg/day. The aspirin dose was reduced to 3–5 mg/kg/day after normalization of C-reactive protein (CRP) and continued for 6–8 weeks. Patients were excluded if they (1) received initial treatment at other hospitals, (2) did not receive IVIG treatment within 10 days of fever onset, or (3) lacked follow-up results. A total of 170 KD patients were enrolled and classified into two groups based on their response to initial IVIG treatment. IVIG responders were defined as patients who remained fever free for at least 36 h after completing their IVIG infusion. IVIG nonresponders were defined as those who required additional treatment due to recrudescent or persistent fever lasting at least 36 h after the initial IVIG infusion.

Data collection

Data were retrospectively collected from medical records, including demographic information, clinical presentations, laboratory findings, and echocardiographic outcomes. Laboratory findings analyzed included white blood cell count (WBC), % neutrophils, hemoglobin, platelet count, CRP, total bilirubin, albumin, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, calcium, sodium, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), pyuria, and PCT. PCT levels were measured using the Elecsys BRAHMS PCT electrochemiluminescence assay (BRAHMS, Hennigsdorf, Germany) on a Roche COBAS SYSTEM (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Echocardiograms were performed on all patients at the time of diagnosis, as well as at 2–4 weeks and 6–8 weeks after the onset of illness. Body surface area and z-scores were calculated using the Haycock and the Kobayashi equations, respectively. The diameter of the coronary artery was converted to a z-score using the regression method for model fitting of body surface area from the study by Kobayashi et al.[1819] A z-score above 2.5 during illness was defined as a CAL.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U-test, while categorical variables were analyzed with the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Data were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges or as numbers with percentages. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the association between PCT levels and IVIG resistance. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal cutoff values for each parameter, and the area under the curve (AUC) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Konkuk University Hospital (No. KUMC 2021-02-019). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 170 patients with KD were included in the study. The median age of the study population was 2.1 years (mean: 2.4 years; range: 0.1–7.5 years). Among the patients, 96 (56.5%) were male, and 74 (43.5%) were female. The median duration of fever was 7 days (mean: 7.5 days; range: 2–17 days), and the median hospital stay was 4 days (mean: 4.5 days; range: 1–20 days). Of the total study population, 137 (80.6%) patients met the diagnostic criteria for complete KD, whereas 33 (19.4%) patients were diagnosed with incomplete KD. Among the 170 patients, 149 (87.6%) were classified as IVIG responders, while 21 (12.4%) patients were IVIG nonresponders. CALs were observed in 44 (25.9%) patients.

Comparison of initial intravenous immunoglobulin responsiveness prediction

Demographic characteristics and laboratory findings of IVIG responders and nonresponders are summarized in Table 1. No significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of age, gender, or the proportion of incomplete KD. However, IVIG nonresponders were treated earlier in the course of illness (median: 4 days [3.5–5] vs. 5 days [5–7]; P = 0.002), had a longer hospital stay (median: 7 days [5–10.5] vs. 4 days [3–4]; P < 0.001), and experienced a longer total duration of fever (mean: 9.0 ± 2.8 days vs. 6.4 ± 1.7 days; P < 0.001). The incidence of CALs during the acute phase was significantly higher in IVIG nonresponders compared to responders (42.9% vs. 16.8%; P = 0.005). Among laboratory findings, IVIG nonresponders demonstrated significantly higher levels of neutrophil proportion (77.9% [63.7–83.4] vs. 62.2% [52.1–72.9]; P = 0.001), total bilirubin (0.8 mg/dL [0.32–2.7] vs. 0.33 mg/dL [0.2–0.5]; P = 0.001), AST (94 IU/L [33–167] vs. 34 IU/L [26–59]; P = 0.004), ALT (76 IU/L [21.5–227.5] vs. 26 IU/L [14–81.5]; P = 0.006), sodium (134 mmol/L [133.5–135] vs. 136 mmol/L [134–137]; P = 0.030), and PCT (1.4 ng/mL [0.64–3.33] vs. 0.32 ng/mL [0.18–0.67]; P < 0.001). Conversely, platelet counts were significantly lower in nonresponders (289 × 103/µL [249–357] vs. 339 × 103/µL [286.5–413]; P = 0.009). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified three independent predictors of IVIG resistance: PCT (odds ratio [OR] = 1.385, 95% CI: 1.071–1.791; P = 0.013), platelet count (OR = 0.992, 95% CI, 0.985–0.999; P = 0.024), and neutrophil proportion (OR = 1.061, 95% CI, 1.019–1.106; P = 0.005) [Table 2]. ROC curve analysis revealed that PCT had the highest AUC at 0.838 (95% CI, 0.774–0.890; P < 0.001), followed by neutrophil proportion (AUC = 0.734, 95% CI, 0.661–0.799; P = 0.001) and CRP (AUC = 0.625, 95% CI, 0.548–0.698; P = 0.042) [Figure 1]. The optimal cutoff values for PCT, neutrophil proportion, and CRP were 1.80 ng/mL, 77.5%, and 8.98 mg/dL, respectively. PCT demonstrated the highest sensitivity (71.4%) and specificity (86.6%).

Comparison of coronary artery lesion prediction

CALs were observed in 44 (25.9%) patients, including 34 (20.0%) patients in the acute phase and 10 (5.9%) in the convalescent phase. The incidence of CALs was significantly higher in IVIG nonresponders compared to responders (42.9% vs. 16.8%; P = 0.005). Laboratory findings of patients with and without CALs are presented in Table 3. Significant differences were observed in neutrophil proportion (72.3% [56.6–72.3] vs. 62.0% [52.4–73.6]; P = 0.023), CRP (10.7 mg/dL [5.5–16.4] vs. 6.49 mg/dL [4.0–10.3]; P < 0.001), total bilirubin (0.5 mg/dL [0.3–0.8] vs. 0.5 mg/dL [0.2–0.5]; P = 0.005), sodium (135 mmol/L [131–136] vs. 136 mmol/L [134–137]; P = 0.006), and PCT (0.60 ng/mL [0.28–1.71] vs. 0.32 ng/mL [0.18–0.69]; P = 0.002). In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, sodium (OR = 1.15, 95% CI, 1.04–1.28; P = 0.007) and CRP (OR = 1.12, 95% CI, 1.02–1.24; P = 0.015) were retained as significant predictors of CALs, whereas PCT was excluded.

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrated the clinical utility of serum PCT as a biomarker for predicting IVIG resistance in patients with KD. PCT levels were significantly higher in IVIG nonresponders compared to responders (1.40 ng/mL vs. 0.32 ng/mL, P < 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed PCT as an independent predictor of IVIG resistance (OR = 1.385, 95% CI: 1.071–1.791, P = 0.013). At an optimal cutoff value of 1.80 ng/mL, PCT achieved an AUC of 0.838, with sensitivity and specificity of 71.4% and 86.6%, respectively, supporting its role as a practical tool for early risk stratification. While elevated PCT levels were also observed in patients with CALs, multivariate analysis did not retain PCT as an independent predictor in this context, highlighting its limitations.

KD remains one of the leading causes of acquired heart disease in children globally.[2] Despite the efficacy of high-dose IVIG treatment (2 g/kg), 10%–20% of acute KD patients still develop CALs, a concerning figure that underscores the importance of timely and effective interventions. Identifying patients at high risk for IVIG refractoriness or CALs before initiating high-dose IVIG therapy could enable more aggressive management, potentially reducing coronary complications. To address this need, various clinical features, biomarkers, and scoring systems have been studied in KD patients.[2021222324] However, the utility of a single biomarker has not yet been conclusively established.

Numerous studies have underscored the role of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1 (IL-1), IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α, in the pathogenesis of KD, highlighting their contribution to systemic inflammation and the induction of PCT secretion.[47252627] Compared to CRP, a widely used clinical marker produced primarily by hepatic cells with a delayed response of approximately 4 h, PCT is secreted by various organs within 2–4 h of the onset of systemic inflammation, providing a more immediate indicator of inflammatory activity.[2527] In addition, PCT’s shorter half-life of around 24 h allows it to dynamically reflect ongoing inflammation, unlike CRP, which can persist for 3–7 days in the absence of further stimulation.[25] However, measurements of pro-inflammatory cytokines are often outsourced to external laboratories in many hospitals, resulting in delays of several days for test results. This limitation makes it impractical to rely on cytokines for establishing treatment strategies during the acute phase. In contrast, PCT is readily available in most hospitals, making it a more practical and beneficial option for clinical use compared to the aforementioned cytokines. These attributes position PCT as a marker of inflammation severity and a potentially reliable predictor of IVIG resistance and CAL development in KD patients.

Our findings align with previous research, showing significantly higher serum PCT levels in IVIG nonresponders. Nakamura et al. reported an OR of 1.34 for PCT as a predictor of IVIG resistance, with a cutoff value of 2.18 ng/mL yielding modest sensitivity (46.4%) and high specificity (93.9%), and an AUC of 0.82. Similarly, Yoshikawa et al. identified elevated PCT levels as a risk factor for IVIG resistance, with a cutoff value of 0.5 ng/mL achieving 85% sensitivity and 64% accuracy.[16] Our results demonstrated a sensitivity of 71.4% and specificity of 86.6% at a cutoff value of 1.80 ng/mL (AUC: 0.838), comparable to these studies. However, notable discrepancies exist in cutoff values, sensitivity, and specificity, which may be attributed to differences in ethnicity, diagnostic criteria, PCT measurement methods, and, importantly, the definitions of IVIG resistance. In this study, IVIG resistance was defined as recrudescent or persistent fever lasting at least 36 h after IVIG infusion. In contrast, Yoshikawa et al. defined resistance as fever persisting beyond 48 h, while Nakamura et al. used a threshold of 24 h.[1617] Standardizing definitions and measurement methods is crucial for future research to ensure comparability across studies. Shao et al. conducted a large prospective study involving 530 Chinese KD patients, reporting significantly higher PCT levels in IVIG-resistant patients (1.70 ng/mL vs. 0.64 ng/mL) with a cutoff value of 1.48 ng/mL, sensitivity of 53.9%, specificity of 71.8%, and an AUC of 0.658.[11] Notably, their multivariate analysis did not identify PCT as an independent predictor of IVIG resistance (P = 0.986). Unlike many other studies with smaller retrospective designs, the prospective design and large cohort size lend robustness to their findings. However, their conclusion that PCT lacks utility as a predictive biomarker contrasts with numerous studies, including ours, that highlight its potential. One key difference between the studies lies in the proportion of incomplete KD cases, which was significantly higher in Shao et al.’s cohort[11] (36% vs. 19.4% in our study). Incomplete KD often presents with atypical inflammatory profiles, which may alter the behavior of biomarkers like PCT.[28293031] This difference in the proportion of incomplete KD cases could partially explain the lack of statistical significance for PCT as an independent predictor in their multivariate analysis. While both studies included similar variables, such as neutrophil percentage and CRP, in their multivariate analyses, the discrepancies in findings suggest that additional factors, such as ethnic and genetic differences, may also influence PCT’s predictive performance. The predominantly Chinese cohort in Shao et al.’s[11] study and the Korean population in ours reflect distinct genetic backgrounds, which might affect inflammatory responses and biomarker dynamics in KD. Due to China’s diverse population and large demographic size, it is challenging to determine the exact annual incidence of KD nationwide. However, some reports suggest an increasing trend, with rates ranging from 5 to 69 per 100,000. While this incidence is higher than that reported in western countries, it remains significantly lower than the rates observed in South Korea or Japan. These ethnic differences may have influenced the predictive performance of PCT in determining IVIG responsiveness.[24]

Despite these differences, our study highlights the significant association between PCT levels and IVIG resistance, emphasizing its potential clinical utility, particularly in populations with a lower prevalence of incomplete KD. However, given the inherent limitations of retrospective studies, further prospective multicenter research is needed to validate PCT’s role and explore its utility in diverse populations with standardized methodologies.

Unlike its demonstrated potential as a predictor of IVIG resistance, the utility of PCT for predicting the development of CALs remains inconclusive. While our study found significantly higher PCT levels in patients with CALs compared to those without, multivariate regression analysis did not identify PCT as an independent predictor of CAL. This finding aligns with the majority of prior studies, which have failed to establish a consistent association between PCT and the development of CALs. Okada et al. were the first to report a significant correlation between elevated PCT levels and the development of CALs in KD.[13] They identified a cutoff value of 3.0 ng/mL with 100% sensitivity, 90% specificity, and an AUC exceeding 0.8. However, their study involved a small cohort of only 25 patients and employed a nonstandard IVIG regimen (400 mg/kg for 5 days), limiting the generalizability of their findings. Yoshikawa et al. conducted a larger study involving 160 patients and reported that PCT outperformed CRP and WBC counts in predicting CALs, with a sensitivity of 67% and specificity of 56%.[16] However, they also concluded that the low sensitivity of PCT precluded its use as a standalone biomarker for predicting CALs. Other studies, apart from these two, have reported no significant association between PCT levels and the development of CALs. Other studies, though limited by small sample sizes and low incidences of CALs, have similarly reported no significant association. Given these limitations, definitive conclusions regarding PCT’s role as a predictor of CALs in KD remain premature.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design inherently introduces potential biases, such as incomplete or inconsistent data collection. Second, the single-center nature of the study may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Third, the relatively small number of patients with CALs, particularly those with CALs during the convalescent phase, reduces the statistical power to definitively evaluate the role of PCT as a clinical predictor of CALs. Finally, while our cohort size is substantial for a single-center study, larger multicenter studies are needed to confirm these findings in more diverse populations.

Clinical implications and future directions

Our findings highlight the potential of PCT as a biomarker for predicting IVIG resistance in KD, providing clinicians with an early tool for risk stratification and informed treatment decisions. Identifying high-risk patients could enable timely adjustments to treatment strategies, potentially reducing the incidence of complications. However, the limited predictive value of PCT for CALs underscores the need for a more comprehensive approach, integrating multiple biomarkers and clinical features to improve risk prediction. Future research should focus on large-scale, prospective, multicenter studies to validate the findings and improve the reliability of PCT as a biomarker in KD. Standardized definitions of IVIG resistance and CALs across studies are essential for consistent comparisons. In addition, the development of predictive models incorporating PCT with other clinical parameters may enhance its clinical utility. Finally, exploring the pathophysiological mechanisms linking PCT to KD outcomes, particularly its potential role in CALs, could provide valuable insights and refine its application in clinical practice.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates that serum PCT is a reliable biomarker for predicting resistance to IVIG in KD. Elevated PCT levels were independently associated with IVIG nonresponsiveness, with an optimal cutoff value of 1.80 ng/mL offering robust sensitivity and specificity. While PCT levels were also higher in patients with CALs, multivariate analysis did not retain them as an independent predictor, underscoring their limitations in this context. These findings highlight the value of PCT as a practical tool for early risk stratification in KD but suggest that it should be used in conjunction with other clinical parameters when assessing the risk of CALs.

To enhance the reliability and clinical application of these findings, future large-scale, multicenter studies with standardized methodologies are needed. Such research will be pivotal in validating the role of PCT and integrating it into comprehensive predictive models for optimizing KD management.
