State Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program Policies and Cardiovascular Mortality
Authors: Sriya Potluri, Nicholas Illenberger, Sameed Ahmed M. Khatana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionAre state Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) policies that impact participation associated with county-level cardiovascular mortality rates in the US?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study of 3127 US counties, a 1-point absolute increase in an index of state SNAP polices (range, 0 to 10) was associated with a 1.02% lower county cardiovascular mortality rate between 2006 and 2019.MeaningThese findings suggest that SNAP policies that increase participation among food-insecure individuals may play a role in improving cardiovascular health.

Introduction

Food insecurity has increased in recent years, and 47.4 million individuals lived in food-insecure households as of 2023.1 Food insecurity is associated with adverse cardiovascular health, including mortality.2,3,4 The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) is the largest US program providing food purchasing assistance, with more than 42 million participants in 2023.5 Previous studies have demonstrated an association of SNAP participation with improved adherence to medications for cardiovascular risk factors, as well as lower diabetes prevalence.6,7,8 More recent studies have demonstrated changes in food insecurity rates and hospitalizations associated with changes in SNAP benefit amounts.9,10 Whether changes in SNAP-related policies, and in SNAP participation, are associated with differences in cardiovascular mortality is unknown.

Although SNAP is federally funded, it is state administered. Therefore, there are state-level variation in policies related to eligibility assessment and administrative burden. Examples of such policies include the exemption of vehicles from asset tests or requirements to be fingerprinted. These policies have been shown to impact SNAP participation, result in variation in participation between states, and are also associated with population-level food insecurity.11,12,13,14,15 Such state-level variation has been proposed as a way of isolating the association of SNAP participation with health outcomes.11 This approach is necessary, as both area-level SNAP participation and cardiovascular mortality are likely to be linked to socioeconomic and demographic factors. In addition, evaluating individual policies also allows for understanding whether more-widespread adoption of such policies may potentially play a role in population-level cardiovascular health. To evaluate the association of SNAP with cardiovascular mortality, we examined the relationship of an index of state SNAP-related policies that have previously been demonstrated to be associated with SNAP participation with county-level cardiovascular mortality rates between 2006 and 2019, using a g-computation framework, a robust causal inference method that can better account for time-varying confounding than traditional regression-based methods.

Methods

This cross-sectional analysis was considered exempt from review and the need for informed consent by the University of Pennsylvania institutional review board guidelines. This study follows the reporting criteria of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for cross-sectional studies.16

SNAP Policy Index

As a measure of adoption of different state-level SNAP-related policies, we calculated a SNAP policy index using data on policy adoption from the US Department of Agriculture’s SNAP policy database.17 A similar approach has been previously used to develop an index of SNAP policies associated with participation.11,12 On the basis of these prior studies, we obtained monthly state adoption data of the following policies: average recertification period (between eligibility assessments), broad-based categorical eligibility, combined application processing with Supplemental Security Income, exemption of vehicles from asset tests, eligibility for legal noncitizens, fingerprinting requirements, online application availability, proportion of benefits accounted for by Electronic Benefit Transfer, and simplified reporting of earnings. Continuous variables were rescaled from 0 to 1. For binary variables, the annual proportion of months in which a policy was adopted was used. As with prior analyses, an unweighted mean of all policies was used.11,12 As the association of different policies with individual or household-level participation was not evaluated in this analysis, an unweighted index was utilized that does not specifically assign greater weight to the association of any particular policy with the outcomes studied. The index was scaled to be from 0 to 10, with higher values denoting adoption of policies associated with higher SNAP participation. Additional details about each policy and the calculation of the SNAP policy index are in the eMethods in Supplement 1.

Cardiovascular Mortality Rates, Demographic Data, and Outcomes

County-level mortality rates for 2006 to 2019 were calculated using restricted mortality data obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics. All deaths among adults aged 20 years or older with cardiovascular disease as the primary cause of death were identified using International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision codes I00 to I99. County-level population estimates were obtained from the US Census Bureau data. Mortality rates were age-adjusted to the 2000 US Census population. Other county-level economic and demographic measures were also obtained from Census data. A small number of counties (16 counties) were excluded from the analysis because of missing data. The study period of 2006 to 2019 was chosen due to availability of covariate and SNAP policy data during this period. The primary outcome of interest was the change in the annual county-level cardiovascular mortality associated with the absolute change in the SNAP policy index from the baseline year, 2006.

Statistical Analysis

We first divided states according to tertiles of the baseline SNAP policy index in 2006 and calculated summary measures of baseline county-level demographic and economic variables in each tertile of states. To evaluate how change in the index is associated with changes in cardiovascular mortality rates, we employed a g-computation framework. G-computation is a robust causal inference method that, compared with traditional regression-based methods, can better account for time-varying confounding.18 In the first step of the g-computation process, estimating the joint relationship between the time-varying exposure, outcome, and covariates, conditional outcome and covariate models are fit using observed outcome and exposure data, as well as any potential confounders. As both SNAP policies and cardiovascular mortality may be associated with potential socioeconomic, demographic, and health care–related confounders, the models included all county-level measures that compose the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Social Vulnerability Index and that have been shown previously to be associated with population-level cardiovascular health (eMethods in Supplement 1).19,20 We also included the proportion of adults aged 18 to 64 years without health insurance, the proportion of the population from different racial and ethnic groups, county metropolitan status (based on the 2013 US Department of Agriculture Rural-Urban Continuum Codes), year, and state fixed effects in the model. Because of the high-dimensional and complex nature of these covariates, the joint relationship between the outcome and covariates was modeled using random forest algorithms and was performed for each individual year separately in the study period. As population-level health outcomes have been shown to change within a 1-year period after changes in SNAP benefit amounts, for all the years after the baseline (2006), 1-year lagged values of all variables, including the exposure, were also included in the models.10 This accounts for the carry-over effect of prior year’s values on both the outcome and potential confounders in the current year. Given the aggregate nature of county-level data, county population was used as case weights for the random forest models, which ensures that the models prioritize learning covariate patterns from more populous counties. After estimation of the joint relationship between measured exposure, outcome, and covariate data, different potential outcomes were generated by inputting different trajectories of the SNAP policy index into this data-generating mechanism. Generated potential outcomes were then used to estimate a marginal structural model summarizing the expected difference in age-adjusted cardiovascular mortality resulting from changes in the SNAP policy index. A generalized linear marginal structural model with a gaussian distribution and log-link and county population weights was then fit with the age-adjusted cardiovascular mortality rate as the outcome, and baseline SNAP index, absolute change in the SNAP index from baseline year, and year fixed effects as the independent variables. The 95% CIs for each of the parameters in the marginal structure model were constructed on the basis of quantiles on the nonparametric state-clustered bootstrap using 1000 iterations. Additional details on this g-computation analysis are in the eMethods in Supplement 1.

As a secondary analysis, to examine the association of each policy composing the SNAP policy index, a model with each of the individual policies was fit. Given the high multicollinearity between changes in different policy components, we also performed a dominance analysis. This was conducted by calculating the proportion of the McFadden R2 explained by the changes in each individual policy relative to the total variance explained by all policy changes combined.

Because cardiovascular mortality rates, SNAP participation, and food insecurity rates vary significantly by demographic characteristics, we conducted subgroup analyses by fitting models for subgroups of age, sex, and race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic other race [American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian or Pacific Islander, multiracial, unknown, or any other race not otherwise specified], and Hispanic [any race]). A model with mortality from unintentional injuries (International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision codes V01-X59 and Y85-Y86), a cause of death that has been associated with socioeconomic conditions but that may not be causally associated with SNAP participation, was used as a potential negative control.21,22

To supplement the results of our marginal structural models, we worked under the longitudinal modified treatment policy framework to estimate how many cardiovascular deaths may have been observed within each year under different levels of SNAP policy adoption.23 Specifically, we constructed 2 hypothetical scenarios: (1) all states adopting state SNAP policies corresponding to the 90th percentile of the SNAP index for each year (high-generosity trajectory) compared with (2) all states adopting policies corresponding to the 10th percentile of the index for each year (low-generosity index). We compared the difference in population-weighted cardiovascular mortality rates across the US from 2007 to 2019 under these 2 scenarios and under the observed covariate and exposure scenario across all counties in the US.

The following additional sensitivity models were fit: (1) inclusion of 1-year lagged outcomes in the outcome random forest models; (2) inclusion of a measure for Medicaid expansion status (the proportion of months in a year after Medicaid expansion); (3) use of a gamma distribution with a log-link for the generalized linear marginal structural model; and (4) use of 2-year lags for all outcome and covariate random forest models. Summary measures and model estimates are presented as medians and IQRs or as means and 95% CIs. All analyses were performed using RStudio statistical software version 4.2.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was defined as 2-sided P < .05.

Results

Among the 3127 US counties included, the median (IQR) SNAP policy index value in 2006 was 3.7 (2.7-4.2). A map of US states based on the SNAP policy index in 2006 and the absolute change from 2006 to 2019 is displayed in Figure 1. Summary measures of the county-level variables included in the analysis, across tertiles of states based on baseline SNAP index levels (tertile 1, 0-2.7; tertile 2, 2.8-3.8; tertile 3, 3.9-9.9), are displayed in Table 1. Counties in the first tertile had a lower median (IQR) proportion of the population who were non-Hispanic Black (0.8% [0.4%-3.2%]) compared with counties in the second (4.6% [1.1%-18.5%]) and third (2.5% [11.5%-18.9%) tertiles. Counties in the first tertile of states also had lower median poverty rates and proportion of counties that were metropolitan compared with the other tertiles of states.

Between 2006 and 2019, the median (IQR) SNAP policy index value across all counties increased from 3.7 (2.7-4.2) to 7.1 (5.6-7.5). The median (IQR) absolute change in the index across the study period was 2.8 (1.1-4.4). Annual values of the SNAP policy index for each state are listed in eTable 1 in Supplement 1. Minnesota had the greatest absolute increase in the SNAP index, with an increase from 1.5 in 2006 to 8.2 in 2019 (Figure 1). Wisconsin had the greatest absolute decrease in the SNAP index, with a decrease from 8.6 to 7.2. Between 2006 and 2019, there were a total of 11 409 251 cardiovascular deaths among adults 20 years and older in the US, of which 5 713 305 (50.0%) occurred among women and 9 266 123 (81.2%) occurred among adults aged 65 years and older.

Marginal Structural Models

In the marginal structural model, after accounting for baseline SNAP index levels and year fixed effects, a 1-point greater increase in the SNAP policy index from 2006 was associated with a 1.02% (95% CI, 0.47% to 1.60%; P < .001) lower county-level cardiovascular mortality rate (Table 2). For the models including change in each SNAP-related policy separately, instead of as a composite index, mortality rates were lower but not statistically significant for adoption of all policies, except for average recertification period and eligibility for noncitizen working age adults. In the dominance analysis, change in broad-based categorical eligibility had the highest mean dominance at 26.9% (Table 3). In the model with mortality from external injury as the outcome, change in in the SNAP index was not associated with a significant change (0.3%; 95% CI −0.5% to 1.2%).

In the subgroup models, a greater increase in the SNAP index was associated with statistically significantly lower cardiovascular mortality rates for all subgroups of age and sex, and for non-Hispanic Black and non-Hispanic White adults (Table 2). There was a significantly greater decrease among women compared with men (−0.24%; 95% CI, −0.45% to −0.03%) (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Estimates for subgroups of age and of race and ethnicity did not significantly differ from one another.

Trajectories

Simulated trajectories of annual cardiovascular mortality rates based on hypothetical high-generosity and low-generosity SNAP index trajectories, as well as a trajectory based on observed levels of the SNAP index, are displayed in Figure 2 and in eTable 3 in Supplement 1. Compared with the low-generosity scenario, the cardiovascular mortality rate in the high-generosity scenario was estimated to be 18.7 deaths per 100 000 adults lower in 2019 (95% CI, 11.6-26.4 deaths per 100 000 adults) (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). On the basis of the adult population in the US in 2019, this translates to 46 123 fewer deaths (95% CI, 28 587-65 096 fewer deaths) due to cardiovascular disease. Over the course of the study period, based on the annual US population, an annual mean of 15 041 fewer cardiovascular deaths (95% CI, 7451-23 375 fewer cardiovascular deaths) would be projected to occur under the high-generosity SNAP policy scenario compared with the observed trajectory.

Sensitivity Analyses

Across all 4 sensitivity models, the estimated association between the SNAP policy index and cardiovascular mortality remained stable. The estimates did not change significantly by the inclusion of lagged outcomes, adjustment for Medicaid expansion, or changes to the model distribution and lag structure (eTable 5 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study found that, between 2006 and 2019, adoption of state policies that expand eligibility and reduce barriers for SNAP participation was associated with lower county-level adult cardiovascular mortality rates. A comparison of different potential SNAP policy trajectories over the study period estimated that if all states had adopted most of such policies, compared with if they had followed a less-generous trajectory, this adoption would be associated with approximately 46 123 (95% CI, 28 587 to 65 096) fewer adult cardiovascular deaths across the US in 2019. A comparison of individual policies suggested that the greatest contribution to the association with mortality was through adoption of broad-based categorical eligibility, a policy that allows states greater flexibility in expanding SNAP eligibility. These findings suggest that policies expanding SNAP eligibility are associated with cardiovascular health.

The association between food insecurity and cardiovascular health is well established.2,3,4 Food-insecure individuals have lower access to healthy foods linked to improved cardiovascular health.24 In addition, food insecurity is associated with lower medication adherence, as well as high levels of psychosocial stress, which both may be directly linked to adverse cardiovascular events.25,26,27 With high and increasing levels of food insecurity in the US, identifying approaches to address food insecurity may have an important population-level impact on cardiovascular health.1 Previous studies have identified an association between SNAP participation and improvements in adherence to medications for cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension and diabetes, county-level diabetes prevalence, and fewer hospitalizations.7,8 The findings in this analysis of the association of SNAP-related policies with lower county-level cardiovascular mortality rates are, therefore, consistent with this prior research. However, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to identify an association with population-level cardiovascular mortality rates.

In the model including individual SNAP policies, likely owing to strong multicollinearity between policies, no change in a single policy was statistically significantly associated with change in mortality rates. However, when assessing dominance—that is, what proportion of the variance is explained by a particular variable—adoption of broad-based categorical eligibility had the greatest dominance. Previous studies of state SNAP-related policies have noted that broad-based categorical eligibility is associated with a greater increase in SNAP participation compared with other policies included in the index.11,12 Broad-based categorical eligibility can expand eligibility for SNAP to households that also qualify for noncash assistance through Temporary Assistance for Needy Families or state maintenance of effort–funded benefits and simplifies the application process for eligible households. As of 2025, 8 states (Alaska, Kansas, Mississippi, Missouri, South Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, and Wyoming) had yet to adopt broad-based categorical eligibility and 1 state (Arkansas) had only done so in a limited manner.28 These states not only have somewhat higher food insecurity rates (13% vs 12% of households in 2021-2023) but many also have higher rates of cardiovascular risk factors and mortality than the US as a whole.1,29 Adoption of broad-based categorical eligibility may, therefore, be an important lever in improving the cardiovascular health of individuals with a lower socioeconomic status in these states.

Although not specifically evaluated in the current analysis, recent changes to the SNAP eligibility and administration, such as expansion of work reporting requirements and greater eligibility restrictions among noncitizens, are projected to lead to lower SNAP participation in the coming years.30 On the basis of the findings of this analysis, in which policies associated with participation were also associated with significant changes in cardiovascular mortality, this therefore raises the concern that lower SNAP participation may result in an increase in adverse health outcomes.

Limitations

This study has limitations. Although the g-computation approach can better account for time dependence among measured confounders than traditional regression, it does not account for unmeasured confounders. Our models included many county-level covariates that have been previously shown to be associated with area-level health outcomes, reducing the risk of confounding bias. In addition, the null association between the SNAP policy index and accidental deaths as a negative control suggests against strong unmeasured confounding, although it cannot definitively rule out residual bias. As this is an ecological county-level analysis, individual-level inferences cannot be made. The county-level cardiovascular mortality rates were not specifically for individuals who may be eligible for SNAP; therefore, the association between cardiovascular mortality and the SNAP policy index may be underestimated.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study of changes in state policies associated with SNAP participation, a greater adoption of these policies was associated with lower county-level cardiovascular mortality. These results suggest that policies that increase SNAP participation and, therefore, decrease food insecurity could improve the cardiovascular health of lower-income US adults.
