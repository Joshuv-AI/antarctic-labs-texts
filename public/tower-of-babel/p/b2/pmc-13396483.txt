Improved dynamic average modeling and impedance analysis of multi-pulse diode rectifiers
Authors: Hani Albalawi, Aadel Mohammed Alatwi, Abdul Wadood, Ibrahem E. Atawi, Shahbaz Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Detailed switching models of power-electronic rectifiers accurately represent diode commutation and waveform discontinuities, but they require long simulation times and may introduce numerical difficulties in system-level studies. Conventional static average-value models reduce computational burden but may not adequately capture transient load-dependent dynamics and impedance characteristics. This study presents improved dynamic average-value models (AVMs) for 6-pulse and 18-pulse uncontrolled diode rectifiers using a first-order Taylor-series representation of the load-current variation. The proposed models preserve the dominant low- and medium-frequency dynamics while eliminating high-frequency switching events. DC output impedance and AC-side dq-domain impedance components are extracted using network-analyzer-based measurement and line-to-line small-signal current injection. The proposed AVMs are validated against detailed switching models and laboratory prototypes using waveform comparison, RMSE, MAPE, and simulation-time reduction. The results show close agreement with detailed models and experimental measurements while reducing simulation time by approximately 100–110 times. These findings demonstrate that the proposed dynamic AVMs provide an efficient and experimentally supported modeling framework for impedance-based analysis of multi-pulse diode rectifiers.

Introduction

Power-electronic converters are widely used in aerospace power systems, shipboard distribution networks, industrial drives, renewable-energy interfaces, and converter-fed AC-DC distribution systems. Advanced converter topologies and control methods, such as dual-active-bridge DC-DC converters and model-predictive control strategies for high-power converters, have been widely investigated to improve efficiency, dynamic response, and system-level performance1,2. In these applications, diode rectifiers remain attractive because of their simple structure, high reliability, and control-free operation. Among them, multi-pulse diode rectifiers are commonly adopted when improved input-current quality, reduced harmonic distortion, and lower DC-side ripple are required. Six-pulse rectifiers are widely used in low- and medium-power applications, while higher-pulse configurations, such as 18-pulse rectifiers, are employed to achieve improved harmonic cancellation and smoother DC output. In practical systems, phase-shifting transformers or autotransformer-based arrangements are used to generate multiple phase-displaced voltage sets that feed several diode-bridge Sects3–6.

Accurate modeling of multi-pulse rectifiers is essential for transient simulation, design evaluation, and stability assessment. Detailed switching models can represent diode conduction, commutation overlap, current transfer between phases, and waveform discontinuities with high fidelity. However, these models require small simulation time steps and involve nonlinear switching events, which increase computational burden and may introduce numerical difficulties during long-duration or repeated simulations. This limitation becomes particularly important in system-level studies, where the rectifier model may be connected with other power-electronic converters, filters, and dynamic loads. Therefore, reduced-order models that retain the dominant low-frequency behavior while reducing computational effort are required7,8. Average-value models (AVMs) provide an effective alternative by replacing discontinuous switching behavior with continuous averaged representations. AVMs for AC-DC converters may be developed using explicit mathematical modeling or functional data-based modeling. Explicit mathematical models are derived from circuit equations, conduction intervals, commutation periods, and converter parameters, while functional models approximate converter behavior from simulation or experimental input-output data9–11. Although conventional static AVMs are useful for steady-state analysis, they often assume a constant or slowly varying DC load current. Related reduced-order and impedance-oriented modeling studies have addressed transient and stability analysis of power-electronics-based systems12, paralleled voltage-source converter interactions13, synchronverter internal-voltage dynamics14, and average-value modeling of flexible converters in hybrid AC/DC distribution networks15. However, conventional static AVMs can still be limited in reproducing transient load-dependent behavior and small-signal impedance characteristics. This limitation is important because modern electrical power systems are increasingly dominated by interconnected converters and tightly regulated loads as shown in Figure 1. In such systems, source-load interaction can affect stability, especially when constant-power loads introduce negative incremental impedance and reduce system damping16,17. Impedance decoupling and impedance-shaping methods have therefore been investigated for improving dynamic performance in DC distribution systems18. Similar impedance-based frameworks have been applied to converter-interfaced generator interconnection studies19, voltage-source-converter HVDC systems20, grid-connected inverters under unbalanced conditions21, and high-frequency resonance analysis of doubly-fed induction-generator systems in weak grids22. For AC converter systems, dq-domain impedance modeling is commonly used to represent coupling between direct- and quadrature-axis voltage and current perturbations23. In DC microgrids and power-electronics-dominated systems, negative-impedance compensation and impedance-based stability methods have also been used to improve stability under tightly regulated loading conditions24,25, while Nyquist-based criteria provide a frequency-domain basis for internal stability assessment26. Impedance-based methods are also widely used for assessing interconnected converter interaction and grid-connected system stability27. Hence, accurate impedance extraction requires a rectifier model that can preserve the relevant dynamic behavior without the excessive computational burden of a detailed switching model.

Several methods are available for small-signal modeling and impedance extraction, including analytical linearization, network-analyzer-based measurement, chirp-signal injection, and frequency-domain perturbation techniques28,29. Comparative studies of 12, 18, and 24-pulse rectifier systems have also shown that increasing the pulse number affects harmonic cancellation, ripple behavior, and dynamic performance30. Impedance modeling of grid-connected voltage-source converters and dq-domain small-signal impedance analysis of grid-tied inverters further provide important foundations for converter interaction and stability studies31,32. Analytical methods provide useful insight, but they become increasingly complex for high-pulse rectifiers because commutation and conduction intervals must be treated separately. Frequency-domain injection methods are particularly practical for validation because they allow impedance to be extracted from measured voltage and current perturbations while the converter operates near its nominal condition. For AC-side impedance measurement, line-to-line current injection followed by dq transformation and frequency-domain processing provides a practical route for obtaining the required impedance components. To address these modeling and impedance-analysis challenges, this paper develops improved dynamic AVMs for 6-pulse and 18-pulse uncontrolled diode rectifiers. Compared with existing AVM studies that mainly replace the switching converter with a static averaged representation, the proposed method incorporates first-order load-current variation into the averaged formulation. This modification allows the model to capture transient load-dependent behavior while preserving the computational efficiency of conventional AVMs. In contrast to impedance-analysis studies that mainly focus on extracting impedance from detailed models or hardware prototypes, this work directly links the impedance-extraction process with the proposed dynamic AVM framework. The first-order Taylor-series approximation is selected because the proposed impedance analysis is based on small perturbations around a continuous-conduction steady-state operating point. Under this condition, the first-order term captures the dominant sensitivity of the averaged rectifier variables to load-current variation. Higher-order Taylor expansion may improve local approximation accuracy, but it increases algebraic complexity and reduces the simplicity required for system-level impedance studies. Similarly, full Jacobian-based symbolic linearization becomes cumbersome for multi-pulse rectifiers because conduction and commutation intervals must be handled separately, while piecewise-linear models increase implementation complexity across operating regions. Therefore, first-order Taylor-series expansion provides an appropriate compromise between analytical tractability, computational efficiency, and dynamic accuracy for the intended low to medium-frequency impedance range.

The novelty of this work does not lie in the Taylor-series expansion itself. Rather, the main contribution is the integration of first-order load-current variation into a dynamic AVM framework for multi-pulse diode rectifiers, followed by validation through detailed switching-model comparison, dq-domain impedance extraction, and experimental prototype measurements. For clarity and reproducibility, the main variables, parameters, and impedance terms used in the proposed dynamic AVM formulation and impedance-analysis procedure are summarized in Appendix Table A1. Quantitative validation further supports the proposed approach. For the 6-pulse rectifier, the DC voltage and current errors were 7.01% and 7.07% MAPE, with RMSE values of 18.06 V and 0.57 A, respectively. For the 18-pulse rectifier, the corresponding errors were reduced to 0.33% and 0.23% MAPE, with RMSE values of 1.06 V and 0.014 A. In addition, the proposed AVMs reduced simulation time from 3526 s to 32 s for the 6-pulse case and from 3700 s to 37 s for the 18-pulse case, corresponding to approximately 110 and 100 times computational improvement, respectively. Mathematical average-value models

The proposed modeling procedure follows a structured sequence. First, the rectifier operation is divided into conduction and commutation intervals according to the diode conduction states. Second, nonlinear circuit equations are formulated separately for each interval. Third, the DC load current is represented using a first-order Taylor-series approximation around the steady-state operating point to include the dominant effect of load-current variation. The interval-based equations are then integrated over their corresponding conduction and commutation ranges to obtain averaged expressions. Finally, the AC-side variables are transformed into the dq-domain for transient simulation and impedance extraction. The derivation assumes continuous conduction mode (CCM), where diode conduction intervals are well defined, and the averaged DC output voltage is a continuous function of load current and commutation angle. Under this condition, the first-order load-current term captures the dominant dynamic effect of current variation while preserving analytical simplicity. In discontinuous conduction mode (DCM), the current becomes piecewise-defined and the first-order approximation around the CCM operating point may no longer remain valid. Therefore, the proposed model is intended for high-power rectifier applications operating primarily in CCM.

The generalized AVM circuit, presented in Figure 2, is used for the rectifier systems considered in this work. The AC-side dq-axis branches, represented by equivalent resistance and inductance terms, describe the averaged input-current dynamics, while the DC branch represents the rectified output-current and voltage dynamics. Simulations and experimental results

The proposed dynamic AVMs were validated by comparing their transient responses with detailed switching models and experimental prototype measurements for both the 6-pulse and 18-pulse rectifiers. The validation focused on DC output voltage, DC output current, and AC-side dq-axis current components because these variables directly reflect the interaction between the AC supply and DC load. All simulations were performed in MATLAB/Simulink 2019 using the electrical parameters listed in Tables 2 and 3. To ensure a fair comparison, the switching model and the proposed AVM were tested under identical load-step conditions. The detailed switching models reproduce diode commutation and waveform discontinuities, while the proposed AVMs remove high-frequency switching events and retain the dominant low-frequency dynamics. Therefore, close agreement between the two models indicates that the proposed AVM preserves the relevant transient behaviour while significantly reducing computational burden. The comparison was further supported by experimental measurements obtained from laboratory prototypes.

Experimental setup for impedance measurements

To verify the accuracy and dynamic performance of the proposed dynamic AVMs, DC and AC impedance measurements were conducted using both simulation models and laboratory prototypes. The same operating conditions were used for the detailed switching model, the proposed AVM, and the experimental prototype to ensure a consistent comparison. The experimental validation focused on the DC output impedance and AC-side dq-domain impedance components of the 6-pulse and 18-pulse diode rectifiers. The hardware setup consisted of the rectifier prototype, input AC supply, load bank, signal-injection circuit, voltage and current sensors, data-acquisition system, and MATLAB-based post-processing environment. The DC impedance was measured using a network-analyzer-based perturbation method, whereas the AC impedance was obtained using line-to-line small-signal current injection followed by dq-domain transformation and FFT-based frequency-response extraction. Figures 19, 20, 21, 22 and 23 present the AC-side dq-domain impedance characteristics obtained from the proposed AVM, detailed switching model, and experimental prototype. The magnitude and phase responses show that the AVM reproduces the dominant low- and medium-frequency impedance trends of the detailed model. For the 6-pulse rectifier, slightly larger deviations occur because the phase currents contain stronger ripple and more pronounced commutation effects, which are present in the switching model and hardware prototype but smoothed in the AVM. For the 18-pulse rectifier, the agreement improves because the phase-shifted structure reduces low-order harmonic content and produces smoother current waveforms. The comparison in Figure 23 confirms that the proposed AVM captures the dominant AC-side impedance behaviour of the physical rectifier, with remaining differences mainly attributed to measurement uncertainty, sensor bandwidth, passive-component tolerances, and unmodelled parasitic effects.

Conclusions

This study presents improved dynamic average-value models (AVMs) for 6-pulse and 18-pulse uncontrolled diode rectifiers by incorporating first-order load-current variation into the averaged rectifier formulation. The proposed models remove high-frequency diode switching events while preserving the dominant low- and medium-frequency dynamics required for transient simulation and impedance-based analysis. The proposed AVMs were validated against detailed switching models and experimental prototype measurements. For the 6-pulse rectifier, the DC output-voltage and output-current MAPE values were 7.01 and 7.07%, with RMSE values of 18.06 V and 0.57 A, respectively. For the 18-pulse rectifier, the corresponding MAPE values were reduced to 0.33 and 0.23%, with RMSE values of 1.06 V and 0.014 A. The simulation time was reduced from 3526 to 32 s for the 6-pulse case and from 3700 to 37 s for the 18-pulse case, corresponding to approximately 110 and 100 times improvement, respectively. DC output impedance and AC-side dq-domain impedance results further confirmed close agreement among the proposed AVM, detailed switching model, and experimental prototype. The present work focuses on balanced supply conditions and continuous conduction mode. High-frequency device-level effects, such as diode reverse recovery and parasitic capacitances, were not included because the objective was to model averaged low- and medium-frequency behavior. Future work may extend the framework to nonlinear loads, unbalanced supply conditions, discontinuous conduction operation, and interconnected multi-converter power systems.
