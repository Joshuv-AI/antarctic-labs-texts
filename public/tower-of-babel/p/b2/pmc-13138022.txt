Evaluation of hydrological models at gauged and ungauged basins using machine learning-based limits-of-acceptability and hydrological signatures
Authors: Abhinav Gupta, Mohamed M Hantush, Rao S Govindaraju, Keith Beven
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrological models are evaluated by comparisons with observed hydrological quantities such as streamflow. A model evaluation procedure should account for dominantly epistemic errors in hydrological data such as model input precipitation and streamflow and avoid type-2 errors (rejecting a good model). This study uses quantile random forest (QRF) to develop limits-of-acceptability (LoA) over streamflows that account for uncertainties in precipitation and streamflow values. A significant advantage of this method is that it can be used to evaluate models even at ungauged basins. This method was used to evaluate a hydrological model –Sacramento Soil Moisture Accounting (SAC-SMA) – over the St. Joseph River Watershed (SJRW) for both gauged and hypothetical ungauged scenarios. QRF defined wide LoAs that yielded a large number of models as behavioral, suggesting the need for additional measures to develop a more discriminating inference procedure. The paper discusses why the LoAs defined by QRF were wide, along with some ways to define more discriminating LoAs. To further constrain the model, five streamflow-based signatures (i.e., autocorrelation function, Hurst exponent, baseflow index, flow duration curve, and long-term runoff coefficient) were used. The combination of LoAs over streamflow and streamflow-based signatures helped constrain the set of behavioral models in both the gauged and the ungauged scenarios. Among the signatures used in this study, the Hurst exponent and baseflow index were the most useful ones. All the 1-million models evaluated in this study were eventually rejected as unfit-for-purpose.Keywords: Streamflow, Model (in)validation, Limits-of-acceptability, Machine learning, Prediction at ungauged basins

Introduction

Environmental models need to be evaluated against field observations for their fitness-for-purpose and their ability to model system dynamics (Beven, 2002; Hrachowitz et al., 2014; Beven, 2019; Parker, 2020; Beven and Chappell, 2021; Beven and Lane, 2022). Hydrological models utilize precipitation data and other meteorological inputs to simulate fluxes such as streamflows and states such as soil moisture as outputs. For a model to be considered good, simulated hydrological quantities should be consistent with corresponding available observations where consistency between observed and predicted quantities is measured using one or multiple objective functions depending upon the purpose of modeling. Model evaluation is complicated by the uncertainty due to the presence of errors in model input and observed output quantities (e.g., Renard et al, 2010; Le Coz et al., 2014; Beven, 2019, 2023; Bárdossy and Anwar, 2023). In what follows, the term ‘model’ will be used to refer to both model structures and any parameter set of a model structure.

Often, a single parameter set of a model structure that optimizes a goodness-of-fit (GoF) measure is used as the optimal parameter set (e.g., Knoben et al., 2020; Kratzert et al., 2019; Mai, 2023). In some cases, several optimal parameter sets, referred to as pareto optimal, are identified based on multi-objective optimization (Harvey et al., 2023). However, the optimal parameter set is not well defined for hydrological models (Beven and Binley, 1992) because there often exist several parameter sets and model structures − referred to as equifinal models − that simulate the observed hydrological quantities with acceptable accuracy over the period of available data (Beven, 2006). A single optimal parameter set obtained by a global optimization routine (e.g., Duan et al., 1992; Tolson and Shoemaker, 2007) depends upon the objective function being considered and the calibration period (Beven, 2023), and hence is ill-defined given a finite calibration period and a small number of observed hydrological quantities – in most studies only observed streamflow data are used to evaluate a model. Even though equifinal models may yield appoximately equally acceptable streamflow estimates over the calibration period, their performance for other periods might be very different. A validation period is typically used for independent assessment of model performance, but different parameter sets may perform acceptably for a validation period also. Further, equifinal parameter sets may yield very different simulations of internal fluxes and states (Gallart et al., 2007; Khatami et al., 2019; Hughes and Farinosi, 2021). Equifinality may also exist in terms of pre-calibration model setup such as model discretization (Refsgaard et al., 2022).

Many methods have been proposed within the hydrologic literature to evaluate models and quantify uncertainties (see Gupta and Govindaraju (2022) for a recent review) including formal Bayesian methods (e.g., Kuczera et al., 2006; Vrugt et al., 2022), frequentist methods (Pande 2013a, 2013b), information-theoretic methods (Gong et al., 2013; Weijs et al., 2010a; 2010b; 2013), and generalized likelihood uncertainty estimation method (GLUE; Beven and Binley, 1992, 2014). The formal statistical methods use a probabilistic likelihood function to quantify the information in hydrological data and are based on aleatoric assumptions about the uncertainties. Thus, these methods are not applicable in hydrological modeling without strong assumptions because uncertainties encountered in hydrology are dominantly epistemic (Beven, 2019). Epistemic uncertainties, by definition, have unknowable statistical properties for a given amount of data (Gupta and Govindaraju, 2022). The errors in hydrology may vary from event to event in an arbitrary but non-random manner and are nonstationary (Beven, 2016). Further, there may be observed events that do not satisfy mass balance or other physical constraints, referred to as disinformative periods (Beven and Westerberg, 2011; Frame et al., 2023). These disinformative periods may introduce biases during parameter estimation and affect the antecedent conditions for subsequent events (Beven and Smith, 2015). Therefore, it is very difficult to realistically define probabilities in the case of epistemic errors (Berger and Smith, 2019) even though probabilities have a sound epistemic underpinning (Jaynes, 2002; Montanari and Koutsoyiannis, 2012; Nearing et al., 2016). To some extent, the problem of wrong assumptions can be addressed by making inference in spectral space, but this method has limitations as well (Schaefli and Kavetski, 2017; Gupta and Govindaraju, 2022). Information-theory based GoF metrics do not account for such uncertainties and are often used for finding an optimal parameter set. Several attempts have been made to model rainfall, streamflow and structural errors separately and explicitly (e.g., Renard et al., 2010), however these studies show that the parameters of these error models cannot be identified without strong prior information on these errors; that is, structural and measurement uncertainties cannot be separated.

The idea of multi-objective optimization has also been used in hydrological modeling (e.g., Yapo et al., 1998; Efstratiadis, & Koutsoyiannis, 2010; Shafii et al., 2015; Harvey et al., 2023) where several models are obtained by optimizing multiple objectives simultaneously. These studies show that no single model is best for all the objective functions. Typically, a set of models, referred to as Pareto front, is obtained such that no model in the set is better or worse than other models in terms of all the objectives. While Pareto fronts address some of the problems with identifying a single model based on a single objective function, they do not address issues arising from uncertainty in the data and several good models within the limits of uncertainty might still be rejected (type-2 error) in the process. Along with streamflow, soil moisture and evapotranspiration data (e.g., Refsgaard et al., 2022; Mei et al., 2023), isotope data (Iorgulescu et al., 2007; Beria, 2020), groundwater levels (Gallart et al., 2007) etc. have been used in earlier studies to reduce equifinality. Typically, uncertainties in these data, including uncertainty due to scale mismatch between some data, are not considered while comparing observed quantities against their corresponding simulated values.

In the GLUE framework, subjective likelihoods based on hydrological reasoning, also referred to as informal likelihoods, are used to evaluate the degree to which a model is consistent with the data. The informal likelihood may be any GoF metric such as Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE). The idea behind using informal measures is to reduce the overconditioning of the parameter sets on data (e.g., Smith et al., 2008). The main limitation of the early application of informal likelihood measures was that the modeler had to decide the threshold value of NSE (or any other GoF metric) beyond which a model could be deemed behavioral. This is a subjective choice given the epistemic uncertainties in hydrological data. Also, GoF measures collapse all the information in hydrological data into a single number, and thus, may not be able to represent information in hydrological data well (Gupta et al., 2008). However, several such GoF measures emphasizing different parts of the hydrograph (or another response variable) may be used in combination to address this problem, as is done in multi-objective optimization, but the fundamental problem remains that these measures may be impacted by the uncertainties (Beven, 2024).

The limits-of-acceptability (LoA) method has been proposed within the GLUE framework to address some of the limitations of early GLUE applications (Beven, 2006; Liu et al., 2009; Krueger et al., 2010; Coxon et al., 2014; Hollaway et al., 2018; Beven, 2019; Beven and Lane, 2022). LoAs are defined as the upper and lower bounds over streamflow (or any other relevant quantity) such that these bounds reflect the effects of errors in hydrological data (Beven et al., 2022; Gupta et al., 2023a). Thus, a model that simulates streamflows within LoAs (while accounting for potential outliers) may be considered as being consistent with the data. The goal is to identify an adequate sample of models that simulate streamflow within LoAs. Note that this approach is different from identifying the good models based on comparing observed and simulated streamflow in that LoAs must be defined before any model calibration to reduce interactions between measurement and structural uncertainties. Further, since LoAs are defined for each time step, a model can be evaluated at the timestep level using the LoA framework, which is also useful in understanding why a model fails.

The main advantage of the LoA method is that the conditions of model acceptability are defined before any model evaluation takes place. Once the LoAs are defined, a likelihood function can be based on the LoAs, which can also be used in Approximate Bayesian Computation framework (Nott et al., 2012; Sadegh and Vrugt, 2013; Vrugt and Sadegh, 2013; Vrugt and Beven, 2018). The traditional applications of formal Bayesian methods do not impose any limits on the spread of the error distribution, allowing a greater interaction between structural and measurement uncertainties. Indeed, the formal Bayesian methods assign a small probability to bad models but never reject them (Lindley, 2013), while GLUE and LoA methods allow rejection of models.

Often, LoAs are defined based on streamflow uncertainty only (e.g., Coxon et al., 2014), and the effect of precipitation uncertainty is included by subjectively increasing the width of the LoA (e.g., Krueger et al., 2010). In some small catchment studies where inputs could be assumed well known and LoAs were based only on streamflow uncertainty (e.g., Hollaway et al., 2018), all the evaluated models were rejected. Beven (2019) proposed a method to define LoAs based on variability of runoff ratios of rainfall-runoff events. This method accounts for both the precipitation and streamflow measurement uncertainties, but the method is applicable to flashy watersheds only and cannot account for timing errors without further assumption (Beven et al., 2022b).

Gupta et al. (2023a) proposed a decision tree (DT) based method to define LoAs. This method accounts for the effects of both streamflow and precipitation uncertainty. One of the advantages of the DT method is that it can use data from donor watersheds (watersheds other than the watershed where the model is to be evaluated) to define LoAs. Hence, DTs can be used to define LoAs for both gauged and ungauged catchments. Gupta et al. (2023a) derived DT-based LoAs for four subbasins located within St. Joseph River Watershed (SJRW). This study builds on Gupta et al. (2023a) by applying these LoAs to evaluate a hydrological model.

One interesting application of DT-based LoA is that it can be used to evaluate a hydrological model even at ungauged locations. Prediction at ungauged basins (PUB) is one of the most important and challenging problems in hydrological science (Hrachowitz et al., 2013). To constrain the simulations in an ungauged basin, information is transferred from similar donor watersheds to the parent watershed by parameter regionalization or streamflow signature regionalization (Razavi and Coulibaly, 2013). Essentially, rainfall-runoff process in the ungauged watershed is inferred using the data from other similar gauged catchments. But the regionalization process has significant additional uncertainty which is also difficult to quantify (Wagener and Montanari, 2011). DT-based LoAs can be useful for addressing the PUB challenge by providing a simple metric to evaluate models at ungauged locations while accounting for data uncertainties. Also, DT-LoAs allow model evaluation at each time-step as opposed to signature-based constraints which are unaffected by the streamflow timing information. Therefore, this study also explores the suitability of ML-based LoAs in evaluating models at ungauged locations.

If the uncertainties in data are large, several models might be consistent with the observed streamflow time series, but many of these models may not necessarily represent catchment dynamics satisfactorily (Hrachowitz et al., 2014; Wagener et al., 2021). To address this problem, the use of hydrological signatures has been proposed (Gupta et al., 2008; Euser et al., 2013; Hrachowitz et al., 2014; Fenicia et al., 2018; Kavetski et al., 2018). According to this methodology, a model can only be accepted as behavioral if it reproduces the observed signatures well, along with observed sequences of streamflow. An example of such a signature-based constraint is the long-term runoff coefficient (LRC) of a watershed (Kiraz et al., 2023); the simulated LRC should equal the observed LRC within the margin of errors. It is believed that the effect of errors in precipitation and streamflow will be reduced over a long timescale because of cancellation of errors (e.g., Kavetski et al., 2018). These constraints can be referred to as soft constraints. In this study, the role of signature-based soft constraints in identifying behavioral models will also be explored. The use of soft constraints can be particularly beneficial in ungauged catchments (e.g., Dal Molin et al., 2023).

The main novelty of this paper lies in using DTs to evaluate a hydrological model in the LoA framework. This method accounts for both model input (e.g., precipitation) and streamflow uncertainty; other LoA-based methods (except the runoff-ratio method) neglect precipitation uncertainty. A significant advantage of DTs is that they can be used for regionalization of streamflow to evaluate a hydrological model at ungauged locations at a time-step level, while accounting for uncertainties. Gupta et al. (2023a) presented an empirical and theoretical analysis of the DTs for defining LoAs, but they did not test the method for a hydrological model. This study presents the modeling analysis using the LoAs thus defined. Also, some new results regarding the behavior of QRF defined LoAs are presented in this study. Further, a well-known spectral property of streamflow time series called long-term persistence (discussed below) has been used as streamflow-based signature – this signature can be applied at both gauged and ungauged locations. The long-term persistence of a time series translates to a Hurst exponent (H) of the time series between 0.5 and 1.0. To the best of authors’ knowledge, this paper provides the first attempt to test the utility of H as a signature for model evaluation at both gauged and ungauged locations (see Westerberg and McMillan, 2015; McMillan, 2021 for a review of signatures used in hydrology; also see Yadav et al., 2007; Shafii and Tolson, 2015). We note that other spectral properties such as the autocorrelation function etc. have been used in earlier studies to calibrate hydrological models (Winsemius et al., 2009; Castiglioni et al., 2010; De Vleeschouwer and Pauwels, 2013), but long-term persistence has not been used in this context. Typical hydrological modeling studies calibrate a model either in time domain or signature domain (e.g., Coxon et al., 2014). The ‘signature domain only’ calibration is done to avoid the biases introduced by systematic errors in hydrological data (Fenicia et al., 2018), but signatures may lose some of the information contained in streamflow time series especially the timing information. Thus, this study combines calibration in time domain and signature domain. It will be shows that using only LoAs over streamflows may result in acceptance of physically unrealistic simulations; signatures are used to identify these unrealistic simulations.

Hydrological model and data

Hydrological model

The Sacramento Soil Moisture Accounting (SAC-SMA; Burnash, 1995) model along with a snow model and a runoff routing model was used in this study. SAC-SMA has been used in several studies to simulate streamflow (Sorooshian et al., 1993; Vrugt et al., 2006; Kratzert et al., 2019). It is a conceptual model with several parameters requiring calibration. Two other models, a snow model and a runoff routing model, were used along with SAC-SMA to model snowmelt and quick runoff routing. The SAC-SMA model simulates infiltration, percolation, evapotranspiration, and surface runoff. The runoff routing model routes the runoff to a streamflow outlet. Evapotranspiration calculations were based on potential evapotranspiration calculated using the Hamon equation (Hamon, 1963). The snow model used was snow-17 (Anderson, 1976), and the routing model used was a unit hydrograph represented by a gamma distribution. The combined model has a total of 24 parameters that were varied within predefined ranges to simulate streamflow and other hydrological fluxes. Study area

Four subwatersheds in the St. Joseph River Watershed (SJRW) were used as test cases in this study (Fig. 1). SJRW drains a total area of approximately 3000 km2, overlapping the states of Michigan, Indiana, and Ohio in eastern USA. Climate in this watershed is characterized by hot summers and cold winters, with precipitation falling throughout the year. Snowfall is an important component of the hydrological cycle in the SJRW with the contribution of snowfall to total precipitation in nearby gauges ranging between 5–7 % – the ratio was computed using the mean snow to liquid ratio of 12:1 (Baxter et al., 2005). The major land use types are cropland and forest (Mallya et al., 2020). The four SJRW stations are identified by their USGS (United States Geological Survey) station numbers at the outlet where streamflows are measured. A list of these stations along with some of the characteristics of corresponding drainage areas are provided in Table 2. Notably, the average lag between precipitation and streamflow at USGS-04178000 was approximately 1.5 days, much larger than that of the downstream station USGS-04180500 (0.886 days). The drainage area of USGS-04178000 also had a larger BFI and is underlaid by the Michindoh aquifer, which may be responsible for the larger delay between precipitation and streamflow peaks in this watershed.

Hydrological data

Data from six NCDC (National Climate Data Center) precipitation gauges outside but near the SJRW were used to compute daily areal average precipitation using the Thiessen polygon method. Other meteorological data required for the SAC-SMA are average daily temperatures which were also available from the six NCDC stations. Mean daily streamflow data were available from the USGS website (USGS, 2016 accessed on Sep 02, 2018). Data from calendar years 2001–2016 were used in this study, with 2001–2010 data being used for identifying behavioral models and the remaining data being used for independent validation. For three of the gauges (04180500, 04180000, 04178000) the year 2001 was used as the warm-up period and the years 2002–2010 were used for model evaluation (or calibration). For the gauge 04179520, year 2002 was used as the warm-up period and the years 2003–2010 were used for model evaluation, because streamflow data for the year 2001 were not available for this station.

In addition, data from 431 watersheds located in Ohio River Basin (ORB) were used to develop a machine learning (ML) model for the ungauged scenario as discussed below. Some details of these watersheds can be found in Gupta et al., (2023a). Data from ORB were used to regionalize streamflow in the ungauged scenario and to augment the training data in the gauged scenario (explained below).

Computation of event runoff ratio

To obtain some insights into precipitation and streamflow measurement uncertainty, event runoff ratios were computed for events in the available data (Beven and Smith, 2015; Beven, 2019). The rainfall-runoff events were separated and a master recession curve (MRC), determined for each catchment, was appended to each event. Subsequently, event runoff ratios of events were computed as the ratio of total observed streamflow volume (after appending the MRC) to total observed precipitation volume. The computed event runoff ratio can be highly uncertain for the events for which snowmelt is important; therefore, these events were excluded from the analysis. Snow affected events were identified as the ones that had average minimum temperature below 0°Cor were preceded by an event with average minimum temperature below 0°C. The first condition removes the events with snowfall, and the second condition removes the events immediately after the snowfall event because melt from the preceding snowfall event may add to the runoff in the current event.

Model evaluation

Limits-of-Acceptability and likelihood function

The details of LoA construction for SJRW and ORB using DTs can be found in Gupta et al. (2023a). Briefly, a DT-based method called quantile random forest (QRF) was used to define LoA. QRF yields a distribution for the response variable for a given predictor vector. It divides the predictor space into several contiguous and non-overlapping sub-regions. This division is carried out using the calibration/training data; the algorithm for defining these sub-regions can be found in any ML textbook (e.g., Hastie et al., 2009). To determine the distribution of the response variable for any predictor vector, first the sub-region to which the predictor vector belongs is determined and then the training points falling in each sub-region are used to define the distribution. The process of division of predictor space into different sub-regions can be visualized as a tree (see Fig. 2 in Gupta et al., 2023a). Division of predictor space is carried out iteratively which can be visualized as growth of the tree into different nodes. The nodes obtained after the final iteration are referred to as leaf nodes. To avoid overfitting, QRF develops several trees by bootstrapping the training data.

In this study, QRF models were developed using the predictor variables listed in Table 3 and streamflow was the response variable. Therefore, QRF yielded a distribution of streamflow values for a given value of predictors at a timestep. This distribution represents the variability in recorded streamflow values for ‘similar’ hydrological conditions including the effects of errors in hydrological data. Note that the data in the relevant leaf nodes from different trees are collated together to define the streamflow distribution at a timestep. A similar argument to define LoAs was also made in Winsemius et al. (2009); these authors defined LoAs over a few streamflow signatures based on interannual variability of the signatures. Gupta et al. (2023a) claimed that LoAs defined using QRF can account for both precipitation and streamflow uncertainty because QRF groups similar predictor variables (trainign points that are close to each other in predictor space) into leaf nodes and the large difference between responses can be attributed to lack of relevant predictor variables, or inability of model to identify hydrologically similar timesteps, and/or errors in predictor variables (primarily precipitation and temperature in this study) and response variables (streamflow in this study). Note that some variability in leaf nodes would still exist even in the ideal case when all the relevant predictor variables were used for QRF development and the model inputs and observed output were error free.

When constructing LoAs using QRF, one has to decide the lower and upper percentiles of a response variable in a leaf node to be used as the lower and upper LoAs. When defining the distribution of streamflow for a timestep, some streamflow values might be repeated across multiple trees. These values will get higher weight during the computations of percentiles. In what follows, the results corresponding to 1st percentile and 99.5th percentiles will be discussed in detail. The choice of these percentiles is somewhat arbitrary. These have been chosen so that as many as possible behaviors of rainfall-runoff response of the watershed, as suggested by the calibration data, are captured. The 97.5th percentiles were also used to define upper LoAs; some of these results are presented in Supplementary Information (SI) and only sparingly discussed in the main text.

In this study, three QRF models were used to test the applicability of LoA concept in three different scenarios:

Gauged-single scenario (LoAGS): The QRF models were trained using data from only the watersheds where the LoAs were to be constructed. For example, to construct LoAs for the station 04180500, data from only this station were used. In this case, the meteorological data were used as inputs and streamflow as output.

Gauged-single scenario (LoAGS): The QRF models were trained using data from only the watersheds where the LoAs were to be constructed. For example, to construct LoAs for the station 04180500, data from only this station were used. In this case, the meteorological data were used as inputs and streamflow as output.

Gauged scenario (LoAG): The QRF models were trained using data from multiple watersheds including data from the station where LoA were to be constructed. In this study, data from 431 watersheds in Ohio River Basin (ORB) along with the data from the four SJRW stations were used to develop this model (see Gupta et al., 2023a for details).

Gauged scenario (LoAG): The QRF models were trained using data from multiple watersheds including data from the station where LoA were to be constructed. In this study, data from 431 watersheds in Ohio River Basin (ORB) along with the data from the four SJRW stations were used to develop this model (see Gupta et al., 2023a for details).

Ungauged scenario (LoAUG): The QRF model was trained using data from only the ORB watersheds; data from the four SJRW stations were not used to train the model. This scenario represents the ungauged case when data are not available at the station where a hydrological model is to be evaluated.

Ungauged scenario (LoAUG): The QRF model was trained using data from only the ORB watersheds; data from the four SJRW stations were not used to train the model. This scenario represents the ungauged case when data are not available at the station where a hydrological model is to be evaluated.

The gauge-single scenario represents the case where we focus on data from a single watershed to develop a hydrological model. The gauged scenario represents the case where data from several watershed are available and are used here to test the utility of such a dataset in terms of model validation in a particular watershed – this has become a popular practice in ML application for streamflow prediction (e.g., Kratzert et al., 2019, 2024). To keep the manuscript concise, the results of gauged scenario are discussed in the SI only (Text S3; Figs. S9–S14). The ungauged scenario is used to test the usefulness of data available across multiple nearby watersheds in terms of model evaluation in a particular watershed where streamflow data are not available. The LoAs were constructed using data from the calibration period (2001–2010) and the remaining data (2011–2016) were kept as an independent validation period.

In the ungauged scenario, the QRF model was trained using data from the 431 ORB watersheds and data from the SJRW stations were not used. Therefore, we expect that the transfer of information from gauged to ungauged location would incur additional uncertainty. Gupta et al. (2023a) tested the developed QRF models in terms of their ability to simulate observed streamflow. The tests were carried out in the ungauged scenario as it is a more stringent test. The NSEs between observed and simulated streamflows obtained for the stations 04180500, 04180000, 04179520, and 04178000 were 0.57, 0.63, 0.60, and 0.36, respectively (See Gupta et al., 2023a for more details). These NSEs seem good enough to define LoAs except for the station 04178000 (Gupta et al., 2023a). Despite the low NSE of 0.36, LoAs developed for station 04178000 were nevertheless quite useful. Gupta et al. (2023a) further showed that LoAs constructed for these four stations accounted for the effects of streamflow and precipitation measurement uncertainty. The effect of aleatoric streamflow uncertainty was approximated using the probabilistic rating curve analysis. Effects of potential epistemic uncertainties in peak streamflow values were also tested: it was shown that the LoAs obtained would envelop the true peak streamflow values even if the observed values underestimated the true values by up to 100 %. The typical errors in peak streamflow have been reported to be 20–40 % (Di Baldassarre and Montanari, 2009). The uncertainty in precipitation was estimated using the Monte-Carlo sampling of the rain-gauges without replacement. Further, the LoAs obtained using the QRF method were compared against those obtained by using the runoff-ratio method. Some other properties of the LoAs obtained using the QRF method are discussed below.

Streamflow time series were simulated by the hydrologic model using a total of 106 parameter sets sampled uniformly from the parameter space (Table 1). While this is a small sample given the large number of free parameters, it is still useful to achieve the objectives of this study. A parameter set was considered behavioral if it satisfied the following four criteria:

At least (1−α)100% of the simulated streamflows are enveloped by the LoA.

At least (1−α)100% of the simulated streamflows are enveloped by the LoA.

At least (1−α)100% of the simulated rising limb flows are enveloped by the LoA.

At least (1−α)100% of the simulated rising limb flows are enveloped by the LoA.

At least (1−α)100% of the simulated recession flow are enveloped by the LoA.

At least (1−α)100% of the simulated recession flow are enveloped by the LoA.

At least (1−α)100% of the simulated peak streamflow values that were greater than 90 percentile streamflow value, are enveloped by the LoA.

At least (1−α)100% of the simulated peak streamflow values that were greater than 90 percentile streamflow value, are enveloped by the LoA.

These criteria were used to ensure that all parts of the hydrograph are well simulated by a behavioral parameter set. Otherwise, it is possible that a parameter set simulates the high streamflow within LoAs but does not simulate the low flows well at several timesteps. These criteria could be varied depending upon the intended application of the model (Beven and Lane, 2022). Two values of α were used: α=0.05 (5 % outliers) and α=0.00 (no outliers). The α=0.05 is used to allow for outliers since LoAs are defined using only 10 years of data, leaving room to accommodate future surprises. When α=0.00, a parameter set was considered behavioral only if it simulated streamflow within LoA at all the time steps (no outliers). Note that the choice of outliers allowed for identifying models and the percentiles range used to define LoAs are two independent choices. The LoAs are defined using the observed data while outliers refer to the timesteps at which a simulation is allowed to violate the LoAs.

The likelihoods were computed using the calibration period and the same likelihoods were used for the validation period. However, observations that are missed by the predictive uncertainty band would indicate either model structural errors and/or large epistemic errors in model inputs and/or observed outputs.

These constraints include the autocorrelation function (ACF) of the streamflow time-series, the Hurst exponent (H) of the streamflow time series, the baseflow index (BFI), the flow duration curve (FDC), and the long-term runoff coefficient (LRC). For a model to be accepted as behavioral by the ACF constraint, the observed and simulated ACF for the lags 1–100 days should pass the nonparametric Wilcoxon paired rank sum test at 5 % significance level. For a model to be acceptable as behavioral by the FDC constraint, the observed and simulated streamflow time series should pass the nonparametric paired Kolmogorov-Smirnov D test at 5 % significance level. For a model to be accepted as behavioral by the LRC constraint, the simulated LRCs should be between 0.6LRCobs and 1.4LRCobs, where LRCobs denotes observed LRC. Signature values may also be affected by data uncertainty (Westerberg and McMillan, 2015); therefore, the acceptance criteria on these signatures were set to wide margins to avoid false negative errors (rejecting a good model). For example, Westerberg and McMillan (2015) reported ≈ 20% uncertainty in LRC with raingauge density of 1135km−2 for the 135km2 Brue catchment, accounting for both precipitation and streamflow uncertainties.

To compute observed BFI, first baseflow was estimated using the method proposed by Collischonn and Fan (2013) and then, BFI was computed as the ratio of total baseflow and total streamflow. Simulated baseflow was obtained directly from the SAC-SMA as one of the outputs. In this study, the method of Collischonn and Fan (2013) was used to compute a range of BFI values instead of just one BFI value (Appendix A). Basically, an Eckhardt filter (Eckhardt, 2005) was applied iteratively where each iteration produced a baseflow time-series. One of the parameters required by the filter is BFImax which is an uncertain parameter; in each iteration BFImax was fixed to the BFI value computed in the previous iteration. The iterative procedure was stopped when the BFI value converged. According to the BFI constraint, a model was accepted as behavioral only if it simulated BFI within this range (see Table 2 for the range of BFI values used for different test watersheds). Again, very relaxed limits are applied on the simulated BFI because of uncertainties involved in computing BFI from the observed streamflow data.

Periodogram of a time series is very noisy irrespective of the amount of data available to estimate it (Priestley, 1982); therefore, a piecewise linear curve was fitted to the estimated periodogram (Kim et al., 2016). The streamflow periodogram can be approximated by |ω|1−2H as ω approaches zero (Beran, 1994), where H denotes the Hurst exponent and ω denotes the frequency in radians/day. Thus, H was estimated by

There are several methods to estimate H (see Montanari et al., 1999) but these methods yield significantly different value of H. A good method to estimate value of H is to fit a stochastic FARIMA model to streamflow time series (Montanari et al., 1997; Gupta et al., 2023b); but this method is computationally infeasible for this study as it takes significant computational resources for just one streamflow time series, making it practically impossible to implement for 106 simulated time series. Therefore, Equation (4) was adopted in this study. It is known that the H value for a typical streamflow time series lies between 0.5 and 1 (Montanari et al., 1997; Mudelsee, 2007) because of long-range memory. Indeed, Gupta et al. (2023b) fitted FARIMA models to streamflow time series from more than 500 watersheds across the USA; all of the models were well fitted to the time series with value between 0.5 and 1. Therefore, H value of a simulated streamflow time series should fall between 0.5 and 1 for a model to be accepted as behavioral according to this constraint. A relaxed limit has been used on H because estimating H values requires large amount of data that are not typically available and to avoid rejecting good models. It will be shown that even this relaxed limit on H can be helpful in identifying non-behavioral simulations. Since the streamflow time series were not de-seasonalized before computing the H values, the computed H values will also reflect the effect of streamflow seasonality.

The periodogram of a stationary stochastic time series is the sample estimate of power spectral density which, in turn, is the square of the absolute values of the Fourier coefficients of the corresponding autocorrelation function (Priestley, 1982). Therefore, signature ACF and H used in this study are closely related to each other. However, the ACF signature cannot be used in the ungauged scenario. But the Hurst exponent can be used in both gauged and ungauged scenarios, since we constrain H value of simulated streamflow between 0.5 and 1.0, irrespective of the H value of the observed streamflow series. Similarly, none of the other signatures (FDC, BFI, and LRC) can be used in the ungauged scenario unless the value of these signatures is estimated using the data from donor watersheds which themselves have significant uncertainties associated with them (e.g., Dal Molin et al., 2023). Nevertheless, the simulations with extremely high or low values of BFI may still be rejected as non-behavioral in ungauged basins. In this study, simulations with BFI values of less than 0.10 or greater than 0.90 were considered non-behavioral in the ungauged scenario.

Results

Limits-of-acceptability (LoAs)

Fig. 2 shows the LoAs obtained by the QRF method in the gauged-single and ungauged scenarios along with observed streamflow and precipitation. Here, 99.5th percentiles were used to define upper LoAs. The LoAs obtained by using 97.5th percentile values are shown in Fig. S1 (SI). The two LoAs envelop the observations at most of the time-steps. This is also evident from Table 5 which lists the fractions of observations enveloped by the LoAs. The LoAs were the widest in the ungauged scenario which is desirable since we want to include as many rainfall-runoff behaviors as possible and we expect larger uncertainty in model simulations at ungauged basins. In the gauged-single scenario, the lower LoAs were close to zero at all the timesteps. Models consistently underpredicting observed streamflow are less likely to be rejected even if they are bad simulators of a watershed’s hydrological processes, thus needing to penalize underpredictions more heavily in the likelihood function (Eq. (2)). Similarly, the LoAs allowed large overpredictions of the low flows.

A wide range of runoff ratios were observed for a given precipitation volume and initial event streamflow. Two events had runoff ratios significantly greater than 1; which are disinformative for model evaluation. The other disinformative event occurred in summer. These results suggest large uncertainties in the model input precipitation and/or streamflow data.

This Figure, along with Fig. 3, suggests that the low value of lower LoAs at peaks is due to errors in the model input precipitation and/or observed streamflow, due to skewness of the streamflow data toward the low values, and due to QRF model grouping hydrologically dissimilar timesteps in the leaf nodes.

In summary, these results suggest large errors in streamflow measurements and the interpolated areal average precipitation, neglect of spatial variability of precipitation, skewness of the streamflow data toward low values, and identification of hydrologically dissimilar timesteps is the reason for wide LoAs at the extreme flow (both high and low flow) timesteps. Some suggestions to improve upon the QRF algorithm to obtain tighter LoAs have been made later in the paper.

In early GLUE applications and even in some recent studies, NSE has been used as the informal likelihood measure. But NSE may be significantly impacted by a few critical timesteps (e.g., Clark et al., 2021) making it unfit for model evaluation, especially if the critical timesteps are impacted by the uncertainties. Fig. 8 shows the scatter plot of likelihood values against NSEs for behavioral models when 5 % outliers were allowed, to understand the effect of errors in data on NSE. The models with low NSEs were always assigned low likelihood values. Models with high NSEs were assigned a range of likelihood values from the smallest to the largest. Thus, behavioral models with low NSE values will have very small contributions to predictive uncertainty computation. Importantly, several models with high NSE values will also have small contributions to predictive uncertainties because many of these models violated LoAs at several timesteps (but at less than 5 % timesteps) and these timesteps were penalized heavily during the likelihood computation. Fig. 13 shows characteristics of behavioral models obtained after applying different constraints: LoA with 5 % outliers (blue dots), LoA with no outliers (orange), and LoA with 5 % outliers and signature constraints (green dots). Note that only BFI and LRC were used to determine fitness-for-purpose among the indices shown in Fig. 13; other indices were used only to get better insights into the modeling. Most of the behavioral models obtained without allowing any outliers, were physically unrealistic with BFIs close to 1 and LRCs smaller than 0.25 (Fig. 13a). Because these models had large evapotranspiration (indicated by small LRC) and simulated baseflow as the only source of water in river, these models had very large relative peak bias (Fig. 13b). This explains why the peaks were missed when no outliers were allowed (Fig. 10). Moreover, all the models that did not violate the LoAs at any timestep had positive recession and rising limb biases (Fig. 13c), implying that these models underpredicted the observed streamflows at most of the timesteps which is again because of the overestimation of ET by these models. Therefore, the models that satisfied the wide LoA without any outliers can be rejected as unfit-for-purpose.

Among the behavioral models that were physically realistic according to the signature constraints, almost all violated the LoAs at some calibration period timesteps, implying that these models would also be unfit-for-purpose if the limits were applied strictly. But allowing for outliers resulted in some simulations that could be used for predictions. Among these models, several had large peak timing errors and large peak magnitude errors (Fig. 13b). The large timing errors of these models was due to slow routing of quick runoff, suggesting that these models are not fit-for-purpose; these models were accepted as behavioral because of the wide LoAs and allowance of outliers. Several models selected based on LoA with 5 % outliers had a wide range of rising and recession limb biases. NSE values were used to assess the quality of fit of the simulated streamflow time series to the observed streamflow time series (Fig. 13d). The NSE values ranged from < 0 to ≈ 0.70. A wide range of validation NSEs were obtained for a given calibration NSE. Many models had a low calibration NSE but a higher validation NSE. In summary, none of the evaluated models simulated all aspects of streamflow time series satisfactorily, even given the wide LoAs defined by the QRF. While some outliers must be allowed since the LoAs are based on the limited calibration period data, we expect that the main difficulty should be in defining LoAs for the extremely low and high flows. Specifically, the lower LoAs during the low flows and the upper LoAs during the high flows may be too close to observations, potentially resulting in violation of these LoAs even by a good model. On the other hand, QRF allowed large underpredictions of the high flows and large overpredictions of the low flows: The model violating these LoAs indicate model structural deficiency. Therefore, the behavioral models obtained by allowing 5 % outliers were further constrained where a model was retained as behavioral only if it did not violate the upper LoAs during the bottom 20 % flows, lower LoAs during the top 10 % flows, and did not violate the LoAs during other timesteps. This stricter constraint reduced the number of behavioral models from 48,345 to 856 for the station USGS-04180500; most of these 856 models were again physically unrealistic (very high BFI and very high ET). Therefore, all the 1-million simulations evaluated in this study can be deemed unfitfor-purpose.

Qualitative analysis of some models to understand the impact of uncertainty

To further understand the effects of various uncertainties on model simulations, Fig. 14 shows the streamflow time series simulated by three models (parameter sets of the SAC-SMA). These models had very different physical characteristics and fits to the observed time series. The model-1 with smallest calibration NSE (=0.14) also had the smallest peak bias. The low NSE of this model resulted from the model significantly overestimating streamflow during summer. Further the model-1 had large LRC of 0.49 indicating underestimation of evapotranspiration and/or overestimation of areal average precipitation in summer. The models 2 and 3 had calibration NSE of 0.54 and 0.69 with peak biases of 0.44 and 0.43, respectively. These two models had different BFIs: 0.35 and 1.00, respectively. Fig. 14 illustrates how uncertainties in model input precipitation introduces errors in simulated streamflow in a complex manner which can be difficult to separate from model structural errors. This also suggests that increasing the number of simulations beyond the 1-million included in the analysis might not result in finding more acceptable and physically realistic simulations.

Discussion

Fig. 14 illustrates that a model may not be able to simulate some events because of uncertainties in data. The errors in streamflow simulation may be due to errors in areal average precipitation time series, uncertainty in true precipitation intensity due to discretization of input precipitation to daily scale which can result in both peak timing and magnitude errors, neglect of spatial variability of precipitation, and errors in extremely low and high streamflow estimation from the gauge height data. Timing error of 1-day may also occur for an event due to daily discretization if the most of the event precipitation occurred later in the day. Therefore, a model evaluation method must consider these uncertainties. This paper presented an application of QRF-based LoA in evaluating the hydrological model SAC-SMA, where the LoA were defined for streamflow time series. This allowed the evaluation of models (i.e., parameter sets of the model structure) individually for each time step. A total of 106 parameters sets were sampled from the prior parameter ranges. This is a small set given that the total number of free parameters was 24. However, useful insights could be obtained using this set. Further, five streamflow-based signatures were used along with LoA to reject the models that could simulate streamflow within the LoA but still could not reproduce specific aspects of streamflow dynamics.

Two of the subjective choices associated with the QRF-based LoA method are: (1) What quantiles should be used to define the lower and upper LoA bounds, and (2) how many and how should the outliers be allowed. Allowance for outliers is required because of the finite amount of data used to define LoAs and the dominance of epistemic uncertainties in hydrological data (Beven and Lane, 2019; Gupta et al., 2023a) along with aleatoric uncertainties (de Oliveira and Vrugt, 2022). It is possible that a model simulates low and medium flows well but provides consistently poor simulations of a few high flows observed in the calibration period. Thus, a poor model might be accepted if allowance for outliers is not made carefully. Similarly, a good model might be rejected because it violates the LoAs at a few time steps if no outliers are allowed. We selected outliers in a manner that a model is accepted as behavioral only if it simulates all parts of the streamflow time series (rising limb, recession limb, and peaks) consistent with the observations. There can be other ways of allowing for outliers, depending on the purpose of the modeling exercise and available information about the uncertainties in data. For example, Beven et al. (2022a, b) accepted some models that satisfied LoAs at largest observed peak timesteps while allowing for outliers at other timesteps.

The behavioral models produced calibration and validation NSE values ranging from less than 0 to ≈ 0.70 (Figs. 13 and 15). The signature-based constraints in addition to LoA significantly reduced the number of behavioral models. Among the five signatures-based constraints used in this study, the baseflow index (BFI) and the Hurst exponent (H) were the most useful for the four watersheds used in this study, but the importance of a signature depends upon the watershed being considered (Coxon et al., 2014). The important point here is that several simulations accepted as behavioral by the LoA constraint alone produced unrealistic streamflow dynamics. In fact, the majority of the models that did not violate LoAs at any calibration timestep were physically unrealistic. One interpretation is that the uncertainties in hydrological data were so large that several models with poor NSE values could not be rejected. On the other hand, many models with high NSE values were rejected (not shown), implying that these models simulated streamflows outside the wide LoAs more than 5 % of the time. Similarly, Beven et al. (2022a, b) rejected all the parameter sets of TOPMODEL after applying all the constraints, even though the NSE values of several rejected models were strong. Beven et al. (2022a, b) defined LoA using the runoff ratio method that accounts for both precipitation and streamflow uncertainties and also allowed for timing errors. Thus, these results suggest a departure from using widely used GoF measures alone as model evaluation criterion, in line with previous studies (e.g., Beven, 2019; McMillan, 2021). Gupta et al. (2008) also suggested a similar shift in model evaluation practice, specifically emphasizing the role of multiple hydrological signatures in model evaluation.

Another interpretation is that the QRF defined LoAs were too wide beyond the uncertainties in data and did not allow a rigorous test for model evaluation where, following Kirchner et al. (1996), a rigorous test should have small false positive error. This study provides insights into the LoAs defined by the QRF model. The QRF defined LoAs were wide at the high and low flow timesteps because of (1) uncertainty in model input and observed output data (Figs. 3–6), (2) grouping of hydrologically dissimilar timesteps in the leaf node, and (3) fewer high and low flow timesteps (Figs. 4–6). Note that the different trees give different importance to a predictor variable while grouping the training points in the leaf nodes. This is why QRF identifies some timesteps as ‘similar’ that are hydrologically dissimilar. The wide LoAs are one of the reasons why several physically unrealistic models were accepted as behavioral. This suggests that a better definition of LoAs is required for a more rigorous test.

In a future study, different ways of identifying similar timesteps using ML will be evaluated. For example, the node split condition in the decision tree may be modified so that the samples with similarity in terms of all the predictors variables are more likely to be grouped together in a leaf node. Streamflows at the previous timesteps could be used as predictor variables to better identify the timesteps with similar antecedent conditions. Other machine learning algorithms such as Long Short-Term Memory Network, which has been shown to yield state-of-the-art performance in streamflow simulations (Kratzert et al., 2019), may be used to better identify hydrologically ‘similar’ timesteps.

These results also illustrate the inadequacy of NSE in terms of evaluating fitness-for-purpose of a model. Several authors (e.g., Gupta et al., 2009; Knoben et al., 2019; Clark et al., 2021) have shown the problems with using NSE as an evaluation metric. The NSE produced by a model can be poor because of errors in model input and observed output data or because of an inadequate model structure. Note that the effects of model inputs may affect the model simulations for several timesteps. Fig. 10 suggests that a reason for the low NSEs of behavioral models was consistent underestimation of high flows, which could have been due to underprediction of areal average precipitation for these events (Bárdossy et al., 2022; Bárdossy and Anwar, 2023) given that only six gauges were available for the watersheds considered in this study (Fig. 1). Further, Fig. 14 suggests the overestimation of the areal average precipitation during some low flow events during summer months (resulting in overestimation of streamflow and lower NSE). Therefore, we conclude that NSE is not a suitable metric to decide fitness-for-purpose.

Despite the wide LoAs, most of the models were still rejected and some outliers had to be allowed to retain physically realistic models. Fig. 9 showed that the outliers had to be allowed mainly for the low flows, implying that the low flows were critical in identifying behavioral and non-behavioral models. Since the QRF LoAs already allowed large overpredictions of the low flows, one interpretation of these results is that the SAC-SMA has structural deficiency in term of simulating low flows. Other interpretation is that the large overpredictions occur because of precipitation overestimation during the preceding events, affecting the simulated antecedent conditions. Given the current lack of knowledge of the errors in model inputs, it is still difficult to decide between the two interpretations. However, these large overpredictions do suggest important structural errors in the SAC-SMA. Large BFI of many behavioral models suggest that either the ET or the baseflow component of the models needs revision. The distribution of water between different storage elements might also be problematic which might also affect ET.

Beven and Lane (2022) proposed eight general principles for defining fitness-for-purpose. One of the principles was that the model should not contradict the secure evidence about the hydrological functioning of the watershed. Following this principle, we would want a model to produce all the possible hydrological signatures for physical consistency, but this may not be possible because hydrological models are necessarily simplified representations of hydrological processes. Therefore, a possible way to apply this principle is to rank the different constraints according to their relevance to the purpose of modeling. Subsequently, the different constraints can be applied progressively, thus, creating a ranking of behavioral simulations – the models identified as behavioral after all the constraints will be assigned the highest rank. The lower rank models can be used progressively to create predictive uncertainty bound that satisfy some decision criteria such as the coverage probability and width of uncertainty bound. Shafii et al. (2015) demonstrated a similar Pareto rank-based approach to identify behavioral simulations, but they did not account for uncertainties in data (the fifth principle in Beven and Lane, 2022).

One of the benefits of the DT-based LoAs is that they can be constructed even at ungauged locations by using data from donor watersheds – so called regionalization. Further, LoAs so constructed provide natural way of quantifying uncertainty in predictions. In this scenario, wider LoAs were obtained and, consequently, additional models were accepted as behavioral. The wider LoAs are desirable since we expect that the regionalization would incur additional uncertainty in modeling. Using the streamflow-based signatures H and BFI significantly reduced the number of behavioral models, but still resulted in some behavioral models that could be used to make predictions (Fig. 15). The LoAs on signatures were defined loosely in this study but tighter limits could be defined using regionalization methods (e.g., Yadav et al., 2007).

Summary and concluding remarks

This study is the first attempt to use ML-constructed LoAs to evaluate the conceptual hydrological model SAC-SMA. Of note, the methodology proposed in this paper can be applied at both gauged (referred to as gauged-single) and ungauged locations. Therefore, LoAs were constructed for both the gauged and the ungauged scenarios in this study. LoAs were wider for the ungauged scenario as expected since transferring information from donor watersheds incurs additional uncertainty and, typically, the donor watersheds do not contain enough information to compensate for the absence of information from the parent watershed (Gupta, 2024). However, very wide LoAs were obtained even in the gauged-single scenario. It is suggested that this is partly due to large uncertainty in precipitation interpolation and streamflow uncertainty. Another reason is that QRF grouped several hydrologically dissimilar timesteps in the leaf nodes, suggesting the need to further explore the ML algorithms to define LoAs. This paper has made some suggestions to better define LoAs using ML.

Allowing violations of LoAs at 5 % of the calibration timesteps yielded several models as behavioral, but many of these models could not simulate specific hydrograph features adequately. Using streamflow-based signatures can help with this limitation. The Hurst exponent (H) was used as one of the signatures with very relaxed acceptability criterion, but it still was useful in constraining behavioral parameter space. Among other signatures, the BFI (applied at both gauged and ungauged locations) also showed significant discriminating power. Since none of the tested models could simulate all aspects of streamflow hydrograph adequately even given the wide LoAs, all the 1-million models tested in this study were rejected as unfit-for-purpose.

The errors in the model input precipitation affects the streamflow residuals in a complex manner that are difficult to model using a formal probabilistic approach. For example, peak timing errors in some the events were due to dry antecedent condition which, in turn, was due to underestimation of the pre-event precipitation. Similarly, precipitation overestimation in an event may affect the low flow simulations for several subsequent days. The LoA approach attempts to define the variability in response variable due to the effects of errors in data, before model simulations. This approach also allows to identify the timesteps where a parameter set and also the model structure is failing, thus, proving hints for model improvement. For example, low flow timesteps were critical for separating behavioral models from non-behavioral models in this study. Beven and Lane (2022) suggest that a model should also be hydrologically consistent to qualify as fit-for-purpose, i.e., the model should give ‘right answer for right reasons’ (Kirchner, 2006). Since all the models will eventually be rejected as more constraints are applied, we suggest that different constraints should be ranked according to their relevance to the purpose of modeling where the models satisfying all the constraints can be given the highest rank (e.g., Beven et al., 2022b).
