Response to “Evaluating Multimodal Large Language Models in Neuroradiology: Methodological Considerations”
Authors: Ji Su Ko, Pae Sun Suh, Chong Hyun Suh
Published: 2026

We sincerely thank Chen and Zhang [1] for their thoughtful and constructive comments on our study [2]. We appreciate the positive reception of our work and welcome the opportunity to address the methodological considerations they have raised.

REGARDING DATA LEAKAGE

We agree with the reviewers that data leakage cannot be entirely excluded, as acknowledged in our original manuscript. As noted, the source figures and legends were drawn from published educational material accessible online prior to model testing [345]. Importantly, if partial exposure to our test cases occurred during model training, one would expect inflated rather than diminished model performance [6]. The relatively low acceptability rate we observed, with fewer than one-third of the large language models (LLMs) responses deemed acceptable, suggests that any data leakage, if present, did not meaningfully benefit the models and may imply that their true limitations are even more substantial than demonstrated. Additionally, although models can recognize radiologic images by recombining learned elements, the diagnostic reasoning is fundamentally different. Therefore, our study focused on evaluating the diagnostic reasoning of LLMs rather than simple diagnostic accuracy. Although complete data independence is difficult to guarantee in studies using published materials, we believe our approach represents a practical compromise for evaluating LLMs in the absence of fully controlled datasets. We agree with the suggestion that future studies should prioritize prospectively assembled institution-specific datasets or post-cutoff cases with documented access control, which would provide stronger guarantees of benchmark integrity.

REGARDING THE ANALYSIS OF CLINICAL HISTORY

We appreciate these important methodological observations. As noted by the authors, cases without a clinical history in our study were not experimentally derived by withholding the history from all cases; rather, they represented cases in which no usable clinical information remained after removing imaging findings and diagnoses from the figure legends. We acknowledge that this design may introduce systematic differences in case characteristics, such as intrinsic difficulty or reliance on imaging alone, between the two subgroups, thereby limiting direct comparability between the two groups. A paired within-case design, in which identical cases are presented both with and without clinical history, would more rigorously isolate the contribution of textual input to diagnostic performance; we agree that this represents a methodologically stronger approach for future research [7].

Nevertheless, we believe that the substantial and consistent decline in diagnostic performance observed across all models in patients without a clinical history provides meaningful evidence that current multimodal LLMs rely heavily on textual information. For instance, the top-3 accuracy dropped from 61.0% to 23.4% for GPT-4V (T1) and from 53.8% to 15.3% for Claude 3.5 (T1) when clinical history was absent. The magnitude and consistency of this decline across all evaluated models support the robustness of this observation, even considering the study design limitations noted above.

REGARDING ASYNCHRONOUS MODEL EVALUATION

Testing different models across non-overlapping time windows is a meaningful limitation, particularly given the rapid pace of development of proprietary multimodal systems [8]. As noted in the limitations section, repeatability was not assessed a priori and could not be examined retrospectively because most proprietary models underwent updates during the study period. Although we attempted to use a fixed version for each experiment, the limited availability of long-term legacy versions for certain proprietary models made it difficult to synchronize the evaluation across all models. We agree that future benchmarking studies should aim for synchronized evaluation periods and explicitly report run-to-run variability to improve reproducibility and, where technically feasible, enable fairer cross-model comparisons.

REGARDING RATIONALE ANALYSIS LIMITED TO GPT-4V (T1)

The rationale evaluation was performed using GPT-4V (T1) because it achieved the highest top-3 accuracy among all models tested; this served as the primary metric in our study, given the clinical importance of generating a comprehensive differential diagnosis list. As noted in the literature, only a limited number of studies have systematically examined LLMs’ response rationales, largely because such evaluation requires time-consuming and labor-intensive human assessment [8]. Owing to this practical constraint, we focused our rationale analysis on the single best-performing model as a representative case. However, we recognize the validity of the letter authors’ point that GPT-4o achieved the highest top-1 accuracy, a metric that may have greater clinical relevance as it reflects the model’s confidence in its leading diagnosis. Although we did not directly compare GPT-4V and GPT-4o in our study, available evidence suggests that GPT-4o may exhibit a similar pattern of overreliance on textual input [9]. Nevertheless, a rigorous evaluation of the diagnostic rationales of the latest reasoning models with respect to lesion localization, imaging findings description, and hallucinations would provide more definitive evidence and remain an important direction for future research. Furthermore, although our study focused on establishing a benchmark against subspecialty-level expertise, we agree that evaluating readers across a broader range of experience levels would enrich future studies and provide a more balanced benchmark for general-purpose multimodal models in clinical settings [1011].

We appreciate Chen and Zhang’s thoughtful engagement with our work. Their comments highlight important directions for methodological refinement, specifically in isolating textual influences and evaluating emerging reasoning models. We hope that this discussion contributes to a more rigorous and clinically meaningful evaluation of multimodal AI systems in radiology.
