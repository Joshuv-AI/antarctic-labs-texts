Rest assured: Promoting sleep health in undergraduate education
Authors: Haley Nowlin, Emily Badin, Jess P. Shatkin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep deprivation among young adults is a pervasive problem driven by high levels of stress, excessive screen time, disruptive school and living environments, irregular sleep habits, high academic demands, early school start times, and low rates of physical activity. Difficulties with emotion regulation, high rates of anxiety and depression, and poor academic performance are only a few of the struggles faced by sleep deprived young adults. Although sleep apps and wearables are increasingly popular, knowledge of positive sleep health without the proper tools to motivate and instill behavior change can contribute to anxiety and negative cognitions about sleep, which only further fuel the problem.MethodsIn this report we describe a series of undergraduate university curricula, housed within a unique undergraduate department, designed to enhance not only knowledge of sleep, but also associated behavior changes that have been demonstrated to improve sleep, mood, and anxiety among university students.ResultsNumerous courses within the Child and Adolescent Mental Health Studies (CAMS) department at New York University address sleep directly, teaching students about the science of sleep and how they can improve their own sleep to enhance overall wellbeing. Our work to date demonstrates that students find these courses desirable and impactful.ConclusionCollege courses present a unique opportunity to improve the health and wellbeing of young adults by teaching about sleep health. Undergraduate campuses may represent an underutilized locale from which to address population health.

Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental pillar of both physical and mental wellbeing, yet young adults remain globally under-slept [1] [2]. Public health initiatives aimed at improving sleep practices have been implemented across a range of settings, including hospitals, primary care clinics, educational institutions, and popular media platforms, such as Instagram [[3], [4], [5]]. Despite widespread efforts [6], sleep deprivation remains a pervasive problem [7,8]. High stress, excessive screen time, disruptive school and living environments, irregular sleep habits, high academic demands, early school start times, and low rates of physical activity all contribute to poor sleep quality and duration — effects that are often amplified during emerging adulthood due to increased independence and developmental changes [7,9,10].

According to the National Youth Risk Behavior Survey [10,11], nearly two-thirds of emerging adults in the United States report insufficient sleep, and 48.2 % report severe sleep deprivation. College students exhibit significantly higher rates of sleep difficulties than the general population [12] with nearly twice the rate of Delayed Sleep Phase Disorder (11.5 %; [13]), more than twice the rate of sleep deprivation and poor sleep quality (60 %; [14,15]), and more than twice the rate of insomnia (18.5 %; [16]). Beginning in late adolescence and extending into early adulthood, melatonin levels typically peak later in the evening and the homeostatic drive to sleep declines, causing an increase in sleep onset latency in college-age individuals [17]. A longer sleep latency is a major indicator of poor sleep health in even the most typical young adult sleepers [18], placing college students at particularly high risk.

Young adults are often exposed to messaging about the importance of healthy sleep habits from both parents and social media [19,20]. Interest in sleep has contributed to the recent surge in popularity of various sleep tracking devices, with over 35 % of adults in the United States now using such devices, ranging from wearables, such as rings and smartwatches, to airables, such as smartphone applications or motion sensors (Celmer, 2023; [21]). The use of these airable sleep-support apps is similarly popular among young adults, with one large UK study reporting that 37 % of young adults use sleep apps [22] Information about sleep is increasingly found on the internet with over 3 million posts on the social media platform, TikTok, contain the hashtag “sleep,” highlighting a widespread public fascination with the topic and its relevance to everyday life. Unfortunately, many of these videos propose sleep improvement strategies that are not empirically supported, raising concerns about the potential dissemination of inaccurate or misleading information [23,24]. Pervasive sleep deprivation in young adults amidst the growing mass production and consumption of sleep-related content highlights a critical gap between sleep health education and the successful translation of that knowledge into sustained behavioral change.

Sleep-deprived young adults often experience negative physical, emotional, and cognitive effects, such as lower academic performance, poorer emotion regulation, and reduced overall wellbeing [25]. Despite awareness of these consequences, young adults struggle to reconcile messaging about the importance of sleep with a society that prioritizes “hustle” and productivity by promoting caffeine consumption, early school start times, and expectations to achieve in academics and extracurriculars, creating a tension between their understanding of sleep's value and the stress-inducing demands placed on them [26]; [27]. As a result, young adults have difficulty initiating and implementing sustainable behavior changes related to their sleep health. Additionally, knowledge of positive sleep health without the proper tools to motivate and instill behavioral change can contribute to anxiety and negative cognitions about sleep, which, in turn, can lead to poorer sleep quality and longer sleep onset latency [28,29]. Young adults are in a challenging position, in which many wish to improve their sleep but find numerous barriers preventing them [30]. Consequently, emerging adulthood represents a critical window for intervention to address sleep health and support new habits.

Young adulthood is characterized by a series of paradoxes. It is an exciting period of life marked by increasing autonomy, a growing capacity to adapt to change, and new academic, interpersonal, and emotional challenges coupled with elevated stress, increasing financial pressures, and a subsequent heightened vulnerability to mental health challenges. These conditions offer a unique opportunity to support the formation of new habits, making college an ideal time and setting for sleep interventions to take hold [31]. The university environment is particularly well-suited for public health intervention, offering a valuable platform to reach young adults on a widespread scale — right where they are, on college campuses. In 2023, 61 % of emerging adults in the U.S. enrolled in a college or university following their high school graduation; 74.6 % of those in a 4-year institution and 25.4 % in a 2-year institution. In Fall 2024, over 19.2 million students were enrolled in a degree-granting undergraduate program [32,33]. A college-based intervention does, inescapably and unfortunately, leave roughly 40 % of the population unaddressed, and those who do not attend college are perhaps at even greater risk for other exposures that may further negatively impact their sleep, such as shiftwork or caregiving responsibilities [34]. Still, an intervention that can address the public health crisis of inadequate sleep on 60 % of the population suggests a reasonable starting point.

Sleep health promotion interventions designed for young adult and collegiate populations have been found to be acceptable, feasible, and efficacious in academic settings [12]; [35]. In one study, college students who received a sleep intervention program that included objective measurement, self-assessment, and behavior change prompts showed a significant increase in their total sleep time and sleep regularity [36]. In another study, participants demonstrated a significant improvement in sleep behaviors related to sleep hygiene, sleep efficiency, sleep latency, and overall sleep quality [37]. Effective sleep health promotion programs typically incorporate two core elements – didactic education about sleep and experiential learning – enabling individuals to apply their knowledge and integrate sleep principles into their daily routines [[38], [39], [40]]. These experiential learning techniques are essential to bridge the gap between education and sustainable action. However, despite the availability of efficacious sleep intervention programs for young adults and the evidence exhibiting the efficacy of these interventions in academic environments, they are not widely employed in university settings.

This paper's aim is to present an empirically effective model of employing sleep interventions in academic settings by combining intervention with required course curricula. We begin by introducing the academic department where this model of sleep health promotion originated, showcasing how sleep health is integrated within the department, as well as research findings that demonstrate the efficacy of our approach. We then explore a specific student assignment that has been found to be widely enjoyable and highly impactful on students' sleep behaviors. We conclude by sharing insights into the benefits, efficacy, and feasibility of implementing sleep health promotion into course curricula on an undergraduate level. We are hopeful that other undergraduate institutions will find our approach useful in improving the health of their student body.

Undergraduate education

In the fall of 2006, the corresponding author of this paper founded a novel academic undergraduate department at New York University (NYU) to broaden student's understanding of child, adolescent, and young adult mental health and wellness through a wide range of structured, instructor-led courses that are open to all students. The Child and Adolescent Mental Health Studies (CAMS) department emanates from the Department of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry at the NYU Grossman School of Medicine and is housed within the College of Arts and Science at NYU. The overall focus of CAMS is to explore the biopsychosocial bases of mental illness, analyze the concepts of “typical” versus “abnormal” behavior and development, and investigate the many different contexts within which mental health, illness, and diagnoses exist [41]. Over the past two decades, the department has experienced significant growth, expanding to offer 55 unique courses covering topics as broad as the diagnosis and treatment of mental illness, cultural and media perspectives on mental health, parenting and divorce, as well as happiness and love. Courses vary in size from intimate seminars of 10–20 students to large lectures of over 400. Over 3000 students enroll in one or more of these courses every semester, and hundreds of students graduate with the CAMS minor each year. Undergraduates can take as many CAMS classes as they wish, but five courses are required to earn the minor degree on their transcript. Instructing nearly 7000 students annually, CAMS is widely popular among students spanning a broad range of interests, passions, and majors, a disproportionate number of whom enter the field of mental health post-graduation [41]; [42]. The self-selecting nature of enrolling for college courses may introduce a selection bias when evaluating the impact of CAMS courses upon student behaviors. Many of the courses offer insights and practical opportunities to study challenges commonly faced by young adults, including building effective relationships, developing resilience during stressful times, and understanding and improving sleep.

Sleep is explored at varying levels across courses in the CAMS department, from basic concepts in a handful of courses to an in-depth study of sleep and dreams in one seminal course. Within the CAMS department, assignments and term papers are due no later than 9 p.m., as we have found that making papers due at 12 a.m. or 9 a.m. (as is common among college courses) inadvertently encourages students to stay up late and get less sleep on the due dates. The primary courses that address sleep in considerable depth are titled While You Were Sleeping, Road to Resilience, and The Science of Happiness. These classes each combine direct instruction with hands-on experiential activities related to sleep, which have been shown to effectively promote behavior change in college students. Over the duration of two semesters, students enrolled in While You Were Sleeping were given the opportunity to participate in a quasi-experimental pilot study examining the impact of sleep education and associated experiential learning activities on their overall sleep health. Students enrolled in the class showed significant improvements in sleep hygiene, decreases in sleep latency, and improvements in their circadian sleep phase compared to controls; all of which were maintained at three-month follow-up [43] Perhaps more importantly, students also reported statistically less depression and anxiety at three-month follow-up. Integration of sleep health training into college curricula appears to be an effective strategy for promoting better sleep and improving the overall health of emerging adults.

Sleep education and self-monitoring of sleep behaviors

Education about sleep health can lead to effective behavior change when students are also given opportunities to practice and apply their learning. CAMS courses highlight the importance of sleep for both physical and mental health, examine its impact on academic performance, and teach the fundamentals of sleep science, including circadian rhythms, sleep hygiene, and the role of melatonin. Equally important, CAMS courses offer opportunities for the students to apply their sleep learning to themselves.

Across the three CAMS courses with formal sleep curricula, students are assigned the American Academy of Sleep Medicine [44] Sleep Diary to track personal data and monitor progress. Through formal assignments and informal check-ins, students are required to reflect on their sleep habits, and they obtain reinforcement for their efforts by being graded on assignments; receiving verbal praise by class instructors when reporting on their sleep (thus experiencing the satisfaction of positive habit change); and observing the tangible evidence of their improvements through ongoing progress monitoring. Students also closely examine their living environments, the amount of coursework required of them, the times of the day or night during which they do various activities (e.g., study, spend time with friends, exercise, etc.), their alcohol and marijuana intake, and their cell phone and social media usage.

In tandem with sleep education around how to improve sleep, progress monitoring is beneficial for increasing awareness of individual sleep-related problems to promote more precise intervention efforts. Students are better able to identify what they may need or want to change while simultaneously self-regulating and self-reinforcing their behavior as the intervention takes shape [45]. Progress monitoring in clinical settings has been shown to prevent treatment failure, positively enhance targeted outcomes, and increase the effectiveness of interventions, particularly when patients are experiencing common or mild problems rather than severe disorders [46,47]. Most college students are not suffering from clinical sleep disorders, but poor sleep quality, decreased sleep duration, and longer sleep latency are epidemic among university students [48]. Progress monitoring serves as a valuable tool to support sleep learning and inspire change for all students, including those who may not even recognize the existence of a problem before tracking their progress.

Each CAMS course approaches sleep education and progress monitoring through distinct methods tailored to its specific objectives and content. In The Science of Happiness, the instructors teach multiple lectures on sleep science and the importance of sleep. Simultaneously, students are assigned to complete one week of the AASM Sleep Diary along with a sleep, food, and mood journal. Students then write a personal reflection on their wellness habits. Over seven days, the nearly 500 students enrolled in this class each term track their sleep habits, food intake, and mood and then analyze the relationships among these key wellness domains. Students then discuss their experiences in smaller recitation groups where they learn more about sleep and gather tips for improving sleep quality. In recitation, students often express their increased confidence in their ability to change their wellness habits and identify specific areas for improvement, develop actionable strategies, and appreciate why these changes are important, which helps motivate them to follow through on their intentions.

In Road to Resilience, a seminar course, students spend two sessions learning about sleep and how it impacts their resilience and overall wellbeing. Before these sessions, students complete the AASM Sleep Diary for two weeks along with multiple sleep-related questionnaires, such as the Insomnia Severity Index, the Sleep Hygiene Index, the Epworth Sleepiness Scale, the Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire, and a survey of their caffeine use. These scales provide students with the opportunity to gather additional quantitative data about their sleep habits, which offers further clarity on how they can make improvements. Students then complete a Sleep Action Plan, which guides them in a structured review of their sleep habits and prompts them to consider specific sleep behavior interventions, such as using blackout curtains and setting consistent sleep/wake schedules. While the students in this course are not required to implement their Sleep Action Plan, they almost universally report that they make significant changes to their sleep behaviors after this assignment.

Finally, While You Were Sleeping provides students with the most comprehensive education and hands-on learning experiences related to sleep. The course is broken into five units: (1) Defining Sleep; (2) Dreams; (3) The Evolution of Sleep; (4) Sleep Regulation; and (5) Sleep Disorders and Treatment. Students obtain a richly detailed understanding of how sleep functions and its multifactorial importance. Each class session is supplemented with live, in-class polls where students are asked to report the amount of sleep they obtained the night before, answer material-centered questions for review, and share opinions on topics, such as the meanings of dreams and the impact of their school start time on their overall functioning. These polls support in-class engagement and learning and provide an opportunity for students to monitor their sleep habits throughout the semester. Students report that simply acknowledging the amount of sleep they receive over the past 24 h, as they do in the class polls, influences them to improve their sleep hygiene. In addition to these in-class methods, students complete various sleep-related assessments at two time points – once at the beginning of the semester to establish a baseline, and again at the end after the implementation of their Sleep Action Plan (akin to the action plan utilized in the Road to Resilience course) so that they may evaluate any improvements in their sleep. The subjective measures utilized here include the AASM Sleep Diary, RU-SATED, Epworth Sleepiness Scale, and the Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire, along with an assessment of their caffeine use. Students review their baseline sleep data to identify opportunities for intervention, which they then apply to their individualized Sleep Improvement Program (SIP). The SIP stands out as one of the most impactful components of sleep health promotion in this course.

Sleep improvement plan creation and integration

Once students in the While You Were Sleeping course have a better understanding of their sleep and have gathered data on areas for improvement, they are prepared and empowered to take meaningful steps to enhance their own sleep habits. Toward the end of the semester, students implement their SIP, a personalized plan incorporating evidence-based strategies to promote positive sleep health. While sleep education alone has not been found to lead to significant sleep behavior improvement [49], we have found that sleep education in combination with required behavioral practice (e.g., graded coursework) can enhance motivation which leads to the adoption of healthier sleep habits [43].

A student's individually curated SIP is born from both their newly informed unease about the consequences of sleep deprivation and optimism about the potential benefits they can experience with quality sleep. The unit on Sleep Regulation within the While You Were Sleeping curriculum begins with a deep exploration of sleep debt and the consequences of inadequate sleep, both acutely and chronically; the curriculum then explores the direct effects of inadequate sleep on human physiology before turning to the value of maintaining a regular circadian rhythm and teaching students the effective tools of sleep hygiene and why they work when properly applied. At the close of the unit on Sleep Regulation, students learn about Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) and the use of stimulus control, sleep restriction, cognitive therapy, behavioral relaxation, and relapse management. These sleep hygiene and CBT-I strategies provide students with evidence-based methods to reach their goals and the understanding that, sometimes, even the smallest of changes in behavior or routine can have massive impacts. Sleep interventions focused on sleep hygiene and CBT-I have been found to be both acceptable and feasible across diverse populations and socioeconomic settings [50]. The literature identifies key barriers to translating sleep hygiene interventions into behavior change, including limited knowledge about sleep, lack of motivation to change, caregiving responsibilities, job strain, and environmental factors, such as a lack of community safety and living in a disruptive neighborhood [50]. The collegiate setting is well-suited for implementing sleep behavior interventions as several of these barriers can be mitigated, and living in a residential dormitory or other group environment offers a unique opportunity for some students to build new routines.

While the SIP includes structured prompts to guide reflection and consideration of which interventions may be beneficial, students individualize their plans by focusing on areas most meaningful to them, thereby avoiding a one-size-fits-all approach. Uniform approaches are often ineffective, as they typically disregard the distinct life factors of each person involved in the intervention and fail to provide proper monitoring of individual progress [51]. On the other hand, individualized approaches help to address the complex and personalized aspects of a student's life, which differ immensely from person to person [51]. Students are empowered to make behavioral changes based on their capacity and preferences, which makes their efforts meaningful and more likely to be successful. As students prepare their SIP, they first reflect on their baseline sleep diary and rating scale data, searching for the current difficulties with their sleep and why they are occurring; they then identify patterns (e.g., drinking caffeine after 5pm typically results in a longer sleep onset latency, going to bed and awakening at different times throughout the week disrupts their circadian rhythm, etc.) that are interfering with their sleep and feeling better rested. Over at least one week, students then implement their SIP while they simultaneously once again track their sleep habits using the AASM Sleep Diary. At the end of this period, the students complete the remaining battery of measures a second time and then write a paper describing their SIP, the class concepts they used to inform their decision-making, and if they noticed any improvements in their sleep. Based on the content of these papers, course reviews, and feedback, most students state that they observe positive changes in their sleep, enjoy the process of implementing their SIP, and see significant improvements in their anxiety and mood to boot [43].

Some research has found that sleep promotion programs may fail to result in sustained improvement at follow-up, especially when habit formation is not a central focus of the program's intervention [40,52]. However, students in While You Were Sleeping have reported significant improvements in their sleep quality and sleep hygiene at the end of the semester, and these changes are maintained at three month follow up [43]. We suspect that the SIP has proven to be efficacious because of the combined emphasis on sleep education, assigned behavioral practice, and habit formation. These findings suggest that integrating sleep health promotion into undergraduate curricula alongside an individualized, assigned intervention can lead to meaningful and sustained improvements in a population for whom achieving sufficient sleep is often challenging.

Conclusion

Poor sleep is a significant public health problem, and relying solely on interventions in clinical settings is not a scalable solution. Creating sleep health promotion programs in university settings provides a novel venue from which to address the needs of vulnerable populations, such as young adults. The breadth of sleep health content in the CAMS curricula provides higher education institutions with the flexibility to incorporate material into existing courses or develop new ones focused on sleep. Offering students sleep education, targeted guidance on sleep interventions, and support for behavior change leads to sleep improvements and empowers them, further enhancing their college experience. Using academic settings to address sleep health allows us to reach a wide audience, particularly young and vulnerable individuals. These interventions have been found to be feasible, adaptable, and acceptable by young adults and are worthy of consideration by other colleges and universities.
