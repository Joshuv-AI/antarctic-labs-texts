Case report of transcatheter flow restriction in isolated non-redundant major aorto-pulmonary collateral arteries
Authors: Nandini Mehra, Hani Najm, Justin Tretter, Nicholas Szugye, Joanna Ghobrial, Constantin Kühl, Luis Antonio Moreno-Ruiz, Melnie Johns, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMajor aorto-pulmonary collateral arteries are congenital remnants of the segmental arteries that supply the lungs. They are commonly associated with congenital heart disease, particularly in cases where the main pulmonary artery branches are not well developed, these segmental collaterals do not regress and continue to supply oxygenated blood to the pulmonary vasculature. Over time, these collaterals can become problematic, particularly if they enlarge, leading to clinically significant symptoms such as haemoptysis, segmental pulmonary hypertension, and left heart volume overload.Case summaryHerein, we describe a rare case of isolated major aorto-pulmonary collaterals without associated congenital heart disease that presented with haemoptysis and managed percutaneously with a modified flow-reducer device.DiscussionThis is a novel therapy for this very unique clinical scenario. Introduction

Major aorto-pulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs) are small vessels that are formed during foetal development and regress around day 40–50 in utero.1 The majority of patients with MAPCAs have associated congenital heart disease that limits blood flow to the lungs (i.e. pulmonary atresia)2, necessitating an alternative pulmonary blood supply, and thus, persistence of these foetal vessels. Pulmonary arteries may or may not be present to the MAPCA-supplied lung segments.

MAPCAs can be classified as redundant or non-redundant. Redundant MAPCAs anastomose to the distal pulmonary arteries so are non-essential because the lung has dual blood supply. Non-redundant MAPCAs provide the sole blood supply to the lung segment and are thus essential. MAPCAs can also have different morphologies (number, origins, branching patterns, lung supply) and are categorized into three types. Type 1 MAPCAs arise from the bronchial artery; type 2 arise from the aorta; and type 3 arise from the aortic branches like the subclavian artery. Lastly, MAPCAs can be classified by their effect on the lung segment (hypertensive or non-hypertensive)3. Hypertensive MAPCAs are larger in calibre with evidence of pulmonary hypertension. Non-hypertensive MAPCAs have some form of stenosis that protects the lung territory from segmental pulmonary hypertension.1 Persistence of these vessels into adulthood can lead to multiple consequences, including but not limited to, haemoptysis, left heart volume overload, and pulmonary hypertension.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old male presented with repeat haemoptysis after activity in the setting of known MAPCAs without any associated congenital heart defects (CHD). He had a relevant history of asthma and multiple upper respiratory infections in childhood. He was seen previously for one episode of small volume haemoptysis at which time the two MAPCAs were discovered via computed tomography (CT) scan of his lungs. The patient was subsequently lost to follow-up during the COVID-19 pandemic and re-presented with multiple episodes of haemoptysis with activity several years later. His physical exam did not disclose any abnormalities. He had no jugular venous distention; lungs were clear to auscultation. He had a regular heart rate and rhythm, normal S1/S2, no S3/S4, no murmurs, heaves, rubs, or gallops. No significant peripheral oedema was found on exam. Peripheral saturations were 97% on room air. Patient had normal haemoglobin 15.8 g/dL, haematocrit 46.8%, platelets 231 k/μL, prothrombin time 11.1 s, and INR 1.1. Hypercoagulable workup was negative. Urinalysis was normal, and pulmonary function testing showed normal diffusing capacity. Bronchoscopy showed mildly compressed right upper lobe (RUL) and right middle lobe (RML) bronchial orifices but no clear aetiology of haemoptysis. Repeat CT of the lungs confirmed the presence of two MAPCAs solely supplying the RUL and upper segment of the RLL (Figure 1). He did not have redundant pulmonary arterial blood supply (Figure 2). Echocardiogram showed a normal ejection fraction of 57%, normal size and systolic function of the right ventricle (RV) with an estimated right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) of 22 mmHg, no significant valvular disease, and no intracardiac shunting. Six-minute walk test showed oxygen saturation > 98% both at rest and with exercise. As part of the diagnostic workup, he also underwent right heart catheterization which showed a normal cardiac index of 3.4 L/min/m2. His pulmonary artery (PA) pressures were normal at 28/12 mmHg with a mean PA pressure (mPAP) of 18 mmHg and a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 7 mmHg. Pulmonary vascular resistance was calculated to be 1.1 WU and systemic vascular resistance to be 955.5 dynes/s/cm5. Notably, these values were obtained from the lung segments supplied by the pulmonary arterial tree. However, pressure in the MAPCAs was the same as aortic pressure of 104/75/87, concerning for segmental pulmonary hypertension (Figure 2). Shunt fraction was calculated to be 1.26. Aortic and PA angiography confirmed the CT findings (Figure 1). A ventilation perfusion scan showed perfusion defects in the lung segments supplied by the MAPCAs.

Given that the right upper and right lower lung segments supplied by MAPCAs had been exposed to systemic pressures for 32 years, there was concern that surgical management via unifocalization (re-routing of the MAPCAs into a functional pulmonary artery) would fail as the vasculature with normal PA pressure would fail to supply blood to the hypertensive lung segments. MAPCA occlusion was also considered but thought to lead to lung infarction given the non-redundant nature of the MAPCAs. Ultimately, after multidisciplinary discussion (with congenital cardio-thoracic surgery, interventional cardiology, pulmonology, pulmonary hypertension, and vascular medicine) and shared decision-making, the recommendation was made to percutaneously reduce the flow to these lung segments by placing a modified vascular plug. The patient was counselled extensively on the novelty of this approach, as well as the potential benefits, risks, and complications. There are rare case reports in the paediatric and congenital literature describing modification of vascular occluders for use in porto-systemic shunts, as well as for intracardiac use.4–8 We utilized the same approach in our patient.

After induction of general anaesthesia, access was obtained with an 8 French right femoral artery sheath, a 7 French left femoral vein sheath for anaesthesia, and a 4 French left femoral arterial line for haemodynamic monitoring. The MVP device was prepared by opening 1 diamond PTFE cell for restriction of flow rather than complete plugging of the MAPCA given that the lung segments were solely supplied by these vessels (Figure 1). The RUL MAPCA was selectively engaged with a 6 F × 55 cm MP1 telescoping guide catheter through which a 5 F × 100 cm angled glide catheter was placed and a 300 cm 0.014-inch Runthrough wire was placed. Confirmatory angiography of the RUL MAPCA was then done with subsequent placement of the modified 9 mm vascular plug (Medtronic MVP™). The pressure in the MAPCA was the same as aortic pressure before deployment and immediately after deployment of the modified vascular plug, pressure wire measurement demonstrated a ∼30% reduction in pressure with a 15–25 mmHg pressure gradient. The same procedure was followed for the RLL MAPCA with similar flow reduction seen on pressure wire measurement post-deployment of the modified MVP plug. The patient tolerated the procedure well and was discharged home the same day on aspirin 81 mg daily. Cardiac MRI (cMRI) three weeks later showed a reduction in shunt fraction from 1.26 invasively to 0.9 by cMRI (Figure 2). Given the rarity of this congenital abnormality, there is lack of data to guide treatment. Our management strategy was to decrease blood flow to reduce risk of haemoptysis and allow for vascular remodelling so that future unifocalization may be successful. The patient has not had any recurrence of haemoptysis since the procedure. We performed repeat imaging to monitor for complete occlusion, and recent imaging 13 months post-procedure demonstrated patent MAPCAs with stable device position.

Discussion

Isolated non-redundant MAPCAs are extremely rare. To date, there is only one case report to our knowledge of an isolated MAPCA in a child. There are no reports of isolated and non-redundant MAPCAs in adults.9 The only anatomic abnormality seen in this patient was lack of pulmonary arterial supply to the right upper, and upper segment of the right lower lobes of the lungs. We postulate that this could have led to persistence of the foetal aorto-pulmonary collaterals supplying these areas.

Given activity-induced haemoptysis, our patient underwent transcatheter flow restriction of the collateral vessels with the desired result. In the rare case of non-redundant MAPCA without CHD, transcatheter flow restriction is a practical option for reducing the risk of haemoptysis and promoting lung vascular remodelling over time. To our knowledge, this is the first such reported case.
