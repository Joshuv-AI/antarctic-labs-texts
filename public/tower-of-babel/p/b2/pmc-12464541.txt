How to Wow From the Podium: Creating and Delivering a Great Presentation in Orthopedic Surgery
Authors: Joshua C Rozell
Published: 2025

Whether presenting research at a conference, teaching at a course, or giving a didactic lecture to trainees, the ability to create a clean, concise presentation is paramount to leaving the listener with a good impression and developing your brand and reputation on the podium. Too often, presentation slides are unnecessarily busy, the presenter reads from the slides, or the slides’ visual esthetic leaves the listener distracted and confused, with no more knowledge of the topic than before. The purpose of this article is to provide high-yield tips and tricks in both written and instructional video formats for developing a crisp, succinct presentation. Creating a great presentation is only half the battle because the execution and delivery also affect the impact the presentation can have on the audience. The importance of practicing and rehearsing the timing of the talk cannot be overstated.

Here are the top 5 important principles that should guide you while making a presentation:

The audience will not be able to simultaneously listen to you speak and glean the necessary text information from your slides

The purpose of a presentation is not to regurgitate information, use complete sentences, or read from the slides. Each slide should have a dedicated purpose that reinforces your message. What should the listener take home from this slide? Do you want to explain a complex topic, emphasize a key learning point, or use an image to illustrate an idea? Whatever the purpose may be, you need to imagine yourself as the listener and figure out how to connect with your audience and communicate the relevant information. Understanding your audience is important as well. If you are presenting to a group of medical students or junior residents, your content and discussion should be more basic than if you were presenting the same topic to fellows.

Image formatting and placement matters and underscores the attention to detail and care you invest in delivering a talk

Images are very important for presentations (Videos 1-3). When given a choice, the audience will always be drawn first to a picture rather than text. If you can show an image or a series of images and explain them in detail as the listener is looking at them, you have tapped into their senses of hearing and sight at the same time, which will work synergistically to improve their understanding. If you have words on the slide that explain a picture, the listener will glance at the picture, read the slide, and miss the important details you are explaining. Therefore, how you format pictures is critical.

Tables can be used tactfully to highlight important findings

Presentations usually contain data and literature to support the presenter’s thoughts (Videos 3 and 4). When creating a table, do not copy and paste the table from a manuscript as an image. Recreate the table so you can format the size, font size, color scheme, and most importantly highlight the key findings. A classic example is including a “demographics” table in a research talk. The slide is filled top to bottom with rows and rows of data and P-values, and the presenter will usually say “these are our demographics, none of which were significantly different between the groups.” The slide is then advanced before the audience can even figure out what the table says. To compound the uselessness of such a slide, sometimes the presenter will even say “I know this is a busy slide,” or “this is hard to see, but ….” In that case, why put the slide in the presentation at all? If there are key aspects from a table you want the audience to understand, create a new table using a larger font size and highlight only those findings. An alternative is to have the initial table shown first and then animate a smaller table to appear with the important findings on top of the first table. This way, you are emphasizing what you want them to take away from the slide. When formatting the cells of the table, align the text to the middle of the cell to improve the esthetic and table symmetry. Row height should be consistent, and when possible, the table should be expanded so each row fits on one line. Sometimes this requires column width to be adjusted (ie, the column with P-values can be sized more narrowly).

Sometimes too much is too much

Color scheme

Color scheme will vary based on institution or practice, and templates are usually suggested. In general, a white background or a dark background and a contrasting color for the text (white or black) should be utilized. Similarly, when highlighting text, be sure to change the text color so it accentuates rather than detracts from the visibility of the text. Be mindful that some listeners may be colorblind (most commonly red-green), so avoid using these colors when possible. Lastly, keep the color scheme consistent. Each slide should use similar colors for text and highlighted portions. This streamlines the talk and is more professional.

Institutional logos

The title slide should have an institutional or practice logo to introduce you and your professional background. Subsequent slides may have smaller logos templated for every slide. If branding is used, the logos should occupy less than 5% of the slide. If your logo banner spans the entire bottom third of each slide, you lose out on precious real estate for important information and distract the audience each time you advance to the next slide. The audience already knows where you work after the title slide; no need to make that the focus on every slide.

References and limitations

The work of others should certainly be acknowledged and cited appropriately in a presentation. In-slide citations are helpful if the shorthand form of the citation is placed in the lower corner of the slide. Particularly for research presentations, there is no need to flash a slide of 20+ references in a tiny font for less than 1 second at the end. Dedicate your time to create the most takeaway messages for the audience. Similarly, understanding that every research study has some limitations is necessary. The limitations of the study might be mentioned briefly but should not be the main focus of a presentation. Most research presentations at conferences are limited to between 4 and 7 minutes, so spending 1 minute of that time on study limitations runs counter to the main purpose of presenting your findings.

Less can be more, and more can be less

Principle number one already highlighted that the listener in unlikely to be able to multitask enough to read the slides and listen to you at the same time. To that end, if you are going to have text on a slide, keep it brief. Instead of writing “this was a retrospective study of 350 patients undergoing primary total hip and knee arthroplasty from June 2012-June 2024 at an urban, tertiary care academic hospital,” use several bullet points as below:

•Retrospective (2012-2024)

Retrospective (2012-2024)

•Single institution

Single institution

•N = 350

N = 350

•1° Total hip arthroplasty/total knee arthroplasty

1° Total hip arthroplasty/total knee arthroplasty

This is a much more concise way to present the data. Use abbreviations; a presentation is not a formal manuscript where you need to spell out everything. As the presenter, you can use the text on the slide as a guide and reinforce how you are going to deliver the information. As long as you know the information from the study, you do not need to include all the details on the slide. If the audience asks questions, this will foster better engagement. Increase the bullet point spacing to 1.5 or 2.0 to occupy more of the space on the slide (Figs. 1 and 2). When including a research article within your presentation, keep the text brief. For an esthetic effect and color, add a small image of the journal cover beside a screenshot of the article header (Fig. 3).

Sometimes more can be less, but in a good way! The best example of this is using additional slides to declutter the presentation and highlight certain points. Just because the talk is only 5 minutes, it does not mean you cannot have as many slides as you need to illustrate your points. The “duplicate slide” and the fade or morph “slide transition” function allows you to seamlessly transition between slides, but only change very small things, such as highlighting different boxes of a table or changing the layout of a slide in a very subtle but impactful way (Videos 2-4). Animations can also serve the same purpose but are generally more difficult to execute. You also want to avoid too much animation that detracts from the message of the slide. Animation should be used to emphasize points, not make the audience dizzy or distracted. Similarly, when adding videos, embed them in the presentation, trim them to remove any unnecessary footage, and animate them to start immediately to avoid having an additional click and then find out the video file does not work.

Presentation delivery

Now that you have a great presentation, the next hurdle is effectively delivering the information. For medical students, residents, and early career surgeons, having a script is critically important to stay on time, express all the information you want to convey, and avoid fumbling. Do not necessarily rely on “presentation mode” to be able to see your script or notes because many podiums will not have this function. Include cues in your script for when you need to advance an animation or transition. In addition, practicing in front of a mirror and recording yourself can uncover verbal or motor tendencies that you can tweak or soften prior to being in front of an audience. Time yourself and leave a 30- to 60-second cushion so you are sure you will not go over time. Lastly, pay close attention to your voice inflections, how often you look up at the audience (if you have your script in front of you), hand gesticulation, and use of filler words such as “um” or “and so forth.” You need to captivate the audience quickly, so no time should be wasted. If you know your presentation well, your brain will not need to find words to explain your points—this will come naturally.

For a conference presentation, triple check that your presentation is uploaded to the conference website and bring backup copies on a flash drive. Ensure compatibility of the presentation software version including fonts, images, videos, and animations. Before the session, go to the presentation room and familiarize yourself with the podium, slide-advancing remote, and screens and ask any questions to the audiovisual team in the room. If there are moderators, introduce yourself before the session and see if they have any questions about your presentation before you begin. This may provide insights about questions you may be asked at the end of your talk and will also ensure that the moderators pronounce your name correctly when introducing you and your topic. Once introduced, you do not need to repeat your name and the title of your talk. The audience can see your topic in the program and on the slide. Use that time for the other slides so your presentation does not feel rushed.

Conclusions

Showcasing your knowledge or research is one important facet for developing a successful career as an orthopedic surgeon. On the podium, you are evaluated not only on the content of your presentation but also how you deliver the information and how the information is shown visually. The overall goal for your time on the podium is to leave the audience with an understanding of your topic and key takeaway points, and thinking “Wow, that was a great presentation.”
