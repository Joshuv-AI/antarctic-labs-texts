Sex-associated differences in deltoid and rotator cuff muscle elongation after reverse shoulder arthroplasty
Authors: Aghdas Movassaghi, Cooper Williams, Humza Bhatti, Lana Smith, Jocelyn Lubert, Vani J. Sabesan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvancements in pre-operative planning and implant design have improved the ability to tailor reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) to patient anatomy. Post-operative changes in deltoid and rotator cuff muscle length are important for function and stability and may vary based on implant configuration and patient-specific factors; however, the influence of sex on these muscle length changes remains poorly defined. The purpose of this study was to evaluate sex-based differences in implant selection and muscle length changes following rTSA and to determine whether these differences persist after accounting for anatomic and implant-related variables.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 507 patients (233 males, 274 females) who underwent computed tomography–based pre-operative planning for rTSA using the INHANCE Reverse Shoulder System (DePuy Synthes) was performed. Standardized 3-dimensional scapular coordinates were used to measure glenoid and scapular morphology. Percent elongation of the deltoid, infraspinatus, teres minor, and subscapularis was calculated between native and planned states. Implant characteristics, including glenosphere size, lateralization, and eccentricity, were recorded. Sex-based differences in anatomy, implant selection, and muscle elongation were evaluated with t-tests, analysis of variance, and multivariable regression.ResultsMales demonstrated significantly larger scapular and glenoid dimensions and were more likely to receive larger and more lateralized glenosphere configurations (P < .001). Deltoid elongation was associated with implant size and anatomic features and did not differ by sex after adjustment (P = .140). In contrast, posterior rotator cuff muscles demonstrated significant sex-based differences, with males exhibiting greater shortening of the infraspinatus (−8.1% vs. −5.1%, P < .001) and teres minor (−14.7% vs. −10.1%, P < .001). Implant size was not consistently associated with muscle length changes, and no significant interaction between sex and implant size was observed.ConclusionMuscle length changes following rTSA vary based on both implant configuration and patient-specific factors, with distinct sex-based differences in posterior rotator cuff response. While deltoid elongation is primarily influenced by implant selection, posterior cuff muscle changes appear to be independently associated with sex. These findings highlight the importance of considering patient-specific anatomy when planning rTSA and may help guide implant selection and soft tissue tensioning strategies.

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) is increasingly performed for a range of indications, and patient-specific factors, such as sex, age, and comorbidities, may influence clinical outcomes.14,15,18,31 Specifically, sex-based differences in outcomes have been observed, with females presenting with worse pre-operative function and lower post-operative scores, and males demonstrating higher complication rates, including infection and revision.8,14,15,28,37 The reasons for these differences are not fully understood but may be due to differences in bone quality, soft tissue integrity, and shoulder morphology.11,30

From a biomechanical perspective, successful rTSA depends on restoration of optimal muscle length–tension relationships, which are critical for joint stability, range of motion, and functional recovery.2,7,19,22,23 Inadequate or excessive tension of the deltoid and rotator cuff muscles has been associated with complications such as instability, acromial or scapular stress fractures, and poor functional outcomes.3,12,23,39 These muscle length–tension relationships are influenced by multiple factors, including patient-specific anatomy, implant design, and implant positioning, with variables such as glenosphere size, lateralization, and humeral neck-shaft angle known to alter muscle lengths.13,19,23,25,33

Despite these insights, the interaction between patient-specific anatomy and implant selection remains incompletely understood, particularly in the context of sex-based differences. Male and female scapulae differ not only in overall size but also in glenoid morphology, with females demonstrating smaller, more oval glenoids and a higher prevalence of anterior notching, whereas males exhibit larger and more rounded glenoid anatomy.21,26 Additionally, prior studies have demonstrated that these sex-based differences in scapular and glenoid anatomy, including size, version, and spatial relationships, may influence implant positioning and post-operative muscle length even when using computed tomography (CT)-based pre-operative planning using 3-dimensional reconstruction software.24,26,29 Clinically, these biomechanical differences may contribute to sex-specific variation in function, stability, and complication rates following rTSA. However, the relative contributions of anatomic and implant-related factors to muscle length changes remain unclear, and no clinical studies have directly evaluated whether these patterns differ by sex. Defining these relationships may help guide more individualized implant selection and intraoperative tensioning strategies.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate sex-based differences in patient morphology, implant selection, and muscle elongation following rTSA and to determine whether these differences persist after accounting for anatomic and implant-related factors. We hypothesized that sex-based differences in shoulder morphology result in distinct elongation patterns that are not fully explained by implant size alone, reflecting the combined influence of soft tissue, bony, and implant-related factors on post-operative biomechanics and outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study utilized a dataset of 507 consecutive rTSA cases performed using the INHANCE Shoulder System (DePuy Synthes, Warsaw, IN) with pre-operative planning conducted using CT-based planning software (Materialise Surgicase, Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). Surgical plans were retrospectively obtained from the Materialise database and were not created specifically for this study, reflecting routine clinical practice; surgeons were not blinded to patient gender at the time of planning. Patients were included if they underwent rTSA and had complete pre-operative CT imaging and corresponding pre-operative planning data available within the Materialise database. Cases were randomly selected from a multicenter database including sites in the United States and Canada. Implant positioning and sizing were guided by the pre-operative plan, with a range of implant configurations selected across cases.

Pre-operative planning and 3-dimensional modeling

Pre-operative CT imaging was uploaded into Materialise Surgicase planning software, where 3-dimensional models of the scapula and humerus were generated to perform pre-operative planning for rTSA implantation. CT-based anatomic measurements were performed using a standardized 3-dimensional scapular coordinate system based on the glenoid center, trigonum spinae, and inferior scapular angle. Using this coordinate system, scapular morphology was characterized by measurements of scapular height, width, and mediolateral length. Glenoid morphology included measurements of height, width, version, inclination, posterior subluxation, and vault depth. The acromion–glenoid relationship was assessed using the critical shoulder angle (CSA), defined as the angle between a line connecting the superior and inferior glenoid and a line extending from the inferior glenoid to the lateral acromion.27

Implant characteristics and classification

Implant characteristics were extracted from the pre-operative planning software. All cases were performed using the INHANCE Shoulder System (DePuy Synthes, Warsaw, IN, USA), which features a 135° humeral neck-shaft angle and allows for variable glenosphere lateralization. Glenosphere configurations were defined by size (32, 36, or 40 mm), type (standard [STD] vs. eccentric [ECC]), and degree of glenosphere lateralization (+4 mm vs. +8 mm).

Muscle elongation measurement

Muscle elongation measurements were performed using the Materialise Surgicase pre-operative planning platform. The computational framework underlying these measurements has been previously described by Pitocchi et al, who developed a validated automated method using statistical shape modeling and a muscle wrapping algorithm to estimate muscle paths and lengths during reverse shoulder arthroplasty planning.32 The muscles evaluated included the deltoid, infraspinatus, teres minor, and subscapularis. Within the software, standardized origin and insertion points were defined for each muscle, and muscle paths were modeled using patient-specific anatomy and the planned implant position.

Elongation was defined as the percentage change in muscle–tendon unit length between the native pre-operative muscle lengths and planned post-operative states consistent with previous studies1,10,25,33,38:MuscleElongation(%)=L−L0L0×100

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Independent t-tests were used to compare anatomic characteristics, implant selection, and muscle elongation between sexes. Differences in muscle elongation across glenosphere sizes were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance. Two-way analysis of variance was performed to assess the independent and interactive effects of sex and glenosphere size on muscle length changes. Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of muscle elongation, including sex, anatomic parameters, and implant size. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp), with significance set at P < .05 and Bonferroni adjustment applied for multiple comparisons.

Results

Patient and shoulder morphologic characteristics by sex

This study included 507 patients who underwent rTSA with complete pre-operative planning data. There were 274 female and 233 male patients included in the cohort. There was no significant difference in age between females and males (72.8 ± 7.7 vs. 72.4 ± 9.1 years, P = .641). Similarly, glenoid inclination did not differ between sexes (6.1 ± 6.4° vs. 5.9 ± 5.6°, P = .664). Males demonstrated significantly greater glenoid retroversion compared to females (−12.5 ± 7.9° vs. −8.8 ± 8.7°, P < .001), as well as increased vault depth (21.9 ± 2.7 mm vs. 20.4 ± 2.4 mm, P < .001) and posterior subluxation (63.0% ± 11.2% vs. 59.1% ± 15.3%, P < .001). In addition, males had significantly larger glenoid dimensions, including greater height (41.4 ± 4.7 mm vs. 35.6 ± 3.6 mm, P < .001) and width (33.6 ± 4.8 mm vs. 28.1 ± 3.8 mm, P < .001). Scapular dimensions were also larger in males, with greater scapular height (174.8 ± 11.6 mm vs. 150.5 ± 9.2 mm, P < .001) and width (147.5 ± 9.2 mm vs. 130.6 ± 8.5 mm, P < .001). Females more frequently received smaller glenospheres, with 57.7% of females receiving 32 mm implants compared to only 5.6% of males. In contrast, males more commonly received larger glenosphere sizes, including 40 mm implants (31.8% vs. 3.3% in females). Eccentric glenosphere configurations were more frequently utilized in males, with 17.6% of males receiving 36 mm ECC +3 mm implants compared to 8.4% of females. Notably, no female patients received the largest glenosphere configuration of 40 mm ECC +4 mm, whereas 7.3% of males were treated with this implant size. Multivariable predictors of elongation

Multivariable linear regression demonstrated distinct, muscle-specific predictors of elongation. For the deltoid, elongation was significantly associated with glenosphere size (β = 0.169, P < .001), glenoid height (β = 0.379, P < .001), and CSA (β = 0.502, P < .001), while sex was not independently associated (β = 0.059, P = .426). In contrast, sex remained an independent predictor of posterior rotator cuff elongation. For the infraspinatus, male sex was associated with greater shortening (β = −0.283, P < .001), independent of implant and anatomic variables, while glenosphere size was not significant (P = .850). Similarly, for the teres minor, sex remained a significant predictor (β = −0.242, P < .001), along with CSA (β = −0.310, P < .001). Subscapularis elongation was not independently associated with sex (P = .389) and was instead influenced by anatomic parameters including scapular dimensions and vault depth (Table V).

Discussion

This study is the first to evaluate sex-based differences in muscle elongation patterns following rTSA using patient-specific, CT-based pre-operative planning. In this cohort, males exhibited significantly larger scapular and glenoid dimensions, as well as greater glenoid retroversion and vault depth, which translated into the use of larger and more lateralized implants. However, these differences in implant selection did not fully account for observed differences in muscle elongation. The primary finding is that male patients demonstrated greater shortening of the infraspinatus and teres minor, and this association persisted after accounting for similar implant configurations. These findings suggest intrinsic variabilities in rotator cuff behavior between sexes which may fully explained by implant construct alone.

The observed differences in posterior rotator cuff elongation are notable given the role of the infraspinatus and teres minor in external rotation and shoulder stability following rTSA. Males exhibited greater shortening of both muscles despite receiving larger and more lateralized implants, indicating that implant selection alone does not account for these differences. One potential explanation is underlying sex-based variation in muscle-tendon properties or shoulder morphology. Cabezas et al5 reported sex-based differences in scapular and glenoid morphology, including glenoid version, scapular neck length, and lateral acromial offset, which all may affect implant positioning and resulting muscle tension. Prior work by Berton et al4 demonstrated that teres minor function following rTSA is influenced by the relationship between muscle length and moment arm, suggesting that alterations in muscle length may affect force-generating capacity. In this context, the greater posterior cuff shortening observed in males in the present study may have biomechanical implications beyond geometric changes alone, as even modest reductions in muscle length could influence force-producing capacity. In addition, sex-based differences in muscle properties may contribute, as Lannersten et al20 demonstrated that females generate substantially lower shoulder muscle strength compared to males, reflected by differences in muscle size and moment arm mechanics. These differences may influence how the posterior rotator cuff responds to implant-induced changes in geometry, contributing to the observed sex-based differences in muscle shortening.

Implant selection differed significantly by sex, with males more frequently receiving larger and more lateralized glenospheres. Despite this, no interaction between sex and implant size was observed, indicating that implant size did not differentially affect elongation between males and females. Prior work supports that implant effects on muscle length are muscle-specific. Imiolczyk et al16 used simulation modeling to demonstrate that females experienced greater relative lateralization than males for the same implant types, while Giles et al9 and Werner et al36 showed that posterior cuff and deltoid muscles respond differently to changes in lateralization and humeral lengthening. Consistent with this, Levin et al22 and Maxwell et al25 demonstrated that deltoid elongation is closely tied to implant geometry, supporting that deltoid behavior is largely implant-driven and responds predictably to changes in humeral offset and lateralization. Together, these studies emphasize that cuff and deltoid elongation and tensioning are governed by distinct, muscle-specific mechanisms that may manifest differently across sexes.

Although statistically significant differences were observed between sexes, the magnitude of these changes is small. There are currently no established thresholds for clinically meaningful changes in muscle length following rTSA, limiting interpretation of these findings. Biomechanical modeling studies have suggested that moderate muscle elongation of approximately 10-20% may improve force generation and muscle efficiency.6,13 However, findings from clinical investigations remain mixed on the impact rotator cuff muscle length changes may have on outcomes.17,19,34 Together, these findings suggest that while muscle length changes influence biomechanics, their translation to clinically meaningful outcomes remains uncertain.

Taken together, the results of this study suggest that posterior rotator cuff elongation following rTSA is influenced by patient-specific factors, including sex, rather than implant selection alone. This distinction may have implications for pre-operative planning and highlights the importance of considering patient-specific anatomy and soft tissue characteristics when evaluating implant positioning. However, given the absence of clinical outcome data, further investigation is needed to determine whether these biomechanical differences translate into differences in functional recovery or patient-reported outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, while our dataset included over 500 cases, it was retrospective and limited to patients with complete pre-operative CT planning, potentially introducing selection bias. Second, although we identified elongation differences, we did not directly correlate these with intraoperative implant placement, clinical outcomes, or patient-reported measures. Third, our assessment of muscle elongation relied on a standardized software-based approach and may not capture dynamic soft tissue behavior under loading. Although we controlled for key anatomic variables, we did not include other potentially relevant factors such as body mass index, tendon quality, or muscle fatty infiltration, which may influence elongation and functional capacity. Patients were included who underwent rTSA for a variety of indications, and the inability to stratify outcomes by underlying diagnosis represents a limitation, as this heterogeneity may influence elongation. As the dataset was geographically limited to the United States and Canada; findings may not be generalizable to international populations, particularly in regions where different implant philosophies or surgical practices predominate. Finally, as all cases were performed using a single implant system, these findings may not be generalizable to other reverse shoulder arthroplasty designs with differing biomechanical properties. Despite these limitations, this study provides foundational data demonstrating sex-based differences in muscle elongation and implant selection. Future prospective investigations with standardized protocols and clinical follow-up are warranted to validate these findings and guide patient-specific surgical planning.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that identical implant configurations do not produce uniform biomechanical effects across patients. While deltoid tensioning can be predictably modulated through implant selection, posterior cuff behavior may be less controllable and more dependent on intrinsic patient factors. Prior studies have demonstrated that increased soft-tissue tension following rTSA may improve stability but at the cost of increased joint reaction forces and potential complications, including acromial stress fractures and pain.9,35 Schmalzl et al35 reported that elevated deltoid tension post-rTSA, as measured by shear wave elastography, was associated with higher post-operative pain. Conclusion

Muscle length changes following rTSA vary based on both implant configuration and patient-specific factors, with distinct sex-based differences in posterior rotator cuff response. While deltoid elongation is primarily influenced by implant selection, posterior cuff muscle changes appear to be independently associated with sex. These findings highlight the importance of considering patient-specific anatomy when planning rTSA and may help guide implant selection and soft tissue tensioning strategies.
