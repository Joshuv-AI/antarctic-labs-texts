Analysis of risk factors and adverse outcomes for hyperlacidemia following acute Stanford A aortic dissection
Authors: Mai Hailing, Li Yan, Zhang Yonghai, Liu Hongli, Gao Xiaofang, Li Youjin, Jin Lijuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute Stanford type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is associated with a high mortality rate. Postoperative hyperlactatemia is significant in predicting mortality and the incidence of major complications. MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 337 ATAAD patients admitted to the Cardiothoracic Surgery Department of Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region People's Hospital from January 2017 to December 2024. According to the highest lactate level measured in the ICU within 12 hours postoperatively, patients were divided into a non-hyperlactatemia group (≤4 mmol/L) and a hyperlactatemia group (>4 mmol/L). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors for postoperative hyperlactatemia.ResultsPotential risk factors identified in univariate analysis, along with those considered clinically significant, were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, which demonstrated that hypertension（OR = 2.016, P = 0.032), preoperative NYHA heart function classification （OR = 2.059, P = 0.029, surgical time (OR = 1.011, P = 0.000, CPB（OR = 1.009, P = 0.039), and DHCA （OR = 1.057, P = 0.002） were independent risk factors for postoperative hyperlactatemia. The combined predictive capability of these independent risk factors exhibited greater predictive value than each indicator alone (P < 0.05), with a sensitivity of 94.5%, specificity of 61.7%, and AUC of 0.836. Postoperative data comparison indicated a higher incidence of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and hospitalization mortality in the hyperlactatemia group (P < 0.05). However, postoperative hyperlacidemia did not significantly prolong the hospital stay in the ICU; the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events in the ICU prolonged group (≥ 7 days) was significantly higher than that in the routine hospitalization group (< 7 days) (P < 0.05). The cause of death in the ICU prolonged group was sepsis first, followed by major adverse cardiovascular events.ConclusionPostoperative hyperlactatemia is common in ATAAD. Effectively addressing the risk factors leading to postoperative hyperlactatemia may help reduce the occurrence of adverse events and enhance clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Aortic dissection (AD) is a life-threatening emergency, particularly acute Stanford A dissection involving the ascending aorta and its branches, requiring rapid surgical intervention. Postoperative complications directly affect the survival rate and quality of life. In patients with ATAAD, the surgical procedure, which typically involves CPB and deep hypothermia to maintain systemic perfusion and oxygenation, is an important mechanism for cardiac surgery that replaces the heart and lungs within a specific and limited period [1] but can also lead to multiple complications after surgery [2]. These complications include metabolic changes and hemodynamic instability [3], which alter serum lactate levels [4]. Elevated lactate suggests tissue hypoxia and systemic hypoperfusion, though it can also arise from non-hypoxic causes [5]. In cardiac surgery, there is a bipolar distribution of early-onset and late-onset hyperlactinemia according to the timing of postoperative onset. The former is defined as elevated blood lactate levels that occur between the onset of CPB and arrival in the ICU, and appears to result from both anoxic and non-anoxic events during CPB. The initial elevation of lactic acid observed during the first 6 to 12 h after admission to the ICU is referred to as delayed hyperlactic acidemia, characterized by normal postoperative cardiac output and no significant impairment of oxygen delivery [6]. The incidence of postoperative hyperlactic acidemia varies widely in the literature, ranging from 20.6% to 71.8% [7, 8]. Many studies have identified the causes of early-onset hyperlactatidemia, while investigations into delayed hyperlactatidemia have concentrated on earlier stages [9], which primarily concern valve replacement and myocardial revascularization, thus lacking focus on patients with ATAAD.

Subjects and methods

General information

This investigation encompassed a cohort of 337 patients diagnosed with acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) who were admitted to the Department of Cardiovascular Surgery at Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region People’s Hospital between January 2017 and December 2024, and subsequently transferred to the Department of Critical Care Medicine following surgical intervention. The mean age of the patient cohort was 53.31 ± 0.61 years, with a median body mass index of 25.25 (interquartile range: 23.44, 28.32) kg/m2. The median duration of surgery was 527 (468, 592) minutes, with a median cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time of 237 (131, 589) minutes, a median aortic cross-clamping time of 142 (118, 168) minutes, and a median duration of deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) of 30 (25, 37) minutes. The median intraoperative total blood loss was 1500 (1200, 2000) ml, with a median volume of red blood cell transfusion of 4 (4–8) units and a median plasma transfusion volume of 800 (800, 800) ml. Postoperatively, the median pericardial drainage over the first 12 h was 210 (100, 400) ml, while the median mediastinal drainage during the same period was 275 (150, 450) ml. Within the overall population, 246 (73%) were male, with ages ranging from 21 to 75 years (mean age: 51.50 ± 0.66 years), and 91 (27%) were female, aged between 33 and 78 years (mean age: 58.20 ± 1.16 years). Notably, 159 (47.2%) of the patients had a history of smoking, and 78 (23.1%) had a history of alcohol consumption. Surgical procedures included isolated Stanford A-type surgery in 237 cases (70.3%) and Stanford A-type surgery combined with valve surgery in 100 cases (29.7%). Preoperatively, 235 (69.7%) patients were diagnosed with hypertension, 24 (7.1%) had coronary heart disease, 83 (24.6%) presented with pericardial effusion, 244 (72.4%) were classified as NYHA functional class III or IV, 35 (10.4%) exhibited liver dysfunction (Child–Pugh classification A/B/C), and 25 (7.4%) had renal dysfunction (CKD stage III-V).

Inclusion criteria

Patients were included in the study if they were diagnosed with ATAAD via imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) angiography or echocardiography, had surgical indications, underwent surgical treatment, and possessed complete case data.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria comprised: ① Patients unable to undergo surgery due to either objective disease progression or subjective factors; ② Uncooperative researchers; ③ Patients who experienced mortality during surgery and did not remain in the intensive care unit postoperatively; ④ Patients with a history of malignant tumors or immunodeficiency disorders.

Research methods

A retrospective analysis was performed on the perioperative clinical data of 337 patients. In accordance with clinical practice and existing literature, hyperlactatemia was defined in this study as an arterial blood lactate level exceeding 4 mmol/L [10, 11]. The peak lactate value was recorded within the first 12 h following admission to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) to ascertain the occurrence of postoperative hyperlactatemia [12]. Patients were categorized into two groups based on whether their lactate levels surpassed 4 mmol/L: the non-hyperlactatemia group and the hyperlactatemia group. Preoperative data encompassed demographic information such as gender, age, and body mass index (BMI), as well as histories of smoking and alcohol consumption, relevant comorbidities, evaluations of cardiac, hepatic, and renal function, and various laboratory indicators including hemoglobin, white blood cell count, platelet count, albumin, creatinine, urea nitrogen, pH, partial pressures of carbon dioxide and oxygen, lactate, oxygenation index, NT-proBNP, Myo, cTnT, PCT, CRP, among others. Intraoperative data included surgical duration, aortic cross-clamping time, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA), blood loss, and the volume of transfused blood components. Postoperative data comprised drainage volume within 12 h post-surgery, duration of ICU stay, incidence of organ damage complications, and mortality during hospitalization. According to whether the postoperative stay in the ICU was longer than 7 days, the patients were divided into: ICU Prolonged Hospitalization Group and ICU Routine Hospitalization Group [13–15].

Evaluation indicators

The study aimed to identify factors influencing postoperative hyperlactatemia, generate Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves for predictive analysis, and compare the incidence of postoperative complications and mortality rates between the two patient groups.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 statistical software. Normality tests were performed on measurement data, with normally distributed data presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s). Comparisons between two groups were executed using the independent samples t-test. Data that did not conform to a normal distribution were expressed as median (M) with interquartile range (P25, P75), and group comparisons were made using the non-parametric rank sum test. Categorical data were reported as rates, with group comparisons conducted via the chi-square test. Variables demonstrating statistically significant differences in univariate analysis, in accordance with the principle of ensuring a sample size at least tenfold the number of predictor variables included, and excluding multicollinearity, were incorporated into a binary logistic regression analysis to identify independent risk factors for postoperative hyperlactatemia. ROC curves were constructed to facilitate the prediction of postoperative hyperlactatemia. The Delong test was employed to further evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of combined predictive indicators in comparison to individual indicators. Additionally, statistically significant differences in postoperative adverse outcomes between the two groups were analyzed. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. The average growth rate method was utilized to address a limited number of missing values.

Ethical principles

This study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region People’s Hospital (Approval Number: Ethical 2025-LL-014).

Multivariate analysis

Variables that exhibited statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in the univariate analysis of hyperlactatemia following aortic dissection surgery (ATAAD) were incorporated into the multivariate logistic regression analysis, as presented in Table 1. This analysis included potential risk factors and clinically relevant variables, adhering to the principle of independent variable selection (where the sample size was at least ten times the number of predictor variables included, and multicollinearity was addressed with a variance inflation factor (VIF) of less than 5). The dependent variable was defined as whether the peak lactate level within 12 h post-surgery exceeded 4 mmol/L. The final analysis identified several independent factors influencing postoperative hyperlactatemia in ATAAD surgery, including a history of hypertension, preoperative New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification, duration of surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA), all of which were statistically significant (P < 0.05). Discussion

Lactate levels are a dependable biomarker and a strong indicator of clinical outcomes for patients undergoing cardiac surgery and those in critical condition [16, 17]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that elevated lactate levels after surgery can predict mortality and significant complications [18–21]. Addressing hyperlactatemia can help reduce postoperative complications and mortality rates [22]. Thus, managing and preventing elevated lactate levels after surgery is essential for enhancing the prognosis of heart disease patients [23].

Serum lactate levels reflect the balance between its production, conversion, and clearance. Lactate can build up due to anaerobic metabolism during tissue hypoxia, increased aerobic metabolism through glycolysis, or a decreased rate of lactate conversion, such as when pyruvate dehydrogenase is inhibited or deficient.

The occurrence of hyperlactatemia after cardiac surgery or during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) varies significantly across studies, with rates reaching as high as 71.8% [12, 24]. In this study, 60.02% of patients with acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) experienced hyperlactatemia post-surgery, attributed to both hypoxic and non-hypoxic factors, including low perfusion, acute hypoxemia during CPB, hypothermia, and drug-induced metabolic dysfunction.

This research identified CPB as an independent factor influencing the development of hyperlactatemia after ATAAD surgery, consistent with previous findings [21, 25, 26]. The duration of CPB was notably longer in the two groups studied: 209 (194, 228) vs. 248 (218, 295), P = 0.000, which aligns with results from Algarni K.D. and Patmano G [11, 27]. This extended CPB time is considered a contributing factor to the increased incidence of postoperative hyperlactatemia. Worrell et al. noted that when extracorporeal circulation exceeds 2.5 h, it correlates with higher peak lactate levels in the ICU [28]. Evans et al. reported that the likelihood of developing postoperative hyperlactatemia increases by 5.8 times if extracorporeal circulation lasts more than 3 h [7]. The relationship between extracorporeal circulation and postoperative hyperlactatemia is likely complex, as the process exposes patients to non-physiological conditions. The interaction of blood components with artificial vessels and oxygenators can trigger inflammatory responses [29, 30], while oxygen free radicals and reperfusion injuries can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in lactate accumulation [31, 32]. Elevated lactate levels are associated with heightened inflammatory responses after CPB, which can include microvascular thrombosis, further increasing lactate levels post-surgery [33]. Microcirculatory hypoperfusion can also contribute to elevated lactate levels, and prolonged CPB is likely to worsen this issue. If lactate levels continue to rise after surgery despite various interventions, it may indicate that the tissue-level imbalance of substrate supply and demand initiated during CPB persists into the postoperative period. Therefore, minimizing CPB duration whenever possible and actively correcting hyperlactatemia is crucial. Additionally, employing pulsatile flow during CPB has been shown to improve both intraoperative and postoperative hyperlactatemia while reducing the need for positive inotropic medications [34].

In this investigation, the duration of surgical procedures emerged as an independent factor influencing the occurrence of postoperative hyperlactatemia. This phenomenon can be attributed to the correlation between extended surgical times and the heightened risk of systemic end-organ hypoperfusion, which can precipitate anaerobic metabolic conditions, elevate glycolytic activity, and subsequently increase lactate production [11]. It may also exacerbate stress, leading to the production of large amounts of endogenous catecholamines. Evidence suggests that catecholamine stimulated aerobic glycolysis is the primary cause of hyperlactatemia in sepsis [35].

Moreover, during the perioperative phase of acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD), the prevalence and severity of low cardiac output syndrome are notable; vasopressors are frequently administered to manage this condition, which may further enhance muscle glycolysis and glycogenolysis, thereby facilitating lactate accumulation. Research has identified the administration of perioperative vasopressors as an independent predictor of lactic acidosis [36, 37]. Additional studies have demonstrated a correlation between the total dosage of inotropic agents utilized during surgery and elevated lactate concentrations following extracorporeal circulation [38].

Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) is utilized in cardiac and aortic arch surgeries to safeguard cerebral and organ integrity [39]. During DHCA, systemic blood flow is halted, leading to ischemia and hypoxia, which can result in cellular injury and metabolic disturbances. In the present study, the duration of DHCA was identified as an independent factor influencing postoperative hyperlactatemia in patients with ATAAD. A comparative analysis of DHCA and warm regional cerebral perfusion (TRCP) revealed that the DHCA cohort exhibited significantly elevated lactate levels prior to aortic cross-clamping, suggesting microcirculatory impairment associated with the deep cooling process. Furthermore, during the reperfusion phase following DHCA, lactate levels were observed to rise exponentially, indicating that the extended interruption of circulation resulted in a critical deficiency of oxygen supply to the organs responsible for lactate metabolism [40].

The statistical results of this investigation indicate that patients with a history of hypertension and compromised preoperative cardiac function exhibit a heightened likelihood of developing hyperlactatemia post-surgery, a relationship that has not been documented in previous research.

Hypertension is recognized as a significant risk factor for the onset of acute aortic dissection (AD), with prevalence rates ranging from 65 to 75% among AD patients [41]. Individuals with hypertension are approximately three times more likely to develop AD compared to those without this condition [42]. The presence of hypertension can compromise the integrity of the aortic wall, rendering it more susceptible to rupture [43]. Specifically, the ascending aorta is particularly vulnerable to dissection or infected aneurysms as a consequence of hypertensive effects [44]. While surgical intervention remains the primary therapeutic approach for acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD), adjunctive pharmacological therapy is crucial in mitigating the progression and rupture of aneurysms, as well as enhancing long-term survival outcomes [45]. Hypertension may precipitate dissection rupture, whereas hypotension can adversely affect blood supply to vital organs. The objective of blood pressure management in patients with AD is to minimize wall shear stress on the aortic wall to avert further tearing and aneurysm formation, while simultaneously ensuring adequate organ perfusion.

However, the existing medical literature has not definitively established the optimal target blood pressure for patients with acute aortic dissection during long-term follow-up, and current clinical guidelines have yet to achieve consensus on this matter. Additionally, data regarding the long-term outcomes of AD patients in relation to specific blood pressure targets remain scarce. Nonetheless, research has demonstrated that a systolic blood pressure (SBP) of less than 130 mmHg at discharge correlates with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality [46]. Evidence indicates that maintaining blood pressure below 120/80 mmHg during conservative management is advantageous for patient prognosis [47]. For patients experiencing rupture of acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) and hemodynamic instability, a lower SBP range of 80–100 mmHg, known as permissive hypotension, is typically maintained [48]. A review of prior studies reveals that some did not specifically isolate hypertensive patients; among those that did, the levels of blood pressure control varied significantly. Clinicians often adopt a conservative approach to medication in the context of hypotension, while exhibiting an excessive antihypertensive response in cases of hypertension. Consequently, we hypothesize that for postoperative ATAAD patients with a documented history of hypertension, the safe blood pressure levels achieved through pharmacological intervention in clinical practice may be inadequate, potentially leading to insufficient tissue perfusion and increased lactate production. Therefore, timely screening and diagnosis of hypertension, along with the establishment of individualized target blood pressure values, are of paramount importance for the prevention, management, and prognosis of acute aortic dissection.

In the context of complex aortic surgeries, the assessment of preoperative cardiac risk is essential and constitutes a critical component of perioperative management. Our investigation revealed that patients with acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) exhibiting New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class III–IV prior to surgery demonstrated a significantly elevated risk of developing hyperlactatemia postoperatively, in contrast to those classified as NYHA class I–II. Furthermore, an increase in postoperative lactate levels is frequently indicative of poor prognostic outcomes, a finding corroborated by this study. A randomized controlled trial focusing on preoperative cardiopulmonary rehabilitation for inpatients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting indicated that such rehabilitation significantly diminished the incidence of postoperative complications and reduced the duration of hospital stays [49]. Patients classified as NYHA class III–IV typically experience considerable limitations in physical activity, which in the case of ATAAD patients, is often compounded by the presence of cardiogenic shock, a recognized predictor of early mortality [50]. This phenomenon may be attributed to concurrent coronary atherosclerosis or extensive dissection that can lead to aortic sinus rupture, resulting in prolonged surgical duration and increased surgical complexity. Prior research has identified high-risk cardiac patients, particularly those with preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) below 35%, as being more susceptible to low cardiac output syndrome, necessitating heightened vigilance during the perioperative phase [51]. A study involving patients with acute heart failure established that even in the absence of overt clinical signs of poor perfusion, elevated blood lactate levels are frequently observed, correlating with increased biomarkers indicative of dysfunction or damage to the liver, myocardium, kidneys, and vascular constriction, ultimately contributing to higher mortality rates [52]. In the present study, we established that a more severe preoperative NYHA heart function classification correlates with a greater likelihood of postoperative hyperlactatemia. The analysis indicates that when patients exhibit significantly diminished exercise tolerance prior to surgery, irrespective of the presence of shock, both cardiac pump function and reserve are already compromised, thereby heightening the risk of postoperative low cardiac output syndrome. This disruption in the balance between lactate production and clearance results in diminished mitochondrial oxygen delivery, augmented lactate synthesis, and the onset of hyperlactatemia.

The literature suggests that elevated lactate levels in critically ill patients are associated with severe circulatory and multi-organ dysfunction. In our study, the incidence of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and in-hospital mortality rates were significantly higher in the hyperlactatemia cohort compared to the non-hyperlactatemia group [49]. Moreover, studies indicate that shorter durations of bodily ischemia should be recognized as modifiable surgical risk factors for postoperative AKI, with renal medullary ischemia or reperfusion injury representing the most critical pathophysiological changes leading to postoperative AKI [53]. It is worth noting that, although univariate analysis revealed differences in preoperative liver function (AST, ALT) and renal function (eGFR) indicators between the two groups, these indicators did not become independent predictors in multivariate logistic regression. This phenomenon may suggest that intraoperative management factors (such as CPB, DHCA, etc.) have a greater weight on lactate levels, which to some extent masks the role of preoperative organ function; on the other hand, the liver and kidneys, as important organs of lactate metabolism, may still have a certain compensatory capacity under stress, while intraoperative ischemia–reperfusion injury and inflammatory response more directly affect the clearance and production of lactate. In addition, the inclusion of eGFR as a more comprehensive indicator of renal function enhances the credibility of the results, but its insignificance in the multivariate model also suggests that the formation of postoperative hyperlactic acidemia is the result of a combination of factors, rather than a single organ function can be fully explained.

And although there were differences between groups in preoperative baseline renal function (as assessed by eGFR), there was no significant difference in the peak intraoperative lactate between the two groups, suggesting that the difference in preoperative renal function may not directly affect postoperative hyperlacidemia through the intraoperative lactate metabolism pathway, but may be related to the overall organ reserve function and the chain reaction of postoperative complications. In addition, the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) was significantly higher in patients with extended ICU stay (≥ 7 days), and the main cause of death was sepsis, followed by MACE, suggesting that postoperative infections and cardiovascular events together constitute the main burden of the severe course of disease, which is particularly significant in patients admitted to the long-term ICU.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, selection bias and information bias cannot be avoided. Preoperative scores such as EuroScore or STS scores were not included due to missing data, limiting our overall assessment of patients’ baseline risk. Second, some indicators such as preoperative lactate and C-reactive protein (CRP) were not analyzed due to lack of data, which may have affected the comprehensiveness of the results. In addition, all patients adopted the DHCA strategy, which was not compared with other brain protection strategies such as room temperature regional cerebral perfusion (TRCP), limiting the generality of the conclusions. In the future, multicenter and prospective studies are needed to integrate more dimensions of perioperative indicators, and combine new biomarkers and dynamic assessment methods of organ function to more comprehensively reveal the pathophysiological mechanism and clinical impact of postoperative hyperlacidemia.

Our study is subject to several limitations. As an observational, single-center, retrospective analysis, it is inherently vulnerable to selection and information biases. Nevertheless, the prospective nature of data collection enhances the internal validity of our findings. Furthermore, existing literature indicates that elevated blood lactate levels are not uncommon among patients undergoing cardiac surgery and are typically well-tolerated. Consequently, the predictive capacity of increased postoperative blood lactate levels for adverse patient outcomes may be constrained. The integration of dynamic lactate metrics, such as lactate clearance rate, could potentially improve the prediction of morbidity and mortality following cardiac surgery. Lastly, our study focused solely on short-term outcomes during the hospital stay, leaving the potential long-term implications of these findings uncertain.

Conclusions

The incidence of postoperative hyperlactatemia in patients diagnosed with acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is contingent upon a variety of factors, including age, pre-existing comorbidities, preoperative organ function, duration of surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time, and the duration of deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA). Collectively, these factors exhibit significant predictive capability regarding the occurrence of postoperative hyperlactatemia in this patient population. Furthermore, postoperative hyperlactatemia is linked to a range of adverse clinical outcomes.

In light of these influencing factors, healthcare practitioners are encouraged to formulate individualized management strategies, perform thorough early assessments of patients, and conduct risk screenings to enhance postoperative recovery, mitigate complications, and improve both survival rates and quality of life. Future large-scale clinical investigations are warranted to elucidate the relationships among these factors and to provide more definitive clinical recommendations.
