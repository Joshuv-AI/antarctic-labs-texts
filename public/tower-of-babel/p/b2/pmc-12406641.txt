Patient Perspectives on What Alleviates Hoarding Disorder Symptoms and Improves Wellbeing: A Systematic Review of Qualitative Data
Authors: Margaret Jones, Benjamin Weir, Keong Yap
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHoarding disorder is a debilitating psychological disorder that is difficult to treat. Poor treatment response to current interventions indicates that new perspectives are needed. This systematic review examines qualitative and case study research on patient‐centred strategies for reducing hoarding disorder symptoms and improving wellbeing. We conducted a comprehensive search of PsycInfo, Medline Complete, CINAHL, SocINDEX and Scopus in May 2025 to identify peer‐reviewed qualitative research and case studies, published in English that examined hoarding disorder patients' first‐hand experiences with strategies that improved their symptoms and wellbeing. We excluded papers that examined animal or digital hoarding, hoarding in the context of another disorder, hoarding in children or animals and papers with perspectives from only service providers or carers. The quality of studies was assessed using the Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research Studies. Results were synthesized using thematic analysis. Twenty‐one studies were included for review. Thematic analysis identified five key themes: interconnection, recognition of individuality and autonomy, self‐care and acceptance, cognitive and behaviour change, and practical help. The papers included descriptions of therapeutic strategies (e.g., discarding tasks) and other factors identified as beneficial (e.g., meditation). Interconnection was the strongest theme, emphasizing the pivotal role trusted relationships play in helping individuals with hoarding disorder. The review indicates that individuals with HD may benefit from personalized care that focuses on interpersonal relationships, emotion regulation strategies, self‐care, and in‐home help for managing their symptoms and improving wellbeing.Keywords: hoarding disorder, interconnection, patient perspective, qualitative, symptom reduction, wellbeing

Introduction

Hoarding disorder (HD) is a complex mental health condition involving excessive accumulation of possessions, difficulty discarding items, and cluttered living environments, which result in significant impairment to daily functioning (American Psychiatric Association (APA) 2022). Although originally conceptualized as a symptom subtype of obsessive‐compulsive disorder, it is now recognized as distinct from OCD (Gordon et al. 2013; APA 2022). People with HD feel an intense need to save items and experience high levels of distress at the thought of giving up possessions, regardless of their utility or worth. They place greater significance on their belongings than the general population (Tolin et al. 2014). Not wanting to be wasteful, seeing beauty and potential in items, associating objects with memories and finding comfort in possessions that are considered more like friends than objects are common themes among this population, resulting in a strong connection between possessions and self‐identity (Moulding et al. 2021; Tolin et al. 2014; Yap and Grisham 2021). This hoarding behaviour results in cluttered, hazardous and unhygienic living circumstances, strains family relationships, has financial repercussions, endangers personal health, and restricts social connections (APA 2022; Taylor et al. 2019).

There are several treatments for hoarding disorder with cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) being the most well‐researched intervention (O'Brien and Laws 2025). CBT for HD is based on Frost and Hartl's (1996) cognitive behavioural model, which posits that hoarding is multifaceted, and due to information processing deficits, difficulties with emotional attachments, avoidance behaviour, and unhelpful beliefs regarding the nature of possessions. CBT for HD (Steketee and Frost 2007, 2014) addresses all aspects of the Frost and Hartl (1996) model; problem‐solving skills and strategies enhance information processing for sorting and organizing possessions, cognitive techniques test unsubstantiated beliefs, thereby decreasing feelings of attachment to objects, and motivational interviewing and exposure techniques reduce avoidance and promote behaviour change by addressing avoidance (Grisham et al. 2023).

CBT for HD has shown some success in treating HD, with randomized controlled trials showing treatment is efficacious and decreases HD symptoms in patients by approximately 25% on average (David et al. 2022; Williams and Viscusi 2016). However, less than one‐third of patients who complete treatment achieve clinically significant change, and approximately one‐third of patients drop out before completion (David et al. 2022). The poor treatment response rates indicate that the treatments based on the cognitive behavioural model may need revision. Older adults with HD have reported in qualitative feedback that cognitive formulations were difficult to recall and that cognitive strategies were too complex and difficult to implement at home (Ayers et al. 2012). In addition, some HD patients who did not respond to treatment noted that CBT for HD did not adequately address underlying emotional issues and their difficulties with emotion regulation (Chou et al. 2019).

In the same way that the CBT model has been adapted for specific types of problems, such as in dialectical behaviour therapy for difficulties with emotional and behavioural dysregulation, it may be that current CBT for HD needs to be better tailored to individual presentations, with different components highlighted in treatment. A range HD interventions involving adjunctive components or modifications to CBT‐based interventions have been examined including contingency management (Worden et al. 2017), cognitive rehabilitation (Ayers et al. 2012), emotion regulation‐enhanced group therapy (Grisham et al. 2022) and social cognition training (Chen et al. 2023). Other stand‐alone interventions have also been evaluated such as compassion focused therapy (Chou et al. 2019), motivational interviewing (Wong et al. 2023; Dozier and Nix 2024) and volunteer support (Noyes et al. 2024; Ryninks et al. 2019). While the outcomes of these interventions are promising, they have not been extensively trialled and the extent to which they improve treatment outcomes beyond CBT is unclear (O'Brien and Laws 2025). Pharmacotherapy for hoarding disorder has also been used, but empirical support is lacking due to nonblind study designs and small sample sizes (Piacentino et al. 2019). Taken together, new perspectives, particularly from patients, are needed to enhance treatments for HD.

Patient Perspectives

The focus on the patient perspective is important because patients and therapists can have different perspectives of what works. Broadly speaking, healthcare professionals and patients have distinct views on the experience of illness. In general, healthcare professionals concentrate on diagnosis and therapy, while patients are more concerned with the way their illness affects their everyday lives (Nunstedt et al. 2017). Previous qualitative research indicates a need for healthcare providers to personalize information to the needs of each patient, thereby enabling the patient to apply the knowledge to their circumstances and obtain a deeper understanding of their condition. Therefore, attempts to incorporate patient perspectives as well as provider perspectives will provide the best and most acceptable overall care (Nunstedt et al. 2017).

Of note is Ayers et al. (2012) study of therapists' and patients' perspectives on the effectiveness of CBT for older adults with HD. Both therapists and patients indicated that psychoeducation, exposure techniques and motivational interviewing were helpful elements of CBT. However, there were differences in perspectives between the two groups. Therapists focused on reducing hoarding behaviours, whereas patients emphasized improving their quality of life and maintaining independence. Therapists favoured structured and systematic approaches to CBT, whereas patients stressed the need for flexibility and personalization in treatment plans. Patients cited social stigma and emotional attachments to possessions as significant barriers to treatment success, while therapists focused on cognitive and behavioural barriers. Both groups agreed on the importance of motivation for treatment success, with patients indicating a need for ongoing motivation and support throughout the treatment process. Ayers et al.'s (2012) study shows the importance of considering the patient's perspectives in addition to the therapist's perspectives, as they may have different goals that need to be addressed.

There is a growing body of qualitative and mixed‐methods studies exploring patient perspectives of HD treatments, which have identified aspects of interventions that lead to positive outcomes. Some studies have noted specific treatment components that were helpful such as exposure to discarding, thought listing and home visits (Ayers et al. 2012; Bratiotis et al. 2019). Several have also highlighted the importance of non‐specific factors such as the therapeutic relationship and peer support (Chandler et al. 2019; Davidson et al. 2021; Ivanov et al. 2018). By exploring patient perspectives beyond standardized tests and questionnaires, these qualitative studies might uncover subtle nuances and generate new avenues of research. However, findings remain dispersed and context‐dependent, and no comprehensive synthesis has been undertaken to clarify what patients perceive as most helpful.

Aims and Objectives

Obtaining the perspective of patients with HD, addressing thoughts, concerns and personal obstacles, listening to what improves symptoms and generating individualized solutions are essential components of successful treatment (Nunstedt et al. 2017). The aim of this systematic review is therefore to synthesize patient perspectives on strategies for managing HD symptoms and improving wellbeing. A patient‐centred approach to identifying what works for people with HD may inform the development of more effective and individualized treatments for those living with this challenging condition.

This review will integrate the available qualitative evidence and highlight common themes across studies on what patients perceive as helpful in alleviating HD symptoms and improving wellbeing. In addition, it will identify gaps in current knowledge, clinical practice implications and avenues for future research.

Method

This systematic review used the Preferred Reporting Items for the Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) statement as a guide (Page et al. 2021). Due to the limited number of qualitative studies examining patient perspectives in the literature on what alleviates hoarding symptoms and increases wellbeing in HD, we included as many types of studies capturing qualitative data as possible (e.g., mixed‐methods, case studies).

Eligibility Criteria

We identified papers that explored strategies that alleviated symptoms or increased wellbeing from HD patients' perspectives. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies that explored participants discussing factors that helped hoarding symptoms or the consequences of hoarding or improved their wellbeing; (2) papers that included qualitative data, including mixed method designs, case study or case series outlining HD patients' perspective; (3) studies that focused on hoarding behaviours of objects; and (4) studies published in English and were peer reviewed. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies concerned with elements of hoarding within another disorder (e.g., OCD, ADHD); (2) studies concerning only the views of the service providers or carers, e.g., psychologists and no patient views; (3) studies not concerned with hoarding in the human population; (4) studies investigating animal or digital hoarding; and (5) studies involving hoarding in children (below 18 years old).

Information Sources

The initial search of the databases PsycInfo, Medline Complete, CINAHL, SocINDEX, and Scopus was conducted in May 2022 as a scoping exercise to identify eligible papers. After this initial scoping exercise, we decided to include case studies and case series to collect more qualitative data. We updated the search in May 2025, with no restrictions on the publication date and the only limiters being that papers were in English and in peer‐reviewed academic journals. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied during the abstract and title screening and in the full text screen. In addition, reference lists were examined from studies in this review and the initial database search for further eligible studies.

Study Selection

The selection of studies for the review was twofold: firstly, to explore the extent of the qualitative literature on HD and identify the literature review parameters, including gaps or uncertainties (Armstrong et al. 2011); and secondly, to ensure that all possibilities for improving symptoms from the perspective of patients with HD were given due notice and selected. This meant that studies of treatment effectiveness and of HD patient perspectives on hoarding were of interest. Initially for the scoping exercise, the inclusion criteria included family and caregiver perspectives; however, the family and caregivers of individuals with HD have different challenges. Therefore, this criterion was removed for the final search, as family and caregiver perspectives were mainly concerned with improving their wellbeing, not the individual experiencing HD (Wilbram et al. 2008).

Data Collection

From the studies selected, the primary author independently collected data from each report; this data were cross‐checked by the second author. Relevant data were collected from the following domains: author and year of publication, country of origin, sample and sample size, participants' mean age, sex or gender and study design. On some occasions, the main findings of some studies were irrelevant to this systematic review. For these studies, we only collected data on the relevant qualitative findings. Most importantly, data were collected explicitly on any discourse reported from a patient perspective on what worked to alleviate hoarding symptoms or improve patient wellbeing. Discussions were ongoing among authors through the data extraction process to clarify any grey areas and resolve any differences of opinion.

Quality Assessment

The quality of the studies was assessed by the first author using the Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research Studies (Treloar et al. 2000, see Supporting Information). The checklist outlines 10 criteria spanning each stage of the research process (e.g., purpose, rationale). Each criterion received 1 point for being sufficiently described, 1/2 point for being insufficient and 0 point for not adequately addressing the criterion. Higher scores indicated a better quality of study with a maximum score of 10. This checklist was selected as it synthesizes guidelines for best practice in qualitative research and covers similar ground to other tools such as the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme qualitative studies checklist (CASP 2024).

Approach to Synthesis

Reviews of qualitative literature seek to develop new cumulative knowledge based on qualitative research studies and can be used when seeking to understand individual experiences of interventions (Flemming and Noyes 2021). The data were synthesized using inductive thematic analysis, as Braun and Clarke (2006) outlined. This analysis is widely employed in psychology as an efficient and effective method for analysing qualitative data. Furthermore, thematic analysis was chosen due to its adaptability in dealing with various studies and data to produce a coherent representation of the data's themes. This approach is similar to the one modelled by Thomas and Harden (2008), who make the case for adapting thematic analysis and applying it to reviews of qualitative studies to build a thematic synthesis. This occurs when data in the form of participant content and researchers' findings in each report are integrated and synthesized in such a way as go beyond the original studies to build a conceptual, theory‐driven interpretation of the data. To achieve this, the results sections of the eligible papers were coded line‐by‐line, including participant quotations and researcher explanations. The codes were further developed by grouping them into similar categories of content and meaning. Using an inductive approach, themes and subthemes were derived from these groupings. The coding and theme development process is iterative and involves going back and forth between the data and emerging themes.

Results

Figure 1 is a visual summary of the study selection process completed in the online review tool Covidence (www.covidence.org), which included the final and all previous searches. The final search was conducted in May 2025. All searches produced 2733 articles and 1806 duplicates were excluded. The remaining 927 articles were assessed for inclusion using a two‐step procedures: (1) screening of title and abstract and (2) screening of the full text. In stage one, the primary author independently reviewed the title and abstracts using the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 123 articles were found to be relevant. In stage two, three authors reviewed the complete texts using all criteria listed; this resulted in removal of 102 studies leaving 21 for the review. Reviewers met regularly through the screening process to discuss discrepancies and reach consensus on studies suitable for inclusion in the final review. The first and third authors independently reviewed 104 papers and obtained a Cohen's kappa of 0.75, while the second and third authors independently reviewed 19 papers with a kappa of 0.68, reflecting substantial agreement.

Themes

After generating codes, five themes were identified from the 21 studies, (1) interconnection, (2) recognition of individuality and autonomy, (3) self‐care and acceptance, (4) cognitive and behaviour change and (5) practical help. Figure 2 shows the five themes with corresponding subthemes. Table 4 shows the themes and subthemes with corresponding patient quotes and/or author explanations expressed throughout the studies. Table 4 is followed by a synopsis of the five themes.

VisualizationLucy admitted that usually she would walk through the hall and ignore the clutter. We completed a visualization task where initially Lucy was asked to look at the clutter and talk through how it made her feel. She disclosed feeling guilty and thinking ‘I should not have this mess’ as it was upsetting for her husband. To build motivation, Lucy was then asked to look at the corner and visualize it as being tidy and organized, which made her feel ‘happy’ (Farquhar and Caiazza 2020).Talking through the process Lucy felt that a CBT approach had been helpful, and she had now reached ‘a turning point’ in how she addressed her belongings. She identified that the most helpful thing had been talking through the process of decluttering (Farquhar and Caiazza 2020). She was able to make a decision quickly after talking through her thoughts and decided she would use up the film later that day when her grandchildren came over (Farquhar and Caiazza 2020). Reappraisal and restructuring ‘I might say well I have not got cancer; you know there's worse off than me’ (Taylor et al. 2019). ‘Just start small and then … I'll often use a sort of analogy … it's the farmer who instead of saying to his wife in the morning “I'm going out to plough that field, the north paddock” says, simply says “I'm going out to plough” so he does not burden himself with an end result … I'll often say that to myself, just go out and plough’ (Taylor et al. 2019). Exposure tasks The most helpful part of all the therapy was direct exposure exercises (10 patients—felt accomplished; 3 patients—were surprised how well they coped) (Ayers et al. 2012). All patients in the focus group noted that a combination of office and home‐based exposure exercises to be very helpful (Ayers et al. 2012). In reference to imaginal exposure task: ‘I think this is a very valuable exercise’ ( Fracalanza et al. 2021 ). Practical helpHome visits and in home help Cleaning and removal service—‘I would love to have help cleaning, decluttering and organizing my home’ (Rodriguez et al. 2016). On remaining in his own home one male patient noted ‘it's the motivation of having someone there plus…the helper doing the heavy lifting and heavy carrying…making..0.76 thousand trips to the garbage bin…it's stressful but helpful[and] I certainly would not have been able to hire a company on my own […] if the programme had not been in effect [and] I would be in deep do‐do with Capital Health’ (Whitfield et al. 2012). Through the course of the home visit decluttering sessions, the language became increasingly positive for some participants (later sessions): (a) ‘it was painful at first but became bearable after a while’ (b) ‘lots more to do, but feel like real progress’ (c) ‘my success this week has been maintaining the uncluttering’ ( Linkovski et al. 2018 ). Create a plan/have a goal Home visits were useful to set goals and plan. Exposure exercises were generalizable in the home (n = 10) (Ayers et al. 2012). On working with the collaborative on personalized goals ‘We set a plan and the social worker would come back and generally we would accomplish that goal whatever it was’. ‘They sort of mediate [between varying agencies] and rub off the sharp corners’ (Whitfield et al. 2012). Able to set my own realistic goals and act on it (Spence et al. 2019). Organization skills Most participants found using a calendar, making a to‐do list, linking tasks with places, problem‐solving, practicing sorting and preventing relapse modules to be somewhat helpful. The most common and consistently rated modules were using a calendar, making to do‐lists and practicing sorting (Davidson et al. 2021). We compiled a small list of questions that would help Lucy to make decisions about what to do with the items, such as ‘do I want it? Do I need it?’ Items would then be placed into a bag of things to keep, and a bag to donate to charity. Lucy was encouraged to treat this like an experiment, noting down what had gone well and what was more difficult (Farquhar and Caiazza 2020). Keep it simple Online support group—‘easier to attend with a busy schedule’ (Rodriguez et al. 2016). Lucy felt that creating a specific to‐do list for each day would help her to achieve these goals (Farquhar and Caiazza 2020). Most patients found this to be the case—Complicated cognitive restructuring (cognitive strategies and case formulation) was unhelpful—simple concrete tasks were better (Ayers et al. 2012). Technology aids Overall COMMIT (computer programme allowing therapist contact between sessions) was perceived to be easy to learn: The most common strength reported was receiving motivation and support from the therapist (Ivanov et al. 2018). Meditation or relaxation was used with CD or Phone (n = 4) (Taylor et al. 2019).

Interconnection

Overall interconnection with others was the dominant theme, appearing in 17 of the 21 studies. The therapeutic relationship highlighted interpersonal connection, where patients valued, enjoyed and at times seemed grateful for the opportunity to have such a caring, empathetic, and nonjudgmental relationship (Ayers et al. 2012; Davidson et al. 2021). Similarly, group therapy provided a space for individuals with HD to connect with others who were facing similar challenges and to receive social support (Chandler et al. 2019; McGrath et al. 2024; Noyes et al. 2024; Orr et al. 2019). Family and friends were also a source of happiness for participants in instances where the relationships remained intact (Taylor et al. 2019). Volunteers who came into the home to assist with decluttering were welcomed. Relationships with volunteers grew more substantial because it was flexible, and there were no time constraints on how long the volunteers could interact with them. The nonprofessional status of the volunteers was perceived to be nonthreatening, enabling participants to develop strong connections with them (Ryninks et al. 2019).

Furthermore, the recognition of hoarding as a mental health issue was a subtheme that fostered interconnection. Patients felt reassured and relieved to know that they were not alone in their struggles and others shared their experiences (Orr et al. 2019). Media and technology, such as television, also played a role in interconnection as they provided information about HD and its treatment options and connected individuals with shared experiences (Orr et al. 2019). Additionally, individuals with HD expressed a craving for social and interpersonal connection, seen through collections of objects to give to others or collections of newspapers and books to stay connected as their social spheres diminished over time (Andersen et al. 2008; Bratiotis et al. 2019).

Recognition of Individuality and Autonomy

The recognition of individuality and autonomy was a significant theme that emerged from 12 of the studies. Participants stressed the need for personalized care programmes that considered their unique experiences, beliefs and values. Each individual had a different cause for their hoarding behaviours and attached different meanings to their hoard, and it was crucial for participants to explain to others the rationale behind their hoarding. As a result, personalized care programmes were well‐received once the therapist established a trusting relationship (Orr et al. 2019; Rodriguez et al. 2016).

Many participants viewed themselves as rational, at times stoic and able to make compelling arguments for why they should keep their items. They resented relinquishing control over what to do with their possessions (Orr et al. 2019) and were more receptive to interventions that put them in control (Rodriguez et al. 2016; Ryninks et al. 2019).

Privacy was another important aspect of recognizing individuality and autonomy. For example, some participants preferred using the self‐help book to go through in the privacy of their own homes and at their own pace; this saved them the embarrassment of others seeing their living circumstances (Rodriguez et al. 2016).

Respect for individual values and beliefs was apparent in some of the studies. Participants had strong values concerning the reuse and recycling of items and wanted to be granted autonomy to reflect those values. Whether the reasons for keeping items were to give to someone else (interconnection), resist social pressure to ‘throw away’ (control) or even the idea of hope for future recreational or practical benefit, the theme was evident, and participants were proud of their commitment to recycling (Andersen et al. 2008; Bratiotis et al. 2019).

The studies also showed that gaining insight was a highly individual process influenced by various factors. Some individuals gained insight when they were given time and established good relationships with others, such as therapists or volunteers (Ryninks et al. 2019). In addition, some participants showed insight as they already had hierarchies for determining the worth of things; however, they were not deemed socially acceptable to others. Nonetheless, participants perceived any gain of insight as a positive thing, indicating their desire for personal growth and development (Farquhar and Caiazza 2020; Orr et al. 2019). In one recent study, participants reported that understanding the root causes of their hoarding difficulties was preferable to a focus on decluttering (Ruby‐Granger et al. 2023). Differing levels of insight highlight the importance of recognizing and respecting the autonomy of individuals with HD, as they may be able to make meaningful choices and pursue their own goals despite their challenges.

Self‐Care and Acceptance

A theme relating to participants use of self‐care and acceptance of their difficulties was found in six studies. Acceptance‐based strategies assisted in managing difficult emotions, and therapy that was focused on compassion was beneficial (Chou et al. 2019; Taylor et al. 2019). Emotion regulation tools benefited self‐perception and helped participants soothe themselves, giving them a better perspective on their troubled minds. Self‐compassion reduced feelings of guilt and increased self‐worth (Chou et al. 2019). In one patient, religious faith helped regulate emotions and brought inner peace and positive feelings (Taylor et al. 2019). Belief in treatments, whether pharmaceutical or personalized care, empowered patients and gave them hope for the future (Rodriguez et al. 2016). Relaxation and recreational strategies helped boost positive emotions and regulated difficult ones. Meditation, walking, yoga, holidaying, and creative pursuits such as dancing and drawing were strategies used to enhance mood (Farquhar and Caiazza 2020; Taylor et al. 2019). Additionally, interests and hobbies, like reading, gardening, and collecting provided patients with a sense of purpose and pleasure (Andersen et al. 2008; Taylor et al. 2019). Compassion‐focused therapy (CFT) was accepted as an effective adjunct to CBT, and both therapies were beneficial to patients, with CFT focusing more on the patients' inner world (Chou et al. 2019). Overall, the findings highlight the importance of incorporating self‐care and acceptance strategies, along with traditional therapy approaches like CBT in the treatment of HD.

Cognitive and Behaviour Change

Participants' use of strategies from cognitive‐behavioural approaches was found in 10 studies. Although some individuals with HD reported that cognitive strategies were unhelpful (Ayers et al. 2012), several reported that modifying cognitions about possessions made discarding more manageable. For instance, if an object was deemed unimportant or if individuals had forgotten they owned it, discarding became easier, was associated with relief and did not trigger guilt or remorse. Instead, the emphasis was on the positives of producing more space and clearing the clutter (Bratiotis et al. 2019).

Specific cognitive strategies that were helpful included visualization tasks, which aided in seeing the possibilities of an uncluttered space and triggered feelings of happiness (Farquhar and Caiazza 2020). Written imaginal exposure, thought listing, and talking through the process of decluttering and discarding were also helpful and enabled participants to evaluate their surroundings and objects and pinpoint emotions attached to them, making them easier to discard (Bratiotis et al. 2019; Fracalanza et al. 2021). Cognitive reappraisal and restructuring techniques assisted participants in gaining a new perspective on their situations, and pressure was alleviated when tasks were simplified, and expectations lowered (Taylor et al. 2019). Performing a cost–benefit analysis and weighing the pros and cons resulted in more prudent decisions, such as allowing interventions or deciding to exercise (Farquhar and Caiazza 2020). Exposure tasks were also helpful and associated with feelings of accomplishment and surprise at what was achieved. A combination of exposure tasks in therapy and at participant homes was thought to be most beneficial (Ayers et al. 2012).

Practical Help

Practical help with the challenges of hoarding were found in eight studies. Home visits assisted in implementing appropriate plans and establishing goals to address individual circumstances. Almost all cognitive‐based therapies include planning and goal setting, essential to address the somewhat overwhelming task ahead (Ayers et al. 2012); however, goal setting was easier to establish when this was done in the home. Additionally, practical home assistance such as waste removal and cleaning services were viewed favourably (Ayers et al. 2012; Rodriguez et al. 2016). Without in‐home assistance, decluttering is essentially impossible for elderly people, who frequently suffer from physical impediments. Overall motivation improved when the task at hand was shared with others (Whitfield et al. 2012). Many strategies for reducing and managing clutter were instigated or managed by family members (although this was not always welcomed) (Subramaniam et al. 2020).

Help with organization skills was beneficial and included simple measures such as using a calendar, to‐do lists, practicing sorting and problem‐solving (Davidson et al. 2021). Accessible and adaptable therapy, such as an online support group, helped some patients accommodate busy lifestyles (Rodriguez et al. 2016). It is important to note that simple concrete tasks were often more effective than complicated cognitive restructuring strategies (Ayers et al. 2012). The use of technology was also viewed as helpful, with some participants using phones and compact discs to access meditation or relaxation programmes. The use of computer platforms to supplement therapy sessions facilitated access to therapists, increased feelings of motivation and support and improved therapy retention rates (Ivanov et al. 2018).

Although not a theme, collaboration among health professionals appeared to be beneficial for both participants and staff in Whitfield et al.'s (2012) study on a collaborative community‐based care initiative. The ability for staff members to access expertise from other members of the group helped them to maximize their own expertise and enhanced their knowledge of hoarding behaviours. The patients found the offering of options in a collaborative model both convenient and helpful and were appreciative that staff could mediate between agencies on their behalf. A similar sentiment was expressed in French et al.'s (2022) study where participants advocated for an improved sharing of information between professionals and closer multiagency workings. In addition French et al.'s (2022) study called for more investment in hoarding support and more accessibility to services, with a need to raise awareness of hoarding and a reduction in stigma and judgement from both professionals and society in general.

Discussion

It is essential to include the patient's perspective in clinical research to gain knowledge and enhance interventions. This systematic review is the first to synthesize qualitative data from the patient's perspective regarding strategies that improve symptoms and enhance wellbeing in individuals with HD. By analysing participant feedback across the 21 studies, we identified five reoccurring themes for analysis: interconnection, recognition of individuality and autonomy, self‐care and acceptance, cognitive and behaviour change, and practical help. These themes shed light on the experiences and insights shared by individuals with HD, providing valuable information for developing future interventions.

These findings are consistent with previous research showing that individuals with HD face additional social and mental health‐related obstacles than the wider population (Taylor et al. 2018). They are also consistent with the view that HD requires both psychological and social interventions (Bratiotis and Woody 2020). Though individual therapy has good acceptability, it may not be sufficient on its own for some patients as it does not address broader systemic problems, such as interpersonal and family conflict, the need for collaboration among various services, or the pressures of stigma associated with the disorder (Chasson et al. 2018; French et al. 2022; Whitfield et al. 2012). While previous research shows that individual therapy has good acceptability among patients with HD (Robertson et al. 2020 and Rodriguez et al. 2016), these studies are based on participant responses to questions about their preferences if they were to seek treatment in the future. This preference for individual therapy is consistent with the theme of recognizing individuality and autonomy and does not necessarily contradict with the theme of interconnection, which participants had found helpful during and after treatment. Our synthesis of the findings thus indicates that in addition to individual therapy, group therapy with peers, continuous social and systemic support, and intervention facilitators who are both highly supportive and nonjudgmental in their approach may be beneficial for individuals with HD (Chandler et al. 2019; French et al. 2022; Whitfield et al. 2012).

In line with research showing that HD is associated with interpersonal problems and insecure interpersonal attachment (Grisham et al. 2018; Yap et al. 2023), our analysis revealed that interpersonal connection and social interactions were the central motivating element for people with HD. When trust was established, interpersonal interactions and collaborative social support were sources of positive emotions and helped facilitate improvement (Davidson et al. 2021; Whitfield et al. 2012).

Our analysis also indicated that interconnectedness was common across the range of interventions included in this review (CBT, peer support, social and home support, and group‐based CBT). However, some interventions (e.g., individual CBT; Ayers et al. 2012) may place less emphasis on interconnectedness than others (e.g., peer‐support group; Frost et al. 2012). Given the strong underlying need for interconnection among individuals with HD, it may be necessary to directly address interpersonal issues and social relationships in therapy. Additionally incorporating group therapy sessions alongside individual therapy may enable social interaction with peers as well as forming deeper one on one connection with the therapist. To further foster interconnection, the addition of a peer‐support group beyond treatment may facilitate an ongoing support network comprised of like‐minded individuals. In addition, easier access to support groups and mediating or linking to other services may improve quality of life by encouraging interconnection and making services easier to obtain. Using internet‐based services as an adjunct to face‐to‐face therapy may also increase access and flexibility (Yap et al. 2022; Yap et al. 2024).

Our findings also indicate that participants preferred personalized care from therapists to get to the core of their hoarding behaviours (French et al. 2022). It may therefore be essential to provide support that is tailored to individual needs (Yarborough et al. 2019). One of the core elements of current therapies is to set goals, and a personalized treatment offers the ability to cater to individual needs (Ayers et al. 2012). For example, adding home visits by intervention facilitators may be a helpful way for participants' current circumstances to be assessed and enable collaboration between patient and therapist to target specific areas of need (Crone et al. 2020; Whitfield et al. 2012). A strong desire by people with HD to be seen as individuals means that without personalized care, patients may lose interest and drop out of treatment prematurely due to their needs not being met.

At a practical level, our findings showed that most patients found it difficult to envisage how to reduce clutter, with rubbish removal alone being overwhelming and beyond their perceived capabilities. Whitfield et al.'s (2012) study on a collaborative approach showed the effectiveness of home visits as part of HD treatment to address practical challenges and mediate between different services. Adding home visits as a mainstay to interventions may be a beneficial part of personalized care.

Our findings also showed that CBT for HD (Steketee and Frost 2007, 2014) was well‐received by participants. The cognitive techniques enabled patients to view possessions and circumstances with a new perspective, which improved hoarding symptoms, supporting the validity of Frost and Hartl's (1996) model and Steketee and Frost's (2007, 2014). Exposure techniques and strategies for sorting and organizing were well‐regarded. However, several patients viewed cognitive reappraisal and restructuring techniques as too complex and found simple concrete tasks more helpful. In addition, some patients did not view homework assignments as being as valuable as therapists did (Ayers et al. 2012). It may therefore be important to tailor CBT‐based homework and tasks to individual clients.

Finally, individuals with HD reported a desire to work with deeper issues such as interpersonal trauma, insecure attachment, and emotion regulation difficulties (Ayers et al. 2012; Chou et al. 2019; Mathes et al. 2020; Taylor et al. 2018; Tolin et al. 2019; Yap and Grisham 2021). Given these challenges, exploring therapeutic approaches beyond CBT may be beneficial. For example, CFT received positive patient feedback because it focused on internal processes, emotions and self‐perception. Combining CBT and CFT may thus have the potential to address broader issues. Other therapies that have not been researched with this population may also prove fruitful. For example, dialectical behaviour therapy, which places an emphasis on distress tolerance and interpersonal effectiveness, or interpersonal psychotherapy, which focuses on relationships and communication, may offer promising directions for further research. Furthermore, incorporating self‐care practices such as self‐compassion, acceptance, relaxation and engaging in recreational activities may improve wellbeing and emotion regulation, which may in turn provide some respite and relief for people with HD from their overwhelming circumstances. Given the importance of emotion regulation in this population (Girme et al. 2021; Taylor et al. 2018; Tolin et al. 2019; Grisham et al. 2022), combining CBT with emotion regulation strategies is also imperative to treat this aspect of hoarding disorder effectively.

Strengths and Limitations

To our knowledge this review is the first to synthesize the available qualitative data on the patient perspective regarding factors that alleviate hoarding symptoms and improves wellbeing in patients with HD. The available evidence supports the benefit of CBT for HD, while suggesting that other aspects of treatment, such as interpersonal connection and emotion regulation, may also be beneficial and should be further investigated. The primary limitation of this review is the lack of comprehensive studies directly addressing patient perspectives on this topic, indicating a gap for further dedicated research. Out of the 21 studies included, 20 were conducted in Western countries, with only one study from an Asian country, suggesting a Western‐centric bias and possible lack of diversity in the available evidence and emphasizing the need for further research in different cultural contexts. Furthermore, several studies, particularly the mixed‐methods or case study designs, allocated only small sections to the qualitative data, which limited the comprehensiveness of their findings.

Although the quality assessment indicated that most studies were of high quality, there was only one reviewer for this assessment, which may have introduced bias into the ratings. However, as all studies were included in the analysis, the bias is unlikely to have affected the overall findings.

We acknowledge that a major limitation of this review is its reliance on qualitative data, which introduces subjectivity and allows for different interpretations. Therefore, the potential for bias and individual interpretation in this review cannot be ruled out. This is always the case for qualitative data, and especially the case for systematic reviews of qualitative data, in which reviewers are interpreting existing data. While some qualitative reviews critique attempts to synthesize findings from qualitative studies, we have followed the pragmatic approach called for by Flemming and Noyes (2021) who make the case that those interested in clinical practice and policy benefit from attempts to synthesize what is known about a topic. This is especially important in hoarding, where consequences of the disorder are severe and treatment outcomes are not yet as satisfactory as we might hope.

Finally, this review is limited to individuals who already received a diagnosis of HD and sought help, which excludes the perspectives of individuals with HD who do not seek out treatment. This restricts the generalizability of the findings and may overlook important insights from the untreated population, which may have different characteristics to individuals who participate in research (Woody et al. 2020).

Future Research

A dedicated qualitative study asking patients what alleviates symptoms and enhances wellbeing may uncover additional themes that better address how patients deal with this chronic mental illness. Further studies looking at the comparison of dropout rates in individual treatment, group treatment and a combination of both would also be beneficial to pinpoint which mode of treatment delivery works best. In addition, looking deeper into specific emotion regulation and interpersonal components of other treatment approaches and how these could be integrated into CBT for HD would be worth considering, as there may be scope to enhance these aspects of CBT as it currently is applied to HD treatment. Finally, studies examining interpersonal functioning with the addition of home visits and collaboration with outside services (the council, fire department) may also bring about better results for this population.

Conclusion

This systematic review highlights the importance of including the patient's perspective in HD research to develop effective interventions. The five themes from the study suggest that in addition to CBT, the inclusion of tailored support, personalized care, a greater focus on underlying social and emotional factors, and practical assistance may be essential for addressing the needs of individuals with HD. Further research is needed to explore the patient perspective, compare treatment modalities and investigate the integration of home visits and collaboration with outside services. By incorporating the patient's perspective, future interventions can better support individuals with HD in their journey towards improved mental health and wellbeing.
