Custom, 3-Dimensional Patient-Specific Instrumentation in Anatomic Total Shoulder Arthroplasty: Part 3—Prosthesis Placement, Subscapularis Repair, and Postoperative Rehabilitation
Authors: Benjamin Kerzner, Zeeshan A Khan, Suhas P Dasari, Christopher M Brusalis, Johnathon R McCormick, Vitor La Banca, Mario Hevesi, Nikhil N Verma
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe proposed benefits of stemless humeral implants include greater bone preservation, decreased cortical stress shielding, less risk of diaphyseal stress risers, decreased surgical time, and greater ease of implant removal during revision surgery. In part 3 of this comprehensive technique series on the management of glenohumeral arthritis, we present our step-by-step surgical technique for use of a patient-specific 3-dimensionally printed glenoid drill guide, placement of a stemless anatomic total shoulder prosthesis, and subscapularis repair, and we highlight our protocol for postoperative rehabilitation.

The primary goal of anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) is to provide a new bearing surface of the glenohumeral joint while reproducing normal anatomy and rebalancing surrounding soft tissues.1 As implants have continued to evolve, there has been a continued emphasis on developing prostheses that can more accurately replicate the native anatomy of the joint while preserving bone stock.2 Over time, this has led to the development of shorter humeral stems and, eventually, a transition to stemless “canal-sparing” humeral components.

The proposed benefits of stemless humeral implants include greater bone preservation, decreased stress shielding, less risk of diaphyseal stress risers, decreased surgical time, and easier revision surgery.3 The popularity of stemless designs is rapidly increasing, making it critical to understand the optimal technique, ideal indications, and benefits and risks of implementing stemless TSA in one’s practice.2,4

In part 3 of this comprehensive technique series on the management of glenohumeral arthritis, we present our step-by-step surgical technique for the use of a patient-specific 3-dimensionally printed glenoid drill guide, placement of a stemless anatomic total shoulder prosthesis, and subscapularis repair, and we highlight our protocol for postoperative rehabilitation (Video 1).

Technique

Glenoid Preparation and Prosthesis Placement

After careful surgical dissection, humeral dislocation, and humeral head cutting are completed as described in part 2 of this technique series, attention is turned to glenoid preparation. A Fukuda posterior glenoid retractor is inserted with the arm in a forward flexed and internally rotated position. The surgeon should aim to position the apex of the Fukuda retractor on the posterior glenoid and, once it is in place, externally rotate the arm to retract the resected humerus posteriorly. The arm is then positioned resting in external rotation on the Mayo stand. Next, a wide anterior glenoid retractor is placed along the anterior neck of the glenoid. If further retraction is necessary, a third retractor in the form of a pointed Hohmann retractor (at the 12-o’clock position) is placed; the surgeon should ensure that this is not placed excessively medially to avoid iatrogenic injury to the suprascapular nerve.

An inferior capsular release is completed by using an elevator to tension the inferior capsule away from the inferior rim of the glenoid and releasing the capsule from the glenoid with electrocautery. Electrocautery must remain on bone, and no arcing occurs from the cautery to the inferior soft-tissue retractors, which are adjacent to the axillary nerve. A comprehensive inferior capsular release is paramount to achieving adequate glenoid exposure. The release is carried posteriorly, with removal of the labrum circumferentially from the glenoid face (Fig 1 A and B).

During preparation of the glenoid surface, patient-specific instrumentation is used to ensure accurate and precise glenoid component positioning. The production of our custom patient-specific instrumentation guide in-house is described in part 1 of this technique series (Fig 1 C and D). The patient’s CT scan is used for preoperative templating and the creation of a patient-specific, resin-based sterile glenoid guide by use of previously described methods.5

Next, the glenoid can be prepared for insertion of an anatomic glenoid component following techniques specific to the commercial prosthesis being implanted. In this example, the procedural steps associated with implantation of the Eclipse prosthesis (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is described. With a standard low-profile reamer, the remaining articular glenoid surface is reamed until a smooth subchondral surface is created, with removal of as little subchondral bone as necessary to ensure appropriate bone stock (Fig 2A). The central peg is drilled to a positive stop with a 6-mm cannulated drill. A drill guide is placed flush on the glenoid face and engaged in the previously drilled central peg hole. A 6-mm non-cannulated drill is used to drill the superior glenoid peg. A 4.5-mm drill is used to prepare the 3 inferior glenoid holes, followed by the use of a glenoid broach or punch to create the inferior slot at the site of the previously drilled 3 holes (Fig 2 B and C).

In parallel, pulsatile lavage of the glenoid is performed and the glenoid peg holes are packed with epinephrine-soaked sponges to aid in hemostasis. Humeral Prosthesis Placement

After glenoid component implantation (Fig 2D), the Fukuda retractor is carefully removed and the humerus is moved anteriorly; the surgeon should ensure that neither the Fukuda retractor nor the posterior edge of the humerus engages and displaces the newly placed glenoid component. The arm is placed in external rotation and adduction to provide nearly circumferential access to the cut humeral metaphysis, and the humeral head cut protector is removed.

During sizing of the humeral implant, the trunnion needs to match the cortical rim to ensure an optimally sized implant is selected. A handheld coring template is centered over the previously performed humeral cut, and the inner centering device is placed into the template to seat it (Fig 3A). A graduated sizer is then drilled through the inner aperture of the centering device in the coring template until it reaches the lateral cortex, determining the size and length of the cage screw to be used (Fig 3B). The coring template is removed, and the trunnion is placed over the centering device left in situ and pressed until it is flush against the cut humeral surface. The trunnion has a bone ingrowth and bone on-growth surface as well as fins to provide primary stability and prevent subsequent rotation. It is imperative that the trunnion is appropriately sized, sits flush against the cut surface, and sits directly over the inferior cortex for a secure fit. Prior to humeral component placement, preparations are made for the subscapularis tendon peel repair. Three small drill holes are made along the anterior aspect of the humerus, approximately 1 to 2 cm below the cut surface of the humeral head. A high–tensile strength, nonabsorbable suture (No. 5 Ethibond; Ethicon, Somerville, NJ) is passed in an outside-in manner through each drill hole, and the limbs of each suture pair are held together with a surgical clamp. Subsequently, the trunnion is impacted into position.

The humeral implant is then placed over the trunnion and impacted into its final position (Fig 4B). For ideal sizing and placement, the trunnion should have circumferential contact with the cortical rim. By placing the ideally sized trunnion directly over the cut surface, we have already guaranteed an optimal humeral head offset. An optimal fit should be confirmed using the 40/50/60 rule: After reducing the humeral head back into position, the surgeon should ensure there is a minimum of 40° of external rotation, 50% posterior translation with good bounce back of the humeral head into the center of the glenoid, and 60° of internal rotation. This confirms that an adequate posterior capsular release has been performed and provides an anatomic reconstruction with good mobility and proper stability.

Subscapularis Fixation

For repair of the subscapularis tendon, the arm is placed in approximately 30° of external rotation, thereby preventing over-tensioning of the subscapularis and decreased external rotation motion postoperatively. Then, beginning superiorly, the medial limb of each suture pair (i.e., the limb closest to the humeral implant) is attached to a free needle and passed in a Mason-Allen configuration through the subscapularis tendon. This limb is re-clamped to its other limb with a surgical clamp, and the remaining 2 suture limbs are passed sequentially in a similar manner more distally within the subscapularis. Once all 3 sutures have been passed, each suture pair is tied sequentially. A previously placed tagging suture in the subscapularis may be pulled laterally by an assistant to aid in mobilizing the subscapularis to its insertion on the humerus. Once all limbs are tied, excess suture limbs may be cut and the tagging suture removed (Fig 4C). An additional high–tensile strength, nonabsorbable suture is used to place a simple suture stitch in the lateral aspect of the rotator interval, between the upper border of the subscapularis and the anterior border of the supraspinatus. Positioning this suture laterally within the rotator interval confers additional stability without compromising range of motion.

Wound Closure

The wound is copiously irrigated with saline solution, and hemostasis is confirmed. One gram of vancomycin powder is applied deep in the wound, and the incision is closed in layers. Multiple absorbable figure-of-8 sutures are placed to close the deltopectoral interval, with care taken to preserve the cephalic vein. Additional layers of absorbable suture are used to close the deep dermal (No. 2-0 Monocryl; Ethicon) and subcuticular (No. 3-0 Monocryl) layers. Sterile dressings are applied, and the patient is placed in an abduction brace. Radiographs are obtained postoperatively (Fig 4D).

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation and postoperative guidelines for stemless TSA do not deviate from those for stemmed TSA.6,7 In the first phase after surgery, the goals of recovery include healing of soft tissue and maintenance of replaced joint integrity. During this phase, a sling should be worn continuously for 6 weeks. Patients can begin passive forward flexion in the supine position as tolerated, along with gentle external rotation in the scapular plane. However, strict avoidance of active range of motion (AROM) and excessive internal and external rotation must be followed during this phase to allow for healing of the subscapularis repair. After 6 weeks, if patients can tolerate a passive range of motion (PROM) program and have shown PROM increases, they can move to the second phase, focusing on early strengthening. During this phase, the goals of rehabilitation include fully restoring PROM, gradually restoring AROM, and restoring dynamic shoulder stability while not overloading the joint to avoid compromising the healing soft tissue. If patients can tolerate their PROM, AROM, and isometric programs, they can then progress to higher stages of rehabilitation after 6 weeks, which include full weaning from the sling, maintenance of function without pain, and return to functional activity. Return to noncontact sports can be achieved at 4 to 6 months postoperatively.6,7

Discussion

To date, several stemless anatomic TSA implants have received US Food and Drug Administration approval. Most recently, in 2019, the Eclipse implant received US Food and Drug Administration approval for stemless TSA, although this system has been used in Europe for over a decade.7 The stemless humeral implant consists of 3 major components: a trunnion, a central cortical cage screw, and a humeral head. It uses both cortical and cancellous fixation.7 The implant has a grit-blasted coating that improves bone on-growth, as well as a full, solid collar, which rests on the cortical rim of the proximal humeral cut surface.8 Additionally, the implant has a central threaded cortical cage with portals for bone capture, making the implant used in this technique a “high bone contact” implant.8 This feature allows for this stemless device to use screw-in implantation to apply compressive force in the metaphyseal region.8

During the anatomic TSA procedure, the primary goal is to reproduce the anatomic center of rotation and reconstruct a stable, pain-free joint based on the patient’s native anatomy.9 The senior author does this by combining an adaptable stemless humeral component with a customized, patient-specific glenoid drill guide and his preferred surgical technique for maximizing exposure (N.N.V.). Pearls and Pitfalls

Recently, 2 large randomized controlled trials have reported clinical non-inferiority of stemless components relative to stemmed components in the setting of anatomic TSA. A 2020 study by Romeo et al.7 compared 237 patients treated with stemless TSA with 90 patients treated with traditionally stemmed TSA. At 2 years of follow-up, the authors found no significant difference in the adjusted Constant-Murley score, Composite Clinical Success score, or revision surgery rate. This prompted the authors to report non-inferiority of the stemless component. They also concluded that stemless components should be implemented instead of stemmed components when the appropriate indications are met. These results were echoed by a separate 2020 randomized controlled trial by Wiater et al.6 The authors compared stemless and short-stemmed components in the setting of anatomic TSA in 265 randomized patients. They reported no significant difference in range of motion or the Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons, or Constant-Murley score at any point over their 2-year follow-up. This Level I evidence would further suggest apparent clinical non-inferiority of stemless components. Although long-term clinical evidence is still needed, these initial short-term outcomes suggest that stemless anatomic TSA is a viable and effective therapeutic intervention that should be considered, when indicated, as opposed to stemmed prostheses.
