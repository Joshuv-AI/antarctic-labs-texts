The Use of Navigation During Total Knee Replacement Improves Precision in Achieving Mechanical Alignment in Obese Patients
Authors: Miriam Zurrón Lobato, Sergio Bartolomé García, Paloma Aragonés Maza, Ana Valverde Villar, Cesar Ramírez Feito, María Soledad González González, María Concepción Payares Herrera, Santiago Perera Fernández de Pedro, Javier Jiménez Martín, Pedro José Torrijos Garrido
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Obesity (body mass index ≥30 kg/m2) is a global health challenge and a known risk factor of knee osteoarthritis (KOA), increasing the need for total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Obese patients face higher risks of early implant failure and revision, often linked to malalignment. Navigation-assisted surgery (NAS) improves precision in achieving mechanical alignment, but its impact in obese patients remains underexplored. This randomized, controlled, open-label, multicenter trial evaluated short-term radiographic outcomes, focusing on coronal alignment, in obese patients undergoing TKA with NAS versus conventional instrumentation. The primary hypothesis was that NAS would result in a higher rate of mechanical axis alignment within a predefined target (180° ± 3°).Methods:A total of 159 obese patients with symptomatic KOA were randomized 1:1 at 2 hospitals to undergo TKA with either NAS or conventional guides. Mechanical axis alignment was assessed 1 year postoperatively using long-standing radiographs. Secondary end points included femoral and tibial component alignment, surgical time, complications, range of motion, Knee Society Score, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index, visual analog scale, and EuroQol-5D.Results:In total, 154 patients were analyzed. Proper mechanical axis alignment (180° ± 3°) was achieved in 69% of NAS cases vs. 47% in controls (p = 0.006; OR = 2.5; 95% confidence interval: 1.29-4.83). The mean deviation was –1.59° (SD 3.02) in NAS vs. –2.15° (SD 3.56) in controls. Tibial alignment outliers occurred in 16% (12/73) of NAS vs. 32% (23/71) in controls (p = 0.026). Surgical time was longer with NAS (70 min [interquartile range (IQR) 63-76] vs. 59 min [IQR 55-67], p < 0.001). No differences were found in complications or hospital stay. Functional outcomes improved similarly in both groups at 1 year.Conclusion:NAS significantly improves precision in achieving mechanical alignment in obese patients undergoing TKA. Despite similar clinical outcomes, NAS offers superior radiographic accuracy. Longer term studies are needed to assess effects on implant survival and patient-reported outcomes.Level of Evidence:Level I. Introduction

Obesity, defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m2, affects over 890 million people worldwide1. In the United States, 2021 data show 41.5% of men and 45.6% of women are obese, with projections indicating 2 in 3 adults (≥25 years) will be obese by 20502. Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) and obesity frequently coexist. Obesity increases KOA risk through joint overload, reduced physical activity, and loss of periarticular musculature3.

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is an effective treatment of KOA, which affects 13.83% of the Spanish population4; approximately 65,517 TKAs were performed in Spain in 20225. Obesity affects 40.1% of those aged older than 65 years in Spain6.

Obese patients undergoing TKA face higher risks of complications such as implant loosening and early failure, often linked to postoperative malalignment7. Achieving neutral mechanical alignment (typically 180° ± 3°) correlates with improved implant survival8, especially in obese patients9. However, some high-quality studies challenge the need for strict alignment. For example, Parratte et al.10 found no significant difference in 15-year survival between aligned and nonaligned knees. Recent analyses emphasize patient-specific factors over alignment, promoting individualized strategies11.

Obesity is linked to increased malalignment rates post-TKA12,13, likely due to technical challenges: excessive soft tissue hinders exposure, makes bony landmarks harder to identify, and complicates accurate placement of cutting guides.

NAS uses optical tracking to register anatomical landmarks and calculate the mechanical axis, providing improved precision in achieving a predefined alignment target compared with conventional TKA techniques14. While NAS improves alignment consistency in general TKA populations, its benefit in obese patients remains underexplored.

Rationale

Given the rising prevalence of obesity and its adverse effect on TKA outcomes, optimizing mechanical alignment is increasingly important. NAS-assisted TKA may enhance component positioning in obese patients.

The objective of this trial was to evaluate the precision in achieving a neutral mechanical axis and safety of NAS-assisted TKA compared with conventional TKA in obese patients. We hypothesize that NAS will improve coronal alignment. Secondary outcomes include surgical time, hospital stay, complications, and functional outcomes, both surgeon-reported and patient-reported.

Material and Methods

Trial Design

This prospective, randomized, controlled, open-label multicenter trial assessed mechanical alignment after TKA in obese patients (BMI ≥30 kg/m2), comparing NAS with conventional instrumentation. Registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03865524), it was conducted in 2 Spanish hospitals: Hospital Universitario Puerta de Hierro Majadahonda and Hospital Universitario Santa Cristina and approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Hospital Universitario Puerta de Hierro (Ref No: 115/18).

Selection Criteria

Adults (>18 years) with symptomatic KOA and BMI ≥30 kg/m2 scheduled for primary TKA were eligible. Exclusions: prior knee surgery within 12 months, bilateral TKA, varus/valgus deformity ≥15° (to minimize bias from severe deformities requiring more constrained implants), and cognitive impairment. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Intervention

All procedures were performed by 7 experienced surgeons across 2 hospitals. Each patient received a cemented, posterior-stabilized Optetrak Logic prosthesis (Exactech) with patellar resurfacing. All surgeons were trained in navigation-assisted surgery (NAS) and had completed over 10 cases before the trial to overcome the learning curve.

A pneumatic tourniquet (300 mm Hg) was used throughout; ischemia time—recorded as surgical time in the protocol—was measured in all cases. A medial parapatellar approach was standard. The aim of bone resections was for neutral mechanical alignment, with femoral and tibial rotation guided by the epicondylar axis and Akagi line, respectively15. Ligament balancing was performed as needed.

In the NAS group, the Exactech GPS system (Blue-Ortho) guided resections through sensors and landmark-based 3D modeling. In the control group, distal femoral cuts used intramedullary guides (fixed 6° valgus), and tibial cuts used extramedullary guides. Postoperative protocols and follow-up (6 weeks, 3, 6, and 12 months) were identical.

Outcomes

Primary outcome: proportion of patients with mechanical alignment within ±3° of 180°, assessed by long-standing radiographs at 1 year by 2 blinded observers using the Cooke method16; disagreements >1° were resolved by consensus. The Cohen kappa assessed interobserver reliability.

Secondary Outcomes

Coronal alignment angles of the femoral and tibial components (CFA and CTA) were measured by the attending surgeon during follow-up using standardized definitions on long-leg radiographs with neutral rotation; outliers were defined as deviations >3° from 90°. Surgical time (tourniquet time), length of hospital stay, and complications were prospectively recorded as perioperative outcomes.

Functional outcomes: range of motion (ROM), Knee Society Score (KSS)17.

Patient-reported outcomes: visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, EuroQol-5D (EQ-5D)18, and Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) score19.

Sample Size

Based on prior data (30% misalignment in control group20 vs. 13.5% misalignment in NAS group21), 134 patients were needed for 80% power (α = 0.05). With 15% allowance for loss, 158 patients were included.

Randomization

Patients were randomized 1:1, using REDCap's computer-generated sequence, stratified by center, after consent and before surgery.

Statistical Analysis

Intention-to-treat analysis was used. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median (IQR). Comparisons used the Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U; categorical variables used χ2 or Fisher exact test. The Cohen kappa assessed radiographic agreement. The primary outcome was compared using χ2 with odds ratios and 95% confidence interval (CI); logistic regression adjusted for confounders. BMI-alignment correlation used the Pearson coefficient. Significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses used Stata v15.1 (StataCorp).

Baseline Characteristics

The preoperative characteristics of the patients were all similar in the 2 groups (Table I). The mean BMI was 34.73 (SD 4.16), with a median of 33.50 and a range from 30.02 to 46.66. Patients were categorized into 3 groups: 30 to 34.9 (moderate obesity), 35 to 39.9 (severe obesity), and ≥40 (morbid obesity). This distribution reflects a relevant degree of obesity in the TKA setting. Race and ethnicity data were not collected because these variables are not routinely recorded in clinical practice in Spain and were not prespecified in the study protocol.

Results

Between February 2019 and April 2023, 159 patients with KOA and obesity were included in this study (Fig. 1).

CONSORT/participant flow diagram.

Of these 159 patients, 3 in the control group were not operated, and 2 (1 per group) missed follow-up. A total of 154 patients were included in the study for final analysis.

Some follow-ups were missed due to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Primary Outcome

The mean for mechanical axis of the limb was 1.59° varus (SD = 3.02) for the NAS group and 2.15° varus (SD = 3.56) for the control group. For the primary outcome, we found that the proportion of patients achieving mechanical alignment within the predefined mechanical target range (180° ± 3°) was higher in the NAS group (69%) compared with the control group (47%) (OR = 2.5 [95% CI 1.29; 4.83; p = 0.006]). Thus, NAS patients were 2.5 times more likely to achieve alignment within the target range.

Interobserver agreement was excellent (kappa = 0.956; 95% CI: 0.942-0.969).

A detailed analysis of alignment deviations showed that, in the control group, 45% of outliers were between 3° and 5°, most outliers had moderate misalignment (50% between 5° and 7°), and only 5% exceeded 7°. By contrast, 67% of NAS outliers fell between 3° and 5°, with only 1 case beyond 7°.

Intraoperatively, 4 bone events were recorded: 3 anterior femoral notchings (2 NAS, 1 control), defined as cortical violation without fracture, and 1 anterior tibial tuberosity avulsion (NAS), fixed with a screw without altering postoperative care. None of the notching cases progressed to fracture or had clinical consequences.

During hospitalization, 1 control patient had deep vein thrombosis (DVT); 2 patients per group required red blood cell transfusions. Other isolated events: vestibular syndrome (1 NAS), respiratory infection (1 control), and angioedema (1 control).

At 6-week follow-up, wound issues (ecchymosis, hematoma, delayed healing) occurred in 10 NAS and 5 control patients, all resolved with nursing care. In addition, 1 control patient had a second DVT, and 1 NAS patient developed a deep infection. It was treated operatively with irrigation and debridement and a course of antibiotics, and infection was resolved

Later complications requiring surgery: 1 control patient had patellofemoral instability from fibrosis, treated with fibrosis clearance and supplemental closure of the arthrotomy with a polypropylene mesh; 1 NAS patient had medial collateral ligament incompetence, likely from intraoperative handling, requiring switch to a more constrained insert. This was not attributed to navigation.

Stiffness: 4 NAS and 1 control patient required treatment. Management included botulinum toxin injection (1 NAS), manipulation under anesthesia (2 NAS), and arthroscopic arthrolysis (1 in each group).

Other complications: 14 events per group during follow-up (falls, unrelated pathologies, emergency department visits). Seven per group were protocol-recorded but deemed unrelated to TKA.

No significant differences in ROM, VAS, KSS, EQ-5D, or WOMAC were found at any follow-up point. VAS, KSS, WOMAC, and EQ-5D scores improved numerically and very similarly in both groups compared with the preoperative value.

Based on accepted MCID thresholds from recent literature, the median improvements observed in both groups exceeded the minimal clinically important difference for all outcome scores (Table IV).

Discussion

This short-term study focused on radiographic alignment as an early marker of surgical precision. NAS significantly improved alignment rates (69% vs. 47%; OR = 2.5; p = 0.006), supporting evidence that navigation enhances precision in achieving mechanical alignment in this population.

The detailed analysis of alignment deviations showed that both the frequency and severity of misalignment were lower in the NAS group. These findings align with previous studies indicating misalignment in 30% to 50% of conventional TKAs in obese patients22,23.

Some studies differ; Rivkin et al.24 found no significant difference, though their small sample (n = 60) limited power. Conversely, Luan et al.25 reported better coronal and sagittal alignment and fewer outliers with navigation. Such improvements matter clinically, as mechanical axis restoration improves implant survival8, supported by Chen et al.26 and Sheridan et al27.

Tibial alignment was significantly better with NAS. This aligns with the study by Goto et al.28, who reported comparable outcomes in obese and nonobese patients using accelerometer-based systems. Tibial malalignment increases polyethylene wear and early loosening29-31, making precision critical in obese patients. Hernandez-Vaquero et al.32 also reported improved tibial component positioning with navigation.

While the need for strict mechanical alignment is debated, several studies support its relevance in obese patients. New evidence33 suggests small deviations may not affect long-term outcomes, favoring individualized alignment. Our study focuses on radiographic precision as a surrogate for surgical accuracy.

The use of cemented PS implants reflected institutional standards and surgeon experience. Despite growing interest in cementless fixation for obese patients, recent evidence34 shows comparable outcomes with cemented designs using modern techniques. Reports of early aseptic loosening with certain cementless tibial baseplates in high-BMI patients35 further support cemented fixation in this group.

The 10-minute increase in NAS surgical time is consistent with prior studies25 and unlikely to be clinically relevant. Hospital stays and complication rates were similar. Gholson et al.36 reported no difference in early recovery. Slightly higher stiffness in NAS may relate to longer soft tissue handling27. Future research should explore whether specific rehabilitation strategies can mitigate this effect.

Although wound complications were more frequent in the NAS group, these were minor, resolved with conservative care, and did not delay recovery. The authors do not consider that navigation contributed directly to these events.

Imageless systems such as Exactech GPS enhance coronal alignment efficiently. While robotic and CT-based systems improve 3D planning, they increase cost and complexity. Current evidence shows no clear long-term outcome superiority among them37.

Study Limitations

Our findings, while promising, have limitations. The sample size was sufficient for alignment comparisons but insufficient for definitive conclusions on long-term complications or implant survival. Larger, long-term studies are needed to confirm whether better alignment translates to lower revision rates.

Functional outcomes were not a primary end point; a descriptive analysis was presented. Both groups exceeded the MCID, supporting clinical value. Although ceiling effects were not formally assessed, score distribution at 1 year showed no upper clustering.

Standardization across centers was ensured by using a fixed 6° valgus femoral cut; however, individualized planning might have optimized femoral alignment in some cases.

Axis measurements were performed at 1 year, but a small proportion of patients had not reached full extension at the time of radiograph acquisition, which may have affected alignment accuracy.

A limitation of this study is the absence of race and ethnicity data, which may affect the generalizability of our findings to populations outside Spain.

Given the demonstrated benefits in alignment precision and the absence of increased complications, the routine use of NAS in obese patients undergoing TKA should be strongly considered. Future research should focus on long-term implant survival and functional outcomes in this high-risk population to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

This randomized clinical trial demonstrates that navigation-assisted TKA improves the precision of coronal alignment in obese patients, increasing the likelihood of achieving the target mechanical axis. The modest increase in surgical time does not affect early recovery or complication rates. While the study was not designed to assess long-term outcomes, these findings support the radiographic and technical value of NAS in this high-risk population. Future research should evaluate whether enhanced alignment translates into improved implant longevity and patient-reported outcomes.
