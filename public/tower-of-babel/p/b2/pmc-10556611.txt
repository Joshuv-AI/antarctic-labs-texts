Sequential *CO management via controlling in situ reconstruction for efficient industrial-current-density CO2-to-C2+ electroreduction
Authors: Mao Wu, Danji Huang, Feili Lai, Ruoou Yang, Yan Liu, Jiakun Fang, Tianyou Zhai, Youwen Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAn ampere-level current density of CO2R is critical to realize the industrial production of C2+ fuels. As such, the high coverage and dimerization of *CO are crucial to meet the sufficient supply and rapid consumption at high current densities, which requires the multiscale design of the surface atom/architecture. Herein, we leverage the synchronous leaching of molecules to manipulate the seeding-growth process during CO2R reconstruction and construct Cu arrays with favorable (100) facets. The gradient diffusion in the array and the lower dimerization barrier of *CO on the (100) facets achieve sequential *CO management of the coverage and dimerization. Due to this self-adaptive dual-optimization, our catalysts achieve an industrial-relevant FEC2+ of 86.1% and an FEC2H4 of 60.8% at 700 mA cm−2.

Environment and energy crises are always the serious challenges our human beings face. According to statistics, every year, about 150 million tonnes (Mt) of European chemical sector greenhouse gas are vented to fill 75% of the energy scarcity hole, and the value will increase to 200 Mt by 2050 (1). Electrochemical CO2R as a promising route to close the anthropogenic carbon cycle and manufacture value-added feedstocks has attracted wealthy attention (2–4). However, there remains a huge distance to implement it in industrial production due to the insufficient activity of high added-value C2+ products with economic competitiveness, especially at industrial high current density (3, 4). Significant attention has thus currently been devoted to improving the thermodynamics and kinetics of CO2R into C2+. Reviewing the electrocatalytic process, the performance is determined sequentially by the reactant interface and energetic interaction (5, 6). As such, for the reaction of CO2 to C2+, the high coverage of key *CO intermediate in the catalyst surface can continuously supply the reactant to match its high-rate consumption at high current densities, which is highly desirable for high partial current density for C2+ products (7, 8). In fact, the competing *H adsorption is the main factor hindering high *CO coverage at high current densities, which will result in a trade-off between CO2R and the competitive hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) (9–11). In this regard, constructing well-defined catalysts with rich confined space has been proven to be an immediate and effective strategy to balance the coverage of *CO/*H via special local field effects or gradient diffusion (12). In addition to high *CO coverage, the subsequent dimerization of *CO dictates the selectivity of C2+, which depends on the energetic interaction between the surface atoms of catalysts and adsorptive *CO. Specifically, for typical Cu catalysts with the capacity to generate C2+ products, both experimental and theoretical simulation suggested that Cu(100) facets favor the dimerization of *CO with the lowest reaction energy barrier and can contribute to the high Faraday efficiency of C2+ (13–15). Along this way, a well-defined morphology with favorable (100) facets by the multiscale design can be a tangible model to sequentially manage the coverage and dimerization of *CO for highly industrial-current-density CO2R to C2+.

Despite this reasoned vision, relevant structures are extremely difficult to implement due to the limitation of crystal growth kinetics. Under the general environment, Cu tends to behave as a polyhedral Wulff construction terminated by (111) facets because it possesses the lowest surface energy, which will inevitably agglomerate on the gas diffusion layer electrodes (GDEs) and thus lack sufficient confined space to engineer the coverage of *CO (15). Inspired by the classical crystal growth theory, capping agents are often used to expose Cu(100) by selectively adsorbing on particular facets to alter the crystal growth habit and thus resolve this contradiction in thermodynamics. Meanwhile, the steric hindrance effect of capping agents can also adjust the final morphology of Cu (16, 17). However, due to the uncontrollable structural reconstruction under operating electroreduction conditions, these oriented design architectonic catalysts may collapse and thus are no longer valid for electrocatalytic applications (18–22). As such, it is reasonable to assume that manipulating operando reconstruction of Cu acted by the synchronized release of agents would be a substantially untapped opportunity to obtain the expectant configuration and sequentially manage *CO for highly industrial-current-density CO2R. Profoundly, this design principle based on material operando changes during electrochemical can ensure that the target catalytic surface drives synchronously the catalytic reaction. Due to the elusive reconstruction process and *CO management, this has rarely been implemented but leaving unpredictable values uncovered.

Obviously, the key to the implementation of this design principle is the selection of capping agents. The tannic acid (TA) molecule has five branches, and multiple hydroxyl radicals are strongly adsorbed to metal ions and are often used for molecular self-assembly, making it a potential candidate as an ideal capping agent for manipulating operando reconstruction of Cu (23). Herein, by using the synchronously leached TA molecule, an array with Cu(100) exposure was obtained via controllable reconstruction from CuTA nanosheets. During this reconstruction, the unique adsorption of TA molecules on Cu not only exposes Cu(100) facets but also prompts the formation of nanowires by inhibiting deposition. By further focusing on their CO2R activity, in situ Raman spectroscopy and COMSOL multiphysics simulations indicate the low *H concentration and a higher *CO coverage around this reconstructed array compared with Cu(100) nanoparticles due to the discriminative diffusion. Subsequently, density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that Cu(100) facets enhance *CO adsorption and lower the energy barrier of *CO dimerization. In addition, the introduction of high OH− concentration further facilitates this improvement. Profiting from this sequential *CO management, the well-defined Cu array delivers an industrial-relevant FEC2+ of 86.1% and an FEC2H4 of 60.8% at 700 mA cm−2. This work provides profound insights into the effect of *CO intermediate under a high current and presents a corresponding solution by catalyst design.

Results

Catalyst Design and Characterization.

Coordination compounds containing oxygen ligands and metal ions tend to leach from the catalyst surface into electrolyte under the CO2R (24, 25). Herein, we chose the CuTA coordination compounds as the precatalyst and utilized in situ TA leaching effects to synthesize well-defined morphology with Cu(100) exposure. Abundant hydroxyl groups in the TA molecule (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) ensure the strong adsorption on Cu during the deposition, which may influence the electrolytic deposition behaviors both in terms of facet exposure and particle stacking (Fig. 1A). Based on DFT calculations, the adsorption energy of the TA molecule on Cu(100) shows lower than that of Cu(111) in either parallel or perpendicular mode (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3). The low adsorption energy suggests that the TA molecule can easily adsorb on Cu(100) surface and protect it from thermodynamical effects during the formation process, leading to preferential growth of Cu(100). Guided by the theoretical prediction, the CuTA coordination compound was prepared via a simple solvent reaction. From scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscope (TEM) images, and atomic force microscopy (AFM), the coordination is composed of uniform ultrathin nanosheets with a thickness of approximately 1.7 nm (Fig. 1 C–E). This ultrathin configuration ensures sufficient electrolyte infiltration for complete reconstruction into Cu. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A) show that it has excellent crystallinity with multiple spikes that match well with previous reporters (26). The High Resolution Transmission Electron Microscope (HRTEM) image of CuTA shows a lattice spacing of about 0.24 nm denoting the (111) crystal plane (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Meanwhile, by comparing the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of TA and CuTA (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C), several additional absorption peaks around 420, 480, and 600 cm−1 are attributed to Cu(+2)-O vibration. The peak splitting near 3,377 cm−1 and the absorption shift in the fingerprint region are caused by the disruption of HO-C vibration of TA after coordinating with Cu(+2) (27), which also confirms that TA-Cu coordination compound is successfully synthesized.

Exploring the Evolution Process of CuTA during CO2R.

To investigate in situ reconstruction acted by the TA molecule, the CuTA electrode was reacted under CO2R condition at 0.5 A cm−2 for 20 min. The TEM and HRTEM images show that the final morphology of the CuTA (CuTA derived Cu, named as TD-Cu) presents a nanowire structure with the interplanar spacing of 0.18 nm corresponding to the Cu(200) plane (Fig. 2 A and B), the phenomenon demonstrates that CuTA has been reduced to metallic Cu. The two-dimensional synchrotron XRD (2D-XRD, Fig. 2C) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, SI Appendix, Fig. S5) further confirm the existence of Cu. There also appear Cu2O species on its surface, which may be caused by the OH•− induced oxidation (28) at open circuit potential (SI Appendix, Figs. S6 and S7). More importantly, based on the integration of diffraction rings, the peak intensity ratio of the Cu(100)/Cu(111) on TD-Cu significantly increases compared with that of CuO nanosheet derived Cu (named as OD-Cu) (Fig. 2D), indicating that the preferential growth of Cu(100) occurred in this reconstruction. OH− electroabsorption test is also used to characterize their relative content (Fig. 2E) (29, 30). By calculating the ratio of peak areas, the ratio of Cu(100)/Cu(111) increases fourfold from 0.50 (OD-Cu) to 2.03 (TD-Cu) (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). In summary, adsorbed TA molecules rather than CO2R intermediates drive the exposure of Cu(100) during CO2R, which is consistent with the DFT calculated adsorption energy.

The mechanism of the nanowire formation was further investigated by time-dependent structural tests (at 0.5 A cm−2) and in situ X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS). The SEM images reflect the morphological changes on the CuTA electrode over different reaction times: 1 min, 2 min, 5 min, and 20 min (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). At the early stage (reaction for 1 min), most nanosheets were broken down into interconnected nanoparticles. One minute later, a few nanowires consisting of particles with a diameter of about 30 nm appeared. When the reaction time was extended, the number of nanowires increased exponentially, and the diameter decreased slightly. Meanwhile, the effects of current density and electrolyte pH were also studied (SI Appendix, Figs. S10 and S11), and the result indicated that both of these factors would affect the formation of nanowires by changing Cu coordination and ligand adsorption (31, 32). Corresponding FTIR spectra were also carried out to monitor the changes in composition at different reaction times (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). The presence of the splitting peak (~3,377 cm−1) and the Cu(+2)-O absorption peak within 5 min and their disappearance after 20 min suggests that TA molecules are coordinated to Cu(+2) during nanowire formation and then leach out of the CuTA framework (27). It is due to this behavior that the small newborn can link together and self-assemble in one direction under the influence of the electrophoresis effect (33, 34). To demonstrate this, CV curves were measured to identify the effects of TA on Cu(+2) deposition (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). The more negative potential for deoxidization peak compared with CuO nanosheet indicates that TA can inhibit this reduction process by reducing the deposition rate, which may have a significant effect on the final morphology (31). In situ XAS was conducted to get more insights into the Cu evolution in CuTA during CO2R (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). In the X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra of Cu K-edge (Fig. 2G), the energy level gradually approaches that of the Cu foil when the potential increases from −0.1 VRHE to −0.4 VRHE, suggesting that the valence state of Cu is between Cu(0) and Cu(+1). As for the corresponding K2-weighted FT extended X-ray absorption fine structure spectra (Fig. 2H), the appearance of the Cu-Cu peak is paralleled by the disappearance of Cu-O, this observation confirms that CuTA frame is destroyed and the reduction of Cu(+2) takes place. Moreover, the Cu coordination number is far lower than Cu foil, and there still maintains a partial Cu-O bonding at the low potential (-0.2 VRHE), it is likely that the growth of metallic Cu particles reduced from CuTA framework is restricted due to the TA ligand tethering effect, leading to the low deposition rate, which agrees with FTIR spectra and CV results (35, 36). As the potentials decrease more negatively, the crystal structure tends to be perfect because of the growth of Cu coordination. The phenomenon reveals that the collapse of the coordination framework and the growth of crystals take place simultaneously, and the TA leaching is determined by applied potential. Meanwhile, the function of copper oxidation state and copper K-edge energy shift suggest that the oxidation state of Cu is between Cu0 (8979.0 eV) and Cu+ (8979.3 eV) during CO2R (Fig. 2I). From the above FTIR spectra, TA molecules only exist at the preliminary formation stage, so the presence of a low oxidation state is most likely caused by OH− adsorption on the electrode surface (28). Guided by DFT simulation, the synchronized release of TA molecules from CuTA nanosheets can manipulate operando reconstruction of Cu to construct a devoted neat Cu array with exposure favorable (100) facets.

CO2 Electroreduction Performance.

Having determined in situ reconstruction and the real active sites, the CO2R performance of the CuTA electrode was carried out to evaluate the activity of Cu array with exposure favorable (100) facets. The current–voltage response of CuTA under the CO2 atmosphere is sharper than that in the Ar atmosphere (Fig. 3A), illustrating its higher intrinsic CO2R and poor HER performance (37). Product distributions including liquid and gas constituents were obtained at a constant current from -0.1 A cm−2 to −1.0 A cm−2 (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Table S1). At low cathode current density (−0.1 A cm−2), CO is the main product with an FE of 30%. When more negative potentials are applied, the figure decreases to within 10%, and FEC2+ dominates, which suggests that the adsorbed CO is an intermediate during CO2R and this catalyst can effectively promote dimerization (38). At −0.7 A cm−2, the CuTA electrode exhibits a maximum FEC2+ of 86.1 ± 3.1%, corresponding to 60.8 ± 2.3% of FEC2H4, 18.6 ± 2.0% of FEC2H5OH, 5.66 ± 0.2% of FECH3COO-, and 1% FEC3H7OH (Fig. 3C). The creasing ratio of FEC2+ to FEC1 also reveals that this catalyst favors C2+ products instead of C1 products (Fig. 3D). In addition, the FEH2 is <20% throughout the potential range from −0.2 A cm−2 to 0.8 A cm−2, the result reflects that this catalyst can effectively suppress HER even at a high current density, this is what we expected. In addition, the C2+ partial current density can meet the industrial level (−0.3 A cm−2) at only −0.5 A cm−2, and the peak value presents nearly −0.77 A cm−2, superior to most previous reports (Fig. 3 E and F and SI Appendix, Table S2) (13, 14, 35, 39–41). To highlight its advantage, Cu(100) exposure nanoparticles and CuO nanosheet were used as two control samples. As for Cu(100) dominated nanoparticle, although it shows >50% FE for C2H4 at −0.2 A cm−2, FECH4, and FEH2 suddenly increase more negatively than −0.3 A cm−2, which may be caused by intense HER and low *CO coverage at a high current density (SI Appendix, Figs. S15 and S16). For CuO nanosheet, it tends to present nanoparticle structure exposed Cu(111) facets after reconstruction (SI Appendix, Figs. S17 and S18) and shows a maximum FEC2H4 of 40% at −0.2 A cm−2, lower than CuTA and Cu(100) dominated nanoparticle, indicating that Cu(100) facets are more beneficial to C2+ products. However, it also faces a similar issue as the CuO nanosheet electrode, and the FEH2 increases substantially over 40% at −0.3 A cm−2. To exclude the active sites and hydrophobicity influence, electrochemical surface area (ECSA) and contact angle were performed (SI Appendix, Figs. S19 and S20). The little improvement between CuO and CuTA suggests that the two factors play a nondominant role in CO2R. These results suggest that crystal faces combined with the microenvironment can promote CO2R performance and inhibit HER under the large current density. The stability of the CuTA electrode was also run under a continuous electrochemical CO2R at 0.5 A cm−2 for 15 h (Fig. 3G). Due to the formation of carbonates on the gas diffusion layer, the active sites of the catalyst are covered, simultaneously preventing the transfer of CO2. Consequently, we must clean the salification on the electrode surface every three hours. As we can see, the FEC2H4 can still maintain >50% at 0.5 A cm−2 for 15 h with a negligible decay, presenting remarkable durability on the CuTA electrode. Moreover, every time we applied potential again, the FEC2H4 could reach the same level, demonstrating that the deactivation of this catalyst did not happen at such a high current density. The electrode morphology and Cu(100)/Cu(111) ratio after reaction were also monitored (SI Appendix, Fig. S21). Although there is a slight decrease in the ratio of Cu(100)/Cu(111), from 2.03 to 1.69, primarily due to the migration of copper atoms under high current conditions (20, 42), the Cu(100) crystal facet still dominates and is three times that of OD-Cu. Meanwhile, the failure of morphology destruction further explains why it could maintain good selectivity without a significant decline in catalytic performance. In a word, the cooperation between facets and morphology engineering shows a huge advantage for C2+ products at high current density compared with a unilateral regulation.

Promotion Effects of Nanowire Structure on *CO Coverage.

The regulation effect of the specific morphology and exposed crystal surface on the intermediates was further investigated. As analyzed, the competing *H adsorption is the main factor hindering high *CO coverage at high current densities. Herein, in situ Raman spectrum was conducted to investigate the local microenvironment to evaluate the competing *CO/*H coverage on Cu electrodes during CO2R (Fig. 4 A and B). The peaks at 1,067 cm−1 and 1,334 cm−1 represent the adsorbed HCO3− and CO32−, respectively, and the surface OH− concentration can be calculated by their relative area ratio (38). By quantitative analysis, the ratio of CO32−/HCO3− exhibits dependence on the potential for all electrodes (SI Appendix, Fig. S22), and the corresponding surface pH varies from 10.17 to 10.99 on the CuTA electrode, from 10.10 to 10.41 on Cu(100) nanoparticles electrode, while from 10.17 to 10.53 on the CuO nanosheet electrode (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S23), indicating that the local OH− concentration on the CuTA electrode surface is constantly higher than that of electrodes made of nanoparticles. To further identify the origin of the discrepancy, COMSOL multiphysics simulations were applied to reflect the OH− concentration of two different modes: nanowire array and nanoparticle stacking. Here, we only consider the CO2R effect on OH− because CO2R dominates the main source of OH− regeneration by consuming protons. At a total circuit current density of 0.7 A cm−2, the electrolyte current density around the nanowire tip shows a significantly higher intensity than that of nanoparticles, and the enhancement was more widespread (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S24), which can be ascribed to the “tip effect” (43). The accumulation of electrolyte currents can enrich the K+ cation on the catalyst surface to provide *H for CO2R by water dissociation, thereby generating larger OH−. At the same time, the diffusion of OH− is further restricted by the confined space between nanowire gaps, resulting in a higher OH− concentration (Fig. 4 E and F) (44). Owning to the electrode structure, HER is suppressed, and *H coverage falls into decline. Subsequently, the peak around 2,068 cm−1 corresponding to the atop-bound CO (COatop) shows a stronger intensity compared with Cu(100) particles (Fig. 5 A and B), and it can maintain at a higher potential, indicating that the CuTA electrode can obtain high COatop coverage even at high voltages. Another *CO stretching mode at 1,860 cm−1, whose peak intensity is indetectable compared with COatop, refers to the bridge-bound CO (CObridge). From previous studies, high *CO coverage tends to drive *CO to be adsorbed in COatop mode rather than CObridge mode (45). These above results demonstrate that the array electrode can make *CO predominance through *H drop in the presence of *CO/*H competition, thereby improving *CO coverage at a high potential.

Theoretical Origin of the Rational *CO Management on TD-Cu.

Apart from the investigation of the coverage of *CO, the subsequent dimerization of *CO was further investigated by in situ Raman spectrum. The adsorption capacity of *CO is closely related to the electronic structure of surface Cu and can affect product selectivity for CO2R (7). The stronger signal of *CO adsorption on Cu(100) nanoparticles than that of CuO suggests that the Cu(100) surface has higher *CO coverage (Fig. 5C), which is attributed to the strong *CO binding on Cu(100) facets. Interestingly, a blue shift of the COatop peak is observed over −0.5 VRHE, which may be caused by the Stack turning effect (46), indicating a turnaround in the binding ability of COatop on the TD-Cu surface. Similarly, OH− concentration increases substantially at −0.5 VRHE, and it has been previously reported that adsorbed OH− can change the electronic structure of the surrounding metal atoms (47, 48). Therefore, the adsorption energy on Cu(100) with different OH− concentrations was calculated to find out the effect of OH− on *CO adsorption (Fig. 5 D and E). The adsorption energy of *CO is positively correlated with the OH− concentrates, and the *CO adsorption is promoted only when the value increases to 3/16 ML. Also, this might demonstrate why the products are mainly dominated by CO at low currents. All of these suggest that Cu(100) facets strengthen COatop adsorption and high OH− concentration provides a further improvement. The high coverage and strong adsorption of *CO do not mean superior C2+ selectivity, *CO dimerization is the decisive step during CO2R (49, 50). From in situ Raman spectroscopy, *CO coverage experiences a sudden drop at −0.6 VRHE on the TD-Cu surface, implying that C-C coupling can occur efficiently. To investigate whether the introduction of OH− would affect *CO dimerization, the reaction barriers of free energy barriers on Cu(100) with different OH− concentrations were calculated (Fig. 5F). The results show that an increase of OH− concentration has a positive effect on the free energy, probably because OH− can prompt the charge imbalance between carbon atoms in *OCCO and decrease the barriers of C-C coupling (47). Moreover, free energy barriers on a different plane with the same OH− concentration were also calculated to distinguish which played a leading role (Fig. 5G and SI Appendix, Fig. S25 and S26). The free energy differences between Cu(100) and other planes are 0.34 eV and 0.57 eV, respectively, while the improvement value is only 0.1 eV between Cu(100) and 1/16-Cu(100). Obviously, facets play a dominant role in the *CO dimerization and inhibit the selectivity of C1 products. In a word, the introduction of OH− to increase *CO coverage, instead of causing side effects, promotes *CO adsorption and dimerization, which is crucial to the achievement of sequential *CO management. Insight into the structural evolution process acted by in situ TA leaching effects, an ordered array with Cu(100) exposure was obtained via controllable reconstruction from CuTA nanosheets to sequential *CO management for highly industrial-current-density CO2R.

Discussion

In summary, using the synchronously leached TA molecule, an electrode combining nanowire nanostructure with high active Cu(100) planes was constructed by in situ reconstruction from CuTA nanosheets. In situ Raman spectroscopy and COMSOL multiphysics simulations demonstrate that the array structure provides rich confined space resulting in slower hydroxyl diffusion and thus improved *CO coverage by hindering the competing *H. The high coverage of key *CO intermediate in the catalyst surface can continuously supply the reactant to match its high-rate consumption at high current densities. Furthermore, DFT results further confirm that Cu(100) facets enhance *CO adsorption and lower the energy barrier of *CO dimerization and contribute to the high selectivity of C2+. Benefiting from this sequential *CO management, the catalyst showed a high FEC2+ of 86.1% and FEC2H4 of 60.8% at an industrial level current (0.7 A cm−2), which even represents the top level of activity and selectivity. Moreover, the catalyst presents satisfactory durability and maintains consistent electrolysis for 15 h without deactivation. We believe that this efficient and stable catalyst has the promising potential for application in the electrocatalytic reduction of CO2 to value-added chemicals and give some advice on product selectivity by managing *CO intermediates. A deep insight into reconstruction and catalytic processes by multiple in situ characterizations and theoretical simulation helps enhance the chemistry, catalysis, and material communities’ accessibilities to industrial-level performances of various electrocatalytic reactions.

Materials and Methods

Materials Characterization.

SEM (Nova Nano-SEM 450) and TEM (Tecnai G2-20) images were collected to obtain the morphology and structure information of samples. The thickness of the sample was characterized by the AFM (Bruker Dimension Icon). The information on chemical compositions and elemental oxidation states was acquired by XPS (AXIS-ULTRA DLD-600W, Shimadzu-Kratos, Japan). 2D-XRD patterns were obtained on VANTEC500. FTIR (Nicolet iS50R, Thermo Scientific Inc., USA) spectra were tested in a KBr tablet from 4,000 to 400 cm−1 at room temperature. Gas products were analyzed by a Shimadzu GC 2030 gas chromatograph. Liquid products were analyzed by an NMR spectroscopy (AscendTM 600 M, Bruker).

Synthesis of the CuTA Nanosheet and Control Samples.

CuTA nanosheets were synthesized via the coordination of metal ions with ligands. In detail, 0.20 g TA and 4.00 g CuSO4·5H2O were, respectively, dissolved in 100 mL ultrapure water to a homogeneous solution. Then, CuSO4 solution was added into the TA solution dropwise under continuous stirring conditions, and the color of the TA solution changed from faint yellow to brown. Next, the mixture above was placed in 60 °C water, and 10 mL KOH (1 M) solution was dropped into with a speed of 0.5 mL/min. Under this condition for 2 h, the sediments were extracted by centrifugal and washed several times with ultrapure water to remove the remaining TA molecules. Finally, the catalyst was obtained by drying in a vacuum at 60 °C overnight. CuO nanosheets were prepared by the procedure method reported (7). Cu(100) nanoparticles were synthesized via electrolytic deposition under the CO2 atmosphere; experimental details can be provided by the previous work (15).

Preparation of Working Electrode.

For the preparation of GDEs, 20 mg catalyst, 200 μL 5 wt% Nafion solution, 450 μL ultrapure water, and 1,350 μL isopropanol were mixed and sonicated for 1 h to form well-dispersed catalyst inks. To ensure a uniform distribution of the catalyst layer, a spray gun was used to airbrush inks onto a gas diffusion layer (3 cm × 3 cm, Sinoro, YLS-30T). Then, the above GDEs were heated at 60 °C for 0.5 h to remove residual isopropanol, avoiding negative effects on CO2R performance and product analysis. The loading density of the catalyst was approximately 1.2 mg/m2.

Electrochemical CO2 Reduction Measurements.

The CO2R performance of the as-prepared samples was measured in a home-made flow cell (Gaoss Union, 1 cm2 active area) with 1 M KOH as the electrolyte, and an anion-exchange membrane (FAB-PK-130, Fumasep) was used to separate catholyte and anolyte. A rod-like Pt foil (1 × 3 cm2) and the Ag/AgCl electrode were used as counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. The electrochemical measurements were conducted in a standard three-electrode system controlled by a CHI1140C, and all test results were processed without IR compensation. Throughout the test process, the CO2 flow rate was kept at 20 mL min−1, and KOH solution was pumped at the rate of 15 mL min−1. The gas products were detected by using an in situ connected gas chromatograph instrument (PANNA, A91lus). Liquid samples were collected after 30 min electrolysis and measured by 1H NMR with dimethyl sulfoxide as an internal standard. The Faradaic efficiency during the CO2R was calculated by the following equation:FE=QiQtotal=Ni∗ni∗FQtotal,

where Qi is the charge transferred into product i, Qtotal is the total charge consumed during CO2R, Ni is the product molar amount, ni is the electron transfer number, and F is the Faradaic constant.

ECSA and OH− Adsorption Measurements.

Electrochemical OH− adsorption was performed by referring to the previous reports (15). Simply, before the test, the prepared electrode was reduced for 30 min at 0.5 A cm−2 under flow-cell conditions and successfully translated into copper. Then the obtained electrode was tested in an N2-saturated 1 M KOH. The potential range of linear sweep voltammetry was from 0.2 to 0.6 V, and the sweep rate was 100 mV s−1. The electrochemical ECSA was evaluated under the same environment above except for potential range and sweep rate. The potential range was changed from −0.04 to −0.08 V, and sweep rates were 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 mV s−1.

In Situ Raman Spectroscopy and XAS.

In situ Raman Spectroscopy was conducted on a confocal microscope spectrometer (WITec Alpha 300 Raman) with a 633-nm solid laser as an excitation source. The laser power was 2 mW, and 1 M KOH was used as the electrolyte. The experiments were carried out in a homemade flow cell (purchased from Gaoss Union) with a quartz window to detect the signal from cathode GDE. In situ XAS at Cu K-edge have been collected at BL14W beamline by fluorescence mode at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. Experimental details can be consulted in our previous literature7. Every XAFS spectrum was tested two or three times. For the sake of consistency, the environment was consistent with the CO2R performance test above.

DFT Calculations.

We have performed the DFT calculations in our system by using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (51, 52). The PBE functional was used for describing electronic exchange (53). The projector-augmented wave method was adopted to describe the interactions between the ion cores and valence electrons (54, 55). The energy cutoff for the plane-wave basis set was 500 eV. The Brillouin zone was sampled by the Monkhorst–Pack scheme using a 3 × 3 × 1 k-point grid (56). During the geometry optimization and electronic structure calculation, the atomic positions were optimized until the energy and the maximum force were less than 10−5 eV/atom and 0.03 eV/Å, respectively. The van der Waals dispersion by employing the DFT-D3 method of Grimme was considered for all the calculation (57). In our simulations, we considered 0, 1, 2, and 3 OH− species on a surface of 16 (4×4) copper atoms, corresponding to 0 ML, 1/16 ML, 2/16 ML, and 3/16 ML concentrations, respectively.

The adsorption energy (Eads) was defined as:Eads=E(A−S)−ES−EA,

where E(A-S), ES, and EA are the total energy of the adsorbate-substrate (A-S), the substrate (S), and the adsorbate (A), respectively. According to the definition of adsorption energy, a larger negative value means a more stable adsorption configuration. To simplify the calculations about TA adsorption energy on Cu, gallic acid was used to represent the huge TA molecule (58).

COMSOL Multiphysics Simulations.

The current density distribution between the nanowire array electrode and nanoparticle stacking electrode was calculated by the two-dimensional finite element model. For consistency with experimental results, the diameter of the nanowire and particle was 50 nm, and the selected area size was about 1 µm × 1 µm. First, the “tertiary current distribution” module was employed to investigate the electrolyte current density distribution (19). Since C2H4 is the main product for CO2R, we only consider the main origin of OH− from the reaction: 2CO2 + 8H2O + 12e−→ C2H4 + 12OH−. The transport of the K+ and the anion was solved by the Nernst–Planck equation. The electrolyte conductivity was assumed to be 22 S/m, and the exchange current density i0 was set to 10−4 A m−2 based on previous work (59). The upper boundary was set as the electrolyte boundary with a current density at 700 mA cm−2. Second, the “Transport of Diluted Species” module was used to solve the transport of OH− species in 1 M KOH. The diffusion constant of OH− was 5.27 × 10−9 m2/s.
