Nasal Colonizing Vancomycin‐Resistant and Intermediate Staphylococcus aureus Among Admitted Patients: A Hospital Based Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Biniyam Kijineh Mengistu, Tsegaye Alemayehu, Techilo Habtemariam Mengesha, Musa Mohammed Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Aim Staphylococcus aureus colonizing the nasal cavity poses a potential risk for infections. Vancomycin is a primary treatment for invasive infections caused by penicillin and methicillin‐resistant S. aureus (MRSA). However, reports of vancomycin‐resistant S. aureus (VRSA) have emerged, highlighting it as a high‐priority pathogen requiring attention. There is limited information on the epidemiology of VRSA and vancomycin‐intermediate S. aureus (VISA) from the Sidama regional state. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of VRSA and VISA among S. aureus colonizing patients admitted to Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HUCSH), as well as to identify associated factors and assess the antimicrobial susceptibility profile.MethodsA hospital‐based prospective cross‐sectional study was conducted from April to June 2023. Socio‐demographic and clinical data were collected using an interviewer‐administered questionnaire. Nasal swabs were obtained from 378 admitted patients, and S. aureus was identified using standard bacteriological methods. VRSA was determined using the Epsilometer test (E‐test), while the antimicrobial susceptibility profile was assessed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22, with a p‐value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsOut of 92 isolated S. aureus strains, 12 (13.04%) were classified as VRSA, 27 (29.3%) as VISA, and 15 (16.3%) as MRSA. The carriage rates among admitted patients were 12 (3.2%) for VRSA (95% CI: 1.7−5.5%) and 27 (7.14%) for VISA (95% CI: 4.8−10.2%). The overall nasal carriage rate of S. aureus was 92 (24.3%) (95% CI: 20.1−29%), with MRSA found in 15 (3.97%) (95% CI: 2.2−6.5%). Among the VRSA isolates, 11 (91.7%) showed susceptibility to tigecycline. Additionally, 40 (43.5%) of the S. aureus strains were positive for inducible clindamycin resistance. Patients with a history of hospitalization in the intensive care unit were 37 times more likely to be colonized with VRSA (p = 0.001), while those with domestic animals were 22 times more likely (p = 0.021).ConclusionsThis study revealed a significant proportion of VRSA and VISA among S. aureus isolates from hospitalized patients in the region. More than 80% of VRSA isolates were susceptible to tigecycline. A history of hospitalization in the intensive care unit and ownership of domestic animals were associated with an increased likelihood of VRSA colonization.

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the most important public health problems in the world disproportionately affecting low‐income countries [1]. Worldwide dissemination of drug‐resistant bacteria with few developments of antimicrobial agents is a formidable challenge that the physicians of today are facing [1].

Staphylococcus aureus is among potential pathogenic bacteria with diverse virulence factors and the potential to develop resistance to commonly used antimicrobial agents. S. aureus are colonizer of the skin and upper respiratory tract without causing diseases. If there are underlying conditions it can cause community and hospital‐acquired infections such as soft tissue infections, bacteremia, endocarditis, pneumonia, meningitis, and osteomyelitis [2].

S. aureus is a frequent inhabitant of the human upper respiratory tract and the skin [3]; however, the carriage rate varies based on geographic location, age, gender, and ethnicity [4]. The nasal carriage of S. aureus has been identified as a risk factor for community‐acquired and nosocomial infections [5].

S. aureus has developed resistance to penicillin and methicillin over time, which makes infection caused by S. aureus difficult to treat [6]. Vancomycin, a glycopeptide antibiotic, has been used as a last‐line antibiotic for treating life‐threatening infections caused by Methicillin‐resistant S. aureus (MRSA) [7]. This option is being challenged by the emergence and spread of S. aureus which are completely resistant to vancomycin or reduced susceptibility to vancomycin [7, 8].

S. aureus strains exhibiting reduced susceptibility to vancomycin, vancomycin intermediate‐resistant S. aureus (VISA), were discovered in the 1990s. S. aureus isolates with complete resistance to vancomycin, vancomycin‐resistant S. aureus (VRSA), was first reported in the United States in 2002. The burden of VISA is higher than VRSA; it is associated with persistent infections, vancomycin treatment failure, and poor clinical outcomes [7, 8]. VRSA was among the list of antibiotic‐resistant microorganisms identified by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2017 for which new antibiotics should be developed [9]. Furthermore, VRSA remained in the 2019 WHO updated list as a high‐priority pathogen [10].

Infections caused by VRSA have been associated with high morbidity and mortality rates due to the limited availability of effective drugs, particularly in low‐income countries. The prevalence of VRSA reported from various parts of Ethiopia [11, 12, 13] is mostly based on the disc diffusion method which is not recommended by Clinical Laboratory Standard Institute (CLSI). There are few studies from Ethiopia which used E‐test and Micro‐dilution methods for the determination of VRSA [14, 15]. In addition to searching for alternative treatments for infection caused by VRSA, it is important to monitor the epidemiology of VRSA using method recommended by CLSI. Considering the scarcity of data regarding VRSA in the southern parts of Ethiopia, this study aimed to determine the nasal carriage rate of VRSA, VISA, MRSA, and ICR S. aureus among admitted patients, its predictors, and antimicrobial susceptibility profile.

Methods

Study Design and Study Population

A hospital‐based prospective cross‐sectional study was conducted among patients admitted to Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HUCSH) from April to June 2023. The hospital is located in Hawassa city, which is the capital city of Sidama regional state, Ethiopia. HUCSH is a referral hospital that provides health‐related services for people coming from the Sidama regional state and other neighboring regional states. It has 284 functional beds and 12 wards such as surgical ward, medical ward, ophthalmology ward, neurology ward, gynecology ward, orthopedics ward, pediatrics ward, pedi‐surgery ward, oncology ward, adult and neonatal intensive care units, and trauma ward. We have included six wards for the present study based on the patient flow.

In this study, we included patients who were admitted to the HUCSH during the study period for treatment. Patients who were critically sick and had nasal bleeding were excluded from the study. The sample size was determined based on a single sample size estimation formula. We have considered the prevalence of VRSA (4.1%) which was reported from Debre Markos, Ethiopia [16]. By assuming a 5% for nonresponse rate, the sample size was 378.

A total of 2222 patients were admitted from September to December. The sample size was proportionally allocated to seven wards by multiplying, 378/2222 (0.17), with a number of patients admitted at each ward between September to December 2022 (Figure 1). A systematic random sampling technique was used to select study participants. The k‐value was determined based on the total number of patients allocated to each ward. The first participant was selected using the lottery method.

Data Collection

A structured questionnaire prepared on kobo‐collect version 2023.1.2 (United States) was used to collect socio‐demographic and clinical data. The questionnaire was prepared after reviewing similar published articles [5, 12, 17, 18]. To ensure consistency, the questionnaire was first prepared in English, translated into Amharic, and then translated back into English. The data was collected using the Amharic version of the questionnaire. The questionnaire was pretested in a population representing 5% (n = 19) of the sample size at Adare General Hospital, Hawassa, Ethiopia.

The anterior nostrils were swabbed aseptically using sterile cotton soaked in saline and placed in a tube containing normal saline. The swabs were inoculated onto mannitol salt agar (MSA) (Oxoid, Hampshire UK) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h in an aerobic environment. After 24 h of incubation, MSA plates were inspected for visible growth of bacteria. In cases where there was no visible growth of bacteria, plates were re‐incubated for an additional 24 h. For bacterial growth with yellow colonies on MSA, Gram staining was performed. Gram‐positive cocci bacteria in a cluster on microscopic examination were tested using catalase, coagulase, and modified oxidase test. Bacteria were considered as S. aureus if it appeared yellow on MSA, catalase positive, coagulase‐positive and modified oxidase test negative. Modified oxidase test to differentiate S. aureus from Micrococcus which may appear yellow on MSA and can wrongly be considered as S. aureus. S. aureus isolates were tested for VRSA, MRSA, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Before use, the sterility of all culture media used in this study was checked by incubating 5% of each batch of the culture media without inoculation. For performance testing of Gram staining, culture media, biochemical tests, and antibiotics reference strain (S. aureus ATCC 25923 and S. epidermidis) were used.

Detection of Vancomycin Resistant S. aureus

The pure isolate of S. aureus was suspended in a phosphate buffer solution until it matched the McFarland 0.5 standard. The suspension was inoculated over the surface of Muller Hinton Agar (MHA) uniformly using a sterile cotton swab dipped into a suspension. Vancomycin E‐test (bioMérieux SA, Marcy‐1'Étoile, France) was applied to MHA in which S. aureus was inoculated and incubated for 24 h aerobically at 37°C. The reading was interpreted according to Clinical Laboratory Standard Institute (CLSI) guidelines as susceptible, intermediate, and resistant [19]. Minimum inhibitory concentrations of ≤ 2 μg/ml, 4–8 μg/ml, and ≥ 16 μg/ml were interpreted as susceptible (S), intermediate (I), and resistant (R) respectively.

To detect MRSA, S. aureus suspension was prepared and inoculated onto MHA as mentioned above; a cefoxitin (30 μg) antibiotic was applied and incubated for 18 h in ambient air at 35°C. Zone of inhibition < 21 mm was interpreted as MRSA. Inducible clindamycin resistance (ICR) was identified using a d‐test which involves the application of erythromycin (E‐15 μg) and clindamycin (CD‐2 μg) disks spaced 15−26 mm on MHA as per CLSI guidelines [19]. Flattening of the zone of inhibition adjacent to the erythromycin disc and cloudy growth within the zone of inhibition around clindamycin indicates ICR.

Antibiotic Susceptibility Testing

The disc diffusion method was used to determine the antibiotic susceptibility profile of S. aureus as described in CLSI guidelines [19]. The suspension of S. aureus was prepared and inoculated onto MHA as described above. The antibiotics used for disc diffusion assays include cefoxitin (30 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole (1.25/23.7, 5 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), clindamycin (2 µg), azithromycin (15 µg), tigecycline (15 µg), aztreonam (30 µg), doxycycline (30 µg), penicillin G (10 unit). MHA was incubated at 35−37°C for 24 h. The zones of inhibition were measured using a caliper, and interpreted as susceptible (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R).

Data Analysis and Interpretation

Data was cleaned, transformed, coded, and analyzed using SPSS version 22. Data was presented in frequency and percent. The association between independent and dependent variables was determined using the binary logistic regression model; variables with p ≤ 0.25 in bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model to identify variables that predict VRSA colonization. In the analysis, the a priori level of significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 for determining statistical significance. The tests conducted were two‐sided, allowing for the evaluation of associations in both directions between the independent and dependent variables.

Operational Definition

Constitutive Methicillin‐Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (cMLSB)

This refers to strains of Staphylococcus aureus that are resistant to both erythromycin and clindamycin.

Inducible Methicillin‐Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (iMLSB)

This describes strains of Staphylococcus aureus that are resistant to erythromycin but susceptible to clindamycin.

Ethical Approval and Consent to Participate

Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (IRB) of Hawassa University College of Medicine and Health Sciences with Ref No of IRB/171/15. Written informed consent was obtained from adults participants. For minors, both consent and assent was obtained. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Socio Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 378 admitted patients at HUCSH were included in the study. The majority (n = 215; 56.9%) of the study participants were males. The mean age of study participants was 44.5 with a standard deviation (SD) of 19.2 years. Most of the study participants (n = 160; 42.3%) were from Sidama Regional State. More than half of the participants were on IV or catheter while 126 (33.3%) reported repeated use of antibiotics (Table 1).

Prevalence of S. aureus, VRSA, and MRSA

In the present study, 92(24.3% (95% CI: 20.1–29%)) S. aureus were recovered from nasal swab of all study participants. 15(16.3%) 95% CI: 9.4–25.5% of S. aureus were MRSA. Out of the total S. aureus isolated, 12(13.04%) 95% CI: 6.9−21.7% and 27(29.3%) 95% CI; 20.3−39.8% were VRSA and VISA. Out of 15 MRSA, 3 were vancomycin resistant. Out of the total S. aureus isolated, 21 (22.83%) were susceptible to both erythromycin and clindamycin whereas 31(33.7%) were resistant to both erythromycin and clindamycin indicating constitutive resistance (cMLSB). Forty (43.5%) of S. aureus were resistant to erythromycin and susceptible to clindamycin indicating ICR (iMLSB). The prevalence of VRSA, VISA, MRSA, and overall S. aureus colonization rate among admitted patients were 12(3.2%) 95% CI: 1.7−5.5%, 27(7.14%) 95% CI: 4.8−10.2%, 15(3.97%) 95% CI: 2.2−6.5%, and 92(24.3%) 95% CI: 20.1−29% respectively.

Factors Associated with VRSA Colonization

In bivariate logistic regression analysis, recurrent wound (p = 0.04, COR = 3.69(95% CI:1.06 − 12.9)), nasal infection (p = 0.024, COR = 4.4(95% CI:1.21−15.9)), history of hospitalization (p = 0.033, COR = 4.5(95% CI: 1.13−17.9)), repeated use of antibiotics (p = 0.04, COR = 3.93(95% CI:1.08−14.2)), history of hospitalization at ICU (p < 0.0001, COR = 31.4(95% CI:6.05−162.7)) and having domestic animal at home (p = 0.019, COR = 12.16(95% CI: 1.49−98.6)) showed p‐value less than 0.25. Variables with p < 0.25 in the bivariate logistic regression model were included in multivariable logistic analysis. History of hospitalization at ICU (p = 0.001, AOR = 37.2(95% CI: 4.32−320.8)) and having a domestic animal at home (p = 0.021, AOR = 22.37(95% CI: 1.59–313.1)) were significantly associated with VRSA colonization in multivariable logistic regression analysis (Table 2).

Discussion

Infections caused by vancomycin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (VRSA) are public health concerns that urgently require attention from both international and national health sectors. The limited treatment options for VRSA infections highlight the critical need for comprehensive epidemiological assessments to combat its spread within communities. To date, only a few studies in Ethiopia have identified VRSA using the minimum inhibitory concentration method, indicating a significant gap in our understanding of this pathogen's prevalence.

The proportion of VRSA in our study is 12(13.04%) which is concerning, as VRSA strains represent a threat to effective treatment, given that vancomycin is often considered a last‐resort antibiotic for treating resistant S. aureus infections. The emergence of VRSA indicates the urgent need for enhanced infection control measures and antibiotic stewardship programs to mitigate the development and spread of such resistant strains. Our finding is consistent with studies from Eritrea (15.9%) [18], Pakistan (11–15%) [20], Egypt (13.8%) [21], and Ethiopia (14–15%) [6, 15, 16, 22], as well as Indonesia (11%) [23]. However, this prevalence is higher than many studies conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia (4–7%) [13, 14, 24], the global pooled estimate (6%) [25], Yemen (5.12%) [26], and Egypt (4.5%) [27]. Conversely, studies from Nigeria (44.5%) [3], Bangladesh (27.6%) [26], and Egypt (24%) [28] reported higher VRSA prevalence rates. The lowest VRSA rate (4.1%) in Ethiopia was reported from Jigjiga [13]. These variations could be attributed to differences in study populations, specimen collection methods, laboratory techniques, study periods, and antibiotic usage practices. Importantly, the current study did not determine the origin of VRSA among patients, who were admitted for various medical conditions, indicating a need for further investigation into how these infections are acquired, whether in the community or during hospitalization.

Additionally, the study identified 27(29.3%) isolates as VISA. The presence of VISA strains raises further concerns, as these bacteria exhibit reduced susceptibility to vancomycin, potentially leading to treatment failures. The relatively high proportion of VISA alongside the other resistant strains suggests a concerning trend in the landscape of S. aureus resistance in the study area. The implications of these findings are insightful, highlighting the critical need for continuous surveillance of S. aureus resistance patterns in clinical settings.

Participants with a history of ICU hospitalization were more likely to be colonized with VRSA (p = 0.001). This aligns with previous studies by Wu et al. [25] and Shariati et al. [29], suggesting that the misuse of antimicrobial agents and the use of contaminated medical devices may contribute to the increased risk of colonization in hospital settings. Additionally, the observation that participants with domestic animals at home were also more likely to be colonized with VRSA indicates that domestic animals could be a potential source of infection. While this study was not designed to determine the source of VRSA, it raises important questions about the role of animal contact in the epidemiology of VRSA.

The resistance profile of VRSA isolates is particularly concerning. The study found that all VRSA isolates were resistant to aztreonam, which is consistent with findings from a study conducted in Hyderabad [30]. Additionally, over 25% of the isolates were resistant to azithromycin and sulfamethoxazole‐trimethoprim, although these rates are lower than those reported in a study from Egypt [21]. The fact that only one isolate was resistant to tigecycline is a positive note, as this antibiotic remains a critical treatment option for resistant infections, similar to observations from a study in Pakistan [20].

The overall nasal carriage rate of S. aureus was found to be 24.3%, comparable to rates reported from Ethiopia (28.8%) [17], China (24.7%) [31], and Iran (21.3%) [20]. However, it was lower than the rates observed in studies conducted in Iraq (39.4%) [32] and other parts of Ethiopia (32–39%) [15, 33, 34]. The higher nasal carriage rate noted in this study compared to other Ethiopian regions (13–16%) [32, 35] suggests that local factors may influence colonization rates, underscoring the importance of targeted public health interventions.

The results of this study reveal significant insights into the antibiotic susceptibility profile of S. aureus isolates. Among the total isolates, 21 (22.83%) were found to be susceptible to both erythromycin and clindamycin. This suggests that a portion of S. aureus strains retains susceptible to these commonly used antibiotics, which is encouraging for treatment options in clinical settings. On the other hand, 31(33.7%) isolates showed resistance to both erythromycin and clindamycin, indicating constitutive resistance (cMLSB). This finding highlights the presence of strains that may pose a substantial challenge in clinical management due to their resistance profile. The implications of cMLSB are critical, as these strains are likely to show resistance to other macrolide‐lincosamide antibiotics, limiting therapeutic options for patients infected with these pathogens.

Additionally, 40(43.5%) isolates were resistant to erythromycin but susceptible to clindamycin, indicating inducible clindamycin resistance (iMLSB). This type of resistance can be particularly insidious, as these strains may appear susceptible to clindamycin in vitro, leading to potential treatment failures if clindamycin is used without prior susceptibility testing.

Our study indicated that male participants were 3.67 times more likely to be colonized with S. aureus, which contradicts findings from other studies conducted in Jimma, Ethiopia [36]. This discrepancy may be related to behavioral differences between males and females in various geographic locations. Furthermore, participants who did not use antibiotics without a physician's prescription were 72% less likely to be colonized with S. aureus, emphasizing the critical role of responsible antibiotic use in preventing the emergence of resistant strains. The study also found that individuals with a history of ICU hospitalization were 3.34 times more likely to be colonized with S. aureus, aligning with the findings of Humphreys et al. [37], thereby reinforcing the connection between healthcare settings and increased colonization risk.

The findings of this study indicate prevalence of MRSA among the isolated strains, with 15(16.3%) identified as MRSA. This level of MRSA prevalence is significant, suggesting that S. aureus infections within the studied population may pose a substantial risk, particularly in healthcare settings where MRSA is known to complicate treatment options and patient outcomes. Our MRSA finding is lower than MRSA reported in a study from Nigeria [38]. However, resistance rates of 69.6% and 25% were observed for erythromycin and clindamycin, respectively, which are lower than those reported in studies from Iran [39]. The magnitude of MRSA detected in this study (16.3%) aligns with reports from various parts of the world (11.2–17.3%) [12, 14, 32, 40], although it is lower than rates reported in studies from Pakistan (76%) [20], Iran (74.2%) [41], Yemen (65%) [26], and various regions of Ethiopia (30–45%) [15, 16, 17, 34].

Interestingly, male participants were about 84% less likely to be colonized with MRSA, which corresponds with findings from other parts of Ethiopia [16, 37]. Similar to the patterns observed for S. aureus and VRSA colonization, participants with a history of ICU hospitalization were about eight times more likely to be colonized with MRSA compared to those without such a history. These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted interventions in healthcare settings to prevent the spread of S. aureus and its resistant forms, ultimately improving health outcomes for affected populations.

Limitation of the Study

The present study did not employ the broth dilution method for the determination of VRSA. As a result, this study should be interpreted while considering the inherent limitations associated with the use of the E‐test technique for VRSA detection.

Conclusions

The study found that the proportion of vancomycin‐resistant and vancomycin‐intermediate Staphylococcus aureus was relatively high in the study area. History of hospitalization in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) and having domestic animals at home were significantly associated with VRSA colonization. Further analysis revealed that most of the VRSA isolates were resistant to aztreonam, azithromycin, clindamycin, and gentamicin. In contrast, more than 80% of the VRSA isolates were susceptible to tigecycline and doxycycline. While a subset of S. aureus isolates remains susceptible to some antibiotics, the notable levels of inducible clindamycin resistance highlight concern for clinical management.
