Alleviation of the effect of salinity on pomegranate seedlings by priming foliar spray of chitosan-silicon nanoparticles (CTS-SiO2 NPs)
Authors: Zeinab Mohammadi, Seyed Morteza Zahedi, Sima Panahirad, Gholamreza Gohari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundToday, improving the nutritional status of plants using external supplements, particularly based on nanotechnology, is of great importance to compensate for the damage caused by abiotic stresses. Salinity is one of the chief environmental stresses limiting the growth and yield of crops by reducing the osmotic potential and disrupting the absorption of water and nutrients. The present study was conducted to compare the priming effect of foliar spraying the following treatments: silicon nanoparticles (SiO2 NPs; 50 mg L−1), chitosan nanoparticles (CTS NPs; 0.1%), chitosan-silicon nanoparticles (CTS-SiO2 NPs; 25 and 50 mg L−1) and distilled water (control). These treatments were assayed on reducing the effects of sodium chloride (NaCl) salinity stress (0 and 150 mM) on two-year old pomegranate seedlings.ResultsThe results showed that under salinity stress, foliar application of combined chitosan-silicon treatment provided a better role in moderating the negative effects of stress than the single use of these nanomaterials and improved seedling height (HT), diameter (DIA), leaf area (LAI), fresh and dry weight, chlorophyll a and carotenoid content, membrane stability index (MSI), APX, concentrations P, K, and K/Na ratio. The combined chitosan-silicon treatment also reduced the rate of electrolyte leakage (EL) under stress conditions. Chitosan foliar application alone increased leaf number (LN) and reduced nitrogen and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) levels under stress. Silicon also reduced the amount of MDA and Na.ConclusionsTherefore, it seems that the application of CTS-SiO2 NPs could be considered as an effective method to reduce the negative effects of salt stress on pomegranate. This study represents one of the early scientific investigations demonstrating the synergistic potential of CTS-SiO2 nanoparticles in boosting pomegranate tolerance to salinity stress, highlighting its novelty while acknowledging that further research is needed for definitive confirmation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07016-5.Keywords: Nanotechnology, Osmolyte, Oxidative stress, Salinity, Plants

Introduction

Pomegranate (Punica granatum L.), cultivated and consumed since 3000 BC, belongs to the Punicaceae family and is one of the important products of tropical and subtropical regions of the world [33]. In recent years, due to the high economic importance and nutritional value of pomegranates, many countries have increasingly focused on this fruit. This growing attention has led to expanded cultivation, increased production, and improved harvesting methods to better meet market and consumer demands. Globally, over 300,000 hectares are dedicated to pomegranate cultivation, with 76% of this area located in China, India, Iran, Turkey, and the United States. Approximately 80% of the world’s pomegranate production comes from India (2.44 million tons), China (1.6 million tons), and Iran (1.1 million tons). In Iran, the provinces of Fars, Markazi, and Yazd are the top producers [1]. Pomegranate has medicinal and high nutritional properties thanks to its high antioxidant activity and bioactive phenolic compounds [12]. Pomegranate is a relatively salt-resistant plant due to its high ability to store sodium (Na+) in the roots as well as transporting potassium (K+) and calcium (Ca) to the leaves, a golden strategy for pomegranates in dealing with salinity [40]. On the other hand, most of the pomegranate cultivation is performed in arid and semi-arid regions of the world, and soil salinity and water-deficit stress are the main limitations of achieving proper yield [58].

Among the abiotic limitations, soil salinity is one of the utmost chief factors that significantly reduces crop productivity [65]. During the past few decades, soil salinity has increased due to human activities, including irrigation with saline water, improper use of fertilizers, and industrial pollution. It is estimated that by 2050, almost half of the agricultural land will be affected by salinity [3]. The accumulation of solutes in the soil, especially Na+ and chloride (Cl)ions, causes salinity of agricultural lands leading to ionic toxicity, osmotic stress, hormonal imbalance, reduced nutrient mobility, and production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which ultimately results in oxidative stress in plants, and finally adversely affects plant growth [4, 38]. Salinity stress leads to various physiological and molecular changes and inhibits plant growth by inhibiting photosynthesis [60].‌ As an example, Gohari et al. [24] reported that the growth of corn salad (Valerianella locusta L.) decreased under salt stress. In recent years, researchers have made significant advances in creating stress management methods, amongst which are applications of engineered nanoparticles, which have demonstrated effectiveness in increasing product yield.

‌Nanoparticles (NPs) potentially act as effective stimulants, especially for germination, growth and yield of various plants. NPs mitigate the adverse environmental effects without negatively affecting soil quality or biodiversity, as well [30]. NPs have been demonstrated to reduce the consequences of salinity stress in plants by regulating the ion balance [18], water and nutrient uptake, photosynthetic pigments and processes, antioxidant system (antioxidant compounds and enzymes), membrane stability and index (MSI) as well as ROS accumulation [22].

Silicon (Si) is the second most abundant element in the earth’s crust, classified as a non-essential mineral, nonetheless with beneficial impacts in plants processes [44, 45]. Si application can positively affect physiological and cellular activities as well as metabolic procedure of plants. Consequently, Si supplementation has various profits for plants, especially reducing the effect of biotic and abiotic stresses [50]. It has been proven that the use of Si is effective treatment in reducing ROS caused by abiotic stressors [4]. The use of this element, whether in nano or bulk form, has a significant efficiency in protecting plants from salinity stress thanks to its effect in altering the cell wall and regulating the genes involved in several pathways including stress protection [50, 57]. For instance, Si application in strawberry reduced H2O2 accumulation and lipid peroxidation under salinity stress [57]. Likewise, Si application in the NPs form (Si NPs) had a significant effect on reducing salinity stress effects in tomato plants [5]. The application of Si NP to mineral nutrition management practices reduced the opposing effects of salinity in fruit trees [46]. It has also been reported that the use of Si in the form of SiO2 has reduced the negative effects of stress in “Valencia” sweet orange plants [43]. All highlight the potential of Si particularly in nanoform and probably with nanocarriers to act better in combating stress conditions.

Chitosan (CTS), a biopolymer obtained from the deacetylation of chitin, is found in the exoskeleton of crustaceans [64]. In addition, nanochitosan (CTS NPs) based on the properties, has important antioxidant and antibacterial activity as well as of the ability to induce an immune response in plants, and can help in the management of biotic and abiotic stresses [31]. For instance, CTS application on pepper plants under salinity stress improved chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, proline accumulation and regulation of enzymes activity, and finally increased fruit yield [7]. CTS had a significant effect in reducing the effects of salinity stress in Moringa oleifera Lam via [17]. Moreover, CTS could be a potential carrier for many biostimulants and other compounds especially in nanoform, improving their efficiency particularly to lessen stress effects, previously confirmed [22, 48], giving the theory behind the current study for CTS-SiO2 NPs synthesizing and applying to decrease salinity effects.

Priming can be described as the physiological process by which plants are stimulated to demonstrate either improvement of biomarkers of growth or tolerance to stresses [34]. Priming is a phenomenon that leads to a modified physiological state causing better and stronger responses of plants exposed to stressors, achieving by activating several defense pathways in plants as well as changes in primary and secondary metabolism [23]. Several studies have applied various compounds through priming method to enhance plant performance under stress conditions in which some applied engineered NPs [23, 59], expressing their combination could be beneficial for preparing plants to deal with stress conditions.

Taking into account the increasing salinity in agricultural areas, there is a need for further research to introduce better and more efficient compounds in order to reduce adverse effects besides being safe for environment. Considering nanotechnology improvements in this regard by introducing engineered NPs using CTS as nanocarrier for more effectiveness, the current study aimed to combine CTS with Si (in SiO2 form) and evaluate some physiobiochemical effects of CTS-SiO2 NPs foliar application besides SiO2 and CTS NPs applications on pomegranate seedlings cv. Malas Saveh under salinity stress condition through priming method.

Although it is known that salinity causes issues such as reduced water uptake, ion imbalance, and increased oxidative stress in plants, the use of nanoparticles to reduce salinity effects in pomegranate has not been well studied. There are few studies on focused nano-delivery methods, nano-formulations, and how they affect antioxidant responses and the regulation of free radicals specifically in pomegranate.

Materials and methods

Plant material and treatments

This study was conducted to investigate the effect of CTS NPs, SiO2 NPs and “CTS-SiO2 NPs”, as priming treatments, via foliar application to aerial parts of two-year-old pomegranate seedlings cv. “Malas Saveh” under salinity stress conditions (0 and 150 mM) in the research greenhouse of Department of Horticultural Sciences of University of Maragheh (Maragheh, East Azarbaijan, Iran; 37º30ˊ N, 46º12ˊ E, altitude 1477.7 m). Pomegranate seedlings cv. Malas Saveh were purchased from a commercial nursery approved by Markazi agricultural and natural resources research and education center located in Saveh city. The seedlings were planted in 7 kg plastic pots (length and diameter of 20*20*15 at the top and bottom) with a combination of field soil and sand in a ratio of 2:1 (v: v) (Table S1) and irrigated with tap water every other day. After establishing the plants in the pots, the height was equalized before applying the treatments; they received Hoagland nutrient solution five times every three days to have more strength. Priming of plants was done by first spraying the plants before applying salt stress and then the plants were stressed.

Foliar application of the treatments: Distilled water (control), SiO2 NPs (50 mg L−1), CTS NPs (0.1%), and CTS-SiO2 NPs (25 and 50 mg L−1) were applied four times (~ 15 mL) for each seedling until the leaves were completely wet, with an interval of three days. The concentration of CTS-SiO2 NPs was selected according to the best individual concentrations reported for each of the two components, silicon and chitosan. The first and last foliar applications were applied on July 19 and 31, respectively. After applying the last foliar application (31 July), salinity stress was applied at two levels [0 Mm (EC = 234 µs) and 150 mM (EC = 18.61 ms)] for approximately three months. Also, in order to prevent salt accumulation, the media were washed every 10 days with distilled water in such a way that drainage removed dried salt concentrations from the bottom of the pots. Sampling was performed at the end of the experiment (September 10) (Fig. 1). Low-molecular-weight chitosan (CAS No. 9012-76-4) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, while SiO2 nanoparticles were obtained from NANOSANY Corporation, Mashhad, Iran. The CTS-SiO2 nanoparticles were synthesized using a modified Gohari et al. [22] method at the Nanochemistry Laboratory, University of Maragheh, Iran. In this process, 0.1 g of low molecular weight chitosan was mixed in 25 mL of 1% acetic acid solution at 70 °C for 2 h. Then, 0.1 g of SiO2 nanoparticle dispersed in 1 mL of distilled water was added and stirred for 1 h. Cross-linking was performed by dropwise addition of tripolyphosphate (TPP) at a TPP to chitosan ratio of 2.5:1, causing nanoparticle coagulation. After overnight stirring, the nanoparticles were washed and dried under vacuum using freeze-drying.

Growth parameters

Height (HT), diameter (DIA), leave numbers (LN), fresh and dry weights of seedling were recorded at the end of the experiment as well as the leaf area (LAI), calculating using Image J software.

Photosynthetic pigments (chlorophyll a, b and carotenoids), chlorophyll fluorescence Y (II) and canopy temperature parameters

Fresh leaves (0.5 g) were digested with 5 mL of 80% acetone. After centrifugation, 6,000 rpm; 10 min and 4 °C, the absorption of the supernatant was finally read by a spectrophotometer (UV1800 Shimadzu, Japan) at 663, 645 and 470 nm [9]:

Quantum yield of photosystem II [Y(II)] was measured using portable photosynthesis meter (Walz, Mini PAm Effeltrich, Germany) at the end of the experiment. The relative humidity of the air at the top of the leaf (RHa), the air temperature of the leaf (Ta), and the temperature of the leaf (Tl) were measured using a portable device (KOREA TECH Digital porometer 1301). The measurements were made on a sunny day from 10 to 12.

Electrolyte leakage (EL) and membrane stability index (MSI)

Leaf disks (0.5 cm diameter) were prepared from fully developed new leaves of the approximately same size and kept in a test tube containing 10 mL of distilled water. The tubes were incubated for 24 h at room temperature and the electrical conductivity of the samples was read by an electrical conductivity meter (JENWAY 4510) (EC1). Total phenol

Folin–Ciocalteu method was used to measure the amount of total phenol. The leaf sample (0.2 g) was digested with 2 mL of acidic methanol. The homogenate was centrifuged for 10 min at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C. Then, 1590 µL of distilled water, 100 mL of 100% Folin–Ciocalteu reagent were combined with 20 mL of the resulting extract, after 10 min, 300 mL of sodium carbonate was added to the solution and after 2 h of darkness, the absorbance was read at 765 nm. Total phenol concentration was expressed in mg of gallic acid [56].

Malondialdehyde content (MDA)

First, leaf sample (0.1 g) was digested with 2 mL of 0.1% TCA and then, the homogenate was centrifuged for 10 min at 1,000 rpm at 4 °C. Next, 1 mL of thiobarbituric acid (0.1% W/V) (TBA) solution containing 20% ​​(W/V) trichloroacetic acid was added to 0.5 mL of the supernatant. Finally, the absorbance of the mixture was measured by the spectrophotometer at two wavelengths of 532 nm and 600 nm. MDA was calculated based on the following formula in terms of nmol g−1 per g fresh weight [29].

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)

The leaf sample (0.5 g) was digested with 5 mL of 1% trichloroacetic acid and centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C. After that, 500 µL of the extract was added to 0.5 mL of phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH = 7) and 1 mL of 1 M potassium iodide. Using the spectrophotometer, the absorbance of the samples was read at a wavelength of 390 nm. The exact amounts was achieved using the standard curve [61].

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) enzyme activity

Briefly, after obtaining enzymatic extract through extracting leaf samples with phosphate buffer, 0.1 mL of 1.5 M sodium carbonate, 0.2 mL of 0.2 M methionine, 0.1 mL of 3 mM EDTA, 1.5 mL of 100 mM buffer phosphate, 9 mL of distilled water, 0.1 mL of nitrobilitarzevolium and 0.05 mL of enzymatic extract along with 0.1 mL of 60 µM riboflavin. The absorbance of the samples was read using the spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 560 nm [25].

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) enzyme activity

The leaf sample (0.5 g) was digested with 2 mL of 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH = 8). Then, after centrifuging (10 min, 12,000 rpm, 4 °C), 2550 µL of ascorbate and 450 µL of H2O2 were added to 50 µL of the extract and the absorbance was read at a wavelength of 290 nm [53].

Elemental analyses

Dried-ash leaves (at 500 ºC) were digested by the method of Jones [32] to measure sodium (Na+), potassium (K+), and phosphorus (P+) concentrations. Na+ and K+ of levels were determined by flame photometer (Jenway PSP7, England) whereas P+ level was established by atomic absorption spectrometry (Pekin Elmer 3110, USA). The N content was determined using the Kjeldahl method as described by Emami [19].

Data analysis

This experiment was conducted as a factorial design based on a completely randomized block design, consisting of 2 salinity levels and 5 foliar spray treatments, with 3 replications. (2*5*3 = 30). The experimental data were analyzed by the two-way analysis of variance using the SAS software version 9.1. Statistical significance was determined by Duncan’s multiple range test at 5% significance level (ρ < 0.05). The loading plot and score plot of all analyzed parameters were determined after principal component analysis (PCA) by using the https://www.bioinformatics.com online program package. Pearson correlation coefficient, and dendrogram clustering were carried out by using R v3.4.3 (www.r-project.org).

Results

Growth parameters

The results of the present study showed that salinity of 150 mM reduced the morphological parameters of pomegranate seedlings including HT, LN and LAI by 23.11, 52.67 and 30.64%, respectively, compared to control. Also, salinity stress decreased the DIA and fresh and dry weight of seedlings by 33.40, 14.09 and 15.09% compared to control, although there was no statistically significant difference. Treatments demonstrated no significant effect on the parameters under control and salinity conditions. On the other hand, foliar application with CTS-SiO2 NPs at 50 mg L−1 concentration increased seedling HT and DIA by 21.56 and 50%, respectively under salinity stress condition. The highest LN under salinity stress was observed in seedlings foliar sprayed with CTS NPs (154.33). Under salinity stress condition, CTS-SiO2 NPs at 25 mg L−1 concentration increased the LAI by 12.20%. The highest fresh and dry weights were obtained by foliar applied CTS-SiO2 NPs (50 mg L−1 concentration) and CTS-SiO2 NPs (25 mg L−1 concentration) under control condition (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Photosynthetic pigments and photochemical quantum efficiency of photosystem II

The results showed that 150 mM salinity reduced chlorophyll a (38.28%), chlorophyll b (31.42%), and total chlorophyll (35.85%), but with no statistical difference. Similarly, although it was not statistically significant difference, carotenoids increased by 2.14% under salinity stress and Y(II) (photochemical quantum efficiency of photosystem II) reduced by 22.96% as compared to control condition. Under salinity stress conditions, 50 mg L−1 CTS-SiO2 NPs increased chlorophyll a by 1.52% and Y(II) by 14.35% as compared to non-treated salt-stressed plants. Moreover, foliar application of SiO2 NPs increased chlorophyll b and total chlorophyll by 12.34 and 4.36%, respectively, under salinity stress condition. The highest carotenoid content (6.88%) was observed in foliar applied CTS-SiO2 NPs at 25 mg L−1 concentration under salinity stress (Table 1).

Canopy temperature and relative humidity indices

Salinity significantly increased the air temperature on the leaf and leaf temperature by 16.01 and 17.14% compared to control. Likewise, salinity significantly decreased the relative humidity of the air at the top of the leaf by 23.40% compared to control. Any treatments caused substantial affection on the air temperature on the leaf and leaf temperature under control condition while SiO2 NPs significantly enhanced the relative humidity of the air at the top of the leaf under control condition. Under salinity stress condition, CTS NPs, SiO2 NPs and 25 mg L−1 CTS-SiO2 NPs significantly reduced the air temperature on the leaf; CTS NPs and 50 mg L−1 CTS-SiO2 NPs caused significant decrease and increase in leaf temperature, respectively and CTS NPs and SiO2 NPs enhanced and 50 mg L−1 CTS-SiO2 NPs reduced the relative humidity of the air at the top of the leaf. In details, foliar application of CTS NPs decreased the air temperature on the leaves by 10.2% and the leaf temperature by 9.3% and increased the relative humidity of the air at the top of the leaf by 9.8% under salinity condition compared to non-treated plants (Table 2).

Biochemical indices (EL and MSI)

The results demonstrated that salinity (150 Mm) significantly increased EL (76.74%) compared to control. Salinity had no noteworthy effect on MSI and phenols however, salinity decreased MSI by 14.27% and phenol by 0.012%. Mostly, any treatments caused significant changes on EL, MSI and phenols under control and salinity conditions. However, foliar application of CTS-SiO2 NPs at 25 mg L−1 concentration reduced EL by 11.57% under salinity condition compared to non-treated salt-stressed plants. The highest MSI (21.80%) under salinity stress was observed in 50 mg L−1 foliar applied CTS-SiO2 NPs. The highest amount of phenols (0.082%) was obtained in SiO2 NPs-treated plants under salinity condition (Table 3).

Antioxidant enzyme (APX and SOD) and oxidative markers activity (H2O2 and MDA)

Salinity caused no statistically difference on APX, SOD, H2O2 and MDA, though salinity increased APX activity, H2O2 and MDA contents in the leaves of pomegranate seedlings by 16.53, 7.61 and 21.02%, respectively, compared to control. Foliar application of CTS-SiO2 NPs at 25 mg L−1 concentration increased APX activity by 132.6% compared to non-treated plants under salinity condition. SiO2 NPs significantly reduced H2O2 under control condition. 50 mg L−1 CTS-SiO2 NPs and SiO2 NPs caused significant increase in MDA content under control and salinity conditions, respectively (Table 4).

Discussion

In the present study, growth parameters decreased under salinity condition. Doaa and Shalan [14] showed that plant growth parameters decreased with increasing salinity in mango. Soluble salts in the soil increase osmotic pressure and reduce the water potential of the whole soil, thereby reducing water uptake by the roots, which ultimately leads to a reduction in plant growth parameters [11]. Also, the decrease in plant growth can be attributed to a decrease in mineral uptake, inhibition of photosynthesis, and inhibition of cell expansion [51], in line with the current findings. The growth-promoting effect of CTS probably could be related to regulating a series of metabolic pathways, including N and carbon (C) metabolism [63] and stimulating some hormonal signaling, such as auxin and gibberellin pathways [52] that thereby help promoting growth. Si also acts as a detoxifier against Na+ in the cell wall of plants, binding to the cell wall instead of Na+, thereby reducing its movement towards the branch and leaf [37] leading to decreased negative effects of salinity. All might highlight the positive effects of the applied treatments on the growth parameters especially under salinity condition.

The decrease in the content of photosynthetic pigments may be due to degradation of chlorophyll through the activation of chlorophyllase enzyme under salinity stress leading to decreased light absorption and photosynthesis [47]. Consistent with our results, El-Dengawy et al. [16] reported that in mango seedlings, the amount of total chlorophyll decreased with increasing salinity.Younas et al. [62] achieved results similar to our findings reporting that Si and CTS application increased the content of photosynthetic pigments. Also, the combined application of Si and CTS mitigated the effects of salt stress in maize by increasing photosynthesis and improving biochemical and physiological traits. The beneficial effect of Si on chlorophyll content may be due to K uptake, preservation of chloroplast metastructure, or chlorophyll stability by accelerating the inhibition of ROS activity [20]. CTS also has been shown to had positive effects on photosynthetic pigments and processes, individually or in combination with biostimulants, under control and stress conditions [24, 48]. Possibly, these could be explained the reasons behind the improved content of photosynthetic pigments and Y (II) after treatments application.

EL is one of the major indicators used to detect the severity of salinity injuries [2]. Khalil et al. [36] reported that under salinity stress, there was an increase in EL in pomegranate, in accordance with our results. MSI is another important parameter used to estimate the stress tolerance of different crops because the relative loss of water from plant cells negatively affects the membrane structure and its function [15]. Elkarmout et al. [17] reported that foliar application of CTS improved MSI under stress conditions. Likewise, Ali et al. [6] reported the positive effect of Si on MSI thanks to Si function in regulating plasma membrane stability and increasing osmolytic accumulation, leading to the removal of ROS, mainly H2O2. These could demonstrate minor effect of CTS-SiO2 NPs as well as other treatments on reduced EL and improved MSI under the stress condition. Species and intraspecies diversity are effective in the accumulation of polyphenols in response to salinity, probably due to their ability in salt splitting processes in cells or in maintaining leaf ion balance [49].

In order to prevent oxidative damage under stressful conditions, plants have a complex antioxidant system that includes both enzymatic and non-enzymatic components. Enzymatic antioxidants play an important role in scavenging ROS and therefore are essential in reducing abiotic stress effects [28]. As the first line of defense, SOD converts the toxic superoxide radical (O2−) on the surface of the chloroplast to H2O2, which is then converted to the water through the help of other peroxidases. Generally, in chloroplasts, APX uses ascorbate to convert H2O2 into water [10]. The increase in H2O2 and MDA means that plants are exposed to oxidative stress under salinity stress, which causes lipid peroxidation [17]. Peroxidation of unsaturated fatty acids of cell membrane causes the accumulation of MDA, as a product of lipid peroxidation [27]. Khalil et al. [36] also reported similar results in pomegranate plants; increased MDA under stress caused significant oxidative damage in the lipid membranes of the plants. Sen et al. [55] showed that CTS induced an enzymatic antioxidant system resulting in decreased H2O2 that increased membrane integrity (MSI) and thereby improved plant tolerance to salinity stress. The application of Si also reduced H2O2 under stress, due to the role of Si in maintaining membrane stability or integrity as well as regulating osmolytes and ROS levels in plants [8]. Considering positive but non-significant effect of the treatments on enhancing the antioxidant enzymes and reducing MDA and H2O2, especially under salt stress condition, the above mentioned studies and reasons could be confirmed, albeit to some extent.

Salinity stress, in the current study, increased Na+ concentration and decreased K+ concentration, which ultimately decreased K+/Na+ ratio and also reduced the amount of N and P. It has been found that the accumulation of NaCl disrupts ion homeostasis that leads to a decrease in the content of essential elements in plant tissues [13], confirming the current findings. Flam-Shepherd et al. [21] reported Na+ reduction with Si foliar application, attributing to the deposition of Si in the inner section of the root endoderm; thereby blocking the uptake of apoplastic Na+ by the roots during salinity stress [41], in line with the current results of SiO2 NPs application, to some extent. Kasem et al. [35] reported that foliar application of CTS on olive trees under salinity stress increased the amount of N, P, and K elements under salinity stress. Since the application of the treatments particularly CTS-SiO2 NPs increased the amount of P, K, and K+/Na+ ratio, the synergistic effect of CTS and Si might be considered.

The use of silicon and chitosan nanomaterials in saline soils can have positive effects on pomegranate cultivation. Nanosilicon promotes plant growth by reducing the negative effects of salts on roots and improving water and nutrient absorption. These nanomaterials can also help improve soil structure, increase permeability and water retention, and strengthen the immune system of plants, making them more resistant to environmental stresses. Chitosan can also act as a growth stimulant and increase the speed of root growth and development. It also provides better conditions for plant growth by improving the physical and chemical properties of the soil. As a result, the use of these materials in saline soils can reduce the negative effects of salinity and strengthen the plant’s immune system, increasing resistance and better pomegranate production. Similar to other nanoparticles, CTS-SiO2 NPs reduce salinity stress by decreasing oxidative stress, improving nutrient homeostasis, stabilizing membranes, and enhancing photosynthesis, which ultimately leads to better plant growth and development under saline conditions. The combination of chitosan and silica properties may produce a synergistic effect, making these nanoparticles more effective than single-component nanoparticles. Studies show that a synthesized material containing chitosan and silicon enhances wheat growth under salt stress by reducing lipid membrane oxidation, photodamage to the photosynthetic apparatus, and protein degradation [26]. In maize, growth enhancement is attributed to the slow release of silicon from the nano-fertilizer [39].

Overall, CTS-SiO2 nanoparticles perform similarly to other nanoparticles in alleviating salt stress and, with the additional benefits of chitosan that enhance defense responses and membrane stability, they represent a promising strategy for using nanomaterials to mitigate the effects of salinity.

Although this study demonstrates the potential of CTS-SiO2 nanoparticles to enhance growth and reduce salt stress, further large-scale field trials and extensive safety assessments are needed. These steps are necessary to confirm their effectiveness, environmental safety, and suitability for widespread agricultural use.

Conclusion

Salinity stress negatively affects the growth and development of pomegranate seedlings, as expected through reducing growth parameters, photosynthetic pigments, relative air humidity at the top of the leaf, phenol, MSI and the amount of N, P, K elements and increasing EL, leaf temperature, MDA, H2O2, APX and SOD enzymes, and Na + element. Foliar application of SiO2 NPs, CTS NPs and CTS SiO2 NPs significantly reduced the negative effects of the stress, by balancing the stress effects. CTS SiO2 NPs caused the most effective results on moderating the negative effects of salinity stress. This study demonstrates, for the first time, the synergistic potential of CTS SiO2 NPs in alleviating salinity stress in pomegranate, offering a promising nano-enabled strategy for sustainable horticulture, and possibly after more investigations, in other crops. Ultimately, to strengthen the reliability of the findings and to gain a more thorough understanding of some observed trends, future studies should incorporate larger sample sizes and prolonged durations of stress exposure.
