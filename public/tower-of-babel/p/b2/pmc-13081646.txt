Plasmid-mediated convergence of multidrug resistance and hypervirulence in community-acquired Klebsiella pneumoniae in Tianjin, China
Authors: Donghang Li, Zhijuan Fan, Sumei Wang, Yanyan Su, Meng Yuan, Hui Fang, Rendong Wang, Huihui Gao, Hangming Bai, Daqing Mao, Yi Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKlebsiella pneumoniae is a major bacterial pathogen responsible for community- and hospital-acquired infections. Although strains associated with hospital-acquired disease have been well described, the genomic and phenotypic characteristics of community-acquired K. pneumoniae among community-acquired isolates from this setting remain insufficiently understood. This study aimed to characterize the molecular features, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and virulence-associated traits of community-acquired strains isolated from a tertiary hospital in Tianjin.ResultsA total of 27 community-acquired K. pneumoniae underwent whole-genome sequencing and comprehensive phenotypic assessment. Molecular typing revealed that the dominant lineages included ST11-KL64-O1/O2v1, ST268-KL20-O1/O2v1, and ST15-KL102-O1/O2v2. All isolates carried at least one virulence-associated gene as defined by established hvKp markers and produced high levels of siderophores (> 30 µg/mL). Most strains displayed multidrug-resistant phenotypes and simultaneously possessed virulence determinants, indicating the frequent detection of multidrug-resistant hypervirulent K. pneumoniae among the studied community-acquired isolates. Genomic analysis showed that the coexistence of resistance and virulence traits was largely associated with horizontal gene transfer mediated by mobile genetic elements such as plasmids, insertion sequences, and transposons. Replicons of the IncHI1B and IncFIB plasmid families were frequently linked to virulence-associated genes. Functional assays, including Galleria mellonella infection, biofilm formation, macrophage infection, and serum resistance testing, confirmed the pathogenic potential of these strains. Integrated genomic and phenotypic findings suggested that multiple virulence factors collectively enhance pathogenicity through immune evasion, increased biofilm production, and modulation of host responses.ConclusionsCommunity-acquired K. pneumoniae strains analyzed in this study exhibit a concerning combination of multidrug resistance and enhanced virulence. The convergence of these traits, largely associated with mobile genetic elements, represents an emerging public health concern and underscores the need for strengthened surveillance and targeted control measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04867-2.Keywords: Klebsiella pneumoniae, Community-acquired infections, Multidrug resistance, Hypervirulent, Mobile genetic elements, Molecular epidemiology

Background

Klebsiella pneumoniae has emerged as a major opportunistic pathogen and a critical threat to global public health in recent years. It is responsible for a wide spectrum of infections, including pneumonia, urinary tract infections, sepsis, liver abscesses, and bloodstream infections [1]. In China, while the molecular epidemiology of hospital-acquired K. pneumoniae (HAKp) has been extensively characterized [2, 3], studies on community-acquired K. pneumoniae (CAKp) remain limited and have primarily focused on a few cities in southern China. Moreover, most existing investigations have emphasized phenotypic characterization, with genome-based analyses still being relatively scarce [4–6]. Given the significant environmental, demographic, and healthcare disparities between northern and southern China, it is plausible that CAKp strains circulating in different regions may exhibit distinct resistance and virulence profiles.

The rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in K. pneumoniae represents a mounting global concern. In particular, the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) [7, 8], carbapenem-resistant (CR) [9, 10], and extended-spectrum β-lactamase producing strains (ESBLs) [11, 12] has posed serious challenges to clinical management, leading to increased mortality and limited therapeutic options. Although MDR strains were historically considered a nosocomial problem, growing evidence indicates that multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae (MDR-Kp) has increasingly spread in community settings, thereby expanding the population at risk [13]. This trend underscores the urgent need to investigate the prevalence and resistance characteristics of CAKp strains to guide effective intervention strategies.

Based on pathogenicity, K. pneumoniae can be broadly classified into two types: classical K. pneumoniae (cKp) and hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (hvKp). CKp strains are typically associated with hospital-acquired infections and often cause localized diseases [14–16]. In contrast, hvKp strains can cause invasive infections such as pyogenic liver abscesses and bacteremia, even in healthy individuals [17]. HvKp is genetically defined by the presence of key virulence genes, including iucA, iroB, peg-344, rmpA, and rmpA2, and phenotypically confirmed by siderophore (SP) levels exceeding 30 µg/mL, which together enable reliable differentiation from cKp [18–20]. First identified in Taiwan, hvKp has since been reported worldwide [18, 21–25], highlighting the importance of monitoring its distribution, particularly in community settings.

Of particular concern is the recent convergence of multidrug resistance and hypervirulence in K. pneumoniae, leading to the emergence of MDR-hvKp strains that harbor both resistance and virulence determinants [26–28]. These genes are often co-located on mobile genetic elements (MGEs) such as plasmids, transposons, and insertion sequences, which facilitate their horizontal transfer and dissemination [29–31]. The global spread of MDR-hvKp has been reported across Asia, Europe, Africa, and the Americas, raising serious concerns for public health and infection control [1, 32, 33]. A thorough understanding of the epidemiology, resistance mechanisms, and virulence profiles of CAKp in community settings is therefore essential to inform surveillance strategies and mitigate the impact of these high-risk clones.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive genomic and phenotypic analysis of 27 community-acquired K. pneumoniae (CAKp) strains isolated from a tertiary hospital in Tianjin, China. We characterized their sequence types, antimicrobial resistance profiles, virulence gene content, and associated MGEs, and compared them with global CAKp isolates. Our findings provide new insights into the genomic and phenotypic features associated with multidrug-resistant hypervirulent K. pneumoniae in community-acquired settings, helping to fill an important gap in the understanding of CAKp epidemiology.

Methods

Isolation of K. pneumoniae

In this study, 27 clinical specimens were collected from patients at Tianjin Third Central Hospital within 24 h of admission, between May and October 2018. These isolates were obtained as part of a routine hospital-based surveillance program, during which a larger collection of clinical K. pneumoniae isolates was established. The 27 isolates analyzed here were randomly selected from this collection for genomic analysis. These specimens, including sputum, urine, blood, and abdomen, were used for the isolation of Klebsiella strains. Isolates obtained within 48 h of admission were operationally classified as community-acquired (CA), in accordance with definitions commonly used in previous genomic studies of Klebsiella pneumoniae [1]. Detailed information on prior healthcare exposure, such as recent hospitalization or outpatient medical procedures, was not available for this retrospective collection. The identity of the 27 K. pneumoniae strains was confirmed by amplification of the 16S rRNA gene. All isolates were stored at -80 °C for future analysis.

Whole-genome sequencing and genome assembly

Twenty-seven non-repetitive strains were selected for whole-genome sequencing (WGS). Genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Bacteria DNA kit DP302 (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). Library preparation was carried out using Nextera technology, and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Raw sequencing reads were assessed for quality using FastQC (version 0.11.6). To ensure robust genome reconstruction and reliable downstream comparative analyses, high-quality reads were assembled de novo using SPAdes (version 4.0.0) [34] with default parameters optimized for bacterial isolate genomes. Contigs shorter than 1,000 bp were excluded from subsequent analyses to reduce assembly artifacts and low-confidence sequences [35]. Assembly quality was evaluated using QUAST (version 5.3.0) [36], and genome completeness and contamination were assessed using CheckM (version 1.2.3) [37] (lineage-specific workflow with default parameters). Taxonomic assignment was performed using the GTDB-Tk [38] web server (version 2.3.2) with default parameters based on the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB).

Bioinformatic analysis

The software MOB-suite was used for clustering, reconstruction and typing of plasmid sequences from WGS assemblies [39]. The software Kleborate (version 2.0.4) was employed to determine phylogroups, multi-locus sequence types (MLST), K-locus types, and O-locus types [40]. HvKp strains were defined as those carrying at least one of the following genes: peg-344, iroB, iucA, rmpA, or rmpA2 and exhibiting siderophore (SP) yields greater than 30 µg/mL, which are considered reliable markers for distinguishing hvKp from cKp strains [18–20]. Antibiotic resistance genes, virulence genes, and insertion sequences (ISs) were identified using the ResFinder [41], Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) [42], and ISFinder [43] databases, respectively. Transposons (Tns) reported in this study refer to well-characterized transposon elements curated in reference databases and were identified based on sequence similarity to annotated transposon sequences, rather than de novo prediction. Plasmid replicon types were determined using the PlasmidFinder [44] database and MOB-suite software [39]. Integrative and conjugative elements (ICEs) were predicted using ICEfinder [45], while phages were identified using PHASTER [46]. All subsequent comparative genomic, plasmid, resistance, and virulence analyses were performed exclusively based on the SPAdes-assembled genomes to ensure consistency and reliability across datasets.

Phylogenetic analysis

The genomes of all isolates were annotated using Prokka [47]. Based on the GFF files generated by Prokka, the core genome was identified using Roary [48] with default parameters. Phylogenetic analysis of the core genome was conducted using the maximum likelihood method implemented in FastTree [49]. The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized using the iTOL platform (https://itol.embl.de/).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

A previously described method [50] was employed with minor modifications. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method (OXOID, UK), and the results were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (M100-S33). Twelve representative antimicrobial agents from six classes commonly used against K. pneumoniae were tested, including β-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, macrolides, and polypeptides. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains were defined as non-susceptible to three or more antimicrobial classes [51].

String test

A previously described method [52] was utilized with slight modifications. The K. pneumoniae isolates were streaked onto blood agar plates and incubated overnight at 37℃. The string test was considered positive when a viscous string longer than 5 mm formed after a single colony was touched with an inoculation loop.

Quantitative determination of siderophore production

Siderophore production was quantified using the Chrome Azurol S (CAS) assay as previously described [53], with minor modifications. Briefly, overnight bacterial cultures were adjusted to approximately 1 × 10⁷ cfu/mL and inoculated into M9 minimal medium (JSENB), followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 3 days. The culture supernatants were collected by centrifugation and used for siderophore measurement. For each sample, 100 µl of the culture supernatant (or uninoculated medium as a blank control) was mixed with 100 µL of CAS reagent (MREDA) and incubated in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance at 630 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioScreen, Finland). Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 and Escherichia coli DH5α were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. Siderophore activity was expressed as the percentage of siderophore units (psu, %) according to the formula: psu (%) = (Ar-As)/Ar×100, where Ar is the absorbance of the reference solution (CAS reagent mixed with uninoculated medium), and As is the absorbance of the sample (CAS reagent mixed with culture supernatant). To further quantify siderophore concentration, deferoxamine mesylate (MREDA, ≥ 98%) was used as a standard. The standard was mixed with CAS reagent and its absorbance at 630 nm was measured to construct a calibration curve using OriginPro 2024. A linear regression model was used for concentrations between 0 and 40 µM (Figure S2A), whereas a four-parameter logistic (4PL) nonlinear fit was applied for 40–200 µM (Figure S2B). The siderophore concentration of each sample was then calculated based on its psu (%) value and the corresponding deferoxamine standard curve.

Galleria mellonella infection model

The virulence of K. pneumoniae isolates was assessed using the Galleria mellonella infection model, following a previously described method with minor modifications [54]. Briefly, the bacterial cells were washed with PBS and diluted to a concentration of 1 × 108 cfu/mL. A microinjector was used to administer 10 µL of the bacterial suspension into the second abdominal segment of G. mellonella. Each strain was tested in 10 groups. After injection, the larvae were incubated at 37℃, and mortality was monitored every 12 h for 96 h. Dead larvae were removed during observation. Survival curves were plotted using GraphPad Prism software. Larvae injected with 10 µL of PBS served as the negative control, and no mortality was observed in this group.

Serum resistance assay

A previously described method [55] was used with minor modifications to assess the serum resistance of K. pneumoniae isolates. Briefly, isolates were cultured to an OD600 of 0.6, washed once with sterile PBS, and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 106 cfu/mL. A total of 50 µL of the bacterial suspension was mixed with 150 µL of human serum and incubated at 37℃. The number of viable bacteria was determined at 0, 1, 2, and 3 h, and bacterial survival rates were calculated. Each strain was tested in triplicate. Survival rates were calculated as follows: SR1 = (cfu surviving after 1 h/cfu at baseline) ×100%, SR2 = (cfu surviving after 2 h/cfu at baseline) ×100%, and SR3 = (cfu surviving after 3 h/cfu at baseline) ×100%. Serum resistance levels were classified as follows: level 1 (SR1 and SR2 < 10%, SR3 < 0.1%), level 2 (SR1 and SR2 between 10% and 100%, SR3 < 10%), level 3 (SR1 > 100%, SR2 and SR3 < 100%), level 4 (SR1 and SR2 > 100%, SR3 < 100%), level 5 (SR1, SR2, and SR3 > 100%, with SR2 > SR3), and level 6 (SR1, SR2, and SR3 > 100%, with SR1 < SR2 < SR3). Levels 1 and 2 indicated serum sensitivity, levels 3 and 4 indicated intermediate sensitivity, and levels 5 and 6 indicated drug resistance [9].

Biofilm formation assay

The ability of K. pneumoniae isolates to form biofilms was assessed using crystal violet staining and categorized as strong, moderate, or weak biofilm producers [56]. A previously described method [57] was employed with minor modifications. Briefly, overnight cultures of the isolates were diluted with LB medium to an OD600 of 0.1. A sterile 96-well plate was filled with 250 µL of the diluted bacterial suspension per well, with five replicates set up for each sample. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. LB medium without bacteria was used as a negative control for biofilm formation. After 24 h, the biofilms were fixed with 250 µL of methanol for 15 min and stained with 250 µL of 1% crystal violet solution for 15 min. The wells were then rinsed three times with PBS and dried at room temperature. The crystal violet was dissolved in 250 µL of 95% ethanol for 15 min, and 200 µL of the eluent was transferred to a new 96-well plate. OD570 was measured using an automated microplate reader (BioScreen, Finland). The cut-off OD (ODc) was defined as three standard deviations above the mean OD of the negative control (measured at 570 nm). The OD570 values of the isolates were compared to ODc, and biofilm forming ability was categorized as follows: OD570 ≤ ODc = no biofilm production, ODc < OD570 ≤ 2 × ODc = weak biofilm production, 2 × ODc < OD570 ≤ 4 × ODc = moderate biofilm production, and OD570 > 4 × ODc = strong biofilm production [56].

Macrophage infection assay

The lab-maintained mouse mononuclear macrophage cell J774A.1 was used as the infection target for K. pneumoniae isolates. The cells were cultured in a complete DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PS) in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. A total of 5 × 105 J774A.1 macrophages were seeded into a 12-well plate and incubated for 12 h under the same conditions. After incubation, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed twice with DMEM pre-warmed to 37 °C. The macrophages were then infected with K. pneumoniae at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 by adding DMEM without FBS and PS containing the bacterial cells. After 3 h of incubation, the supernatant from the infected macrophages was collected. The levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6 were measured using an ELISA kit, while the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was evaluated. Each sample was tested in triplicate. Uninfected macrophages were used as the negative control for inflammatory cytokine secretion.

Growth curve experiment

A previously established method [58] was slightly modified and followed. Briefly, overnight-cultured K. pneumoniae was diluted in LB medium to an OD600 of 0.1. A total of 200 µL of the diluted bacterial suspension was added to each well of a 96-well plate, with three replicates set up per sample. The OD600 was measured at 5-minute intervals using an automated microplate reader from BioScreen, Finland. After 20 h, the data were processed, and the lag phase, maximum specific growth rate, and maximum population density were analyzed using the grofit package in R [59] for growth curve analysis.

Statistical analyses

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and standard deviation (SD) values were calculated. In the macrophage infection assay, Student’s t-test was used to compare changes in the expression of immune factors in the infected isolates with those in the negative control. All statistical tests were performed as two-tailed analyses. p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. No multiple-testing corrections were applied.

Results

Taxonomic features of community-acquired Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates from a tertiary hospital.

Within 24 h of patient admission to a tertiary hospital in Tianjin between May and October 2018, multiple strains of K. pneumoniae were isolated from clinical samples. These isolates were therefore classified as community-acquired Klebsiella pneumoniae (CAKp). The diverse range of clinical samples from which isolates were obtained included 8 sputum specimens (30%), 11 urine samples (41%), 3 blood cultures (11%), and 5 abdomen samples (18%) (Fig. 1A), illustrating the varied infections caused by K. pneumoniae in human communities associated with this tertiary hospital. Among these, 27 non-repetitive strains were subjected to whole-genome sequencing and subsequent analyses. After reassembly using SPAdes, all genomes showed sizes within the expected range for K. pneumoniae and high completeness with low contamination, as assessed by QUAST and CheckM (Table S1).

Detailed characteristics, including phylogroups, MLSTs, K-locus types, and O-locus types, were annotated using Kleborate. Collection sites are indicated by color-coded patches

Kleborate assigned the 27 CAKp strains to K. pneumoniae phylogroup I (KpI) (Fig. 1B), indicating diverse origins of these isolates. KpI is the most prevalent among the seven distinct K. pneumoniae phylogroups and has been found in various environments, including nature water sources, human feces, blood, liver and others [60]. MLST assesses genetic relationships by analyzing specific gene sequences within the core bacterial genomes. This method is valuable for tracking pathogen transmission, identifying emerging clones, and providing essential support for epidemiological research [61]. In this study, the CAKp isolates were classified into 5 distinct ST lineages, including ST11, ST15, ST268, ST50 and ST65 (Fig. 1B, Table S1). Notably, ST11, ST15, and ST268 were the dominant sequence types contributing to community infections. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that ST11 [62] and ST15 [63] are the dominant K. pneumoniae subtypes in China. Although research on ST268 remains limited [64], its significant presence in Tianjin CAKp isolates suggests that this lineage warrants further investigation in future studies. One isolate belonging to ST50 was identified, which had previously been commonly reported as Klebsiella michiganensis [65] but not as K. pneumoniae. In contrast, ST65 is frequently associated with hypermucoviscous K. pneumoniae (HMKp) [66, 67].

Both K-locus and O-locus types are closely associated with the virulence of K. pneumoniae, as they are determined by the genetic regions responsible for capsular polysaccharide (CPS) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) antigen synthesis. These locus types facilitate the identification of genetic diversity and pathogenicity in K. pneumoniae strains, providing critical support for epidemiological research [68, 69]. In this study, as shown in Fig. 1B, the 27 CAKp strains were classified into five K-locus types, namely KL64, KL47, KL102, KL2, and KL20. Among these, the three most prevalent types were KL64 (12 strains, 44%) corresponding to ST11 lineage, KL20 (6 strains, 22%) corresponding to ST268 lineage, and KL102 (6 strains, 22%) corresponding to ST15 lineage. Notably, this result revealed that the three predominant ST-KL types of K. pneumoniae in community infections were ST11-KL64, ST268-KL20 and ST15-KL102. Notably, O-locus types were highly concentrated in this dataset, with 19 isolates classified as O1/O2v1 and 7 as O1/O2v2. Therefore, the predominant genotypic patterns observed in this study were ST11-KL64-O1/O2v1, ST268-KL20-O1/O2v1, and ST15-KL102-O1/O2v2 (Fig. 1B).

CAKp strains exhibit high levels of antimicrobial resistance

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of 27 CAKp strains was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method in accordance with CLSI guidelines. Twelve antimicrobial agents from six classes were tested, including tetracyclines (tigecycline, TGC), polypeptides (polymyxin B, PB), macrolides (erythromycin, EM), aminoglycosides (gentamicin, GM), fluoroquinolones (levofloxacin, LEV), and β-lactams (imipenem, IPM; meropenem, MEM; ampicillin, AMP; cefazolin, CEZ; cefuroxime, CXM; ceftazidime, CAZ; cefepime, FEP). According to CLSI clinical breakpoints (Table S2), isolates were categorized as resistant, intermediate, or susceptible (Table S3, Fig. 2A). Among the 27 CAKp strains, 26 isolates showed resistance to the majority of the tested antimicrobial agents, while all isolates remained susceptible to polymyxin B. The remaining isolate, belonging to the ST65 lineage, was resistant to seven of the twelve antibiotics. Notably, five ST15 isolates and this ST65 isolate were also susceptible to gentamicin, whereas the remaining isolates were resistant to all tested agents except polymyxin B, substantially limiting available therapeutic options.

Overall, all isolates were confirmed as extended-spectrum β-lactamase producers (ESBLs). In addition, 26 of 27 isolates (96%) met the criteria for carbapenem resistance (CR), and all isolates were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR) according to established definitions (Fig. 2B). These findings indicate a high burden of antimicrobial resistance among community-acquired K. pneumoniae isolates in this study.

The high prevalence of antibiotic resistance genes associated with CAKp

Previous studies have identified three intrinsic antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in K. pneumoniae, including the β-lactamase genes blaSHV (or blaOKP or blaLEN), the fosfomycin resistance gene fosA, and the quinolone efflux pump gene oqxAB [70]. The other ARGs present in K. pneumoniae are generally considered to be acquired. In this study, we separate whole genome sequencing (WGS) data into chromosomal and plasmid sequences for subsequent ARG analysis. Among the 27 CAKp strains, the three intrinsic ARGs (blaSHV, fosA, and oqxAB) were predominantly encoded on the chromosome. Notably, blaSHV and fosA were also detected on plasmids in 11 and 9 strains, respectively, indicating potential horizontal gene transfer (Fig. 3A).

Beyond the intrinsic genes, plasmids from CAKp strains carried 18 acquired ARGs conferring resistance to β-lactams, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, quinolones, chloramphenicol, trimethoprim, macrolides, rifampicin, glycosides, and sulfonamides (Fig. 3A). Notably, all of these 18 acquired ARGs were also integrated into the chromosome, reflecting frequent horizontal gene transfer. The total number of acquired ARGs per strain, including both plasmid- and chromosome-encoded genes, ranged from 0 to 31, with a median of 17 (Fig. 3A). On average, each plasmid carried nine resistance genes, and one plasmid harbored up to 20 ARGs, highlighting their crucial role in ARG acquisition and dissemination. Interestingly, one ST65 strain carried only the three intrinsic ARGs (blaSHV, fosA, and oqxAB), exhibiting resistance to ampicillin and cefuroxime but remaining susceptible to other β-lactams such as imipenem, meropenem, cefazolin, ceftazidime, and cefepime (Fig. 2A). This finding suggests that blaSHV alone confers narrow-spectrum β-lactam resistance in CAKp. In contrast, broad-spectrum β-lactam resistance required the accumulation of multiple ARG subtypes, as exemplified by an ST11 strain harboring blaTEM−1B, blaCTX−M, blaKPC−2, blaOXA, and blaADC (Fig. 2A). These genotypic patterns were consistent with the observed resistance phenotypes, indicating a substantial burden of antimicrobial resistance among CAKp strains.

To further investigate the potential mechanisms of ARG acquisition via mobile genetic elements (MGEs) in CAKp strains, we analyzed their occurrence and distribution in both chromosomal and plasmid genomes. The identified MGEs included insertion sequences (ISs), well-characterized transposons (Tns) curated in reference databases, integrative conjugative elements (ICEs), integrative mobilizable elements (IMEs), and prophages, all of which are known to facilitate horizontal gene transfer and ARG dissemination [71]. As shown in Fig. 3A, both chromosomes and plasmids harbored abundant ISs and Tns, with ISs per chromosomal genome ranging from 613 to 4869 (mean 1749) and Tns ranged from 33 to 496 per chromosome (mean 156), based on annotation of well-characterized transposon elements. In plasmids, IS counts ranged from 118 to 432 (mean 276), and Tns from 13 to 75 (mean 47). In contrast, ICEs, IMEs, and prophages were relatively sparse, showing no clear distribution patterns. ICEs ranged from 1 to 11 per strain (mean 3), IMEs from 0 to 3 (mean 1), and prophages from 1 to 7 (mean 4), with IMEs and prophages detected in only 4 and 6 plasmid genomes, respectively (Fig. 3A). Further analysis revealed that 21 ARGs were located on Tns, ICEs, IMEs, or prophages, but none were directly linked to ISs (Table S4). As shown in Fig. 3B, some representative ARGs were associated with MGEs, such as blaTEM−1B (89%, 24/27 strains) on Tn2, aadA (78%, 21/27) on TnAs3, and aph (56%, 15/27) on Tn5393 or ICEs (3 strains). Notably, blaSHV was detected on a prophage in only one ST65 strain, and ICEs and IMEs harboring multiple resistance genes were identified in 11 and 2 CAKp strains, respectively (Table S4). Collectively, these findings reveal the widespread presence and diversity of MGEs that play a key role in mediating the acquisition and dissemination of ARGs in CAKp strains.

Plasmid replicon analysis further revealed a high diversity and prevalence of plasmids among the CAKp strains analyzed in this study. In total, 11 distinct plasmid replicon types (Inc groups) were identified, including IncHI1B, IncFII, IncR, IncFIB, IncFIA, ColpVC, Col, rep_cluster, IncQ1, Col440II, and IncB/O/K/Z. Individual isolates carried an average of six plasmid replicons (range: 2–11; Fig. 3C), indicating the frequent presence of multiple plasmids or multireplicon plasmid structures within single genomes. Among these replicons, IncFIB was detected in all isolates (100%), demonstrating its universal presence in this dataset. IncFIA, rep_cluster, IncR, IncHI1B, IncFII, and ColpVC were detected at progressively lower frequencies, whereas Col and IncQ1 were each identified in only two strains, and IncB/O/K/Z and Col440II were detected in a single strain each. The frequent co-occurrence of multiple plasmid replicons within individual CAKp strains suggests that plasmids with diverse replication systems are stably maintained in these genomes. These results indicate that CAKp strains in this study harbor complex plasmid repertoires, providing genetic platforms capable of accommodating multiple resistance- and virulence-associated determinants. Such multireplicon architectures may facilitate the accumulation and maintenance of adaptive genes within individual strains.

The prevalence of pathogenic phenotypes and genotypes in CAKp strains

Bacterial growth capacity is a key phenotypic trait influencing pathogenesis. We systematically measured bacterial growth kinetics using an automated microplate reader and extracted three key parameters: the lag phase, maximum specific growth rate, and maximum population density, using R-based growth analysis packages. Among the 27 CAKp strains tested, lag phases ranged from 2.01 to 4.50 h, maximum specific growth rates varied between 0.27 and 0.48 h⁻¹, and maximum population densities ranged from 0.9 to 1.4 OD₆₀₀. These results reveal substantial strain-dependent heterogeneity in growth kinetics (Figure S1, Tables S5 and S6). The observed variations indicate that the pathogenic potential of CAKp may not be directly associated with its proliferative capacity measured in vitro microplate assays. The growth capacities of CAKp in infected lesions of patients might differ considerably from those observed in laboratory assays.

Based on combining genotypic and phenotypic characteristics, all 27 CAKp strains were classified as hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp). Specifically, all strains harbored at least one of the five hypervirulence genes (peg-344, iroB, iucA, rmpA, and rmpA2) and exhibited siderophore (SP) yields exceeding 30 µg/mL (Fig. 4A and B). The string test was further applied as a supplementary phenotypic indicator [52], with 10 of the 27 strains testing positive (Fig. 4A), consistent with their hypervirulent phenotype.

Among the 27 CAKp strains, 13 carried all five hypervirulence genes and 9 carried four, together accounting for 81% (22/27) of the total, indicating a high virulence gene burden. In most strains, the chromosomal genomes encoded iucA, rmpA, and rmpA2, and all five hypervirulence-associated genes were also detected on plasmids. Notably, several plasmids harbored all five virulence genes simultaneously. These findings suggest that plasmids play an important role in mediating hypervirulence in CAKp strains.

Further plasmid analysis revealed that 85% (23/27) of CAKp strains harbored plasmids carrying hypervirulence genes, mainly belonging to the IncHI1B (15/23), IncFIB (16/23), and rep_cluster (5/23) replicon types. Consistent with previous reports, IncHI1B and IncFIB were the most prevalent plasmid groups associated with hypervirulence [72]. All eight plasmids carrying all five hypervirulence genes belonged to the IncFIB group, while six of the ten plasmids carrying four genes contained both IncHI1B and IncFIB replicons. Notably, an association was observed between molecular typing and plasmid groups, ST11 strains frequently carried IncHI1B plasmids, whereas ST268 strains exclusively harbored IncFIB plasmids. Co-occurrence of IncHI1B with IncFIB or rep_cluster replicons was common among ST11 strains (Fig. 4A). These results indicate that IncHI1B and IncFIB plasmids are frequently involved in virulence gene dissemination.

Interestingly, multicopy occurrences of the same virulence genes (such as iucA, rmpA, and rmpA2) were detected on both chromosomes and plasmids within individual CAKp strains. A total of 22 out of 27 strains exhibited this multicopy pattern (Fig. 4A). In genetically similar backgrounds, an increased copy number of virulence genes has been reported to correlate with enhanced virulence. Furthermore, these genes may contribute to environmental adaptability, such as iuc genes encode the synthesis of aerobactin, facilitating bacterial survival under iron-limited conditions [73], while rmpA and rmpA2 regulate capsule production, promoting invasion and colonization [74]. Recent studies have shown that community-acquired hvKp can colonize healthy individuals [75] and have been detected in animals [76, 77] and aquatic environments [78, 79], suggesting that hypervirulence genes may facilitate CAKp persistence and transmission in community settings.

Genome-wide analysis of virulence factors based on the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) annotation identified an average of 106 virulence genes per CAKp chromosome and 10 virulence genes per plasmid. These genes were functionally diverse, encompassing effector delivery systems, adherence, biofilm formation, nutritional/metabolic factors, and immune modulation (Figures S3 and S4). Collectively, these findings highlight the substantial virulence potential of CAKp strains.

We further employed the Galleria mellonella (G. mellonella) infection model to evaluate the virulence of the CAKp strains. ST268 strains carrying all five hypervirulence genes showed markedly higher virulence, with five out of six isolates causing 100% larval mortality (Fig. 4C and D). In contrast, other sequence types exhibited lower lethality: only 5 of 13 ST11 strains (38%) reached 100% mortality, while none of the ST15, ST65, or ST50 strains achieved this threshold. These findings indicate an association between hypervirulence gene load and virulent phenotype, suggesting that increased gene carriage contributes to enhanced pathogenicity. Moreover, community-acquired hvKp strains displayed distinct ST-specific virulence profiles, with ST268 emerging as the predominant hypervirulent lineage in the studied community.

Biofilm formation is another key phenotype associated with infection persistence and antimicrobial tolerance. As shown in Fig. 4E, ST268, ST11, and ST50 strains exhibited strong biofilm formation abilities, consistent with the presence of multiple biofilm forming genes. Notably, ST11 isolates S24 and S48, which harbored the highest number of biofilm-related genes, showed strong biofilm formation. Interestingly, strains such as S49 (ST11) and S45 (ST268) also formed robust biofilms despite harboring fewer such genes, suggesting that biofilm formation may not be solely determined by the number of annotated biofilm-related genes.

Further validation using a murine macrophage infection model (J774A.1 cells) revealed that CAKp strains induced significantly higher secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) while suppressing the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 4F). The cytokine responses correlated with the lethality observed in the G. mellonella model, as well as with the previously observed biofilm formation capacities. ST11, ST50, and ST268 strains induced the most pronounced inflammatory responses, consistent with their hypervirulent phenotypes. Collectively, these findings indicate that CAKp subtypes harboring a higher load of hypervirulence genes and exhibiting enhanced biofilm formation have increased invasive potential, thereby posing greater risks in both clinical and community settings.

Serum resistance assays further revealed sequence type–associated differences among CAKp strains. ST11, ST15, and ST50 strains exhibited relatively high serum resistance, whereas ST65 and ST268 were more susceptible (Fig. 4F). The molecular mechanisms underlying these variations remain unclear and warrant further investigation to better understand CAKp pathogenesis.

Previous reports from southern China, including isolates from Taiwan (2014–2016) and Wenzhou (2016), described predominantly hvKp with relatively limited antimicrobial resistance. More recent studies from Zhongshan (2020–2023) reported increased multidrug resistance while maintaining a high prevalence of hypervirulence, suggesting an increasing coexistence of resistance and virulence in some community-acquired isolates. In contrast, CAKp isolates reported from Zibo (2021–2023) exhibited lower frequencies of hypervirulence and minimal carbapenem resistance. The CAKp strains collected in Tianjin in 2018 and analyzed in the present study displayed a comparatively high prevalence of both multidrug resistance and hypervirulence, indicating the coexistence of these traits within this local dataset. Internationally, CAKp isolates reported from India (2019–2021) and Iran (2019) showed substantial levels of multidrug resistance (33–56%) but relatively low frequencies of hypervirulence (7–11%), whereas isolates from Croatia (2022–2023) were uniformly carbapenem-resistant, although virulence-related data were not available. Together, these observations illustrate considerable heterogeneity in resistance and virulence profiles among CAKp strains reported from different geographical settings.

Collectively, this comparative analysis highlights the diversity of antimicrobial resistance and hypervirulence patterns among community-acquired K. pneumoniae reported across different studies. The detection of CAKp strains harboring both multidrug resistance and hypervirulence determinants in this study underscores the importance of continued molecular surveillance and cautious interpretation of regional trends.

Discussion

K. pneumoniae is a globally significant opportunistic pathogen that poses a major public health threat due to the convergence of multidrug resistance and hypervirulence. While hospital-acquired K. pneumoniae strains in China have been extensively characterized in terms of resistance mechanisms and transmission dynamics [7–12], community-acquired K. pneumoniae strains remain poorly understood, particularly at the genomic and mechanistic levels. In this study, we performed an integrated genomic and phenotypic analysis of 27 CAKp strains collected from a tertiary hospital in Tianjin, providing a focused snapshot of the molecular features associated with community-acquired infections in this setting.

Compared with isolates from other regions of China and some international isolates, the CAKp strains isolated in this study exhibited unique molecular and phenotypic patterns, enriched with multidrug resistance and key hypervirulence determinants. This regional difference suggests that local ecological or clinical factors may promote the emergence and persistence of MDR-hvKp strains. These findings underscore the need for regional surveillance and targeted interventions to reduce the risk of community transmission of high-risk CAKp lineages.

At the sequence type level, several dominant lineages identified in this study warrant further discussion. ST11, the most prevalent lineage among the CAKp isolates analyzed here, is a well-recognized high-risk clone in China and has been extensively associated with carbapenem resistance, multidrug resistance, and the KL64 capsule type in hospital-acquired settings [86, 87]. Its detection among community-acquired isolates suggests a possible extension of this traditionally hospital-associated lineage into community settings, consistent with recent reports describing the spillover of high-risk clones beyond healthcare environments [88]. ST15, another dominant lineage observed in this dataset, is an internationally disseminated clone frequently linked to extended-spectrum β-lactamase production and multidrug resistance in both China and other regions [89], reflecting its strong capacity for dissemination across diverse ecological niches. In contrast, ST268 remains relatively understudied, with limited genomic and epidemiological data available to date [90]. Its notable representation among CAKp isolates in Tianjin, particularly in association with the KL20 capsule type, suggests that this lineage may be emerging in community-acquired infections and merits further investigation.

Moreover, our findings suggest that current surveillance systems may systematically underestimate antibiotic resistance levels in CAKp strains. While the data from China Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network (CHINET) indicates that carbapenem resistance among K. pneumoniae strains remained below 30% nationally in 2018 (with even lower resistance rates for other antibiotics), the CAKp strains in this study exhibited substantially higher resistance rates. This striking discrepancy likely stems from a critical surveillance gap. Over 95% of current resistance data originates from HAKp strains, potentially leading to significant underestimation of CAKp resistance. Most alarmingly, the current surveillance suffers from a critical lack of CAKp resistance data, leaving CAKp resistance profiles largely undefined. Our results demonstrate an urgent need to establish separate reporting frameworks for CAKp and HAKp strains in national antimicrobial resistance surveillance programs.

A key finding of this study is the frequent co-occurrence of multidrug resistance and hypervirulence traits within individual CAKp isolates. Rather than arising independently, these traits were strongly associated with plasmid-mediated genetic architectures. Specifically, resistance genes and hypervirulence determinants were often co-localized on plasmids, particularly those belonging to the IncHI1B and IncFIB replicon groups [72, 91, 92]. These plasmids therefore function as shared genetic platforms that enable the simultaneous acquisition and maintenance of resistance and virulence traits within a single host background. Importantly, IncHI1B and IncFIB replicons were not only prevalent but also frequently co-existed within the same strains, suggesting plasmid recombination or fusion events. Such hybrid or composite plasmids have been increasingly reported in hypervirulent K. pneumoniae and are thought to facilitate the accumulation of diverse adaptive traits [93, 94]. In this context, plasmids are likely to facilitate evolutionary convergence by integrating resistance genes, virulence factors, and mobile genetic elements into cohesive units capable of horizontal transfer [95].

The role of MGEs further reinforces this mechanism. Many resistance and virulence genes identified in this study were associated with transposons, integrative elements, or prophages, highlighting the importance of horizontal gene transfer in shaping plasmid content. The abundance of MGEs within both chromosomal and plasmid compartments suggests an active genetic exchange environment, accelerating the dissemination and stabilization of high-risk gene combinations in community-acquired strains.

Phenotypic assays provided functional support for the biological relevance of these plasmid-mediated architectures. Strains harboring a higher burden of plasmid-encoded virulence determinants exhibited enhanced pathogenic potential in Galleria mellonella infection models, increased biofilm formation, and pronounced inflammatory responses in macrophage infection assays. Together, these observations indicate that plasmid-borne genes are strongly associated with enhanced pathogenic phenotypes, rather than representing incidental genetic cargo.

The G. mellonella infection model is commonly used for preliminary assessment of bacterial virulence. In this study, we found that while this model partially reflects the relationship between pathogenicity and hypervirulence in MDR-hvKp, the results were not completely consistent with those observed in mammalian models. A previous comparative study on the lethality of hvKp and cKp strains demonstrated superior predictive accuracy in outbred murine infection models compared to G. mellonella, highlighting the limitations of the insect system. Particularly for strains with undefined pathogenic potential, including those with incomplete hvKp biomarker profiles, pLVPK-like virulence plasmids, antibiotic resistance phenotypes, or atypical clinical manifestations, the murine infection model provides more reliable virulence assessment [96].

Previous studies have shown that the hypervirulent genes rmpA and rmpA2 enhance bacterial systemic dissemination by upregulating capsular polysaccharide CPS synthesis [97, 98]. However, CPS overexpression may incur a trade-off, leading to the downregulation of lipopolysaccharide (O antigen) synthesis, consequently reducing biofilm formation capacity, compromising serum resistance, and weakening immune evasion capability [9]. This inverse relationship between hypervirulent genes and biofilm formation may account for the characteristically moderate biofilm-producing phenotype observed in MDR-hvKp strains. These findings highlight the need for future investigations to (1) elucidate the precise regulatory interplay between hypervirulent determinants and biofilm formation, and (2) uncover the complex mechanistic relationships governing bacterial pathogenicity and antibiotic resistance in these clinically significant strains.

This study systematically analyzed 27 CAKp strains from Tianjin, and several limitations should be acknowledged. The study is based on a relatively small number of isolates collected from a single hospital within a defined timeframe, and therefore cannot represent the broader epidemiological characteristics of CAKp at the regional or national level. Differences in sampling strategies, study periods, and analytical approaches across published studies may influence the interpretation of comparative analyses. In addition, community-acquired infection in this study was defined based on the timing of isolate recovery (within 48 h of admission). While this definition is consistent with previous large-scale genomic studies, it cannot completely exclude prior healthcare-associated exposures, such as recent hospitalization or outpatient medical procedures, due to the lack of detailed patient-level exposure data in this retrospective analysis. Furthermore, the use of short-read sequencing limits the resolution of complete plasmid structures; future studies incorporating long-read sequencing will be essential for a more comprehensive understanding of plasmid architecture and recombination dynamics. The incorporation of detailed clinical metadata and exposure histories in future prospective studies would further enable more refined classification of community- and healthcare-associated infections. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the molecular characteristics of the CAKp strains analyzed here. Antimicrobial resistance and virulence-associated genes were frequently detected in community-acquired hvKp strains and were predominantly associated with mobile genetic elements, particularly plasmids and transposons. By integrating plasmid composition, gene co-localization patterns, the distribution of mobile genetic elements, and corresponding phenotypic observations, our findings support an important role for plasmids in facilitating the accumulation and dissemination of resistance and virulence determinants in CAKp strains. Collectively, these results are consistent with a plasmid-mediated contribution to the evolutionary adaptation of CAKp and highlight the value of plasmid-focused genomic surveillance in future investigations.

Conclusions

This study provides a systematic genomic and phenotypic characterization of 27 community-acquired Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates collected from a tertiary hospital in Tianjin. Predominant sequence types included ST11-KL64-O1/O2v1, ST268-KL20-O1/O2v1, and ST15-KL102-O1/O2v2. Multidrug resistance in these isolates was largely associated with the horizontal acquisition of antimicrobial resistance genes mediated by mobile genetic elements, particularly plasmids. All isolates harbored at least one hypervirulence-associated gene, which was frequently encoded on IncHI1B and IncFIB plasmids, supporting an important role for plasmid-mediated gene transfer in shaping pathogenic potential. Although limited in scope, these findings offer important insights into the genetic basis underlying the convergence of resistance and virulence in community-acquired K. pneumoniae. Continued genomic surveillance incorporating larger and more diverse datasets, together with improved plasmid resolution, will be critical for monitoring the evolution and spread of high-risk K. pneumoniae lineages and for informing targeted prevention strategies.
