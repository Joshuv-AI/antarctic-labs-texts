Low-dose abiraterone plus Olaparib as a late-line treatment for mCRPC patients without BRCA1/2 mutations: a multicenter retrospective pilot study
Authors: Sijin Chen, Dewen Zhong, Chenbo Yu, Desheng Cai, Qichen Wei, Minggen Yang, Tao Li, Qingguo Zhu, Liefu Ye, Yongbao Wei, Jinfeng Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough overall survival data are still premature, the PROpel study found radiological progression-free survival (PFS) benefits of abiraterone and olaparib in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC). However, for patients who have not been genetically tested or lack BRCA1/2 mutations (BRCAm), this combination therapy has been questioned as a first-line conventional treatment for mCRPC, mainly due to significant health economics and side effects. In our retrospective study, we found that treatment with low-dose abiraterone plus olaparib as a late-line treatment for mCRPC could lead to prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and symptom PFS in selective cases even without BRCAm. The median PSA–PFS was 8 months (IQR: 6.5–11.5), with a median follow-up duration of 39.0 months (IQR: 27.5–64.5). Gene tests were conducted in all patients, identifying non-BRCA mutations through ctDNA testing (24%), tumor tissue testing (12%), or both (64%). Adverse events occurred in 72% of patients, with 16% experiencing Grade ≥ 3 events. Common adverse events included anemia (64%), decreased appetite (48%), and fatigue (25%). Our findings support low-dose abiraterone plus olaparib as a potential option for mCRPC patients without BRCAm, offering manageable safety and efficacy profiles.Keywords: Prostate cancer, Abiraterone, Olaparib, Late-line treatment, mCRPC, BRCA1/2 mutationsSubject terms: Prostate cancer, Drug development

Introduction

Advanced prostate cancer seriously threatens the life and quality of life of patients, while patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) have a worse prognosis and are more challenging to treat1. Based on genetic testing and the discovery of homologous recombination repair (HRR) gene mutations (HRRm), reports of PROfound opened a new era of targeted therapy—PARP inhibitors (PARPi) for prostate cancer patients2,3. Subsequent PROpel study confirmed the radiographically progression-free (PFS) benefits of Olaparib in combination with abiraterone in patients with non-selective mCRPC4. However, due to immature OS data and the apparent increase in side effects of this combination, thus its first-line treatment for non-selective mCRPC was challenged5. In fact, for patients who had a disease controlled by abiraterone, 250 mg of abiraterone could be taken with meals instead of 1000 mg to reduce its dosage and side effects, and its therapeutic effect could still be guaranteed6. This method of abiraterone dosage reduction, combined with Olaparib therapy, maybe a new treatment method and worth trying. Based on this idea, we used Olaparib 300 mg twice daily combined with abiraterone 250 mg once daily with a low-fat breakfast as a late-line treatment for some mCRPC patients without BRCA1/2 mutations (BRCAm) and provided a new treatment choice for patients with mCRPC. The primary aim of our study was to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of the combination therapy of low-dose abiraterone and olaparib in heavily pretreated mCRPC patients. The secondary aim was to explore the feasibility of using low-dose abiraterone to minimize toxicity while maintaining efficacy.

Methods

We retrospectively analyzed patients over 18 years of age with advanced prostate acinar adenocarcinoma diagnosed and treated by the Alliance for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Prostate Cancer led in Fujian province (including a total of 45 medical centers) from January 2015 to May 2024 and screened patients eligible for this study according to the following criteria: (a) patients treated with abiraterone (250 mg once daily) plus prednisone (5 mg twice daily) and Olaparib (300 mg twice daily) for at least 3 months; (b) patients accepting at least once genetic test, exclude BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations; (c) patients diagnosis and treatment information was available and participated in this study. Exclusion Criteria were patients with incomplete treatment duration or insufficient follow-up data. The approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital (K2023-07-011). The patients/participants provided written informed consent to participate in this study.

Results

Out of the 43 patients screened, 18 were excluded. Among these, 12 patients did not meet the inclusion criteria: 8 patients had received less than 3 months of treatment with abiraterone, prednisone, and olaparib, and 4 patients either did not undergo genetic testing or had BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations. Additionally, 6 patients were excluded due to incomplete treatment or insufficient follow-up data. As a result, 25 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the study. The median age of patients was 71 years (IQR: 64.5–77.0) (Table 1). The median PSA level was 78.5 ng/ml (IQR: 24.4–185.2), and the median cGleason score was 9 (IQR: 8–10). Special pathological differentiation was observed in 20% of cases. For cTNM staging, 20% were T2, 36% T3, and 44% T4. Additionally, 28% were N0, and 72% N1. Distant metastasis stages were M1a (8%), M1b (76%), and M1c (16%). The median time to mCRPC was 14.0 months (IQR: 9.5–19.5). Cytoreductive radical prostatectomy was performed in 28% of cases. Re-biopsies occurred once in 20% and twice in 80%. Gene tests were conducted once in 60% and twice in 40% of patients. Non-BRCA mutations were identified through ctDNA testing (24%), tumor tissue testing (12%), or both (64%). HRRm status was positive in 12% and negative in 88%. Prior to low-dose abiraterone plus olaparib treatment, 12% had 2 treatment lines, 48% had 3, 28% had 4, and 12% had 5. Monotherapy was used in 12% and sequential multiple drugs in 88%. The median PSA level at the start of low-dose abiraterone plus olaparib was 36.5 ng/ml (IQR: 15.9–130.9) and 40.7 ng/ml (IQR: 5.1–322.5) at the last follow-up. The median PSA-PFS since starting low-dose abiraterone plus olaparib was 8 months (IQR: 6.5–11.5). At last follow-up, 32% continued the treatment, while 68% discontinued. The median follow-up duration was 39.0 months (IQR: 27.5–64.5), with 84% alive and 16% deceased.

Adverse events occurred in 72% of patients, with 16% experiencing Grade ≥ 3 events (Table 2). Puffiness was observed in 8% of patients, none severe. Anaemia was present in 64%, with 8% having Grade ≥ 3 severity. Fatigue was reported by 25%, with 4% experiencing Grade ≥ 3. Decreased appetite was seen in 48% of patients. Other adverse events included constipation (20%), peripheral edema (8%), bitter taste (12%), and weight loss (16%). Dose reduction or discontinuation of abiraterone due to adverse events occurred in 4% of patients, and 16% had a dose reduction or discontinuation of olaparib. No deaths were attributed to adverse events in this study. Additionally, no new adverse reactions were reported.

Discussion

To our knowledge, our study first found that patients with mCRPC without BCRAm, low-dose abiraterone plus Olaparib as a late-line therapy, resulting in a promising PAS-PFS and low rate of adverse reactions.

BRCAm is a critical biomarker for poor prognosis for mCRPC patients7. Fortunately, mCRPC patients with BRCAm who progressed on NHT therapy benefitted from Olaparib significantly more than those who received NHT alone3. However, less than 20% of patients with mCRPC included in the PROfound study tested positive for BRCAm, suggesting that most patients may not benefit from Olaparib monotherapy. The PROpel study found that for patients without BRCAm, compared with abiraterone alone, combination therapy of abiraterone 1000 mg once daily and Olaparib brought benefits of radiological PFS, but patients without BRCAm had less benefit. This result was similar to our findings, and all 25 patients we enrolled had no BRCAm and benefited from this novel combination therapy. The difference was that we used abiraterone in a low-dose form, 250 mg once a day, with a low-fat breakfast.

Controversy exists about whether low-dosage NHT treatment brings benefits to mCRPC patients. A Japanese study found that NHT dose reduction resulted in poor oncological outcomes in these patients8. But this study did not state whether NHT reduction was based on its treatment effectiveness. 250 mg abiraterone, taken with a low-fat meal, could be used for CRPC patients with disease well controlled in its treatment of 1000 mg, which could reduce the dosage of abiraterone and its side effects6. This low dosage of abiraterone has also been affirmed by consensuses and NCCN guidelines9–11. Prescribing low-dose abiraterone with limited resources helped to increase access to treatment for Indian patients and resulted in significant cost savings12. In our study, we observed a benefit of PAS-PFS in limited selective cases by reducing the dose of abiraterone. If this novel treatment does benefit a relative proportion of patients with mCRPC, it might help more patients with mCRPC and reduce the global health economic burden.

Caution is needed when Olaparib plus abiraterone is first-line used in non-selective patients with mCRPC. Although the PROpel study and a meta-study supported that their combination therapy may prolong survival in unselected patients with mCRPC4,13. Based on more comprehensive data from PROpel, TALAPRO-2, and MAGNITUDE, it is crucial to reconsider which mCRPC patients should accept PARPi combined with NHT, especially for patients without HRRm; these expensive combinations do not improve OS but do present severe cytopenia and other toxic side effects including the risk of inducing leukemia5. In fact, both the subgroup analysis of the PROpel and TALAPRO-2 studies found that the benefit of PARPi combined with NHT was observed only in the patients with HRRm, BRCAm to be more exact; thus, more genetic testing is needed to support its first-line treatment of mCRPC5. The difference was that our patients had undergone at least one genetic test, and no case had BRCAm. And our patients accepted this novel combination therapy not as a first but late line, as 22/25 patients were treated with 3 lines or more after progression to mCRPC. Our results supported that low-dose abiraterone plus Olaparib might be an alternative late-line treatment for patients with mCPRC who even had a negative genetic test.

Our study had several limitations. Firstly, the retrospective design and small sample size (25 patients) limit the generalizability and robustness of our findings. The absence of a pharmacokinetic (PK) analysis is another significant limitation, as understanding the interaction and optimal dosing of the combination therapy would provide valuable insights. Additionally, while our study aimed to evaluate the combination therapy in a heavily pretreated cohort, it cannot be directly compared to larger, first-line setting trials like PROpel. Not all genetic testing was performed using both cancer metastases tissue and liquid biopsy, and in some cases, the liquid biopsy was performed only once; thus, uncertainty might exist about the presence of BRCA mutations. Finally, the lack of a control group further constrains the strength of our conclusions.

Future work should focus on prospective case–control studies with larger sample sizes and comprehensive PK evaluations. These studies should also ensure thorough and repeated genetic testing using both tissue and liquid biopsies to accurately determine BRCA mutation status. Conducting such studies in first-line mCRPC settings could provide more definitive evidence for the use of low-dose abiraterone plus olaparib, helping to validate our findings and potentially offering a novel combination therapy for mCRPC patients.

Conclusions

We first reported that low-dose abiraterone plus Olaparib might be an alternative late-line treatment with a manageable safety profile for selected patients with mCPRC who had no mutations of BRCA1/2. Prospective case–control studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm the benefit of this novel combination therapy.
