Relationship between social media addiction and exercise behaviors in nursing students
Authors: Sezer Avcı, Hüseyin Yaşar, Tülay Ortabağ
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial media addiction is common among university students and may have consequences for their physical activity. This study examines the relationship between nursing students’ social media addiction and both their engagement in physical activity and their perceptions of the benefits and barriers of exercise.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 346 nursing students enrolled at three universities in different regions of Türkiye. Participants completed a demographic questionnaire, the Social Media Addiction Scale (SMAS), and the Exercise Benefit/Barrier Scale (EBBS), which measure social media addiction and perceptions of exercise benefits and barriers, respectively. Data were analyzed with t tests, one-way ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and linear regression, with sex, year of study, and body mass index treated as comparison or control variables.ResultsA weak but statistically significant negative correlation was found between social media addiction and exercise benefit perception (r = − 0.188, p < 0.01), and this association persisted after adjusting for age, sex, year of study, and BMI (β = −0.164, p = 0.001). Longer daily social media use was associated with higher addiction scores (heaviest-use group 107.30 ± 35.26; p < 0.001), and students who exercised regularly scored lower than those who did not (p = 0.046). No significant difference in social media addiction was found between female and male students.ConclusionBecause the design is cross-sectional, these findings reflect an association rather than a causal link: higher social media addiction was weakly related to a less favorable perception of exercise benefits. Approaches that encourage physical activity may be worth evaluating in future longitudinal and interventional studies.Keywords: Addiction, Behavior, Exercise, Nursing students, Social media

Introduction

Social media and emerging communication technologies are significantly changing the lives and habits of university students. Young individuals tend to use social media applications more to escape from the obligations of daily life [1]. This addiction has become more pronounced in parallel with the increase in the number of social media users and the duration of their use [2, 3]. Studies have shown that social media addiction is associated with eating disorders, increased body mass index (BMI), obesity in children and adolescents, sleep disorders, and psychosocial adjustment problems in youth [4–8].

Social media addiction is a psychological addiction considered a subtype of internet addiction, characterized by an urge to use social media excessively and an inability to control that urge [9–11]. One pathway through which this matters for health is physical activity: increased time spent on social media may promote unhealthy lifestyles by reducing physical activity [12]. This link is perhaps best understood in biopsychosocial terms. Biologically, the hours absorbed by screens displace time that might otherwise be spent moving and can disturb sleep and reward-related regulation; psychologically, addictive use is bound up with mood regulation and a reduced willingness to undertake effortful activity such as exercise; and socially, online interaction may take the place of the offline and physical activities that ordinarily accompany an active lifestyle.

Consistent with this, daily physical activity among university students tends to decline as the time spent in digital environments increases, with negative consequences for health indicators [13]. Globally, the proportion of individuals who do not meet physical activity recommendations is already high [14, 15]. Physical activity may, however, work in the opposite direction: physical-activity- and exercise-based interventions are thought to play a significant role in mitigating internet addiction [16], and such approaches have been suggested to reduce, and perhaps help prevent, addiction-related problems [17].

Although the broad relationship between social media addiction and physical activity has been studied, relatively little is known about nursing students specifically [18–20]. The intense academic and clinical responsibilities of this group suggest that their social media habits and addiction tendencies may differ from those of other students, yet how such addiction relates to their own health behaviors has rarely been examined directly. The present study addresses this gap by focusing on nursing students and by treating exercise behavior in a clearly defined sense: students’ self-reported engagement in regular physical activity together with their perceptions of the benefits and barriers of exercise.

Because the design is cross-sectional, the study was framed in terms of association rather than effect. Its primary expectation was that a higher level of social media addiction would be associated with a less favorable perception of exercise benefits among nursing students. Two further expectations were examined separately: first, that students who do not engage in regular physical activity would show higher social media addiction scores than those who do; and second, that longer social media use would be associated with lower physical activity levels. Clarifying these associations in a population whose professional role is built around health is the original contribution this study seeks to make.

Materials and methods

Study design and hypothesis

This was a cross-sectional study examining the association between social media addiction and exercise benefit/barrier perceptions in nursing students. Because the design measures both constructs at a single time point, the study hypothesis was framed as a relationship rather than a causal effect: H1 — there is a negative association between social media addiction and nursing students’ perception of exercise benefits.

Setting, sampling, and participants

The study was conducted with nursing students enrolled at a state university and a foundation university in eastern Türkiye and a foundation university in western Türkiye. Data were collected between February and June 2022 through a web-based form hosted on the Google platform. Participants were recruited by voluntary, convenience sampling rather than by random selection; the online format allowed students from all three institutions to be reached and produced a broad demographic range. The accessible study population comprised 1,200 nursing students enrolled in these departments. The inclusion criterion was being an enrolled nursing student who volunteered to take part, and students who left the questionnaire incomplete were excluded. General health status was not used as an eligibility criterion; chronic illness was instead recorded only as a descriptive characteristic, and because no structured psychiatric evaluation was performed, mental health was not applied as a formal inclusion criterion. Sample size and power

Using the known-population sample-size formula with N = 1,200, p = 0.50, q = 0.50, t = 1.96, and d = 0.05, the minimum required sample was 292; a total of 346 students were ultimately included. As this formula estimates the sample needed to characterize a finite population rather than the power of inferential tests, a sensitivity analysis was additionally performed with G*Power 3.1. With the final sample of 346, α = 0.05 (two-tailed), and 80% power, the study was able to detect a correlation as small as |r| ≈ 0.15; the observed primary association (r = − 0.188) therefore exceeded the smallest effect the study was powered to detect.

Data collection instruments

Data were collected with an author-developed demographic questionnaire, the Social Media Addiction Scale (SMAS), and the Exercise Benefit/Barrier Scale (EBBS). The author-developed questionnaire contained 18 items covering general information such as age, gender, height, weight, and chronic health conditions, together with the age at which the student first owned a mobile phone, average daily phone-use time, social media use, and physical activity. Physical activity was captured with a single self-report item with three response options (yes / no / sometimes), and this categorical variable—not a weekly-hours measure—was used in all analyses. Several variables that were recorded as continuous (e.g., age, daily phone-use time, daily social media time) are reported both as means and in grouped categories; the categories were created only to make subgroup comparisons more interpretable and clinically readable, while the descriptive means preserve the original continuous information. Body Mass Index (BMI) was classified according to the World Health Organization criteria [21]. Height and weight were not measured directly but were taken from students’ self-report, and BMI was calculated from these self-reported values.

The SMAS was developed by Tutgun-Ünal and Deniz [22] to measure social media addiction among university students. It uses a 5-point Likert format and comprises 41 items grouped into four subdimensions: Engagement, Mood Modification, Repeat, and Conflict. Engagement reflects the preoccupying effect of social media on the individual; Mood Modification reflects its effect on the individual’s emotions; Repeat reflects the inability to control use and the repetition of use at the same level; and Conflict reflects the adverse consequences of social media in a person’s life. Higher scores indicate higher social media addiction. In the original development study the scale showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.967) [22], and a comparably high value was obtained in the present sample (α = 0.986). Subdimension reliabilities in the present study were α = 0.963 (Engagement), 0.954 (Mood Modification), 0.952 (Repeat), and 0.984 (Conflict). The EBBS was developed by Walker et al. [23] to assess perceptions of the benefits and barriers of exercise, and its Turkish validity and reliability were established by Ortabag et al. [24]. The scale was included to capture how students appraise exercise—its perceived benefits and barriers—because attitudes toward exercise represent a plausible behavioral correlate of social media addiction; it complements the categorical physical-activity item by characterizing the perceptual dimension referred to in the study hypothesis. It uses a 4-point Likert format and contains 43 items organized into two components: the Exercise Benefit Scale (EBeS) and the Exercise Barrier Scale (EBaS). Higher EBeS scores indicate greater perceived benefits, whereas higher EBaS scores indicate greater perceived barriers. EBeS comprises five dimensions—Life Enhancement (8 items), Physical Performance (8 items), Psychological Perspective (6 items), Social Interaction (4 items), and Preventive Health (3 items)—and EBaS comprises Exercise Environment (6 items), Time Expenditure (3 items), Physical Effort (3 items), and Family Obstacle (2 items). The scale showed a Cronbach’s α of 0.95 in its validation study [24] and 0.965 in the present sample. Present-study subdimension reliabilities were α = 0.922 (Life Enhancement), 0.945 (Physical Performance), 0.908 (Psychological Perspective), 0.773 (Social Interaction), 0.804 (Preventive Health), and 0.972 for total EBeS; and α = 0.887 (Exercise Environment), 0.841 (Time Expenditure), 0.823 (Physical Effort), 0.852 (Family Obstacle), and 0.947 for total EBaS. Values between 0.7 and 0.9 indicate good reliability, and values above 0.9 indicate excellent reliability [25].

Ethical approval and consent to participate

Prior to data collection, ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Health Sciences Ethics Committee of a foundation university in the Southeastern Anatolia Region of Türkiye, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (January 10, 2022; number 2022/003). Permission was also obtained from the universities where the study was conducted. After being informed about the study, participants provided written consent by selecting the “I agree to participate in the study” option on the Google form before being given access to the questionnaire.

Data analysis

Analyses were performed in SPSS 25.0. Continuous variables were summarized as mean (M) and standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequency (n) and percentage (%). Normality of the continuous outcomes was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, skewness–kurtosis values, histograms, and Normal Q–Q plots. Because SMAS, EBBS, and their components were approximately normally distributed, independent-samples t tests were used for two-group comparisons and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons of three or more groups, with nonparametric tests applied where the homogeneity of variances was not met. For significant ANOVA results, pairwise differences were examined with the Tukey HSD post hoc test, and effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for t tests and η² for ANOVA. Relationships between continuous variables were examined with Pearson’s product-moment correlation. The association between social media addiction and exercise benefit perception was further tested with linear regression: an unadjusted model and a multivariable model adjusting for age, sex, year of study, and BMI, with standardized coefficients (β) reported as effect sizes. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Total SMAS scores differed across several participant characteristics (Table 3). One-way analysis of variance showed that third-year students scored higher than first- and fourth-year students (F(3,342) = 5.13, p = 0.002), and addiction scores rose with both daily phone use (F(2,343) = 9.37, p < 0.001) and daily time spent on social media (F(3,318) = 7.10, p < 0.001); in each case post hoc comparisons confirmed that the heaviest-use groups scored significantly higher than the lightest-use group. Students who used social media scored higher than non-users (t(344) = 3.36, p = 0.001), and those who exercised regularly scored lower than those who did not (F(2,343) = 3.12, p = 0.046). Neither age group nor sex, however, was associated with a meaningful difference in total SMAS scores (p = 0.353 and p = 0.175, respectively).

The correlations among all scales and subdimensions are reported in full in Table 5. As expected, SMAS correlated strongly and positively with its own subdimensions (r = 0.86–0.94), and EBeS showed the same internal pattern with its components (r = 0.65–0.97). More relevant to the aim of the study, social media addiction was linked to exercise perceptions only weakly: SMAS correlated negatively with EBeS (r = − 0.188, p < 0.01) and positively, though to a smaller degree, with EBaS (r = 0.145, p < 0.01). This weak pattern was consistent across the subdimensions, with the cross-scale coefficients ranging from − 0.12 to − 0.19 for benefits and from 0.12 to 0.15 for barriers. In practical terms, students with higher addiction scores tended to perceive slightly fewer benefits and slightly more barriers to exercise, but the strength of these associations was modest.

Discussion

In the present study, students reported low-to-slight levels of social media addiction overall, with the lowest scores in the Mood Modification, Repeat, and Conflict subdimensions. This pattern is broadly consistent with Tutgun-Ünal and Deniz [22], who described low addiction in the Mood Modification and Repeat subdimensions, moderate addiction in Engagement, and a non-addicted profile for Conflict.

The central aim of the study was to describe how social media addiction relates to students’ exercise perceptions, and the two were only weakly connected. A weak but statistically significant negative correlation was found between SMAS and the Exercise Benefit Scale (r = − 0.188, p < 0.01; Table 5). Linear regression pointed in the same direction: in the unadjusted model each one-point increase in SMAS corresponded to a 0.067-point lower benefit score (β = −0.188, p < 0.001), and the association persisted after adjustment for age, sex, year of study, and BMI (β = −0.164, p = 0.001; Table 6). Because the model explained only about 3.6% of the variance, this relationship is best read as a modest association rather than as evidence that social media use lowers exercise motivation. A plausible interpretation is biopsychosocial: students who are more absorbed in social media may have less time and energy for physical activity, may experience the mood- and reward-related effects of heavy use that compete with the effort exercise requires, and may substitute online interaction for active, in-person pursuits. Alemdağ [26] similarly reported that more physically active students used social media less, and Precht et al. [27] described an inverse association between physical activity and social media use. While social media addiction has been linked to physical, mental, and academic problems [28], regular exercise is associated with better academic performance and a lower risk of conditions such as coronary artery disease, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, depression, anxiety, and certain cancers [29]. Taken together, these associations suggest that raising students’ awareness of how heavy social media use and physical inactivity tend to occur together may be worthwhile, although the present design cannot confirm that such efforts would change behavior.

Heavier and more frequent social media use was associated with higher addiction scores (Table 3). Total SMAS scores were significantly higher among students with greater daily phone use and greater daily social media time, and post hoc comparisons confirmed that the heaviest-use groups scored higher than the lightest-use group; third-year students also scored higher than first- and fourth-year students. This is in line with several reports: Özdemir [30] and Güler et al. [31] found that longer social media use was associated with higher addiction among university students, Altayef and Karacı [32] reported a similar pattern with more years of use among secondary-school students, Bal and Balcı [33] linked more frequent application use to greater smartphone-addiction risk, Kircaburun et al. [34] found that heavier users were more likely to show problematic use, and Uslu [35] reported a positive association between time on social media and addiction. Whether academic level itself plays a role is less clear, since some studies report an association [32] and others do not [30].

Students showed a good level of perceived exercise benefits alongside a high level of perceived barriers. Bakır and Hisar [36] reported moderate benefit and high barrier perceptions among nurses, and Ortabag et al. [24] described good benefit and barrier perceptions, so the present results are partly similar to and partly different from the literature. Students of normal weight reported more favorable benefit perceptions (Table 4), consistent with Çakır [37], who observed lower motivation for physical activity among students with higher BMI.

No significant sex difference emerged for social media addiction (Table 3), which agrees with Andreassen et al. [10], Kircaburun et al. [34], Demircan et al. [38], Wenkai et al. [39], Arıbaş and Özşahin [40], and Güler et al. [31]; other studies, by contrast, have reported higher addiction among women [30, 32, 41, 42]. On the barrier subscale, however, male students reported significantly more barriers to exercise (Table 4), and Kasırga et al. [43] likewise found that physically inactive women perceived fewer exercise benefits than men.

Several other characteristics were related to exercise perceptions rather than to addiction. Students who obtained their first mobile phone at age 17 or later perceived more exercise barriers than other groups (Table 4); this may reflect the demands of university-entrance examination preparation, which in Türkiye typically begins around that age and leaves little time for exercise. Students who switched their phones off or used airplane mode before sleeping reported more favorable benefit perceptions (Table 4). The literature on night-time phone use is mixed: Hoşgör [44] found no effect of switching phones off at night on smartphone addiction, whereas Sırakaya [45] reported higher addiction among those who did not. Finally, students who did not engage in regular physical activity had higher social media addiction scores (Table 3), while those who were active reported more favorable exercise benefit perceptions (Table 4). Çam and Nur [46] found that exercise did not affect problematic internet use, whereas Kasırga et al. [43] reported higher benefit perceptions among physically active students, which is closer to the present findings.

This study has several strengths: it focuses on a defined, health-relevant group—nursing students—and examines social media use together with exercise perceptions using established scales in a reasonably large, multi-institution sample. At the same time, several limitations should be kept in mind. The cross-sectional design measures both constructs at a single point in time and therefore cannot establish whether social media use shapes exercise behavior or the reverse; the relationships reported here are correlational only. The observed associations were also weak and accounted for little of the variance, so they should not be over-interpreted clinically. Reliance on self-report—including self-reported height and weight, from which BMI was calculated—may introduce inaccuracy, and the voluntary, convenience-based recruitment from three specific universities limits the generalizability of the findings. Longitudinal and interventional studies in broader age and occupational groups would be needed to clarify the direction of these associations.

Conclusion and suggestions

In this cross-sectional study, a higher level of social media addiction was associated with a slightly less favorable perception of exercise benefits, although the association was weak. Overall, students reported a low-to-slight level of social media addiction—particularly in the Mood Modification, Repeat, and Conflict subdimensions—together with a good level of perceived exercise benefits and a high level of perceived barriers. Heavier and more frequent daily phone and social media use, and longer daily social media time, were associated with higher addiction scores, with no clear relationship to exercise behavior.

Because the data are cross-sectional, these results describe associations rather than causes and should be interpreted cautiously. Reducing or limiting social media use is one possible strategy against addiction, although complete restriction is unlikely to be practical or sufficient. As a direction for future research rather than a conclusion drawn from the present data, applications designed to encourage physical activity and to give users feedback on relevant health indicators during use may be worth evaluating in interventional and longitudinal studies.
