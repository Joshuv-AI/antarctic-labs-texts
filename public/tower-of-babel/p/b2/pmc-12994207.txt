Banana aroma is a result of acetohydroxyacid synthase and isopropylmalate synthase alternative isoforms that bypass feedback inhibition
Authors: Philip Engelgau, Randolph M. Beaudry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceDuring banana fruit ripening, the two feedback-inhibited, rate-limiting enzymes of the valine and leucine biosynthetic pathway are simultaneously alternatively spliced, effectively losing their feedback regulation and promoting carbon flux into the pathway for the accumulation of precursors to branched-chain amino acids and volatile branched-chain esters. The fact that the two reactions are positioned sequentially in the pathway may help explain why the production of significant levels of “banana” flavor compounds is so rare in nature. Understanding this phenomenon is timely given the current threat to global Cavendish production due to the spread of Fusarium wilt. Coordinated, dual alternative splicing of rate-limiting enzymes is a means to metabolic dysregulation to enable unidirectional, committed processes like aroma formation during fruit ripening.

The characteristic flavor of banana fruit (Musa spp.) is exceedingly rare in nature. Among commercially produced fruits, the compounds that are responsible for a banana fruit’s distinctive aroma, 3-methylbutyl esters, are occasionally emanated in trace amounts, but no other fruit generates these volatiles at levels comparable to banana (1, 2). The synthesis of these volatiles, as well as “fruity” (2) 2-methylpropyl and butyl esters, is dependent upon the de novo production of branched-chain precursors via the same metabolic routes as branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis, with 2-methylpropyl and 3-methylbutyl esters derived from the α-ketoacid precursors of valine and leucine, respectively (Fig. 1) (3–5).

These iso-branched-chain amino acids, and by implication their α-ketoacids, have been observed among free amino acids to uniquely accumulate in ripening banana fruit pulp, the tissue of aroma biogenesis (6). However, it is not clear how this is enabled; the biosynthetic pathway is regulated by strict feedback mechanisms that should prevent such stark increases under normal metabolism (Fig. 1) (7, 8). Specifically, acetohydroxyacid synthase (AHAS) acts as a regulator of the total pool of iso-branched-chain amino acids and is synergistically inhibited by valine and leucine. Isopropylmalate synthase (IPMS) acts as a fulcrum that balances these compounds by facilitating the extension of α-ketoisovalerate to α-ketoisocaproate and is regulated by leucine (9). The observed phenomenon of banana fruits accumulating an ample supply of precursors for aroma biosynthesis is paradoxical to these canonical regulatory networks.

Apple fruit (Malus × domestica Borkh.) form anteiso-branched-chain ester precursors by circumventing isoleucine’s feedback of threonine deaminase via citramalate synthase (CMS): a neofunctionalized IPMS that is not feedback inhibited (10). This unique regulatory feature raised the possibility that bypassing or compromising feedback regulation may be thematic in branched-chain ester precursor biosynthesis in ripening fruits. In banana, regulatory bypasses would be predicted to occur at both AHAS and IPMS in order to facilitate the increased formation of iso-branched-chain ester precursors. The occurrence of two regulatory modifications in the same pathway would potentially explain the rarity of banana fruit aroma.

We investigated this possibility via physiological, genetic, metabolic, enzymatic, and transgenic analyses of ripening banana fruit tissues.

Results

Discovery and Characterization of the Alternate Splice Forms and Their Predicted Proteins.

IPMS.

In addition to CMS in apple, neofunctionalized forms of IPMS have been recruited to specialized metabolism in Solanaceae, Brassicaceae, and Lamiaceae (11–13). However, unlike in apple, these IPMS derivatives provide precursors for defensive compounds in vegetative tissues. Nonetheless, each instance of neofunctionalization has involved reduced leucine feedback inhibition and, in some, shifts in substrate specificity. Given the numerous examples of IPMS deregulation and neofunctionalization, we first considered this noted gateway enzyme of specialized metabolism.

PCR of MaIPMS from ripe banana fruit pulp cDNA yielded two products differing by approximately 200 bp (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). While sequencing revealed the longer product to be the complete MaIPMS sequence, the shorter product lacked the entirety of exons 9 and 10, a 219 bp loss, and, of the predicted protein, an internal deletion of 73 amino acids with no frameshifts or substitutions (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Genomic resources for banana indicate only one copy of IPMS to be present (SI Appendix, Table S1), and no evidence was found to indicate that the transcripts are allelic or of different genes, thus, they were inferred to be the result of alternative splicing. 3’ RACE further confirmed the presence of only two transcript variants. The longer isoform was named MaIPMS.1 and the shorter was named MaIPMS.2.

Alignment of the predicted MaIPMS structures with the solved structure of Mycobacterium tuberculosis IPMS (14) indicated that the missing regions of MaIPMS.2 correspond with α13 of linker subdomain II, and β11, β12, β13, and α14 of the leucine-binding regulatory (R) domain (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Figs. S3–S5). This eliminates the R-domain’s upper βββα motif that normally folds with another βββα to form a pair of β-wrapped α-helices that allosterically bind the feedback regulator leucine. It also exposes a relatively large and otherwise internal region of the enzyme. The catalytic TIM barrel, however, is not predicted to be affected.

Given the expected maintenance of the catalytic domain’s structure, the disruption of the leucine binding sites between the N-terminals of α14 and α15’ of the R-domain (14), and the numerous other neofunctionalized IPMS enzymes that have modified R-domains but preservation of catalytic activity (10–13) (SI Appendix, Figs. S6 and S7), we hypothesized that MaIPMS.2 lacks feedback inhibition to leucine, but retains enzymatic activity.

AHAS.

After screening existing transcriptomic data (15) and PCR-based confirmation, an alternative transcript of the regulatory subunit (RSU) of AHAS was confirmed to be present in ripe fruit pulp. The shorter transcript lacked exon 3, corresponding with an 84 bp loss that results in a protein with an internal deletion of 28 amino acids with no frameshifts or substitutions (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). 3’ RACE confirmed the presence of only two variants. These transcripts were named MaAHAS_RSU1.1 and MaAHAS_RSU1.2. The second copy of the gene, MaAHAS_RSU2, was not found to undergo alternative splicing in ripening fruits (SI Appendix, Fig. S9).

Analysis against the solved Arabidopsis thaliana AHAS structure (16) indicated the predicted MaAHAS_RSU1.2 structure to lack the residues comprising the β4 C-terminal of the first ACT domain’s signature βαββαβ motif. In the linker domain, the entirety of β5 and some of α3 is missing (SI Appendix, Figs. S10–S12). Linker folds β8 and β9 were predicted to be disordered.

AHAS ACT domains confer feedback inhibition to the greater holoenzyme (7). When valine and/or leucine is present, the ACT domain α-helices bend and cause disruption to the catalytic sites of the bound catalytic subunit (CSU) dimer (16). While the altered β4 of MaAHAS_RSU1.2 is on the periphery of the ACT domain and farthest from the amino acid binding sites, it does contribute to stabilization of the CSU dimer (16). Further, the linker domain’s role of forming the holoenzyme’s multimeric core is also thought to stabilize the ACT domain-CSU dimer interface (16).

It is unclear how the loss of residues may affect catalytic activity or feedback inhibition in MaAHAS_RSU1.2. However, when considered with the predictions for MaIPMS.2, we hypothesized that both MaAHAS_RSU1.2 and MaIPMS.2 lack feedback regulation and yet retain catalytic activity, providing an uninhibited supply of precursors to iso-branched-chain ester biosynthesis in ripening banana fruit.

Gene Expression and Frequency of Splicing Relative to Ripening Physiology and Biochemistry.

To discern gene expression of MaAHAS and MaIPMS relative to changing metabolic physiology during ripening, a population of banana fruit was ripened in the laboratory via exposure to propylene and had pulp sampled daily. Pyruvate, the entry metabolite to iso-branched-chain metabolism, spiked in content a day after the fruit entered the climacteric (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Figs. S13 and 14). The next day iso-branched-chain α-ketoacids, iso-branched-chain amino acids, and α-isopropylmalate began to rapidly accumulate (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S14). Interestingly, the ratios of iso-branched-chain amino acid to iso-branched-chain α-ketoacid shifted at least ten-fold in favor of the α-ketoacids soon after accumulation began (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). Volatiles derived from iso-branched-chain metabolism (e.g., 2-methylpropyl acetate, 3-methylbutyl acetate, and butyl acetate) accelerated in emanation 3 to 4 d after the climacteric peak (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Figs. S15–S17). Values and trends of previously analyzed metabolites (pyruvate, amino acids, and esters) were consistent with past findings (6, 17, 18).

MaAHAS_RSU1.2 and MaIPMS.2 steadily increased in proportion throughout ripening, comprising 6.1 and 24.1% of MaAHAS_RSU1 and MaIPMS transcripts at maximum, respectively (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Figs. S18–20). MaAHAS_RSU1.2 expression peaked a day after the climacteric peak to be 9-times greater than unripe fruit and was thereafter maintained close to double that of unripe fruit (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S19). MaIPMS.2 expression steadily increased after the climacteric peak, eventually reaching levels of expression 95-times greater than unripe fruit (Fig. 3 and S20). While both of the long isoforms underwent increases of expression during ripening, their shorter isoforms were more sharply up-regulated (SI Appendix, Figs. S19-S20). A copy of the catalytic subunit of AHAS, MaAHAS_CSU1, increased in expression 10-fold over unripe levels a day after the climacteric peak and was thereafter maintained (SI Appendix, Fig. S18).

MaAHAS_CSU2 and MaAHAS_RSU2 failed to undergo such marked increases of expression compared to their respective copies. It was interpreted that these genes are not involved in shifting carbon allocation during ripening (SI Appendix, Fig. S18).

In nonfruit tissues (i.e., bract, anther, ovary, peduncle, pseudostem, leaf, and root), no expression of MaAHAS_RSU1.2 was detected (SI Appendix, Table S2). In ripe fruit peel, which notably produces very little aroma compared to fruit pulp (6), MaAHAS_RSU1.2 was found to comprise 3.4% of MaAHAS_RSU1 transcripts. While MaIPMS.2 was detected in several of the tissues tested, it made up no more than 1.5% of MaIPMS transcripts. Within ripe fruit peels, MaIPMS.2 comprised 1.1% of MaIPMS transcripts. Collectively, these results indicate a highly coordinated, developmental, and tissue-specific pattern of expression for the shorter transcripts.

Equal molar mixtures of both regulatory subunits had intermediate levels of in vitro activity and feedback inhibition (SI Appendix, Table S3), as would be expected given the minor functional interaction between RSU subunits within the AHAS holoenzyme (16).

MaIPMS.

We encountered difficulty in successfully expressing soluble MaIPMS.2 in bacteria (Escherichia coli) or tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana), described below. Instead, we prepared two mutants of MaIPMS to simulate the lost residues of MaIPMS.2 (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). In the mutant MaIPMS.PV523-524AA, the terminal residues of α14 that bind leucine (14) and are among those lost in MaIPMS.2 were substituted to alanine. MaIPMS.PV523-524AA was analyzed for kinetics and feedback inhibition, substrate preference tests, and expression in tobacco. In MaIPMS.α14del, the entirety of α14 was omitted. This mutant was only used for leucine inhibition studies.

Overall, MaIPMS.PV523-524AA had about half the activity of MaIPMS.1 (Table 1). MaIPMS.PV523-524AA was less efficient in terms of Vmax, Km, and kcat when activity with α-ketoisovalerate was assessed. This was found to be the same regarding activity with acetyl-CoA, save that the Km was one-quarter that of MaIPMS.1. This ultimately translated into comparable kcat/Km values for the holoenzymes with acetyl-CoA, but a 3.7-fold reduction of the kcat/Km of MaIPMS.PV523-524AA with α-ketoisovalerate.

MaIPMS.1 was steeply inhibited by leucine to 42.1% maximum activity with 0.1 mM leucine, and 30.2% with 0.5 mM, reaching 27.3% activity at 5 mM (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S21). As with MaAHAS_RSU1.1, MaIPMS.1 would be at or nearly at full inhibition throughout the entirety ripening given that the leucine content of ripening banana fruit increases from 0.5 to 2.75 mM. MaIPMS.PV523-524AA was only mildly inhibited, declining to no less than 91.5% maximum activity with up to 5 mM leucine. MaIPMS.α14del was unaffected by leucine.

Equal molar mixtures of MaIPMS.1 and MaIPMS.PV523-524AA had intermediate levels of in vitro activity and feedback inhibition (SI Appendix, Fig. S21 and Table S4). We speculate that these results likely indicate that the different isoforms preferentially form homodimers, thus denying the opportunity for a MaIPMS.2 monomer to “poison” the inhibitory function of MaIPMS.1.

To discern possible shifts of α-ketoacid preference between isoforms, we assessed the preferred substrates of other neofunctionalized IPMS enzymes; however, no substantive difference was observed between isoforms. MaIPMS.1 and MaIPMS.PV523-524AA had the highest activity with the canonical IPMS substrate α-ketoisovalerate. Levels of activity with other α-ketoacid substrates roughly mirrored the accumulation of their products in ripening banana fruit pulp (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 and Table S5).

In Planta Enzymatic Roles.

If a linear effect of inhibition on kcat is assumed, MaAHAS_RSU1.1 and MaIPMS.1 within ripening banana fruits will have lower kcat values with their corresponding α-ketoacid substrates than MaAHAS_RSU1.2 and MaIPMS.PV523-524AA, respectively (SI Appendix, Table S6). MaIPMS.1 and MaIPMS.PV523-524AA would have comparable kcat values with acetyl-CoA.

To observe how these kinetic shifts may manifest in differences of metabolite accumulation within a living system, the enzyme isoforms were transiently expressed under the CaMV 35S promoter in tobacco leaves (N. benthamiana). As this tissue is not undergoing rapid physiological and metabolic shifts (as in ripening banana fruit), expression within this system provides a well-controlled testing environment. MaAHAS_RSU1 isoforms, as for in vitro experiments, were coexpressed with MaAHAS_CSU1. Initial results indicated expression of MaIPMS.2 to have also failed in tobacco, thus MaIPMS.PV523-524AA was expressed in order to observe the in planta effects on branched-chain amino acid metabolism when MaIPMS is deregulated.

Expression of MaAHAS_RSU1.2 led to greater amounts of α-ketoisovalerate, valine, and leucine than when MaAHAS_RSU1.1 was expressed (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Figs. S22 and S23). When the IPMS isoforms were expressed alone there were no significant changes to the metabolite pools save that the ratio of valine:leucine was shifted to favor leucine (Fig. 4 and S22-S23).

Coexpression of MaAHAS_RSU1.2 with MaIPMS was required for plants to accumulate α-ketoisocaproate, α-isopropylmalate, and greater amounts of leucine than when only MaAHAS_RSU1.2 was expressed (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Figs. S22 and S23). However, while the combined total of valine and leucine was not affected when MaAHAS_RSU1.2 and MaIPMS were coexpressed, the distribution of the two had shifted, as demonstrated by a significantly reduced valine:leucine ratio. Expression of MaIPMS.PV523-524AA with MaAHAS_RSU1.2 led to greater amounts of all three leucine-related metabolites compared to the analogous treatment with MaIPMS.1 (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Figs. S22 and S23). Thus, in both cases, the alternative isoforms resulted in a greater accumulation of iso-branched-chain metabolites than the full-length variants.

Transient RNAi and overexpression of the transcript variants within ripening banana fruit pulp was attempted, as was the use of toxic branched-chain amino acid analogs to selectively knock out the full-length enzyme isoforms; however, these methods were found to not be amenable approaches. These and other efforts to further investigate the contribution of each isoform in vivo are further discussed in SI Appendix, Supporting Text.

Discussion

The virtual exclusivity of MaAHAS_RSU1.2 and MaIPMS.2 to the tissues of aroma biogenesis and their concomitant expression with iso-branched-chain ester precursor accumulation and aroma biosynthesis during ripening strongly implicates these isoforms as being involved with these processes. While the longer enzyme isoforms were observed in vitro to have greater activity than the shorter isoforms when valine and leucine content is low, they were rapidly inhibited by relatively minor increases of them. Alternatively, the shorter isoforms, which are only mildly feedback inhibited, outperform their canonical counterparts when in the presence of the high levels of valine and leucine found in ripening fruit pulp. Indeed, when expressed in planta, the alternative isoforms or their surrogates led to a greater accumulation of iso-branched-chain metabolites than the variants of normal metabolism. Thus despite shortfalls of catalytic efficiencies, their demonstrated lack of feedback inhibition ultimately allows for a greater capacity to produce the precursors of aroma compounds. The collected evidence points toward MaAHAS_RSU1.2 and MaIPMS.2 as being incumbent for the biosynthesis of banana fruit’s characteristic flavor.

AHAS.

MaAHAS_RSU1.2 is a natural variant of a plant AHAS_RSU to have impaired feedback inhibition as a function of specialized metabolism recruitment. The catalytic activity and feedback inhibition of AHAS are interconnected through the ACT and linker domains of the RSU (16). The alterations of MaAHAS_RSU1.2 appear to have disrupted the ability of the ACT domains to impart feedback regulation and yet provide enough stability to maintain catalytic activity—an impressive feat when one considers it was done via exon exclusion.

It has been proposed that each of the two ACT binding site of the RSU is preferential to valine or leucine (7). The complete relief of MaAHAS_RSU1.2 from leucine inhibition suggests that this inhibitor’s site is being substantially affected.

Finally, since treatment of banana pulp with sulfonylureas almost completely inhibits AHAS activity (3), the substrate channels of the CSUs [the binding site of sulfonylureas (19)] are seemingly unaltered in the alternative isoform.

IPMS.

The difficultly to express MaIPMS.2 in E. coli and the lack of perceived activity in N. benthamiana suggest that if MaIPMS.2 is utilized by banana, some additional form of stabilization (absent in these model systems) is seemingly necessary. Difficulty to fold properly likely stems from the large area of otherwise internally folded residues exposed by the deletion of one of the R-domain’s two βββα motifs. Stabilization may take the form of a unique chaperone protein or posttranslational modification, such as proteolytic truncation of the apparently disordered C-terminus of MaIPMS.2. There is a distinct poetry to this latter possibility: The resulting IPMS protein, lacking the regions downstream of linker subdomain II, would be analogous to the evolutionary modifications that have occurred in other IPMS neofunctionalizations (SI Appendix, Figs. S6 and S7) (10–12). On the other hand, it may also be possible that MaAHAS_RSU1.2 is dominant in facilitating the metabolic shifts observed in ripening bananas and that a modified IPMS variant is not essential. The MaIPMS.2 transcript may be a splicing byproduct or is perhaps vestigial.

Still, the coordinated and specific expression of MaIPMS.2 with MaAHAS_RSU1.2 in ripening fruit pulp strongly argues joint involvement. Our designed MaIPMS mutants likewise indicate that the residues lacking in MaIPMS.2 are sufficient to relieve feedback inhibition by leucine, agreeing with the observed properties of other neofunctionalized IPMS enzymes (SI Appendix, Fig. S24). In contrast to other members of this growing family, MaIPMS.2 is produced by developmentally specific alternative splicing instead of alteration to a duplicated IPMS gene. Remarkably, MaIPMS.2 appears to be another IPMS recruited to specialized metabolism to produce aroma compounds in fruit, joining CMS of apple fruits (10).

Alternative Splicing As a Metabolic Regulator in Plants.

Alternative splicing allows for proteome diversification with minimal genomic expansion. While alternative splicing is widely recognized as an important process of human transcriptional regulation, the extent and importance of alternative splicing in plant specialized metabolism is burgeoning. Advances in the understanding of alternative splicing with regard to fruit development and plant metabolism are a part of this growing field (20–24). It is conceivable that alternative splicing is an important means to enact the rapid physiological and metabolic changes that occur during fruit ripening.

The alternative splicing of AHAS and IPMS in ripening banana pulp demonstrates the finding of a pair of rate-limiting enzymes within the same pathway being recruited to specialized metabolism via alternative splicing. The necessity of dysregulation for both enzymes likely explains the rarity of substantial 2-methylpropyl and 3-methylbutyl ester production by fruits.

Conclusion

Fruits are sacrificial organs that often are dependent upon the development of color, taste, and aroma cues to attract a consumer for successful seed dispersion. Failure translates into a substantial waste of resources and a loss of fitness. Deliberate, programmed dysregulation of primary metabolic processes to procure precursors for specialized metabolites is an emerging theme of fruit biochemistry. The circumvention of feedback inhibition by increased expression of CMS in ripening apple fruits (10) and methionine gamma lyase in muskmelons (25) demonstrates targeted dysregulation of primary branched-chain amino acid metabolic pathways for the purpose of instigating specialized aroma ester formation. This, coupled with our findings of a mechanism in banana fruit involving alternative splicing of AHAS and IPMS, indicates ripening-dependent dysregulation to be a recurring theme in branched-chain ester biosynthesis.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material and Ripening Scheme.

United States Department of Agriculture certified organic, mature, unripe, green bananas (Musa spp. AAA group, Cavendish subgroup, cv. Valery) originating from Ecuador, that were not treated with ethylene, were obtained from a local supermarket produce distribution and ripening center on the day of their arrival at the facility (Meijer/Chiquita, Lansing, MI). Ripening was initiated by exposure to ~1,200 µL L−1 propylene, equivalent to 12 µL L−1 ethylene (26), for 12 h. Propylene use allowed for endogenous ethylene analysis. Subsamples were analyzed and collected daily throughout ripening until 7 d after the climacteric peak. Nonfruit tissues were collected from the University of Florida’s Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences Tropical Research and Education Center. See SI Appendix, Materials and Methods for further detail.

Internal Gasses.

Internal CO2, ethylene, and propylene concentrations were measured by sampling the headspace of a septum-sealed silicone-attached glass vial on the peel as previously described (27). CO2 was measured via an infrared gas analyzer (225-MK3; Analytical Development Company, Hoddesdon, UK); ethylene and propylene were measured via a gas chromatograph equipped with a flame ionization detector (Carle Series 400 AGC; Hach Company, Loveland, CO). See SI Appendix, Materials and Methods for further detail.

Aroma Analysis.

Fruits selected for aroma analysis had glass cylinders and silicone gently removed before being incubated for 20 min in 2 L sealed Teflon jars (Savillex Corporation, Minnetonka, MN). Headspace volatiles were then sampled via solid-phase microextraction and analyzed on a gas chromatograph (GC; HP-6890, Hewlett-Packard, Wilmington, DE) coupled to a time-of-flight mass spectrometer (MS; Pegasus II, LECO, St. Joseph, MI) using cryofocusing during desorption as previously described (3). See SI Appendix, Materials and Methods for further detail.

Nonvolatile Metabolite Analysis.

Valine, leucine, isoleucine, threonine, citramalate, α-isopropylmalate, α-ketoisovalerate, α-ketoisocaproate, α-ketovalerate, α-ketocaproate, and α-ketooctanoate were extracted from ground tissue in 65 °C 1:1 water:acetonitrile and derivatized via methoxyamination and tert-butyldimethylsilylation prior to analysis on a GCMS as previously described (3). Compounds were quantified against U-13C,15N-labeled amino acid internal standards. Pyruvate was found to evaporate quickly from this extraction method and was instead cold-extracted in 0.6 N HClO4 solution for 30 min prior to enzymatic analysis with L-lactic acid dehydrogenase from rabbit muscle (Sigma Aldrich) on a spectrophotometer (U-3000, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) as previously described (28). See SI Appendix, Materials and Methods for further detail.

Nucleic acid extraction.

DNA was extracted from leaf tissues using a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide buffer and chloroform two-phase extraction before several salt-alcohol precipitations and washes, inspired by previous work (29). RNA was extracted from tissues with a hot borate buffer and chloroform-phenol two-phase extraction before several salt-ethanol precipitations and washes, inspired by previous work (30). See SI Appendix, Materials and Methods for further detail.

Quantitative PCR.

SYBR-based quantification.

Quantification of total expression of the genes of interest was performed with a SYBR-based qPCR approach using primers targeting genetic regions that are not alternatively spliced. Amplicons were under 250 bp in length. Primers used can be seen in SI Appendix, Table S7 and were optimized to have similar melting points. MaDNAJ and MaRPS4 were used as reference genes (31). Fold-change of expression was calculated with the 2–∆∆Ct method and normalized against the average of preinduced fruit. See SI Appendix, Materials and Methods for further detail.

Gel densitometry-based quantification.

Quantification of transcript isoform distribution for MaAHAS_RSU1 and MaIPMS was performed with a gel densitometry approach. Primers facing toward the splice junction were used to produce amplicons of two different lengths, one including the spliced portion and one without. PCR products were then viewed after gel electrophoresis and the band areas and densities were digitally integrated. The percent of each transcript was calculated by dividing the response of each band by the total response of the two combined. The method was verified with standards derived from plasmids prepared for protein expression and proved to be reliable and precise for both genes, particularly so for the higher concentrations of template (SI Appendix, Fig. S25).

The fold-change of specific isoforms was calculated by multiplying the percent of an isoform (determined by gel densitometry) by the normalized fold-change expression of both isoforms together (determined by SYBR qPCR). This product was then renormalized against preinduced fruit. See SI Appendix, Materials and Methods for further detail.

In Vitro Enzyme Analysis.

Enzymes were expressed and extracted from Escherichia coli using various affinity tags. All in vitro analyses were performed without predicted chloroplast targeting peptides. MaAHAS was assessed with the MaAHAS_CSU1 subunit present via an endpoint Voges-Proskauer test. MaIPMS was assessed with an endpoint 5,5-dithio-bis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) test. These methods were inspired by past studies (7–9). See SI Appendix, Materials and Methods for further detail.

In Planta Enzyme Analysis.

Shifts of metabolite content in planta was assessed by transiently overexpressing (CaMV 35S) enzyme isoforms in N. benthamiana leaves for 6 d prior to extraction and methoxyamination and tert-butyldimethylsilylation derivation and GCMS analysis, as above-described. Methods were inspired by past studies (10, 32). See SI Appendix, Materials and Methods for further detail.
