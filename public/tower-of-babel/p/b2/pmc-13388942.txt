High glucose levels sensitize neuron-like cells to damage induced by oxidative and neurotoxic agents
Authors: Jéssica Ellen Barbosa de Freitas Lima, Natália Chermont dos Santos Moreira, Larissa de Oliveira Piassi, Renata Melo dos Santos Ono, Elza Tiemi Sakamoto-Hojo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Type 2 diabetes has been associated with cognitive decline and an increased risk of neurodegenerative diseases, although the cellular mechanisms underlying this relationship remain unclear. We investigated whether high-glucose exposure induces neuronal damage and increases susceptibility to secondary oxidative and neurotoxic insults in vitro. SH-SY5Y cells were differentiated into neuron-like cells and exposed to increasing glucose concentrations. Based on the viability results, cells exposed to high glucose (HG; 100 mM, 120 h) were further analyzed and challenged with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) or okadaic acid (OA). Cell viability, DNA damage, mitochondrial superoxide production, cell cycle distribution, cell death, and gene expression were evaluated. Differentiation was confirmed by increased neurite length and expression of neuronal markers (β-III-tubulin and MAP2). High glucose reduced cell viability in a concentration- and time-dependent manner. HG exposure increased oxidative DNA damage, mitochondrial superoxide levels, and apoptosis, accompanied by reduced OGG1 gene expression. Combined treatments with HG and H2O2 or OA further decreased cell viability and increased the sub-G1 fraction compared with controls and single treatments. These findings demonstrate that HG exerts cytotoxic and genotoxic effects and sensitizes neuron-like cells to subsequent oxidative and neurotoxic insults, thereby contributing to mechanisms underlying neuronal dysfunction and neurodegenerative processes.

Introduction

The number of individuals aged 65 years or older is projected to reach approximately 1.6 billion worldwide by 20501. As life expectancy increases, the prevalence of age-related diseases, including diabetes, dementia, and neurodegenerative disorders, is expected to rise substantially in the coming decades.

Diabetes currently affects more than 537 million people worldwide, and this number is projected to increase by 46% by 20452. Type 2 diabetes (T2D) accounts for more than 90% of all diabetes cases and is characterized by insulin resistance and chronic hyperglycemia, frequently accompanied by compensatory hyperinsulinemia in the early stages. These metabolic alterations contribute to glucolipotoxicity, which can damage pancreatic beta cells and impair insulin production3, leading to multiple systemic complications, including neuropathy, retinopathy, cardiovascular and renal diseases4.

Beyond peripheral complications, growing evidence indicates that T2D also affects the central nervous system3. Patients with T2D exhibit poorer performance in several cognitive domains, including memory, attention, and information processing speed5, as well as brain atrophy and hippocampal shrinkage6,7. Recent epidemiological studies indicate that diabetes nearly doubles the risk of developing dementia8–11.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) accounts for 60 to 80% of dementia cases12 and is characterized by amyloid beta (Aβ) accumulation, tau hyperphosphorylation, synaptic dysfunction, inflammation, and progressive neuronal loss13,14. Although extensive research has linked T2D to neurodegenerative diseases, such as AD, the underlying mechanisms by which glucose may contribute to neurodegeneration in patients with T2D remain poorly understood.

Due to its high oxygen demand, the brain is particularly vulnerable to oxidative stress. Neuronal cells contain large amounts of polyunsaturated fatty acids, which are prone to peroxidation by reactive oxygen species (ROS), and express relatively low levels of antioxidant enzymes, further increasing their susceptibility to oxidative damage15,16. Evidence indicates that oxidative stress markers precede the onset of neurodegenerative diseases, such as AD, and may contribute to disease pathogenesis16. Moreover, chronic hyperglycemia in patients with T2D has been associated with DNA damage accumulation, altered gene expression profiles17, as well as mitochondrial dysfunction and other metabolic disturbances, potentially contributing to neuronal dysfunction and neurodegenerative processes18–20.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate whether long-term exposure to high glucose concentrations induces cytotoxic and genotoxic effects in differentiated SH-SY5Y neuron-like cells and whether it increases susceptibility to oxidative and neurotoxic damage. Differentiated SH-SY5Y cells are widely used as a human neuron-like model for investigating mechanisms related to neuronal damage, oxidative stress, and neurotoxicity in vitro21,22. Understanding these mechanisms may provide insights into how chronic high-glucose exposure exacerbates cellular processes that promote neuronal vulnerability during aging and neurodegeneration.

Results

Differentiation of SH-SY5Y cells into neuron-like cells

RA treatment for eight days induced morphological changes consistent with neuron-like cells, such as increased neurite extension and reduced cell body size compared with undifferentiated cells (Fig. 1A). Differentiated cells also showed evident expression of the neuronal markers β-III-tubulin and MAP2 (Fig. 1B) and quantitative analysis confirmed a marked increase in neurite length in these cells (57.92 μm) compared with undifferentiated cells (9.75 μm; p < 0.001; Fig. 1C). The proportion of differentiated cells was also significantly higher after RA treatment (differentiated; ~96%) than in untreated cells (undifferentiated; ~1%; p < 0.001; Fig. 1D). These results indicate successful differentiation of SH-SY5Y cells into neuron-like cells, which were used in the subsequent experiments.

High glucose reduces cell viability in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells in a concentration- and time-dependent manner

Cell viability was evaluated in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells exposed to increasing glucose concentrations (25, 50, 75, and 100 mM) at different time points (24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h). Glucose exposure significantly reduced cell viability in a concentration- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 2A–E). Treatment with 100 mM glucose significantly reduced cell viability at all time points, resulting in approximately 65% viability after 120 h compared with the negative control (p < 0.001; Fig. 2E). Exposure to mannitol (100 mM, 120 h), used as an osmotic control, did not cause significant differences in cell viability (p > 0.05; Fig. 2F) compared with the negative control. Undifferentiated SH-SY5Y cells were more sensitive to glucose exposure, showing a significant reduction in cell viability at all tested concentrations (25–100 mM glucose) after 120 h (p < 0.001; Fig. 2G). Based on these results, 100 mM glucose for 120 h (HG) was selected as the high-glucose condition for subsequent experiments.

High glucose increases oxidative DNA damage in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells

To determine whether HG induces DNA damage (DNA strand breaks), differentiated cells were analyzed using the comet assay. HG treatment (100 mM for 120 h) did not significantly increase DNA strand breaks compared with the control (p > 0.05; Fig. 3A; without hOGG1). However, HG significantly increased oxidative DNA damage detected after treatment with the hOGG1 enzyme (p < 0.01; Fig. 3A, B).

High glucose increases mitochondrial superoxide production, alters gene expression, and promotes apoptotic cell death

Given the increase in oxidative DNA damage observed after HG exposure, mitochondrial superoxide levels, cell death, and gene expression were subsequently evaluated. HG-treated neuron-like cells exhibited significantly higher mitochondrial superoxide levels than control cells (p < 0.01; Fig. 4A). HG exposure also significantly increased the proportion of apoptotic cells (p < 0.05; Fig. 4B), whereas no significant effect was observed for necrotic cells (p > 0.05; Fig. 4B). The SOD1 (superoxide dismutase 1) gene expression remained unchanged relative to control cells (p > 0.05; Fig. 4C). By contrast, OGG1 (8-oxoguanine DNA glycosylase) gene expression was significantly reduced in HG-treated cells (p < 0.05; Fig. 4D).

High glucose potentiates H2O2-induced oxidative damage in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells

To determine whether HG increases susceptibility to oxidative damage, we first established H2O2 treatment conditions in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells. H2O2 reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, with 50 µM decreasing viability to approximately 50% (p < 0.001; Fig. 5A).

Differentiated cells previously exposed to HG (100 mM for 120 h) were then challenged with H2O2 (50 µM, 30 min). H2O2 alone (50 µM) significantly reduced cell viability to approximately 50% (p < 0.001), whereas HG alone reduced viability to 81% (p < 0.001) compared with the negative control (Fig. 5B). Combined HG + H2O2 treatment further decreased viability compared with H2O2 alone (p < 0.01), HG alone (p < 0.001), and control cells (p < 0.001; Fig. 5B), indicating that HG enhanced H2O2-induced cytotoxicity.

Moreover, combined HG + H2O2 treatment significantly reduced the proportion of differentiated cells in the G0/G1 phase compared with control cells (p < 0.05; Fig. 5C, F). In parallel, the sub-G1 fraction was significantly increased compared with both control cells (p < 0.05) and HG-treated cells (p < 0.01; Fig. 5D, F), consistent with increased cell death. Although significant differences were not detected in apoptosis or necrosis rates among groups, we observed a trend toward increased apoptosis in HG-treated, H2O2-treated, and combined HG + H2O2-treated cells (p > 0.05; Fig. 5E).

High glucose sensitizes differentiated SH-SY5Y cells to OA-induced neurotoxic damage

To assess the impact of HG on OA-induced neurotoxicity, OA treatment conditions were first established in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells. OA reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, with 10 nM significantly decreasing viability to approximately 65% (p < 0.01; Fig. 6A). Based on these results, 7.5 nM OA, a non-cytotoxic concentration, was selected for subsequent experiments.

Differentiated cells previously exposed to HG (100 mM for 120 h) were then challenged with OA (7.5 nM, 24 h). Combined HG + OA treatment markedly reduced viability to approximately 52% compared with OA alone (p < 0.001), HG alone (p < 0.01), and control cells (p < 0.001; Fig. 6B), indicating that HG sensitized neuron-like cells to OA-induced neurotoxicity.

Moreover, combined HG + OA treatment significantly reduced the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase of the cell cycle compared with control cells (p < 0.05; Fig. 6C, F). In parallel, the sub-G1 fraction was significantly increased compared with OA-treated cells (p < 0.01), HG-treated cells (p < 0.01), and control cells (p < 0.05; Figs. 6D, F), consistent with increased cell death. Although no significant differences in apoptosis or necrosis rates were detected among groups, a trend toward increased apoptotic rates was observed in HG-treated, OA-treated, and combined HG + OA-treated cells (p > 0.05; Fig. 6E).

Discussion

T2D has been consistently associated with an increased risk of cognitive decline and neurodegenerative diseases8,20,23. Although the cellular mechanisms linking chronic hyperglycemia to neuronal dysfunction remain incompletely understood, these processes likely involve progressive oxidative stress, DNA damage accumulation, and mitochondrial dysfunction, which together may compromise neuronal homeostasis and survival.

In the present study, using differentiated SH-SY5Y neuron-like cells, we showed that prolonged high-glucose exposure induces cytotoxic and genotoxic effects and increases susceptibility to secondary oxidative and neurotoxic insults.

Differentiated SH-SY5Y cells were found to be more resistant to glucose exposure than undifferentiated cells. This finding is consistent with previous reports showing that undifferentiated SH-SY5Y cells are highly sensitive to glucose-induced toxicity, with a marked reduction in viability after short-term exposure to 50 mM glucose24. For this reason, subsequent experiments under the same high-glucose conditions were not feasible in those cells and were restricted to differentiated cells. Neuronal differentiation is known to alter metabolic demand and glucose utilization25,26, which may explain the greater tolerance to glucose observed in differentiated cells. We showed that high glucose (100 mM for 120 h) significantly reduced viability, indicating that sustained high-glucose conditions impair neuronal survival.

High-glucose exposure also increased oxidative DNA damage and mitochondrial superoxide production, supporting a central role for oxidative stress in high-glucose-induced neuronal injury. Hyperglycemia has been associated with increased ROS generation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative DNA damage in several experimental models27–29. In parallel, OGG1 gene expression was significantly reduced, whereas SOD1 gene expression remained unchanged. Because OGG1 is a key enzyme in the base excision repair pathway (BER) responsible for recognition and removal of oxidized DNA bases30, its reduction may indicate impaired repair capacity, thereby favoring the accumulation of oxidative lesions under high-glucose conditions. The absence of changes in SOD1 gene expression despite increased superoxide levels may suggest an insufficient antioxidant response to high-glucose-induced oxidative stress, potentially contributing to redox imbalance and persistent oxidative damage.

In addition, we observed that high glucose increased apoptotic cell death, as determined by Annexin V/PI staining, a widely accepted quantitative method for detecting early and late apoptotic events31,32. Similar findings were reported by Tan et al.33, who showed that high glucose induces neuronal apoptosis through Fyn activation, a kinase associated with oxidative stress34 and Aβ aggregation35. Shi et al.36 also reported that glucose-induced neuronal apoptosis may involve reduced SIRT1 signaling, dysregulation of p53-dependent pathways, and impaired DNA repair processes. Consistent with these observations, our results suggest that neuronal death may be associated with increased ROS levels and oxidative DNA damage under high-glucose conditions. Furthermore, the altered expression of genes involved in the BER pathway, such as OGG1, suggests that some DNA lesions may not be efficiently repaired, thereby contributing to apoptosis, although this aspect requires further investigation.

Long-term high-glucose treatment further enhanced the effects of H2O2, significantly reducing cell viability and increasing the proportion of cells in the sub-G1 fraction, a parameter associated with DNA fragmentation and cell death37. These findings suggest that prolonged high-glucose exposure increases sensitivity to subsequent oxidative insults, leading to cell death in neuron-like cells. A similar effect was reported by Bahniwal et al.38, who showed that glucose treatment potentiated H2O2-induced toxicity in undifferentiated SH-SY5Y cells. However, those authors did not evaluate apoptosis, necrosis, or cell cycle alterations, and their study was performed in undifferentiated cells rather than differentiated neuron-like cells, a model that may better represent neuronal responses.

We also investigated whether prolonged high-glucose treatment alters neuronal responses to a neurotoxic challenge using OA, a phosphatase inhibitor widely used to model tau dysregulation and neurotoxic stress in neuronal cells39. Notably, OA was tested at a non-cytotoxic concentration that did not significantly affect cell viability when administered alone. However, HG-treated cells exhibited markedly reduced viability when subsequently challenged with OA, indicating that prolonged glucose stress sensitized neuron-like cells to a non-cytotoxic neurotoxic insult.

The combined treatment (HG + OA) also increased the sub-G1 fraction, consistent with greater DNA fragmentation and enhanced cellular damage under these conditions. In contrast to the experiments with H2O2, in which HG potentiated the effects of a directly cytotoxic oxidative challenge, the OA treatment suggests that high glucose may sensitize neuron-like cells, rendering them vulnerable even to subtoxic stimuli.

To our knowledge, studies evaluating the combined effects of high-glucose exposure and OA treatment in differentiated neuron-like cells remain limited. Similar studies showed that acute high-glucose exposure (100 mM for 24 h) aggravated bupivacaine-induced neurotoxicity and mitochondrial dysfunction in undifferentiated SH-SY5Y cells40. In the present study, although apoptosis and necrosis were not significantly altered, additional cell death pathways should be investigated to better understand how HG enhances susceptibility to induced neurotoxic insults.

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that prolonged high-glucose exposure exerts cytotoxic and genotoxic effects in differentiated neuron-like cells, characterized by reduced cell viability, oxidative DNA damage, mitochondrial superoxide accumulation, impaired DNA repair-related responses, and apoptotic cell death. In addition, chronic high-glucose exposure increased cellular susceptibility to subsequent oxidative and neurotoxic insults, as evidenced by reduced viability and increased sub-G1 fraction after H2O2 or OA challenge.

These data support the concept that persistent hyperglycemia may trigger mechanisms capable of sensitizing neuronal cells to secondary oxidative and neurotoxic insults. In spite of limitations imposed by the use of a neuronal cell model in vitro, our study provides insight into how chronic metabolic imbalance (as observed in diabetes) may compromise neuronal function and survival over time, with implications for neurodegenerative processes. Future studies using additional human neuronal models, such as patient-derived neural progenitor cells (NPCs) or iPSC-derived neurons, may help elucidate the cellular mechanisms linking T2D to neurodegenerative processes.

Methods

Cell line and culture conditions

The human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y was obtained from the Cell Bank of Rio de Janeiro (CBRJ; RRID: CVCL_0019). Cells were maintained in 25 cm2 culture flasks containing Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; D5523, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and Ham’s F-10 Nutrient Mixture (N6635, Sigma-Aldrich) in a 1:1 ratio, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), 1% penicillin (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1% streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 and subcultured at approximately 80% confluence.

Neuronal differentiation and morphological characterization

SH-SY5Y cells were differentiated into neuron-like cells using 10 µM retinoic acid (RA; 11017, Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) for 8 days, as previously described by Kunzler et al.41. Briefly, 1 × 105 cells were seeded in 24-well plates containing DMEM/Ham’s F-10 (1:1) supplemented with 10% FBS. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with differentiation medium consisting of DMEM/Ham’s F-10 (1:1), 1% FBS, and 10 µM RA. The medium was renewed every 48 h during the differentiation period. For morphological analyses, images of 20 randomly selected fields per group were acquired at 20x magnification using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (EVOS XL Core Imaging System, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

Neurite length was evaluated in 100 randomly selected cells per group in each independent experiment using ImageJ (Fiji) with the Simple Neurite Tracer plugin, as previously described42,43. The mean neurite length from each experiment was used for statistical analysis.

For analysis of the percentage of differentiated cells, 100 randomly selected cells per group were evaluated in each independent experiment. Cells were considered differentiated when at least one neurite exceeded the diameter of the cell body44. The percentage of differentiated cells was calculated for each experiment and used for statistical analysis. All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence assays, 1 × 105 cells were seeded onto glass coverslips and differentiated as described above. After differentiation, cells were fixed with methanol for 10 min, washed three times with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 15 min. Cells were then blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 1 h and incubated with the following primary antibodies diluted in 2% BSA/PBS: anti-β-III-tubulin (rabbit, 1:500; Abcam, Cambridge, UK, ab18207) and anti-MAP2 (rabbit, 1:500; Abcam, ab183830) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing three times with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (Sigma-Aldrich), cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488- or Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (1:200; Invitrogen, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) diluted in 1% BSA/PBS for 30 min. Cells were then washed again with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 and stained with Hoechst 33342 (0.15 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich). Fluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss Axio Imager microscope (Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

High glucose treatment conditions

To mimic high glucose levels in vitro, D-glucose (Mallinckrodt GenAr, WI, USA) was prepared as a 1 M stock solution in DMEM/Ham’s F-10 (1:1) containing 1% FBS and sterilized through a 0.22 μm filter. Following 8 days of neuronal differentiation, cells were exposed to differentiation medium (DMEM/Ham’s F-10 (1:1) + 1% FBS + 10 µM RA) supplemented with D-glucose at final concentrations of 25, 50, 75, or 100 mM for 24–120 h. A concentration of 5 mM glucose was used as the negative control. The high-glucose (HG) condition selected for subsequent experiments was 100 mM glucose for 120 h.

Mannitol (JT Baker, Center Valley, PA, USA), prepared in DMEM/Ham’s F-10 (1:1) containing 1% FBS and sterilized through a 0.22 μm filter, was used as an osmotic control (100 mM) for 120 h. Doxorubicin (0.2 µg/mL) was used as a positive control.

For experiments using undifferentiated SH-SY5Y cells, 1 × 105 cells were seeded in 24-well plates in DMEM/Ham’s F-10 (1:1) containing 10% FBS. After 24 h, cells were exposed to the same medium supplemented with D-glucose (25–100 mM) for 120 h before analysis. Based on the marked sensitivity of undifferentiated SH-SY5Y cells to high glucose observed in viability assays, subsequent analyses were performed only in differentiated cells.

Oxidative and neurotoxic challenge assays

After establishing the HG condition, combined treatment experiments were performed. To induce oxidative damage, HG-treated and untreated differentiated SH-SY5Y cells were exposed to H2O2 (50 µM; Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min on day 12 of the protocol. The medium was then replaced with fresh differentiation medium without H2O2, either with or without HG, and cells were analyzed on day 13.

To induce neurotoxic damage45, okadaic acid (OA; Abcam, ab120375) was added on day 12 to cells previously treated with or without HG and OA was maintained in the medium for 24 h. Analyses were performed on day 13. OA was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a 100 mM stock solution and used at a final concentration of 7.5 nM. The experimental design of neuronal differentiation and treatment conditions is shown in Fig. 7A, B.

Cell viability assay (XTT)

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Proliferation Kit II (XTT; Roche Molecular Biochemicals, 11465015001), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After treatments, cells were incubated with the XTT reagent, and absorbance was measured at 492 nm and 690 nm using an Epoch microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, Vermont, USA). For each independent experiment, the negative control group was defined as 100%, and all treatment groups were expressed relative to the respective control values. Each condition was analyzed in technical triplicate wells within each experiment, and technical replicates were averaged. Mean values from three independent biological replicates were used for statistical analysis.

DNA damage analysis (comet assay)

DNA damage (DNA strand breaks) was evaluated using the alkaline comet assay as described by Singh et al.46, with minor modifications. After treatments, cells were detached with trypsin, washed with PBS, and mixed with 0.5% low-melting-point agarose (Invitrogen) at 37 °C. Cell suspensions were layered onto slides pre-coated with 1.5% normal-melting-point agarose (Sigma-Aldrich) and kept at 4 °C for 20 min to allow solidification.

Slides were immersed in freshly prepared cold lysis solution (2.5 M NaCl, 100 mM Na2EDTA, 100 mM Tris, 10% DMSO, 1% Triton X-100, pH 10) for 12 h at 4 °C. Two slides were prepared for each treatment in the conventional comet assay, and two additional slides were prepared for the enzyme-modified comet assay.

For detection of oxidative DNA damage, after lysis, slides were washed twice with enzyme buffer (40 mM HEPES, 0.1 M KCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.2 mg/mL BSA) and incubated with 0.1 U of hOGG1 enzyme (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C.

All slides were then immersed in alkaline electrophoresis buffer (0.3 M NaOH, 1 mM Na2EDTA, pH > 13) for 20 min at 4 °C and subjected to electrophoresis (25 V, 300 mA, 20 min). After neutralization in 0.4 M Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), slides were air-dried, fixed in absolute ethanol, stained with SYBR Green (Invitrogen), and analyzed using an epifluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) equipped with an Axiocam camera (Carl Zeiss), an excitation filter of 516–560 nm, and a 590 nm barrier filter (20x objective). All steps were performed under dim light.

In each independent experiment, 100 nucleoids per group were scored. DNA damage was quantified as the percentage of DNA in the tail (tail intensity) using CometAssay IV software (Perceptive Instruments Ltd., Suffolk, UK). The mean value from each experiment was used for statistical analysis. All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates.

Mitochondrial superoxide production analysis

After treatments, cells were resuspended in PBS containing 5 mM glucose and incubated with MitoSOX™ Red (1.5 µM; Molecular Probes, Thermo Fisher) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Fluorescence intensity was analyzed using a Guava EasyCyte Mini System flow cytometer (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) with Guava CytoSoft 4.2.1 software (Merck Millipore). A total of 5,000 events were acquired per sample. Mean values from three independent biological replicates were used for statistical analysis.

Cell cycle analysis

After treatments, cells were detached with trypsin, washed with PBS, and fixed in ice-cold 70% ethanol. Samples were kept at − 20 °C until analysis. For cell cycle assessment, cells were resuspended in 200 µL of propidium iodide solution (5 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), incubated for 30 min at room temperature, and analyzed using a Guava EasyCyte Mini System flow cytometer (Merck Millipore) with Guava CytoSoft 4.2.1 software (Merck Millipore). A total of 5,000 events were acquired per sample, and results were expressed as the percentage of cells in each cell cycle phase. Mean values from three independent biological replicates were used for statistical analysis.

Cell death analysis

Cell death was evaluated using the FITC Annexin V/Dead Cell Apoptosis Kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA, V13242), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after treatments, the culture medium containing detached cells was collected, and adherent cells were detached with trypsin and combined with the previously collected fraction. Cell suspensions were washed with ice-cold PBS and resuspended in 100 µL of 1X Annexin-Binding Buffer. Subsequently, 5 µL of FITC Annexin V and 1 µL of propidium iodide (100 µg/mL) were added to each sample, followed by incubation for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Then, 400 µL of 1X Annexin-Binding Buffer was added to each sample, and samples were kept on ice until analysis. Flow cytometry was performed using a Guava EasyCyte Mini System (Merck Millipore), and 10,000 events were acquired per sample. Data were analyzed using Guava CytoSoft 4.2.1 software (Merck Millipore). Mean values from three independent biological replicates were used for statistical analysis.

RNA extraction and qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was determined with a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher), and purity was assessed by A260/230 and A260/280 absorbance ratios. Only samples with ratios within the ranges of 1.8–2.0 and 1.8–2.2, respectively, were used for gene expression analyses. One microgram of RNA was treated with DNase I (Invitrogen) and reverse transcribed using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Vilnius, Lithuania).

Real-time PCR was performed using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Invitrogen), 30 ng cDNA, and 750 nM gene-specific primers (IDT Technologies, Coralville, Iowa, USA): OGG1 (NM_016819.4), forward 5’-AGAAATTCCCCAAGCACCTT-3’ and reverse 5’-CCTGGCCTTTGAGGTAGTCA-3’; SOD1 (NM_000454.5), forward 5’-GGGCCTCAGACTACATCCAA-3’ and reverse 5’-GGCAAAGGTGGAAATGAAGA-3’; B2M (NM_004048.4), forward 5’-AGGCTATCCAGCGTACTCCA-3’ and reverse 5’-TCAATGTCGGATGGATGAAA-3’.

Reactions were performed in technical triplicate for each sample in 96-well plates sealed with MicroAmp® Optical Adhesive Film using a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) under the following cycling conditions: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 2 min, and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 1 s and 60 °C for 30 s.

Technical triplicates were averaged for each biological replicate prior to the calculation of relative expression. Relative gene expression was determined using the 2−∆∆Ct method47, normalized to B2M expression. All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

All data obtained from three independent biological replicates are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For quantitative assays involving technical replicates or multiple measurements within each experiment, mean values were first calculated for each group within each biological replicate and subsequently used for statistical analysis. All statistical analyses were performed based on biological replicates (independent experiments) using GraphPad Prism v.9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. Since all datasets showed normal distribution, comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way or two-way ANOVA tests, as appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
