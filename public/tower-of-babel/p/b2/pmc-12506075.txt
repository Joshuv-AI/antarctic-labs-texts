Identifying the effective strategies in reducing the effects of mental health issues caused by COVID-19 pandemic in healthcare providers: a systematic review
Authors: Naser Derakhshani, Emir Tahmazi Aghdam, Hamideh Nafar, Mohamad Esmaeel Tavakoli, Lubov Gladkova, Seyedeh Narges Pouyan, Nasrin Joudyian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic was among the most stressful global events that caused a significant workload on healthcare systems and profoundly impacted the mental health of healthcare workers. Therefore, this study aims to determine the effectiveness of strategies to reduce the effects of mental health issues caused by the COVID-19 pandemic on healthcare providers.MethodsThe current research is a systematic review. The required data was obtained from the databases ProQuest, Embase, Web of Knowledge, Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar search engine using related keywords. The study had no time limit. EndNote 20 was used to manage the articles. Various quality appraisal JBI tools were used to assess the quality of studies. Content analysis was used to analyze the obtained data.FindingsOut of the total 7933 primary articles, 20 were selected and entered the current study. The implemented interventions were analyzed based on the utilized strategies and categorized into four primary groups: comprehensive support package strategy, psychological training, psychological support with online consultations, and strategies related to sports and music therapy. These interventions were generally effective in promoting the mental health of healthcare providers in the short term.ConclusionThe results indicated that appropriate interventions for critical conditions and the utilization of modern technologies positively affected healthcare workers’ mental health during the COVID-19 pandemic. To improve the sustainability and effectiveness of such interventions, it is recommended that healthcare systems institutionalize psychosocial support within occupational health programs, develop organizational policies for continuous mental health support, and enhance access to digital platforms. Furthermore, incorporating mental health education, conducting regular psychological assessments, and adapting interventions to local socio-cultural contexts can foster greater acceptance and lead to more sustainable outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13513-y.Keywords: Mental health, COVID-19, Pandemics, Healthcare workers, Systematic review

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic was among the most stressful recent global events [1]. This epidemic disease severely affected healthcare systems and, most importantly of all, imposed a severe challenge on the mental health of the general population and healthcare specialists who provide patient care [1–4]. Healthcare Workers (HCWs) are the most exposed group who care for patients during these crises. When exposed to infection, they are susceptible in terms of psychological distress and other symptoms of mental health, such as high-stress levels, depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress, which can be observed later, after the end of the critical conditions [1, 5].

Furthermore, during critical conditions and epidemics, many HCWs face unprecedented responsibilities in a field of care that they are unfamiliar with, such as high-risk units with high workloads probably associated with high levels of psychological distress [6]. HCWs were exposed to various dangers during the COVID-19 pandemic and faced anxiety, depression, distress, and sleep disorders. Most of the distress was caused by insufficient rest and sleep due to the high workload, inadequate equipment and personal protective necessities, and lack of sufficient training for the HCWs, intensifying the psychological burden [7].

Early research into how the COVID-19 pandemic affected healthcare specialists was carried out in China; however, after the spread of the epidemic, other countries also started publishing cross-sectional studies to analyze the mental reactions of HCWs to the pandemic [8]. Most studies reported anxiety (from 30% to 70%) and symptoms of depression (20% to 40%) as the most frequent disorders. Insomnia, burnout, emotional exhaustion, and physical symptoms were among the other reported conditions [9–15]. There is insufficient data on how to prevent psychological disorders in healthcare workers during the pandemic, and for mental health interventions to support the healthcare providers during the medical pandemic, as in previous pandemics, the emergencies were usually local and limited to a particular area. However, during the COVID-19 pandemic, we faced an unknown virus that could be transmitted everywhere by every individual, which intensified its negative mental impact on HCWs [2, 16].

During the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS) and Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) outbreaks, healthcare workers experienced severe emotional problems and psychological symptoms during and after the event, as the subsequent studies revealed that emotional distress caused by such pandemics were primarily long-term and caused individuals to feel lonely and helpless, or a set of adverse emotions such as stress, irritability, physical and mental fatigue, and hopelessness [5, 8]. Studies reveal that excessive workload can make HCWs increasingly more susceptible to mental suffering and increase the risk of psychological disorders [1, 5, 7, 17]. In addition, the alarming incidence of mental disorders associated with burnout lowers the quality of life, increases the frequency of chronic diseases, and raises the risk of suicide [1].

The COVID-19 pandemic has placed significant psychological pressure on healthcare workers (HCWs), increasing their vulnerability to anxiety, depression, insomnia, and burnout [9–14]. Despite their critical role in maintaining health system operations during public health emergencies, the current literature remains fragmented and lacks consensus on the most effective strategies to support their mental well-being [7]. Many existing studies have limited their scope to specific interventions or focused narrowly on certain professional groups. The widespread prevalence of mental health disorders among HCWs not only undermines individual well-being and job satisfaction but also negatively affects clinical performance, staff retention, and patient safety. These urgent concerns highlight the need for comprehensive, evidence-based approaches tailored to address the psychological needs of this vital workforce.

Although numerous studies have explored the psychological effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on HCWs, most reviews have either concentrated on the prevalence of mental health conditions or assessed interventions in the general population or select subgroups (e.g., ICU staff or nurses). A significant gap remains in the form of a systematic review that consolidates and categorizes effective mental health interventions specifically designed for frontline healthcare workers across diverse health systems and cultural contexts. This review addresses that gap by synthesizing evidence from a broad range of global sources. It not only identifies successful strategies but also exposes contextual limitations, implementation challenges, and areas in need of further investigation. It focuses exclusively on evidence-based interventions targeting HCWs—who faced disproportionate levels of psychological stress during the pandemic. Additionally, the review organizes these strategies into key thematic categories, including psychological counseling, peer support, organizational initiatives, policy-level actions, and digital mental health tools. As such, this work contributes to the literature by providing a structured, practical evidence base to guide future policy-making, resource distribution, and mental health preparedness for healthcare systems.

Accordingly, this study aims to systematically review the interventions implemented in this field to identify effective strategies for reducing the negative impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on healthcare workers’ mental health.

Method

The present study was a systematic review designed and conducted in 2024. Its systematic review approach was adopted from the book ‘Systematic Reviews in Support of Evidence-Based Medicine’ [18] and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [19].

Search strategy

The required information was gathered from ProQuest, Embase, Web of Knowledge, Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar Search Engine without any time restrictions and in English. We have manually reviewed the final articles included in this study. The World Health Organization’s website was exclusively investigated (Appendix 1, search strategy sample).

Study selection/screening

Article selection and screening processes were performed independently by two research team members. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion; if needed, the article review was referred to the third individual who is more experience and knowledgeable. Our team reviewed and selected only the articles that fit the research objectives. In later stages, we investigated the abstracts and full texts of the articles, respectively.

Studies that matched the exclusion criteria or had weak associations with study objectives were identified and excluded. Endnote X20, reference management software, was used to organize and read the titles and abstracts and identify duplicate articles. The PRISMA flowchart was used to report the results of the screening and selection process [20].

Quality assessment

Two individuals independently evaluated the bias in research using the JBI-related checklists [21–24]. JBI’s critical appraisal tools were designed to assess the methodological quality of a study and determine the risk of bias in the study’s design, implementation, and analysis processes. The tools include options ‘yes, no, unclear, and not applicable.’ The final evaluation score of each article was determined by the two evaluators’ agreement (number of ‘yes’ options). The discrepancy cases were referred to the third evaluator. Four studies were overviews or surveys; one used a mixed method, which were excluded.

In this study, the research team used a threshold-based scoring method for quality assessment. Each question was scored based on a “yes” response. A clear threshold was set to decide which studies to include or exclude. Studies with scores above 7 were included directly. Those with scores between 5 and 7 were reviewed by a third person to make a final decision. Studies with scores below 5 were excluded.

Data extraction

To extract data based on the study objectives, data from five publications were first extracted experimentally, and the primary form’s difficulties and weaknesses were addressed. Two research team members independently extracted the information from the articles. The extracted data included the author’s name, publication year, country of publication, study objective, country of origin, suggested strategy/intervention type, target group, challenges and limitations, facilitators, and study results. The data extraction form was designed using Microsoft Word 2016 software.

Data analysis

The extracted data in this study were manually analyzed using content analysis, then summarized and reported. Content analysis is a method for identifying, analyzing, and reporting patterns (themes) in text and frequently in analyzing qualitative data [25–28]. Two researchers independently coded the data.

Findings

Databases and other sources yielded 7933 articles; 220 of these were eliminated as duplicates. Following an examination of the titles and abstracts, 7241 studies were eliminated. The analysis eventually contained 20 papers [29–48] after another 452 were eliminated after the full texts were reviewed (Fig.1 and Appendix 2).

Examination of the study types demonstrated that six studies (approximately 30%) were interventional, five (25%) were Randomized Clinical Trials (RCT), three (15%) were qualitative studies, and cohort, descriptive, cross-sectional, ecological, survey, and overview studies each had one study among the final articles. Information obtained from the articles was analyzed and reported based on the countries studied, location/study background, type of interventions, target group, results, strategies utilized, challenges, and suggestions.

Implemented interventions for preventing disorders caused by COVID-19 in HCWs

Mental health

Most studies tried to implement interventions at all levels of target groups in the healthcare system; however, considering the special conditions of the pandemic and its short duration, the interventions were executed on the front-line HCWs (those who directly provided healthcare services to COVID-19 patients). In all of the interventions, the aim was to reduce the susceptibility and promote the mental health of HCWs. The majority of research found that interventions had beneficial effects for brief periods of time and were well-received by target groups.

HCWs’ resilience was among the most critical aspects of every intervention. Considering the physical limitations imposed during the epidemics, every intervention utilized technological capacities and applications to establish connections and transfer content, which may have been a turning point in the implementation of interventions throughout the world.

Based on the utilized strategies, interventions were classified into four primary groups and analyzed

A. Comprehensive support package strategy

This type of intervention presents a care package that considers the HCWs’ stressful environment and high workload. In most programs, an entire support team, including a social worker, a psychologist, and a senior colleague, plays an axial role. Such interventions provided facilities such as communication with peer doctors in WhatsApp, optional one-on-one meeting with the appointed supervisor, determining unknown support and welfare personnel on a 24-hour basis, holding regular meetings for the team, support from peer psychologists, support from clergies and influential religious figures, developing protocols of action, regular availability of latest information, establishment of remote education system, programs for early detection of infection for specialists, interrupting the education of professionals and scholars, soothing techniques (for example, breathing exercises, diaphragmatic breathing, and progressive muscle relaxation), working with thoughts (for instance, Cognitive-Behavioral Therapies (CBT), postponing concerns, working on sleep to prevent insomnia, including sleep hygiene and planning), positive thinking (achievements and acknowledgement), mindfulness and mediation, sleep relaxation (guided sounds, ambient sounds, soundscapes), physical health (health life habits, exercises, and physical activity programs) [1, 2, 9, 13, 23].

B. Educational strategies

This type of intervention included training on the axis of psychology and mental health techniques to increase resilience, programmed as dedicated modules. The training included short online meetings and short video clips focusing on mindfulness-based intervention, self-compassion, electronic training courses, asynchronous and self-paced learning models (self-assessment of burnout and psychological well-being, meditation, creating a self-care program) [5, 8, 10, 16].

C. Strategy of psychological support with online counseling

Considering the critical conditions and previous experiences, such interventions utilize a hotline to provide consultation services and psychological support. We developed policies and materials for psychological interventions, creating a system to connect certified volunteers – psychologists, psychiatrists, and resilience coaches – to students. These volunteers will provide academic support and foster positive interactions through counseling and psychotherapy. Online consultation is available for individuals susceptible to traumatic experiences or other stressful incidents. Such online consultations are provided based on evidence-based cognitive behavioral therapies. In such interventions, a weekly program addresses various subjects, including happiness and positive thinking, cognitive restructuring, behavioral activation, emotion regulation, positive workplace atmosphere, and problem-solving in the form of short films, tests, and assignments in a web forum and through the interaction of participants with each other, the interventions tried to transfer social support in the mentioned contents. For instance, a counseling intervention used creative writing of daily emotions as a novel to rank positive and negative emotions after each daily activity, and smartphone intervention to support specialists exposed to risks caused by occupational stresses [6, 11, 12, 14, 18].

D. Supportive strategies based on sports and music

This type of intervention tries to use the effects of sports and music to reduce the tension and stress of the HCWs and provide relaxation to renew energy to continue activities. The used items included ‘internet in addition to music therapy’ (playing recorded information with the sound of nature through WeChat for the medical staff to remember memories and experience peak emotions and relax their body and mind), group yoga exercises, regular defined exercises, acupressure to reduce stress, and using relaxing music for deep sleep [29, 32, 35, 40, 41].

Considering the results obtained from the determined studies, implementation of the interventions faced several challenges, which were categorized into three primary categories: human resources (nine cases), information sources (four cases), and process-related challenges (ten cases). The reviewed studies suggested functional strategies for the identified challenges, and the lowest number of strategies were related to information sources (Table 1).

Results of the bias assessment of articles

The majority of the investigations were quasi-experimental and clinical trials. The clinical trial publications’ mean bias evaluation score was calculated to be 9.8 out of 13. The most critical areas for improvement of this article in this regard included insufficient reports, the method that individuals were assigned to control and intervention groups, unclear information regarding the blinding and blinded groups, and how they dealt with patients who exited the study. The researchers estimated the mean score of the quality of quasi-experimental studies as 6.85 out of 9. Insufficient information regarding how they assigned individuals to control and intervention groups was one of the most critical flaws of the articles (Appendix 3: Results of the assessment of the quality of articles’ reporting).

Discussion

Considering the increased social isolation that was experienced during the COVID-19 pandemic, HCWs were among the target groups of society who were dealing with social isolation and psychological problems. This group had to work long shifts while wearing masks and other protective equipment (removed while trying) to maintain ‘social distance’ from others. In line with promoting the mental health of this group, interventions were necessary to promote the mental health of HCWs while not causing any drop in the quality of the provided services [42].

Due to the outbreak of pandemics, some of the HCWs who were among the front-line providers of healthcare services experienced negative emotions such as different levels of anxiety and depression, which had significant impacts on their personal lives and also the quality of the healthcare services they provided. Therefore, timely recognition of HCWs’ mental condition and taking effective measures for prevention or treatment during critical conditions is essential. Notably, a study conducted by Sanja Filkova and colleagues in 2021 similarly underscored the critical necessity of safeguarding the mental health of healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic. The study reported widespread experiences of burnout, fear, and inadequate institutional support among HCWs, emphasizing the urgent need for systematic, organization-level interventions to ensure their psychological well-being [49]. Also, in developed countries such as the United States, Japan, and Britain, efforts were made to manage the psychological health of their HCWs during the pandemic by conducting positive interventions [41].

Considering the incidence of special critical conditions during the outbreak of pandemics, many of the designed interventions that require principled planning and execution face several challenges. Some researchers designed a three-stage theory of crisis development to remove the obstacles of interventions during the crises to promote the mental health of HCWs, which must be conducted based on the psychological characteristics of the HCWs in different stages, including before the crisis (teaching daily hygiene to increase the mental preparedness of individuals to face the crisis), during the crisis (practical intervention methods, such as individual and group consultation), and post-crisis (learning self-regulation techniques from the crisis) [50, 51].

Johannes H De Kock et al. (2022) similarly emphasized the critical importance of proactively identifying risk factors such as pre-existing mental health vulnerabilities among HCWs and workplace-related stressors before the onset of public health crises. Their findings underscore the value of anticipatory strategies aimed at strengthening psychological resilience and minimizing the mental health burden during future emergencies [52]. Also, studies by Kate Robins-Browne et al. (2022) and Ashley Elizabeth Muller et al. (2020) [7, 53] underscored the importance of implementing interventions during a crisis. These investigations emphasized the dual necessity of providing individualized psychosocial support and strengthening communication and support systems within healthcare environments. Such approaches were found to play a critical role in enhancing psychological resilience and alleviating emotional strain associated with excessive workload and acute stress [7, 53]. Importantly, this dual-focus framework extends beyond reactive, short-term responses, contributing instead to the development of robust and sustainable infrastructures capable of supporting HCWs in future crises. In addition to short-term interventions implemented before and during crises, sustained psychological support and post-crisis mental health screening are considered essential for addressing long-term outcomes such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression [54]. Moreover, the systematic evaluation of interventions applied during the crisis can inform future strategies and enhance resilience among healthcare personnel [53]. While structured frameworks—such as the three-phase crisis intervention model—offer valuable guidance, variability in HCWs’ needs and preferences may limit the effectiveness of uniform approaches [7]. Therefore, the development of adaptive, context-sensitive interventions is critical to ensuring their long-term impact and sustainability. Accordingly, supportive strategies such as providing psychological and social consultations, holding stress-management workshops, and promoting mental resilience for these individuals appear necessary.

Furthermore, results obtained from studies indicated that social-psychological interventions, such as group therapy and training anxiety coping techniques, can help reduce the mental impacts. Localization of interventions and attention to the cultural requirements of the HCWs are necessary to increase the efficiency of the programs and must be extensively implemented in the work environments [1, 2].‌ High levels of managerial commitment and support also guarantee the effective implementation of interventions that align with encouraging and supporting HCWs [33]. A study conducted by Ricardo F Muñoz and Lisa A Coope (2022) demonstrated that Social-psychological interventions, when specifically tailored to the needs of distinct groups or populations, can be highly effective in preventing the onset of mental health disorders during crises [55]. These results underscore the importance of broad, strategic implementation of such population-sensitive approaches within the healthcare workforce, particularly during large-scale emergencies such as the COVID-19 pandemic. By addressing the unique vulnerabilities of at-risk groups, these interventions have the potential to significantly reduce mental health inequities and improve psychological outcomes across diverse and underserved healthcare populations [56].

HCWs usually face unfamiliar and uncommon conditions during crises. The stress of the new situation and high workload, on the other hand, limits their communication, leading them to become overwhelmed by work, forget their needs, and sometimes cultural issues related to the social stigma of mental health problems disrupt the environment for expressing their needs [30]. Hence, healthcare system managers must consider every aspect with a systematic vision and provide an environment for establishing a solid support system that creates peace of mind and promotes HCWs’ mental health.

Results of reviewing the studies indicated that despite the limitations of physical communication during the pandemic, which is among the most critical factors for the occurrence of mental health problems among HCWs, it is possible to use practical platforms of cyberspace to hold safe individuals and group meetings with a support team so that HCWs can receive the required support and information through social workers, consultants, and their senior peers [45, 57]. Support meetings can provide a solid ground to relieve stress and feel refreshed and energetic, away from stressful work conditions [37, 58]. Accordingly, HCWs can train skills and techniques of grief management to reduce their stress and enhance their resilience, which can bring them peace of mind and promote their mental health by creating an atmosphere that helps healthcare providers feel that they receive attention, their needs are identified, and they are in a condition that no one expects to receive care from them. They can train far away from stress [58]. Utilization of various techniques, such as meditation, self-care programs, psychotherapy, life coaching, management skills, mindfulness, art, and music therapy, can be significantly beneficial [30, 37, 59–62].

Studies investigated in the present systematic review indicated positive effects within a short time; however, considering the low number of samples and short follow-up, additional studies on larger scales are required to confidently generalize the results and use them in future planning to enhance pandemic preparedness. It also appears that the continuous use of a suitable psychometric scale to measure anxiety and depression levels by managers may be an effective option for pre-crisis preparations and designing appropriate interventions to plan for the mental health of their workers. It’s suggested that training on epidemic preparedness, specifically focusing on flexibility and resilience, be integrated into educational programs.

Conclusion

Based on the results obtained from this systematic review, effective interventions on the mental health of HCWs during the COVID-19 pandemic indicated that supportive psychological measures and increasing the resilience of healthcare professionals played a vital role in reducing the disorders caused by this crisis. Using comprehensive supporting packages, online consultations, and music- and sports-based techniques are appropriate and vital strategies that can be effective in case of epidemic outbreaks such as COVID-19. Also, using the latest modern technologies, such a strategy can positively affect the psychological health of healthcare workers in short periods. Nevertheless, considering the various challenges, including the limited resources and time, further studies on larger scales with extended follow-up periods are necessary to generalize desired results. Using suitable interventions for critical conditions and digital tools can promote HCWs’ mental health and resilience. Policy-makers and senior managers at different healthcare system levels can promote the mental health of their healthcare professionals in addition to their performance by using suitable strategies for their countries.

Most current studies assess only the short-term effects of mental health interventions. Future research should use long-term designs to evaluate how these strategies impact HCWs over time. In addition, mixed-methods approaches, combining surveys with in-depth interviews, can offer a deeper understanding of both the outcomes and the real-world challenges of implementation. This approach will strengthen the evidence base and help design more effective, culturally appropriate mental health programs.
