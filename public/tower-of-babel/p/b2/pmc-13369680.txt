Effects of Selenium Supplementation on Growth Performance, Antioxidant and Metabolic Status in Fattening Lambs: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Oscar Enrique Del Razo Rodríguez, Juan Manuel Casso Gaspar, Raymundo Lira Casas, Efrén Ramírez Bribiesca, Navid Ghavipanje, David Alejandro Contreras Caro del Castillo, Rodolfo Vieyra Alberto, Juan Carlos Ángeles Hernández
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelenium (Se) is an essential trace element that supports cell membrane integrity and regulates antioxidant activity. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to analyze the overall effects of Se supplementation on productive performance, antioxidant status, metabolic profile, and methane emissions in lambs. Five independent researchers conducted the literature search, three extracted the data, and two cross-checked the database. Effect sizes were estimated using standardized mean difference (SMD) and raw mean difference (RMD), and heterogeneity was quantified with I² statistic. For variables with substantial heterogeneity (I² > 50%), meta-regression models were applied considering breed, Se source (organic, inorganic, yeast, and nano), dose, body weight, and age. Results, from a total of 28 studies included, show that Se improves average daily gain (RMD = + 0.02 kg/d; p = 0.0001) and reduces feed conversion ratio (RMD = -0.88 kg/kg; p = 0.0001). Average daily gain heterogeneity (I² = 56.4%) was mainly attributed to the Se source, with Se nanoparticles being the best source across most outcomes. No significant effects were observed for dry matter intake (RMD = + 0.004 kg/d; p = 0.71) and blood glucose concentration (RMD = + 1.21 mg/dL; p = 0.56). Glutathione peroxidase activity increased with Se supplementation (SMD = + 1.44; p = 0.0001), while methane emissions remained unaffected (SMD = + 0.19; p = 0.53). Overall, dietary Se supplementation enhances growth efficiency and antioxidant status in lambs, however, the magnitude of responses is modulated by Se source, production level, age, and basal diet composition, reflecting the complex interaction between animals and supplementation strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12011-026-05078-6.Keywords: Antioxidants, Glutathione peroxidase, Growth performance, Methane, Sheep

Introduction

Sheep production is a key component of the global livestock sector, supporting food security through the provision of meat, milk and wool [1]. Despite its modest share of total livestock output, demand for sheep-derived products has steadily increased over the past five decades, reflecting sustained consumer preference and market resilience, particularly for smallholders and resource-limited producers in marginal environments [2]. However, the environmental footprint of intensified livestock production has become a growing concern [3], as the sector accounts for 14.5% of global anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, approximately 7.1 Gt CO₂-equivalent, annually. Small ruminants, including sheep, account for 0.474 Gt CO₂e (6% of livestock emissions) [4, 5], primarily through enteric methane (CH₄) production, which also represents an energy loss of up to 12% of dietary intake [3, 6]. Consequently, optimizing nutritional strategies to enhance productivity while potentially reducing enteric CH₄ emissions has become a priority for sustainable sheep production.

Selenium (Se), an essential trace element, stands out as a key nutritional factor with multifaceted physiological roles in ruminants. As a critical component of selenoproteins, Se supports cellular redox homeostasis, enhances immune function, and regulates thyroid hormone metabolism [7]. Specifically, Se serves as a cofactor for glutathione peroxidase (GPx), a key antioxidant enzyme that protects cell membranes from oxidative damage and influences the conversion of thyroxine (T4) to the biologically active triiodothyronine (T3), thereby modulating basal metabolic rate and energy utilization [8, 9]. Consequently, Se deficiency in sheep has been linked to impaired growth, reduced reproductive efficiency, increased susceptibility to disease, and elevated neonatal mortality [10–13]. Conversely, supra-nutritional Se supplementation has been shown to improve reproductive [14] and productive performance [15], boost immune response and antioxidant function [13, 16], and enhance general health status [12]. Se benefits are especially relevant under environmental stress conditions, such as heat stress induced by climate change, which amplifies oxidative burden and metabolic inefficiency in sheep [3]. By reinforcing endogenous antioxidant systems, Se may help mitigate the physiological disruptions caused by climatic stressors, thereby supporting production resilience in a warming world.

Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that Se supplementation has been hypothesized to influence ruminal microbiome and fermentation by improving nutrient digestibility, increasing volatile fatty acid (VFA) production, and reducing the acetate-to-propionate ratio, which could be associated with changes in enteric CH₄ emissions [17, 18]. However, current evidence in lambs remains limited and inconsistent, and Se cannot be considered an established CH₄ mitigation strategy based on the available literature.

Despite a substantial body of evidence, findings on the effects of Se supplementation in sheep remain inconsistent across studies. For instance, Kojouri et al. [19] reported an improved weight gain in lambs supplemented with Se nanoparticles at a dose of 0.1 mg/kg. In contrast, Bai et al. [16] observed no significant changes in production or slaughter performance in fattening lambs receiving 0.8 mg/kg of Se-enriched yeast, selenized glucose, or sodium selenite. Similarly, Mariezcurrena-Berasain et al. [20] found that dietary supplementation with organic Se (0.35 ppm as Se yeast) did not affect growth performance but reduced feed intake in finishing lambs. These discrepancies likely reflect variations in experimental design, animal characteristics, Se source (organic vs. inorganic), and dosage levels. Moreover, although multiple studies report antioxidant and metabolic markers (including Se concentration, GPx, and thyroid hormones), evidence has not been consistently synthesized across outcomes, and data on CH₄ emissions are comparatively scarce.

Such heterogeneity across studies underlines the need for a quantitative synthesis to clarify the magnitude and direction of Se effects across diverse production and environmental contexts. Therefore, the present study aimed to perform a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis to quantify the overall impacts of dietary Se supplementation on growth performance, antioxidant status, metabolic indicators, and CH4 emissions in lambs. It was hypothesized that Se supplementation would enhance productivity and oxidative balance and would be associated with favorable changes in metabolic indicators; additionally, we tested the hypothesis that Se supplementation may affect CH₄ emissions, acknowledging that evidence for this outcome may be limited. We further hypothesized that effect sizes could be moderated by factors such as Se source and dosage, and animal characteristics.

Materials and Methods

PICO Question

To assess the effect of Se supplementation on production performance, antioxidant activity, metabolic status, and CH₄ emissions in fattening lambs, we formulated an answerable question using the PICO framework [Participant (P), Intervention (I), Comparator (C), Outcomes (O)]. The Participant (P) comprised fattening lambs of all breeds and ages, the Intervention (I) was Se supplementation administered by injection or orally, and the Comparator (C) was an unsupplemented control group. The Outcomes (O) included productive performance (average daily gain, ADG; dry matter intake, DMI; and feed conversion ratio, FCR), antioxidant activity (Se concentration and GPx), metabolic status (glucose, T4, T3, cholesterol, albumin, globulin, urea, and total protein), and CH₄ emissions. Accordingly, the PICO question was: What is the effectiveness of Se supplementation on productivity, antioxidant and metabolic status, and CH₄ emissions in lambs compared with unsupplemented controls?

Search Strategy

A compressive and structured search strategy was developed using the following Boolean search string: (lamb OR ewe OR sheep) AND (selenium OR Se OR selenite OR selenate OR enrich-yeast Se OR nano-Se) AND (growing OR fattening OR weight gain) AND (volatile fatty acids OR ruminal fermentation) AND (methane OR greenhouse gases). The search was conducted by five researchers to minimize bias across the following databases: Google Scholar (http://scholar.google.com), Web of Science (https://www.webofscience.com), PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and Scopus (https://www.scopus.com/). Only peer-reviewed articles published in the English or Portuguese languages were considered in the current study. Full database-specific search strings are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The search strategy was refined iteratively; intermediate versions were not fully archived, and we therefore complemented electronic searches with backward and forward citation tracking to minimize the risk of missing eligible studies.

Eligibility Criteria

The study selection process followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (Fig. 1). The review protocol has been formally registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) platform and is publicly accessible at https://osf.io/ztgf9/overview. Three authors independently screened the search output to identify manuscripts that met the following inclusion criteria: studies published in peer-reviewed scientific journals (unpublished data, conference proceedings, and dissertations were excluded); in vivo randomized trials comparing Se-supplemented lambs with unsupplemented controls; absence of concomitant interventions (e.g., supplementation with additional minerals, vitamins, or additives); clear description of random assignment procedures of animals (i.e., experimental design); reporting of effect estimates (e.g., least square means, coefficient of variation, standard deviation, or standard error) for both control and supplemented groups; and reported sample size for each experimental group.

Database

A total of 28 studies comprising 72 experimental reports were selected for inclusion in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1). Data from eligible studies were extracted independently by three researchers into a standard Excel spreadsheet (version 2016, Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA). Two reviewers cross-checked database, and any inconsistencies were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. The final database included the following response variables: ADG (kg/d), DMI (kg/d), FCR (kg/kg), and CH₄ emissions, as well as blood (serum/plasma) concentrations of glucose (mg/dL), total cholesterol (mg/dL), albumin (mg/dL), globulin (mg/dL), blood urea (mg/dL), total protein (mg/dL), thyroid hormones T3 (ng/mL) and T4 (µg/dL), and Se (µg/dL), together with GPx activity (U/L). Also, the following explanatory variables were recorded to capture between-study heterogeneity: breed, Se source, dosage level, lamb body weight and age (months), and production level (ADG categories: low, < 0.14 kg/d; medium, 0.14–0.20 kg/d; and high, > 0.2 kg/d). In the current study, Se source was classified as: (i) inorganic Se (e.g., sodium selenite, sodium selenate); (ii) Se-yeast (selenium-enriched yeast products reported as Se-yeast/selenium-enriched yeast); (iii) organic Se (non-yeast) (purified or non-yeast organically bound Se sources, e.g., selenomethionine, as reported in the original studies); and (iv) nano-Se (selenium nanoparticles/elemental Se in nano-form as described by the study authors). Se-yeast was analyzed separately from other organic sources because yeast-based products contain mixed Se species and variable release characteristics, which may affect comparability across studies.

Meta-Analytical Procedure

The meta-analysis was conducted using the Meta package version 6.1-0 [21] in R (version 4.0.2 [22], Vienna, Austria). Effect sizes were measured using both the raw mean difference (RMD) and standardized mean difference (SMD). The SMD was calculated using Hedges’ g correction [23] and pooled estimates were obtained using the inverse-variance weighting method. A random-effects model was implemented, assuming that, the observed variation among studies resulted from both sampling error (chance) and true heterogeneity in the intervention effects. Each study was treated as an independent comparison between an experimental and control group. RMD was applied to outcomes measured on the same scales, allowing direct interpretation of pooled effect in their original measured units. When studies reported multiple eligible Se-supplemented arms sharing a single control group (e.g., different Se sources or doses), we handled multi-arm designs using the pairwise() function in the R package meta, splitting the shared control group across comparisons as recommended to avoid unit-of-analysis errors and overweighting of such studies.

All extracted outcomes were verified against the original articles and unit labels were corrected where necessary; when the same biomarker was reported in different units, values were harmonized to a common unit prior to analysis (e.g., Se to µg/dL and T4 to µg/dL), whereas RMD was not estimated for GPx and CH₄ due to non-comparable measurement scales (reported as NE), and unit harmonization did not change the overall interpretation of results.

Assessment of Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity was assessed through the estimation of between-study random-effects variance (t2) and the percentage of variability explained by heterogeneity rather than simple variance (I2 index) [24]. In the current study, the potential sources of heterogeneity were explored for outcomes showing I2 > 50% of and statistically significant (p < 0.05) under the random-effects model. For these outcomes, the random-effects model was expanded to a mixed-effects model (meta-regression) that defines the role of explanatory moderators as sources of heterogeneity. Mixed-effects regression models were constructed in the metafor package version 3.1-1 [25] following a stepwise procedure. Models were compared and selected based on Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Regarding the metabolic variables analyzed, blood glucose was not affected by Se supplementation (p > 0.05), averaging 68.38 mg/dL in controls. The RMD for GPx could not be estimated due to inconsistent units across studies, preventing data standardization. Therefore, only SMD was reported. Se supplementation significantly increased in GPx activity in lambs (SMD = + 1.44; 95% CI: +1.06, + 1.82; p = 0.001). Se concentration was higher in supplemented lambs (RMD = + 5.13 µg/dL; 95% CI: +1.83, + 8.43; p = 0.01), with the largest SMD (+ 4.92; 95% CI: +3.44, + 6.41; p = 0.0001). ADG showed moderate heterogeneity (I² = 56.4%), which was fully explained (I² = 0 and R² = 100) by production level, Se source, and lamb age (Table 2). Subgroup analysis (Fig. 2) illustrates that the effect of Se supplementation was greater in animals with lower production levels (SMD = 1.56). Regarding Se source, the highest response was observed with Se nanoparticles (SMD = 3.24), followed by 99% pure inorganic Se (SMD = 1.72) and organic Se (SMD = 1.29). FCR exhibited high heterogeneity (I² = 78.9%), which decreased to 7.77% by including production level, Se source and lamb live weight (LW) as covariates in the meta-regression (Table 2). Discussion

The present results demonstrate that, compared with non-supplemented animals, Se supplementation exerts a positive effect on lamb performance, primarily by increasing ADG without altering DMI, thereby improving FCR. This outcome may be explained by the higher serum Se concentration observed in supplemented lambs, together with concurrent improvements in metabolic status and redox balance, reflected by increased T3, cholesterol, and globulin concentrations. Antioxidant capacity was also enhanced, as indicated by higher GPx activity. Taken together, these responses support the view that Se influences growth through coordinated endocrine–metabolic and antioxidant pathways, in line with evidence reported in other species, including broilers [27], goats [28], and cattle [29], and consistent with Se acting as a metabolic modulator rather than merely a micronutrient additive.

Selenium is an essential trace element incorporated into more than 30 biologically active selenoproteins, many of which participate in antioxidant defense and endocrine regulation [30]. Across animal species, three complementary mechanisms are commonly proposed to link Se supplementation with improved productive performance: (1) regulation of thyroid hormone metabolism, which is critical for growth and development [31–33]; (2) support of muscle protein accretion and water-holding capacity, partly mediated by selenoprotein W during muscle differentiation and development [27, 34]; and (3) provision of the functional component of GPx, a key enzyme that limits oxidative cellular damage [32, 35]. Collectively, these processes likely create a more favorable physiological environment for growth and efficiency, which is consistent with recent syntheses and experimental reports highlighting Se’s multifaceted role in ruminant homeostasis and production efficiency [17, 36]. These physiological mechanisms are discussed in greater detail later in this Discussion.

One potential contributor to the enhanced productive performance observed in Se-supplemented lambs is the crucial role of Se in thyroid hormone metabolism [33]. The thyroid gland contains one of the highest Se concentrations per gram of tissue in mammals, and thyroid hormones are crucial for maintaining normal metabolic activity. The importance of selenoproteins, particularly iodothyronine deiodinases (type I, II and III), for thyroid function has been well-documented [35, 37]. These enzymes catalyze the conversion of T4 into the active hormone T3, thus regulating thyroid hormone metabolism [35]. In small ruminants, including lambs, Se supplementation has been shown to elevate thyroid hormone levels and selenoenzyme activities, directly linked to improved growth regulation [38]. This meta-analysis confirms a significant increase in T3 concentrations in lambs Se-supplemented lambs. It has been well established that the elevated T₃ accelerates basal metabolic rate and promotes anabolic processes (e.g., protein synthesis), which plausibly contributes to higher ADG without increased feed intake [30]. Conversely, Se deficiency reduces deiodinase activity, leading to decreased plasma T3 and increased T4 levels, which impair growth [31, 39]. Also, Se deficiency is associated with decreased type II iodothyronine deiodinase activity in the pituitary gland, ultimately reduce T3 production and thus growth hormone synthesis [39]. In lambs, which undergo rapid growth, Se supplementation likely enhances T3 production by supporting deiodinase activity, thereby boosting the metabolic processes necessary for growth [35].

The significant increase in GPx activity observed herein reflects improved cellular antioxidant capacity, which may partially contribute to the improved performance. Se’s incorporation into selenocysteine-containing isoforms, whose expression is directly proportional to dietary Se intake [38]. As the first selenoprotein with a defined function [40], GPx activity is positively correlated with Se intake [41]. Most GPx isoforms (GPx1, GPx2, GPx3, and GPx4) contain selenocysteine at their catalytic site, making their activity closely dependent on dietary Se availability [42]. GPx protects cells from oxidative damage by reducing hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxide, particularly in mitochondria, blood cells, and intestinal cells [7]. During the growth phase, when lambs experiment elevated metabolic stress, especially on high-grain diets, this antioxidant function fosters a stable cellular environment. Consequently, this protection enables nutrients to be primarily directed towards anabolic processes, such as muscle protein synthesis, rather than towards responses to oxidative stress [43]. This redox-to-growth linkage is supported by literature showing direct correlations between dietary Se, GPx activity, mitochondrial efficiency, and production outcomes in ruminants [36, 38].

Muscle development, accounting for 57.0–59.5% of a growing lamb’s carcass weight, plays a significant role in weight gain [44, 45]. Therefore, clarifying the possible effects of Se supplementation on muscular development is important to explain the improved growth performance in supplemented lambs. Selenoprotein W (SelW), a small cytosolic selenoprotein (approximately 10 kDa) containing selenocysteine (Sec, U), is prevalent in muscle tissue and plays a pivotal role in this process [34, 46]. SelW transcriptional regulation is closely linked to key myogenic factors (Jeon, 2014). In fact, SelW was initially identified as a deficient protein in lambs with white muscle disease [46]. During the postnatal hypertrophic phase of skeletal muscle growth, several myogenic regulatory factors are required, including MyoD (myoblast determination protein 1) [47]. MyoD plays a critical role in organizing the muscle stem cell genome and promoting gene expression specifically in muscle tissue. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays have confirmed that MyoD activates SelW transcription by binding to a specific E-box during the early stages of skeletal muscle differentiation, demonstrating that MyoD is essential for the transcriptional activation of SelW via E-box elements. It reinforces a plausible mechanistic link between key myogenic regulators and the presence of selenoproteins involved in muscle development [46]. Accordingly, and consistent with our meta-analysis showing higher ADG in Se-supplemented lambs, these biological pathways may help contextualize the observed performance response without implying direct evidence of causality from the present dataset. However, available evidence suggests that this effect is mediated primarily by post-transcriptional mechanisms rather than direct transcriptional activation [46]. The physiological relevance of Se and SelW in muscle health is further supported by the well-established association between Se deficiency and myopathies, such as white muscle disease in ruminants and Keshan disease in humans, affecting both cardiac and skeletal muscle [48]. Although our meta-analysis could not directly assess SelW or muscle endpoints, the higher ADG observed with Se supplementation is compatible with improved muscle function and oxidative status reported in the literature, which may help contextualize the performance response.

Subgroup and meta-regression analyses suggest that the magnitude of Se responses is associated with production level (defined by ADG categories), together with other study- and animal-level characteristics (e.g., Se source, age, and breed). Importantly, because “production level” is derived from an outcome (ADG), these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal evidence of underlying differences in diet quality or baseline Se status. Although detailed diet composition and basal Se concentrations were not consistently reported across the included studies—and were therefore insufficient for a formal moderator analysis—the partial dietary information available indicates that ADG categories tended to co-occur with differences in diet composition. For example, mean forage inclusion was 34.6% in the low-production group compared with 21.7% in the high-production group, with a similar pattern for NDF (39.22% vs. 31.6%). These descriptive patterns provide context and may help generate hypotheses for future trials, but they should not be interpreted as evidence that diet composition or baseline Se status explains the observed subgroup differences.

Consistent with this broader context, diet can influence Se bioavailability: Koenig et al. [49] reported higher intestinal Se absorption in sheep fed concentrate-based diets than forage-based diets (52.8% vs. 41.8% of Se intake). Likewise, rumen microbial communities and Se transformations may differ with dietary substrate [50, 51]. In general, high-forage diets tend to favor fibrolytic taxa (e.g., Butyrivibrio spp. and Prevotella ruminicola), whereas high-concentrate diets more often promote amylolytic taxa such as Selenomonas ruminantium, Bacteroides amylophilus, Streptococcus spp., and Lactobacillus spp. Moreover, Se metabolism can vary among rumen species: S. ruminantium and B. fibrisolvens can incorporate Se into selenoamino acids, whereas P. ruminicola may reduce Se to elemental forms with lower availability [50, 51]. Although rumen microbiota was not measured in our dataset, these mechanisms are discussed as plausible biological context for future work on diet-dependent variation in Se utilization and responses to supplementation.

The responses were also influenced by the source of Se, with variables such as ADG and FCR showing better outcomes with organic Se sources. While both organic Se and selenate can be absorbed at rates of 70–95%, selenite absorption is lower in sheep [52]. For the purposes of this study, organic Se sources were defined as those bound to proteins or amino acids (e.g. selenomethionine). However, some authors classify yeast as an organic Se source. Although most Se in yeast is organic, only around 50% of it is in the form of selenomethionine, much of which is incorporated into protein; also, due to its mixed organic/inorganic composition and reliance on full digestion for Se release, its absorption and bioavailability are limited [53]. This aligns with our findings, in which Se yeast had a lower impact on ADG than other organic sources, justifying its evaluation as a distinct source. Conversely, the largest responses for several outcomes were observed in trials using nano-Se; however, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously because the number of available nano-Se comparisons was limited and study conditions may not be fully comparable across Se sources. From a mechanistic perspective, nano-Se has been proposed to exhibit higher bioavailability due to its particle size and surface properties, potentially enhancing cellular uptake and incorporation into selenoproteins while reducing toxicity relative to some inorganic forms [54–56]. As noted by Malyugina et al. [30], biogenic Se nanoparticles may present lower toxicity and higher bioavailability than inorganic sources, which supports nano-Se as a promising, hypothesis-generating option for future ovine studies rather than a confirmed superior source.

The increase in globulin concentration with Se supplementation reflects enhanced both innate and adaptive immune responses and is likely to reduce the susceptibility to infection [57]. This is supported by studies showing increased immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels in Se-supplemented sheep [58]. Selenium is a crucial micronutrient for the optimal functioning of the adaptive immune system as it promotes lymphocyte proliferation and supports antibody production by B cells, which differentiate into plasma cells that are responsible for synthesis of gamma globulins [59]. One selenoprotein involved in antibody-related processes is selenoprotein K (SelK), located in the endoplasmic reticulum of both B and T cells [60]. Notably, the immunological benefits of Se supplementation are more evident in animals with marginal Se status [61]. In this meta-analysis, the larger increase in globulin observed in the lower-production subgroup is consistent with this concept and suggests that baseline Se status may modulate the magnitude of the response, which is relevant for interpreting heterogeneity and targeting supplementation strategies.

In an experimental design context, the meta-regression patterns suggest that the biological matrix is a key driver of heterogeneity in GPx responses. In practical terms, blood-based samples appear to be a more sensitive and feasible matrix for detecting supplementation effects under field conditions, whereas muscle and hepatic tissues showed smaller pooled effects and involve greater logistical and methodological constraints. Therefore, sample selection should align with each study’s primary objective; the present results can guide future ovine Se trials by prioritizing blood (and, when relevant, erythrocytes) for routine monitoring, while reserving tissue sampling for studies specifically targeting Se deposition or organ-level mechanisms.

Although the present meta-analysis did not detect a significant effect of Se supplementation on enteric CH₄ emissions, several mechanistic pathways have been hypothesized that warrant further investigation. Emerging studies suggest that Se may influence rumen fermentation and the microbiome, for example by supporting fibrolytic and amylolytic bacteria (e.g., Fibrobacter succinogenes, Ruminococcus flavefaciens) and potentially favoring propionate formation over acetate, which could reduce hydrogen availability for methanogenic archaea [17]. Some individual trials have also reported shifts in methanogen abundance (e.g., changes in Methanobrevibacter spp.) and fermentation markers (including VFA profiles and the acetate: propionate ratio), with effects that may differ by Se source (e.g., organic Se vs. Se nanoparticles) and experimental context [17, 28, 30, 62, 63]. However, these proposed mechanisms should be interpreted as hypotheses rather than evidence of a confirmed mitigation strategy, because our pooled results did not show a consistent reduction in CH₄ emissions. Therefore, additional well-designed studies with standardized CH₄ measurement protocols and adequate reporting are needed to clarify whether Se can meaningfully affect enteric CH₄ output in lambs.

Regarding the limitations of the current study, the authors acknowledge that the evidence base becomes substantially smaller when eligibility is restricted to trials in which the effect of selenium can be clearly isolated, because Se supplementation in lambs is frequently evaluated alongside concomitant interventions—most commonly vitamin E—which would confound attribution of effects to Se alone. We consider this restriction necessary to minimize confounding; however, it may limit generalizability and should be recognized. In addition, although multi-arm studies were handled to avoid unit-of-analysis errors, residual heterogeneity remains, and findings for nano-Se and CH₄ should be interpreted with particular caution due to the limited number of available comparisons. Specifically, the CH₄ dataset is small and measurement approaches vary across studies; therefore, additional well-designed trials using standardized and precise enteric methane quantification methods are needed, and nano-Se should be viewed as a promising but non-conclusive source pending further evidence.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis indicates that Se supplementation is associated with improved productive performance in lambs, reflected by a significant increase in ADG and a reduction in FCR, alongside consistent improvements in antioxidant and metabolic indicators. Response magnitude varied across studies and appeared to be associated with Se source, production level, age, and available dietary characteristics, underscoring the complex interaction between the animal, the supplement, and the feeding context. Although nano-Se showed larger pooled responses in several outcomes, these patterns should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited number of comparable trials and study-specific conditions. Finally, the evidence for CH₄ mitigation remains inconclusive because few studies reported CH₄ and measurement approaches were heterogeneous; additional well-designed trials using standardized, precise enteric methane quantification—ideally combined with rumen microbiome/omics profiling—are needed to clarify mechanisms and identify robust Se supplementation strategies for lamb production systems.
