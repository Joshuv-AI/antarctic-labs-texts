Circulating miR-146a-5p and miR-98–5p as Indicators of Inflammatory Response and Joint Pain in Chikungunya Fever
Authors: Delong Xie, Shaohua Ling, Liping Lu, Yujia Huang, Huitian Huang, Yuanyuan Huang, Sangui Yi, Zongling Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Novel circulating biomarkers miR-146a-5p and miR-98–5p identified for CHIKV infection.•miR-98–5p downregulation specifically associated with CHIKV arthralgia.•miRNAs show potential as therapeutic targets for CHIKV-related complications.Keywords: Chikungunya, miR-146a-5p, miR-98–5p, Arthralgia, Inflammation

Introduction

Chikungunya fever (CHIKF) is a mosquito-borne illness triggered by the Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), an arthritis-inducing alphavirus classified under the Togaviridae family(Pedi et al., 2025). Globally, >75% of the population live in countries with documented endemic transmission of CHIKV, while approximately 50% reside in areas where Zika virus circulation has been reported(Puntasecca et al., 2021). While seldom fatal, chikungunya can result in persistent arthralgia and chronic fatigue, markedly reducing quality of life. Management strategies are primarily aimed at alleviating symptoms, with no specific agents currently available(Rattanapan et al., 2025).

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding single-stranded RNAs, typically 21–25 nucleotides in length. By modifying chromatin structure, governing chromosome segregation, controlling transcription, maintaining RNA stability, and modulating protein synthesis, miRNAs regulate gene expression at multiple levels(Das and Rao, 2022). Several studies have shown that miRNAs play a crucial role in viral infections, with viruses potentially modulating host miRNAs to evade the immune system, while host miRNAs can exert either antiviral or proviral effects(Ostrycharz and Hukowska-Szematowicz, 2022). Recent advances in biomarkers for assessing vaccine immunogenicity and efficacy have highlighted miRNAs as promising candidates, owing to their unique roles in immune regulation and their considerable potential in vaccine development(Anticoli et al., 2025).

Chikungunya patients often present with chronic polyarthritis, with clinical manifestations resembling those of autoimmune inflammatory arthritis. Research has reported that miR-98–5p can be induced by estrogen signaling pathways and participates in regulating inflammatory responses and antiviral immune processes by targeting immune regulatory genes such as LIFR(Ramundo et al., 2025). In osteoarthritis-related studies, miR-10a-5p has been shown to promote the progression of joint lesions by inhibiting HOXA3, thereby modulating signaling pathways such as PPAR, PI3K-Akt, and p53, suggesting its potential role in the development of joint pain(Li et al., 2020). Meanwhile, miR-146a-5p, as a key regulator of IL-1β-induced inflammatory and catabolic responses, is upregulated in human and mouse OA cartilage; animal experiments have demonstrated that inhibiting miR-146a-5p can alleviate cartilage damage and mitigate OA-related joint pain(Qin et al., 2023). Additionally, small extracellular vesicles (FS OA sEV) derived from synovial fibroblasts can deliver miR-21–5p to downregulate KLF6, thereby promoting inflammation and catabolism in chondrocytes and advancing osteoarthritis progression, indicating a regulatory role of miR-21–5p in joint inflammation and pain(Konteles et al., 2025). In the context of COVID-19 infection, elevated expression of miR-155a-5p has been observed in various bodily fluid samples, suggesting its potential role in early inflammatory stress triggered by viral infection(Soltane et al., 2023). In summary, miRNAs exhibit significant regulatory functions in inflammation and joint diseases, providing a basis for further investigation into their roles in Chikungunya-related arthralgia.

Despite these insights, the specific roles and diagnostic potential of miRNAs in CHIKF, particularly their association with its hallmark arthritic symptoms, remain to be elucidated. Based on the known functions of miR-98–5p, miR-146a-5p, miR-21–5p, miR-10a-5p, and miR-155a-5p in joint inflammation, viral infection, and immune regulation, we hypothesize that they may be involved in the pathological process of CHIKF, especially in the development of arthralgia. Therefore, this study aims to quantitatively analyze the expression of these miRNAs in the serum of CHIKF patients, evaluate their diagnostic value as biomarkers, and examine the correlation between their expression levels and arthralgia symptoms, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying CHIKF-related arthralgia and developing novel intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Patient enrollment, sample collection, and clinical data acquisition

In this study, 55 patients with laboratory-confirmed CHIKV infection attending Baise People's Hospital between August and September 2025 were enrolled as cases, along with 55 healthy individuals undergoing routine physical examinations at the hospital’s Health Examination Center as controls. Cases of CHIKV infection were defined as patients exhibiting clinical signs consistent with arboviral infection and confirmed by RT-PCR. Patients were excluded if they had co-infection with Dengue virus, Zika virus, or malaria, severe underlying chronic diseases (e.g., advanced liver disease, renal failure, or malignancy), or incomplete clinical or laboratory data. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Baise People’s Hospital (No. KY2025100903).

Basic clinical data were collected at the patients’ first hospital admission, including sex, age, and medical history of hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, stroke, atrial fibrillation, and heart failure. Laboratory parameters were collected, including complete blood count (CBC) and serum biochemical markers such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), albumin (ALB), urea, creatinine (Cr), and uric acid (UA). In addition, the Aggregate Index of Systemic Inflammation (AISI = NEU × PLT × MONO / LYM) was calculated to evaluate systemic inflammatory status.

To accurately interpret miRNA dynamics in relation to disease progression, the exact day post-symptom onset was recorded for each patient sample. The day of symptom onset was defined as the first day the patient reported fever, rash, or arthralgia. Blood samples were collected at the first hospital admission, which occurred within a window of 0.3 to 4 days after symptom onset. The specific post-onset day for each individual patient is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

To avoid potential confounding effects due to hemolysis, all collected samples were assessed for hemolysis using a Canon fully automated biochemical analyzer (model TBA-FX8, serial number: FCA1982015) prior to RNA extraction. Only samples classified as non-hemolyzed by this instrument were processed for RNA extraction and RT-PCR. This procedure ensured that the miRNA expression profiles analyzed in this study were not influenced by hemolysis. The hemolysis classification criteria were defined based on the manufacturer's operational guidelines (Supplementary Table S2). Furthermore, 5 mL of whole blood was collected in yellow-top vacuum tubes. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min, transferred to clean Eppendorf tubes, and stored at −80 °C until use.

Serum miRNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from 250 uL of serum samples using the Body Fluid RNA Kit (Magigen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The miRNA extraction efficiency was evaluated by ultraviolet spectrophotometry (manufacturer's specifications).

Reverse transcription reactions

According to the manufacturer's (Ceville) instructions, the first-strand cDNA was synthesized from extracted miRNAs using the miRNA First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (tailing method).

Quantitative real‑time PCR

The expression levels of miR-10a-5p, miR-21–5p, miR-98–5p, miR-146a-5p, and miR-155a-5p were detected using the 2 × SP SYBR Green PCR kit (Fanerwen), with miR-16–5p serving as an endogenous reference for normalization(Xiang et al., 2014). Reverse transcription and PCR amplification were performed using miRNA-specific forward primers (sequences provided in Supplementary Table S3) and a universal reverse primer (Universal Reverse PCR Primer for miRNA RT-PCR; Servicebio, Cat No G3042–500UL). The specificity and size of the PCR amplicons were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and the amplification efficiency of the primers was evaluated using the standard curve method. The relative expression of target miRNAs was calculated using 2-ΔΔCt method, where Ct denotes the threshold cycle (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001; Winer et al., 1999). Statistical comparisons of miRNA expression levels between the control and case groups were performed using Student's t-test, with P values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.41). For comparison between continuous variables, Student’s t-test or Mann Whitney test was performed depending on the distribution of the data. Differences in the proportion of patients were analyzed by Chi-square test or Fischer’s exact test depending on the normality of the data. P values < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Clinical and hematological features of chikungunya patients and healthy controls

The baseline characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table 1. There were no significant differences in gender distribution or age between the Chikungunya group and the control group (P > 0.05). Compared with healthy individuals, Chikungunya patients showed a significant decrease in white blood cell (WBC) count (P = 0.0059) and lymphocyte (LYM) count (P < 0.0001), while monocyte (MON) count was significantly increased (P = 0.0037). The aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI) was markedly elevated in the Chikungunya group (P < 0.0001), indicating a strong systemic inflammatory response.

Differential expression of miRNAs between chikungunya patients and healthy controls

To validate the specificity and amplification efficiency of our qPCR assays, we performed a series of quality control experiments for the key miRNAs of interest. Agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed that miR-146a-5p, miR-98–5p, and the reference miRNA (miR-16–5p) each produced a single, specific amplicon of the expected size (approximately 80 bp; Supplementary Figure S1). The PCR amplification efficiencies, determined experimentally, were 92% for miR-146a-5p, 115% for miR-98–5p, and 108% for miR-16–5p (Supplementary Table S4), confirming that all assays were suitable for precise relative quantification. The expression levels of five selected miRNAs (miR-10a-5p, miR-21–5p, miR-98–5p, miR-146a-5p, and miR-155a-5p) were quantified in serum samples from CHIKV patients and healthy controls. As shown in Fig. 1, miR-10a-5p (P = 0.3172), miR-21–5p (P = 0.1955), and miR-155a-5p (P = 0.4921) showed no significant differences between the two groups. However, miR-98–5p was significantly downregulated in CHIKV patients compared with controls (P = 0.0007), whereas miR-146a-5p was markedly upregulated (P < 0.0001). These findings suggest that miR-98–5p and miR-146a-5p may serve as potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets associated with Chikungunya infection.

Diagnostic potential of circulating miRNAs in chikungunya infection

To assess the diagnostic performance of the identified miRNAs, ROC curve analysis was performed to distinguish Chikungunya patients from healthy controls. As shown in Fig. 2 and summarized in Table 2, miR-146a-5p exhibited the highest discriminative ability with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.768 (95% CI: 0.676–0.861, P < 0.0001), followed by miR-98–5p (AUC = 0.692, 95% CI: 0.593–0.791, P = 0.0005). The remaining miRNAs, including miR-10a-5p (AUC = 0.564, 95% CI: 0.456–0.673, P = 0.246), miR-21–5p (AUC = 0.556, 95% CI: 0.448–0.664, P = 0.312), and miR-155a-5p (AUC = 0.547, 95% CI: 0.439–0.655, P = 0.398), showed limited diagnostic power with AUCs close to 0.5 and non-significant P values. These findings suggest that among the tested miRNAs, miR-146a-5p and miR-98–5p have potential as diagnostic biomarkers for Chikungunya virus infection.

Discussion

Chikungunya fever (CHIKF) is a mosquito-borne viral infection characterized by pronounced arthritic manifestations. Its pathogenesis involves systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation; however, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly elucidated. MiRNAs are key regulators of immune and inflammatory responses during viral infections, acting through the post-transcriptional modulation of inflammatory cytokines and their upstream signaling pathways to maintain the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory processes. In this study, we identified distinct serum miRNA expression profiles in CHIK patients. Specifically, miR-98–5p was significantly downregulated, whereas miR-146a-5p was markedly upregulated, while miR-10a-5p, miR-21–5p, and miR-155a-5p showed no significant changes. Further subgroup analysis based on the presence of arthralgia revealed a notably lower expression of miR-98–5p in patients with joint pain, suggesting a potential association with the development of CHIK-associated arthralgia.

This study revealed a significant upregulation of miR-146a-5p in the serum of patients infected with Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), which is consistent with a role for miR-146a-5p as a key inflammation-associated miRNA involved in CHIKV infection. Although miR-146a-5p expression was markedly elevated following CHIKV infection, no significant difference was detected between patients with and without arthralgia. These findings suggest that miR-146a-5p may be related to the systemic inflammatory response triggered by CHIKV infection rather than being specifically associated with CHIKV-induced joint pain

This finding is consistent with previous studies on miR-146a-5p in osteoarthritis (OA). It has been reported that miR-146a-5p is significantly upregulated in chondrocytes and cartilage tissues of OA patients, and its expression can be strongly induced by key pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)(Shao et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2021; Zhao and Gu, 2020). However, the precise functional role of miR-146a-5p in OA pathogenesis remains controversial, which may provide a clue to its nonspecific involvement in CHIKV-associated arthralgia. On the one hand, multiple studies have confirmed the pro-inflammatory and cartilage-degrading effects of miR-146a-5p For example, Shao et al.(Shao et al., 2020) and Zhang et al.(Zhang et al., 2021) demonstrated that miR-146a-5p targets TRAF6 and NUMB to activate the NF-κB signaling pathway, promoting chondrocyte apoptosis and inhibiting autophagy, ultimately exacerbating cartilage degeneration. In addition, in vivo studies by Qin et al.(Qin et al., 2023) showed that knockdown of miR-146a-5p in chondrocytes effectively alleviated injury-induced osteoarthritis severity in mice and mitigated OA-associated pain behaviors. Collectively, these findings indicate that miR-146a-5p may play a promotive role in OA progression. On the other hand, some studies suggest that miR-146a-5p may exert protective effects. For instance, Jia et al.(Jia et al., 2019) reported that upregulation of miR-146a-5p attenuated SDF-1-induced cartilage degradation. Similarly, Yu et al.(Yu et al., 2021) demonstrated that in temporomandibular joint OA, miR-146a-5p may negatively regulate T cell-mediated immune responses by targeting OX40L, thereby alleviating inflammation.

This functional dichotomy may arise from the cell type-specific, disease-dependent actions of miR-146a-5p, as well as the complexity of its regulatory network. In our study miR-146a-5p is markedly upregulated during CHIKV infection, which likely reflects the host’s inflammatory status. In contrast, in OA, it participates in the regulation of apoptosis, autophagy, and extracellular matrix metabolism in tissue cells, including chondrocytes. In the present study, no association was observed between miR-146a-5p and CHIKV-specific arthralgia, which may be explained by the fact that acute joint pain in CHIKV infection is mainly driven by direct viral invasion and acute inflammatory mediators, whereas miR-146a-5p predominantly mediates systemic or chronic inflammatory responses.

In this study, miR-98–5p was significantly downregulated in CHIKV-infected patients. Further subgroup analysis based on the presence of arthralgia revealed that patients with joint pain exhibited markedly lower miR-98–5p expression compared to those without, suggesting a potential correlation with CHIKV-associated inflammatory responses and joint symptoms. This finding is consistent with the reported expression patterns of miR-98–5p in various inflammatory and joint-related disorders. For instance, miR-98–5p has been reported to be downregulated in the serum of children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia(Li et al., 2025), rheumatoid arthritis(Wang et al., 2021), and osteoarthritis(Lv et al., 2024), where it is involved in the regulation of inflammatory cytokine release and chondrocyte homeostasis.

Studies by Lv(Lv et al., 2024) and Pei et al.(Huang et al., 2021) reported that miR-98–5p may inhibit IL-1β-induced chondrocyte inflammation, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix degradation by targeting CASP3. Additionally, another study(Du et al., 2021) demonstrated that miR-98–5p can suppress the secretion of TNF-α and IL-6 by targeting BACH1. Thus, based on these reported mechanisms, it is plausible to hypothesize that, in CHIKV-infected patients, the downregulation of miR-98–5p may relieve the suppression of BACH1, resulting in a marked upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNF-α, which might in turn exacerbate inflammatory infiltration and tissue damage in the synovium. However, this proposed mechanism requires direct experimental validation in the context of CHIKV infection. Our findings suggest that the downregulation of miR-98–5p may be closely associated with systemic inflammation, such as elevated AISI, and lymphopenia observed following CHIKV infection. This notion is further supported by studies in autoimmune diseases. In systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), downregulation of miR-98–5p has been shown to drive aberrant monocyte-dendritic cell differentiation and excessive interferon signaling via IFI44L, thereby contributing to immune imbalance(Huang et al., 2024). We therefore speculate that during CHIKV infection, the marked downregulation of miR-98–5p may similarly disrupt the normal regulation of key immune cells, such as monocytes and dendritic cells. This may act synergistically with mechanisms directly targeting joint tissues (e.g., CASP3/BACH1), collectively creating a dysregulated immune microenvironment that promotes the onset and persistence of chronic arthralgia. Future functional studies are essential to establish any causal relationship and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of modulating miR-98–5p in CHIKF.

Although this study preliminarily revealed the expression profiles of CHIKV infection-related miRNAs and their potential association with arthralgia, several limitations should be noted. First, from a methodological perspective, we selected miR-16–5p as the reference gene for normalization in miRNA quantification. Although literature has reported that miR-16–5p exhibits relatively stable expression in certain biological fluid systems(Xiang et al., 2014), it must be acknowledged that the expression of some miRNAs, including miR-16–5p, may be influenced by sample hemolysis, potentially introducing bias into the quantification results. While we performed rigorous hemolysis screening using a biochemical analyzer on all samples prior to RNA extraction and used only those classified as “non-hemolyzed” in subsequent experiments to minimize this risk, any normalization strategy relying on a single reference gene cannot theoretically completely exclude fluctuations in reference gene expression due to trace hemolysis or other pre-analytical variables. Therefore, the potential limitation associated with using miR-16–5p for normalization in body fluid samples should be considered a methodological constraint of this study. Second, this study has limitations in the analysis of clinical phenotype subgroups. It is particularly noteworthy that the subgroup “without joint pain” included only 14 patients. This small sample size reduces the power of statistical inference within this group, making comparisons based on this subgroup and the “with joint pain” group (e.g., the interpretation of differences in miR-98–5p expression) require extra caution. Although we observed suggestive trends, the reliability of any association drawn from this comparison is limited. Future larger-scale, prospective cohort studies are needed to validate these preliminary findings. Third, the sample size of this study is relatively limited. While this does not affect the statistical significance of the intergroup differences, it may constrain the ability to conduct more in-depth subgroup analyses and could potentially affect the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. More importantly, this study has limitations in terms of mechanistic elucidation. Our work primarily describes the association between miRNA expression profiles and clinical phenotypes at the level of patient samples. It has not yet performed functional validation through in vitro or in vivo experiments to elucidate the specific molecular pathways by which key miRNAs (e.g., miR-98–5p and miR-146a-5p) regulate inflammatory responses and arthralgia following CHIKV infection. Consequently, whether these miRNAs serve as potential disease “biomarkers” or play an active role as “functional regulators” in pathogenesis, along with their upstream and downstream target genes and signaling networks, remains to be clarified in future studies. As exemplified in both B-CLL research—where bioinformatic predictions of miRNA-target interactions require subsequent validation by assays like luciferase reporter tests(Bagheri et al., 2021)—and in nanomedicine development—where in vitro and in vivo functional assessments are crucial(Shohani et al., 2017), moving from associative findings to mechanistic insight and therapeutic potential is a critical, multi-step process.

In summary, this study elucidates the complexity of serum miRNA expression profiles in patients infected with Chikungunya virus. The significant downregulation of miR-98–5p is associated with arthralgia symptoms, hinting at its potential involvement in processes related to local inflammatory responses and joint pathology. In contrast, the marked upregulation of miR-146a-5p primarily reflects a systemic inflammatory state. While its expression is universally elevated following infection, it shows no direct association with joint pain, further highlighting the distinct regulatory pathways between acute joint symptoms and systemic inflammation during CHIKV infection. Notably, miR-98–5p is the only miRNA that exhibits a significant expression difference between patients with and without arthralgia, and the degree of its downregulation shows a potential correlation with joint symptoms. This characteristic identifies it as a candidate worthy of further investigation, both as a potential biomarker for assessing arthralgia risk following CHIKV infection and as a molecular focus for exploring future therapeutic strategies. Future research should involve larger-scale prospective cohort studies combined with in vitro and in vivo experiments to further elucidate the specific mechanisms and regulatory networks through which miR-98–5p and miR-146a-5p may contribute to the pathogenesis of CHIKV infection, thereby providing a more robust scientific foundation for the future development of precise diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for Chikungunya fever.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that Chikungunya virus infection significantly alters serum miRNA expression, with miR-146a-5p and miR-98–5p potentially contributing to systemic inflammation and joint pain, suggesting their potential as biomarkers or therapeutic targets.
