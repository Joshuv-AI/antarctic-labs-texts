Regional corneal biomechanics assessment as a function of age using Strain–Stress Index maps
Authors: Vahoura Tahsini, Marta Jiménez‐García, Ahmed Makarem, Ahmed Elsheikh, Philippe Büchler, Sabine Kling, Jos J. Rozema
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo gain a better understanding of corneal Stress–Strain Index (SSI) maps in healthy eyes and to determine their changes with age.MethodThe eyes of 72 participants (age 43.1 ± 20.9 years, 69.5% female) were included in the analysis, considering the left and right eyes separately. Corneal biomechanics were assessed using a combination of the Pentacam and Corvis instruments, whose data enabled the production of the SSI maps. The corneas were divided into nine zones to facilitate zone‐specific analysis of biomechanical behaviour. Regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the relationship between age and SSI values in each corneal zone.ResultsThe mean overall SSI value of the cornea for all participants was 1.075. Considering different age groups, significant differences in SSI were seen between the young and older groups in the overall map from 0.938 ± 0.067 in 20–50‐year‐olds to 1.143 ± 0.064 in 50–80‐year‐olds (ANOVA, p < 0.001). The same effect was seen for each zone separately (ANOVA, p < 0.001). The corneal central apex and peripheral zones showed higher mean SSI values (1.153 ± 0.079) and hence, higher corneal stiffness compared with paracentral zones (0.890 ± 0.057).ConclusionThis paper showcases a series of maps depicting corneal elasticity and explores the differences in corneal stiffening with age across various regions of healthy corneas. The results reveal that stiffening tends to accelerate in areas that are already stiffer and decelerate in weaker regions. This deeper insight into ocular physiology could enhance clinical care by enabling more personalised treatments based on the patient's age and the specific corneal regions being addressed.

Key points This study presents region‐specific age‐related changes that deepen our understanding of ocular biomechanics.Corneal stiffening occurs more rapidly in regions that are already stiff and more slowly in weaker regions, which has implications for disease progression and treatment modalities.Findings support personalised corneal treatments by showing that stiffness varies across corneal zones and changes with age, potentially improving care in conditions such as keratoconus.

INTRODUCTION

Biomechanics plays a crucial role in maintaining the structural integrity and optical properties of the cornea. The distribution of collagen fibres within the stroma, along with their orientation, significantly influences the biomechanical behaviour of the cornea. 1 Early evidence suggests that alterations in the corneal microstructure occur with age, which can affect visual quality, ocular health and increase susceptibility to certain ocular conditions. 2 Understanding these changes is essential to optimise patient care and manage age‐related ocular conditions effectively. Therefore, in vivo, biomechanical characterisation of the cornea is crucial to optimise several treatments of age‐related problems, as the cornea is getting stiffer due to progressive collagen cross‐linking and glycation‐induced fibre modifications. Additionally, optimisation of age‐related properties of the cornea can be helpful for the treatment of common corneal issues such as keratoconus.

The evolution of non‐invasive techniques for assessing corneal biomechanics has been significant, with improved accuracy and clinical applicability. Early non‐contact techniques relied on air puffs or ultrasound to induce and measure corneal deformation. 3 Later, the Reichert Ocular Response Analyser (ORA, reichert.com) introduced a novel approach to analyse the corneal response through dynamic bidirectional applanation, providing new deformation parameters such as corneal hysteresis (CH) and the corneal resistance factor (CRF), 4 making it suitable for evaluating corneal biomechanics in eyes with keratoconus and glaucoma. 5 , 6 Meanwhile, the Corvis ST (Oculus GmbH, oculus.de/en/products/corvis‐st/) combines the response to an air puff with ultra‐high‐speed Scheimpflug imaging to assess corneal biomechanics. It produces estimates of dynamic corneal deformation parameters such as deformation velocity and amplitude that provide detailed information about the cornea's mechanical behaviour. 7 , 8 The Corvis is being utilised increasingly in both research and clinical settings for various applications, including refractive surgery screening, keratoconus detection and glaucoma management. 9 , 10 , 11 In addition to these techniques, Optical Coherence Elastography and Brillouin microscopy represent significant experimental advancements in the field of corneal biomechanics, promising new non‐invasive and depth‐resolved imaging capabilities that can complement existing techniques. 12 , 13 These imaging modalities have the potential to enhance the understanding of corneal biomechanics, including elasticity and stiffness in health and corneal diseases such as keratoconus, improve diagnostic accuracy and guide personalised treatment approaches such as corneal cross‐linking.

Recently, the Stress–Strain Index (SSI) map was introduced to retrieve the distribution of biomechanical properties of the cornea from air‐puff deformation imaging and has been used in both research and clinical studies. 14 Based on finite element modelling and the collagen fibre distribution in the cornea, this method produces two‐dimensional maps of corneal stiffness that are intended to be independent of corneal thickness and intraocular pressure (IOP). The method also enables estimating the tissue's tangent modulus (Et) at different IOP levels. 15 Although SSI facilitates the diagnosis of various corneal problems by assessing the tissue stiffness directly, it needs to be interpreted with caution as the biomechanical properties of the cornea also change with age due to alterations in collagen structure, crosslinks' density, hydration level and cellular activity. 16 To this end, this study used SSI data to analyse the influence of age and sex on the biomechanical properties of the cornea.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Participants

Seventy‐two healthy participants, that is, without known corneal pathology or prior surgery, were recruited for this study from the Antwerp University Hospital. Only right eyes of the participants were included in the analyses, except to determine the correlation between the left and right eyes. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Antwerp University Hospital (B300201732465). All volunteers provided written informed consent prior to their participation.

Measurement and analysis

Each participant underwent testing with the Pentacam and Corvis instruments, from which the SSI was calculated using a stand‐alone MATLAB (mathworks.com) program described elsewhere. 15 Once data became available from all participants, the average and standard deviation of the SSI were calculated for the entire cohort, separated into left and right eyes and for sub‐cohorts based on sex and decade of age.

In this work, data were converted into SSI maps using a method based on inverse analysis and the corneal tissue's microstructure distribution as previously reported. 14 , 15 To assess regional differences across the cornea, the SSI maps were divided into nine regions as shown in Figure 1: a central region with a diameter of 1.6 mm and two surrounding annular regions with diameters of 4.4 mm and 6.0 mm, corresponding to the paracentral zones (2–5) and the peripheral zones (6–9), respectively, each divided into four quadrants. This regional analysis of corneal biomechanics can provide insights into the pathophysiology of ocular diseases and inform personalised treatment approaches.

Cornea zones considered in analysis: Central zone (1), paracentral zones (2–5), peripheral zones (6–9).

Statistical processing

Statistical analysis was conducted with IBM SPSS 26 (ibm.com). The normality of the data was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test and parametric tests (ANOVA, Student's t‐test and ANCOVA) were applied to compare parameters with normal distributions. A threshold of p < 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance.

RESULTS

Patient population

This study examined the demographic and clinical characteristics of 72 healthy participants (30.5% male and 69.5% female). The mean age of the entire cohort was 43.1 ± 20.9 years (range, 20–78 years), with the average age for men and women being 51.6 ± 22.3 and 39.8 ± 19.3 years, respectively. The study was conducted across two distinct age groups: 20–50 and 50–80 years. The sample sizes for each group were 41 and 30 participants, respectively.

Regional differences

Figure 2 shows the average and standard deviation of the SSI maps obtained for all participants. Specifically, zones 6–9 (closer to the corneal periphery) exhibited higher SSI values than the centre and zones 2–5 (ANOVA with Student–Newman–Keuls post hoc test, p < 0.001). Significant differences were found between the SSI values of the inferior (I) paracentral (SSI_I = 0.833) and superior (S) paracentral zones (SSI_S = 0.945) (p < 0.001). The lowest values were found in the inferior paracentral zones (4, 5). Significant differences were also observed in SSI value between the temporal (T) paracentral (SSI_T = 0.879) and nasal (N) zones (SSI_N = 0.903; p < 0.001), which confirm higher stiffness in the nasal area.

Distribution of average (a) and standard deviation (Std) (b) of the corneal Stress–Strain Index (SSI) of the right eyes. In the map of average SSI, warmer colours represent weaker areas and cooler colours represent stiffer areas, while in the standard deviation map warmer colours represent more inter‐individual variations.

The standard deviation of the SSI maps showed a pattern that was remarkably similar to that of the average values, suggesting that the inter‐individual variations of the maps lie more in the amplitude of the SSI rather than in regional differences.

There was a highly significant correlation between the overall biomechanical properties of right and left eyes (Pearson r = 0.96, p < 0.001). Therefore, for the next comparison, only data from the right eyes were considered.

Influence of sex

While initially there seemed to be a significant difference in overall SSI between men and women (ANOVA test, p < 0.001), further ANCOVA analysis revealed that this difference disappears after correcting for the average 8‐year age difference between both sexes (ANCOVA test, p = 0.06; Figure 3).

Stress–Strain Index (SSI) comparisons between male and female eyes.

Influence of age

The overall corneal stiffness increased significantly with age from SSI = 0.938 ± 0.067 in 20–50‐year‐olds to SSI = 1.143 ± 0.064 in 50–80‐year‐olds, with a Pearson correlation of r = 0.92 (p < 0.001). The same was seen in each of the nine corneal zones, with correlations ranging between r = 0.72 (p < 0.001) for paracentral zones 2–5 and r = 0.73 (p < 0.001) for central and peripheral zone 1 and 6–9 (Figure 4). The rate of increase was lower in the inferior paracentral zones (4, 5) at 0.0039/year and higher in the periphery zones (6–9) at 0.0058–0.0067/year.

In the average maps, warmer colours represent weaker areas and cooler colours represent stiffer areas, while in the standard deviation maps, warmer colours represent more inter‐individual variations.

DISCUSSION

This approach allowed for a comprehensive analysis, providing insights into variations across different parameters and contributing to a more nuanced understanding of the dataset as previous studies demonstrated an age‐dependence. 14 This study is the first to present a series of maps of the in vivo corneal SSI in healthy participants and cross‐sectionally assess how these change with age. The SSI maps were based on two main assumptions: firstly, the direct reliance of corneal stiffness on the stromal collagen fibrils, which has been confirmed experimentally. 17 The second assumption is that the collagen fibril arrangement in the stroma of healthy eyes follows consistent trends. This assumption was adopted as the fibril density distribution was found to vary between healthy eyes within a standard deviation of <4% of mean values. 18 While these assumptions were adopted based on strong evidence of their basis, the fact that no patient‐specific microstructure measurements could be carried out is a limitation of the investigation.

In a previous paper, 14 SSI analysis, by focusing on corneal stiffness, could effectively evaluate age‐related changes in stiffness across the entire cornea. The current work provides a more comprehensive analysis of zonal age‐related changes in corneal biomechanics to characterise corneal material properties. The results suggest an age‐related increase in corneal stiffness (Figure 4), but the rate at which this occurs is not uniform, with the stiffening being slower in the weakest areas (Figure 5). This age‐related stiffening was observed by previous studies employing different methodologies such as the ORA, 19 in vitro radial shearing speckle interferometry, 20 Brillouin microscopy, 21 nanoindentation 22 and in vitro inflation testing. 23 This consistency across measurement techniques strengthens confidence in the observed relationship and provides complementary insights into the mechanical behaviour of the cornea, such as stiffness and tangent modulus as assessed using different methods.

Comparison of Stress–Strain Index (SSI) values versus age for each corneal zone (depicted in the lower right corner of each figure).

Analysis of the SSI maps revealed relative weakness in the peripheral corneal zones and a relatively high stiffness in the central and peripheral zones.

Optical coherence elastography studies suggest that the limbus has a different behaviour to the central cornea, primarily due to differences in collagen fibre orientation and density. 24 This property helps to provide support and stability to the cornea, enabling it to maintain its shape and resist deformation. However, the lack of information about the limbus represents a limitation in this study. Interestingly, the overall weakest area of the cornea was the inferior paracentral zone (zones 4–5), which corresponds with the location of the weak spot in keratoconus, which may partly explain why this disease typically occurs inferiorly. 25

Furthermore, corneal densitometry found that the corneal scattering properties, which may be linked to its material properties, change differently with age in the centre, paracentral and peripheral zones due to differences in age‐related corneal‐limbal degenerations. 26 Combining this densitometry information with the current findings could help provide a more complete description of the corneal structure in the future. On the other hand, the compatibility of these results with ex vivo findings confirmed the stiffening of the cornea with age. 23 It would be advantageous to examine the regional stiffness of the cornea using ex vivo methods in future studies.

To enhance the validity and reliability of the results, it would be beneficial to seek independent confirmation using alternative techniques, such as Optical Coherence Elastography or Brillouin microscopy. Employing these different methods could provide further validation of the observed patterns and help to address any potential biases or limitations associated with the current study's approach.

In conclusion, this paper presents a series of maps of corneal stiffness and investigates how the stiffness varies with age in different areas of healthy corneas, that stiffening takes place faster in already stiffer areas and slower in weaker areas. This will enhance understanding of ocular physiology based on the patient's age and the corneal area. Understanding these age‐related changes in different corneal regions may improve the clinical management of conditions such as keratoconus, which involves changes in localised stiffness. Regional biomechanical assessment across different age groups can also contribute to optimising surgical interventions, such as corneal cross‐linking or implanting intrastromal corneal rings by modifying treatment strategies to improve corneal stiffness more effectively in patients with keratoconus.
