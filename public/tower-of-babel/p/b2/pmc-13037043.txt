Attitudes toward statistics: relationship with nursing students’ attitudes toward evidence-based nursing and research anxiety
Authors: Öznur Erbay Dallı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe statistical course, which plays an integral role in the curriculum of nursing programs, is often perceived as challenging by students. This may negatively affect students’ attitudes and ability to engage in evidence-based practices and scientific research, where statistics are crucial. The purpose of the study was to examine nursing students’ attitudes toward statistics (ATS), explore its relationship with attitudes toward evidence-based nursing (EBN) and research anxiety (RA), and identify the factors influencing it.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted with 288 undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the second, third, and fourth years of a nursing program at a university, using a convenience sampling method. Data were collected using the Nursing Student Characteristics Form, the Survey of Attitudes Towards Statistics (SATS), the Evidence-Based Nursing Attitude Questionnaire (EBNAQ), and the Research Anxiety Scale (RAS). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation analysis, and multiple linear regression analysis.ResultsThe mean scores were 152.15 ± 29.04 for SATS (range: 36–252) and 48.69 ± 8.74 for EBNAQ (range: 15–75), with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes, and 30.11 ± 9.25 for RAS (range: 12–60), with higher scores indicating higher anxiety. ATS showed a moderate positive correlation with attitudes toward EBN (r = 0.402, p = 0.001) and a moderate negative correlation with RA (r = −0.546, p = 0.001). In the multiple linear regression analysis, prior interest in mathematics/statistics, participation in scientific meetings/events, and participation in a training/course on conducting scientific research were positively associated with ATS, whereas perceived difficulty in the statistics course and higher RA were negatively associated with ATS.ConclusionsThe results underscore the relevance of ATS within nursing education, particularly in relation to EBN and research-related experiences. Educational and experiential factors linked to statistics learning should therefore be taken into account in nursing programs. Collaboration between nursing educators and statistics instructors may help address students’ challenges and support a more accessible and student-centered approach to statistics education.Trial registrationNot applicable.Clinical trial numberN/A.

Background

The nursing profession is dedicated to providing high-quality patient care and improving healthcare outcomes [1]. As nursing roles have expanded to include research and evidence-based nursing (EBN), nurses are increasingly expected to interpret scientific findings and integrate research evidence into clinical decision-making [2–5]. In this context, statistics provides the methodological basis through which research findings are generated, summarized, and interpreted for clinical decision-making [6].

Statistics education constitutes an indispensable component of nursing curricula [7, 8]. Statistical literacy supports nurses and nursing students in interpreting key elements of research reports (e.g., effect estimates, variability, confidence intervals, and statistical significance) and in judging the strength and applicability of evidence to patient care [9, 10]. Accordingly, limitations in statistical understanding may create barriers not only for conducting research, but also for engaging with EBN, since evidence appraisal and translation into practice require an ability to interpret quantitative findings [11–15].

Despite this central role, statistics is frequently perceived as difficult and anxiety-provoking by nursing and other health sciences students [16–19]. Challenges have been attributed to limited mathematical background, difficulty recognizing the practical value of statistics, and pedagogical approaches that emphasize theory over application [20–22]. These experiences contribute to attitudes toward statistics (ATS), a multidimensional construct reflecting students’ affective responses, perceived competence, perceived value, and perceived difficulty regarding statistics learning [23]. ATS is educationally important because it may shape students’ engagement and persistence with quantitative content, which in turn affects their willingness to read, interpret, and use research evidence [24].

Within EBN, attitudes toward EBN reflect students’ beliefs, feelings, and intentions regarding the use of research evidence in nursing care and decision-making [25]. However, a positive orientation toward EBN may not be sufficient when students experience difficulties in understanding research methods and statistical results, as these difficulties can limit their ability to appraise evidence and reduce confidence in evidence-informed decisions [26]. Thus, statistics-related perceptions may intersect with EBN attitudes by shaping how feasible and approachable evidence use feels to students during training [27].

Similarly, research anxiety (RA) refers to apprehension or discomfort related to understanding and conducting scientific research [28]. RA may be reinforced when students perceive statistics as difficult or threatening, because quantitative components are often central to research design, analysis, and interpretation [20]. Students who feel anxious about research may avoid research activities, feel less confident interpreting scientific articles, and engage less with research learning opportunities; patterns that may also co-occur with less favorable ATS and weaker readiness to use evidence in practice [29].

Although ATS, EBN attitudes, and RA are conceptually connected through the shared demands of statistics and quantitative evidence appraisal, the existing literature often examines these constructs separately [8, 17–21, 25, 27–29]. Clarifying these inter-relationships is important for nursing education because it can help identify whether statistics-related perceptions represent a key educational context in which students’ evidence orientation and research-related affective responses are shaped. Therefore, this study aimed to examine nursing students’ ATS, explore their relationships with attitudes toward EBN and RA, and identify factors associated with ATS.

Methods

Design, setting and sample

This cross-sectional study was conducted between April and July 2024 among second-, third-, and fourth-year undergraduate nursing students at a public university located in Bursa, Türkiye, within a Faculty of Health Sciences nursing program. The convenience sampling method was employed to include students who had volunteered to participate in the study and had completed the statistics course (titled ‘Biostatistics’ in the curriculum) as part of their undergraduate education. Students who exhibited difficulties with reading and comprehension in the Turkish language, who had previously withdrawn from the nursing program for a period of time, or who had repeated a grade were excluded from the sample.

The total number of second-, third-, and fourth-year students enrolled in the undergraduate program in which the study was conducted was 449, comprising 164, 150, and 135 students, respectively. The statistics/biostatistics course is delivered by faculty members whose academic background is in mathematics/statistics, and who have teaching experience in quantitative methods for health sciences.

Data collection and instruments

The data were collected by the principal investigator using four instruments: the Nursing Students Characteristics Form, the Survey of Attitudes Towards Statistics (SATS), the Evidence-Based Nursing Attitude Questionnaire (EBNAQ), and the Research Anxiety Scale (RAS). The students were identified based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the purpose of the study was explained, and written consent was obtained before they were asked to fill out instruments. As the statistics course was offered in the final semester of the second year, the data pertaining to second-year students was collected within the time interval following the completion of the course (June-July 2024).

The primary study variable was ATS, operationalized using the SATS total and subscale scores. Additional variables were attitudes toward EBN (EBNAQ total and subscale scores) and RA (RAS total score). Potential associated factors were assessed via the Nursing Students Characteristics Form and included student characteristics and educational experiences related to statistics learning, research engagement, and EBN training.

Nursing students characteristics form

This form included sociodemographic information (age, gender, year of study, and type of high school education) and items assessing students’ prior experiences related to statistics learning, research engagement and general opinions on the statistics course (open-ended). Items covered prior interest in mathematics/statistics, perceived difficulty in the statistics course, participation in scientific meetings/research activities, participation in training/courses on conducting scientific research and evidence-based nursing/practices, and regular reading of scientific articles. Responses were recorded as categorical self-reports (e.g., yes/no).

Survey of the Attitudes Towards Statistics (SATS)

This scale was developed by Schau et al. (1995) for the purpose of assessing ATS of students as a multidimensional construct [31]. The scale comprises a total of 36 items, evaluated within six subscales: Affect (six items), Cognitive Competence (six items), Value (nine items), Difficulty (seven items), Interest (four items), and Effort (four items). Each item is rated on a seven-point Likert-type scale ranging from “Strongly Disagree” (1) to “Strongly Agree” (7). Negatively worded items are reverse-scored. Total scores range from 36 to 252, with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes toward statistics [31]. The Turkish version of the scale was validated by Sarıkaya et al. (2018), and the internal consistency for the sub-dimensions was reported to range between 0.69 and 0.90 [32]. In the present study, the Cronbach alpha coefficient for the overall questionnaire was found to be 0.92.

Evidence-Based nursing Attitude Questionnaire (EBNAQ)

This scale, developed by Ruzafa-Martinez et al. (2011) and adapted into Turkish by Ayhan et al. (2015), was used to determine nursing students’ attitudes towards EBN [33, 34]. The five-point Likert-type scale comprises 15 items and three sub-dimensions: beliefs and expectations towards EBN, intention of conduct towards EBN, and feelings towards EBN. Seven items on the scale are reverse-scored. The total score ranges between 15 and 75, with an increasing score indicating a positive attitude towards evidence-based nursing [33]. The Turkish validation study reported the internal consistency of the scale to be 0.90 [34], while the present study found it to be 0.91.

Research Anxiety Scale (RAS)

This scale, developed by Büyüköztürk (1997) to assess research anxiety levels among university students, comprises 12 items, rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree), with a single factor [35]. Five items on the scale that do not directly reflect anxiety (positive) are reverse-coded. The range of possible scores on the scale is between 12 and 60. A high score indicates that the individual exhibits anxiety about conducting research [35]. The internal consistency of the scale was reported to be 0.87 [35], and the present study found a value of 0.92.

Data analysis

The study data were analyzed on SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences, version 28.0) software package for Windows. The normality of the data was evaluated through the use of skewness/kurtosis and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive statistics (e.g., number, percentage, mean, standard deviation) were used to summarize the sociodemographic and other characteristics of the students. For comparisons across year of study (2nd, 3rd, and 4th years), one-way ANOVA was used for continuous variables (e.g., SATS, EBNAQ, and RAS scores), whereas the chi-square test was used for categorical variables (e.g., sociodemographic and educational experience variables). Pearson correlation analysis was employed to ascertain the interrelationships between the scales and their respective sub-scales. The strength of the correlation was determined based on the coefficient, with values ranging from 0.20 to 0.40 indicating a low correlation, 0.40 to 0.70 indicating a moderate correlation, 0.70 to 0.90 indicating a high correlation, and 0.90 to 1.00 indicating a very strong correlation. Finally, a forward multiple linear regression model was used to identify factors independently associated with ATS. Variables hypothesized to be associated with attitudes toward statistics (ATS) and presented in detail in Table 1 were considered as candidate independent variables for the regression model. The presence of multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values for all independent variables; tolerance values < 0.20 and VIF values > 10 were considered indicative of multicollinearity [30]. Results

Characteristics of the nursing students

The mean age of the participating students was 22.56 ± 1.59 years and 85.1% were female (Table 1). The majority of students (88.9%) had completed their high school education in a public school, while 71.1% indicated an interest in mathematics/statistics in their previous education. The majority of students (83.3%) reported experiencing difficulties in the undergraduate statistics course. General opinions were gathered through an open-ended question (multiple responses allowed) and categorized; the most frequently reported themes were that the course was boring (88.5%), too theoretical (85.4%), lacked practical teaching (71.9%), and was anxiety-producing (65.9%). A total of 41.7% and 33.7% of students, respectively, reported participation in scientific meetings/events and research activities. These rates were significantly higher (p < 0.05) for final-year students than for students in other years (Table 1). A total of 35.5% of the students indicated that they were able to understand the statistical terminology used in a scientific article. However, only 12.8% of the students reported that they regularly read scientific articles. Among the students, 12.5% and 25.3% reported having participated in training/courses on conducting scientific research and EBN practices, respectively. The number of students in their final year who reported participation in a training program on EBN practices was significantly higher than that of students in their second and third years (p < 0.05).

The total SATS, EBNAQ, and RAS scores of the sampled students were 152.15 ± 29.04, 48.69 ± 8.74, and 30.11 ± 9.25 points, respectively. The total SATS scores were similar between classes (p > 0.05); however, students in their final year had significantly higher EBNAQ scores (p = 0.041) and lower RAS scores (p = 0.026) than students in other years (Table 1).

Significant correlations were also observed between SATS subscales and the RAS total score (p = 0.001 for all): Affect, Cognitive Competence, Value, Interest, and Effort showed moderate negative correlations (r range: −0.444 to −0.538), whereas the Difficulty subscale showed a low positive correlation (r = 0.384, p = 0.001). Factors affecting attitudes towards statistics

In the multiple linear regression model, ATS was positively associated with participation in a training/course on conducting scientific research (B = 17.47, p = 0.001), prior interest in mathematics/statistics (B = 16.77, p = 0.001), and participation in scientific meetings/events (B = 8.44, p = 0.001), whereas ATS was negatively associated with perceived difficulty in the statistics course (B = −20.51, p = 0.001) and higher RA (B = −3.12, p = 0.001) (Table 3). Overall, the regression model explained 70.2% of the variance in ATS (R2 = 0.702). Discussion

Based on the existing literature, this study is among the first to examine ATS, EBN attitudes, and RA together in undergraduate nursing students. The results indicated that more positive ATS were associated with more positive attitudes toward EBN and lower levels of RA. In addition, prior interest in mathematics/statistics and participation in scientific meetings were associated with more positive ATS, whereas perceived difficulty in statistics courses and higher RA were associated with less positive attitudes. These findings indicate that ATS are closely linked to nursing students’ engagement with research-related learning and their orientation toward EBN, suggesting that statistics teaching strategies may benefit from being reviewed to better support students’ learning needs.

The present study demonstrated that nursing students’ positive ATS contributed to the development of a more positive approach toward EBN practices. In particular, the stronger correlations between the Value and Effort dimensions of ATS and attitudes toward EBN suggest that students who perceive statistics as valuable and invest more effort tend to report more favorable orientations toward EBN, highlighting the relevance of statistics-related attitudes to evidence appraisal in clinical decision-making. Additionally, a higher perceived difficulty regarding statistics was negatively associated with attitudes toward EBN. The literature reports that student nurses’ attitudes towards EBN practices are moderate, ranging from 47.51 to 57.3 points, which aligns with the study [36–38]. In addition, studies have reported that insufficient knowledge and skills in research and statistics are among the important barriers at the practitioner level for EBN practices among health professionals, including nurses [39–41]. The foundation of effective decision-making and EBN practice necessitates that nurses possess a robust comprehension of statistics [42, 43]. A study reported a 40% reduction in nursing students’ fear and anxiety about statistics following the implementation of a statistics course that was transformed into a hands-on, engaging, and enjoyable learning experience [44]. In this study, improvements to the statistics course included consideration of students’ preferred learning styles, incorporation of examples from the clinical nursing field, provision of computer labs, utilization of visual learning aids (humor, cartoons, and funny/interesting videos), and administration of weekly exams [44].

The findings indicated that more positive ATS were associated with lower levels of RA during the research process. In particular, an understanding of the value of statistics and competence in this field is associated with a greater reduction in research anxiety among students. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that difficulties related to statistics are associated with an increase in research anxiety among students. This suggests that strengthening statistics education may help better support students as they engage in research-related learning and processes. The moderate level of RA of nursing students in the study is consistent with previous studies [45–47]. Nevertheless, the difficulties encountered in statistics courses and the challenges associated with grasping statistical terminology in scientific studies may have exerted a detrimental impact on students’ attitudes towards research. Previous studies investigating students’ barriers to conducting scientific research have frequently reported statistics-related barriers, such as limited knowledge of statistical methods and poor interpretation and comprehension of statistical data [48–50]. Additionally, higher levels of RA were negatively associated with attitudes toward EBN. Conducting scientific research is fundamental to establishing an EBN, which is an applied science. This is essential for improving clinical practice and enhancing the quality of patient outcomes [51]. The study revealed a low level of participation among students in scientific research activities or training. The increased involvement of fourth-year students in such activities can be considered a consequence of the knowledge and experience they gained during their education. Nevertheless, encouraging such participation at an earlier stage may assist in reducing students’ RA and facilitate the development of more constructive attitudes towards EBN practices. In this context, the scientific research methods and statistics course, which is available in most nursing programs, should be strengthened with strategies such as organizing activities to read scientific research articles, using interactive teaching methods, and providing humor and active learning practices to make the science of statistics more understandable by eliminating classical lecturing and teaching techniques [14, 52]. It is expected that these enhancements to nursing education will enhance the quality of clinical practice over time and contribute to improved patient outcomes. In one study, a research course process was adopted for nursing students that included instruction in statistics and research design, as well as training in how to integrate research articles and clinical practice guidelines into nursing practice. The course methods included lectures, seminar assignments, discussions, and small group work. The implementation of this research course concept was observed to yield favorable outcomes, including enhanced perceptions of and confidence in research and EBN practices among students [53]. Another study indicated that the research course, which included a literature review, obtaining ethical and institutional approvals, data collection, data analysis, tabulation, academic writing, journal selection, and publishing steps, and was reinforced with weekly assignments, enhanced graduate nursing students’ scientific research, article writing, and publishing abilities [54].

The study revealed a number of significant factors affecting ATS. Prior interest in mathematics or statistics was identified as a contributing factor in students developing more positive attitudes towards these fields. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that early acquisition of mathematical and statistical skills can facilitate the development of a positive attitude toward these fields throughout a student’s academic career [17, 55, 56]. Furthermore, it was determined that participation in scientific meetings and training programs on conducting research had a positive impact on students’ ATS. Scientific conferences and research courses provide an environment conducive to learning, offering students the opportunity to expand their experience, knowledge, and research competencies, develop networking strategies, and establish connections with other professionals [57]. It is therefore important to promote student participation in scientific conferences and courses on research processes, as this will facilitate the development of their statistical thinking skills and enhance their self-confidence in this area. It was determined that perceived difficulty in the statistics course and higher RA were significantly associated with less favorable ATS. Cohorts of students studying health-related fields, such as nursing, frequently face difficulties in statistical course and feel a great deal of anxiety when conducting research [58, 59]. This may impact students’ willingness to engage in evidence-based decision-making processes, their confidence in conducting research, and their ability to integrate research findings into clinical practice. Nursing educators should work with faculty members from other disciplines who teach statistics courses to develop strategies for improving students’ ATS, with an emphasis on lowering the difficulties, fears, and anxieties that are frequently associated with learning statistics.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides an integrated examination of nursing students’ ATS in relation to EBN attitudes and RA. Data were collected using validated instruments with high internal consistency in the current sample, and the analysis included both group comparisons and multivariable modeling to explore factors associated with ATS. However, this study has several limitations. First, the study was conducted at a single university and employed convenience sampling, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, excluding students with Turkish reading/comprehension difficulties or prior academic interruption (e.g., withdrawal or grade repetition) may further restrict the representativeness of the sample. Second, data were based on self-reports, which may be prone to response and social desirability bias. Although the regression model explained a substantial proportion of variance in ATS (R2 = 0.702), this estimate should be interpreted cautiously. The inclusion of conceptually proximal predictors (e.g., RA and perceived difficulty in the statistics course) may partly account for the high explained variance within the same sample. Moreover, the forward selection approach and the absence of external or cross-validation may have resulted in an optimistic model fit; therefore, replication in independent samples is recommended. Finally, the cross-sectional design captures associations at one point in time; therefore, longitudinal studies are recommended to clarify the directionality of associations between attitudes toward statistics and related factors.

Conclusion

This study showed that more positive ATS were associated with lower RA and more favorable attitudes toward EBN practices. In addition, prior interest in mathematics/statistics and participation in scientific meetings and research-related training were associated with more positive ATS, whereas perceived difficulty in statistics courses and higher RA were associated with less favorable attitudes. These findings highlight practical implications for nursing education: statistics teaching may be strengthened through clinically contextualized examples, hands-on data interpretation using real research articles, scaffolded exercises with timely feedback, and integrated journal clubs or student research activities to support confidence and reduce anxiety. Accordingly, nursing educators are encouraged to collaborate with statistics instructors to revise curricula toward these applied and supportive strategies, thereby facilitating students’ engagement with evidence appraisal and evidence-informed clinical decision-making.
