Current Landscape of iPSC Haplobanks
Authors: Rubén Escribá, Meral Beksac, Annelise Bennaceur-Griscelli, Joel C Glover, Satu Koskela, Helen Latsoudis, Sergi Querol, Belén Alvarez-Palomo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of allogeneic induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived cell therapies for regenerative medicine offers an affordable and realistic alternative to producing individual iPSC lines for each patient in need. Human Leukocyte Antigens (HLA)-homozygous iPSCs matched in hemi-similarity could provide cell therapies with reduced immune rejection covering a wide range of the population with a few iPSC lines. Several banks of HLA-homozygous iPSCs (haplobanks) have been established worldwide or are underway, to provide clinical grade starting material for cell therapies covering the most frequent HLA haplotypes for certain populations. Harmonizing quality standards among haplobanks and creating a global registry could minimize the collective effort and provide a much wider access to HLA-compatible cell therapies for patients with less frequent haplotypes. In this review we present all the current haplobank initiatives and their potential benefits for the global population.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Induced pluripotent stem cells, HLA-match, HLA-homozygous, Haplobanks, Regenerative medicine

HLA-Homozygous iPSC Lines as a Source of Hypoimmunogenic Cell Therapies

Since the original description of induced reprogramming of terminally differentiated adult cells to pluripotency, substantial efforts have been made in regenerative medicine and stem cell biology using induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). Their unlimited proliferative capacity and ability to differentiate into cells of the three germ layers makes pluripotent cells a valuable source for cell-based therapies for various diseases and injuries. Whereas the first pluripotent stem cell-based clinical trials used human embryonic stem cells (hESCs), the field is now moving toward iPSCs. In contrast to ESCs, the use of iPSCs is not as hampered by ethical considerations, and consequently can be used worldwide without restriction given appropriate informed consent, which has favored their use in the clinic in recent years [1].

One of the major concerns regarding the use of human ESCs in clinical settings is immunological compatibility after transplantation. In this sense, iPSC technology offers the possibility to generate patient-specific cells that can be tolerated in autologous therapies. The first iPSC-based clinical trial was initiated by Takahashi and colleagues in 2014 [2]. They generated autologous iPSC-derived retinal pigment epithelial cells to treat a patient with age-related macular degeneration. They observed no signs of cell rejection after implantation and a delayed progression of the disease during follow-up. However, whole-genome sequencing revealed potentially adverse genetic variants in the iPSCs generated from the second patient, underscoring the need for ensuring the generation of safe cell products [3]. The researchers then decided to turn to an allogeneic approach using off-the shelf HLA-homozygous iPSC lines to treat HLA-compatible patients, and observed successful engraftment without a need for systemic immunosuppression [4]. An additional advantage to using allogeneic iPSCs is that the elevated costs and time required to generate clinical-grade autologous iPSCs limits their wide adoption. Furthermore, in severe indications such as heart failure or spinal cord injury, the protracted time required to produce clinical-grade autologous cell products is not amenable to successful treatment during the acute phase of these conditions.

Therefore, the use of highly characterized allogeneic HLA-compatible iPSC lines has come to the fore as an attractive alternative for clinical applications.

Since the first successful treatment with allogeneic iPSC-derived cells, numerous allogeneic clinical studies have been approved, targeting different conditions including cardiological diseases, neurological disorders and cancer [5]. As of July 2023, 13 allogeneic and 10 autologous therapeutic clinical trials have been registered, most of which are in Phase I or Phase I/II [6]. The eye, particularly in the context of macular degeneration [7], has emerged as the most frequently targeted organ system in these trials, likely due to its inherent immune privilege and straightforward accessibility. Following the eye, the nervous system, with treatment aimed primarily at Parkinson's disease [8], and the cardiovascular system [9], with a focus on heart failure [10], are the next most frequently targeted organ systems. Clinical trials are also exploring the use of iPSC-based therapies for neoplasms [11], underscoring the broad potential of this innovative therapeutic approach.

HLA antigens are among the major role players for immune rejection in organ and tissue transplants [12]. HLA-A, -B and -DRB1 are widely considered the most immunogenic proteins coded by the classical HLA genes [13]. However, generating iPSCs with unique HLA haplotypes to match each patient is unfeasible. Taylor and colleagues [14] proposed the use of HLA-A, -B and -DRB1 homozygous hESCs to create banks of highly immunogenetically compatible lines to cover a large proportion of the population. With a data set of 10,000 cadaveric organ donors from the UK, they estimated that ten donors homozygous for the most common ten HLA haplotypes would provide a complete HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-DRB1 match for 37.7% of the respective population [14]. Similarly, a study with 2,578 unrelated Japanese individuals estimated that 55 HLA-homozygous hESC lines would cover 80% of the respective population [15]. Subsequently, with the advent of iPSC technology the task of creating an HLA-homozygous bank became a much more feasible task, since screening large databases of HLA-typed donors would allow the targeted reprogramming of the most appropriate donors. Later studies using large databases have provided a more accurate picture of the estimated coverage that the HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-DRB1 homozygous iPSC collections (haplobanks) would have among different populations, including USA [16, 17], UK [18], Japan [19], Spain [20], Finland [21] and Brazil [22].

Sources of Starting Cells

Taylor and colleagues [14] estimated that large numbers of potential donors, in the order of dozens of thousands, had to be screened to identify triple HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-DR homozygous donors to match a large proportion of the UK population. Bone marrow donor (BMD) registries and umbilical cord blood (UCB) banks offer this scale of HLA-typed donors with 41 million donors being registered globally according to World Marrow Donor Association (WMDA). It is estimated that in 2020, about 800,000 and 4 million units were stored in public and private UCB banks, respectively [23]. UCB presents an excellent source of starting material for the production of iPSC lines because it is enriched in hematopoietic stem cells with negligible chances of accumulated mutations and epigenetic changes [24]. Also, the units collected in UCB banks are HLA-typed, screened for contaminating agents and stored under clinical-grade and good manufacturing practice (GMP) standards. Although initially the most frequent cell type for reprogramming was fibroblasts obtained from small skin biopsies, blood cells have gained popularity due to the less invasive access and the good efficiency of reprogramming mononuclear cells and hematopoietic progenitors using non-integrative reprogramming methods such as episomal or Sendai virus derived vector [25]. However, most blood cells cannot be efficiently reprogrammed using mRNA, which is the cleanest reprogramming method for making clinical grade iPSCs [26, 27]. Less abundant cell types present in the blood, such as endothelial progenitors, or alternatively, fibroblasts obtained from skin biopsies, grow in attachment and can be reprogrammed by mRNA. Of note, the inevitable damage caused by UV radiation of the skin may lead to point mutations, genomic variation and sub-clonal heterogeneity in skin fibroblasts. On the other hand, the risk of positive selection of somatic mutations in cancer driver genes during iPSC propagation should be taken into account [28]. Somatic BCOR (BCL6 corepressor) gene mutations –associated with some types of blood cancer, were indeed reported with a high prevalence in iPSCs derived from blood cells despite lower rates of genome-wide mutagenesis compared to iPSCs reprogrammed from fibroblasts [28].

Worldwide Haplobanks

HLA haplotype frequencies show a widespread geographical distribution, reflecting the diversity of populations [29]. Therefore, most haplobanking initiatives have been country-based and aim at achieving the maximum coverage of the respective country’s population. Both public and private haplobanks are already offering clinical-grade haplolines to be used as starting material for cell therapies, and several others are underway or have performed the immunogenetic studies in preparation (Table 1).

In 2013, Japan’s Center for iPSC Research and Application (CiRA), a public institution, started the construction of their national centralized haplobank, CiRA’s iPSC Stock Project, within the context of a national strategy prioritizing key areas that included regenerative medicine. The first report about this haplobank described 27 cells lines from seven donors, some from peripheral blood and some from UCB, representing the four most frequent haplotypes in Japan and covering 40% of the Japanese population through HLA-matching [30]. A later report described how in 2022, those haplolines had already been used in more than 10 clinical trials [31]. For applications in which the degree of HLA-matching might not be sufficient to avoid tissue rejection, CiRA research is investing on low-cost production methods for autologous iPSC manufacturing to overcome the prohibitive cost of goods of iPSC lines from a single donor, which is estimated at approximately 300,000 USD.

Subsequent efforts to establish functional haplobanks were carried out by two different research institutes in South Korea. The firs, in 2017, aimed for the identification of the top frequency haplotypes using 30,632 UCB donations by the Catholic iPSC Research Center at the Catholic University of Korea, an initiative funded by the South Korean Government [32]. Homozygous blood samples were reprogrammed in the GMP-compliant facility of the Catholic Institute of Cell Therapy. Thirteen clinical grade haplolines were manufactured from UCB, peripheral blood or bone marrow, representing the seven most frequent haplotypes in Korea.

The same year the CHA Stem Cell Institute, at CHA University in South Korea, published a second haplobank with Korean haplotypes [33]. Ten HLA-homozygous iPSC lines were created based on the ten most frequent haplotypes in South Korea by repurposing units from within a dataset of 4,205 HLA-typed UCB units at the CHA Medical Center Cord Blood Bank. The authors estimated a cumulative HLA-matching of 41% of the Korean population. Small variations in the ranking of haplotype frequencies compared to the Catholic University of Korea estimates are probably due to the different cohorts used in the study pools. Even though the manufacturing of the haplolines was carried out according to GMP conditions, in clean room facilities, six out of ten showed integration of the episomal reprogramming vector, which would question their fitness for clinical applications.

In addition to the public haplobanks described in Japan and Korea, a successful public effort was also made by a European country with the development and establishment of the public Spanish haplobank, named IPS-PANIA [34]. Funded by the Spanish Government, the project was carried out by a collaboration between the Blood and Tissue Bank (Banc de Sang i Teixits, BST) in Barcelona and the Biomedical Research Institute of Bellvitge (IDIBELL, Barcelona). Estimation of the most frequent haplotypes was done using a cohort of 30,000 randomized high-resolution HLA-typed adult BMD from the Spanish BMD registry (REDMO). Then, UCB homozygous units were identified searching all the public cord blood (CB) banks in Spain accounting for 60,000 clinical-grade units reported to REDMO. Seven of the most frequent haplotypes were represented in the homozygous iPSC lines, covering an estimated 21% of the Spanish population [34]. These haplolines were reprogrammed in standard facilities but using GMP reagents and full traceability. Subsequently, the established haplolines were expanded and banked (Master Cell Banks and Working Cell Banks) in clean rooms following GMP-compliant procedures, in a “cleansing” approach to obtain the clinical grade lines. Adaptation of research lines to clinical grade has been a strategy adopted for pluripotent cell lines in many on-going clinical trials [35].

In Germany, the HLA-iPS-GMP project has been funded by the German Government for the establishment of a GMP-compliant haplobank to produce allogeneic cell therapeutics. In 2021, the CB bank Dusseldorf performed the identification, selection and CD34 + population isolation of 10 homozygous donors of the most frequent haplotypes in Germany, following a GMP-compliant protocol [36]. Another German initiative is planning to build a haplobank with a different source: skin fibroblasts of homozygous donors identified from a blood bank reprogrammed with mRNA; presenting this as more reliable approach [37].

In France, the Center for iPS Cell Therapy, CiTHERA, of the National Infrastructure INGESTEM of INSERM and the University of Paris Saclay, is developing the French allogeneic and universal iPSC bank [38]. A cGMP-compatible reprogramming protocol for CD34+ cells from cord blood, adult leukapheresis from platelet donors, and peripheral blood CD3 + lymphocytes has already been validated at CiTHERA and six iPSC lines (named Cith-01 to 06) have been manufactured and characterized. Moreover, in agreement with the French Blood Establishment and the Biomedicine Agency, fifteen CB units from triple HLA homozygous donors have been identified for the future manufacturing of clinical grade haplolines.

In Norway, the Norwegian iPSC Biobank for Clinical Applications (NIBCA) initiative is establishing a GMP-compliant haplobank for allogeneic cell therapy. This is a collaboration between the Norwegian Core Facility for Human Pluripotent Stem Cells (under the Norwegian Center for Stem Cell Research), the Ex Vivo Clinical Cell Production Facility and the Norwegian Bone Marrow Donor Registry (NBMDR), all at Oslo University Hospital. Through NBMDR, 9 homozygous donors have been selected to match the most frequent haplotypes in Norway, with a coverage of about 70% of the ethnic Norwegian population [39]. Screening to avoid the most common cancer driver gene mutations is an integrated step. Regulatory approval has been obtained, GMP-compliant protocols established, and a bank of skin fibroblasts is currently being generated. mRNA reprogramming will be utilized to generate the iPSC haplobank.

The creation of an Australian public haplobank has been undertaken by the AusCord Network of public UCB banks. To identify the most frequent haplotypes, 13,679 HLA-typing records were interrogated and the homozygous CBU were identified, assuming that the frequency of homozygous units will largely reflect the population haplotype frequency [40]. Moreover, a very interesting GMP-compliant methodology has been established to obtain enough erythroid progenitors and to reprogram them into iPSCs using just the small blood volume in the testing segments of the CBU, avoiding in this way to compromise the integrity of the CBU and its availability for hematopoietic progenitor transplantation [41].

In the United States, the public New York Blood Center (NYBC) filed a patent application (PCT/US2018/064009) [42] claiming a method of producing a library of HLA-homozygous iPSCs. The objective of creating a haplobank using CBU is still described as one of the research objectives of the NYBC, although progress on this initiative has not been published so far.

A haplobank in Saudi Arabia is being created at the King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC) and the King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences. 64,315 high-resolution HLA genotypes from the Saudi Stem Cell Donor Registry were interrogated to identify the most frequent haplotypes [43]. It was estimated that 13 haplolines would cover 30% of the Saudi population. The authors argue that a feasible goal would be producing enough haplolines to cover 30–50% of the Saudi population (13–51 haplolines). As a proof-of-concept, using peripheral blood from a donor homozygous for the most common haplotype, two clones of one homozygous haploline were manufactured in research-grade labs, with the intention of re-deriving them to clinical grade. This and future haplolines are planned to be derived and cryo-preserved in the clinical-grade facilities at KAIMRC.

In Taiwan, the National Taiwan University together with the Taiwan Human Disease iPSC Service Consortium created 13 haplolines representing the most frequent haplotypes in Taiwan according to previous studies, covering 16% of the Taiwanese population [44]. However, the haplolines were created as research grade only and their purpose is to be used as a drug screening platform for toxicity assessment reflecting the particular sensitivities of the Taiwanese population [45].

At least two private companies are offering clinical-grade haplolines to be licensed for the development of cell therapies. In 2015, Fujifilm Cellular Dynamics Inc. announced they had created two GMP-compliant iPSC lines homozygous for HLA haplotypes covering 19% of the United States population [46]; however, the specific haplotypes are not publicly disclosed. Those haplolines are available to be purchased for commercial use including the development of cellular therapies within a clinical setting. CATALENT Biologics, a gene and cell therapy developer, offers a GMP-compliant haplobank covering “a large fraction of HLA haplotypes in the Caucasian population” [47]. This company is part of the consortium that is developing the EU-funded project HEAL: “HLA-homozygous iPSC-cardiomyocytE Aggregate manufacturing technologies for allogeneic cell therapy to the heart” [48].

Joining Forces to Increase Global Coverage

HLA genes are the most polymorphic genes in the human genome [49–51] and HLA frequencies markedly diverge across human populations, with a strong geographical and population effect on their distribution. The high population-specific genetic diversity of HLA, combined with the complexity of its molecular variation and the limitations of traditional HLA typing, confines the effectiveness of BMD registries and public UCB banks [52]. This is particularly evident in countries that include ethnic minorities and is also characterized by distinct population structures related to geographic/genetic isolation, inbreeding or common founders [53]. Even though in Japan 50 haplolines would cover 90.7% of the potential recipients [54], other populations with a more diverse HLA pool would need even higher numbers: more than 600 lines would be necessary to cover 90% of the Spanish population [20] and more than 500 to cover 90% of the Brazilian population [22]. Similarly, interrogation of the HLA typing data from 3,012 CBUs and 76,689 BMDs by the network of the Greek public UCB and BMD registries that operate under the auspices of the Hellenic Transplant Organization estimated that while the ten most common haplotypes would cover 27.6% of the Greek population, 463 lines would be required to cover 89% of the population (in preparation). Such a heterogeneity highlights the importance of the public UCB banks and BMD registries for an efficient donor recruitment, particularly in countries of relatively small population size and significant ethnic and genetic diversity [55]. Under this prism, mapping the regional HLA haplotype spectrum, taking into account specific inter-population characteristics (e.g., genetic and geographic distances, gene flow) and intra-population natural selection and evolutionary processes (e.g., genetic drift, balancing and positive selection), will increase the efficiency of donor search and selection [56].

Consequently, sharing haplolines could not only overcome the barrier of inter- and intra-population diversity but could also help cover the populations in countries that have not created their own haplobanks and also the minorities in countries that have. For example, an HLA-matching coverage calculation performed with the most frequent Korean haplotypes showed these would be of great benefit for the Japanese population but would provide poor coverage for the UK population unless a very high number of haplolines were made [33]. Taylor et al. calculated that 405 homozygous HLA-A-B-DRB1 combinations would be needed to provide coverage for near 100% of the UK population [14]. These numbers show the convenience and importance of having a world iPSC haplobank or common registry that would allow access to all existing haplolines from the regional haplobanks. Until a joint international effort is mustered to implement a global immunogenetic study, it is hard to calculate the number of haplolines necessary to match a significant percentage of the world population. For some regions lacking a large enough registry of BMD or UCB donors, it is not possible to calculate accurately the most frequent haplotypes in the population, and coverage calculations will have to rely on small cohort studies [57].

Some initiatives are already starting to coordinate the different haplolines sources and to help harmonize production and quality control criteria to make them universally acceptable. One of these examples is the Global Alliance for iPSC Therapy (GAiT, www.global.gait), an international resource supporting implementation and clinical application of therapies derived from immunogenetically matched, clinical-grade pluripotent stem cells [58]. To contribute in this sense, GAiT has published recommendations and guidelines to build consensus around standards for iPSC manufacturing and characterization [59].

In the same vein, EU-funded COST action HAPLO-IPS is a collaborative network that provides a framework for hiPSC generation of clinical-grade haplolines, aiming to harmonize production and quality control criteria and to create a future common registry. This network includes 28 countries, mostly European, and all the relevant stakeholders: CB banks, iPSC manufacturers, immunologists, regulatory bodies, etc [60].

A global haplobank could work as a virtual repository that would gather information and channel access to all available haplolines from each separate haplobank. Besides allowing cell therapy developers and manufacturers access to the less common haplotypes in a given region, it would help reduce redundancy to optimize resources. Moreover, given the intrinsic donor and clonal variability in iPSC lines, generating and maintaining multiple clones for any given haplotype which can differ in their potency for differentiation of specific cell types, may speed the optimization and implementation of certain therapeutic cell products.

Harmonizing Haplolines

To create a global registry of mutually recognized haplobanks for maximized access by patients, minimum acceptability criteria should be agreed on internationally. Those criteria should satisfy the different involved stakeholders, most importantly the cell therapy developers and regulatory agencies in the different countries. For example, international accreditations for providers of hematopoietic progenitors for transplantation, such as JACIE, allow international donations on a daily basis with an improved standard of care [61]. Similarly, a universal accreditation could pave the path for harmonization towards a common clinical iPSC repository.

Harmonizing haplobanks will require setting common standards of different aspects such as starting cell procurement, GMP manufacturing and quality control [62]. The International Stem Cell Banking Initiative (ISCBI) has published several reports with considerations and guidance for the development of pluripotent stem cell banks for clinical applications [63].

Proper procurement of the initial cells is a highly relevant aspect for the final acceptability of the iPSC lines. Working with donations from public UCB banks assures a suitable clinically acceptable procurement of the starting cells [64]. However, a new informed consent has to be signed by the donor, given that the original donation will potentially be used for new applications, including the commercial development of new cell therapies. Therefore, special care has to be put into formulating the informed consent document, to protect the donors and to comply with the requirements of all the possible regulatory agencies that will have to approve the future cell therapies [35].

Manufacturing procedures and quality control criteria will have to be globally harmonized for universal acceptance. Guidelines for characterization and quality control have been proposed by GAiT [59] and the International Society of Stem Cell Research [65].

One of the most debatable aspects for a harmonized consensus is the validation of key international quality standards complying with cGMP, and the definition of the Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs) related to safety agreed by the scientific community and regulatory agencies. GAiT has gathered a multidisciplinary network of recognized experts to build greater understanding and agreement on Critical Quality Attributes (QCAs). International External Quality Rounds of the same cell lines between laboratories will ensure reproducible mandatory quality testing assays, for which there are no pharmacopeial guidelines currently available. The demonstration of cell comparability between various batches is also a crucial challenge to maximize strategic development of cell therapy. An important issue is the genetic stability of the clinical grade haplolines. Although ISSCR and GAiT recommend going beyond mandatory karyotyping and performing a more detailed genomic analysis such as comparative genome hybridization microarray, SNP microarray or whole exome sequencing, there is still no consensus on how to interpret the resulting data or where to set the acceptability threshold [31].

Finally, the acceptance of iPSC-derived cell therapies by the relevant regulatory agencies will be the ultimate test of harmonization among the different haplobanks.

Accessibility of the Haplolines

To benefit patients world-wide, clinical grade haplolines need to be available for advanced therapy developers and manufacturers. The haplobanks from South Korea (both from CHA and Catholic University) are not yet readily available for clinical studies or cell therapy developers, although presumably they will be soon. The research grade counterparts of the haplolines from the Spanish bank IPS-PANIA are already available from the Spanish Cell Lines National Bank and soon the clinical grade haplolines will be available as well. The haplobank from CiRA, Japan, has already provided lines to 30 institutions for clinical purposes and both Fujifilm-CDI and CATALENT lines are also available. In any case, accessibility to clinical lines to develop iPSC-derived therapies requires licensing and involves agreements with the provider. It could be that an advanced therapy medicinal product manufacturer will need to access lines from more than one haplobank to cover the demand in a certain population. Different licensing restrictions from different providers could create difficulties in the successful development of the therapy. Again, a will to maximize patient benefits should prevail and encourage different providers to harmonize and join efforts.

Alternatives: HLA Editing

Alongside the construction of haplobanks in each country, universal hypo-immunogenic IPS lines evading the immune radar can be complementary and clinically useful cell lines in some particular contexts. This strategy could be valuable for rare haplotype cells, and in certain clinical applications, such as hematopoietic cell transplantation, in which HLA mismatches profoundly affect engraftment, and autoimmune diseases, in which autoantigen presentation would cause side effects. Creating immune-evasive iPSC by targeting disruption of HLA antigen expression has been proposed as an alternative to HLA matching [66]. The idea is to create from a unique donor a universal iPSC that does not express HLA antigens, to avoid recognition and targeting by T lymphocytes. However, lack of expression of HLA class I would activate attack by NK cells through “missing-self” recognition by KIR receptors. Different strategies have been designed to make iPSCs less immunogenic using engineering approaches, such as knocking out beta-2-microglobulin and CIITA to eliminate the expression of HLA-class I and class II genes, respectively, and overexpressing CD47 to silence the innate immune response [67], or disrupting both HLA-A and -B alleles and retaining HLA-C antigen presentation to suppress the NK cell response [68]. The CiRA’s iPSC Stock Project, besides establishing the haplobank and providing the cell lines for both research and clinical trials, has been following the long-term clinical outcome and establishing new iPSC stocks with other approaches such as pseudo-homozygous lines with HLA-II, HLA-A and HLA-B knockout and HLA-C retention [31]. This is still considered an experimental approach by CIRA.

In particular, creating immune evasive cells for transplantation is not devoid of risk, since the immune system might be hampered in the case of oncogenic transformation [69] or viral infection [70].

Conclusions

As an increasing number of clinical trials using iPSC-derived cell therapies brings clinical application closer to reality, the use of HLA-homozygous donors for a match in hemi-similarity is becoming more and more the approach of choice. This would eliminate the costly and time-consuming need of creating personal iPSCs for each patient. Haplobanks containing collections of HLA-homozygous iPSCs could provide appropriate starting material to make immune-compatible cell therapies available to large proportions of the population. Several public haplobanks have already been established or are underway in different countries, and a couple of private initiatives have also established a few haplolines each so far. Given the great diversity of HLA alleles in any given population, and their marked geographical distribution, a large number of haplolines would have to be created to match the least frequent haplotypes in any given population. However, sharing haplolines among the different haplobanks could greatly help to maximize coverage and minimize efforts by avoiding redundancy. For this common pool of accessible haplolines to be effectively beneficial for all potential patients would require harmonization of quality requirements across haplobanks. International/European coordination of the different health regulatory agencies is needed to establish consensus criteria for accepting iPSC lines as starting materials for their use in cell therapy manufacturing.
