Changes in Housework Frequency and Subsequent Cognitive Function and Rate of Decline Among Adults Aged ≥ 65 in the United States, 2008–2018
Authors: Nan Wang, Wenjie Cai, Xiaoyu Pan, Tianci Wang, Alden Gross, Changchuan Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPhysical activity level plays a role in modifying the likelihood of developing Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias. However, little is known about how changes in housework affect cognitive function among US adults over the age of 65. This study investigates the correlation between changes in housework frequency and cognitive function over 10 years.MethodsData from 8141 adults aged ≥ 65 years in the Health and Retirement Study were analyzed, with changes in housework frequency categorized as “consistently high,” “low to high,” “high to low,” and “consistently low” from 2008 to 2010. Cognitive function was measured from 2010 to 2018 using a composite score (range = 0–35), and mixed-effects linear regression models were fitted.ResultsOf 8141 participants (median age: 75 years [SD: 6.6] and 59.3% female), individuals who reported changes in housework frequency from high to low and consistently low were associated with an additional cognitive decline of 0.079 (95% CI, −0.117 to −0.042) and 0.090 (95% CI, −0.126 to −0.054), respectively, relative to those whose housework frequency remained consistently high. There was no statistically significant additional decline among adults reporting low to high frequency compared with those reporting consistently high housework frequency (β = −0.027; 95% CI, −0.074 to 0.019; P = .252). This association was similar among women and men (Pinteraction = .765) and was also similar among adults aged ≥ 80 years and those aged 65–79 years (Pinteraction = .069).ConclusionTransitioning from low to high or maintaining consistently high housework engagement in later life was associated with a delay in cognitive decline, regardless of gender and age.

Introduction

In 2023, it was estimated that 6.7 million adults over the age of 65 had Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) in the United States, 1 with projections exceeding 13 million adults with ADRD by 2060. 2 In 2019, ADRD accounted for nearly 10% of deaths in the United States. 3 ADRD is also associated with high economic burdens, with health care costs related to ADRD treatment in the United States estimated at $321 billion in 2022. 4 Preventing and managing ADRD is complex and challenging. Although major strides have been made in identifying genetic risk factors for ADRD (ie, apolipoprotein E [APOE] ε 4), the understanding of modifiable risks for mitigating cognitive decline remains incomplete. 5

Low physical activity is 1 of the 12 modifiable risk factors for ADRD, 6 with data showing that addressing this factor might prevent or delay up to 21% of ADRD cases. 7 Although numerous research studies have explored the positive effects of physical activities in preserving cognition reserve and potentially delaying cognitive decline in older adults, 8–11 the majority of previous research has only assessed recreational physical activity based on data from a single time point. 9,12 Notably, nonrecreational activities like housework contribute substantially to overall health, especially for elderly individuals without an exercise routine. 13,14 Despite some studies examining longitudinal housework frequency trajectories simultaneously with cognitive outcome trajectories or utilizing cross-sectional designs, 15–18 these approaches may not offer robust evidence concerning the temporal association between housework engagement and cognitive outcomes. Consequently, there is limited understanding of how changes in housework frequency over time affect individuals and whether these effects vary across different life stages, as well as among women and men.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study investigates the association between changes in housework frequency over a 2-year period and subsequent cognitive function aging among adults aged ≥ 65 years in the United States. The study also examines whether age and gender act as effect modifiers over an 8-year follow-up period with the same group. The study hypothesized that consistently low housework frequency or a shift from high to low frequency would be linked to lower subsequent cognitive function and a faster rate of cognitive decline.

Methods

Data source and study sample

Health and Retirement Study (HRS) data from 2008 to 2018 were utilized for this study. The HRS is a prospective cohort study conducted by the National Institute on Aging and the Institute for Social Research at the University of Michigan. Every 2 years, the HRS collects data from a nationally representative sample of individuals aged ≥ 50 years in the United States. The HRS uses a refreshed sample from over 40,000 adults and follows a steady-state design—every 6 years a new, nationally representative birth cohort is added while prior cohorts continue to be followed. Details of the multistage sampling design, execution, and response rates for the HRS have been covered at length. 19,20 The HRS has been approved by the University of Michigan institutional review board (IRB number: HUM0061128). This study was determined to be exempt from institutional review board review as it involved secondary analysis of deidentified data and did not include any interaction or intervention with human participants.

A total of 15,031 respondents participated in both the 2008 and 2010 survey modules and were followed for up to 8 years. HRS 2010 was considered the baseline. Participants < 65 years old were excluded in 2010 (n = 4607); missed the answers to the frequency of housework in either 2008 or 2010 (n = 29); missed the response to cognitive function or had prevalent ADRD (self-report clinician diagnosis) in 2010 (n = 971); had < 2 measurements of cognitive scores during 2010–2018 (n = 1162); or had missing data on covariates (n = 121). From this, 8141 participants were included in the data analyses (Supplemental Figure 1).

Housework

Housework was assessed by the question, "And how often do you take part in sports or activities that are mildly energetic, such as vacuuming, laundry, home repairs?" with answers including: every day, more than once per week, once per week, 1–3 times per month, or never. Participants who indicated their frequency as "every day" or "more than once per week" were categorized as high frequency, while those who chose any other response were considered low frequency. 21 Changes in housework frequency were classified as “consistently high,” “low to high,” “high to low,” and “consistently low.” The authors used the self-reported housework information collected from 2008 to 2010.

Cognitive function

In each wave, the HRS assessed respondents’ overall cognitive functions, including orientation, executive function, and memory, through a battery of cognitive tests. 22 These tests consisted of 6 parts: backward counting (scored 0–2), naming the president and vice president (scored 0–2), recall of the current date (scored 0–4), immediate and delayed word recall (scored 0–20), serial 7′s test (scored 0–5), and objective naming test (scored 0–2). 23,24 A total cognition score, ranging between 0 to 35, was calculated by adding the correct answers from each assessment; higher scores represented higher cognitive function. 23,24 The authors used the total cognition score collected from 2010 to 2018.

Covariates

Based on prior studies, 6,25,26 the authors included covariates associated with cognitive function. Demographic background included age (in years), race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, or non-Hispanic others), and gender (male or female). Socioeconomic factors included household income (in thousands of dollars), educational attainment (less than high school, high school graduate, some college, or college and above), and employment status (currently employed or not). Psychosocial and behavioral resources included marital status (married/partnered or not), depressive symptoms measured by the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression (range 0–8), activities of daily living (ADLs), alcohol use per day (never or 0, 1, or ≥ 2 drinks per day), tobacco use (never smoked, currently smoke, or formerly smoked), vigorous physical activity (≥ 1 per week, ≥ 1 per month, or never), and moderate physical activity (≥ 1 per week, ≥ 1 per month, or never). Health-related factors included body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) and disease diagnoses (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, stroke, lung disease, and cancer; yes or no for each). All of these covariates were at baseline (2010).

Statistical analysis

Pearson’s χ2 and Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum tests were used to compute the baseline characteristics of research participants across changes in housework frequencies. To examine the relationship between changes in housework frequency and cognitive scores in 2010 and the rate of decline from 2010 to 2018, mixed-effects linear regression models were fitted with person-specific random intercepts and slopes, including a statistical interaction term between changes in housework frequency and follow-up years (range: 0–8). To examine effect modification by gender and age, the authors conducted additional analyses, including an interaction term between changes in housework frequency, follow-up years, and each of baseline age (≥ 80, < 79) or sex (female, male). Gender- and age-specific subgroup analyses were subsequently performed for direct presentation of the differences. Two sets of models were conducted to sequentially control for covariates at baseline (age, gender, race, marital status, employment status, education, and household wealth), 1,2 BMI, smoking status, alcohol use, vigorous physical activity, moderate physical activity, chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, heart, stroke, lung diseases, and cancer), ADL scores, and Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression scores.

This study used 3 sensitivity analyses. First, in order to rule out possible reverse causation between changes in housework frequency and cognitive function, modeling analyses were repeated, restricted to adults in the top 50th percentile of cognitive scores in 2010. Second, the authors additionally controlled for baseline cognitive scores to help exclude potential reverse causation, even if these model sets may be over-adjusted because cognitive function at baseline may be impacted by previous housework frequency status and therefore fall on the causal pathway. 27 Third, the authors excluded participants who “never” did housework from 2008 to 2010 from being considered “low frequency,” as their cognitive scores were lower than those who did not do housework more than once a week. Fourth, the authors excluded participants with at least one family member residing in a nursing home and additionally adjusted for changes in living situations (ie, transitioning from living with family to residing in a nursing home).

This study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) reporting guideline for cohort studies. SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc) was used for all analyses, with P values < .05 considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Among 8141 participants with a 4-year median follow-up from 2010 to 2018, the mean age was 75 years, females accounted for 59.3% of participants, and 77.4% were non-Hispanic White. Individuals with consistently low household frequency were characterized by being older, predominantly male, non-Hispanic Black, less educated, with lower household income, a higher prevalence of former smoking, lower levels of vigorous and moderate physical activity, higher ADL scores, higher BMIs, a higher proportion of chronic comorbidities (excluding cancer), more depressive symptoms, and higher cognitive scores at baseline (all P values < .01) (Table 1). Lower housework frequency was cross-sectionally associated with lower cognitive scores at baseline (Supplemental Table 2). Experiencing changes in housework frequency from high to low and consistently low was associated with an additional decline of 0.079 (95% CI, −0.117 to −0.042) and 0.090 (95% CI, −0.126 to −0.054), respectively, during the 8-year follow-up compared with those who had consistently high housework frequency. However, there was no statistically significant additional decline among adults experiencing low to high frequency (low to high vs consistently high, β = −0.027; 95% CI, −0.074 to 0.019; P = .252) (Table 2). Although changes in housework frequency were not cross-sectionally associated with lower cognitive scores at baseline among adults aged ≥ 80 years (Supplemental Table 3), individuals undergoing transitions from high to low and those with consistently low housework frequency experienced additional declines of 0.170 (95% CI, −0.271 to −0.069) and 0.145 (95% CI, −0.237 to −0.052) per year, respectively (Table 3). Participants who transitioned from high to low and those who consistently engaged in low housework frequency showed an additional annual decline of 0.041 (95% CI, −0.080 to −0.001) and 0.042 (95% CI, −0.081 to −0.003), respectively, among older adults aged 65–79 years. This association was similar across participants aged 65 to 79 years and those aged 80 years and older, as evidenced by a 3-way interaction term (P trend = .069, Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses

The outcomes of the authors’ sensitivity analyses consistently corroborated their primary findings. Analyses focused on individuals in the upper 50th percentile of baseline cognitive function (Supplemental Table 5, n = 4533), and those additionally adjusting for baseline cognitive scores (Supplemental Table 6) demonstrated negligible differences from the main findings, indicating that reverse causation is unlikely. Additionally, analyses limited to individuals who did not consistently do housework from 2008 to 2010 (n = 6824) showed similar results to the authors’ main findings (Supplemental Table 7), suggesting that their measure of changes in housework frequency remained robust even when considering specific subpopulations. After additionally adjusting for changes in living conditions, the results were consistent with the authors’ main findings (Supplemental Table 8).

Discussion

Utilizing longitudinal data from a nationally representative study of 8141 adults 65 years of age or older, changes in housework frequency from high to low or maintaining consistently low housework frequency over a 2-year period were linked to a faster decline in cognitive function during the 10-year follow-up period. Individuals experiencing changes in housework frequency from high to low or who maintained consistently low frequency were associated with significantly lower cognitive function and a higher rate of cognitive function decline compared with those maintaining a consistently high frequency. These findings suggest that an individual’s perceived frequency of household tasks, regardless of exact caloric cost, signals meaningful engagement that is predictive of later cognitive health. The results also showed that doing housework in later life was consistently beneficial for cognitive function, regardless of age and gender.

This study focused on how changes in housework frequency may slow cognitive function decline. Although much research on physical activities has focused on leisure-time activities, such as walking, cycling, sports, and active recreation, 6–12,28 these studies overlooked or combined housework with recreational activities. Also, these studies assessed physical activity at a single time point or focused on intense physical training interventions, delivered in well-controlled settings, and were often short in duration (eg, 8–12 weeks). 9,14,29–31 In this study, the authors assessed the cognitive benefits of changes in housework frequency among older adults in a real-world setting. By accounting for the time-varying nature of housework frequency, results from this study demonstrated that participating in housework at a consistently low frequency or changing from high to low frequency was associated with significantly worse cognitive function. These findings align with previous studies based on other countries or cross-sectional designs. 16–18 Analyzing changes over a specific timeframe enables exploration of how short-term alterations may contribute to cognitive function trajectories in older adults, thereby offering further evidence for a causal relationship.

Furthermore, the authors found that the cognitive benefit of consistently high housework frequency can be translated to a 2-year delay in cognitive decline compared with those who had a consistently low housework frequency. Taken together, these results contribute robust empirical evidence to the emerging literature that participating in housework may be a viable approach to prevent the development of ADRD. The observed association is biologically plausible: inactive housework is considered to be an unhealthy coping behavior associated with higher likelihood of developing hypertension, 32 stroke, 33 heart disease, 34 and depression, 35 which are all key factors contributing to dementia risk and faster cognitive decline in older adults. 6 Moreover, housework comprises tasks that require both physical and cognitive engagement, including planning and organizing activities—both of which provide cognitive benefits for older adults. 36,37 Previous studies also revealed that combined cognitive and physical exercise is beneficial to cognition, indicating cognitive exercise and physical exercise may synergize each other’s beneficial effects. 38 Completing daily tasks may also provide a sense of accomplishment and contribute to a positive self-identity. 39 Recognition from others for these contributions can, as well, enhance self-esteem and a sense of purpose. 39

The results from this study showed that the benefits of increasing housework frequency or maintaining a high frequency on cognitive function did not significantly differ by age and gender. One possible explanation is that, similar to educational level, being active in housework influences cognition through cognitive reserve, meaning individuals who engage in housework can process and retain new information in their minds, which helps them maintain typical cognitive performance even in the presence of deteriorating age-related pathology. 40 This may hold true irrespective of age or gender. Numerous studies have demonstrated that being female, belonging to racial and ethnic minority (non-White) groups, and having low socioeconomic status are associated with a disproportionate increase in ADRD in older adults. 41–43 Therefore, promoting household participation may be an effective real-world public health strategy to promote cognitive aging at a population level.

Despite the potential cognitive benefits of high housework frequency, this study reveals that fewer than half of the participants were highly engaged in these activities. Therefore, it will be essential to develop programs and policies to further promote housework among older adults. Integrating housework into existing public physical activity campaigns through posters, websites, videos, and interactive tools could be an effective strategy. Moreover, given their influential role, health care professionals have a unique opportunity to recommend housework as a simple and accessible form of physical activity, particularly for patients facing challenges in participating in more traditional exercise regimens. Future research could further delineate the specific content, modality, intensity, and duration of housework that is both cognitively beneficial and appealing to older adults.

Strengths and limitations

There are several strengths in this study. First, the authors used a large, national, and representative sample of older US adults, providing enough statistical power as well as a relatively good generalizability of the results. Second, the longitudinal analysis added causal evidence to the relationship between housework and cognition by considering the temporal relationship between the two. Additionally, the authors used the change in housework frequency as the exposure in the analysis. Moreover, we adjusted for a range of covariates to adjust for potential confounders in the relationship between housework frequency and cognition. The covariates cover demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, psychosocial and behavioral resources, and health-related factors.

Several limitations should be noted for this study. First, although the median follow-up time in this longitudinal study was 4 years, aiming to tease apart the possibility that changes in housework frequency are attributed to subclinical cognitive decline, the reverse causation could still exist, considering the neuropathological change in dementia happens decades before symptom onset. The authors tried to rule out reverse causation in the sensitivity analysis, and the results corroborate the main findings. Additionally, the authors’ prospective study design with a median follow-up of 4 years could still help clarify the potential effect of housework frequency on cognition. Thus, this study could still provide some preventative implications for delaying cognitive decline by doing housework more frequently. Second, the self-reported questionnaire in assessing housework might be subject to bias. Third, a majority of the participants were non-Hispanic White individuals, limiting the generalizability of the study and calling for more diverse studies in the future. The fourth limitation is that the excluded sample tended to be younger but had lower cognitive scores than the analytical sample, which might lead to some selection bias. Fifth, the measures for cognitive function in HRS were relatively crude and lacked direct clinical assessment. Finally, other potential confounders—such as personality, health literacy, and family history of ADRD—were unavailable in the current data and were thus not included in the analyses.

Conclusion

Maintaining consistently high levels of perceived housework engagement or transitioning from low to high frequency in later life were linked to improved cognitive function, irrespective of gender and age. This study presents new evidence highlighting that perceived regular housework was associated with better subsequent cognitive function and underscores the idea that it is never too late to initiate such activities among older adults.
