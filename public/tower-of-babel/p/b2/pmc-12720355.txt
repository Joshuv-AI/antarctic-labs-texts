Interactome Quantitation Reveals Non-Energetic Mitochondrial Roles in Cell Type Specialization in Murine Kidney
Authors: Anna A Bakhtina, Matthew D Campbell, Britta D Sibley, Monica Sanchez-Contreras, Andrew Keller, Mariya T Sweetwyne, James E Bruce
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEvolution of multicellular life forms has involved adaptation of organs that consist of multiple cell types, each with unique functional properties that as a collection, achieve complex organ function. Since each cell type is adapted to deliver specific functionality within the context of an organ, knowledge on functional landscapes occupied by individual cell types could improve comprehension of organ function at the molecular level. In kidney, podocytes and tubules are 2 cell types of the nephron, each with vastly different functional roles. Podocytes envelop the blood vessels in the glomerulus and act as filters while tubules, located downstream of the glomerulus, are responsible for reabsorption of important nutrients. Mitochondria hold a critical and well-studied role in tubules due to the high energetic requirements required to fulfill their function. In podocytes however, questions remain regarding the relevance of mitochondrial function in both normal physiology and pathology. Quantitative cross-linking mass spectrometry and proteomics together with a transgenic mitochondrial tagging strategy were used to investigate kidney cell-type specificity of mitochondria. These efforts revealed that despite similarities of podocyte and tubule mitochondrial proteomes, each contain unique features corresponding to known distinct functional roles. These include increased demand for energy production through the tricarboxylic acid cycle in tubules and increased detoxification demand in podocytes. Moreover, tubule and podocyte mitochondrial interactome differences revealed additional cell-type specific functional insights with alterations in betaine metabolism, lysine degradation, and other pathways not regulated through proteome abundance levels. Most importantly, these efforts illustrate that cell specific mitochondrial interactome differences within an organ can now be visualized. Therefore, this approach can generally be used to map cell-specific mitochondrial changes in disease, aging or even with therapy to better understand the roles and contributions of each cell type in normal physiology and pathology within an organ in ways not previously possible.Keywords: cross-linking mass spectrometry, mitochondria, kidney, podocyte, tubules, interactome

Large-scale biological studies such as genomics, transcriptomics and proteomics have advanced studies on cell type heterogeneity that were made possible by developments in single cell technologies (1, 2, 3, 4). Interactomics, which involves the study of intra- and inter-protein interactions on a systems-level, usually with cross-linking mass spectrometry (XL-MS), has inherent constraints that make single cell studies particularly challenging. Cross-linked peptides or “cross-links” are formed by addition of a cross-linking reagent reactive toward specific protein residues (most commonly lysine amino acids) within proteins. As such, in vivo or in situ XL-MS studies where cross-linkers are applied to intact tissues, cells or organelles often result in low yield of cross-links due to the high lysine and low cross-linker concentration levels inside cells. This requires relatively large protein input to produce detectable cross-link signals (5). Cell type-specific protein-protein interactions in cultured cell lines have been studied by other means, for example AP-MS and interactome characterization of several mouse tissues by SEC-MS has recently reported (6). However, changes in protein-protein interactions within cell types that coexist within an organ or tissue of high cellular complexity have not previously been identified. Advancing interactome studies to the quantitative level (i.e., finding differences that are not just present/absent but have a more subtle variation) is an even bigger challenge needed to better comprehend cellular functional divergence (7). Quantitative XL-MS (qXL-MS) allows for the detection of changes that are driven by interaction (protein-protein and protein-ligand), conformation, and lysine post-translational modification (PTM) driven. Combining this approach with implementation of data independent acquisition (DIA)-based quantitative proteome comparisons further revealed interactome changes that can promote cell type functional specialization and are independent of protein abundance level regulation. Thus, quantitative interactome comparisons offer unique insight into molecular changes important for defining cellular functional landscapes such as within the multiple unique and disparate cell types of the kidney nephron.

Here, we report the first quantitative comparison of mitochondrial interactomes of two disparate cell types originating in the same organ substructure: podocytes and tubule cells of the kidney nephron (Fig. 1A). Kidney tubules comprise at least a dozen unique kidney epithelial cell types, which are mitochondrially dependent due to high energetic demand to fuel active resorption of nutrients into the blood and secretion of excess nutrients, metabolites, and toxins into the urine. Because tubule cell reliance on mitochondria is well studied, and because these cells comprise more than 90% of the kidney cortex (8), their mitochondria are often described interchangeably as the ‘kidney’ mitochondria writ large, supplanting our understanding of mitochondrial functions in less abundant renal cell types. Podocytes are also unique kidney epithelial cells located within the nephron glomerulus but unlike tubule cells, podocytes make up less than 2 to 3% of cells in the kidney cortex and are considerably less mitochondrially endowed (9). The main function of podocytes is to maintain their elaborate branching ‘foot process’ structure, which is required for formation of the kidney filtration slit diaphragm. Mitochondrial dysfunction and damage are detected in both tubules and podocytes with kidney diseases of various etiologies and in aging (9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17). In tubules, mitochondrial injury is known to be a driver of acute kidney injury progression (10, 12, 14, 18, 19). In podocytes however, the extent to which mitochondrial damage is the result or cause of disease progression has been difficult to parse because less is known about the reliance of podocytes on their mitochondria.

Many studies have shown that mitochondria contain functionally diverse populations, not only between cell types, but even within cytoplasmic regions within a cell (20, 21, 22). To address the challenges of separating podocyte mitochondria from those of tubules we took advantage of novel mouse models that express hemagglutinin (HA) tags on kidney cellular mitochondrial outer membrane proteins with cell type specificity for either podocytes or a subset of tubule cells (23) thus allowing for rapid isolation of cell type-specific mitochondria from the whole kidney. Our previous work using these mice showed that isolated mitochondria from either podocytes or tubule cells demonstrate a significant functional difference in respiratory capacity of mitochondria between cell types (23) and yet, the interactome differences between these mitochondrial populations remained a mystery.

To determine whether functional differences could be codified into the mitochondria themselves, we used an applied approach that combines DIA proteomics, qXL-MS technologies based on isobaric quantitative protein interaction reporter (iqPIR) (24) reagents and the HA-tag isolated mitochondria. Demonstrating the unique insights generated by this approach, pathway analysis of mitochondrial proteome differences between podocytes and tubules was limited to only 45 mitochondrial proteins observed with significant differential abundance levels. On the other hand, thousands of differing protein cross-links was identified. Using proteins with differential cross-link abundance levels for enrichment analysis enabled identification of several pathways that were differentially regulated including betaine metabolism, lysine metabolism, and tryptophan metabolism. Furthermore, we show how quantitative interactomics can be used to predict different activity levels of an enzyme even when the total protein abundance is the same between cell types. Together, these results indicate that mitochondria play important and distinct roles in podocytes compared to tubules and this specialization is mediated at least in part, at the interactome level. These efforts reveal the first observed quantitative mitochondrial interactome differences that underpin distinct cellular functional roles driven by mitochondria functions between cell types of a single organ.

Experimental Procedures

Animal Care

All strains of mice used in this study are on a C57BL/6 background. Founding breeder mice used to produce all genotypes of mice included in this experiment were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. The NPHS2-cre transgene was introduced into the colony with mice of the B6.Cg-Tg(NPHS2-cre)295Lbh/J strain (The Jackson Laboratory, strain #008205), the CDH16-cre transgene with mice of the B6.Cg-Tg(Cdh16-cre)91Igr/J strain (The Jackson Laboratory, strain #012237), and the MITO-HA mutation with mice of the B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm1(CAG-EGFP)Brsy/J strain (The Jackson Laboratory, strain #032290). Mice were housed at 20 °C under diurnal conditions with ad libitum food and water in an AAALAC-accredited facility with supervision by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Washington. Genotypes of all animals in this study were obtained through the automated genotyping service provided by TransnetYX. Protocol (PROTO202000161) has been approved by the IRB.

Cell-Specific MACS HA-Pulldown for Mitochondrial Isolation

Mice were sacrificed, and kidneys removed, decapsulated on a cold marble block, and weighed before being placed into ice-cold potassium-based phosphate buffered saline. 100 mg of tissue per column was weighed and then placed into 1 ml of potassium-based phosphate buffered saline in a glass Dounce homogenizer on ice and ground to a slurry. Tissue homogenates were poured through a 100 μM cell strainer to remove large debris and then mitochondria were isolated with the MACS column system (Milteny,) using a proprietary kit designed specifically for mitochondrial isolation and following all instructions with two exceptions. First, to isolate HA-tagged mitochondria, the mitochondrial kit components were used, but with Milteny anti-HA beads substituted in place of the TOMM22 beads. Second, all bead incubations were performed for 45 min. After immunoprecipitation of mitochondria following kit protocol, mitochondria pellets are washed twice and resuspended in 1.5 mM EGTA, 3 mM MgCl2-6H2O, 10 mM KH2PO4, 20 mM Hepes, 110 mM Sucrose, 100 mM Mannitol, 60 mM K-MES, pH 7.1. For podocyte mitochondria, two columns were run to ensure enough mitochondria were collected for cross-linking. For tubule isolations, one column yields sufficient mitochondria. Following the modified kit protocol, time from animal euthanasia to isolated respiring mitochondria is ∼90 min.

Cross-Linking of Mitochondria

Mitochondria were cross-linked and processed as reported before (25). Briefly, mitochondria were cross-linked with either reporter heavy (RH) iqPIR reagent (podocytes) or stump heavy (SH) reagent (tubules). Reaction was allowed to proceed for 30 min. Mitochondria were then lysed in 8 M urea, sonicated, reduced with tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine and alkylated with iodoacetamide. Protein content was measured with Bradford assay and then protein from podocytes and tubules were mixed pairwise in 1:1 to maximize total protein per pair (podocyte sample with highest protein amount was mixed with tubule sample with highest protein amount and so on). Mixed samples were digested overnight with trypsin and cleaned up using c18 SepPak coloumns (Waters). Peptides were then fractionated using a strong cation exchange column (Luna column, Agilent HPLC) and fractions containing cross-links or deadends, that are attached to the cross-linker at only one end, were used for avidin capture. Fractions were then separated on Aquity nanoHPLC (Waters) and analyzed on a Q-extactive plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific). The data was acquired in data dependent acquisition mode with top five peaks with charge >3 chosen for MS2.

Data Processing and Analysis

Mass spectrometry data was then searched with Mango software (https://brucelab.gs.washington.edu/software.html) to identify spectra containing cross-linked peptides (26). The spectra was then searched with Comet engine against MitoCarta3.0 3.0 database (1140 genes, released in January 2021) or whole mouse database and cross-link identifications verified with XLinkProphet (Uniprot, downloaded on 092319) (27, 28). Peptide mass tolerance was set to 20 ppm, isotope error was set to 5. Fragment mass tolerance was set to 0.02 Da. Trypsin was selected as the enzyme with up to two missed cleavages allowed. Methionine oxidation (+15.9949) and iqPIR stump (+197.032422 with −2.00671 possible offset for the second channel) were set as variable modifications. Cysteine carbamidomethylation (+57) was set as fixed modification. False discovery rate was set to 1% on PSM, peptide and cross-link level. Cross-links and deadends in each podocyte/tubule biological replicate pair were then quantified with iqPIR algorithm to produce log2 ratios and cross-link one-sample t test p-values.

Each cross-link p-value represents the probability of observing the reported ratio based on the observed quantified iqPIR ions, assuming the null hypothesis is true (24), that isthat the actual log 2 (ratio) is 0 and the reported value is by random chance. The larger the number of quantified iqPIR ions, and the better the agreement of each ratio determination from each iqPIR-labeled species, the smaller the p-value and lower the likelihood that the null hypothesis is true for that biological comparison.

Combined biological replicate cross-link and deadend log2ratios were then calculated by including observed quantified iqPIR ions of all scans of all biological replicates. They thus indicate the likelihood that the log2ratios are 0 with unchanged levels in the analyzed podocyte versus tubule samples.

To identify cross-link level changes that hold significance for meaningful podocyte/tubule mitochondrial differences, further filtering was done with combined ratios from all replicates. This filtering required a minimum of five quantified ions from at least two of the three biological replicate pair samples and with 95% confidence of 0.5 or less. Thus random biological variation that may affect podocyte or tubule cross-link levels and spuriously yield a log2 ratios far from 0 are excluded by these filters. t test p-values are then calculated on the combined filtered data. Cross-link IDs and associated quantitation were then uploaded into XLinkDB for structure mapping, further analysis and visualization (29). XLinkDB formatted table was exported for downstream analysis in R using tidyverse environment (30).

Cross-links ratio was filtered to be considered as confidently quantified as follows. At least five ions contribution to the ratio, 95% confidence no more than 0.5 and no more than one missing value in three biological replicates. Thus, cross-links quantified in only a single biological replicate Podocyte/tubule pair sample or with large variations in observed ratios from multiple biological replicates are removed from further consideration with these filters. Pathway enrichment analysis for both XL and DIA data was performed using STRING (https://string-db.org/). The strength indicated in the figures is defined as follows: “Log10 (observed/expected). This measure describes how large the enrichment effect is. It’s the ratio between i) the number of proteins in your network that are annotated with a term and ii) the number of proteins that we expect to be annotated with this term in a random network of the same size.”

DIA Based Protein Quantitation

After cross-linked mitochondrial samples were lysed and alkylated, 20 μg of each sample was set aside for whole proteome quantitative analysis. The samples were digested overnight at 37 °C with trypsin, peptides were then cleaned up using c18 SepPak columns (Waters), dried down, and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid to a final concentration of 0.5 μg/μl. DIA data was acquired and analyzed as reported before (31, 32). Briefly, QExactive plus (Thermo Fischer Scientific) coupled to Waters Acquity nano LC system fitted with 30 cm c18 column and 2 cm c8 trap column was used). A pooled sample was made by mixing all samples in equal amounts. Six gas phases fractionated narrow window (4 m/z) libraries were then acquired using the pooled sample. Followed by wide window (24 m/z) DIA acquisitions for each sample. Acquisition parameters were as follows. MS1 scan resolution was set to 35,000, max IT to 60 msec and AGC to 1e6. MS2 scans were acquired at 17,500 resolution with 60 msec max IT and AGC of 1e6. Loop count was set 25 with 27 NCE and default charge of 3.

DIA Data Analysis

Raw files were converted to MZML files and overlapping windows deconvoluted in MSConvert from ProteoWizard suite (ver. 3.0.21291). The chromatographic libraries were constructed in EncyclopeDIA’s (ver. 0.9.0) Walnut module using gas phase fractionated runs searched against mouse MitoCarta3.0 database (1140 entries) or whole mouse database downloaded from Uniprot (downloaded 092319) with 10 ppm mass tolerance for both precursors and fragments and HCD-only fragmentation (33). Trypsin was selected as enzyme, C + 57 (carbamidomethyl) was selected as fixed modification. Number of quantitative ions was set to 5. Wide window runs were searched against the libraries with same parameters. False discovery rate was set to 1% on PSM, peptide and protein levels. Table with protein quantitation was then imported into R for downstream analysis.

Glutamate Dehydrogenase Assay

Mitochondria were either prepared from fresh isolations or thawed on ice from frozen preparations and 25 μg from each sample was prepared for glutamate dehydrogenase assay according to manufacturer instructions (Millipore Sigma MAK099). The absorbance at 450 nm was then measured every 2 min for 4 h on Cytation 5 (Biotek) in 96-well plate. The data was then imported into R for further analysis. Half-time absorbances were calculated for each sample as a time of the first measurement after half maximum of each sample’s absorbance is reached.

Liver Mitochondria Treatment with ADP/GTP and Cross-Linking

Liver mitochondria were isolated as described in Fezza et al (34). Briefly, tissue was homogenized in glass homogenizer, cellular debris was cleared by centrifugation at 800g at 4 °C and mitochondrial pellet was isolated by centrifugation at 10,000g. Mitochondria were washed in isolation buffer and stored in −80 °C until cross-linking experiments. For treatments and cross-linking experiments mitochondria were thawed on ice and split into pools. Substrates and inhibitors/activators were added as following: (1) only ADP, (2) only GTP, (3) glutamate, NAD+ and ADP, (4) glutamate, NAD+ and GTP and (5) glutamate, NAD+, ADP and GTP. Mitochondria were then cross-linked as described for podocyte and tubule mitochondria cross-linking. Samples were processed and analyzed in a similar fashion.

Experimental Design and Statistical Rationale

Three pairs of biological replicate samples were separately combined together for quantitative XL-MS. Resulting cross-link log2ratios of each pair were calculated from their multiple independently quantified peptides and fragment ions identified in one or more MS2 scans. Ion log2ratios from biological replicate pairs were furthermore combined together into a single combined log2ratio (see above). These log2ratios routinely include numbers of technical and biological replicate ion log2ratios ranging to excess of 100 providing great statistical power to identify those log2ratios different from 0 using a one-sample t test p-value. Data was filtered for maximum 95% confidence of 0.5 and minimum four replicate ion ratios.

For DIA quantitative proteomics we have analyzed three biological replicates of podocyte pull-down and three biological replicates of tubule pull-down (same samples as used for qXL-MS) for a total of six samples. With three replicates per group, we can use statistical methods like the Welch’s t test to compare protein abundances between the two groups. Implementing multiple testing corrections ensures that we control over the number of false discoveries.

Results and Discussion

Quantification of Proteome Differences Between Podocyte and Tubule Mitochondria

Mitochondria were isolated from mouse kidneys with HA-based affinity enrichment from tagged proteins expressed in either podocyte (NPHS2-mtHA) or tubule (CDH16-mtHA) mitochondria using an anti-HA affinity purification column (Fig. 1B). Protein quantification revealed lower protein abundance in podocyte versus tubule mitochondria isolations, as was expected based on the lower number of podocytes to tubule cells in the kidney, and significantly higher mitochondrial abundance in tubule cells. To complement quantitative interactome data, we acquired proteome measurements for the same samples utilizing a DIA-based quantitative proteomics workflow (Supplemental Table S1) (31, 32). Having information about protein level changes allows us to distinguish cross-links that are different due to changes in protein abundance from the cross-links that are different due to protein-protein interactions, conformations, protein-ligand interactions or PTMs. Utilizing chromatographic libraries and DIA based quantitation we identified and quantified 779 protein groups searching against mitochondrial proteome database MitoCarta3.0 (33). Overall, mitochondrial proteomes of tubules and podocytes are similar (although separated in the principal component analysis plot, Fig. 1C) with only 55 proteins out of 779 exhibiting significantly different abundance levels (Benjamin-Hochberg corrected p-value <0.05, Fig. 1D, Supplemental Table S2). This result is somewhat surprising considering that intact tubules have a significantly higher basal respiration rate than do podocytes (9). This could partially be explained by the fact that we normalize mitochondrial protein amounts, and mitochondrial density is lower in podocytes (35). However, this indicates that even if podocyte mitochondrial content is lower, the mitochondrial proteomes are generally similar in podocytes and tubule cells. Due to the observation of only 27 and 28 proteins significantly increased in podocytes and tubule cells, respectively, only limited meaningful pathway enrichment analysis resulted, including upregulation of tricarboxylic acid cycle and aerobic respiration in tubule mitochondria, and upregulated cellular detoxification in podocytes (Fig. 1, E and F, Supplemental Fig. S1, A and B). While the number of differential proteins is low for an extensive pathway analysis, these results generally agree with known functions of tubules and podocytes. Tubules have high energy demand due to active transport across the cell membrane. Podocytes are located at the interface with blood to be filtered by the kidney, so a robust antioxidant system that can help counteract cytotoxicity is needed.

Quantified Interactome Differences are Much More Abundant than Proteome Differences Between Podocyte and Tubule Mitochondria

Mitochondria from podocytes and tubule cells were cross-linked with RH and SH iqPIR cross-linkers, respectively. Combination of RH and SH samples enables pairwise comparison of one podocyte sample to one tubule sample. Using the same samples analyzed by DIA, podocyte mitochondria isolation samples from each of three NPHS2-mtHA mice were combined with tubule mitochondria samples each from one of three CDH16-mtHA mice, creating three biological replicate pairs (P1 = pod1/tub1, P2 = pod2/tub2, P3 = pod3/tub3) in a 1:1 ratio (Fig. 1G, Supplemental Table S3). Each combined sample was processed and analyzed with mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS2). To identify cross-links from mitochondrial samples, the data was searched against the mitochondrial database MitoCarta3.0 (33). Unique cross-linked peptides are referred to as cross-links throughout the rest of the manuscript. A previously published iqPIR algorithm (24) was applied to calculate log2 ratios as well as p values for observed cross-link levels (Pod/Tub) based on all detected iqPIR isotopic labeled released peptide and peptide fragment ions. This allowed for relative quantitation of cross-link levels within and between proteins in all three podocyte/tubule sample pairs (Supplemental Table S2). A total of 5119 cross-links were identified and of these, 835, 1994 and 1996 were confidently quantified in P1, P2 and P3 respectively, with four or more ions contributing and a 95% confidence interval of the log2 ratio distributions 0.5 or less (Supplemental Fig. S1B). A quarter of cross-links confidently identified are inter-links (cross-links formed between lysines from two distinct proteins) and the rest are intra-links originating from the same protein. Although not conveying information about protein-protein interactions directly, these cross-links can change due to differences in conformations, ligand binding or PTMs (Fig. 1H). There was an instrument issue during fractionation of P3 cross-links resulting in the lower number of IDs, but since the mitochondria from podocytes and tubules for pairs is mixed prior to digestion; the ratios for cross-links are preserved. We then calculated a combined ratio based on all ions from all replicates and filtered combined ratios requiring at least two individual quantified ratios which resulted in 2040 total combined ion ratios. The combined ion ratios include all of the quantified species from all biological replicates and their associated t test p-values indicate the probability that the ion log2ratios are actually 0, that isunchanged in the analyzed podocyte versus tubule samples. Log2 ratios for each pair are approximately normally distributed with the majority of cross-links not changing (Supplemental Fig. S1C). Reproducibility among biological replicates was observed as indicated by R2 values of 0.80, 0.82 and 0.84 (Fig. 1I). Mitochondrial cross-links detected in at least two out of three pairs show reproducible ratios across the pairs (Fig. 1J). Applying Student’s t test with Bonferroni correction, we were able to identify 366 mitochondrial protein cross-links with ratios different from 0 (|log2 ratio| ≥0.5) with statistical significance (adj. p-value ≤0.01) (Fig. 1K).

Cross-Linked Peptides with Significant Abundance Level Differences Identify Altered Mitochondrial Pathways in Podocytes and Tubule Cells

While mitochondrial proteome comparisons show few protein abundance level differences, quantitative interactome data acquired in this study further extend knowledge of functional specialization through observed differences in mitochondrial protein interactions and conformations, even for protein levels that are unaltered. Proteins with observed significantly altered cross-link levels (both increased and decreased in podocyte, compared to tubule, mitochondria) were used for pathway enrichment analysis. From this investigation, the top identified gene ontology biological process was betaine metabolic process (Fig. 2A). Cross-links belonging to nine proteins (Fig. 2B) from this pathway have been identified with both significantly increased and decreased levels in podocytes (Fig. 2C). Moreover, none of the proteins in this pathway show significantly changed abundance levels (OCTC, peroxisomal carnitine O-octanoyltransferase, exhibited the largest change with log2 FC = 0.76 and adjusted p-value = 0.07), making the identification of this functional specialization not possible based on proteome data alone (Fig. 2D). Betaine (also known as trimethylglycine) is an amino acid that is important for osmotic regulation in kidney medulla, regulation of homocysteine levels in blood and also possesses anti-oxidant properties (36, 37, 38). The role of betaine metabolism in the podocyte is less explored, however, since osmotic pressure at the glomerulus acts counter to glomerular filtration by podocytes, altered betaine pathways could be consistent with podocyte specialization. In kidney, it has been shown that supplementation with betaine can have a protective effect during chronic kidney disease and doxorubicin induced toxicity, both characterized by moderate to severe podocytopathy (39, 40). Further investigation of differences in betaine processing between podocytes and tubules could shed light on protective mechanisms of betaine supplementation and its connection to podocytes.

Similarly, KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) analysis of the interactome data uniquely revealed the existence of altered lysine degradation pathways in podocytes (Fig. 2E, Supplemental Fig. S2, A–C). The kidney plays an integral role in lysine metabolism (41) and among urinary metabolites assessed for association with genetic variants and incident chronic kidney disease, aminobutyric acid and lysine were observed with the most significant p values (42). The effect of lysine supplementation on the kidney proteome has been investigated and one of the enzymes highlighted is alpha-aminoadipic semialdehyde synthase (AASS), which catalyzes the first step in lysine degradation through ε-deamination (41). AASS is composed of two domains: lysine-2-oxoglutarate reductase and saccharopine dehydrogenase (43). Protein levels of AASS are unchanged between podocytes and tubule mitochondria (Fig. 2F); however AASS cross-link levels differed significantly in podocytes and tubules (Fig. 2G) and a subset of cross-links were detected with significantly increased levels in podocyte mitochondria. Mapping these four links on structures of AASS lysine-2-oxoglutarate reductase and saccharopine dehydrogenase domains the link (K728-K732) is most consistent with a homodimeric link, while another link (K728-K739) is possible as both an intra- or homodimeric link. Similarly, although possible as an intralink, the K253-K70 cross-link is most consistent with a homodimeric link. Only the K253-K259 cross-link is more likely to be an intralink. Taken together, the interactome data indicate that despite no change in protein levels in podocyte mitochondria AASS proteins are conformationally altered and exist with increased levels of assembled multimers. Since AASS was reported to be active only as oligomers (44), the increased AASS multimer cross-link levels in podocyte mitochondria could reflect increased enzymatic activity and suggest a larger role of podocytes in lysine degradation. The majority of studies of the role of lysine in kidney physiology and pathology use whole kidney tissue or isolated proximal tubules. Thus, quantitative comparisons of tubule and podocyte mitochondrial interactomes and proteomes could help guide improved studies of lysine metabolism at higher resolution in kidney.

Proteins in lysine degradation are also part of tryptophan metabolism with an addition of several other proteins specific to tryptophan metabolism (Fig. 2E, Supplemental Fig. S2, D and E). One of the proteins that is in the tryptophan, but not in the lysine degradation pathway, is kynurine 3-mono-oxygenase (KMO). KMO is one of the few proteins differentially expressed in podocyte and tubule mitochondria with higher abundance in podocytes based on our proteome data searched against MitoCarta3.0 (Fig. 2, H and I). Although high expression of KMO in proximal tubules on both transcript and protein level has been reported before, our data points to higher expression of KMO per mitochondria in podocytes (45). This could be explained by the fact that both single cell transcriptomics and kidney tissue immunohistochemistry would show higher KMO levels in tubules due to their higher overall mitochondrial content. It has been shown that loss of KMO causes proteinuria directly linked to podocytes (46). Tryptophan degradation metabolites have also been linked to renal failure (47). In both example proteins highlighted here, AASS and KMO, the podocyte-specific mitochondrial differences in either cross-links or abundance would not have been recognized without comparison of cell type isolated mitochondria and the combined analysis of DIA and X-link proteomics. Overall, this illustrates how this model and dataset is uniquely positioned to distinguish podocyte-specific mitochondrial biology from the otherwise high background of mitochondrial abundance imparted by tubule cells.

Glutamate Dehydrogenase Conformation and Activity Levels are Altered in Podocyte Compared to Tubule Mitochondria, Despite No Significant Change in Protein Levels

Glutamate dehydrogenase (DHE3), encoded by the Glud1 gene, is a mitochondrial matrix enzyme responsible for oxidative deamination of glutamate to produce alpha-ketoglutarate, a key tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediate that also participates in multiple signaling pathways related to cellular redox regulation, epigenetic processes, and inflammation response (Fig. 3A) (48, 49). The measured protein levels of DHE3 in podocyte and tubule mitochondria show no significant difference (Fig. 3B); however, DHE3 cross-link levels were observed with significantly differing levels in podocytes and tubule mitochondria (Fig. 3C). These results suggest that despite no DHE3 protein level differences between podocyte and tubule mitochondria, the enzyme is conformationally altered in these 2 cell types. Notably, the subset of cross-links that are significantly decreased in podocyte mitochondria are localized to the antenna regions of hexameric DHE3 (Fig. 3D). We previously observed these same cross-links (inter-links between residues 477 and 480 on adjacent subunits of DHE3) decreased in female mouse muscle mitochondria with age (25). Moreover, in the same study we showed that the antenna cross-link levels increase after treating mitochondria with ADP, a well-known activator of DHE3 activity (50) which indicates these cross-links are reflective of enzymatic state of DHE3 activity regulated by ADP. To further investigate DHE3 activity in podocyte and tubule mitochondria, we employed a DHE3 activity assay that measures NADH absorbance produced during the DHE3 oxidative deamination reaction, using mitochondria from the same mice as were cross-linked, as well as three additional pairs of animals. As predicted by antenna cross-link levels, time to reach half maximum of the NADH absorbance in podocytes mitochondria was longer compared to those from tubules, indicating lower DHE3 activity in podocytes (Fig. 3E, Supplemental Fig. S3A, Supplemental Table S4). DHE3 is an important mitochondrial enzyme that operates as a metabolic switch by employing an intricate regulatory system that, curiously, coevolved with the antenna (48). To investigate a possible underlying mechanism driving increased activity we also performed activity assays and quantitative cross-linking experiments treating mouse liver (which has the highest expression of DHE3 for maximum depth of coverage) mitochondria with either a DHE3 activator (ADP), inhibitor (GTP), or both (Fig. 3F, Supplemental Fig. S3B, Supplemental Table S5). The antenna cross-links increased in response to ADP or ADP + GTP treatment but not to GTP treatment alone. Additionally, no change in antenna links was observed with either ADP or GTP when no substrates were added (the mitochondria were previously frozen, so it is expected that due to membrane rupture, small molecules like glutamate and cofactor NAD+ would be lost). These results indicate that antenna cross-links are responsive to ADP activation but not to GTP inhibition, indicating that differences in DHE3 activity in podocytes in tubules might be driven by ADP differences between these cell types. These observed DHE3 interactome differences and measured enzyme activity differences reveal new molecular details on podocytes and tubule cell type specialization within the kidney.

Analysis of Total Cellular Proteome by Both Quantitative Proteomics and Interactomics Reveal Cell-Specific Differences in the Close Organelle/Protein Environment of Podocyte Versus Tubule Mitochondria

Our results in Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3 centered on proteins classified through the MitoCarta3.0, however, the HA-encoded affinity tag placed at the outer mitochondrial membrane may also allow for enrichment of organelles and proteins that have surface contact with mitochondria. To investigate how this background compares between podocyte and tubule mitochondria we searched both interactome and proteome data against the whole mouse database (Supplemental Tables S6 and S7). This resulted in quantitation of 2024 proteins with 943 exhibiting differential abundance levels (Fig. 4A), a much larger number than in our searches derived only from search with MitoCarta3.0. Because of the larger number of differential proteins, correlation between fold changes in proteins as measured by DIA proteomics and in Intralinks is higher for searches against whole mouse database compared to Mitocarta, but still relatively low (R2 = 0.50), as there is still a large subset of cross-links that change independently of protein levels (Supplemental Fig. S4). Pathway enrichment analysis with these ‘total’ proteins showed that the majority of proteins (95%) increased in tubule samples are mitochondrial (Fig. 4B), while proteins with higher levels in samples from podocytes are lysosomal (28%), cytosolic (79%) and cytoskeletal (18%) (Fig. 4C). Thus, mitochondrial pull-down from podocytes also includes a large amount of nonmitochondrial proteins. It is particularly interesting that lysosomal proteins represent a large portion of the nonmitochondrial proteins in podocyte mitochondrial isolation samples. For example, all detected components of the lysosomal proton pump, V-ATPase, had a significantly higher abundance in podocyte samples (Fig. 4D). This is in agreement with our previous work showing an abundance of lysosomal marker LAMP1 in podocyte, relative to tubule, mitochondria pull-down samples by immunoblotting (23). Therefore, because we began with mitochondrial enrichment, our approach identified not only mitochondrial proteome differences, but also differences in subcellular organelle interactions.

Analyzing cross-links detected in total pull-down proteins in our mitochondrial isolations (as opposed to MitoCarta3.0 proteins only) confirmed our DIA findings that mitochondria-organelle interactions differed between tubule and podocyte mitochondrial pulldowns. On a cross-link level we saw an increase in lysosomal proton pump cross-links (Supplemental Table S8), which agreed with our DIA results. Besides intra-links in lysosomal pump subunits VATA, VATE1, VATB2, inter-protein cross-links connecting the pump subunits were also detected and quantified, indicating that the intact lysosomal proton pump was present in the HA-affinity enriched podocytes samples (Fig. 4E). Taken together, these data suggest that podocyte mitochondria have more lysosomal contacts as indicated by an increase in lysosomal protein abundances and cross-links. Lysosomal clearance of damaged mitochondria is important for podocytes since podocytes are a nonrenewing cell type and exist on the frontline of filtration with exposure to toxic compounds. Therefore, adaptation of increased toxicity protective mechanisms, including mitochondrial quality control, would offer selective advantage and improved kidney function.

Discussion

This work is the first to demonstrate the ability to quantitatively compare interactomes of mitochondria originating from different cell types within a single organ. Within an organ or tissue, cells adapt specific functional roles needed to coordinate the diversity of purposeful attributes realized at the organ level. Deciphering how cells achieve this specialization, what factors disrupt or can restore balance are challenges that will require new approaches and future investigation. Mitochondria are most often considered “powerhouses of the cell” and provide ATP needed for a broad range of cellular functions. Modulation of mitochondrial density and networks are well-recognized contributors to cell type-specialization by maximizing ATP production for cell types with high energetic demands, such as kidney tubule cells. On the other hand, although tubule cells contain the majority of kidney mitochondria, podocytes are essential for glomerulus filtration function and are the locus of podocytopathies, at least some of which are driven by mitochondrial dysfunction (51). This suggests diversity exists in mitochondrial roles involved in kidney cell type specialization, in addition to that of meeting energetic cellular demands. Proteome and interactome studies were employed to gain insight on ways in which mitochondria in podocytes and tubule cells differ and help identify differential pathways that support cell type specialization. Both large-scale datasets reveal pathway differences in podocyte and tubule mitochondria consistent with known specialized function of these cell types. While podocyte and tubule cell mitochondria exhibit some, but not extensive, differences on the proteome abundance level, we observed significant differences on the interactome level indicating podocyte specialization in betaine metabolism, lysine and tryptophan degradation and glutamate metabolism. It bears noting that our data include only mitochondria from healthy, young, female animals and further studies that include sex, aging, and kidney disease models are still needed. Despite these limitations, using observed interactome differences in young female mice, pathways were identified that functionally differentiate podocyte and tubule cells but are otherwise, not readily identified in proteome data due to the overwhelming abundance of mitochondria in tubules relative to podocytes. Proving that these data can be used to make mechanistic predictions of cell-specific mitochondrial functions, we have discovered podocyte versus tubule mitochondria differential activity of glutamate dehydrogenase through cross-links in its antenna region and confirmed this by orthogonal assay. Data generated in this study are publicly available and could be used by the community to gain a deeper understanding in differences between mitochondrial biology of tubules and, in particular, the underexplored mitochondrial biology of podocytes. These changes in conformation, protein interaction and/or modification levels indicate that mitochondria can shape cell type specialization in ways other than energy production. Moreover, the observed results also suggest that cell type-specific affinity labeling and enrichment of mitochondria combined with qXL-MS can emerge as a general method to reveal mitochondrial differences in multiple cell types in any organ or tissue.
