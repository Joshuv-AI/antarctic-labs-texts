Background

Nodoventricular and nodofascicular accessory pathways (APs) represent uncommon variants of atrioventricular (AV) connections characterized by decremental conduction properties and proximal insertion within or near the AV node. First described anatomically by Mahaim and Winston, these pathways have historically been grouped under the term ‘Mahaim fibres’, although subsequent electrophysiological and surgical studies have demonstrated considerable heterogeneity in their anatomical and functional properties.1

Clinically, nodoventricular pathways often pose a diagnostic dilemma. Their decremental conduction and frequent sensitivity to adenosine may resemble AV nodal physiology, while their participation in re-entrant tachycardias can mimic both atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT) and orthodromic AV re-entrant tachycardia (AVRT).2 Furthermore, the absence of overt pre-excitation on surface electrocardiography may obscure recognition of an AP altogether.3

We present a case of a young man with recurrent supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) in whom careful electrophysiological assessment revealed a nodoventricular AP with left-sided extension, a rare configuration that further complicated the diagnostic process.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old male with no relevant past medical or family history was referred for electrophysiological evaluation due to recurrent episodes of paroxysmal SVT. Pre-procedural evaluation showed no structural heart disease with echocardiographic examination. The episodes had persisted for several years despite medical therapy with beta blockers, characterized by sudden start and cessation (‘on–off phenomenon’), lasting up to 45 min. During tachycardia, the patient experienced pronounced palpitations, cold sweating, and mild retrosternal pressure, without syncope or presyncope.

Baseline physical examination and resting 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) during sinus rhythm were unremarkable, with no evidence of pre-excitation. A documented episode of narrow-complex tachycardia with a relatively short RP interval had been recorded previously at another institution, prompting referral for invasive evaluation.

An electrophysiological study was performed using standard diagnostic catheters positioned in the coronary sinus (C.S), His bundle area, and right ventricular apex. Baseline intervals were within normal limits, and no evidence of dual AV nodal physiology was observed during atrial extra stimulation as there was no AV, ventriculoatrial (VA) jump, or echo beats.

Incremental ventricular pacing from the right and left ventricles demonstrated associated VA conduction with a concentric activation pattern and clear decremental properties (Figure 1A). The VA effective refractory period was prolonged (under 300 ms), consistent with nodal retrograde conduction. (Figure 1B). No VA jump was observed during programmed ventricular pacing. Adenosine induced complete anterograde and retrograde block. (Figure 2).

Taken together, these findings were inconsistent with AVNRT and traditional AVRT using a non-decremental AP. The most likely diagnosis was orthodromic AV re-entrant tachycardia utilizing a nodoventricular AP with decremental conduction and left-atrial extension. Nodofascicular AP can be excluded here because the tachycardia terminated by a fusion beat under ventricular stimulation in a transition zone, which means that the ventricle is a part of the tachycardia circuit. Permanent Junctional Reciprocating Tachycardia (PJRT) could also be excluded here because the VA time is relatively shorter in this case than the AV time, which is against the slowly conducting AP in PJRT.

Detailed programmed stimulation protocols and response patterns were essential in distinguishing this arrhythmia from AVNRT and conventional concealed AV APs, particularly through the assessment of decremental VA conduction, tachycardia termination mechanics, and adenosine response (see Supplementary material online, Table S1).

Following diagnostic confirmation, we decided to perform catheter ablation. Transseptal puncture was performed to allow left atrial access and mapping, after which a 4-mm tip ablation catheter (NaviStar; J&J MedTec, Irvine, CA, USA) was advanced and positioned along the posterolateral portion of the mitral annulus at the site showing the earliest atrial activation during tachycardia with obvious AP potential (Figure 5) using 3D Carto mapping system (J&J MedTec). Radiofrequency energy was applied at 40 W for approximately 60 s. The tachycardia terminated during energy delivery within 4 s and could not be re-induced thereafter, despite repeated programmed atrial and ventricular stimulation. No further evidence of AP conduction was observed within 20 min of waiting time, consistent with successful ablation.

Follow-up

The patient underwent follow-up for 6 months after the index procedure. Clinical status, symptom burden, rhythm documentation, and echocardiographic data were obtained. Rhythm surveillance included standard 12-lead electrocardiography and 7-day Holter ECG monitoring at 3 and 6 months.

Discussion

This case illustrates several important electrophysiological principles relevant to the diagnosis of rare APs.1 Nodoventricular pathways are defined by their proximal connection to the AV node and distal insertion into ventricular myocardium, typically near the septum. Unlike traditional AV APs, they typically demonstrate decremental conduction and sensitivity to adenosine, reflecting their nodal tissue composition.2

In the present case, the absence of dual AV nodal physiology and the termination of the tachycardia by a fusion ventricular beat in a transition zone during ventricular overdrive pacing without atrial capture excluded AVNRT and AT.4 Although a left lateral AP was initially suspected due to earliest atrial activation in the distal coronary sinus, the presence of concentric, decremental VA conduction and adenosine-induced bidirectional block was atypical for a conventional AV pathway.

Several reports have emphasized that nodoventricular pathways may masquerade as free-wall APs when they exhibit preferential atrial exit sites remote from their ventricular insertion.5 Surgical and mapping studies have previously demonstrated that pathways initially labelled as nodoventricular may in fact represent AV pathways like a concealed left AV AP with AV node–like conduction properties and atypical insertion sites.6 Nevertheless, the electrophysiological behaviour observed in this case—particularly adenosine sensitivity and node-like decremental conduction—strongly supports a nodoventricular mechanism.

Left-sided extension of nodoventricular pathways is distinctly uncommon. Most described pathways insert into the right ventricular septum, with the earliest ventricular activation near the right ventricular septum.7 The eccentric atrial activation pattern observed here suggests that while the proximal insertion was nodal, the retrograde atrial exit occurred along the left atrial myocardium, adding an additional layer of diagnostic complexity.5

PJRT also could be excluded here because the septal VA interval was relatively shorter than the AV interval, which is against the slowly conducting decremental AP in PJRT. Also, PJRT is usually associated with incessant tachycardia in younger children and is mostly associated with impaired LV function.8

This case underscores the importance of comprehensive pacing manoeuvres, careful analysis of activation sequences, and cautious interpretation of adenosine response. Recognition of nodoventricular pathways is crucial, as inappropriate ablation targeting the AV node carries a risk of iatrogenic AV block.9,10

Patient’s perspective

The patient reported significant relief and reassurance after the procedure, particularly given the long-standing history of symptoms and prior recurrence after ablation. He expressed satisfaction with the explanation of the mechanism and the structured follow-up for 6 months.

Conclusion

Nodoventricular APs remain an important but often overlooked cause of SVT. This case highlights a rare presentation involving left-sided extension and demonstrates how detailed electrophysiological assessment can distinguish this entity from AVNRT and other SVTs. Awareness of this mechanism is essential to ensure accurate diagnosis and safe therapeutic decision-making.
