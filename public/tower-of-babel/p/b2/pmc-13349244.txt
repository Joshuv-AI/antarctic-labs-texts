An Unusual Presentation of Juvenile Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis with Superoxide Dismutase 1 Mutation: Subacute Bulbar Palsy With Asymmetric Limb Weakness
Authors: Venkataraman Viswanathan, Guna Madhuri, Kumaresan Ganapathi, Nirmal Kumar
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We describe a 15-year-old male, previously healthy, who developed progressive dysarthria and dysphagia 3 months after a febrile illness. This was followed by asymmetric proximal weakness of both upper limbs predominant over left. Examination revealed multiple lower cranial nerve palsies, areflexia, fasciculations, and wasting over left deltoid muscle and normal sensation. Nerve conduction studies showed preserved compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes without conduction block, while electromyography (EMG) demonstrated left deltoid fasciculations. Genetic testing confirmed a pathogenic superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD 1) mutation.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder involving both upper and lower motor neurons. ALS has two typical presentations with the spinal form of disease characterized by muscle weakness and atrophy in limbs and trunk. The rarer presentation is with bulbar changes, affecting speech and swallowing musculature. Juvenile ALS (JALS), defined as onset before 25 years, is rare, accounting for <5% of ALS cases. Fused in Sarcoma gene (FUS) mutations are predominant in JALS. SOD1 mutations account for 20% of familial ALS cases or 2% of all ALS.[1] Mutations in alsin (ALS2), senataxin (SETX), and spatacsin (SPG11) genes have been associated with familial ALS.[2] Respiratory failure is the most common cause of death.[3]

Most of the JALS cases present with limb-onset weakness, and bulbar onset in adolescent age group is rare. We report a genetically confirmed case of bulbar-onset JALS due to SOD1 mutation, initially mimicking post-infectious neuropathy. SOD1 is a ubiquitously expressed copper/zinc metalloenzyme that converts reactive oxygen species to hydrogen peroxide and molecular oxygen.[4] The clinical phenotype is highly variable and patients with a particular SOD1 mutation show intrafamilial differences in the severity of symptoms and disease progression.[5]

A 15-year-old male, born out of third-degree consanguinity, with normal development and good school performance, presented with gradually progressive change in voice and difficulty in speech over 3 months, followed by difficulty in swallowing both solids and liquids equally. Over a month, he had regurgitation and pooling of secretions. He developed weakness of left upper limb, noticed in the form of difficulty in raising arm above the shoulder level. No history of difficulty was observed in performing activities of daily living. However, parents had noticed change in speed of writing with his right hand. There was no history of seizures, altered sensorium, bowel/bladder involvement, and sensory loss. A febrile illness lasting for a week was reported 3 months before symptom onset.

Vital signs were normal and higher mental functions were normal. Speech was unclear with nasal twang, but sentences could be formed. There was mild facial asymmetry with deviation of angle of mouth to right, incomplete eye closure on left side, and bilateral palatal weakness with absent gag reflex. Tongue showed no wasting or fasciculations. Drooping of left scapula was noticed. Motor examination revealed generalized wasting, left deltoid fasciculations, and reduced shoulder power. Deep tendon reflexes (DTR) were absent in all limbs; plantar responses were flexor bilaterally. Gait was normal. Sensory and cerebellar systems were intact.

With a diagnosis of atypical Guillain-Barre Syndrome with acute onset of areflexic flaccid paralysis (GBS), the child was given intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) but showed no benefit. High dose riboflavin and vitamin B12 supplementation were given in view of consideration of Fazio–Londe syndrome. The child appeared to be stable on follow-up 2 weeks later, while we were awaiting genetic report. The child passed away at home due to respiratory issues 2 weeks later.

Investigations showed normal basic blood workup. A lumbar puncture was then performed with normal biochemistry and cell count, and testing for infection was negative. Nerve conduction studies showed preserved CMAP amplitudes, no conduction block, normal distal latencies. EMG showed fasciculations in the left deltoid, and a neurogenic pattern. Magnetic resonance imaging brain and spine: normal. Whole exome sequencing revealed homozygous variant (c.43G>C) in SOD 1 gene, Exon 1. The variant has been classified as likely pathogenic [Figure 1].

This case is notable for its rare age of onset, bulbar predominant presentation, and misleading post-infectious history initially suggesting atypical GBS. The possibility of Fazio–Londe syndrome was initially considered given the progressive bulbar involvement at adolescent age and history of consanguinity. However, the absence of sensorineural hearing loss, the presence of early limb weakness with fasciculations, and confirmation of SOD1 mutation on genetic testing favored a diagnosis of JALS. Awareness of such atypical presentations is important to avoid misdiagnosis. JALS is a rapidly progressive rare neurodegenerative disorder with onset before 25 years. Among genetic causes, FUS mutations are more frequent, while SOD1 mutations are rarely reported in adolescents. Bulbar-onset ALS in this age group is even rarer and can mimic post infectious neuropathies.

This is a rare case of bulbar-onset JALS due to SOD1 mutation in a 15-year-old boy, initially misinterpreted as post-infectious neuropathy. This case emphasizes the clinical variability of JALS and highlights the need for early genetic evaluation in progressive motor syndromes of childhood and adolescence.
