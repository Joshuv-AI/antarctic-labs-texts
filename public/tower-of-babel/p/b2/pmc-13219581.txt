Exploring intermittent pneumatic compression parameters to optimize venous peak velocity and flow duration
Authors: Nutta Homdee, Chayapat Supachatwong, Benjawan Srisantithum, Chavarat Jarungvittayakon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a significant risk in patients with prolonged immobility. Intermittent pneumatic compression devices (IPCDs) are recommended for DVT prevention, yet optimal compression parameters remain poorly defined. This proof-of-concept pilot study evaluated the hemodynamic impact of varying IPCD compression pressures and sequences in healthy volunteers simulating lower-limb immobilization, using a programmable prototype with adjustable settings. Twelve participants underwent continuous Doppler ultrasound measurement of peak systolic velocity (PSV) in the common femoral vein during three compression modes: simultaneous 30 mmHg, simultaneous 50 mmHg, and sequential 30 mmHg. All modes significantly increased PSV compared to resting baseline (per-participant means, n = 12, paired t-tests). Simultaneous 30 mmHg produced the highest and most consistent augmentation (mean PSV 40.06 ± 1.53 cm/s; 90% increase from baseline; t(11) = 29.1, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 8.39). Simultaneous 50 mmHg showed significant but more variable augmentation (30.45 ± 6.33 cm/s; t(11) = 4.26, p = 0.001), with a dipping phenomenon in five participants consistent with possible venous occlusion at higher pressures. Sequential 30 mmHg produced the longest augmented flow duration (mean 4.49 s versus 1.85 s for simultaneous 30 mmHg), suggesting greater total venous volume displacement despite lower peak velocity. These findings provide a hemodynamic foundation for IPCD parameter optimization, with clinical validation in patient populations required before implementation.Keywords: Intermittent Pneumatic Compression Device (IPCD), Venous thrombosis, Hemodynamics, Mechanical prophylaxisSubject terms: Cardiology, Diseases, Engineering, Health care, Medical research, Physiology

Introduction

Intermittent pneumatic compression devices (IPCDs) reduce the risk of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) by augmenting venous blood flow in the lower limbs of immobilized patients. Immobilization often occurs in patients undergoing orthopedic surgeries such as hip replacements, knee replacements, and fracture repairs1. These patients are at a heightened risk of developing DVT due to prolonged periods of immobility, which can lead to venous stasis and blood clot formation2,3.

DVT complications include pulmonary embolism, which arises when a thrombus dislodges and occludes the pulmonary vasculature and may prove fatal4,5. Older patients, in particular, face an increased risk of DVT, as aging is associated with various factors that predispose individuals to clot formation, including decreased mobility, comorbidities, and changes in blood coagulation6,7. In many countries, such as Thailand, the number of older adults is rising, further amplifying the importance of effective DVT prophylaxis in this demographic8.

Conventional methods for preventing DVT typically involve the use of anticoagulant drugs, which help thin the blood and reduce the likelihood of blood clot formation9. However, these medications carry a risk of bleeding, making their use problematic in certain patient populations10. Consequently, newer clinical guidelines, such as the American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS), advocate for the use of IPCDs as a safer alternative for DVT prevention2,11. IPCDs enhance blood flow through intermittent compression of the legs, thereby reducing venous stasis and promoting circulation12,13.

Although IPCDs effectively augment venous blood flow and reduce DVT incidence, the specific effects of varying compression pressures and inflation sequences on hemodynamic response remain poorly characterized14–16. Existing research has primarily concentrated on the general effectiveness of IPCDs, leaving a knowledge gap regarding how tailored compression settings might optimize patient outcomes17,18.

This study addresses this gap through two objectives: first, developing a programmable IPCD prototype with adjustable compression pressures and sequences as a hemodynamic research tool; and second, characterizing the acute venous hemodynamic responses to different compression parameters in a controlled healthy-volunteer model. While current ACCP guidelines strongly recommend mechanical prophylaxis, they provide limited specificity regarding optimal compression parameters. The present study provides hemodynamic data to inform future device optimization, with the understanding that clinical translation to immobilized patient populations will require dedicated trials.

Methods

IPCD prototype

This research investigated IPCD characteristics, and their impact to lower limbs venous blood flow. The main IPCD characteristics in this study are the compression pressure and the compression sequence. To be able to operate IPCD with multiple compression pressures and multiple compression sequences, we built an IPCD prototype. Prototyping of our IPCD consists of a processing unit, portable pneumatic pump, pneumatic cuffs, and an air-pressure sensor. The prototype uses a microcontroller (Arduino Uno; Arduino, Ivrea, Italy) as the processing unit. The processing unit is responsible for gathering sensor data and activating pneumatic pumps and cuffs. For the pneumatic pump, air-pressure sensor, and pneumatic cuffs, commercially available items are utilized to develop an affordable pneumatic compression system. A low-cost pneumatic pump is essentially an electronic-powered DC motor, such as a portable air pump and air-pressure sensor (Adafruit Industries, New York, NY, USA). The pneumatic cuffs are constructed using IPCD sleeves. Figure 1 shows the IPCD prototype with programmable compression pressures and compression sequences.

We enrolled 12 healthy participants for our study. They took part in the data collection phase at Ramathibodi Hospital, Thailand, to assess IPCD effectiveness in increasing leg blood flow which prevent DVT caused by prolonged immobility. Participants wore the prototype’s pneumatic cuff and lay down for 15 min to an hour to simulate immobility (resting state) while a radiologist used an Ultrasound Machine to identified and measure their venous blood flow velocity in the common femoral vein at proximal thigh level using PW mode20–23. Once their blood flow decreased and stabled at its baseline velocity, indicating their resting state, we initiated our pneumatic compression systems. These systems were tested in three active compression modes to identify the optimal balance between flow velocity and duration: (1) simultaneous compression of all three cuffs (lower-leg, middle-leg, and upper-leg) at 30 mmHg; (2) simultaneous compression at 50 mmHg to evaluate the effects of higher external pressure; and (3) sequential compression at 30 mmHg, inflating from the distal (lower) cuff to the proximal (upper) cuff. These specific pressure thresholds were selected based on standard clinical ranges for mechanical prophylaxis to ensure participant safety while providing sufficient pressure to overcome baseline venous resistance. To gather sufficient data to evaluate the effects of each IPCD compression mode on blood flow, we conducted multiple sessions for each mode. For each participant, 12 baseline sessions were recorded, along with 12 sessions for each compression mode and sequence. This approach allowed us to collect over 200 measurements of lower limb venous blood flow during IPCD operation, providing a robust dataset for analysis while adhering to ethical safety guidelines. To ensure independent measurements for each experimental phase, participants were provided a standardized recovery period (the baseline sessions) of at least five minutes between compression modes. The radiologist confirmed a return to physiological baseline by monitoring the common femoral vein until the resting velocity stabilized within ± 10% of the initial recorded baseline for at least 120 consecutive seconds. While the pneumatic compression system operated, we continued to monitor the participants’ blood flow to evaluate the effects of the pneumatic compressions. Participants rested between each compression mode to ensure their blood flow returned to its baseline level. Figure 2 shows the data collection sessions with the IPCD.

Data collection sessions at Ramathibodi Hospital to assess IPCD effectiveness while a radiologist used a Doppler Ultrasound Machine to measure participant’s peak systolic velocity.

Each compression cycle comprised an inflation phase of approximately 10 s, a hold (dwell) phase of 5 s at target pressure, and a deflation phase of 25 s, yielding a cycle repetition rate of approximately 1.5 cycles per minute. For the sequential 30 mmHg mode, inflation was applied to the distal cuff first, followed by the middle and proximal cuffs with an inter-cuff delay of 3 s. These cycle parameters are consistent with those reported in published intermittent pneumatic compression hemodynamic studies.24–26 Target pressures were verified using an integrated pressure transducer calibrated prior to each data collection session. The cuffs covered the lower-leg, mid-leg, and thigh segments, with widths of 17 cm (lower and mid cuffs) and 22 cm (thigh cuff), sized and fitted to ensure circumferential contact without skin folding. Venous blood flow was assessed using pulsed-wave Doppler ultrasound (model: GE LOGIQ E9; probe frequency: 7.5 MHz; Doppler angle: 60°; sample volume: 2 mm positioned within the common femoral vein lumen). Peak systolic velocity (PSV) was identified as the maximum spectral envelope velocity recorded during each compression event, measured using electronic calipers on the Doppler spectral display by a single trained radiologist.

The radiologist was positioned at the Doppler display and was not informed of the active compression mode during measurements, providing effective blinding to condition assignment. Compression modes were administered in a fixed order for all participants (simultaneous 30 mmHg → simultaneous 50 mmHg → sequential 30 mmHg). While this sequence was not randomized, the mandatory washout period with confirmed return to physiological baseline (resting velocity within ± 10% of the initial recorded resting value for ≥ 120 consecutive seconds) between each mode mitigated potential carryover effects. Augmented flow duration was defined as the continuous interval from compression onset to the point at which the Doppler spectral velocity returned to within ± 10% of the pre-compression resting velocity; this interval was measured from the recorded spectral Doppler trace for each compression cycle.

Data analytic

Statistical analysis was performed using a within-subject design to account for individual physiological variability across the 12 participants. The primary outcome measure was the peak systolic velocity (PSV), which serves as a reliable surrogate for instantaneous venous flow acceleration20,27. Peak systolic velocity (PSV) was selected as the primary hemodynamic marker because high-velocity venous flow is directly associated with increased wall shear stress. This mechanical effect is critical for preventing the coagulation and promoting thrombus prophylaxis in the deep venous system. While PSV quantifies the maximum speed (cm/s) of the blood during the compression phase, the total volume of venous blood displaced is a function of both this velocity and the duration of the hyperemic phase12. The PSV is calculated by averaging Doppler ultrasound readings for the baseline and each of the three active compression modes28.

To determine the efficacy of the prototype, paired t-tests were utilized to compare the baseline PSV of each participant directly to their flow during the 30mmHg simultaneous, 50mmHg simultaneous, and sequential compression modes. This approach allows each subject to serve as their own internal control, effectively isolating the hemodynamic impact of the IPCD from baseline differences in vessel diameter or resting heart rate. Before analysis, the assumption of normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test on the differences between pairs; if the data exhibited significant deviation from a normal distribution, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was planned as a non-parametric alternative29,30. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used to define statistical significance for all comparisons. For each participant, the mean PSV across all sessions within each compression mode was computed as the unit of analysis for all inferential comparisons (n = 12 participant means per mode). The multiple sessions per participant (12 baseline sessions, 12 sessions per active compression mode) were used to obtain stable within-participant velocity estimates prior to group-level inference. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s d with 95% confidence intervals derived from the paired t-distribution (t-critical = 2.201, df = 11).

Lastly, as an exploratory analysis, Pearson correlations were computed between ten participant characteristics (BMI, resting heart rate, height, weight, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, body fat percentage, muscle mass percentage, and thigh and calf circumferences) and the mean PSV achieved during simultaneous 30 mmHg compression (N = 12)31. Given the small sample (N = 12) relative to the number of predictors (k = 10), these correlations are hypothesis-generating only; p-values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. This exploratory analysis aimed to identify candidate anatomical and physiological predictors of compression efficacy for confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies.

Results

The results of this study demonstrated that all pneumatic compression modes significantly increased venous blood flow compared to the baseline. Preliminary analysis using the Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed that the differences between the baseline and active compression sessions followed a normal distribution (p > 0.05 for all pairs). Consequently, paired t-tests were utilized for all subsequent hemodynamic comparisons as planned in the study protocol. No device-related adverse events, such as skin irritation, nerve compression, or venous congestion, were observed during the protocol.

Peak venous velocity augmentation

Figure 3 illustrates the peak venous blood flow velocity across baseline and three pneumatic compression modes. All three active compression modes produced statistically significant increases in common femoral vein PSV compared to baseline (Rest baseline: mean ± SD = 21.12 ± 1.67 cm/s; n = 12 participant means per mode). The simultaneous 30 mmHg compression mode demonstrated the most consistent performance (mean PSV 40.06 ± 1.53 cm/s; 90% above baseline; t(11) = 29.1, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 8.39, 95% CI for mean difference: 17.50–20.37 cm/s). The simultaneous 50 mmHg mode showed greater individual variability (mean PSV 30.45 ± 6.33 cm/s; 44% above baseline; t(11) = 4.26, p = 0.0013, Cohen’s d = 1.23, 95% CI: 4.51–14.14 cm/s). The sequential 30 mmHg mode produced an intermediate peak velocity with low variability (mean PSV 33.56 ± 4.48 cm/s; 59% above baseline; t(11) = 10.08, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 2.91, 95% CI: 9.72–15.16 cm/s). Individual hemodynamic responses

To further investigate the variability observed in the high-pressure mode, Fig. 4 presents the individual participant hemodynamic response curves. The analysis reveals that while simultaneous 30 mmHg consistently augmented flow velocity for all participants, the simultaneous 50 mmHg mode produced a dipping phenomenon in five participants. This dipping pattern is consistent with possible compression-induced flow resistance or venous collapse in susceptible anatomical profiles, and underscores the potential importance of individualized pressure selection in IPCD therapy.

Individual participant hemodynamic response curves across experimental phases.

Flow sustenance

While peak velocity is a critical measure, the duration of augmented flow reflects the total volume of blood moved. Figure 5 presents the duration of sustained elevated blood flow (seconds) per compression cycle across modes. Sequential 30 mmHg compression significantly outperformed simultaneous modes in flow duration (4.49 s vs. 1.85 s for simultaneous 30 mmHg and 2.34 s for simultaneous 50 mmHg)27. This indicates that although sequential compression achieves a lower peak velocity, it moves a greater total volume of blood by sustaining the hyperemic phase for more than twice the duration of simultaneous modes.

Duration of sustained elevated blood flow (seconds) per compression cycle.

Anatomical predictors of IPCD efficacy

Finally, as an exploratory analysis (N = 12), Fig. 6 presents Pearson correlations between ten physiological parameters and the mean PSV during simultaneous 30 mmHg compression. Resting heart rate demonstrated the largest inverse correlation (r = − 0.54, p = 0.069), followed by height (r = − 0.45) and calf circumference (r = − 0.41); no correlation reached statistical significance (α = 0.05) in this small sample. These exploratory associations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and the candidate predictors identified here require confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies before conclusions about anatomical determinants of IPCD efficacy can be drawn32.

Discussion

This proof-of-concept pilot study characterized how compression pressure and sequence affect acute venous hemodynamics in a healthy-volunteer model simulating lower-limb immobilization. Using a programmable IPCD prototype, we quantified peak systolic velocity (PSV) and augmented flow duration in the common femoral vein across three compression modes. The findings confirm that all tested modes significantly augmented venous PSV and demonstrate that the hemodynamic response varies meaningfully with compression parameters. These results represent hemodynamic observations in healthy volunteers and should not be directly extrapolated to clinical outcomes in immobilized patients without further validation.

The observed hemodynamic differences across compression modes are contextually consistent with current clinical practice guidelines from the American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS), which endorse IPCD use for mechanical prophylaxis but provide limited guidance on optimal compression parameters. These pilot findings should be interpreted as hemodynamic characterization of a prototype device in healthy volunteers, not as direct clinical guidance. Translation of these parameter recommendations to immobilized patient populations requires dedicated randomized trials with clinical DVT outcomes.

Our results demonstrated that the simultaneous 30 mmHg compression mode was the most effective at augmenting peak venous blood flow velocity, showing a 90% increase from baseline with minimal variability across participants (SD = 1.53 cm/s; t(11) = 29.1, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 8.39, 95% CI: 17.50–20.37 cm/s). This suggests that a moderate pressure setting provides optimal compression for consistent venous return augmentation, likely balancing effective venous emptying against the risk of compression-induced flow resistance at higher pressures. Given the consistency of the simultaneous 30 mmHg mode across participants in this healthy-volunteer pilot study, this parameter warrants further investigation in patient populations before clinical recommendations can be made.

The variability observed in Fig. 4, where certain participants experienced a decrease in flow velocity at 50 mmHg, indicates the need of individualized pressure selection. This dipping phenomenon is consistent with possible venous occlusion or compression-induced flow resistance at higher external pressures; however, direct confirmation would require assessment of vessel lumen diameter during compression, which was beyond the scope of this pilot study. Furthermore, the exploratory correlation analysis (Fig. 6) identified an inverse relationship between resting heart rate and PSV at 30 mmHg (r = − 0.54), which may reflect the shortened diastolic interval associated with higher heart rates, limiting the time available for venous filling and potentially increasing baseline venous tone33. Higher intraluminal pressure or sympathetic-mediated vasoconstriction in these individuals may offer greater resistance to external pneumatic pressure, thereby dampening the device’s ability to accelerate venous flow. This correlation further reinforces the study’s central theme that a one-size-fits-all pressure setting is insufficient. Instead, we may need an automatic algorithm to consider systemic cardiovascular markers, such as heart rate, when setting IPCD parameters to ensure optimal flow augmentation for patients with varying autonomic or cardiovascular profiles.

The sequential compression mode, while not generating the highest peak blood flow, exhibited the longest duration of increased blood flow, more than double that of the 30mmHg mode. This prolonged enhancement of blood flow suggests that while sequential compression may not maximize peak velocities, it may be beneficial in sustaining higher venous blood flow over a longer period which increases total venous return volume34. This characteristic could be particularly useful in clinical scenarios where prolonged periods of improved blood flow are necessary, such as during extended immobility.

This study has several limitations. This pilot study utilized a cohort of 12 healthy participants to establish the baseline hemodynamic performance and safety profile of the IPCD prototype. This demographic was specifically selected in accordance with the Human Research Ethics Committee’s requirements to ensure participant safety during the initial operation of a custom-engineered medical device. By conducting this investigation on healthy participants with standardized venous anatomy, we were able to study the mechanical effects of varying compression parameters from the pathological variables, such as venous insufficiency or existing thrombi, present in high-risk orthopedic populations35. While the sample size is limited to 12 participants, the multiple repeated compression cycles per participant were used to compute stable within-participant mean peak velocities, which served as the unit of analysis for all inferential comparisons (n = 12 participant means per mode). These pilot results provide preliminary evidence to justify subsequent clinical trials in immobilized patient populations.

Conclusions

This proof-of-concept pilot study explored the hemodynamic effects of varying IPCD compression pressures and inflation sequences in a healthy-volunteer model simulating lower-limb immobilization. The results demonstrate that different parameter combinations significantly alter venous flow dynamics, with simultaneous 30 mmHg compression producing the greatest and most consistent augmentation in peak venous velocity and sequential 30 mmHg compression producing the longest duration of augmented flow. The variability in individual responses to simultaneous 50 mmHg compression indicates the importance of individualized parameter selection. These findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating; direct translation of these parameter recommendations to clinical practice requires dedicated trials in immobilized patient populations.

In conclusion, this healthy-volunteer pilot study demonstrates that both compression pressure and inflation sequence significantly influence venous peak flow velocity and augmentation duration. Simultaneous 30 mmHg compression offers the most consistent peak velocity augmentation, while sequential 30 mmHg compression substantially prolongs the duration of augmented flow. These preliminary findings support further investigation of individualized IPCD parameter optimization, and clinical trials in immobilized patient populations are warranted to determine whether these hemodynamic advantages translate to improved DVT prophylaxis outcomes36.
