Role of dynamic changes in human sperm heat shock protein A2 in male infertility
Authors: Akiko NATSUME, Yasuhiko OZAKI, Yu KITADATE, Shotaro HAYASHI, Yosuke MATSUMOTO, Tomoki TAKEDA, Shoichiro IWATSUKI, Seiji MABUCHI, Yukihiro UMEMOTO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Male factors account for approximately 50% of infertility cases; however, the mechanisms underlying sperm dysfunction remain unclear. Heat shock protein A2 (HSPA2) is a biomarker associated with sperm fertilization; however, few studies have examined changes in its localization in vitro. This study investigated the in vitro localization dynamics of HSPA2 in sperm and its association with male infertility. Fertility status was determined according to the 2021 World Health Organization criteria. Semen samples were collected from 28 men with normal findings and 57 men with varicocele or oligozoospermia. Samples were subjected to density gradient centrifugation and the swim-up procedure, followed by a 24 h incubation. HSPA2 protein levels were analyzed using sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and western blotting, and localization patterns were evaluated using immunofluorescence staining. Western blot analysis showed that HSPA2 levels were significantly lower in samples from men with oligozoospermia (P < 0.01) and varicocele (P < 0.05) than in those from men with normal findings. Moreover, HSPA2 levels were correlated with total motile sperm counts and sperm motility. The percentage of sperm with HSPA2 localized in the acrosomal region immediately after sperm processing was lower (P < 0.01) and peaked later in samples from men with oligozoospermia and varicocele than in those from men with normal findings. These findings indicate that the reduced acrosomal localization of HSPA2 and delayed acrosomal localization dynamics after sperm processing represent a shared feature of male infertility rather than condition-specific differences and may be linked to impaired sperm function.

Approximately 8–12% of couples worldwide are affected by infertility, with male factors involved in 50% of the cases [1]. Conventional semen analysis, including the measurement of sperm concentration, motility, progressive motility, and morphology, is commonly used to evaluate sperm conditions. The World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines are the standard references for such assessments [2]. However, 30% of patients diagnosed with infertility show normal results in these assessments, indicating that these methods do not accurately evaluate the functional potential of sperm [3]. Therefore, identifying the specific causes of fertilization failure and embryonic developmental disorders in clinical practice remains challenging. Functional assessments of sperm, such as tests for DNA damage and measurements of reactive oxygen species, have been suggested as potential predictive tools for in vitro fertilization (IVF) outcomes [4]. However, no definitive evaluation method has been established, and identifying the specific causes of fertilization or developmental failure in clinical practice is difficult. Furthermore, optimal culture conditions and sperm selection methods for IVF remain unclear [5,6,7].

Heat shock protein A2 (HSPA2), a member of the heat shock protein 70 family, is a molecular chaperone that assists in protein folding, transport, and assembly in the cytoplasm, mitochondria, and endoplasmic reticulum [8, 9]. Heat shock proteins have a protective effect on cellular autoregulation in response to heat and homeostasis by maintaining the balance between protein synthesis and degradation [10, 11]. HSPA2 is a testis-specific heat shock protein that is believed to play a role in meiosis during spermatogenesis, thereby promoting sperm development [8]. HSPA2 protein levels are reduced in testicular tissues from patients with azoospermia or severe oligozoospermia [12, 13]. Recent studies have suggested that HSPA2 relocates during fertilization, potentially regulating sperm binding to the zona pellucida of oocytes [14]. Additionally, HSPA2 mRNA levels are significantly lower in patients with asthenozoospermia and teratozoospermia than in healthy individuals [15]. In patients with varicocele, HSPA2 gene expression is also decreased [16, 17], possibly because of subtle increases in testicular temperature that affect spermatogenesis [18]. Furthermore, oxidative stress is closely associated with male infertility [19].

Although studies on HSPA2 are limited, it has been suggested that it can serve as a novel biomarker for sperm function. However, few studies have examined changes in HSPA2 localization in sperm during the culture process associated with sperm capacitation. Moreover, reports on changes in HSPA2 protein levels or localization during in vitro culture remain limited. Therefore, this study focused on investigating temporal alterations in HSPA2 localization in the acrosomal region of the sperm head to elucidate their potential relevance to male infertility.

Materials and Methods

Ethical approval

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to their participation in the study. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Nagoya City University Graduate School of Medical Sciences (no. 60-24-0019).

Patient selection

We assessed semen samples from 85 men who visited our institute between May and November 2024. Based on the 2021 WHO semen analysis criteria, 28 patients met the reference values and were classified into the normal group. Eighteen patients whose semen parameters were below the reference values were diagnosed with oligozoospermia without varicocele (oligozoospermia group). Twenty-one patients were classified as normozoospermic with varicocele (varicocele-normal group) and 18 as oligozoospermic with varicocele (varicocele-oligozoospermia group). Patients with sperm concentrations below 1 × 106 ml−1 were excluded from the study because of insufficient material for protein extraction.

Semen analysis and preparation

After 10 min of liquefaction, all samples were analyzed for total sperm concentration and motility using the CASA system (Sperm Motility Analysis System; DITECT Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), according to WHO guidelines. Sperm were prepared using two-layer density gradient centrifugation (45% and 90% SpermGrad and Gx-IVF; Vitrolife, Tokyo, Japan), and the isolated sperm were subjected to the swim-up procedure for 1 h. After sperm preparation, the sperm suspension was analyzed using the method described above. Subsequently, the sperm suspension obtained by swim-up was incubated for up to 24 h at 37°C, 6.0% CO2, and 5.0% O2. Samples were collected 1, 3, 6, and 24 h after sperm preparation.

-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and western blot analysis of human sperm HSPA2

Samples were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and western blot analysis. First, the samples were lysed for 30 min at 4°C using RIPA buffer (ULTRARIPA kit for Lipid Raft; Bio Dynamics Laboratory Inc., Tokyo, Japan) containing protease inhibitors. Each sample was then vortexed for 1 min and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 g for 30 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant fraction was collected, and the total protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific K.K., Tokyo, Japan). The samples were diluted with Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). Electrophoresis was then performed using 4–20% polyacrylamide gradient gels (Criterion TGX Precast Gel; Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc.) with standard markers (Precision Plus Protein Dual Xtra Prestained Protein Standards; Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc.), a power supply (PowerPac Universal Power Supply; Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc.), and electrophoresis buffer (Tris/Glycine/SDS Buffer; Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc.). The total protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay, and the samples were diluted accordingly. Equal amounts of total protein (3 μg/lane) were loaded for all samples prior to SDS-PAGE. The gels were run at 200 V for protein band separation using a western blotting system (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc.). Subsequently, proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Trans-Blot Turbo Mini 0.2 mm PVDF Transfer Pack; Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc.). The membranes were incubated with 5% skim milk (Snow Brand, Tokyo, Japan) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBS-T) as the blocking solution for 1 h and then overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody (1 μg ml–1 rabbit polyclonal anti-HSPA2 antibody; catalog no. HPA000798; Sigma-Aldrich Japan G.K., Tokyo, Japan). The secondary antibodies were horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) or goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc.). Housekeeping proteins are usually used as loading controls to normalize protein expression; however, the protein levels of sperm samples have recently been reported to vary widely among specimens, and the widely utilized normalization proteins are not suitable for data normalization [20]. Protein staining of internal controls was primarily performed using Ponceau S staining (Beacle, Inc., Kyoto, Japan) to confirm the amount of total protein in each sample [21, 22]. SYPRO Ruby staining (Thermo Fisher Scientific K.K.) was used to visually confirm overall protein patterns [23]. The western blot band intensities were quantified using Image Quant™ LAS 4000 software (GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, UK). Control samples were included in each membrane, and the intensity values were normalized to the corresponding control.

Immunostaining for HSPA2 in human sperm

Immunocytochemical analysis was performed to investigate the localization of HSPA2 in the sperm. Samples were smeared on poly-L-lysine-coated glass slides (Matsunami Glass Ind., Ltd., Osaka, Japan), dried, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the slides were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline solution containing Tween 20 (PBS-T). After washing, the slides were blocked with blocking solution (Blocking One-P; Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The slides were incubated with the primary antibody (anti-HSPA2 antibody, 1:2,000) at 4°C (final concentration: 0.05 μg/ml). The slides were then washed three times with PBS-T for 5 min and then incubated with the goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:400; Alexa Fluor 546 goat anti-rabbit IgG; Thermo Fisher Scientific K.K.) for 1 h in the dark. To confirm the localization of HSPA2 in the acrosome and nucleus, multiple staining was performed using peanut agglutinin (a lectin from Arachis hypogaea), Alexa Fluor 488 Conjugate (1:400; Thermo Fisher Scientific K.K.) at a final concentration: 2.5 μg/ml, and Hoechst 33342 (1:2,000; Thermo Scientific K.K.) at a final concentration: 5.0 μg/ml. After staining, the slides were washed three times at 5 min intervals and mounted with a mounting medium. Negative controls were prepared without primary antibodies. Fluorescence images were captured using a laser fluorescence confocal microscope (Confocal Microscope A1+; Nikon Instruments Inc., Tokyo, Japan), and ≥ 100 sperm were observed for each sample. To ensure objectivity of the six-pattern classification of HSPA2 localization, immunofluorescence images were coded and evaluated under blinded conditions. Specifically, images were assigned anonymized identifiers, and evaluators were blinded to the sample group information during classification. HSPA2 localization patterns were scored according to predefined criteria by a single observer (A.N.).

Statistical analysis

The Student’s paired t-test was used to evaluate differences in mean ± standard deviation (SD) changes in HSPA2 localization and protein levels over time. One-way ANOVA was followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference test for pairwise between-group comparisons. The relationship between HSPA2 levels and the total motile sperm count and motility was examined using Pearson’s correlation analysis. All variables are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (version 1.68; Jichi Medical University, Tochigi, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [24]. EZR is a modified version of R Commander, designed to add statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics.

Discussion

Sperm from patients with oligozoospermia or varicocele showed significantly lower HSPA2 levels immediately after sperm preparation. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies on HSPA2 expression [16]. A similar trend was observed at the protein level. HSPA2 levels were correlated with both the total motile sperm count and sperm motility in the semen, indicating that HSPA2 levels were independently associated with each of these parameters. A previous study reported that the percentage of HSPA2-positive cells is correlated with the sperm concentration [25]. HSPA2 levels are closely associated with oxidative stress, because antioxidant treatment has been reported to increase HSPA2 levels and improve sperm parameters, whereas oxidative stress leads to decreased HSPA2 levels [19, 26]. Decreased sperm motility is associated with oxidative stress [27]. Collectively, these findings suggest that oxidative stress contributes to the association between HSPA2 levels and sperm motility observed in this study. However, as oxidative stress parameters were not evaluated in this study, further investigations are required to clarify the relationship between oxidative stress and HSPA2 levels in male infertility.

In the present study, we observed variations in the distribution of HSPA2 across the acrosomal, equatorial, posterior, and neck regions within sperm heads immediately after ejaculation. This observation aligns with previous findings [25]. Time-dependent changes in HSPA2 localization during in vitro incubation have been described previously [28]. However, these findings were derived from an investigation that primarily focused on sperm from men with normal findings and did not examine temporal alterations in the context of male infertility. A novel contribution of this study is the comparative analysis of the time-dependent HSPA2 redistribution between men with normal findings and those with infertility.

In addition to the acrosomal pattern, we evaluated the time-dependent changes in other HSPA2 localization patterns, including the equatorial segment, whole head, post-acrosomal region, and cervical and plasma membrane patterns. However, these patterns showed no significant differences between men with normal findings and those with infertility.

Notably, the acrosome plays a central role in key fertilization processes, including sperm–zona pellucida binding and acrosome reactions. HSPA2 has been implicated in the regulation of sperm surface remodeling and the presentation of receptors required for sperm-oocyte recognition [14]. Therefore, localization to the acrosomal region is functionally important for fertilization competence. However, the functional significance of HSPA2 localization in other regions of the sperm head remains poorly understood, and evidence linking these localization patterns to fertilization outcomes or male infertility is limited. Taken together, these findings suggest that the acrosomal localization pattern of HSPA2 may serve as a sensitive and functionally relevant indicator of sperm quality and fertilization potential. This may explain why alterations were observed only in the acrosomal pattern of men with infertility in the present study. Accordingly, the present study focused on acrosomal patterns as the primary outcome. Further studies are required to clarify the biological significance of HSPA2 localization to other regions.

We demonstrated that although acrosomal localization increased after sperm preparation, the frequency of acrosomal localization was significantly reduced, and the time required to reach peak localization was markedly delayed in men with infertility compared to those with normal findings. These findings indicated that the temporal dynamics of HSPA2 redistribution differ in male infertility. Notably, no significant differences in the HSPA2 localization pattern or the time course of acrosomal localization were observed between the varicocele and oligozoospermia groups, indicating that delayed HSPA2 acrosomal localization is a feature shared across these male infertility categories. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that the delayed acrosomal localization of HSPA2 is associated with male infertility. Previous studies have reported impaired acquisition of fertilization capacity and delayed fertilization in patients with oligozoospermia [29, 30], and similar findings have been reported in patients with varicocele [31]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the delayed acrosomal localization of HSPA2 observed in male infertility may be associated with the impaired or delayed acquisition of fertilizing capacity.

According to WHO guidelines, a minimum swim-up time of 1 h is recommended during sperm processing; however, no clear consensus has been established [5, 6]. Based on our findings, a longer incubation period may be required for spermatozoa from patients with oligozoospermia or varicocele to reach optimal HSPA2 acrosomal localization and potential functional maturation. However, extending the post-preparation incubation period may lead to significant tradeoffs. Previous studies have reported a time-dependent increase in sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF) in prepared/processed spermatozoa during in vitro incubation [32,33,34], which is thought to be mediated, at least in part, by the oxidative stress induced by handling and culture conditions. Accordingly, although a longer incubation period might allow a larger fraction of spermatozoa, particularly those with oligozoospermia and varicocele, to reach the peak of HSPA2 acrosomal localization, excessive incubation could concurrently increase the risk of SDF. Therefore, future studies should define an optimal incubation window that balances functional maturation (e.g., HSPA2 dynamics and capacitation-related markers) with the maintenance of low SDF levels.

This study had some limitations. First, the study included a relatively small number of specimens. Secondly, our experiments focused on HSPA2 alone. Therefore, whether the presence of molecules capable of regulating HSPA2 dynamics can prevent its localization to the acrosome and thus affect male fertility should be investigated. Third, the degree of capacitation was not examined. Future studies should focus on the role of HSPA2 in capacitation. Finally, although HSPA2 is functionally involved in the binding between sperm and the zona pellucida, clinical outcome measures such as fertilization rates, embryo development parameters, and pregnancy outcomes were not available in the present study. Therefore, we could not directly examine whether the delayed HSPA2 acrosomal localization peak translated into differences in assisted reproductive technology outcomes. Addressing this limitation by integrating the HSPA2 dynamics with clinical endpoints is important for future studies.

In conclusion, men with infertility showed reduced acrosomal localization of HSPA2 immediately after sperm preparation and a delayed peak of acrosomal localization during subsequent incubation compared to men with normal findings. Importantly, these alterations were observed as a shared feature of male infertility rather than condition-specific differences between varicocele and oligozoospermia. These altered HSPA2 dynamics are associated with sperm motility parameters, suggesting that HSPA2 may reflect aspects of sperm functional competence. Future studies are warranted to determine whether optimizing the incubation conditions can improve functional outcomes while preserving DNA integrity.
