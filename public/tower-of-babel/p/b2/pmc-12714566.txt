Characterization and engineering of a type IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system for genome editing in Escherichia coli
Authors: Szabolcs Semsey, Emilie Søndberg, Mathilde Røen, Björn Hallström, Anders Østergaard Petersen, Lauge Alfastsen, Berta Rosich Bosch, Benjamin Wohl, Jasper Clube, Eric van der Helm, Christian Groendahl, Ioannis Mougiakos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCRISPR–Cas systems have revolutionized genome engineering technologies, but type IV CRISPR–Cas systems and their genome engineering potential have been critically underexplored. In this study, we identified a type IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system from a clinical Klebsiella pneumoniae isolate and characterized its plasmid targeting activity and capacity to suppress chromosomal and plasmid gene expression in Escherichia coli. We revealed the pivotal role of Csf3 (Cas5) and the dispensable roles of Csf1 (Cas8-like) and Csf4 (DinG helicase) subunits in IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas complex formation. The system prevents plasmid propagation via interplay between DinG helicase activity and strategic protospacer positioning relative to plasmid replication and maintenance components. We enabled the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system to introduce lethal, sequence-specific double-stranded (ds)DNA breaks in the E. coli chromosome by fusing the nuclease domain of the I-TevI nuclease to the Cas8 N-terminus. Further, we developed a series of base editors, with various editing efficiencies and windows, by fusing the PmCDA1 cytidine deaminase to the Cas8, Cas5, and DinG subunits. Finally, conjugative transfer of the Cas5–PmCDA1 base editor into E. coli deactivated the tryptophan repressor gene, boosting IAA production. Our study provides new insights into type IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas systems and highlights their potential in genome engineering applications.

Introduction

Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) and their associated (Cas) proteins function as bacterial and archaeal adaptive immune systems that protect host cells from invading mobile genetic elements (MGEs), such as plasmids and phages [1, 2]. CRISPR–Cas systems are classified into two classes, seven types, and multiple subtypes, based on their gene compositions and locus architectures [3]. The adaptation modules of the CRISPR–Cas systems incorporate small DNA fragments from the invading elements into their CRISPR arrays, in the form of spacers, which serve as records of these encounters [1]. Transcription and processing of the CRISPR arrays produces CRISPR RNAs (crRNAs), which guide the effector modules of the CRISPR–Cas systems to protect the hosts against future invasions from the same MGEs [2, 4, 5]

Type IV CRISPR–Cas systems are multi-subunit class I CRISPR–Cas systems divided into three subtypes (A–C) [3, 6], with some studies reporting up to five subtypes (A–E) [7–9], and they are widely distributed on conjugative plasmids or integrative conjugation elements [7–11]. Research on type IV CRISPR–Cas systems has remained limited, mostly focusing on type IV-A systems [9, 10, 12–17]. Cryo electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of IV-A systems show a Cascade-like architecture built around a Cas7-multimer (Csf2). The Cas7 backbone oligomerizes along the crRNA, capped at the 5′ handle by Cas5 (Csf3) and Cas8 (Csf1) subunits positioned for protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sensing and target engagement [6, 17]. On the opposite side of the complex, Cas6 performs crRNA maturation, delivering the 5′ crRNA handle to Cas5 and Cas8 while stabilizing the 3′ crRNA loop [13]. Type IV-A CRISPR–Cas systems are—with rare exceptions [18]—nuclease-deficient and recruit a DinG helicase that has been linked with inter-plasmid warfare and transcriptional regulation of host genes [7, 8, 11, 15, 17]. Type IV-A systems lack adaptation modules but use Cas1–Cas2 complexes from other host CRISPR–Cas systems to incorporate new spacers into their CRISPR-arrays [11]. Despite their multi-subunit nature, the operons expressing type IV-A systems are rather compact compared to other class I systems. Despite the unique characteristics of type IV-A systems, their biotechnological exploitation remains largely unexplored, apart from proof-of-concept work demonstrating their use in resensitizing an antibiotic-tolerant Klebsiella pneumoniae [11]. In this study, we extend the mechanistic understanding of a type IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system and harness it for the development of IV-A3-based genome editing tools.

Fast and efficient microbial gene editing facilitates fundamental research and biotechnological applications [19, 20]. The targeted nuclease activity of CRISPR–Cas systems has accelerated the field of microbial metabolic engineering, as it has been adapted into highly efficient counter-selection and homologous recombination promoting tools against unedited cells [21]. The stable DNA binding activity of engineered, nuclease-deficient CRISPR–Cas variants [22] has been employed for gene silencing and gene activation [23]. Nickase CRISPR–Cas variants have been combined with deaminases [24–27] and reverse transcriptases [28, 29] for gene silencing and targeted genome editing applications that do not require double-stranded (ds)DNA breaks. The discovery and exploitation of CRISPR-associated transposons has further enabled the targeted insertion of long DNA fragments without the need for homologous recombination templates [30, 31]. Hence, investigating the mode of action of new CRISPR–Cas systems can facilitate their utilization in the development of new genome editing technologies.

In this study, we identified, characterized, and engineered a type IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system from K. pneumoniae and demonstrated its effectiveness as an efficient dsDNA cleaving and base-editing platform. We discovered that Cas5 is essential for IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas backbone assembly, unlike Cas8 and DinG. Furthermore, plasmid inhibition efficiency depends on the targeted protospacer’s position relative to replication and maintenance elements. We further fused the Cas8 component of the system with the nuclease domain of the I-TevI endonuclease [32], thus creating a synthetic system that introduces lethal dsDNA breaks into the E. coli genome. Additionally, we fused the PmCDA1 cytidine deaminase to the Cas8, DinG, and Cas5 subunits of the system, thus constructing a collection of base editors that generate vastly different editing outcomes. Finally, we demonstrated the potential of the base-editing platform for metabolic and microbiome engineering applications; the conjugation of the Cas5–PmCDA1 base editor together with the indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production pathway into E. coli resulted in the deactivation of the tryptophan repressor gene and a simultaneous boost in IAA production. Our findings advance the current understanding on the plasmid targeting activity of type IV-A CRISPR–Cas systems and demonstrate that IV-A3 systems can be engineered into powerful new tools for precise genetic modification.

Methods

Strains, media, and growth conditions

Strains, plasmids, spacer, and genetic sequences used in the study are listed in Supplementary Tables S6–S9, respectively. Bacterial cultures for assays involving fluorescent measurements were grown in M9 minimal medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics for plasmid maintenance at 37°C, 180 rpm agitation. The rest of the bacterial cultures in this study were grown under the same temperature and agitation conditions but in lysogenic broth (LB) medium with appropriate antibiotics for plasmid selection and maintenance. LB agar plates supplemented with appropriate antibiotics were used for cloning and for plasmid targeting assays. Amounts of antibiotics: 10 µg/ml tetracycline, 20 µg/ml chloramphenicol, 50 µg/ml kanamycin. All cloning related polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were performed with Q5 Hi Fi 2X Master Mix (NEB) and all screening related PCRs were performed with Dreamtaq Green PCR Master mix (Thermo Fischer Scientific), unless otherwise stated. All electrocompetent cells were prepared employing the protocol described in [33]. All plasmids were sequence verified either with Sanger sequencing or NGS.

In silico identification of the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system and its PAM

We screened the genomes of previously sequenced strains in the bacterial collection of SNIPR Biome for CRISPR–Cas systems using CRISPRCasTyper v1.8.0 [34]. We identified a candidate IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system, with a CRISPR array containing 12 spacers, on the plasmid of the K. pneumoniae b1533 strain. We downloaded the complete Genbank genome assemblies (n = 4123) for the genus Klebsiella (NCBI taxonomy ID: 570) using the NCBI Datasets tool [35]. Each genome was queried with the 12 spacer sequences using blastn-short [36]. For all complete matches with ≤2 mismatches, the sequences 6 bp up- and downstream of the putative protospacer were extracted. Hits with flanking sequences matching the repeat sequences of the CRISPR array were considered to be part of other CRISPR arrays and were excluded from further analysis. The PAM sequences were extracted from the remaining matches.

Plasmid targeting assays (both pTarget 1 to 4, 1-4, and mismatches)

We constructed the p1793 targeting plasmid by cloning the five predicted cas genes of the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system (csf1/cas8, csf5/cas6, csf4/dinG, csf3/cas5, csf2/cas7) with the first five spacers of the accompanying CRISPR array into a low copy number backbone for expression in E. coli. For this cloning, we PCR-amplified the corresponding fragment from the genome of b1533 with appropriately designed primers and we used NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (NEB) to clone the PCR fragment into a linearized low copy number plasmid, putting the operon under the transcriptional control of a constitutive E. coli promoter (PbolA). p1793 was transformed into electrocompetent E. coli NEB Turbo cells, generating strain b5408. We constructed the non-targeting control plasmid p1985 by substituting the CRISPR array of p1793 with a non-targeting repeat-spacer-repeat sequence, using appropriately designed primers. p1985 was transformed into electrocompetent E. coli NEB Turbo cells, generating strain b6259.

We constructed the pTarget1-4, pTarget1, pTarget2, pTarget3, and pTarget4 target plasmids by cloning each full-length (32 bp) spacer of the p1793 CRISPR array (spacers 1, 2, 4, and 5)—separately and together—into a non-target, high copy number plasmid. The spacers were cloned with the in silico predicted 5′-AAG-3′ PAM. For this cloning, we PCR amplified the high copy number plasmid with appropriately designed primers that carry the protospacers as overhangs, and we circularized the products with KLD enzyme mix (NEB) and transformed the reactions into electrocompetent E. coli NEB Turbo cells. We transformed the resulting non-target and the target plasmids into electrocompetent b5408 and b6259 cells and we spot plated the recovered cells on LB agar plates supplemented with tetracycline and chloramphenicol. The transformation efficiencies of b5408 and b6259 when transformed with the non-target plasmid was divided by the transformation efficiency of the strain when transformed with the target plasmids. The calculated numbers represented the reductions in the transformation efficiencies caused by the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system.

spacer–protospacer mismatch assays

We constructed the “spacer–protospacer mismatches” plasmid library by introducing various mutations in the 32-bp-long protospacer region of pTarget1 with Q5® Site-Directed Mutagenesis (NEB) and appropriately designed primers (Supplementary Table S9). The resulting PCR products were circularized using KLD Enzyme Mix (NEB) reactions and transformed into electrocompetent E. coli NEB Turbo cells. Each member of the generated plasmid library was separately transformed into electrocompetent b5408 cells and spot-plated on LB agar plates supplemented with chloramphenicol and tetracycline. We divided the transformation efficiency of b5408, when transformed with the non-target plasmid, by the transformation efficiency of the strain when transformed with each member of the plasmid library and we normalized for the transformation efficiency of each strain with the control, empty vector. The calculated numbers represent the reductions in the transformation efficiencies caused by the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system and indicated how the corresponding spacer–protospacer mismatches affect the targeting efficiency of the system.

PAM identification assay

We constructed the p2259 PAM plasmid library by employing pTarget1 as PCR amplification template with appropriately designed primers. The forward primer was designed to introduce a 5-nt-long degenerate sequence at the 5′ end of the protospacer present on pTarget1. The PCR product was used for in-fusion cloning, employing 5× In-Fusion HD enzyme premix (Takara Bio). The in-fusion cloning reaction was transformed into 50 µl of electrocompetent E. coli NEB10β cells. The cells were left to recover in SOC medium (500 µl final volume) for 1 h at 37°C and shaking at 200 rpm. The recovered cells were transferred into 500 ml of LB liquid medium, supplemented with chloramphenicol. One hundred microliters from the 500 ml culture were plated on an LB agar plate, supplemented with chloramphenicol. Culture and plate were incubated overnight at 37°C, with rigorous shaking for the former (200 rpm). We counted 536 colonies on the plate, and we calculated that 2 680 000 successfully transformed cells were added into the 500 ml culture. For this calculation, we used the following equation:

No of transformed cells in culture = (volume of culture/volume of plated culture) × no. of colonies in selection plate.

The calculated number corresponded to 2617 × coverage of the PAM library members, according to the following equation:

Plasmid library coverage = (no. of transformed cells in the culture/no. of distinct plasmid library members).

We prepared glycerol stocks from the 500 ml culture, and we isolated the constructed p2259 plasmid library from the remaining culture using the Qiagen plasmid midi kit. We electroporated the p2259 plasmid library into electrocompetent b5408 and b6259 cells. We recovered the cells in 500 μl of SOC medium for 1 h at 37°C and shaking at 200 rpm. We inoculated the recovered cells into 500 ml of LB liquid medium, supplemented with chloramphenicol and tetracycline. The cultures were incubated overnight at 37°C with rigorous shaking at 200 rpm. We isolated the p2259 plasmid libraries from each culture, using the Qiagen plasmid midi kit and we used them for NGS. The 1st NGS PCR amplification step on both p2259 preps was performed using appropriately designed primers in 50 µl KAPA HiFi Hotstart ReadyMix reactions (Roche) (25 cycles, 30 s denaturation, 20 s annealing, 15 s extension). The reaction products were purified with AMPure XP Reagent (Beckman Coulter). Subsequently, Illumina indexes and flow cell binding sequences were added to the purified products with a 2nd NGS PCR amplification step. The 1st NGS PCR products were subjected to the 2nd NGS PCR amplification step using appropriately designed primers in 50 µl KAPA HiFi Hotstart ReadyMix reactions (Roche) (10 cycles, 20 ng of template DNA, 30 s denaturation, 20 s annealing, 15 s extension). Both reaction products were purified with AMPure XP Reagent (Beckman Coulter). The purified products were sequenced using Illumina MiSeq® with single-end 150 cycles.

PAM-library depletion assay sequencing data analysis

Overlapping paired-end reads were merged using PEAR version 0.9.6 [37] with a minimum overlap of 50 bp, marking all non-congruent sites as N. The PAM sequence was extracted from anything downstream of “AGTTATTGGTGCCCC” and upstream of “TCGTGTTGTCCACGG” (allowing for two mismatches). Other lengths than 5 or reads with Ns were discarded. The occurrences of all 1024 possible PAM sequences were counted and normalized as transcripts per million (TPM). The TPM log fold-change between the control library and the depleted library was calculated. Since the first two positions did not appear to affect depletion, depletion scores for the 64 three base pair variants were calculated by adding the individual fold-changes. A PAM wheel was plotted using KronaTools [38], with the depletion scores as input.

Crispr interference assays

We constructed strain E. coli_gfp (b5815) for the chromosomal IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas interference assays. Using an established genome integration method [33], we inserted the gfp gene under the control of the strong p70a E. coli promoter into the genome of E. coli MG1655, in the region between the ybhB and ybhC genes. We employed E. coli MG1655 as negative control strain for the CRISPR interference assays.

We constructed strain b6386 for the plasmid IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas interference assays, by transforming strain E. coli MG1655 with plasmid p2370. The plasmid contains the gfp gene under the control of the p70a promoter in a high copy number backbone that confers resistance to chloramphenicol.

We constructed the non-targeting p2062, p2064, p2065, and p2160 plasmids, which express different truncated variants of the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system, by employing p1985 as PCR amplification template with appropriately designed primers. The resulting PCR products were circularized using KLD Enzyme Mix (NEB) reactions and transformed into electrocompetent E. coli NEB10β cells. We cloned the targeting variants of the non-targeting plasmids used in the CRISPR interference assays by digesting the non-targeting plasmids with the BsmBi-v2 (NEB) restriction enzyme and then ligating pre-annealed complementary single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) oligos, which code for targeting spacers, into the digested plasmids using T4 DNA ligase (NEB). The ligation reactions were transformed into electrocompetent E. coli NEB10β. The constructed plasmids were transformed into electrocompetent b5815 cells. Three colonies were randomly selected from each transformation, and they were used for inoculation of 5 ml M9 minimal medium cultures supplemented with glycerol (0.4% w/v) and tetracycline. The cultures were incubated overnight at 37°C with rigorous shaking (200 rpm). Two microliters from each culture were transferred to 198 µl of M9 medium supplemented with glycerol (0.4% w/v) and tetracycline. The resulting 200 µl cultures were transferred to distinct wells in Thermo Scientific™ Nunc microWell 96-well optical-bottom plates with polymer base. The plates were sealed with Breathe-Easy® sealing membranes (Sigma–Aldrich) and loaded into Synergy H1 microplate readers (Biotek) for incubation at 37°C with continuous shaking. The optical densities (absorbance at 600 nm) and the green fluorescence emissions (excitation at 485 nm, emission at 516 nm) of the cultures were measured in 10-min intervals for a 24-h period. The fluorescence emissions data from each culture and each time point were normalized with the corresponding OD600 nm data and the emissions data from the negative control strain. The normalized data from the cultures were averaged and the corresponding standard deviations were calculated. At 8 h, all the cultures showed the highest fluorescence emission to growth ratio and the data from this time point were plotted in the presented graphs.

Plasmid targeting assays with I-TevI_Cas8

We constructed the target plasmids for the I-TevI_Cas8 DNase system by PCR amplifying pTarget1 with appropriately designed primers. The primers introduce, right upstream of the PAM sequence, the 5-nt-long I-TevI 5′-CAACG-3′ cleavage site followed by 27- to 34-nt-long variations of the natural I-TevI DNA spacer sequence. The PCR fragments were employed for KLD Enzyme Mix (NEB) reactions. The reactions were transformed into electrocompetent E. coli NEB10β cells.

We constructed the plasmid that expresses the I-TevI_Cas8 chimera. We synthesized as gBlock (IDT) the initial 618 bp of the i-tevI gene, which express the N-terminal 206 amino acids of the I-TevI nuclease, and we fused the fragment by cloning right upstream–and fused to–the cas8 gene in a high copy, chloramphenicol resistance backbone. We used NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (NEB) to clone the fragments in the plasmid backbone under the transcriptional control of the arabinose inducible promoter. The reactions were transformed into electrocompetent E. coli NEB10β cells.

We used the p2160 plasmid as the non-targeting control plasmid. We cloned the targeting variant of the non-targeting plasmids by digesting the non-targeting plasmids with the BsmBi-v2 (NEB) restriction enzyme and then ligating pre-annealed complementary ssDNA oligos, which code for the targeting spacer, into the digested plasmids using T4 DNA ligase (NEB). The ligation reactions were transformed into electrocompetent E. coli NEB10β.

We performed the plasmid targeting assays by constructing two E. coli MG1655 strains that express the IV-A3_Δcas8_ΔdinG system, the I-TevI_Cas8 chimera and either a targeting or a non-targeting crRNA. We transformed the strains with the target plasmid library. We counted the formed colonies from each transformation, and we calculated the relative reduction in the transformation efficiency of the targeting strain compared to the non-targeting strain that express the targeting crRNA and we normalized for the transformation efficiency of each strain with the control, empty vector.

Chromosomal targeting assays with I-TevI_Cas8

We inserted the 29-, 30-, 31-, 32-, and 33-nt-long I-TevI spacers into the chromosome of the E. coli MG1655 strain, using a previously established protocol [33], generating five I-TevI_Cas8 chromosomal target strains. All constructed strains were transformed with the plasmid that expresses the I-TevI_Cas8 chimera and the IV-A3 Δcas8_ΔdinG system. We counted the formed colonies from each transformation, and we calculated the relative reduction in the transformation efficiency of the strains when transformed with the targeting plasmid compared to the non-targeting plasmid. We normalized for the transformation efficiency of each strain with the control, empty vector.

Base-editing assays

We employed the b5815 strain for the base editing assays. The six protospacer-targets for the base-editing assays were selected to have a 5′-AAG-3′ PAM at their 5′ end and be located within a 249-bp-long region, comprised of the last 52 bp of the p70a promoter and the first 197 bp of the gfp sequence. Three of the protospacers were on the template strand and three were on the non-template strand.

Two categories of plasmids were designed and constructed for the base editing assays. The 1st category contained plasmids responsible for the expression of the pm_cda1 gene, which was fused to either the C-terminus of either the cas8,the dinG, or the cas5 gene. The expression of the chimeras was set under the transcriptional control of the arabinose inducible promoter. The backbone of the plasmids contained the chloramphenicol resistance marker gene, for selection on the antibiotic chloramphenicol, and the p15a ori. The 2nd category contained plasmids responsible for the expression of (i) the targeting and non-targeting crRNA expressing modules and (ii) different versions of the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas operon with either cas8, dinG, cas5, or both cas8 and cas5 genes being deleted. The expression of the crRNAs was set under the control of the original leader (promoter) sequence as it was found in the CRISPR locus of the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system. The expression of the operons was set under the transcriptional control of the constitutive promoter of the bolA gene present in the E. coli genome (pbolA). The backbone of the plasmids contains the tetracycline resistance marker gene, for selection on the antibiotic tetracycline, and the pSC101 ori.

For the base-editing assays, we transformed the 1st category of plasmids into electrocompetent b5815 cells, generating strains that express the Cas8_CDA1, DinG_CDA1, and Cas5_CDA1 chimeras. The strains were used for the preparation of electrocompetent cells, which were transformed with the appropriate plasmids from the 2nd category and either plated on LB agar plates supplemented with chloramphenicol and tetracycline or used to inoculate LB liquid cultures supplemented with the same antibiotics. The cultures were incubated for 20 h at 37°C with shaking at 220 rpm. Single colonies from the plates were randomly selected and subjected to colony PCR with genome specific primers. The PCR products were sequenced by Sanger sequencing and the results were analyzed for the detection of modifications. Genomic DNA was isolated from samples of the liquid cultures and sequenced using Illumina MiSeq®. Comparative analysis of the sequencing data was performed using the SNIPR Biome in-house bioinformatics pipeline, and the heatmap shown in Fig. 5E was generated using ggplot2 (v. 3.5.2).

Sanger base editing data analysis

The electropherogram files (.ab1) were parsed using BioPython SeqIO [39]. Trace data representing the four bases were extracted and peaks were detected using the find_peaks function from scipy.signal [40] with a minimum height of 20. If more than one base showed a peak at a position, the base editing ratio was calculated between the second highest peak and the sum of it and the highest peak. The consensus sequence was aligned to the reference sequence of the sequenced region using EMBOSS needle [41], and the base editing fraction was corrected to the reference sequence. The average editing fraction was calculated for each position across replicates, and positions with ≥10% base editing were plotted in a bar plot, with positions relative to the start of the protospacer on the X-axis.

Conjugation assay

Donor and recipient cells were inoculated in LB liquid medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics and 20 µg/ml DAP (diaminopimelic acid) and grown to early exponential phase (OD600 = 0.4). Donor and recipients were spotted for CFU counting and mixed at 5:1 (v:v) ratio. For each, 100 µl of mixed cells was pipetted onto the middle of an LB plate supplemented with 20 µg/ml DAP and incubated overnight at 37°C. The cells were scrapped off the plate and resuspended in 300 µl of LB. Ten-fold dilutions were spotted onto nonselective LB plates, as well as plates selective for donor and transconjugants. The plates were incubated overnight at 37°C, and the resulting colonies were counted.

IAA quantification in bacterial supernatant

The Salkowski assay was used for the detection of IAA [42]. The assay is based on the detection of pink color derived from the reaction of the Salkowski reagents (ferric chloride and perchloric acid) with IAA. In brief, bacterial overnight precultures, grown in LB medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics, were used for inoculation of M9 medium supplemented with tryptophan (1 mM), casamino acids and 0.4% (w/v) glucose, followed by overnight incubation at 37°C with rigorous shaking at 200 rpm. Bacterial cell cultures (OD600 = 0.3) were pelleted, and supernatants were incubated in the presence of Salkowski reagents for 45 min. The color change was assessed by measuring absorbance at 530 nm. A standard curve was used to calculate the IAA concentrations of each bacterial supernatant.

3D model illustration of IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system

The illustration in Supplementary Fig. S1A was rendered using blender v.4.2.3, using the Molecular Node add-on (https://extensions.blender.org/add-ons/molecularnodes/). The illustrated protein structure is obtained from the previous publication of a relative IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system [16].

Statistical analysis

All P-values shown as stars above individual bars in the bar plots were obtained from one-sample, one-sided Student’s t-tests in R version 4.3. Each test evaluates whether the mean of the log-transformed fold-changes (log2 or log10, as shown in the respective figure) is significantly greater than 0 (µ0 = log(1) = 0). This corresponds to testing whether the true average fold-change is greater than 1 (i.e. whether there is an increase compared to no change or a reduction). The data used to generate these P-values were all carried out in biological triplicates. P-values were all FDR-corrected together using p.adjust, with method “fdr” in R version 4.3.

For the results illustrated in Fig. 6E, measurements were obtained across four biological replicates, each with two technical replicates. To account for within-replicate correlation and allow each biological replicate to contribute independently to total variance, a series of linear mixed models were constructed. The response variable was the measured IAA concentration, with the biological construct used in the given experiment as the fixed effect, and a unique biological replicate ID used as the random effect. Models were constructed only using the data related to the biological constructs for that comparison. Estimated marginal means and pairwise contrasts were generated using the emmeans package (v. 1.11.2), and models were fitted using the lmer function from the lme4 package (v. 1.1-37).

Results

Characterization of a type IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system from K. pneumoniae

Using in silico analysis and wet lab experiments, we identified a type IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system on the putative conjugative plasmid of a pathogenic, carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae isolate that is part of the SNIPR Biome strain collection (Fig. 1A). The CRISPR array of the system contains 12 spacers, and in silico analysis identified some of the corresponding protospacers (Supplementary Table S1) that mostly reside on K. pneumoniae phages and plasmids (Supplementary Table S2). Plasmid-derived protospacers are primarily located within genes associated with plasmid conjugation and integration, whereas phage-derived protospacers are mainly found in genes encoding DNA- and substrate-binding proteins. Most protospacers were preceded by a 5′-AAG-3′ DNA motif (Supplementary Table S3), which aligned with the reported PAM for a previously characterized IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system [11]; hence, we considered this motif a probable PAM in our system. To verify the plasmid targeting activity of the system with the predicted PAM, we cloned the five predicted cas genes (csf1/cas8, csf5/cas6e/f, csf4/dinG, csf3/cas5, csf2/cas7) together with the first five spacers of the accompanying CRISPR array into a plasmid for expression in E. coli. Moreover, we constructed five target plasmids (pTarget1, pTarget2, pTarget3, pTarget4, and pTarget1-4) that carry the first four full-length spacers (32 bp each) of the native CRISPR array as targeted protospacers with the 5′-AAG-3′ motif at their 5′ end (Fig. 1B). All pTarget plasmids position the protospacer(s) between the origin of replication (ori) and antibiotic-resistance marker gene (arm), in close proximity to both. This positioning creates conditions in which interference with replication initiation and repression of antibiotic-resistance expression will simultaneously ensure a strong IV-A3 effect under selection—resulting in cell death—in line with previous observations for another IV-A3 system [11]. Transformation of the E. coli strain that expresses the wild-type IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system with the target plasmids reduced the transformation efficiency of the strain by three orders of magnitude compared to a non-target plasmid, demonstrating the functionality of the system (Fig. 1C).

After validating the plasmid targeting activity of the IV-A3 system, we sought to identify its PAM preferences. We substituted the PAM region on pTarget1 with a 5-nt-long degenerate sequence to construct a PAM plasmid library (Supplementary Fig. S1A). We electroporated the library into two IV-A3-expressing E. coli strains; the first expressed the wild-type CRISPR array, and the second expressed a non-targeting crRNA (Supplementary Fig. S1A). We deep-sequenced plasmids isolated from survivor colonies from both transformations, and we considered the 5′-AHN-3′, 5′-KAG-3′, 5′-AGG-3′, 5′-GAC-3′, and 5′-GTG-3′ motifs, which were comparatively depleted from the former strain, as type IV-A3 PAMs (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S1B).

Extending the characterization of the system, we investigated how mismatches between the targeting spacer and the targeted protospacer affect the plasmid targeting efficiency of the IV-A3 system. Based on pTarget1, we constructed a target plasmid library with each member containing a different number of mutations positioned among different protospacer regions (Fig. 1E). We then electroporated the library into an E. coli strain that expresses the IV-A3 system with the wild-type CRISPR array. Single or multiple mismatches that involve the first two PAM proximal nucleotides blocked the targeting function of the IV-A3 system, highlighting the expected importance of these positions (Fig. 1E). Notably, stretches of mismatches that involve the 6th to 9th PAM proximal nucleotides had limited effect on the targeting efficiency of the system (Fig. 1E). Meanwhile, stretches of three or more mismatches involving the 10th PAM-proximal nucleotide had detrimental effects on the targeting efficiency of the system (Fig. 1E), indicating the importance of the 10th PAM-proximal position in the seed region. On the PAM-distal side, only extensive consecutive mismatches (i.e. eight or more) hindered targeting (Fig. 1E). These results are consistent with previous in vitro findings showing that mismatches between the 4th and 8th PAM-distal nucleotides impair target binding by the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system, whereas mismatches at the two most PAM-distal positions have no negative effect and may even enhance binding [16].

Functional IV-A3 complexes assemble without Cas8 and DinG

Chromosomal targeting by nuclease-deficient type IV-A systems is not lethal but induces helicase-mediated transcriptional repression of targeted genes [6, 11]. We examined whether the same principle applies to chromosomal targeting in the present IV-A3 system. We integrated the green fluorescent protein (gfp) gene into the E. coli genome (Fig. 2A) and then transformed the resulting E. coli_gfp strain with plasmids that express the IV-A3 system (Fig. 2B), and each carried a distinct crRNA-expressing module that drove the targeting of a different protospacer, located either within, upstream, or downstream of the gfp gene (Fig. 2C). The IV-A3 system reduced GFP expression to negative control levels for protospacers within or in proximity (i.e. 200 bp upstream or downstream) to the gfp open reading frame (ORF; Fig. 2D), with the exception of protospacer 6R. This exception may reflect context-dependent variability in targeting efficiency, where proximity to the 3′ transcriptional terminator, with simultaneous dislodging orientation of the DinG helicase toward the terminator, negatively affects the helicase activity. GFP expression was also significantly downregulated when the IV-A3 system targeted protospacers were located up to 1.5 kb upstream of the gfp gene, whereas it was completely unaffected for protospacers 2 kb upstream of the gfp ORF (Fig. 2E). These results demonstrate the strong in vivo transcriptional silencing activity of the IV-A3 system, not only for targets within the gene or its promoter—as previously observed [11]—but also for targets located outside the gene cassette.

Aiming to explore the essentiality of the IV-A3 components in establishing an active complex, we constructed deletion mutant versions of the IV-A3 system that lacked either the Cas8 subunit, the DinG subunit, the Cas5, or both the Cas8 and DinG subunits (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S2A). The Cas5 and Cas8 subunits are involved in PAM sensing, R-loop formation, and DinG engagement [16], while DinG is the helicase expected to be responsible for the silencing activity of the system [11]. We employed these systems to target protospacers within or just upstream and downstream of the gfp gene in the E. coli_gfp chromosome (Supplementary Fig. S2B). The IV-A3_ΔdinG system strongly suppressed GFP expression when targeting protospacers located in the promoter region. In contrast, targeting the coding strand in a region that partially overlaps with the promoter, as well as protospacers located in the first half of the gene on the forward strand, resulted in only moderate to low GFP downregulation. The remaining protospacers did not affect GFP expression (Fig. 2F). The IV-A3_Δcas5 system did not affect GFP expression (Supplementary Fig. S2C), indicating that the Cas5 subunit is essential for the formation of IV-A3 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex with DNA binding activity strong enough to induce transcriptional downregulation.

The IV-A3_ΔCas8 and IV-A3_ΔCas8_ΔdinG systems reduced GFP expression in a similar manner when they targeted the coding strand of the promoter region (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Fig. S2D). These results indicate that Cas7, Cas5, and Cas6e/f subunits can form RNP complexes that bind their DNA target, however this binding does not suppress GFP expression as strongly as in the presence of Cas8. Moreover, the results underscore the crucial role of Cas8 in facilitating DinG loading onto the complex, consistent with a previously published IV-A3 structure [16]. This structure also confirmed the expected role of Cas8 in PAM recognition. Consequently, the absence of Cas8 in the active IV-A3_Δcas8_ΔdinG system may lead to relaxed PAM requirements. To test this hypothesis, we designed targets corresponding to protospacer sequences lacking a valid PAM at their 5′ end (“no-PAM” targets) within the promoter region of the gfp gene on the template strand (Supplementary Fig. S2E). The IV-A3_Δcas8_ΔdinG retains some binding activity, as observed by the statistically significant downregulation of GFP expression. This may be attributed to its capacity to bind pre-unwound or ssDNA, such as that found in transcription bubbles [16].

Protospacer positioning is key for plasmid targeting

Since type IV CRISPR–Cas systems are primarily involved in interference against conjugative plasmids [11], which are generally maintained at low copy numbers in bacterial hosts [43], we examined the transcriptional repression activity of the deletion mutant IV-A3 systems using a low-copy plasmid context. To this end, the chromosomally tested gfp expression cassette was cloned onto a low-copy plasmid, and protospacers located within the gfp gene and its promoter region were targeted (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S3A) with plasmids expressing the deletion mutant systems (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S3B). The IV-A3_ΔdinG system downregulated the GFP expression only when targeting the promoter region, showing higher efficiency for the spacer complementary to the coding strand used by RNA polymerase for binding and transcription initiation (Fig. 3C). No repression was observed for the other deletion variants (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S3C and D). The differences observed between chromosomal and plasmid repression may reflect the slightly higher copy number and distinct genetic context of the plasmid-borne gfp cassette, as well as the reduction in DNA-binding stability of complexes lacking Cas8, as we previously observed in our study.

Most spacers in the IV-A3 CRISPR array target protospacers within or near plasmid genes involved in integration and conjugation (Supplementary Table S1). This motivated us to test whether the system interferes with plasmid replication and/or with expression of a plasmid-maintenance gene (an antibiotic-resistance marker, arm). In parallel, we evaluated plasmid targeting efficiency as a function of the target’s distance from the ori and arm elements. Guided by our prior observation that DinG helicase activity on the chromosome appears confined to ~2 kb upstream and downstream of the protospacer—while acknowledging that this window may be context-dependent—we used pTarget1 to construct a library of plasmids carrying defined extensions between the protospacer, ori, and arm (Fig. 3E). Plasmid targeting assays (Fig. 3F) showed that placing a plasmid-borne protospacer 5 kb away from both arm and ori reduces targeting to no-protospacer control levels (Fig. 3G). In contrast, keeping either the arm or the ori proximal to the target yields transformation efficiencies as low as pTarget1, indicating that IV-A3 can efficiently block replication (when near ori) and antibiotic-resistance expression (when near arm) (Fig. 3G). Targeting plasmids with progressively increased protospacer-arm and protospacer-ori distances demonstrated that for distances up to 500 bp the IV-A3 system retained its targeting efficiency (Fig. 3G). However, for distances above 1 kb, the targeting efficiency of the system was lost (Fig. 3G) even when the arm and the protospacer were separated by a 1.2-kb region lacking predicted genes or promoter elements with transcriptional activity that could interfere with the function of DinG. Since IV-A CRISPR–Cas systems are involved in plasmid competition, this result indicates that the spacers within the wild-type IV-A3 CRISPR array do not necessarily have to be within important replication elements of targeted plasmids and phages, as previously demonstrated [11], but can also occur in close proximity to these elements.

We evaluated the potential of the I-TevI_Cas8 chimera to introduce dsDNA breaks on chromosomal and plasmid targets. These targets were designed to contain the canonical I-TevI recognition site (5′-CNNNG-3′) followed by a 32-nt-long linker sequence, the PAM (5′AAC-3′) and the protospacer (Fig. 4A).

Adapting the IV-A3 system into a base-editing platform

Aiming to explore the potential of the IV-A3 system as a base-editing platform, we connected the sea lamprey cytidine deaminase (PmCDA1)-uracil glycosylase inhibitor (UGI) fusion [24–26] to the C-termini of the Cas8, DinG, and Cas5 subunits. We anticipated that each base editor would orient the PmCDA1 domain differently with respect to its substrate, the non-target DNA strand. In addition, we expected that the helicase activity of DinG would increase the accessible PmCDA1 substrate by unwinding dsDNA beyond the protospacer region. The chromosomally integrated gfp of the E. coli_gfp strain was employed as target for the base-editing assays, and two groups of plasmids were designed and constructed (Fig. 5A). The first group contained plasmids expressing either the IV-A3_Δcas8, IV-A3_ΔdinG, IV-A3_Δcas5, or IV-A3_Δcas8_ΔdinG system and crRNAs expressing modules that target protospacers within the gfp gene (Fig. 5B). The second group contained plasmids expressing the Cas8_PmCDA1, DinG_PmCDA1, or Cas5_PmCDA1 chimera (Fig. 5B). The four combinations of plasmids depicted in Fig. 5B yielded the Cas8_BE, Cas8(ΔdinG)_BE, DinG_BE, and Cas5_BE base-editing systems, each of which was electroporated into E. coli_gfp. The gfp region from the resulting colonies was PCR-amplified and sequenced.

The base-editing assays showed that each system yielded different base-editing profiles on the same targets. The Cas8_BE system did not introduce any mutations when targeting protospacers within the promoter region (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B, and C) but did generate clones with multiple and efficient C-to-T mutations for three out of the remaining four tested protospacers (Fig. 5C amd Supplementary Fig. S4D and E). Especially for protospacer 3F, the editing window of the Cas8_BE system extended 79-bp downstream of the protospacer (Fig. 5C). In contrast, the Cas8_BE system produced a higher number of C-to-T substitutions (Fig. 5C and D, and Supplementary Fig. S4D), which were frequently observed both within and, predominantly, outside the protospacer region, whereas the Cas8(ΔdinG)_BE system generated fewer edits overall. These results suggest that the helicase activity of DinG increases the base-editing efficiency and extends the base-editing window of the Cas8_BE system, possibly by extending the exposed ssDNA substrate of the deaminase. The Cas5_BE system edited all six targeted protospacers, regardless of positioning and orientation, successfully converting, in most cases, all the cytidines that were outside the seed region within these protospacers and, in some cases, a bit downstream of the protospacers (Fig. 5C). The DinG_BE system resulted in less efficient base editing compared to the other systems. Nonetheless, the editing window for protospacer 3F, which was efficiently edited, extended 178-nt downstream of the protospacer sequence. Aiming to examine whether further incubation of unedited clones could enhance base editing, we streaked a single unedited colony that expressed the DinG_BE system targeting protospacer 3R and incubated for 20 h at 37°C. This approach resulted in colonies with edits at multiple positions, albeit not as many as observed among the other systems, and within a window well above 300 nt that extended both upstream and mostly downstream of the protospacer (Supplementary Fig. S4F).

We further sought to investigate how the DNA scanning activity of the type IV-A3 system, its previously observed spacer–protospacer mismatch tolerance, the relative positioning of PmCDA1 with respect to the formed R-loops and ssDNA, and the recruitment of DinG—particularly in the DinG_BE system—affect the generation of genome-wide off-target edits by the designed base editors. We transformed E. coli_gfp with the dual-plasmid base-editing systems, this time expressing the 3F, 3R, and non-targeting crRNAs. Recovered transformants were inoculated into liquid medium for overnight growth, and the resulting cultures were used for whole-genome DNA isolation and sequencing. The on-target editing profiles confirmed our observations from the colony screening experiments, except for the DinG_BE 3F system, which in this experiment showed extensive but low-depth editing across a broad editing window (Fig. 5E). Surprisingly, the Cas8_BE, Cas8(ΔDinG)_BE, and DinG_BE systems displayed low-depth off-target editing at multiple genomic loci (Supplementary Table S4). The off-target patterns were highly similar between the two Cas8-based systems and appeared largely independent of the expressed crRNAs or the presence of DinG. Notably, 17 off-target edits identified for the Cas8_BE and Cas8(ΔDinG)_BE systems targeting the 3F protospacer shared a common pattern corresponding to the first six nucleotides of the 3F protospacer and were preceded by a 5′-AAG-3′ PAM (5′-AAGTTCAGC-3′, with the final cytidine being edited). This suggests that the system may require only short spacer–protospacer complementarity to enable detectable cytidine deaminase activity. No further sequence similarity could be detected between the targeted protospacers and the identified off-target sites, warranting further investigation into the underlying mechanisms. Similar trend was observed for the off-target sites edited by the DinG_BE system, with the notable difference that the majority of the observed edited cytidines were preceded by a thymine, consistent with the reported preference of PmCDA1 for cytidines flanked by thymines at the 5′ side [44, 45]. In contrast, the Cas5_BE system showed only a limited number of low-depth off-target edits, demonstrating that it represents the most precise variant within the IV-A3 base-editing platform. Further testing demonstrated that expression of the Cas–PmCDA1 chimeras alone does not produce detectable off-target edits (Fig. 4E).

We repeated the same process with base editors containing PmCDA1 fused to the N-termini of the Cas8, DinG, and Cas5 subunits, but we detected no notable editing (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Employing IV-A3 base editing to enhance IAA production

The absence of identified anti-CRISPRs for type IV CRISPR–Cas systems renders the base editors reported here particularly suitable for microbiome engineering applications. One such application involves the in situ engineering of gut microbes to produce IAA, a molecule previously reported to modulate gut inflammation and support intestinal homeostasis [46]. This can be achieved by using a donor strain carrying the IAA production pathway (Fig. 6A) on a conjugative plasmid, enabling in situ conjugation of the IAA plasmid to the gut microbiome. To further enhance IAA production, it is essential to deactivate the tryptophan repressor gene (trpR), which represses chorismate synthesis, trpEDCA expression, which converts chorismate to indole, and mtr proton symporter expression, which facilitates tryptophan and indole uptake (Fig. 6A).[47]

Discussion and conclusions

In this study, we identified and characterized a type IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system, found on a putative conjugative plasmid of a pathogenic carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae isolate. Using deep sequencing of a PAM plasmid library after differential selection [48, 49], we observed a nuanced PAM preference dominated by 5′-AAN-3′, 5′-ANG-3′ and 5′-AHA-3′ sequences. This specificity is more refined than the 5′-GNAWN-3′ PAM for the IV-A1 CRISPR–Cas system [17], but less strict than the 5′-AAG-3′ PAM previously reported for another IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system, despite the sequence similarity between the two systems [11]. Sliding window mutagenesis of targeted protospacers indicated that single or double spacer–protospacer mismatches in the first two PAM-proximal nucleotides block targeting, highlighting the importance of these positions. However, the targeting efficiency of the system is not affected by single mismatches after the two PAM proximal nucleotides or by double and triple mismatches that do not involve the first three PAM proximal nucleotides. Unexpectedly, three or more mismatches that involve the 10th PAM-proximal nucleotide markedly impaired targeting efficiency, highlighting the importance of this position in the seed region [16]. In juxtaposition, the previously reported IV-A1 CRISPR–Cas system was intolerant to single mismatches at the 1st and 4th PAM-proximal positions, as well as multiple mismatches that include the 4th PAM proximal nucleotide [16].

Various IV-A CRISPR–Cas systems have been used for DinG-based transcriptional repression of targeted genes [11, 15, 16]. Similarly, the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system reported in this study downregulated the transcription of a chromosomally integrated gfp gene to negative control levels when it targeted protospacers within the promoter region, the gfp ORF or regions directly upstream or downstream of the gfp gene. The targeting of protospacers up to 1.5 kb upstream of the promoter region and up to 1 kb downstream of the gfp ORF also resulted in repression of GFP expression, albeit with less efficiency. Comparable activity has been reported for the Pseudomonas oleovorans type IV-A1 CRISPR–Cas system [6]. Furthermore, our findings on plasmid targets indicate that the transcriptional silencing activity of DinG, in conjunction with the protospacer’s location relative to key plasmid replication and maintenance elements, dictate the system’s capability to prevent plasmid propagation. These findings demonstrate that the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system can targeting regions outside genes and DNA elements that are vital for plasmid propagation and conjugation or phage infection. Hence, the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas system could be leveraged to efficiently and effectively screen for critical, yet unidentified and uncharacterized, components of genetic elements such as large conjugative plasmids and phages.

We further assessed the essentiality of DinG, Cas8, and Cas5 in establishing a stable and active IV-A3 complex. We excluded the Cas6 and Cas7 subunits from this assessment, as the forme—a homolog of the type I-E and I-F Cas6—is essential for CRISPR repeat processing in type IV systems and the latter forms the backbone of the IV-A3 complex [13, 16]. We deleted each corresponding gene from the IV-A3-expressing operon, and we employed the resulting deletion mutant systems for the transcriptional repression of a chromosomally inserted gfp gene. The IV-A3_ΔdinG system downregulated GFP expression when targeting the promoter region or the template DNA strand at the beginning of the gene, but it did not affect gene expression when targeting the coding DNA strand or any target further downstream of the promoter, corroborating previous findings [6, 11]. The IV-A3_Δcas8 and IV-A3_Δcas8_ΔdinG systems reduced GFP expression to comparable but low levels when targeting the coding strand of the promoter region. Given the role of Cas8 in PAM recognition, its absence in the active IV-A3_Δcas8_ΔdinG variant resulted in relaxed PAM requirements and the system mediated significant GFP downregulation when targeting no-PAM sites. These results suggest that truncated IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas complexes, capable of binding DNA, can assemble without Cas8 and DinG, but not without Cas5. This is consistent with the published IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas structure, which highlights Cas5 as essential for complex formation, as it interacts with the Cas7 backbone, links Cas8 to the rest of the complex, and contributes to DinG recruitment [16].

The discovery or design of highly specific and compact CRISPR-based DNA nucleases is sought after to advance precise genome editing tools and CRISPR-based antimicrobials [50–52]. A rare IV-A system with dsDNA nuclease activity was recently reported [18], and its nucleolytic activity was found to rely on an HNH domain at the C-terminus of its DinG subunit. To create a compact and specific nuclease, we decided to pursue an alternative approach, taking advantage of the strong binding activity of the helicase-deficient IV-A3_ΔdinG system and the small size of the corresponding operon (3.2 kb). We engineered a chimeric system with dsDNA nucleolytic function by fusing the N-terminal nuclease domain of the I-TevI nuclease [32] to the N-terminus of Cas8. We demonstrated that the developed chimera has nucleolytic activity that reduces the transformation efficiency of E. coli by up to four orders of magnitude when targeting chromosomal and plasmid-borne targets. The IV-A3_I-TevI-Cas8 chimera can be expressed from an operon with smaller size than the spcas9 gene (3.8 kb versus 4.1 kb), and it requires correctly interspaced PAM and I-TevI recognition sites for dsDNA cleavage, increasing the specificity of its nuclease activity. However, this increased specificity comes with a trade-off: since the chimera requires both a PAM and an adjacent I-TevI recognition site, its targeting range is narrowed, restricting the number of genomic loci it can efficiently target compared to SpCas9.

The rapid and efficient nature of base-editing tools has popularized their utilization in microbial and microbiome gene-editing applications that focus on the exploration of microbial phenotypes by introducing premature stop codons and the generation of mutation libraries [53–57]. Even though the use of alternative CRISPR–Cas orthologs [58, 59] and deaminases [60] has somewhat enlarged the base-editing windows in microbes, bacterial cytidine deamination tools usually have small windows of activity [25, 26] and show a preference for the sequence context of the targeted cytidines [61], thus limiting or complicating their application. Furthermore, Class 1 CRISPR–Cas systems have rarely been utilized for developing base editors, with the deaminase being fused to the nuclease subunit [62].

We hypothesized that fusing the PmCDA1 cytidine deaminase to different subunits of the IV-A3 complex would position it at distinct locations relative to the non-target strand, thereby producing variable base-editing outcomes. To test this, we fused PmCDA1 to either terminus of the Cas8, DinG, or Cas5 subunits, and we tested each system on the same protospacers. Base editors with C-terminal PmCDA1 fusions successfully edited some or all targets with varying efficiencies, exhibiting distinct context preferences and differences in the size of their editing windows. More specifically, the Cas5 base editor achieved efficient editing of nearly all cytidines in the post-seed region across most tested protospacers, minimizing context preference effects generally observed by other CRISPR–Cas–PmCDA1 base editors [44, 45]. This result indicates stable and proximate positioning of PmCDA1 relative to the non-target strand. The helicase activity of DinG substantially improved the editing efficiency and expanded the activity window of the Cas8 base editor, possibly prolonging and extending the exposure of the non-target strand to the deaminase. The DinG base editor produced the broadest editing window (up to 388 bp) with extended incubation time, offering the potential for long-range in vivo genome mutagenesis for pathway optimization. This approach mirrors the recently reported HACE system in mammalian cells [63], achieving results without inducing dsDNA breaks that might compromise genome stability.

Detection of base-editing off-targets, via whole-genome sequencing, revealed that several IV-A3-based base editors, particularly those containing Cas8, introduce off-target edits of low editing depth at multiple genomic loci. These off-targets often shared short regions of complementarity to the 3F protospacer and an AAG PAM, suggesting that limited spacer–protospacer pairing is sufficient for PmCDA1-mediated deamination. The DinG_BE variant displayed a similar pattern but with a sequence bias consistent with PmCDA1’s TC preference [44, 45], while the Cas5_BE system exhibited the highest precision with minimal off-target activity. No off-target edits were detected when expressing the Cas–PmCDA1 fusions alone, indicating that guide RNA-dependent binding is required to initiate unwanted deamination events.

Type IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas systems have been identified exclusively in Klebsiella strains, with no anti-CRISPR proteins yet reported to deactivate these systems. This makes IV-A3 systems promising candidates for adaptation into microbiome engineering tools, delivered via conjugation to a microbiome. To explore this potential, we utilized the Cas5-based base-editing system to enhance production of IAA, a compound known to promote gastrointestinal mucosal function [46, 64–66], by an E. coli strain. We achieved this by introducing the IAA biosynthesis pathway into the β10a conjugative plasmid, alongside the Cas5_BE base editor—the most accurate and consistent of the base-editing systems developed in this study—and a crRNA-expressing module designed to target the tryptophan repressor (trpR) gene in the E. coli genome, with the aim of inserting a premature stop codon into trpR. Deactivation of trpR removes the feedback on the synthesis and transport of two precursors in IAA production, indole and tryptophan, thus increasing the intracellular IAA pool [67]. Upon conjugating the engineered plasmid into the E. coli recipient strain, the base editor successfully deactivated trpR, resulting in a strain with a 30% increase in IAA production compared to a strain with the IAA pathway and an intact trpR gene.

In summary, we refined the known protospacer specificity and identified the crucial role of Cas5 in the formation of the IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas complex. To enable, the naturally nuclease deficient, IV-A3 system for DNA sequence specific killing, we fused the nuclease domain of the I-TevI nuclease to the N-terminus of Cas8, resulting in dsDNA breaks in the E. coli chromosome. Additionally, we enhanced the IV-A3 system with a cytidine deaminase resulting in various genome editing efficiencies depending on the attached subunit (Cas8, Cas5, or DinG). Finally, to demonstrate the usefulness of this platform for microbiome engineering, we mobilized the Cas5–PmCDA1 base editor on an engineered conjugative plasmid delivered into E. coli and deactivated the tryptophan repressor gene, resulting in increased IAA production. Our study offers new insights into type IV-A3 CRISPR–Cas systems and their potential applications for microbiome engineering therapeutics.
