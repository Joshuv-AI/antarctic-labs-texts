Electrical storm (ES) is a cardiovascular emergency defined by ≥3 episodes of sustained or treated ventricular tachycardia (VT) or ventricular fibrillation (VF) separated by ≥5 minutes within 24 hours.1 Treatment of ES is challenging. Consensus guidance from the European Society of Cardiology recommends stellate ganglion blockade (SGB) when ES is refractory to beta-blockers and amiodarone.1 Although SGB has been shown to be an effective treatment for ES, less is known about what factors may help mark or predict procedural success. In addition, it is unknown whether or how SGB affects electrocardiographic (ECG) changes that might serve as markers of successful therapy. We hypothesized that changes in heart rate and conduction intervals would occur and would predict a positive procedural response.

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study. The research reported in this article adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study was approved by the Duke Institutional Review Board. Data from patients undergoing SGB for ES between 2015 and 2020 at Duke University Hospital were included for analysis. Patients with active ischemia before revascularization were excluded. Heart rate, PR interval, QRS duration, corrected QT (QTc) interval, and T peak to T end (Tp-Te) duration were collected from standard 12-lead ECGs of patients before and after undergoing SGB. Type of pacing, if present, was also noted. A paired Student t test or Wilcoxon signed rank test was used to compare measurements within subjects. P < .05 was considered significant. Preprocedure values were subtracted from postprocedure values to calculate differences, which were then compared across the entire cohort and subgroups of patients: 24-hour responders, 48-hour responders, and bilateral vs unilateral blocks.2 Response is defined as the absence of VT/VF at 24 or 48 hours.

Among 49 patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 47 were male with a mean age of 61 ± 11 years, a body mass index of 32.4 ± 6.5 kg/m2, and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 23% ± 9%. Overall, 26 had ischemic cardiomyopathy, 23 had nonischemic cardiomyopathy, 37 had monomorphic VT, 30 had polymorphic VT, 20 had VF, and 19 had both VT and VF. Among 33 paced patients, programmed mode was DDD in 17, VVI in 8, AAI to minimize ventricular pacing in 5, and unknown in 3. Rate-responsive programming was enabled in 14 of 30 (47%). We also analyzed ECG changes according to therapeutic response to SGB (Table 1). Like the overall results, in the 24-hour responders and 48-hour responders, there were no statistically significant changes in heart rate, PR, QRS, QTc interval, or Tp-Te duration. Given that anesthetic half-lives differ, we performed subgroup analyses by bupivacaine (n = 15) vs ropivacaine (n = 33) and by repeat injection (n = 23); significant ECG changes were not observed in any of these subgroups. Finally, we also evaluated ECG changes in those who underwent bilateral (n = 19) and unilateral SGB (n = 30). There were no differences in ECG changes in those treated with unilateral vs bilateral SGB.

In our single-center analysis, we did not observe any ECG changes after SGB. In particular, there were no changes in the RR interval, PR interval, QRS duration, QTc interval, or Tp-Te duration. Furthermore, there were no changes regardless of whether the rhythms were paced, response to SGB, anesthetic agent, repeat injection, or laterality of the block. The numerical decrease in heart rate of just 3 bpm is also well below a threshold of 15 bpm, which has previously been used to denote therapeutic response to autonomic treatments of arrhythmias.3 These lack of changes were not caused by inefficacy of the procedure, given that the cohort experienced a significant reduction in VT/VF.2 The results contrast with some studies after surgical sympathectomy where decreases in heart rate and increases in PR and QTc intervals were observed.4,5

The limitations of our study include the modest cohort size and heterogeneity of ECG collection times. The variability in ECG acquisition (median pre-SGB 27 hours [IQR 52 hours]; post-SGB 37 hours [IQR 124 hours]) reflects real-world practice but introduces potential temporal confounding given that short-lived ECG changes may have gone undetected. Still, sensitivity analyses limited to ECGs obtained within 48 hours periprocedurally and when stratified by pacing yielded consistent findings, suggesting that these factors are unlikely to account for the negative results (Table 1). In addition, antiarrhythmic drug therapy, medications used for sedation, and anesthesia could affect ECG findings, and their timing relative to ECG acquisition was not systematically captured.

In our periprocedural ECG analysis of SGB for ES, standard 12-lead intervals did not change meaningfully despite clinical arrhythmia suppression, arguing against the surface ECG as a procedural biomarker and redirecting the search for response predictors toward dynamic autonomic measures. Frequency- and time-domain indices of heart rate variability derived from telemetry or ambulatory monitoring were not available in this retrospective cohort.6 Objective physiological confirmation of sympathetic block such as skin temperature rise by infrared thermography or development of Horner syndrome could verify technical success of the block independent of arrhythmia outcome,7 and pairing these with heart rate variability metrics may yield more sensitive surrogates of procedural efficacy. Future studies investigating the utility and success of SGB in ES should include assessments of ECG parameters, including both static and dynamic measurements.
