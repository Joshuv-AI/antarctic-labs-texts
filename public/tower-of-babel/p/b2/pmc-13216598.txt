Physics-guided contrastive temporal graph learning for anomaly detection and root-cause localization in industrial control systems
Authors: M. Rajalakshmi, T. Velmurugan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Industrial control systems generate complex multivariate time series and detecting anomalies without labelled attacks is still difficult. In this work we propose a physics guided contrastive temporal graph learning framework for anomaly detection and root-cause localization in ICS. The method follows three main steps. First, a self-supervised contrastive encoder is trained only on normal data to learn robust representations of sensor windows. Augmented views of the same signal are used so that normal behaviour is pulled together in latent space. Second, a physics guided graph is built from the plant P&ID where sensors and actuators are nodes and physical dependencies form edges. Third, a temporal graph module operates on the learned embeddings to capture both time evolution and cross-sensor relations. Anomaly score is computed from graph representations and node level scores are used to rank possible root causes. Experiments on ICS dataset show higher detection performance than LSTM-AE and CNN-AE baselines. Additional robustness tests such as distribution shift, sensor drift and missing channels are conducted to study practical behaviour. The main innovation of this work is the combination of contrastive learning with physics guided graph reasoning without using any attack labels, and the ability to provide interpretable sensor ranking for operators.

Introduction

Industrial Control Systems (ICS) are becoming more important than ever because most of the essential services we use in our daily life depends on them, such as electricity supply, clean water distribution, oil and gas pipelines, manufacturing plants and many others. In the past, these systems were mostly isolated and worked as stand-alone setups inside a plant. But with the fast growth of digital technologies, cloud platforms, IIoT devices and remote monitoring features, ICS are now integrated with corporate networks, and sometimes even connected indirectly or directly to the internet. This new connectivity brings many advantages but it also creates several new types of cyber security risks. Attackers now have more ways to enter these systems, manipulate the sensors or actuators, and disturb the physical processes. When this happens, it can cause serious safety issues, financial losses, and may even lead to complete shutdown of the plant.

Due to these emerging risks, anomaly detection in ICS has become an essential research area. The goal of anomaly detection is to identify unusual behaviors in sensor data or control actions that do not follow the normal operation of the system. Detecting anomalies in Industrial Control Systems is still a very challenging task because the data is multivariate, highly non-linear and deeply connected with the physical relations between different components. Simple threshold methods or traditional machine learning models are usually not sufficient, since they cannot capture the complicated interactions happening inside a real industrial process. In addition, labeled attack data is very rare, as most industries do not share their security incidents openly. Due to this shortage of labeled attack samples, supervised learning approaches become less practical in real scenarios.

To overcome these problems, many researchers have started to use intelligent techniques such as deep learning and representation learning. In recent years, contrastive learning has received much attention because it can learn useful features from unlabeled normal data by comparing different augmented views of the same time-series signals. This approach helps to learn a strong representation of normal ICS behaviour without depending on attack labels. But even though contrastive learning is effective, it still does not consider the physical structure of the ICS environment. In actual systems, sensors and actuators are not working independently. They follow strict physical rules and process flows, and any abnormal event normally propagates through these connections. Ignoring these relationships may cause false alarms or even miss some real attacks.

Combining self-supervised contrastive learning with physics-guided graph modelling gives a more reliable solution. A physics-guided graph can be constructed from the system P&ID diagrams, where every sensor and actuator is considered as a node and their physical interactions are modeled as edges. When this graph structure is integrated with graph neural networks or graph transformers, the model can understand both the temporal behaviour and the physical dependencies at the same time.This hybrid approach helps in detecting zero-day attacks, reducing false positives, and identifying the exact location of the anomaly inside the process.

The SWaT (Secure Water Treatment) and WADI datasets are main benchmark for evaluating such techniques because it contains real industrial sensor data with different types of cyber attacks in addition HAI and BATADAL are also used. Using SWaT along with physics-guided contrastive graph learning provides a very promising direction for building more secure and intelligent ICS anomaly detection systems. Many previous works rely on labelled attack data and treat sensors as independent time series, which ignores the real physical relations in ICS. Network based or statistical approaches cannot explain process level faults, while graph models often need supervised training and are not suitable for zero-day situations. In this work, we try to address these gaps by combining self-supervised contrastive learning with a physics guided graph model. The main contributions are: learning representations only from normal data without attack labels, integrating P&ID based physical dependencies into temporal graph reasoning, and providing interpretable root-cause ranking with additional robustness tests. These points highlight how our approach differs from existing methods.

Literature review

Industrial control systems (ICS) and supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) infrastructures are at the heart of modern critical services such as power grids, water treatment, transportation and manufacturing. Early work mostly focused on explaining what SCADA actually is and how it differs from classical IT systems, emphasizing their real-time and safety-critical nature3,4. As more case studies of cyber incidents emerged, forensics and incident response in control systems became an active topic, highlighting the difficulty of reconstructing events and attributing attacks in such environments1. More recently, Nankya et al. gave a comprehensive overview of ICS components, cyber threats and machine-learning driven defense strategies, underlining that traditional signature-based tools alone are no longer sufficient for protecting these systems2. Buchanan’s systematic review since Stuxnet further shows how attack sophistication has grown over the last decade and how adversaries increasingly target physical processes rather than just network perimeters4. Alongside, broader surveys on anomaly detection strategies for cyber–physical systems (CPS) stress that the interplay between cyber and physical domains introduces new failure modes and evaluation challenges5,46.

Classical and process-oriented anomaly detection in ICS

Before deep learning became dominant, a significant body of work explored process-aware and statistical approaches for ICS anomaly detection. Myers et al. proposed one of the earlier methods using process mining, learning normal process models from event logs and then flagging deviations as anomalies7. In a related work, the same authors used process discovery techniques to detect cyber attacks by identifying unusual control flow patterns in ICS logs8. reveal critical yet unresolved challenges across privacy, application, and network security domains. The membership inference analysis in transfer learning demonstrates that training data of teacher models can be exposed through student models, even under restricted access; however, the work is confined to white-box settings and limited datasets, leaving black-box scenarios, large foundation models, and practical defense mechanisms largely unexplored9. These approaches are attractive because they provide interpretable models that can be related back to engineering workflows, but they tend to struggle when the system exhibits high variability or when the logs are incomplete.

To better capture the tight coupling between control logic and physical behaviour, Sheng et al. developed a cyber–physical model for SCADA systems and embedded intrusion detection rules into that model10. Their work shows that combining cyber events with process variables improves detection, but at the cost of significant manual modeling effort. Cheng et al. argued that anomaly detection in industrial CPS must rethink the notion of “operation pattern” and proposed methods that explicitly model operational modes and transitions, rather than assuming a single stationary regime7. Other studies have used probabilistic models and automata to capture temporal dynamics. Venkatraman, for instance, designed a time-dependent anomaly detection system for smart environments using probabilistic timed automata23, while Bhattacharya and Ray used probabilistic finite state automata to classify chaotic dynamics and combustion instability25, pointing to the usefulness of symbolic dynamics in complex processes.

Network-level monitoring and periodicity-based methods

Another large branch of ICS anomaly detection focuses on communication patterns rather than sensor values. Flow-based monitoring has been widely studied in smart grids and other ICS networks. Matoušek et al. proposed flow-based monitoring of ICS communication in a smart grid context, extracting features from network flows to detect unusual patterns11. Exploiting periodicity is a recurring idea: Barbosa et al. showed that ICS traffic is often highly periodic and demonstrated how deviation from this periodic structure can reveal anomalies12. Building on this, Van Splunder and others studied periodicity detection in network traffic for anomaly purposes14, and Akpinar and Ozcelik proposed a methodology to determine device-level periodicity in EtherCAT-based control networks for anomaly detection13. More recently, Smolarczyk et al. addressed anomaly detection in cyclic communication for OT protocols, showing that subtle timing and sequence deviations can be used as indicators of compromise36. The Vulseye framework advances smart contract testing through stateful directed fuzzing, but its dependence on static vulnerability patterns and Ethereum-centric design restricts generalization to cross-chain and logic-level vulnerabilities [w].

Network-based methods are appealing because they can be deployed without touching the control logic or PLCs, but they often do not fully capture the physical side of the process. Flame and flow inconsistencies might not be clear from traffic alone. Jiang and Chen therefore combined network-traffic features with machine-learning classifiers to perform anomaly detection and classification for ICS12, showing improved performance but still largely focusing on the cyber layer. Gutierrez-Rojas proposed a three-layer machine-learning framework that combines different views of the industrial environment (e.g., host, network and process) to detect anomalies in a more holistic way27. These works hint that multi-layer or multi-view approaches are needed in practice.

Statistical, correlation and hybrid models

Statistical approaches remain important both as baselines and as components inside hybrid methods. Classical three-sigma rules and hypothesis testing are still often used to set thresholds in industrial contexts10,44. Saci et al. introduced an anomaly detection method that integrates autocorrelation with Gaussian-based modeling using sensory data from industrial manufacturing42, capturing temporal dependencies in a simple but effective way. Jadidi et al. proposed a correlation-based anomaly detection framework in ICS, using correlation changes between sensor readings as an indicator of abnormal conditions36. Hybrid approaches combine behavioural modeling with probabilistic reasoning. Faramondi et al. developed a framework that integrates behaviour-based detection with Bayesian networks for cyber–physical anomaly detection37. Their method can encode expert knowledge about causal relationships, but still relies on fairly explicit modeling of dependencies. Although WAFBooster significantly improves rejection of mutated payloads, its regex-based rule generation and reliance on existing attack corpora may not effectively address semantic zero-day threats or high-throughput deployment constraints41.

Another line of work investigates transfer learning and domain adaptation. Wang proposed an anomaly detection framework for ICS based on transfer learning, which aims to reuse models trained in one plant or configuration for another, thereby reducing data requirements31. This is particularly relevant because many ICS sites cannot easily label attack data or share it. Taken together, these works show that purely static or purely statistical approaches are usually insufficient, but they still contribute useful ideas such as correlation analysis, Bayesian fusion and principled thresholding.

Deep learning and representation learning in ICS and IIoT

With the rapid progress in deep learning, many authors have applied neural networks for anomaly detection in ICS and IIoT. Priyanga et al. proposed an enhanced PCA combined with a hypergraph-based convolutional neural network (EPCA-HG-CNN) to detect cyberattacks in ICS30. Their approach uses PCA to reduce dimensionality and then employs a hypergraph convolution structure to capture higher-order relationships among process variables. Du introduced RRCNN, a request–response-based convolutional neural network designed specifically for ICS network traffic anomaly detection, focusing on modelling the semantics of ICS protocol exchanges38. Huang et al. looked at false data injection (FDI) attack detection using both time- and frequency-domain analysis of sensor data, and employed deep learning models to learn discriminative features from these domains40.

Beyond individual models, several papers tackle the broader challenge of limited labeled data and unbalanced classes. Abusitta et al. presented a deep learning–enabled anomaly detection framework for IoT systems that discusses model architectures, training strategies and deployment considerations27. Gao et al. proposed a deep process anomaly detection method that explicitly deals with low discriminative and insufficient abnormal data by learning representative latent features29. Bi et al. combined stacked sparse “shrink” autoencoders with an improved XGBoost classifier for network anomaly detection, showing that combining deep feature extraction with gradient-boosted trees can yield strong performance28. The TCG-IDS model achieves impressive detection using temporal contrastive graph learning, yet evaluation on limited datasets, vulnerability to concept drift, and computational overhead for real-time edge environments remain open issues45. These directions move closer to representation learning, where the focus is on learning good latent spaces rather than hand-crafted features.

Federated and communication-efficient anomaly detection in IIoT

In parallel, anomaly detection in industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) scenarios has raised concerns about privacy, bandwidth and scalability. Genge et al. studied anomaly detection in aging IIoT systems, addressing the fact that device behaviour and data distributions drift over time31. Liu proposed a deep anomaly detection approach for time-series data in IIoT using a communication-efficient on-device federated learning framework36. This work integrates an attention-based CNN–LSTM anomaly detector with gradient compression to reduce communication while preserving accuracy. Extending this idea, Liu et al. designed communication-efficient federated learning schemes for anomaly detection in IIoT, exploring aggregation and compression strategies at the edge37.

These federated learning techniques address data privacy and bandwidth constraints by keeping raw data local and sharing only model updates. They are highly relevant for modern ICS environments, where multiple plants or remote stations may contribute to a global detection model but cannot centralise sensitive operational data. However, one limitation in many of these works is that they often treat the time series as independent across devices and do not fully exploit the physical or logical topology of the underlying control process.

Time-series and service-level anomaly detection

Time-series anomaly detection has also been extensively investigated in cloud and service environments. Zuo et al. proposed an intelligent anomaly detection scheme for micro-services architectures that jointly analyses temporal and spatial data patterns38. Huang et al. presented a time-series anomaly detection framework for ensuring trustworthy services in cloud computing systems, showing how deep models can capture complex service performance dynamics39. While these works are not strictly ICS-specific, they introduce powerful ideas such as context-aware anomaly detection and spatio-temporal feature fusion that can be transplanted into industrial settings. Similarly, general reviews such as Ruff’s unifying survey on deep and shallow anomaly detection summarize a wide range of architectures (autoencoders, one-class classifiers, GAN-based methods) and training regimes that are applicable to both IT and OT domains42.

Graph-based and structure-aware methods

A key trend in recent ICS research is the move towards structure-aware models that explicitly encode process topology or interaction graphs. Shuaiyi Lyu et al. introduced a process-oriented heterogeneous graph learning framework for GNN-based ICS anomalous pattern recognition43. Their method constructs heterogeneous graphs that capture associativity between different process variables and uses graph neural networks to recognise fine-grained anomaly patterns. This work demonstrates that incorporating process structures can significantly improve detection and interpretability compared to purely sequence-based models. In a similar spirit, Priyanga’s EPCA-HG-CNN already hinted at the usefulness of hypergraph structures for ICS30, although it did not fully address temporal dynamics.

Recent advances in graph-based learning have demonstrated the effectiveness of contrastive and augmentation strategies for improving representation quality in graph neural networks. Zhao et al.32 introduced graph data augmentation to enhance semi-supervised node classification, while Yin et al.33 proposed AutoGCL with learnable view generators to adaptively create contrastive samples. Hassani and Khasahmadi34 explored multi-view contrastive learning on structural and diffusion graphs, and Chen et al.35 further incorporated augmentation-aware objectives to improve generalization. However, these methods are mainly designed for generic graph benchmarks and do not consider the physical semantics of industrial networks, where node relations are governed by process topology and system dynamics. Moreover, prior works focus on representation learning and classification accuracy, with little support for root cause localization required in real fault diagnosis scenarios. To address these gaps, the proposed work integrates contrastive learning with a physics-informed graph construction and introduces a localization mechanism that links learned embeddings to specific process components, enabling interpretable and actionable diagnosis beyond existing approaches.

Beyond ICS, Zhao et al. proposed a combination of Graph Attention Network (GAT) and Informer for multivariate time-series anomaly detection44. They showed that combining attention-based graph modelling with efficient long-sequence transformers can handle complex dependencies and long histories in time series. Although evaluated on more general multivariate datasets, their architecture is highly relevant for industrial processes that involve many interacting sensors and actuators. Hybrid behaviour- and Bayesian network-based frameworks such as the one by Faramondi et al.37 can also be seen as a step towards structural modelling, although they rely more on expert-defined causal graphs than on learned graph representations.

Datasets and benchmarks for ICS anomaly detection

The availability of realistic datasets is crucial for evaluating anomaly detection methods. Goh et al. introduced the ICS dataset, which has become a de-facto benchmark for ICS anomaly detection40. Built from a scaled-down water treatment plant, ICS dataset provides both normal and attack traces with labelled attack intervals, making it particularly suitable for testing detection algorithms under realistic conditions. More recently, Dehlaghi-Ghadim et al. presented an anomaly detection dataset for industrial control systems, aiming to cover a broader set of ICS scenarios and attack types46. Together, these datasets enable controlled comparison of different approaches, but they also reveal limitations: many methods are evaluated on a small number of plants or configurations, and generalisation across sites remains under-explored.

In summary, the literature shows a clear evolution from classical threshold and correlation-based methods12,23,36 towards deep learning–based and structure-aware approaches29,30,43,44. Network-based and periodicity-oriented methods provide useful early warning signals but often lack direct connection to the underlying physical processes11–14. Process-aware models using process mining, cyber–physical modeling or Bayesian networks capture rich semantics but can be hard to scale and adapt7,8,10,17. Deep learning and federated learning have improved detection accuracy and scalability in IIoT settings27–29,31,36,37, yet most works treat time series in a largely uni-modal way and do not systematically integrate physical topology or design knowledge into the representation learning process.

Graph-based models, especially recent heterogeneous and attention-based GNNs, start to close this gap by encoding process structure directly in the model30,43,44. However, many of these approaches are still trained in a supervised fashion or rely on substantial labelled attack data, which is not realistic for many ICS operators. In addition, only a few works combine self-supervised representation learning with physics-guided graphs in a fully unified anomaly detection framework. This leaves room for approaches that learn robust latent representations from normal data only, respect the physical graph derived from P&IDs, and provide node-level anomaly and root-cause scores in a way that is practical for operators in real plants.

In addition to security-oriented learning methods, recent studies on fault detection and diagnosis for HVAC and industrial systems have demonstrated the effectiveness of advanced data-driven models. Zhang et al. developed a KFDA-IGCN framework that combines kernel Fisher discriminant analysis with an improved graph convolutional network to utilize class labels and spatial information of air handling unit (AHU) data, achieving superior diagnostic performance on ASHRAE RP-1312 datasets16. To address the challenge of imbalanced fault samples in chillers, Yang et al. proposed an IGAN-EDELM model that integrates multi-head attention-based generative adversarial networks with an AdaBoost-enhanced deep extreme learning machine, significantly improving minority class recognition21. Furthermore, Zhang et al. introduced a global modelling strategy using dynamic kernel canonical variate analysis (GDKCVA) to capture two-directional dynamics of AHU processes, reporting detection rates above 99%26. Although these approaches exhibit high accuracy in equipment health monitoring, they remain largely domain-specific, depend on labeled historical faults, and do not consider adversarial robustness, privacy leakage, or integration with cybersecurity-aware frameworks. Future research should therefore explore the fusion of FDD models with secure, explainable, and real-time learning mechanisms.

From the literature we can see that most works use either self supervised learning or graph based models, but not both together. Contrastive learning is good to learn features from normal data, but these methods usually ignore the physical structure of the plant. On the other hand physics guided graph models can capture relations between sensors, but many of them need labelled attacks and supervised training. There is very few studies that combine contrastive representation learning with P&ID based graphs in one framework. Because of this gap, existing models are either data hungry or not physically meaningful. Our work try to connect these two ideas and build a method that can learn from normal data while using real process dependencies.

Proposed methodology

This section explains the complete workflow of the proposed anomaly detection framework, which integrates self-supervised contrastive pretraining with a physics-guided temporal graph model. The aim is to learn deep representations of normal ICS operations and detect any deviation caused by faults or cyber-attacks. The methodology includes seven major stages, starting from dataset understanding and ending with anomaly scoring and root-cause localization.

System overview and problem definition

Overview of the ICS dataset testbed and dataset

The ICS dataset testbed is a fully functional miniature water treatment plant developed for cybersecurity research. The dataset contains long-running normal operation data and more than 30 different cyber-attacks, including valve manipulation, forged sensor packets, abnormal pump behavior, and level overflow attacks. The sampling interval is 1 s, producing a dense, high-correlation multivariate dataset.

Because edges reflect real physical flow, abnormality spreads from source node to dependent nodes. The highest-ranked node is typically the root cause, and neighbouring nodes show secondary deviations.

In this work, a 1D-CNN encoder was finally selected for the contrastive module because it provided better stability than TCN and Transformer in preliminary tests. The encoder consists of three convolution layers with 64, 128 and 128 filters, kernel size 3, followed by ReLU and max pooling. The output is projected to a latent dimension k = 128 using a two-layer MLP. For contrastive learning, the temperature τ was set to 0.2. Window length L = 60 s with step size 10 was used. Training was done with Adam optimizer, learning rate 1e−3, batch size 256, and 100 epochs. Early stopping based on validation loss was applied. Data augmentation included Gaussian noise (σ = 0.01), scaling (0.9–1.1) and random cropping of 10% window. These settings were kept fixed for all experiments. The temporal graph module used a 2-layer GNN with hidden size 128 and ReLU activation. The same architecture was used for all ablation models to ensure fair comparison.

Results and discussion

This section presents the experimental findings of the proposed anomaly detection framework. Since the system combines contrastive learning and physics-guided graph reasoning, the results are discussed in multiple dimensions such as the representation learning quality, anomaly detection accuracy, comparison with existing baselines, and the model’s capability to identify root-cause sensors inside the plant. All experiments were carried out on the ICS dataset, and only the normal data was used for training the representation model.

The model was trained only on normal segments of ICS Datasets. Data was divided into training 70%, validation 10% and test 20% by time order. Validation set contained only normal data and was used for threshold and early stopping. All experiments were repeated three times and average values are reported.

Each sensor and actuator is mapped to one node, resulting in 51 nodes in total. Edges are created based on real process relations such as pump → flow, valve → level and tank → downstream flow. The graph is directed, following the physical direction of material movement.

Two types of edges are used: control edges (actuator to sensor), process edges (sensor to sensor along pipeline). The final one contains 87 edges. Adjacency matrix is manually verified with plant documentation. Variables in dataset are matched to P&ID tags using tag names and location information.

Root cause localization

One of the biggest strengths of the framework is that it does not only say “anomaly occurred,” but also indicates where it originated. The node-wise anomaly score highlights sensors that deviate from their expected behaviour shown in Fig. 7.

The primary root cause is defined as the sensor or actuator that was directly attacked according to the dataset description. Other sensors that change only because of physical process flow are treated as propagated effects. For multi-stage attacks, more than one point can be attacked at same time. In such cases all directly attacked nodes are considered as correct roots. For cascading attacks, we take the first sensor that becomes abnormal in time as the root and later changes are considered secondary. This definition helps to separate real attack source from natural propagation in the plant.

To make the evaluation fair, we also added comparison with simple baselines. The baselines include: maximum deviation sensor, Granger causality ranking, SHAP or Integrated Gradients explanation, random ranking as lower reference.

These baselines show whether our method really gives additional value.

We also included failure case analysis. In some situations the graph may mislead the model. If the P&ID connections are not accurate, neighbouring sensors can get higher score than the real attacked one. Another problem happens in slow attacks where the effect spreads through process. Downstream sensors sometimes show larger deviation and this confuses single root attribution.

To verify whether the model can work in real industrial conditions, we conducted additional robustness tests. These tests are necessary because in real plants the data is not clean like benchmark datasets. First, we performed distribution shift simulation. The model was trained on one portion of the data and tested on another time period where the process behaviour was slightly different. This experiment helps to understand if the model only memorizes fixed patterns or it can adapt to normal variations in the plant. Second, we evaluated sensor drift. In real factories sensors gradually change their readings due to calibration issues or aging effects. To mimic this situation, we added small bias to selected sensors from 1% to 5% and observed the change in detection performance. Third, we carried out missing channel test. In practice, sensors may fail or communication can be interrupted. We randomly removed 10% to 30% of sensor signals during testing and examined whether the model can still detect attacks with partial information. Fourth, we applied noise injection. Industrial sensors always contain measurement noise, and we added Gaussian noise and small impulse disturbances to the input data. This experiment shows the sensitivity of the model to noisy environments. Finally, we conducted long term operation test. The model was executed on continuous data for several days without retraining and with a fixed threshold. This test helps to check the stability of the method over time and the need for frequent tuning. From these experiments we observed that performance dropped slightly under difficult conditions, but the model continued to work reasonably well. The graph structure reduced the impact of drift and missing sensors because related sensors supported each other. The long term test indicated that the threshold remained mostly stable.

Overall, the results indicate that the proposed system performs well across different kinds of attacks in the ICS dataset. The contrastive encoder learned stable and meaningful patterns of normal behaviour even without labelled attack data, which matches the reality of industrial environments where such labels are rarely available. The physics-guided graph played an important role by capturing relationships between sensors that ordinary deep learning models usually ignore, and this helped the system to recognize abnormal behaviour spreading through physical dependencies. By combining both modules, the framework achieved better detection performance, especially for slow or hidden attacks that are difficult to notice. A few false positives were still observed, mainly during sharp operational transitions, but they were manageable after threshold tuning.

The root-cause scores were also helpful, giving operators a clear idea of which sensors might be responsible when something goes wrong. At the same time, some limitations remain: multi-stage attacks were occasionally difficult to isolate, and the model sometimes ranked secondary sensors higher than the truly attacked one, probably because the physical effects naturally propagate through the system. Even with these issues, the approach still strikes a good balance between accuracy, interpretability, and the ability to detect zero-day attacks, making it quite suitable for practical ICS monitoring.

Conclusion

In this paper, we focused on the problem of detecting anomalies and cyber attacks in industrial control systems without relying on large amounts of labelled attack data. The proposed framework combines a self-supervised contrastive encoder with a physics-guided temporal graph model and is evaluated on the ICS testbed. The contrastive learning stage, trained only on normal data, was able to learn stable latent representations that capture the typical behaviour of multivariate sensor streams. On top of these embeddings, the physics-guided graph, derived from the plant’s P&ID, allowed the model to take into account real physical dependencies among sensors and actuators, instead of treating them as independent time-series.

The experimental results showed that this hybrid design improves anomaly detection performance compared with several baseline methods, including LSTM autoencoders, CNN-based models and other graph-based approaches. The proposed model achieved higher precision, recall and F1-score, and at the same time kept the false positive rate at a level that is more acceptable for real operators. In addition, the node-level anomaly scores enabled a practical form of root-cause localization: in many attack scenarios, the true compromised sensor or actuator appeared within the top-ranked positions, which can significantly reduce investigation time in a real plant.

At the same time, some limitations were also observed. Multi-stage attacks and complex scenarios where several parts of the plant are affected at the same time remain challenging, and the model occasionally gives higher scores to secondary sensors because anomalies propagate along the physical connections. The method also depends on the quality and completeness of the P&ID-based graph; missing or incorrect links may reduce performance. Nevertheless, the results suggest that physics-guided contrastive temporal graph learning offers a good balance between accuracy, interpretability and data efficiency for ICS anomaly detection and can be considered as a realistic step towards deployment in critical infrastructures.

Future enhancements

There are several directions in which this work can be improved or extended in the future. First, the current framework is mainly trained and evaluated in an offline setting. Extending it to an online or streaming mode, where the model adapts gradually to slow changes in the process while still remaining sensitive to real attacks, would make it more suitable for long-term deployment. Incremental or continual learning strategies could be integrated so that the system does not need to be retrained from scratch whenever the plant configuration or operating regime changes.

Second, the physics-guided graph used in this work is built manually from P&ID information. In a large industrial site, this manual step can be time-consuming and may miss some hidden dependencies. Future work can look into semi-automatic or data-driven graph construction, for example by combining engineering diagrams with correlation or Granger-causality analysis, while still keeping a strong link to physical knowledge rather than using a purely learned graph. More advanced graph architectures, such as dynamic graphs where edges can vary over time, may also help to capture mode changes or maintenance events.

Third, although the model already provides sensor-level rankings, the interpretability can be improved further. Techniques from explainable AI for graphs and time-series could be integrated to show which time intervals, paths in the graph or combinations of sensors contributed most to a particular decision. This may increase trust from engineers and operators. Finally, the approach should be validated on additional datasets and across different kinds of ICS, such as power grids or chemical plants, to study how well the method generalises beyond the ICS environment. Combining the proposed system with network-level intrusion detection and process-level safety constraints is another promising direction for building a more comprehensive defence-in-depth strategy in industrial cyber-physical systems.
