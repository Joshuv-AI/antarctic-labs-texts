Numerical investigation of acoustic cavitation characteristics of a single gas–vapor bubble in soft tissue under dual-frequency ultrasound
Authors: Zhenxiang Ji, Dingjie Suo, Jie Jin, Xinze Liu, Ye Wang, Shintaro Funahashi, Wei Li, Tianyi Yan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe viscoelastic tissue under dual-frequency ultrasound excitation affects the acoustic cavitation of a single gas–vapor bubble. To investigate the effect of the cavitation dynamics, the Gilmore-Akulichev-Zener (GAZ) model is coupled with the Peng-Robinson equation of state (PR EOS). Results indicate that the GAZ-PR EOS model can accurately estimate the bubble dynamics by comparing with the Gilmore PR EOS and GAZ-Van der Waals (VDW) EOS model. Furthermore, the acoustic cavitation effect in different viscoelastic tissues is investigated, including the radial stress at the bubble wall, the temperature, pressure, and the number of water molecules inside the bubble. Results show that the creep recovery and the relaxation of the stress caused by viscoelasticity can affect the acoustic cavitation of the bubble, which could inhibit the bubble’s expansion and reduce the internal temperature and pressure within the bubble. Moreover, the effect of dual-frequency ultrasound on the cavitation of single gas–vapor bubbles is studied. Results suggest that dual-frequency ultrasound could increase the internal temperature of bubbles, the internal pressure of bubbles, and the radial stress at the bubble wall. More importantly, there is a specific optimal combination of frequencies for particular viscoelasticity by exploring the impact of different dual-frequency ultrasound combinations and tissue viscoelasticity on the acoustic cavitation of a single gas–vapor bubble. In conclusion, this study helps to provide theoretical guidance for dual-frequency ultrasound to improve acoustic chemical and mechanical effects, and further optimize its application in acoustic sonochemistry and ultrasound therapy.Keywords: Bubble dynamics, Dual-frequency, Viscoelastic tissue, GAZ-PR EOS model

Introduction

The phenomenon of acoustic cavitation occurs when ultrasonic waves irradiate a liquid with a gas nucleus, forming stable oscillating cavities that eventually undergo violent oscillation and collapse [1], [2]. The cavity generated by acoustic cavitation can be filled with various gases and water vapor, facilitating heat exchange through the bubble wall, which could lead to an instantaneous temperature rise of approximately 6000 K at the center of the bubble [3], creating chemical bonds within water molecules to break, and combine with other free radicals to produce new compounds [4], [5]. Cavitation has great significance in focused ultrasound therapy [6], the chemical industry [7], and biomedical engineering [8], such as focused ultrasound combined with microbubbles targeted drug delivery [9], [10], ablation [11], [12], ultrasonic cleaning [13], [14], sonoluminescence [15], [16], etc. Therefore, it is important to study the characteristics of acoustic cavitation.

Multi-frequency ultrasound [17], [18], [19] has been widely used to enhance the acoustic cavitation, chemistry, and therapeutic effects. Klapcsik et al. [20] investigated the shape stability threshold of acoustic bubbles excited by dual-frequency, the result showed that bubble collapse can be stronger in dual-frequency cases compared with a single frequency. Mehrdad et al. [21] investigated the effect of ultrasonic processing power on ultrasonic cavitation and acoustic chemistry in single and dual-frequency acoustic wave generators. The results showed that dual-frequency ultrasound improves the degradation efficiency of the same acoustic chemical activity and reduces the ultrasound power. Yusuf et al. [22] degraded methylene blue by comparison of single and dual-frequency ultrasound, their result showed that dual-frequency ultrasound consistently outperforms single-frequency modes by enhancing the quality of acoustically generated cavitation bubbles. Edsall et al. [23] compared the ablation effects between dual-frequency and single-frequency ultrasound excitation. They found the potential for using dual-frequency histotripsy methods to increase the ablation efficiency. Our group [24] found that dual-frequency ultrasound could improve ultrasound energy utilization and reduce the acoustic cavitation threshold by comparing the single/dual-frequency ultrasound for thrombolysis in-vitro.

To understand the mechanism of cavitation, theoretical calculations of the cavitation process are developed. There are many models [25], [26], [27] that can predict the temperature field of a single gas–vapor bubble under ultrasonic excitation. Prosperetti [28] et al. coupled the Keller equation with the internal and external equations of the bubble and proposed a new dynamic formula for bubble oscillation. Pahk et al. [29] evaluated bubble dynamics in water and liver tissue and transient temperature inside bubbles by coupling the Gilmore equation and VDW equation with experiments, then the GAZ model is coupled with the VDW equation [30], and the boiling steam bubble cavitation experiment is carried out under the sound irradiation of boiling tissue fragmentation. Zilonova et al. [31] coupled the GAZ model [32] with the Wulff [33] equation, and considered the bubble's inner temperature field, they found that the temperature inside and outside the foam increased significantly during a dramatic bubble bursting. Denner et al. [34] coupled the Gilmore equation with the Noble-Abel-stiffened-gas (NASG) EOS, the ability to simultaneously predict pressure and temperature values in gases, vapors, and liquids broadens the application scenarios of the equation. Lee et al. [35] coupled the Keller-Miksis (KM) model with the VDW EOS, and by considering the shell effect to solve for the spatial variation of chemical species and energy in the bubble and the surrounding liquid, the heat and mass transfer during cavitation can be better predicted. Qin et al. [36] coupled the Gilmore equation with the PR EOS revealing that, in comparison to the Gilmore-VDW [29] model, the Gilmore-PR model predicts a more pronounced collapse of bubbles with higher temperature and pressure, accompanied by an increased number of water molecules within the collapsed bubble.

In contrast to what [30], [33], [34], [35] have studied, this paper aims to explore the utility and effects of acoustic cavitation of dual-frequency ultrasound excitation of individual gas–vapor bubbles in viscoelastic tissues by coupling the GAZ equations with the PR EOS equations of state to accurately quantify the cavitation kinetics as well as the resultant temperatures, pressures, number of gas molecules inside the bubbles, and radial stresses at the walls of the bubbles to show the enhancement of the chemical and mechanical effects of dual-frequency ultrasound. This manuscript is organized as follows: Firstly, the predictions of the GAZ-PR EOS equation are compared with the Gilmore-PR EOS model and GAZ-VDW EOS model; Secondly, the acoustic cavitation effects (radial stress at the maximal bubble wall, maximal internal bubble temperature, maximal internal bubble pressure, maximal internal bubble pressure, maximal internal bubble number of water molecules) are compared for different viscoelasticity. Thirdly, we compared the effects of different dual-frequency ultrasonic combinations on the cavitation effect of microbubbles. Finally, the effect of different second frequencies on acoustic cavitation in different viscoelastic and dual-frequency ultrasound are studied.

Results and Discussion

Comparison of models

To prove the rationality of the numerical scheme adopted, we compare the GAZ-PR EOS model with the Gilmore-PR EOS model. The simulation conditions are f = 45 kHz, R0 = 6 μm, P0 = 1 atm, andT0 = 293.15 k, P=1.42 atm, medium: water. Numerical results show that GAZ-PR EOS is consistent with Gilmore-PR EOS. Fig. 1(a) shows the GAZ-PR EOS model shows the same expansion. This maximum radius(about 37 μm) is similar to that reported by Ahmed et al.[48] with the same conditions. However, the minimum radius obtained with GAZ-PR EOS is larger than the one without the Zener model, which means that the viscoelastic model seems to influence the bubble collapse stage. The Zener model includes the representation of the radial stress as shown in Fig. 1(f), which leads to a decrease in the internal pressure of the bubble, making it less compressible. Moreover, viscosity generates viscous forces when the bubble expands and compression. Fig. 1(b) shows the number of water molecules within the bubble was not affected. Fig. 1(c) illustrates the GAZ-PR EOS reduces Mach number as the present research [32]. In addition, the maximum temperature and pressure inside the bubble are lower, as shown in Fig. 1(d-e).

The simulation conditions are R0 = 6μ m, f = 45 kHz, P0 = 1 atm, T0=293.15 K.

However, to achieve sufficient precision for the current numerical solutions of the GAZ-PR EOS model, the GAZ-VDW EOS model [30] is used for simulation cases. The simulation conditions of the models are the same as those in Fig. 1, which are simulated in water and stimulated by low-intensity ultrasound. As shown in Fig. 2, GAZ-PR EOS shows the same expansion but high compression, which are consistent with Qin et al. [36] which bubble dynamics predicted by PR EOS is more intense when the bubble collapses. Moreover, the maximum temperature predicted by the GAZ-PR EOS exhibits an increase (about 94 K higher, 2.5 %), as well as the maximum internal pressure predicted (about 49.4 MPa higher, 4.3 %). More importantly, we also find that the radial stress at the bubble wall predicted by the GAZ-PR EOS model exhibits an increase (about 0.077 MPa higher, 7.1 %). This proves the necessity of integrating the GAZ model with the PR model, as its absence could result in an underestimation of the internal temperature, internal pressure, and radial stress at the bubble wall.

Comparison of acoustic cavitation between various viscoelasticity

The effect of acoustic cavitation is evaluated by the change of bubble radius, the poincaré section, the temperature inside the bubble, the number of water molecules inside the bubble, pressure inside the bubble, and radial stress at the bubble wall. The elastic modulus in the tissue ranges from 0 to 0.2 MPa. Fig. 3 describes the influence of different elasticities on the acoustic cavitation of the bubble. When the elastic modulus is larger, the oscillation and internal temperature of the bubble are significantly reduced. The velocity of motion at the bubble wall is greatly affected as the elastic modulus higher. Similar to previous research findings [30], [32], [49], the elastic modulus plays a significant role in constraining bubble oscillation and expansion, thereby lowering the internal temperature of the bubble. As shown in Fig. 3(e, f), the pressure inside the bubble is reduced by 2.2 MPa, and the radial stress at the bubble wall is also reduced by about 1.5 MPa. Therefore, elasticity affects acoustic cavitation by influencing the radial stress at the bubble wall and the bubble expansion, thereby reduce the number of water molecules inside the bubble and decrease the temperature inside the bubble.

Comparison of acoustic cavitation between dual-frequency

The bandwidth of a traditional focus ultrasound transducer is about 20 %. The simulation is carried out at a fixed frequency of 1 MHz, 1 MHz + 0.9 MHz, and 1 MHz + 1.1 MHz. Fig. 6 shows the comparison of the acoustic cavitation effect between different dual-frequency ultrasounds and single-frequency ultrasounds, dual-frequency ultrasound could improve the acoustic cavitation effect at low-intensity ultrasound. The temperature inside the bubble stimulated by dual-frequency ultrasound reaches up to 1200 K(1 MHz + 0.9 MHz) and 1050 K(1 MHz + 1.1 MHz). Compared with 450 K by single-frequency ultrasound, the pressure inside the bubble is more than 10 times that of single-frequency ultrasound. The radial stress at the bubble wall is also about 4–5 times higher, and the number of water molecules in the bubble is increased by more than 3 times. As anticipated, the utilization of dual-frequency ultrasound has been shown to enhance the acoustic cavitation characteristics [12], [24], [50], this is due to the low frequency component generated by dual-frequency ultrasound.

Fig. 7 shows the acoustic cavitation effect of dual-frequency ultrasound at different phase and amplitude ratios. The amplitude ratio is the ratio of 1 MHz first frequency and 0.9 MHz second frequency, and the phase is the phase difference between second frequency and first frequency. When the amplitude ratio is 1:1 and the phase difference is less than π/6, the radial stress at the bubble wall is the largest, and the number of water molecules, temperature, and pressure inside the bubble is the largest. Conclusion

This study presents the bubble dynamics of a single gas–vapor bubble in soft tissues. The proposed model analyzed the sonochemistry effects and radial stress analysis of ultrasonic cavitation. By coupling the GAZ models with PR EOS, the creep recovery and stress relaxation of bubbles in soft tissues are better described. The results showed that GAZ-PR EOS has the same maximum expansion ratio compared to Gilmore PR EOS, and the temperature appears to decrease, due to the radial stress at the bubble wall; secondly, the effect of viscoelasticity in acoustic cavitation is investigated. The results indicate that viscoelasticity can reduce the number of water molecules within bubbles by inhibiting their expansion, thereby leading to a decrease in both the internal temperature and pressure of the bubbles, due to the viscous force and elasticity in tissues. Furthermore, under the stimulation of dual-frequency ultrasound, the internal temperature, internal pressure, and radial stress at the wall of the gas–vapor bubbles could be effectively increased to improve the acoustic cavitation effect, which was the strongest when the phase tended to be π/6 and the amplitude ratio tended to be 1:1. Finally, by changing the frequency of dual-frequency ultrasound combine with the viscoelasticity of the tissue, the viscoelasticity has a certain effect on the resonance frequency of a single gas–vapor bubble, and it seems that each specific viscosity and elasticity has a specific optimal combination of frequencies, which can extremely improve the acoustic cavitation effect.

The presented study may provide insights into the sonochemistry effects of dual-frequency ultrasound in tissues and the associated stress analysis, and contribute to further studies of ultrasonic sonochemistry as well as ultrasonic-mechanical effects in tissues. However, gas-bubble internal coupling with external heat, bubble–bubble interactions, lipid envelope of bubbles, and internal gas content were not considered and will be accounted in future studies.
