Bioinspired total synthesis of (+)-pleuromutilin
Authors: Shiyu Hua, Wenshuai Zhong, Lijun Chen, Yanxing Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract(+)-Pleuromutilin, a natural antibiotic, possesses an intriguing and complex 5/6/8 tricyclic skeleton with densely packed functional groups. It attracts extensive attention in the field of total synthesis due to its promising antibacterial activity and distinctive structural framework. Here we report the total synthesis of (+)-pleuromutilin inspired by its biosynthetic pathway, which expands the available toolbox, particularly by providing a practical and efficient entry to (+)−12-epi-mutilin. The key steps of the synthesis include a t-BuOK-catalyzed Michael addition to establish the C9 quaternary center, a LiDBB-promoted reductive coupling to construct the five-membered ring, and a bioinspired Zn-mediated Barbier reaction to connect C11 and C12, ultimately enabling the construction of the eight‑membered ring. Starting from (+)-pulegone, we accomplish the total synthesis of (+)-pleuromutilin in 18 steps and (+)−12-epi-mutilin in 16 steps, respectively.Subject terms: Natural product synthesis, Natural product synthesis, Biomimetic synthesis

Introduction

(+)-Pleuromutilin (1), a diterpene antibiotic, was first isolated from the fungus Clitopilus passeckerianus in 1951 (Fig. 1)1. (+)-Pleuromutilin exerts its mechanism of action by selectively binding to the highly conserved peptidyl transferase center of bacterial ribosomes2. Thus, the resistance to 1 is slow to develop, and it displays minimal cross-resistance with existing antibiotics. So far, four semisynthetic derivatives of 1 have been approved by the FDA3–5. (+)-Mutilin (2), an interconvertible deacylated derivative of 1, was found during the spectroscopic and degradation studies6. Interestingly, 12-epi-pleuromutilins, derivatives of 12-epi-mutilin, have recently been demonstrated to exhibit potent activity against Gram-negative pathogens, which remain a global health concern7,8. Therefore, a flexible synthetic strategy enabling access to both pleuromutilin (1) and 12-epi-pleuromutilin would be valuable.

Reported synthetic strategies for the eight-membered ring of (+)-pleuromutilin.

Structures of (+)-pleuromutilin (1), (+)-mutilin (2), and 12-epi-pleuromutilins, as well as partial strategies for constructing the eight-membered ring of (1 and 2).

Structurally, pleuromutilin (1) and mutilin (2) possess an intriguing and complex 5/6/8 tricyclic skeleton, and contain eight contiguous stereocenters, three of which are quaternary carbon stereocenters. The densely substituted 8-membered carbocycle is rare among natural products9, and represents a pivotal challenge in the total synthesis of pleuromutilin10,11. To date, seven total syntheses of 1 have been reported, all of which involve strategic construction of eight-membered ring12–21. In 1982, Gibbons and coworker accomplished the first total synthesis of racemic pleuromutilin, in which the 8-membered ring was generated by a Grob fragmentation12. In 2013 and 2018, Procter’s group13,14 and Reisman’s group15 independently utilized a SmI2-mediated cyclization to establish the 8-membered ring via the formation of C5 − C14 bond, respectively. In 2017, Herzon’s group creatively employed a nickel-catalyzed reductive cyclization to assemble the 8-membered ring by the formation of C10 − C11 bond16–18. Furthermore, the groups of Boeckman19, Pronin20, and Luo21 have each developed distinct ring-expansion strategies to construct the 8-membered ring.

The biosynthetic pathway of 1 has been well elucidated (Fig. 2A)22–24. Cyclisation of geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP) with the class II terpene synthase produced mutildienyl-PP (3), which was further catalyzed by the class I terpene synthase and underwent formation of the 8-membered ring through ionization-dependent dephosphorylation, hydride shifts from C14 to C10, and then trapping with water to generate the tricyclic premutilin (4). Premutilin (4) was converted to 1 via further oxidation and modification.

Bioinspired design strategy.

A Biosynthetic pathway of (+)-pleuromutilin (1). B Our bioinspired retrosynthetic analysis of 1. GGPP cyclisation of geranylgeranyl diphosphate, FGT functional group transformation.

In connection with our ongoing studies towards bioinspired total synthesis of natural products25, and inspired by the biogenetic pathway of 1, we envisioned that the formation of C11 − C12 bond could be used to assemble the 8-membered ring, and the hydride shifts from C14 to C10 could be utilized to set the C10 stereocenter (Figs. 2B, 7 to 6). Thus, (+)-pleuromutilin (1) could be derived from compound 5 through an intramolecular Barbier reaction. Compound 5 could be generated from 6 through the functional group transformation (FGT). Accordingly, the C10 stereocenter of 6 could be generated from 7 through bromonium ion-promoted 1,5-hydride shift. The cyclopentanone moiety of compound 7 could be derived from ketone-ester 8 by the formation of C3 − C4 bond via an intramolecular reductive coupling26. In turn, compound 8 can be synthesized from commercially available and inexpensive (+)-pulegone (10) through 9 by the construction of two quaternary carbon stereocenters.

Optimization of Barbier reaction or NHK reaction.

The use of Zn in DMF at 40 °C provided the highest yield of (+)-mutilin (30%). Interestingly, the NHK reaction selectively afforded (+)-12-epi-mutilin (60%).

Completion of the total synthesis.

With a revised strategy, compound 7 yields (+)-mutilin (10steps), (+)-12-epi-mutilin (10 steps) and (+)-pleuromutilin (12 steps).

Results

Preparation of the 1,5-hydride shift precursor

Our synthesis commenced with the preparation of the key 1,5-hydride shift precursor 7 (Fig. 3). The sequential acylation of (+)-pulegone (10) with ethyl cyanoformate (Mander’s reagent) followed by alkylation with methyl iodide (MeI) in a single synthetic operation proceeded smoothly to provide the desired product 11 and its C5-epimer (C5-epi-11) in 85% combined yield as a mixture of two inseparable diastereoisomers (5:1 at C5) in favor of 1127, thus constructing the first quaternary carbon stereocenter. Selective reduction of the ester group in 11 by sequential deprotonation of 11 with LDA followed by reduction with LiAlH4 afforded the corresponding primary alcohol28, which was then protected with TBSCl to afford ketone 9a in 57% yield and ketone 9b in 6% yield by two steps.

Synthesis of the 1,5-hydride shift precursor (7).

Compound 7 can be efficiently synthesized on a gram scale in 6 steps, starting from the natural chiral pool (+)-pulegone.

Initially, Michael addition of compound 9 to methyl acrylate with DBU in DMSO at 120 °C successfully provided the desired ketone-ester 8 in 27% yield with 30% conversion (Fig. S1)29, which gave us strong confidence in optimizing this reaction. The low conversion is partially due to the polymerization of methyl acrylate. After extensive screening, we found that the Michael addition promoted by a catalytic amount of t-BuOK (0.10 equiv) and t-BuOH (0.10 equiv) in THF at -20 °C to provide the desired product 8 as a single diastereomer in 90% yield30, thus constructing the second quaternary carbon stereocenters. It should be mentioned that the reaction temperature dramatically affected the diastereoselectivity, and lower temperature favored the desired C9 configuration. Michael addition of ketone 9b to methyl acrylate could also provide 8 in the same yield, and the mixture of ketone 9a and 9b could be directly used in large scale. The excellent facial selectivity of the reaction could be explained as depicted in Fig. 4. Two possible transition states, TS-1 and TS-2, could be considered. Because of the significant steric hindrance of the OTBS group in TS-2, the less hindered TS-1 should be favored, allowing the formation of the desired 831,32. Notably, when ketone 11 was used in the same Michael reaction, the facial selectivity is reversed (see SI).

Proposed mechanism for the formation of 8.

The principle of least conformational change dictates the predominantly favored formation of compound 8.

With compound 8 in hand, we proceeded to strategically construct the cis-fused 5/6 ring system via a direct reductive coupling between C4 ketone and C3 ester, which provided a step-economic transformation (Fig. S2). After many experimental trials, we found that LiDBB (Lithium 4,4’-Di-tert-butylbiphenylide) gave the best result, affording not only the desired α-hydroxyketone 14, but also 21% of the over-reduction diol 1333. Diol 13 could be readily converted to 14 by oxidation with SO₃·Py and TEA34. Dehydroxylation of α-hydroxyketone 14 with SmI2 furnished the desired ketone 735.

Having secured the stereocenters of compound 7, we investigated the key 1,5-hydride shift (Fig. S3). Treatment of compound 7 with NBS in ethanol induced an intramolecular 1,5-hydride shift to afford C10-epi-6 as the sole product in 70% yield, whose structure was confirmed by X-ray crystallography analysis. Unfortunately, the C10 stereocenter of 6 was not the desired configuration. Two possible transition states, TS-3 and TS-4, could be considered (Fig. 5)36,37. Because of the significant steric hindrance of the C10 methyl group in TS-4, the less hindered TS-3 should be favored, allowing the formation of the undesired C10-epi-6.

Proposed mechanism for the formation of C10-epi-6.

Steric hindrance makes it challenging to obtain the product with the correct configuration at C10.

Total synthesis of (+)-pleuromutilin (1), (+)-mutilin (2), and 12-epi-mutilin (23)

We turned our attention to convert the olefin 7 into the corresponding primary alcohol 15 (Fig. 6). After several trials, we found that hydroboration-oxidation of 7 with Thx-BH2 afforded the desired alcohol 15 in 60% yield with a 2:1 diastereomeric ratio in favor of the desired C10 stereocenter38,39. Acetylation of alcohol 15 with Ac2O delivered compound 16 in 95% yield. Deprotection of the TBS group in 16 with HF/pyridine followed by oxidation of the resulting alcohol with IBX provided aldehyde 17 in 90% overall yield. Inspired by Herzon’s work and in order to induce the desired C14 stereocenter, titanocene-mediated addition of 3-bromo-2-methylpropene to aldehyde 17 was conducted16–18. Gratefully, the desired diastereomer 18 was formed with 10:1 stereoselectivity.

Removal of the acetyl group of 18 with Me3SnOH provided alcohol 1940, and the structures of compounds 18 and 19 have been unambiguously confirmed by X-ray crystallography analysis. Subsequently, compound 19 underwent intermolecular olefin metathesis with cis-1,4-diacetoxy-2-butene to furnish compound 2041,42. The C11 primary hydroxyl group played a pivotal role in olefin metathesis reaction, as evidenced by the sluggish conversion and negligible product formation when compound 18 was subjected to identical condition. Subsequently, selective oxidation of primary hydroxyl of 20 with IBX afforded the desired aldehyde 21 in 82% yield. X-ray crystallography analysis of compound 21 revealed that the olefin metathesis products exclusively adopted the trans-alkene configuration.

With 21 in hand, attempts to assemble the eight-membered ring were investigated (Fig. S4). Initially, a palladium-catalyzed allylation was explored43,44. There was no reaction at low temperature, and the starting material decomposed at high temperature. A SmI₂-mediated reductive cyclization of 21 was further conducted. However, the aldehyde reduction product, compound 20, was exclusively obtained as the sole product. These results indicated that the poor leaving group ability of the acetate moiety may account for this outcome. After systematic evaluation, we replaced the acetate group with a halogen to enable Barbier reaction. Removal of the acetyl group of 21 with Me3SnOH furnished alcohol 22. Treatment of allylic alcohol 22 with TsCl successfully provided the corresponding allyl chloride 545. However, the allyl chloride 5 proved unstable and underwent degradation during the concentration process. Thus, a one-pot procedure was implemented wherein the allyl chloride was generated in situ and subsequently subjected to zinc-mediated Barbier reaction (Fig. 7)46–48. The condition of Zn in DMF gave the best result (entry 5), successfully affording both (+)-mutilin and 12-epi-mutilin. Surprisingly, under the NHK reaction condition49, ( + )−12-epi-mutilin was obtained as the sole product (entry 6). We speculate that this outcome is presumably due to the steric hindrance of the two chlorine atoms attached to the trivalent chromium, which prevents the formation of the six-membered ring transition state TS-5, thus allowing only the TS-6 transition state to operate, leading exclusively to (+)-12-epi-mutilin (see Fig. S5). Finally, (+)-pleuromutilin (1) was obtained in 74% yield by stepwise acylation of the C11 and C14 alcohols followed by methanolysis of the corresponding trifluoroacetyl esters11.

In summary, we have achieved the total synthesis of (+)-pleuromutilin (1) in 18 steps and (+)-12-epi-mutilin (23) in 16 steps from commercially available (+)-pulegone (10). The catalytic Michael addition followed by LiDBB-mediated reductive coupling constructs the cis-fused 5/6 ring system concisely, while the bioinspired Barbier reaction forges the C11 − C12 bond of the 8-membered ring collectively representing previously unreported annulation strategies for pleuromutilin (1). Concurrently, we explored a new strategy to selectively synthesize (+)-12-epi-mutilin (23) via NHK reaction. This work demonstrates the efficiency and versatility of bioinspired synthesis, providing new avenues for the derivatization of pleuromutilin-type antibiotics and thus supplying new chemical entities for breakthroughs in combating antibiotic resistance.

Methods

All reagents were obtained from commercial suppliers unless otherwise stated. Tetrahydrofuran (THF), diethyl ether (Et2O), toluene (PhMe), and dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) were purified by solvent purification system. Flasks were flame-dried under vacuum and cooled under a stream of argon. Flash chromatography was performed using silica gel (200–300 mesh) with solvents distilled before use.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (1H and 13C NMR) spectra were recorded on Bruker AVIII–400 [1H NMR (400 MHz), 13C NMR (101 MHz)]. The solvent signal was used for 1H NMR (CDCl3, 7.26 ppm), 13C NMR (CDCl3, 77.16 ppm). The following abbreviations are used for the multiplicities: s: singlet, d: doublet, t: triplet, q: quartet, m: multiplet, brs: broad singlet for proton spectra and carbon spectra. Coupling constants (J) are reported in Hertz (Hz).

Mass spectra were recorded using a PE SCLEX QSTAR spectrometer. Optical rotations were measured on a Rudolph Autopol VI Polarimeter, 1 cm, 0.2 mL cell. Melting points were measured on a Buch Melting Point M–560. X-ray single crystal was selected on an XtaLAB Synergy R, HyPix diffractometer.
