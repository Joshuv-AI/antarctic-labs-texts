Donning Gloves Using the Sliding-Closed Technique Has a Larger Distance to Contamination at the Gown-Glove Interface
Authors: Hailee E. Reist, Michael B. DiCosmo, Luke C. Seeker, Andrew H. Root, James D. Michelson, Nathaniel J. Nelms
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe surgical gown-glove interface is a known cause of bacterial contamination. Minimizing contamination from this interface can be achieved by optimizing the technique for donning gloves with the help of a sterile assistant. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the level of contamination of the inside of the glove cuff when employing a novel sliding-closed (SC) gloving technique vs a traditional open gloving technique.MethodsWe conducted a comparative study between a traditional open gloving technique and a novel SC gloving technique. Surgeons applied ultraviolet indicator lotion before donning a surgical gown and sterile gloves using either technique. Three surgeons were experienced with the SC technique and 3 surgeons had no experience with this technique. Contamination of each glove was analyzed by 2 blinded inspectors in a darkened room by measuring the distance from the cuff to the closest contamination.ResultsRegardless of previous experience, the SC technique had a significantly greater distance to contamination compared to the open technique (open = 65 mm [interquartile range 46.3-98.8], SC = 150 mm [interquartile range 110-170], P ≤ 0.001). Surgeons with experience using the SC technique had a greater median distance to contamination (125 mm) than those who without experience with this technique (90 mm, P = .002).ConclusionsPerforming the SC-assisted gloving technique results in a significantly greater cuff to contamination distance, compared to the traditional open gloving technique. Surgeons should consider adopting this technique to help minimize infection risk alongside standard infection control measures.

Introduction

Across all subspecialties of orthopaedic surgery, postoperative infection is one of the most devastating possible outcomes, causing significant increases in patient mortality, morbidity, and healthcare costs [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Prosthetic joint infections (PJI) are especially difficult to manage in total joint arthroplasty as the implants and prostheses are prone to biofilm formation, which makes eradicating an infection extremely challenging [5]. Treatments for orthopaedic infections have improved outcomes over time, but in the age of antibiotic resistance and absent antibiotic innovation, nonpharmaceutical prevention is key. Donning gown and gloves is a high-risk portion of any case, going from nonsterile to sterile. Any break in sterility at this point creates an infection hazard and a significant risk to the patient. Reducing the risk of contamination by optimizing technique for donning surgical gloves reduces the risk of postoperative infections [[6], [7], [8]].

Surgeons typically don gloves using one of a variety of methods with the help of an assistant to maintain sterility [8,9]. When using the closed method, the surgeon's hand remains within the cuff while an assistant places the glove over the enclosed hand. In the open method, the fingers protrude through the end of the sleeve and are exposed. The assistant will then stretch open the glove while the surgeon's hand slides into the glove. Often times during any surgery the cuff of the glove will “turn down” or roll up on itself exposing the inside of the glove cuff that could possibly be contaminated. Jones et al. measured the distance from the cuff edge to contamination and found that the closed technique leaves a significantly greater sterile distance compared to the open glove technique leaving only 1 cm of glove edge [10]. However, because the closed method often requires multiple adjustments to maneuver the cuff into a more comfortable position it has not been very widely adopted [8].

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the level of contamination of the inside of the glove cuff with an open vs a novel sliding-closed (SC) gloving technique. Secondarily, we sought to evaluate whether being familiar with the SC gloving technique influenced the level of contamination. We hypothesize that a SC gloving technique will result in less cuff contamination.

Material and methods

The group of gloving participants who completed the gloving events consisted of 2 board-certified orthopaedic surgeons, 3 residents, and one medical student. Twenty certified surgical assistants were recruited and were a combination of surgical technologists and nurses from a range of surgical specialties. Their familiarity with the open vs SC gloving technique was variable. We did not ask whether they were or were not experienced with either technique. Three surgeons were experienced with the SC technique (one board-certified orthopaedic surgeon and 2 orthopaedic surgery residents), all who used the technique in practice for over 1 year. Three surgeons (one board-certified orthopaedic surgeon, one orthopaedic surgery resident, and one medical student) had no experience with the SC technique prior to their participation in the study. Each surgeon performed both the open and SC gloving technique for a total of 25 gloving events. The types of gloves used were ENCORE brown latex “ortho” gloves, ENCORE green latex under gloves, and ENCORE white “textured” gloves (Ansell healthcare Europe NV. Brussels, Belgium). The type of glove being donned for each event was randomized.

The right hand was always gloved first regardless of method. Prior to every gloving event, each gloving participant lathered their hands in ultraviolet (UV) lotion (GlitterBug UV disclosing lotion, Brevis Corporation, Salt Lake City, UT) prior to being gowned. In the SC gloving technique, the surgeon's hand remained in the sleeve until the gown sleeve end was inside the glove cuff (Fig. 1, Video 1). The surgeon used their contralateral hand (from within the gown sleeve) to pull back the sleeve of the gloving side as the glove slid on to place the fingers in the glove, all in a smooth motion. With this technique, no part of the hand is ever exposed out of the sleeve. The contralateral hand was gloved in the same fashion (Fig. 1, Video 1). The gown sleeves were then adjusted by gently pulling on the sleeve to remove any bunches or folds in the cuff that were created in the gloving process. The gowns were adjusted so the sleeve height was just above the metacarpophalangeal joint. The open gloving technique was such that the hand was exposed prior to placing the glove. The contralateral hand did not assist in holding the glove open. The contralateral hand was gloved in the same fashion. Again, the gown sleeves were adjusted by gently pulling on the sleeve to remove any bunches or folds in the cuff to the level of the metacarpophalangeal joint as needed. Each gloving event only consisted of a single pair of gloves; the surgeons were not double gloved. After the surgeons performed a scrubbing event using UV gel, they performed the appropriate gowning and gloving technique. They then immediately entered the dark room where 2 blinded inspectors peeled the cuff back and analyzed the distance from the most proximal edge of the glove to the first area of contamination on the dorsal and volar side of the glove. A 9-bulb light emitting diode black light (Phillips, Amsterdam, Netherlands) was used to visualize the UV detectable gel on the glove. The closest contamination to the glove cuff end was then considered the distance to contamination, regardless of if it were on the dorsal or volar side (Fig. 2). The surgeons were analyzed as a whole and then stratified into 2 groups based on experience level with the SC technique.Figure 2Photograph demonstrating an example of the measurement of distance to contamination: Distance to contamination “X” is measured from the cuff edge to the first speck of contamination with UV lotion on either the volar or dorsal side of the glove (Intended for color).

The distance from the proximal edge of the glove to the first speck of UV glitter was treated as continuous non-normally distributed data. The results were then expressed as median interquartile range and comparisons between study groups and previous experience were by independent samples median test with P < .05 set as detecting a statistically significant difference. All surgeons were included in the initial analysis. Subgroup analyses were then performed for surgeons who were experienced with the SC technique compared to those who learned the technique during the trial. As the right hand was always gloved first, the data were also analyzed for possible side-to-side differences. Initial analysis of data demonstrated that there was no left-right difference in distance to contamination for the open (left = 77 mm [95% CI: 68-86] vs right = 72 mm [95% CI: 62-83], P = .52) or SC (left = 140 mm [95% CI: 131-148] vs right = 138 mm [95% CI: 130-147], P = .87) techniques therefore all subsequent analyses were performed using the pooled data from both sides. There was no power analysis as there is no proven baseline for what the differences in distance to contamination might be. All analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel, 2024.

Results

Twenty surgical assistants performed both the SC and open gloving techniques. All 6 gloving participants completed the protocol with these surgical assistants. There were a total of 150 gloving events. Distance to contamination was independently recorded by blinded inspectors for every gloving event.

Overall comparison of the 2 methods showed that the SC technique had a significantly larger distance to contamination (open = 65 mm [interquartile range {IRQ}: 46.3-98.8] vs SC = 150 mm [IRQ: 110-170], P ≤ 0.001) (Table 1). Discussion

The results of our study demonstrate that when using the SC gloving method, the distance to contamination is significantly farther from the glove cuff than the open gloving technique regardless of previous experience using this technique. Despite innovations in aseptic technique, antibiotic prophylaxis and double gloving, the incidence of prosthetic joint infection is still 1%-2% [1,2]. Even though this is a rare complication, it is a devastating one that leads to a large increase in morbidity and mortality for the patient and is associated with a tremendous economic burden on the healthcare system [8,[10], [11], [12]]. Because of the severity of the sequelae that come with PJI, any possibility for improvement in technique that could lead to a decrease in PJI prevalence should be investigated. During any surgical procedure, the glove cuff will commonly turn down or fold inside-out exposing the cuff edge and exposing the inside of the glove cuff to the sterile field, creating a possible break in sterility and increased risk of intraoperative contamination [6,10]. Thus, the distance from the glove cuff to the first possible area of contamination is of utmost importance.

Several previous studies have investigated various gloving techniques and their risk of contamination, with the hopes of eliminating the concern of contamination at the gown-glove interface. Byrd et al. demonstrated that using a gloves-first technique led to significantly less contamination of the surgeon's gown sleeve [6]. However, the downside of this technique is that it leads to increased contamination of the surgical assistant's gloves [8]. Despite multiple studies, we have yet to find a solution to contamination of the gown-glove interface that does not create sterility concerns elsewhere.

A previous study by Jones et al. that compared staff-assisted closed vs open gloving techniques found similar results with significantly lower distances to contamination using the open gloving technique [10]. This study, however, only compared 2 surgeons, one who was familiar with the closed technique and one who preferred the open technique for a total of 20 gloving events [10]. The staff-assisted nature of the Jones study vs the SC technique in our study is a similar but different technique, with the SC technique providing more ease to the gloving event [10]. They also found no difference in distance to contamination when comparing surgeons' left and right hands [10]. Similarly, our study did not demonstrate a significant difference when comparing left and right hands.

When looking at only the SC gloving technique and comparing between surgeons who used the SC gloving technique prior to the study to those with no exposure, there was a significantly longer distance to contamination for those that were familiar with the technique. As with any new skill, there is likely a learning curve that requires a certain number of repetitions before seeing improvement and proficiency in that skill. However, those unfamiliar with the new technique still showed that the technique improved the distance to contamination as compared to the standard closed technique. Interestingly, in our study, when evaluating the surgeons who were using the SC technique for the first time, there was no clear improvement in the distance to contamination as the study went on. This lack of clear improvement over the length of the study likely indicates that the learning curve may require more than 20 gloving events before improvement or proficiency is seen.

This study is not without limitations; although the transfer of UV lotion identifies where contamination is expected to occur, it does not necessarily correlate with the actual infectious risk that skin contact with the inside of the glove cuff may carry. The surgical assistants used were of varying familiarity with the SC technique, this was not investigated during this study. It is possible that there may be a learning curve for the surgical tech as well, although the technique has little to no change in the responsibility for the surgical assistant. Lastly, there is no established distance of inner glove sleeve contamination that is considered a threshold for clinical significance or safety. This is something that should be investigated in future studies. However, it makes clinical sense that the greater the distance to contamination from the cuff edge, the less likely any potential contamination will be exposed to the surgical field as the glove cuff rolls down. Another possible solution is the addition of a sleeve after gloving to reduce exposure to the gown-glove interface; this could be investigated in future studies

Conclusions

The SC-assisted gloving technique results in a significantly greater glove cuff contamination distance compared to the more commonly used open gloving technique. The extra margin of protection against glove-gown interface contamination provided by SC-assisted gloving is expected to be clinically meaningful. There appears to be a learning curve associated with this more streamlined variation on a traditional closed gloving technique. Yet, even staff who had no prior experience with the technique, appeared to generally be at less risk of contamination when it was employed as compared to the open technique. Given the frequency of glove cuff turndown and the consequences of contamination at the surgical glove-gown interface, we favor the use of the SC-assisted gloving technique.
