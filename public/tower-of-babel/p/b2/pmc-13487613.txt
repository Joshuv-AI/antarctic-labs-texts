High-Resolution and Multimodal Structural Phenotyping of Intermediate and Advanced Non-Neovascular Age-Related Macular Degeneration
Authors: Chiara M Eandi, Valerie C Snyder, Kate Grieve, Kiyoko Gocho, Celine Chaumette, D Castro-Farias, Nate Norberg, Grace Eddy, Thomas R Friberg, Andrew W Eller, Kunal K Dansingani, Joseph N Martel, Jay Chhablani, José-Alain Sahel, Michel Paques, Ethan A Rossi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo characterize structural phenotypes in intermediate and advanced non-neovascular age-related macular degeneration (AMD) using flood-illumination adaptive optics (FIAO) and to explore their relationship with established multimodal imaging biomarkers, including OCT-based atrophy classifications.DesignProspective, cross-sectional observational study.SubjectsA total of 208 eyes from 117 patients (mean age 74 years; 63% female) with intermediate AMD (iAMD) or geographic atrophy (GA) were enrolled. Eligible participants had visual acuity ≥20/63, refractive error between –6 and +2 diopters, and sufficient media clarity for high-quality imaging.MethodsParticipants underwent multimodal imaging, including color fundus photography, spectral-domain OCT, infrared reflectance, fundus autofluorescence, and FIAO. Structural phenotypes (drusen, subretinal drusenoid deposits, pigmentary changes, and GA margins) were assessed qualitatively across modalities. Flood-illumination adaptive optics features were compared with OCT-based atrophy classifications (incomplete and complete retinal pigment epithelium and outer retinal atrophy [iRORA and cRORA]) using descriptive analyses and cross-tabulation.Main Outcome MeasuresPrevalence and multimodal characteristics of AMD structural phenotypes, and their correspondence with FIAO features.ResultsDrusen and pigmentary abnormalities were detected in the majority of eyes across modalities, with variability in prevalence depending on imaging technique. Flood-illumination adaptive optics identified drusen in 70% of eyes revealing a consistent reflectivity pattern of hyperreflective borders and hyporeflective centers. Hyporeflective clumps corresponding to pigmentary changes were always observed on FIAO, frequently localizing to areas of atrophy or transition zones. Distinct FIAO-defined GA border phenotypes were observed, including well-demarcated and indistinct margins. In descriptive cross-tabulation, well-demarcated FIAO borders were more frequently associated with OCT-defined cRORA, whereas indistinct or hyporeflective margins were with iRORA.ConclusionsFlood-illumination adaptive optics provides high-resolution visualization of structural features in iAMD and GA, revealing distinct patterns of drusen, pigmentary changes, and atrophic margins. While certain FIAO features show qualitative correspondence with OCT-defined atrophy stages, these relationships remain descriptive. Further quantitative and longitudinal studies are needed to determine their clinical relevance and role in disease characterization.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Adaptive optics, Age-related macular degeneration, Drusen, Geographic atrophy, Pigment

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is the leading cause of blindness amongst the elderly in the developed world,1, 2, 3 with prevalence rising due to population aging. Several aspects of AMD are still poorly understood, partly due to a lack of appropriate imaging tools for studying this slowly progressing disease at a microscopic scale. The hallmark pathologic feature of early AMD is extracellular deposits, called drusen, both in the macula and peripheral retina.1,4 There are different types of drusen classified according to their location and size. In particular, subretinal pigment epithelium (RPE) deposits are classified based on size as small (<63 μm), intermediate (>63 and <125 μm), or large (>125 μm) according to the Beckman simplified clinical classification5 based on color fundus imaging. Subretinal drusenoid deposits (SDDs) are another type of deposit characterized by accumulation of material in the subretinal space with direct contact with photoreceptors. Subretinal drusenoid deposits are classified into 3 stages (stage 1–3) according to the thickness of the deposit visible on spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT) scans.6 They are present in approximately one-quarter (23%) of healthy adults, and their prevalence increases with age and AMD severity in subjects with early to intermediate AMD (iAMD) (49% and 79%, respectively). Subretinal drusenoid deposit numbers, size, and distribution, particularly when accompanied by pigmentary changes, are associated with increased risk of progression to advanced disease.7, 8, 9, 10

While clinical classification systems based on color fundus imaging exist,5 they are not routinely applied in practice, and contemporary management relies on multimodal imaging.

OCT is the clinical gold standard for assessing retinal structure and identifying biomarkers of progression, including drusen characteristics, hyperreflective foci (HRFs), and alterations of the outer retina.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 More recently, the Classification of Atrophy Meeting consensus classification has standardized OCT features of atrophy, introducing entities such as incomplete and complete RPE and outer retinal atrophy (iRORA and cRORA), thereby improving the consistency of structural endpoints in AMD.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Fundus autofluorescence (FAF), including both short-wavelength and near-infrared modalities, complements OCT by enabling sensitive detection of RPE dysfunction. It represents the gold standard for visualizing geographic atrophy (GA) and monitoring its progression.20,21

Age-related macular degeneration is a multifactorial disease, and its pathogenesis is not completely understood yet. Despite these advances, current clinical imaging modalities do not resolve cellular-scale changes, limiting our understanding of how microscopic alterations in RPE, photoreceptors, and pigment redistribution relate to established imaging biomarkers. In particular, the biological basis and spatial organization of pigmentary changes, recognized as key risk factors for progression,5 remain incompletely defined. Senescent RPE cells exhibit impaired function, including reduced autophagy and increased oxidative stress, which promote chronic inflammation and may contribute to disease progression.22,23 Genetic associations, particularly involving the complement pathway, further implicate inflammation in AMD,24, 25, 26, 27, 28 although the mechanisms linking these processes to structural retinal changes remain unclear. Recent evidence suggests that retinal melanophages may underline hyperreflective clumps (HRCs) and are associated with RPE dysmorphia,29 highlighting a potential cellular basis for observed imaging features.

Retinal pigment epithelium degeneration is a critical early event in outer retinal atrophy, disrupting photoreceptor support and the visual cycle. This process leads to the release and redistribution of intracellular components, including melanin and lipofuscin, which can trigger immune cell activation.22,30, 31, 32, 33 This in turn can lead to microglial cell activation and mononuclear phagocyte infiltration.34,35

Altered melanin compartmentalization, detectable with near-infrared FAF,30 may contribute to the pigmentary changes observed in AMD; however, the relationship between these cellular processes and clinically visible phenotypes remains poorly defined.

Despite recent progress, current clinical tools lack the resolution to evaluate the phenotype at the level of single cells. Adaptive optics (AO) ophthalmoscopy36, 37, 38, 39, 40 enables in vivo imaging of the retina at near-cellular resolution.41, 42, 43, 44 Adaptive optics ophthalmoscopy has been implemented in both flood-illumination (flood-illumination AO [FlAO]) and scanning laser systems (AO scanning laser ophthalmoscopy [AOSLO]).36, 37, 38 Each implementation has inherent strengths and weaknesses. In particular, FlAO offers practical advantages for clinical research, including a larger field of view, distortion-free imaging, and availability in commercially standardized systems that facilitate multicenter studies. While its resolution is lower than that of AOSLO,39 FIAO uniquely supports robust, repeatable imaging over larger retinal areas and longitudinal assessment of disease dynamics.

Previous studies with AO imaging found photoreceptor loss in regions that appear unremarkable in subjects with iAMD44 and perturbation of cone reflectivity in AMD patients when compared to histology.45 Histological studies described the migrating process of RPE cells in AMD, which may, at least in part, explain the HRCs visualized on AO ophthalmoscopy.46 This perturbation might reflect changes in the underlying RPE mosaic and perhaps in the relative distribution of melanin across the RPE layer.47 Moreover, with FIAO time-lapse imaging, we have shown that atrophy progression can be measured across tiny areas and that highly mobile small hyporeflective structures precede and accompany the emergence and progression of GA.41,42 Imaging drusen with AO ophthalmoscopy can be challenging. Drusen arise in different sizes and locations that impact their visualization on AO ophthalmoscopy.48 Drusen displace photoreceptors, causing incident light to be differentially scattered depending on the orientation of the photoreceptors across the aspect of the drusen. We recently capitalized on this to show that by varying the gaze position in FIAO we can delineate the borders of drusen with high precision and even detect tiny drusen that are too small to be visualized using any other method.49

The purpose of this study is to define cellular-level structural phenotypes in iAMD and advanced non-neovascular AMD using FIAO and to relate these findings to established multimodal imaging biomarkers, including OCT features and Classification of Atrophy Meeting atrophy classifications,12 with a focus on drusen, pigmentary changes, and GA.

Methods

Participants

In this cross-sectional observational study, we prospectively enrolled patients with either iAMD or early signs of GA from the Quinze-Vingts National Ophthalmology Hospital, Paris, France and the University of Pittsburgh, Department of Ophthalmology, United States, over a period of 2 years (January 2018–December 2019). The study was approved by the institutional review boards of the Quinze-Vingts National Ophthalmology Hospital and the University of Pittsburgh and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was acquired before enrollment for each participant following a detailed explanation of experimental procedures both verbally and in writing.

The inclusion/exclusion criteria are specified in Table S1 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Briefly, participants with intermediate or advanced non-neovascular AMD had best-corrected visual acuity of at least 20/63; the ocular media were sufficiently clear to obtain good quality clinical imaging and FIAO. The exclusion criteria included refractive error > +2 diopters or > –6 D; and nystagmus, choroidal neovascularization, diabetic retinopathy, or other macular diseases such as central serous chorioretinopathy.

Clinical Examination and Image Acquisition

All participants underwent a routine ophthalmologic eye examination and clinical imaging. Best-corrected visual acuity was measured using the ETDRS chart (Snellen equivalent). Standard clinical imaging was performed after pupil dilation with tropicamide 1% and phenylephrine 2.5% ophthalmic solution and included color fundus photography (30° and 50°; Topcon TRC-50IX), dense SD-OCT (Volume scan macular 20×20, 25 sections, 9 frames; Volume scan macular 15 × 5, 131 sections, 9 frames; Vertical and horizontal lines macular, 24 frames; Spectralis HRA + OCT, Heidelberg Engineering), and SLO (infrared reflectance), blue FAF (excitation = 488 nm; Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering). Spectral-domain OCT, infrared reflectance, and FAF images were all acquired over a 30-degree field of view centered on the fovea. Image quality was checked and image excluded if signal strength index was below 20 db.

Identical, commercially available FIAO systems (rtx1-e, Imagine Eyes) were used at each study site. The acquisition protocol was the same in the 2 centers, as follows: FIAO camera was focused on the cone photoreceptors and 9 4° × 4° images with 50% overlap at 9 locations surrounding and including the fovea were acquired, then formed into montages covering an 8° region centered on the fovea. Poor quality was defined as excessive blurring or poor contrast in the image. Clinical imaging and FIAO were performed on the same day or within 1 week to minimize any potential changes between sessions.

Multimodal Registration and Image Processing and Analysis

Flood-illumination adaptive optics montages were generated for all participants. Image registration was performed using i2k Retina x64 Pro (DualAlign LLC) installed on the rtx1 system. Multimodal en face images, including FlAO montages and clinical imaging modalities (color fundus photography, infrared reflectance, and blue autofluorescence [AF]), were subsequently aligned in Adobe Photoshop to generate composite multi-scale images. Registration was achieved through manual alignment based on local anatomical landmarks, primarily retinal vasculature.

Structural phenotypes were independently evaluated across multiscale images by 2 masked graders (C.M.E. and K.G.). In cases of disagreement, adjudication was performed by a senior retinal specialist (C.M.E.). Selected parameters were graded in a binary fashion (present/absent), and intergrader reliability was assessed using percent agreement (Table 1).

Clinical imaging was graded on a per-eye basis. The presence of a phenotype was recorded if identified, regardless of its number or spatial distribution. Statistical analyses were primarily descriptive, given the qualitative and exploratory nature of the study. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation and range, while categorical variables are summarized as counts (n) and percentages (%). Given the descriptive design, no inferential statistics or hypothesis testing were performed. All analyses were performed using R version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Study Population

Two hundred eight eyes of 117 consecutive patients (34 in Paris and 83 in Pittsburgh) with intermediate or advanced atrophic AMD were enrolled in this study. Twenty-six eyes of 234 (11%) were excluded from analysis due to poor FlAO image quality. All were White (except for 1 Asian female patient) with a mean age of 74 years (range, 57–90). There were 74 (63%) women. One hundred sixty-seven of 208 eyes (80%) were classified as intermediate stage (iAMD) and 41 of 208 eyes (20%) presented GA lesions according to the Beckman Simplified Clinical Classification System.5 The mean best-corrected visual acuity for the overall cohort was 20/50 Snellen equivalent (standard deviation ± 0.2 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution; range 20/20–20/63). Prevalence of Imaging Biomarkers across Modalities and Disease Stages

The prevalence of AMD biomarkers varied substantially depending on imaging modality and disease stage (Table 1). Notably, the proportion of eyes with GA differed between modalities: GA was identified in 41 eyes (20%) using Beckman color-based classification, compared with 62 eyes (30%) using FAF, reflecting the higher sensitivity of FAF in detecting early or subclinical atrophic changes. Unless otherwise specified, percentages are reported per total number of eyes (n = 208).

Across the cohort, medium-sized drusen were present in 74% of eyes and large drusen in 61% on color imaging. Subretinal drusenoid deposits showed marked variability by modality, ranging from 10% on color fundus photography to 24% with infrared reflectance imaging and 16% on SD-OCT.

When stratified by disease stage, most biomarkers were more prevalent in GA eyes. On FAF, GA was detected in 100% of GA eyes. On SD-OCT, advanced atrophic features such as cRORA and external limiting membrane descent were present in 95% of GA eyes, compared with 24% and 30% in the overall cohort, respectively. Hyperreflective foci (76%) were also more frequent in GA eyes, indicating greater structural disruption. Similarly, SDD were more commonly identified in GA eyes on infrared imaging (34%) compared with the overall cohort (24%).

Drusen

We classified drusen according to their size and type, according to the Beckman simplified clinical classification.5 All eyes exhibited ≥1 type of macular drusen. Small drusen (<63 microns), also called drupelets, are not thought to have pathological significance, but considered a normal aging-related finding; for this reason, we excluded them from our analysis.

On FIAO imaging, drusen were identified in 70% of eyes overall and in 54% of GA eyes. The appearance of drusen on FIAO varied with size and morphology. We consistently observed a hyperreflective halo with a hyporeflective center (Fig 1), with larger drusen demonstrating more pronounced hyperreflective borders (Fig 2), which can vary according to the gaze position and angle of incidence of the light on the retina, as previously reported.49 The pattern of stage 3 SDD on FIAO (Fig 3) is characterized by a double signal of hyperreflective and hyporeflective border.

Pigmentary Changes

Pigmentary abnormalities (Fig 4) were observed in 75% of eyes on color fundus photography with similar prevalence between iAMD and GA eyes. Pigment appearance varies by imaging modality. Pigment could manifest as areas of hyperautofluorescence on both blue and near infrared FAF yet the pattern and brightness varied between the 2 (as shown in Fig 4). Spectral-domain OCT B-scans precisely visualize pigmented clumps as HRF in 47% of eyes located in different layers both internal and external and mostly originating from the RPE layer. On FIAO imaging, pigmentary changes consistently appeared as HRCs, which were particularly concentrated at or near the margins of atrophic lesions that have a well-defined border.

Margins of GA

On FIAO imaging, GA margins were categorized into distinct phenotypes, including well-defined borders, fuzzy borders, marginal hyporeflectance, and marginal pigmented clumps (Table 1). Well-defined borders were observed in 25% of all eyes and in 90% of GA eyes, while fuzzy borders were present in 13% of all eyes and 61% of GA eyes.

To quantitatively assess the relationship between FIAO border phenotypes and OCT-defined atrophy, a per-eye cross-tabulation was performed (Table 3). Well-defined FIAO borders were strongly associated with cRORA, being present in 67% of cRORA eyes, compared to 30% of iRORA eyes. Conversely, fuzzy borders and hyporeflective margins were more frequently observed in eyes with iRORA, 75% and 59%, respectively (Figs 5 and 6).

Discussion

Our study describes common structural phenotypic characteristics of iAMD and advanced non-neovascular AMD through high-resolution multimodal imaging techniques. We focused here on 3 key well known diseases biomarkes: drusen, pigmentary changes, and GA margins. By integrating FIAO with established clinical imaging modalities, we aimed to relate microscopic phenotypes to current clinical classifications contributing to a deeper understanding of AMD pathology. Importantly, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, given the predominantly qualitative nature of the analysis.

First result of this study is the confirmation of well-established knowledge that the prevalence of AMD biomarkers varies substantially across imaging modalities and disease stages. In particular, GA was more frequently detected using FAF than color fundus photography, consistent with the higher sensitivity of FAF for early or subclinical atrophic changes. Similarly, features such as SDD and HRF showed marked variability depending on the modality used, highlighting the importance of a multimodal approach for comprehensive phenotyping. These findings are in line with previous studies demonstrating that individual imaging techniques capture complementary aspects of AMD pathology.7,11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16

One of the main contributions of this work is the systematic comparison between FIAO-derived GA border phenotypes and OCT-defined atrophy stages. While prior studies have described AO features in AMD,40,42,50 correlations with standardized OCT-based classifications13, 14, 15, 16 have remained limited. In our cohort, distinct or well-defined FIAO borders were more frequently associated with cRORA, whereas indistinct or hyporeflective margins were more often observed in incomplete atrophy (iRORA) and transition zones. The strong association between FIAO features and external external limiting membrane descent further supports the interpretation that these imaging patterns reflect different stages of atrophic progression. However, these associations remain descriptive and require validation in longitudinal and quantitative studies.

Regarding drusen, our observations confirm previous reports that multimodal imaging improves detection and characterization compared to color fundus photography alone.7,11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 Additionally, our study underscores the significance of FIAO in visualizing subtle changes associated with drusen, overcoming resolution challenges posed by conventional imaging modalities.36, 37, 38, 39, 40 In fact, FIAO can detect drusen with high variability in contrast and morphology according to size and type.

In the literature, drusen detection and contrast in en face modalities including FIAO and AOSLO are not always concordant.42,48,50,51 In fact, the gaze position variation plays an important role on contrast reflectivity mostly of smaller and intermediate size drusen.

We recently developed an image acquisition and processing method to enhance the contrast of drusen from FIAO images acquired at different gaze positions.49 Using this method, more drusen were detected including those of smaller dimensions than those captured with the clinical gold standard technique, giving this approach potential to detect the development of small drusen in early-stage AMD. Directional imaging and the Stiles–Crawford effect are another important factor responsible for the contrast of drusen. Photoreceptor misalignment secondary to the presence of deposits might alter their waveguiding, thus reducing their backscattered light and creating variations in drusen contrast with gaze position. This appears principally at the borders of the drusen where there is a transition from normal pointing to directionally altered photoreceptors and seems to be consistent with the hyperreflective halo.52

Similarly, the characteristic multimodal imaging appearance of stage 3 SDD aligns with previously reported findings, supporting the reproducibility of these imaging signatures, while the characteristic double hyperreflective and hyporeflective halo we observed on FIAO is partly in accordance with what is reported by other authors.48 In fact, in our cohort, all SDDs presented a double hyper- and hyporeflective band, while Querques et al48 described a continuous/discontinuous halo on 14 images from 8 eyes with SDDs using FIAO. Stage 1 and 2 SDD are not discriminated, while only stage 3 presents this typical aspect probably secondary to the dislocation of cone outer segments.45,49 Recently published results of the Pinnacle Study reported similar results regarding medium and large drusen as well as stage 3 SDD, while we did not evaluate earlier stages of SDDs.51 These results confirm the reproducibility of this technique and the potential impact on clinical findings at earlier stages of the disease.

Pigmentary changes represent another key feature of AMD progression. In agreement with prior literature, HRF on OCT were frequently observed, particularly in GA eyes, and corresponded to HRCs on FIAO, typically accumulating at the margin of the GA area.42 On a longitudinal time-lapse study, we observed that some of the clumps migrate, and their pathway might depend on the various locations within the retina layers.41

While these structures are commonly attributed to migrating or transdifferentiated RPE cells,34,47,53,54 increasing evidence from immunohistological studies suggests a potential contribution from melanin-laden inflammatory cells (melanophages).29,55 Our multimodal observations are consistent with this hypothesis but do not allow definitive identification of the underlying cellular origin. These observations resonate with emerging evidence highlighting the role of inflammation, complement activation, and RPE dysfunction in AMD pathophysiology.23,29,56, 57, 58, 59 Thus, the biological interpretation of these features remains speculative and warrants further investigation combining imaging with histological or molecular approaches.

At the level of GA margins, FIAO enabled detailed visualization of transition zones that are not fully captured by conventional imaging. The accumulation of HRCs and their association with well-defined borders may reflect dynamic processes occurring at the interface between viable and atrophic retina.41, 42, 43,58

In our cohort, we observed HRCs mostly accumulated in correspondence of the well-defined borders. This is also the area of greatest progression of GA, and therefore different hypothesis could explain the possible nature of these HRCs. Histological studies show the presence of migrating subducted RPE cells,46 while near infrared AF images revealed intracellular melanosomes suggesting melanin loaded cells.42 We recently evaluated the AF of HRCs using fluorescence AOSLO combined with FIAO and showed that most HRCs are autofluorescent in the near infrared, consistent with the hypothesis that they contain AF organelles, most likely melanin acquired through phagocytosis of degenerated RPE.60

These observations are consistent with previous AO studies42,58 suggesting that cellular-scale changes at GA margins may precede or accompany lesion progression. If confirmed, such features could represent potential imaging biomarkers for monitoring disease progression or treatment response.

Despite these insights, several limitations should be considered. First, the study is cross-sectional, precluding assessment of temporal relationships or predictive value of the observed features. Second, the analysis is primarily qualitative, which limits the ability to define reproducible structural phenotypes or establish robust associations between imaging biomarkers and disease stage. The absence of quantitative measurements represents an important limitation. Future studies incorporating standardized quantitative metrics and automated analysis pipelines will be essential to validate and extend these findings.

Third, attempts to quantify cone photoreceptors using FIAO were unsuccessful due to the inability of both automated algorithms and manual grading to reliably distinguish cones from pathological structures in diseased retina. This highlights an important technical limitation of FIAO in AMD and underscores the need for multimodal validation, including OCT and higher-resolution AO modalities such as AOSLO.

Finally, the study population was almost exclusively White, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to more diverse populations. Given known differences in AMD prevalence, phenotype, and genetic risk across ethnic groups, future studies including more diverse cohorts will be important to assess the broader applicability of these imaging biomarkers. Moreover, it would be important to have the genetic analysis in a larger cohort to correlate cellular phenotype with genetic risk of disease progression.

In summary, we performed an ultrahigh-resolution analysis of phenotypic characteristics of an iAMD and advanced non-neovascular AMD cohort. Based on FIAO images, we described the drusen pattern typical for different types of drusen. We identified HRCs mostly located in the transition zone near the well-defined margin of the GA and corresponding to pigment redistribution as hallmark of disease. Finally, we reported detailed cellular characteristics of the GA borders and related these findings to their entities across clinical modalities. Further studies with longitudinal data will better define the role of these characteristics in the progression of the disease.
