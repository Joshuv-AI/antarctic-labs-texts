Medical research barriers, facilitators, and motives in a developing country: a mixed-methods single-centre study
Authors: Mohammad Abed, Farhang Rashidi, Mohammad Hassan Bagheri, Alipahsa Meysamie, Milad Shafizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch engagement is essential for advancing clinical care and strengthening academic capacity. However, sustained participation is often limited by institutional and workflow constraints. This study assessed perceived research barriers and related factors at Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS) and developed and preliminarily evaluated a context-specific instrument.MethodsA sequential, exploratory, mixed-methods, single-centre study was conducted. Qualitative interviews and focus groups informed the development of a 45-item questionnaire covering 10 domains of research barriers and motives. The final instrument was administered via an online survey, with quota-based stratified sampling across academic roles. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha.ResultsThe scale showed acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.85), but domain-level reliability was mostly modest (α range: 0.36–0.76), and the a priori 10-domain structure was not confirmed (CFI = 0.69, TLI = 0.66, RMSEA = 0.062, SRMR = 0.17); exploratory analysis supported five to six broader dimensions. A total of 358 participants completed the survey (mean age 28.66 ± 7.38; 62.6% male). The total questionnaire score ranged from 113 to 198, with a mean of 153.66 ± 16.24, indicating moderate-to-strong endorsement of perceived research challenges. The highest domain means were observed for data access and quality (27.01 ± 4.05), financial resources and infrastructure (23.01 ± 4.10), and authorship, ethics, and research performance (17.80 ± 2.95). Total scores did not differ by academic position (P = 0.27), but significant differences were observed in time, workload, and organizational support, as well as in skills, training, and methodological consultation (both P < 0.001). Total scores had a weak but significant correlation with years of research experience (P < 0.001) and H-index (P = 0.01).ConclusionPerceived research barriers at TUMS are broadly endorsed and appear concentrated in modifiable institutional domains, particularly data access and quality, resource constraints, and governance-related factors. Institutional strategies that strengthen data systems, improve access to evidence, and enhance organizational support may improve the feasibility and sustainability of research engagement in similar academic medical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12961-026-01517-x.

Introduction

Research engagement among medical students and faculty supports advances in clinical care and sustainable academic capacity. Universities that actively promote research involvement tend to achieve stronger educational outcomes and more effective health systems [1, 2]. Despite this consensus, research participation varies across institutions, and persistent obstacles discourage meaningful involvement, particularly where clinical workload is high and organizational support is limited [3, 4].

Across settings, global evidence indicates that several recurring challenges affect students' and faculty's willingness and ability to engage in research. These include limited protected time, administrative delays, inadequate mentoring structures, restricted access to key resources, and regulatory complexity [5–7]. In many student populations, motivation exists but often declines once students encounter practical barriers to starting and completing projects [8]. Surveys consistently identify inadequate training in research methodology and biostatistics as an early bottleneck [3, 9, 10]. In several universities, students report that course load, examination pressure, and limited guidance reduce their ability to plan and execute research [9, 11, 12]. When these structural factors persist, they limit output and weaken research culture.

In Iran, the pattern closely mirrors the international literature. Studies from medical sciences universities highlight time scarcity, administrative burden, and restricted access to scientific databases [13–15]. At Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS), qualitative work suggests that educational structures and assessment practices may reduce opportunities for authentic research engagement [16]. Work in areas such as health informatics has also highlighted distinct local challenges, including limited access to data, specialized software, and technical support [17].

Against this backdrop, interventions to address these challenges have produced mixed results. Structured research programmes, mentoring systems, and training workshops have improved research participation in some settings, although effectiveness varies across contexts. These programmes appear to act by providing structured supervision, protected time, and early wins in dissemination, though effects vary with local workload and incentives. However, their success depends strongly on the alignment between programme design and local institutional conditions [18–20]. In low- and middle-income countries, participation is also shaped by access to literature, navigation of the ethics review process, and financial support for publication fees [21–24]. These additional layers of complexity can discourage early career researchers who are already balancing competing professional responsibilities.

Given TUMS's central role in the national research landscape, a comprehensive assessment of perceived barriers to research engagement is essential. Existing studies have largely relied on predefined questionnaires or focused on isolated populations and single dimensions of the problem, limiting their ability to capture the institutional, cultural, and organizational factors that shape research participation in specific settings. Because many of these barriers are context-dependent, a qualitative exploration was first required to identify issues meaningful to stakeholders at TUMS and to ensure that the resulting measurement instrument reflected the local research environment. These qualitative findings subsequently informed the development of a context-specific questionnaire, which was subsequently administered to a larger population to quantify the prevalence and relative importance of the identified barriers, facilitators, and motivational factors. Neither qualitative inquiry alone, which cannot estimate the distribution of perceptions across the institution, nor a purely quantitative survey using predetermined items would have adequately addressed these objectives. Accordingly, we employed a sequential exploratory mixed-methods design to identify context-specific determinants of research engagement, develop a valid measurement instrument, and examine their distribution among students and faculty at TUMS.

Methods

The current study employed a sequential exploratory mixed-methods design comprising three interconnected phases: (1) a qualitative phase focused on identifying multilevel barriers, facilitators, and motivational factors affecting research engagement among members of TUMS; (2) an instrument-development phase where qualitative findings were converted into a structured quantitative questionnaire; (3) a quantitative phase aimed at assessing the prevalence and relative significance of these determinants within the broader TUMS population. The integrated phases provided a comprehensive, synergistic framework for examining the contextual, organizational, and individual factors influencing research participation.

The research complied with ethical standards for studies involving human participants. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, with informed consent secured before the commencement of interviews or survey completion. Audio recordings and transcripts were stored securely, with no identifiable information retained in the final dataset. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences under the code IR.TUMS.SHARIATI.REC.1404.119.

Phase I: qualitative exploration

A maximum-variation sampling strategy was employed to ensure a wide range of diversity across disciplines, academic rank or training level, gender, and clinical versus non-clinical roles. Recruitment was conducted via institutional email announcements, the school and hospital's official communication channels, and referrals from department heads. Recruitment continued until the dataset was judged adequate for the study aims. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews conducted either in person or online, following a pilot-tested interview protocol developed explicitly for this study. Each interview lasted approximately 30–60 min. Three focus group discussions were also conducted as needed to enrich the data. Audio recordings of interviews were conducted with informed consent, and field notes were documented during and after each session. Audio files were transcribed accurately, anonymized, and securely stored on university servers. Quality procedures encompassed a pilot interview to refine the guide and internal peer debriefing; coding and theme development were undertaken collaboratively, with the team meeting to discuss and refine interpretations; coding was treated as interpretive rather than reliability-oriented; the audit trail, analytic memos, and documentation of coding decisions were maintained. Themes and subthemes were generated inductively and served as the conceptual basis for developing the quantitative instrument. Reporting of the qualitative strand follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [25].

Phase II: instrument development

The qualitative analysis themes were converted into questionnaire items that represent barriers, facilitators, and motivational factors related to research engagement. Items were formulated as statements evaluated using a five-point Likert scale. A panel consisting of six experts (three attendings and three students) in research methodology and medical education assessed the items for clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness. Items were modified or eliminated according to their assessments. A pilot study involving 20 participants from the target population was conducted to evaluate item comprehensibility, response time, and technical functionality. Following pilot findings, ambiguous items were revised, and the final version of the instrument was developed for the quantitative phase.

Phase III: quantitative study

A quota-based stratified sampling method was employed to guarantee proportional representation among faculties, academic ranks (for faculty), training levels (for students and residents), and clinical versus non-clinical categories. The sample composition was monitored in real time, and targeted recruitment was implemented to maintain representativeness across underrepresented strata. The sample size was calculated using Cochran's formula for proportion estimation, incorporating a 95% confidence level (z = 1.96), a margin of error of d = 0.05, and an assumed proportion of P = 0.5. With an estimated population size of N = 4000, the minimum required sample size was 350 participants, consistent with findings from a comparable study [26].

Data were collected via the "Porsline" online survey platform (https://porsline.ir/), accessible on mobile and desktop devices. Recruitment was conducted through personalized emails, announcements on the institutional educational portal, official Telegram channels, and QR code posters displayed in clinical and educational settings. Two reminder messages were dispatched to mitigate non-response rates. To maintain data integrity, each participant was permitted a single response utilizing their institutional identifier, which was not retained in the final dataset. Invalid responses were flagged and removed based on predefined criteria.

Integration of the qualitative and quantitative strands occurred at three points, consistent with established mixed-methods integration frameworks [27]. Connecting occurred when Phase I themes directly informed item generation and the 10 a priori content domains (Phase II). Building occurred when this coding structure was carried forward to define the domains tested in the reliability analysis and confirmatory factor analysis (Phase III). Merging occurred when domain-level quantitative findings were compared against the qualitative theme structure to identify confirmation, expansion, or discordance.

Participants and final questionnaire

The study participants comprised medical students, clinical residents and fellows, and full- and part-time faculty members affiliated with TUMS. Eligibility for participation required individuals to be employed by or enrolled at TUMS during the year preceding the study period, possess a minimum of 1 year of relevant experience with TUMS, be at least 18 years old, and demonstrate the ability to engage in Persian, either for interviews in the qualitative phase or for completing the questionnaire in the quantitative phase. Phase-specific criteria were established: participants in the qualitative phase had to consent to an interview or focus group, while those in the quantitative phase had to complete the survey in either online or paper format.

Exclusion criteria for participation included lack of affiliation with TUMS in the past year, extended leave that limited or rendered engagement with the institutional research environment outdated, or refusal of informed consent at any stage. During the qualitative phase, participants who declined audio recording and for whom detailed note-taking was considered inadequate were excluded from the study. During the quantitative phase, responses deemed duplicate, incomplete, or of insufficient quality, including those with excessively brief completion times or patterned response behaviors, were excluded from the dataset. Additionally, administrative or non-academic staff whose roles did not align with the educational-research target population were deemed ineligible for inclusion.

The final questionnaire consisted of demographic and professional variables, including age, gender, academic role, research experience, and H-index; items derived from the qualitative phase reflecting barriers, facilitators, and motivations; and a small number of open-ended items for participants to provide additional information and suggestions. The questionnaire was distributed, and 1 month was designated to gather data; it was then deactivated for export and analysis.

Statistical analysis

Reliability of the full instrument and of each a priori domain was estimated with Cronbach's alpha, and item performance within domains was examined through corrected item-total correlations. The dimensional structure of the 45-item instrument was then assessed. Sampling adequacy was verified with the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett's test of sphericity. Because the instrument was newly developed and its 10 domains reflected content areas derived from the qualitative phase rather than a previously established factor model, the number of retainable dimensions was determined empirically using parallel analysis with 200 random datasets, complemented by inspection of the scree plot and the Kaiser criterion. An exploratory factor analysis (minimum residual extraction, oblique promax rotation) was performed with the retained number of factors, and its pattern was compared with the content-defined domains. In a separate step, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) specifying the 10 a priori domains as correlated latent factors was estimated so that the fit of the intended structure could be reported explicitly; model fit was judged against the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) with its 90% confidence interval, and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Analyses were conducted in SPSS version 26 and R.

Results

Qualitative findings

Twenty-four semi-structured interviews and three focus group discussions were conducted with medical students (n = 11), residents (n = 6), faculty members (n = 5), and stand-alone researchers (n = 2). Reflexive thematic analysis generated 10 themes; the full coding hierarchy, with illustrative codes and the number of contributing participants, is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

The most pervasive theme concerned access to and quality of research data. Participants described restricted formal access to clinical data, unstandardized data structures within hospital information systems, and uncertainty about completeness and reliability. A closely related but distinct theme addressed data ownership, governance, and sharing, characterized by ambiguity over who may authorize data use and apprehension about legal or privacy consequences. Authorship, research ethics, and scientific performance formed a third theme. Participants reported the absence of transparent criteria for author order, direct experience or observation of honorary and ghost authorship, and informal pressures during publication, as well as a lack of formal training in publication ethics. Financial resources and infrastructure encompassed scarce seed funding, protracted budget procedures, equipment shortages aggravated by sanctions, and publication costs. Time, workload, and organizational support were among the most frequently coded themes; participants across roles described a lack of protected research time amid heavy clinical, educational, and administrative loads.

The remaining themes comprised skills, training, and methodological consultation (limited practical competence in methods and statistics, and scarce access to methodologists); publication and access to resources (database restrictions, filtering, and pressure toward quantity over quality); collaboration and internationalization (legal and political risks of foreign partnership and weak institutional support for networking); bureaucracy, permits, and ethics committees (lengthy multistage approvals with opaque criteria); and research culture and motivation (promotion incentives, unhealthy competition, and conflicts of interest). Each theme mapped directly onto a questionnaire domain, as summarized in the joint display (Supplementary Table S2).

Participants characteristics

Three hundred fifty-eight individuals affiliated with TUMS participated in the study. The average age of respondents was 28.66 ± 7.38 (range: 20–63) years. Participants indicated that they had a median of 2 years of research experience (IQR: 1–2), had published a median of two articles over the past 5 years (IQR: 0–2), and had a median Google Scholar H-index of 1 (IQR: 0–1). Among the total participants, 62.6% were male (n = 224), and 37.4% were female (n = 134). Regarding academic positions, 49.7% were medical students (n = 178), 22.9% were residents (n = 82), 6.1% were assistant professors (n = 22), 3.1% were associate professors (n = 11), 2.8% were full professors (n = 10), and 15.4% were affiliated or stand-alone researchers (n = 55) affiliated with TUMS.

In terms of prior research experience, 67.3% (n = 241) indicated they had engaged in original research, 13.7% (n = 49) reported having some form of such experience, and 19.0% (n = 68) stated they had none. Additionally, 70.1% (n = 251) were actively engaged in a research project. Access to high-quality internet was affirmed by 25.7% (n = 92), indicated as "somehow" by 48.9% (n = 175), and denied by 25.4% (n = 91). Reliability and internal consistency

The full 45-item instrument showed acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.85 for raw items and 0.86 for standardized items, and no single item improved the overall coefficient on deletion. Reliability at the domain level was more variable. Only the financial resources and infrastructure domain reached a conventionally adequate level of agreement (α = 0.76). The time, workload, and organizational support domain (α = 0.67), data access and quality (α = 0.61), and skills, training, and methodological consultation (α = 0.60) were modest, while the remaining six domains ranged from 0.36 to 0.53. A few items behaved poorly within their assigned domain: the corrected item total correlations for the privacy and legal risk items (Q6, Q11), the ethics-training item (Q17), and the unhealthy competition item (Q45) were near zero or negative, indicating that these items did not cohere with the barrier direction of their domains. Full item statistics are given in Supplementary Table S3.

The correlation matrix was suitable for factor analysis (KMO = 0.83; Bartlett's test of sphericity χ2(990) = 4718.0, P < 0.001). Parallel analysis supported the retention of five to six factors, considerably fewer than the 10 content domains, and the empirical eigenvalues exceeded their random counterparts only up to the sixth factor. A six-factor exploratory solution accounted for 33.9% of the common variance and produced an interpretable, though not domain-identical, structure. The first factor combined institutional data and governance items (data access, standardization, internal policies, secure infrastructure, and ownership) into a single dimension, suggesting that participants did not cleanly distinguish between data access and data governance as the content model assumed. A second factor drew together externally imposed constraints that cut across the original domains, namely import and sanctions effects (Q23), internet cost and filtering (Q34), political sensitivity of topics (Q36), and legal or political risk in foreign collaboration (Q38). A third factor combined individual capacity, protected time, and methodological training (the support and skills items); a fourth captured authorship and research integrity; a fifth isolated privacy and legal apprehension; a sixth reflected research culture and institutional incentives. The full pattern matrix is presented in Supplementary Table S4.

When the a priori 10-factor model was tested for confirmation, its fit was poor (χ2(900) = 2129.0; CFI = 0.69; TLI = 0.66; RMSEA = 0.062, 90% CI 0.058–0.065; SRMR = 0.17), and 11 items loaded below 0.40 on their designated factor.

Intergroup comparisons and correlations

Intergroup comparisons were conducted to assess differences in questionnaire scores across academic positions. The total scores did not differ significantly among groups (P = 0.27). Two subscales exhibited statistically significant differences between groups: (1) time, workload, and organizational support and (2) skills, training, and methodological consultation.

In the domain of time, workload, and organizational support, significant differences were observed (P < 0.001). Post hoc analysis revealed that stand-alone researcher participants had significantly lower scores than all other groups, except for full professors. A significant effect was observed for skills, training, and methodological consultation (P < 0.001), with significant differences observed among residents, medical students, and researchers. No significant differences were observed across positions in other domains.

Correlational analyses indicated a weak but significantly positive relationship between participants' total scores and years of research and H-index (Pearson correlations: 0.18 and 0.12; P < 0.001 and P = 0.01, respectively). Discussion

The present study aimed to characterize perceived barriers, facilitators, and motives for conducting medical research within a developing-country context. Taken together, the reliability and dimensionality findings indicate that the instrument functions well as an overall index of perceived research barriers but that its 10 content domains are better understood as thematic groupings for interpretation than as separately validated subscales. The outcomes represented moderate-to-strong endorsement of research barriers, with the highest scores in domains related to access to evidence, data systems, and governance. Total scores do not differ by academic position (P = 0.27), but significant differences appear in time, workload, and organizational support, as well as in skills, training, and methodological consultation (both P < 0.001). Participants also describe mentorship and institutional support as facilitators of sustained engagement.

The two phases converged on some points and diverged on others, and both patterns are informative. Financial resources and infrastructure, and authorship and research integrity, emerged as coherent domains in both strands: they were reported as distinct concerns during interviews and retained as separate, internally consistent factors in the quantitative analysis. Access to evidence and data governance told a different story. Participants described these as separate problems qualitatively, restricted access on the one hand and unclear ownership on the other. However, the exploratory factor analysis collapsed them into a single dimension, suggesting that, in practice, these concerns are experienced as a single, undifferentiated barrier rather than two distinct ones.

Some qualitatively prominent themes lost weight once quantified. Research culture and motivation, despite receiving considerable attention in interviews, produced the lowest domain mean in the survey, and one of its constituent items showed poor psychometric properties. This gap between qualitative salience and quantitative endorsement is itself a finding: it points to a barrier that participants readily raise in conversation but do not rate as pressing when asked to weigh it against more immediate constraints such as time and data access.

The integration also presented a structure that neither phase produced on its own. Four items addressing sanctions, internet filtering, political sensitivity, and the legal risk of foreign collaboration, originally assigned to three separate content domains, loaded onto a single factor in the quantitative analysis. This cross-cutting dimension of externally imposed constraint was not anticipated by the qualitative coding, which had organized these concerns under existing institutional categories. Only by testing the qualitative structure against the quantitative data did this distinct barrier become visible, which is a genuine example of quantitative results expanding rather than merely confirming the qualitative phase. The confirmatory model built on the original 10 domains fit poorly, and the exploratory solution favored five to six broader dimensions instead. Read together with the interview material, this indicates that participants' lived experience of these barriers is more consolidated than the initial coding framework assumed, a conclusion that would not follow from either phase in isolation.

The present findings align with evidence from settings where routine health information systems are increasingly used for research but remain limited by data quality concerns, restricted access, and uneven analytic capacity. A systematic review of routine health information system data use in low- and middle-income countries reports growing research utilization alongside persistent challenges in availability and quality, consistent with the barriers described by participants in this study [28]. In addition, a systematic review focused on medical and nursing professionals and trainees in low- and middle-income countries identifies financial and institutional barriers to accessing scholarly literature and highlights practical solutions, such as improving internet access and increasing awareness of free resources [22]. Together, these studies suggest that access to evidence and data systems is an upstream constraint on research productivity.

Some publications present digital health tools as important accelerators of health-system performance and related research activity, suggesting that expanded access to digital platforms can reduce certain bottlenecks [29, 30]. This emphasis contrasts with the present study, where participants describe persistent constraints that depend more on governance, institutional support, and the everyday conditions required to use data and evidence effectively. In other words, even when technology is available, its practical value for research can remain limited if data access pathways are unclear, documentation is inconsistent, or oversight and incentives are weak.

The barrier structure identified here overlaps substantially with findings from other low- and middle-income settings, though the emphasis differs in instructive ways. In Tanzania, a mixed-methods study of healthcare providers converged on training and mentorship, infrastructure and funding, and collaboration as core obstacles, closely paralleling the skills/training, financial resources, and collaboration/networking domains identified in the present sample [4]. A cross-sectional study of Egyptian medical students similarly ranked funding, time, and methodological training among the most frequently endorsed barriers, again matching several of the highest-scoring domains here [31]. The present findings diverge from both in one respect: data access and quality, rather than funding or time, had the highest domain mean in this sample. This may reflect a setting where restricted database subscriptions and inconsistent internet connectivity, reported by roughly three-quarters of participants, function as a more immediate constraint than they do in contexts with more stable institutional infrastructure. A qualitative study of Pakistani undergraduates identified time pressure, motivational and training deficits, and inadequate mentorship as dominant themes. However, it did not show data governance or externally imposed access restrictions as a distinct category [32]. This gap may reflect differences in interview guides rather than a true absence of the concern. Taken together, these comparisons suggest that while core categories of research barriers recur across low- and middle-income settings, their relative salience is shaped by local infrastructural and regulatory conditions, a pattern consistent with the present study's cross-cutting external-constraint factor.

At the same time, studies that focus on implementation help explain why these views differ. A scoping review of open-source electronic health record systems in low- and lower-middle-income countries shows that adoption often slows because of limited organizational capacity, insufficient staff preparation, and broader social and operational constraints [33]. This suggests that variation across settings may reflect differences in implementation context rather than solely in software features.

A second contrast comes from research conducted during public health emergencies in Iran, where investigators frequently identify time pressure, constraints on consent, and complex ethics approval processes as dominant barriers [23]. While the present study addresses ethics-related concerns, participants more often describe barriers that persist in routine academic activities.

One possible explanation for the prominence of system-level barriers is that research activities depend on workflows that require reliable access to the literature, consistent data collection, and clear institutional incentives, which also align with the weak positive association between overall barrier scores and both years of research experience and H-index. When any of these inputs is fragile, individual capability can be insufficient to compensate. This is consistent with literature on the reuse of electronic health records, which shows that data completeness, accuracy, and suitability for secondary use influence the validity of results even when analytic methods are robust [34]. Notably, the time, workload, and organizational support domain shows an inverse association with publication count, suggesting that researchers with higher output have greater time protection or more stable support structures. Another interpretation is that unclear authorship practices and limited procedural transparency may contribute to perceived difficulty and uncertainty, particularly for junior researchers. Guidance from the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors emphasizes accountability and responsibility for authorship, but achieving this is difficult when rules on contribution and supervision are unclear or inconsistently applied [35]. In parallel, the emergence of questionable research practices is linked to incentive environments, publication pressure, and weak monitoring systems. Empirical work in health services research suggests that individual and institutional environments can shape how messages and conclusions are reported, which supports the concern that governance influences credibility [36]. Broader metaresearch similarly argues that biased incentives and selective reporting can inflate the prevalence of unreliable claims, reinforcing the importance of integrity-oriented infrastructure [37, 38].

The domain hierarchy and intergroup disparities indicate a limited number of institutional objectives rather than a broad demand for increased training. The most robust and consistently supported obstacles are access to evidence and the quality of research data, a trend observed across academic positions. Given that only approximately 25% of participants indicated consistent internet access, the most justifiable initial investment is a regulated data and evidence framework: negotiated access to databases and journals, enhanced library mediation, and a formalized, time-constrained process for requesting and reusing institutional clinical data. Implementing recognized data quality dimensions in standard datasets and aligning them with FAIR principles would enhance the feasibility of secondary use and reduce unnecessary duplication, as participants implicitly indicated [34, 39]. The findings on time and workload advocate for an alternative tool, given that perceived time constraints predominantly affect residents and junior faculty. At the same time, being least significant for those with the highest productivity, it is more effective to allocate protected research time as a role-specific entitlement linked to the training stage rather than as a standardized policy.

Additionally, it should be accompanied by methodological consultation integrated at the residency level, where the perceived skills deficit is most pronounced. At the governance level, formalizing authorship and contributorship regulations provides a cost-effective solution that directly addresses the third-ranked domain; the ICMJE criteria and structured credit taxonomies furnish institutions with a ready framework to mitigate ambiguity and perceived inequity, particularly affecting researchers [35, 40, 41]. The order is significant in this context. Evidence and data infrastructure constitute the common basis, while protected time and methodological assistance serve as the specific levers that influence how individuals advance their research careers. Finally, research integrity initiatives that incentivize transparent methods and reporting may limit avoidable waste and improve credibility. Open science recommendations emphasize that reproducible workflows, preregistration where feasible, and clearer reporting standards can make results more robust and easier to evaluate [42, 43]. Protocol standards such as SPIRIT can improve planning and reporting consistency [44]. These changes collectively address not only output volume but also the trustworthiness and usability of the output.

Limitations, strengths, and future directions

Several limitations qualify these findings. The study was conducted at a single institution: Tehran University of Medical Sciences, the largest and most established medical university in the country, with comparatively greater access to funding, infrastructure, and library resources than most Iranian medical schools. The perceived barriers reported here may therefore represent a conservative estimate, and institutions with fewer resources could experience these constraints more acutely, which limits the transferability of both the overall profile and the between-group differences. The instrument's psychometric properties are a further limitation. Although internal consistency for the full scale was high, reliability at the domain level was modest for most subscales and low for research culture, and the a priori 10-domain structure was not confirmed; exploratory analysis instead pointed to five or six broader dimensions. The domains should thus be read as content-based groupings that organize interpretation rather than as independently validated subscales, and several individual items performed weakly and warrant revision.

Additionally, content validity was established qualitatively through expert panel review rather than through formal indices; item-level relevance and essentiality ratings were not recorded in a form that permitted computation of the Content Validity Index or Content Validity Ratio, and a quantified content-validity evaluation therefore remains an objective for the planned multicentre validation. The instrument was also developed and administered in a single Persian-speaking population, so its structure requires external validation before use elsewhere. Beyond measurement, reliance on self-report leaves the data open to recall and social desirability biases, and sensitive issues such as authorship disputes or data ownership conflicts may be underreported. The completion rate among those who began the survey raises the possibility of non-response bias, and the cross-sectional design cannot establish whether changes in institutional policy or infrastructure would causally alter research engagement.

Notable strengths balance these limitations. The instrument was grounded in qualitative data from the target population, anchoring its content in locally salient concerns rather than in items imported from other settings. Recruitment used quota-based stratified sampling across faculties and academic roles. It yielded a large sample that spanned students, residents, faculty, and independent researchers, supporting reasonably representative profiling within the institution. Combining qualitative depth with quantitative breadth improved interpretability and allowed the barrier hierarchy to be characterized rather than merely enumerated. Finally, the full and transparent reporting of the instrument's psychometric properties, including the analyses that did not support the intended structure, provides a realistic basis for the refinement and multicentre validation set out below.

Future multicentre studies should assess whether the pattern of barriers observed here is consistent across institutions with different funding models, supervisory norms, and data infrastructures. Longitudinal evaluations could examine whether reforms, such as formal contributorship policies or improved data access, lead to measurable changes in research activity and publication quality. Methodologically, future work should test interventions to improve data quality and readiness for reuse, including standardized audits and documentation routines grounded in established data-quality dimensions [34]. Studies should also evaluate the impact of structured protocol development and reporting guidance, such as SPIRIT-based templates, on project completion and dissemination timelines [44]. Implementation research is needed to identify which institutional investments most effectively improve research integrity outcomes [45].

Conclusion

This study provides an institutionally grounded assessment of perceived barriers to medical research in a resource-constrained academic setting. The results indicate moderate-to-strong endorsement of research challenges, with the highest burden concentrated in domains related to access to evidence, data systems, and governance. Total barrier scores do not differ by academic position. However, significant differences are observed in the domains of time, workload, and organizational support, as well as in skills, training, and methodological consultation. Overall, the findings suggest that strengthening research quality and sustainability requires institutional reforms that improve access to high-quality evidence, strengthen routine data capture and secondary-use readiness, and clarify governance and integrity-related processes. Addressing these modifiable system-level constraints is likely to support more credible and sustainable research output in similar academic medical environments.
