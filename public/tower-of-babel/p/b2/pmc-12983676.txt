Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF inhibited by early-life lincomycin exposure alleviate intestinal damage and inflammation sensitivity
Authors: Shunfen Zhang, Shanlong Tang, Hui Han, Ye Xu, Dadan Liu, Huixin Wang, Ruqing Zhong, Liang Chen, Hongfu Zhang

Introduction

Antibiotics are widely used in both clinical medicine and livestock production to prevent and treat bacterial infections. However, their extensive and often inappropriate use has raised increasing concerns about antibiotic resistance and adverse effects on host health [1]. Lincomycin, a lincosamide antibiotic, has long been utilized in the treatment of bacterial infections and sepsis, addressing respiratory infections, soft tissue infections, and severe gastrointestinal infections in young children [2, 3]. Nonetheless, its adverse effects, including pseudomembranous enteritis or antibiotic-associated diarrhea, remain significant concerns [4, 5]. These complications are particularly prevalent and severe in early life, a critical window for the development of the gut microbiome and immune system [6]. Therefore, understanding the specific mechanisms by which early-life lincomycin exposure disrupts intestinal homeostasis is of paramount clinical importance.

The detrimental effects of antibiotics are increasingly attributed to their disruption of the commensal gut microbiota [7], a complex ecosystem essential for host health [6]. Antibiotic-induced dysbiosis can compromise intestinal barrier function, alter immune responses, and promote inflammation [8]. Recent research has reported early-life lincomycin exposure’s correlation with an altered composition of gut microbiota [9, 10]. However, the precise, causative role of these microbial changes in driving intestinal pathology remains inadequately defined. Moreover, whether disordered microbiota induced by lincomycin can directly exacerbate host inflammatory responses has not been fully elucidated.

Emerging evidence has shown the potential of probiotics in rebalancing microbial communities and mitigating intestinal damage post-antibiotic exposure [11, 12]. However, the outcomes of probiotic supplementation during antibiotic treatment on microbial composition and diversity are inconsistent [13–15]. Therefore, continued research and personalized treatment approaches are essential for strategies to mitigate antibiotic exposure-related intestinal damage. Lactobacillus reuteri, an intestinal symbiotic bacterium prevalent in humans, rodents, pigs, and chickens, has increasingly been studied for its therapeutic potential in treating intestinal diseases like inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and colorectal cancer [16, 17]. Numerous studies have shown that Lactobacillus reuteri has beneficial functions such as inflammation regulation, immune modulation, maintenance of intestinal microbiota balance, reinforcement of the intestinal barrier, and metabolic regulation [18, 19].

This study reports that early-life lincomycin exposure decreased the abundance of beneficial probiotics such as Lactobacillus reuteri, while increasing the abundance of potentially pathogenic bacteria, consequently precipitating intestinal barrier impairment and inflammation. Furthermore, colonization by lincomycin-exposed microbiota exacerbates the host inflammatory response and intestinal damage after lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation. Therefore, Lactobacillus reuteri was chosen for further validation to determine whether its supplementation could mitigate lincomycin-induced intestinal inflammation. The results showed that supplementation with Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF after lincomycin exposure alleviates the host inflammatory response and intestinal barrier damage after LPS stimulation through inhibition of the TLR2/MyD88/NLRP3 pathway. The findings of this study provide new insights into the mechanisms by which antibiotic-induced dysbiosis influences intestinal health and highlight the therapeutic potential of Lactobacillus reuteri in mitigating antibiotic-associated intestinal damage.

Results

Lincomycin exposure impacts intestinal barrier and inflammation of the piglets

Our previous research found that lincomycin exposed piglets still showed weight loss 3 weeks after discontinuation of exposure [20]. Exposure to lincomycin significantly reduced liver and heart weights, as well as shortened the length of cecum (P < 0.05, Fig. 1A). Compared with the control group (CON), the ileum and colon villus of piglets in the lincomycin exposed group (LC) were sparse and short, and the crypts showed irregular arrangement (Fig. 1B). Lincomycin exposure significantly reduced the ratio of ileum villus height to crypt depth (P < 0.05, Fig. 1C). Additionally, it led to a reduction in the mRNA levels of Claudin-1 (P < 0.05, Fig. 1D). Western blot analyses further revealed a significant decrease of Occludin and Mucin2 expression in the colon of piglets (P < 0.05, Fig. 1E). In addition, cytokines expression levels assessed via western blotting indicated a significant reduction in IL-10, IL-17A, and RORγt following lincomycin exposure (P < 0.05, Fig. 1E), and tended to increase NLRP3 expression (P = 0.07, Fig. 1E). Collectively, these results suggest that lincomycin exposure adversely impacts intestinal morphology, intestinal permeability, and inflammation in piglets.

Lincomycin exposure impacts gut microbial communities in piglets

To elucidate the impact of lincomycin exposure on gut microbial communities, we conducted an analysis of the 16S rRNA gene sequencing. This analysis showed no significant differences in the gut bacterial diversity between CON and LC groups in the ileum and colon (Fig. 2A). Nonetheless, there was a tendency for lincomycin exposure to decrease the Ace and Sobs indices in the cecum, although this did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.06, Fig. 2A). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) demonstrated that the gut microbiota of the CON and LC groups formed distinct clusters in the ileum, colon, and cecum, indicating a clear separation in microbial composition (Fig. 2B). The microbiota compositions of the CON and LC groups were further examined at both the phylum and genus levels (Fig. 2C). At the phylum level, Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, and Actinobacteriota were predominantly found in the ileum, colon, and cecum. At the genus level, Lactobacillus, Clostridium sensu stricto 1, Streptococcus, Prevotella, Blautia, and Coprococcus were prevalent in the ileum. In contrast, in the colon and cecum, Clostridium sensu stricto 1, Subdoligranulum, Prevotella, Ruminococcus torques group, Blautia, Phascolarctobacterium, Prevotellaceae NK3B31 group, and norank_f__norank_o__Clostridia UCG-014 were identified as abundant bacterial species. This comprehensive analysis provides insight into the shifts in gut microbial communities following lincomycin exposure.

Lincomycin exposure also showed a tendency to decrease Lactobacillus abundance in cecum (P = 0.08, Fig. 3C) and Lactobacillus reuteri abundance in colon of the piglets (P = 0.06). Furthermore, the abundance of Lactobacillus reuteri in cecum of the LC group was significantly lower than that in the control group (P < 0.05). On both the 7th and 28th days of the experiment, the fecal abundance of Lactobacillus in the LC group was significantly reduced compared to the control group (P < 0.05, Fig. 3D).

FMT from piglets to mice transfers the intestinal microbial composition and subsequent susceptibility

To investigate the impact of microbiota disruption caused by lincomycin exposure on susceptibility to further challenges, fecal microbes of piglets in both CON and LC groups were transplanted into mice (Fig. 4A). These mice were subsequently exposed to LPS. The results showed that both received microbiota from control piglets (FMT-CON) and received microbiota from lincomycin exposure piglets (FMT-LC) group mice exhibited a significant decrease in body weight compared to the control group after LPS administration (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B), with the FMT-LC group showing a trend towards greater weight loss compared to the FMT-CON group (P = 0.06). Feed intake was significantly reduced in both FMT-CON and FMT-LC groups compared to the CON group, but there was no significant difference between the FMT-CON and FMT-LC groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 4C). However, there was no significant difference in water intake among the three groups (P > 0.05, Fig. 4D). Colonic H&E staining revealed significant inflammatory infiltration in FMT-CON and FMT-LC groups after LPS stimulation, with the FMT-LC group showing more severe inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 4E). The colonic inflammatory score in the FMT-CON and FMT-LC groups was significantly higher than in the CON group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4E).

At the phylum level, Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Actinobacteriota, and Patescibacteria predominated in the CON, FMT-CON, and FMT-LC groups. Genera such as Roseburia and Gordonibacter were significantly more abundant in the FMT-LC group compared to the CON and FMT-CON groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 4G and S1 B), with Lactobacillus showing a decreasing trend in the FMT-LC group relative to the CON group (P = 0.06, Fig. 4H). At the species level, the top five species in the CON and FMT-CON groups included Lactobacillus murinus (47.87% and 46.67%, respectively), unclassified_g__Lactobacillus (9.3% and 11.58%, respectively), Lactobacillus intestinalis (9.64% and 5.55%, respectively), Lactobacillus reuteri (7.48% and 4.60%, respectively), and uncultured bacterium_g__norank_f__Lachnospiraceae (3.05% and 4.79%, respectively). The mRNA expression levels of inflammatory factors were measured by qPCR. The results showed that the expression of IL-1β, IL-18, TNF-α, NLRP3, TLR4, and RORγt was significantly higher in the FMT-LC group compared to the CON group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4I and J). Notably, the mRNA expression of TNF-α and NLRP3 was significantly lower in the FMT-CON group compared to the FMT-LC group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4J).

Supplementation with Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF alleviates intestinal damage, inflammation, and susceptibility in mice

Lincomycin was added to Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF culture medium in vitro, and the results showed that lincomycin inhibited the growth of Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF (Fig. 5A). To explore the roles and mechanisms of Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF in the context of lincomycin exposure, mice were orally administered Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF one week after lincomycin exposure (Fig. 5B). Lincomycin exposure had a tendency to reduce the body weight of mice before LPS challenge (P = 0.07), but the body weight was no significant difference of mice between Lactobacillus reuteri management group (LC + L.r) and CON group (P > 0.05, Fig. 5C). The results indicated a significant reduction in body weight following LPS stimulation (P < 0.05, Fig. 5C). However, the LC + L.r group displayed a higher body weight compared to the LC group, although this difference was marginally significant (P = 0.07, Fig. 5C). The colon length in the LC group was significantly shorter than that in the CON, LPS, and LC + L.r groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 5D). Histological analysis of the ileum showed that compared with CON, LPS, and LC + L.r, the villus in the LC group were more sparse, messy, and shorter, and the crypts were shallower and irregularly arranged (Fig. 5E). The ileum villus height in the LC group was lower than in the LC + L.r group (P = 0.06, Fig. 5F), and the crypt depth in the ileum of the LC group was significantly less than in the CON and LC + L.r groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 5F). The intestinal epithelial damage score was significantly higher in the LC group compared to the CON group (P < 0.05, Fig. 5G), showing a trend towards being higher than in the LPS group (P = 0.07, Fig. 5G). Nevertheless, the epithelial damage score in the LC + L.r group was comparable to that in the LPS group (P > 0.05, Fig. 5G). Colonic H&E staining revealed significant inflammatory infiltration in the LPS, LC, and LC + L.r groups after LPS stimulation, with the LC group showing more severe inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 5E). The colonic inflammatory score in the LC group was significantly higher than in the CON and LC + L.r group (P < 0.05, Fig. 5E).

The mRNA expression levels of tight junction-related genes and inflammatory factors were also measured by qRT-PCR. The mRNA expression of Occludin was significantly reduced in the LC group compared with the CON, LPS, and LC + L.r groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 6C). Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF administration also increased the mRNA expression of ZO-1, IL-10, IL-22, and FOXP3 compared with the CON, LPS, and LC groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 6C). The mRNA expression of TLR4, MyD88, and NLRP3 was increased in the LC group compared with the CON group, but alleviated by Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF administration (P < 0.05, Fig. 6D). Collectively, these findings suggest that Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF administration can alleviate intestinal damage and inflammatory infiltration induced by lincomycin exposure, thereby reducing the susceptibility of lincomycin-exposed mice to LPS-triggered effects.

Discussion

The present study developed a lincomycin exposure model in piglets, replicating its effects on gut microbes and health. The results showed that lincomycin exposure induced the imbalance of microbiota in the ileum, colon, and cecum, leading to shaping distinct microbial clusters on cessation. Compared with the ileum, lincomycin exposure changed the microbial composition of the colon and cecum more significantly. This is consistent with previous studies reporting that lincomycin treatment leads to loss of microbial diversity and changes in microbial composition and relative abundance [10, 21, 22]. Lincomycin exposure remarkably reduced symbionts’ abundance like Lactobacillus, norank_f_Muribaculaceae, norank_f_Eggerthellaceae, and Mycoplasm, but the abundance of Eggerthella, Dorea, Ruminococcus torques group, and Candidatus Soleaferrea was increased after lincomycin exposure. Lactobacillus plays an important role in intestinal health, maintaining microbial balance, improving digestive function, boosting immunity, and inhibiting pro-inflammatory mediators [23, 24]. Further, the abundance of high abundant and beneficial microbiota Lactobacillus reuteri was enormously diminished, which can improve the distribution of intestinal microbiota and antagonize the colonization of harmful bacteria [25, 26]. Conversely, Ruminococcus torques group, Eggerthella, Dorea, and Candidatus are known as potentially pathogenic bacteria, which were reported to impact inflammation and immunoregulation, and are abundant in IBD and IBS patients [27–30]. This result is consistent with previously reported studies in which pathogenic bacteria became the dominant genus of intestinal bacteria after antibiotic exposure [13].

Previous studies have reported that short-term lincomycin exposure disturbs fecal microbiota composition and affects SCFA synthesis, resulting in lasting effects on the host for more than 3 weeks [9]. The intestinal microbiota, pivotal for homeostasis, succumbs to disruption from lincomycin exposure, curtailing cecal length and distorting ileum and colon morphology [31, 32]. Moreover, lincomycin escalates intestinal permeability, evident in compromised tight junction proteins Claudin-1, Occludin, and Mucin2 [33]. This allowed pathogenic pathogen infiltration, corroborated by heightened NLRP3 expression and diminished anti-inflammatory IL-10 [34]. These results are consistent with antibiotic-induced dysbiosis and subsequent inflammation, despite seemingly contradictory IL-17A and RORγt downregulation. However, IL-17A plays an important role in strengthening the intestinal barrier and defending against bacterial and fungal infections, which means that barrier defenses are weakened after lincomycin exposure [35, 36]. These results are consistent with previous reports that pre-exposure to vancomycin resulted in dysplasia of Th17 cells and significantly reduced expression of IL-17A and RORγt, which were associated with increased mortality after non-inflammatory bacterial translocation, systemic bacterial co-infection, and fungal infection [37].

Early-life disruption of the microbiota has enduring health ramifications, spanning asthma, obesity, metabolic disorders, neurocognitive development, and neonatal morbidity and mortality [38, 39]. Dysregulation of the microbiota caused by antibiotic exposure has been reported to be sufficient to induce leukemia in genetically susceptible mice even in the absence of infection stimulation [40]. Previous studies have shown that antibiotic preexposure leads to increased mortality and bacterial load in mice attacked by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus [41], Streptococcus pneumoniae [42], and invasive candidiasis [37]. In this study, mice colonized by antibiotic-exposed microbes developed more severe weight loss and intestinal inflammation in the case of LPS infection. However, there was no significant difference in feed intake between mice that received the microbes exposed to lincomycin and those that did not, although weight loss was more severe in mice that received the antibiotic-exposed microbes. The more severe inflammatory response observed in the FMT-LC group suggests that inflammation increases the basal metabolic rate, and associated physiological stress may play an important role in driving weight loss [43]. This is consistent with previous reports that animals exposed to antibiotics are more likely to be subsequently infected with Vibrio cholerae, Salmonella enterica, and Clostridium difficile [44]. Microbial composition may also play a role in these results [45], mice receiving antibiotic-exposed microbiota observed less Lactobacillus and more Rhodococcus, Gordonibacter, and other pernicious bacteria colonization, which is consistent with lincomycin-exposed piglets. Gut microbiota disorders have been widely reported to interfere with host energy absorption by affecting short-chain fatty acid synthesis and bile acid metabolism [46]. Lactobacillus is one of the core bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids, and it uses carbohydrates to produce short-chain fatty acids in vivo to participate in some important metabolic activities of the host [47]. A healthy microbiome fights infection by interacting with bacteria, secreting antimicrobials, or boosting host immune resistance [48, 49]. However, antibiotic exposure disrupts microbial colonization resistance and leads to overgrowth of pre-existing pathogens, increasing infection risk in many ways [44]. These results demonstrate that transplantation of antibiotic-exposed microbiota alters the microbial composition of mice and increases the likelihood of subsequent LPS infection.

Supplementation with probiotics has emerged as a promising way to restore intestinal symbiotic bacteria post-antibiotic exposure [50, 51]. In our piglet experiment, lincomycin exposure markedly reduced the abundance of Lactobacillus reuteri in the cecum, colon, and feces. Considering the essential role of Lactobacillus reuteri in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and regulating inflammation [52, 53], we hypothesized that supplementing the diminished Lactobacillus reuteri population could alleviate lincomycin-induced intestinal injury. The results showed that supplementation with Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF following lincomycin exposure protects the host from intestinal damage and alleviated inflammation during LPS infection in this study. Furthermore, Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF supplementation increased the expression of intestinal tight junction proteins, including Occludin, ZO-1, and Claudin-4, thereby fortifying intestinal barrier integrity and defending against pathogenic bacterial infiltration. Notably, Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF supplementation significantly elevated IL-22 expression, renowned for its gut-protective properties [54]. TLR4, a pivotal transmembrane pattern recognition receptor, regulates inflammation and immune responses, often via NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome activation through MyD88 interaction [55, 56]. Heightened TLR4, MyD88, and NLRP3 expression in lincomycin-exposed mice was observed upon LPS stimulation, whereas Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF supplementation effectively dampened their expression [57]. Moreover, Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF supplementation inhibited the release of inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-18, and TNF-α mediated by NLRP3 activation [58]. Th17 cells, along with their hallmark cytokines IL-17 and IL-23, typically propagate inflammation [59, 60]. In this study, heightened IL-17 and IL-23 expression in antibiotic-exposed mice, and alleviated by Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF supplementation. Conversely, Treg cells were known for their immunomodulatory function. Increased expression of FOXP3, a Treg cell marker, and IL-10 was observed following Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF supplementation [61, 62]. These results suggest that Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF may inhibit the TLR4/MyD88 pathway activation and reduce NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby reducing subsequent inflammatory cascades.

Conclusion

This study found that early-life lincomycin exposure leads to dysregulation of the gut microbiota, including depletion of Lactobacillus reuteri, and these microbial compositions influence subsequent intestinal inflammation susceptibility. Supplementation with Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF after lincomycin exposure protects the inflammatory response and intestinal damage during LPS infection. Mechanism studies suggest that Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF may reduce the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by inhibiting the activation of the TLR4/MyD88 pathway, thereby inhibiting intestinal inflammation induced by LPS. These results are valuable in alleviating the gastrointestinal side effects associated with lincomycin. Limitations of the study need to be discussed. It should be noted that during the preparation and administration of fecal suspensions, certain microbial taxa may be reduced or lost due to filtration, centrifugation, or storage steps, which could partially alter the microbial composition transferred to the recipient mice. This potential limitation should be considered when interpreting the FMT results. The broader effects of lincomycin on Lactobacillus genera other than Lactobacillus reuteri remain to be fully elucidated. Other potentially functional lincomycin-inhibitory species should not be overlooked, and further research should focus on the function of these intestinal symbiotes in restoring intestinal disturbance and intestinal damage.

Materials and methods

Experiment design

Newly weaned piglets (Duroc-Landrace-Yorkshire; half male and half female) were randomly assigned into two groups based on their weight to ensure uniformity: a control group (CON) and a lincomycin-exposed group (LC), each comprising 16 piglets. Piglets in the LC group received a diet containing 1000 mg/kg lincomycin for the first week, followed by a basal diet during the second week. In contrast, piglets in the CON group were fed a basal diet throughout the 2-week experimental period. All piglets had ad libitum access to food and water and were housed in comfortable living conditions. At the end of the two-week period, 6 piglets were randomly selected and euthanized in each group, and their livers, hearts, spleens, and kidneys were harvested for weighing. Additionally, the lengths of the jejunum, ileum, colon, and cecum were measured. These gastrointestinal segments, along with ileal, colonic, cecal contents for microbiological analysis, were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 ℃ for further investigation.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

At the end of the experiment, fresh feces were collected from 6 piglets each in the control group and the lincomycin exposure group, respectively. The samples were diluted 1:1 with 50% glycerol and stored at –80 °C for subsequent preparation of fecal suspensions. Before use, the feces were thawed, and samples from the 6 piglets within the same group were pooled and diluted tenfold with sterile physiological saline. The diluted mixture was shaken for 3 min to ensure thorough mixing and then filtered through a 70 μm sterile cell strainer. The resulting filtrate was allowed to stand for 5 min in a 4 °C refrigerator to obtain the supernatant. The fresh filtrates were immediately stored on ice and administered to mice within 2 h to minimize microbial loss.

Thirty-six 21-day-old ICR mice of similar body weight, obtained from Peking University Health Science Center (Beijing, China), were randomly divided into three groups, each consisting of 12 mice: a control group (CON), a fecal microbiota transplantation control group (FMT-CON), and a fecal microbiota transplantation lincomycin group (FMT-LC). Daily, mice in the FMT-CON group were administered 200 μL of supernatant derived from the fecal samples of the CON group piglets, whereas mice in the FMT-LC group received 200 μL of the supernatant from the fecal samples of the LC group piglets. Mice in the CON group received 200 μL of sterile normal saline. All fecal suspensions were freshly prepared each day and administered by gavage once a day for two consecutive weeks. On the last day, mice in both the FMT-CON and FMT-LC groups were intraperitoneally injected with LPS at a dose of 10 mg/kg body weight. After 24 h, the mice were euthanized, and their colons and colonic chyme were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at − 80 ℃ for further analysis.

Isolation and culture of Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF

Fresh colonic chyme samples from healthy piglets were collected and used for Lactobacillus reuteri isolation. The 1 g of colonic chyme samples was mixed with 9 mL of normal saline, and the supernatant was filtered using a 70 μm filter membrane. Subsequently, the supernatant was diluted to different proportions and coated on MRS agar medium. After culture at 37 ℃ for 48 h, single colonies were selected and striated on MRS agar medium to purify colonies. The resulting single colonies were sequenced using universal primers 27 F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). The 16S rRNA gene sequence was compared with the NCBI nucleotide sequence database to identify Lactobacillus reuteri strains. A total of 8 strains of Lactobacillus reuteri were identified, and these strains were tested by simulated gastric fluid (pH = 5), simulated intestinal fluid (pH = 7), and 0.3% pig bile salt solution to select the strains that can better survive in the gastrointestinal tract. The results showed that L.r2, L.r29, and L.r32 had a better survival rate in 0.3% pig bile salt solution; L.r2, L.r12, and L.r9 had a better survival rate in simulated gastric fluid, and L.r14, L.r2, and L.r13 had a better survival rate in simulated intestinal fluid (Figure S2). Therefore, L.r2 was selected for the subsequent experiment and named Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF. The 16S rRNA gene sequencing results of the Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF strain have been uploaded to the NCBI GenBank database (Accession Number: PP911398). The isolated Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF was preserved at the Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences under the reserve number CGMCC NO.30023.

Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF supplementation experiment

For cultivation, Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF was grown in MRS medium, followed by centrifugation. The resultant pellet was rinsed with normal saline, and a bacterial suspension was then prepared by diluting it in normal saline. Forty-eight 21-day-old ICR mice, obtained from Peking University Health Science Center (Beijing, China), were evenly distributed into 4 groups with 12 mice per group, based on the criterion of comparable body weights. The groups were designated as CON (control), LPS (lipopolysaccharide), LC (lincomycin), and LC + L.r (lincomycin and Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF). Mice in the LC and LC + L.r groups received water mixed with 1 g/L of lincomycin for one week. Starting from the second week, mice in the LC + L.r group were administered 200 μL of Lactobacillus reuteri SKLAN202402ZF (4.05 × 107 CFU/mL) suspension daily, while mice in the other groups were given an equivalent volume of normal saline. The experiment period was 3 weeks. On the last day, all mice, except those in the CON group, were intraperitoneally injected with LPS at a dosage of 10 mg/kg body weight. The CON group received a comparable volume of normal saline through intraperitoneal injection. Twenty-four hours after the administration, the mice were euthanized, and their ileum and colon were harvested for further analysis.

Intestinal morphology

Ileum and colon tissues from piglets and mice were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde for more than 24 h before being embedded in paraffin wax, resulting in 5-μm thick sections. These sections underwent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for histological examination. Following staining, measurements were taken for villus height and crypt depth in the ileum, and the ratio of villus height to crypt depth was calculated. Additionally, the muscular layer width of the colon was measured. Histological specimens were randomized and scored blind by a board-certified veterinary pathologist. The epithelial damage scoring system was performed to assess the extent of intestinal damage in mice, according to the method (Table S1) described by Zhang [63].The pathological scoring of the colon was conducted according to the method (Table S2) reported by Wang [64].

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and western blot (WB)

Colonic and ileal mucosa samples were processed for RNA extraction using TRIzol reagents (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), adhering to protocols outlined in our previously published work [20]. RNA concentration for each sample was determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA). Reverse transcription of RNA to cDNA was carried out using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan). qRT-PCR analyses were performed using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara), with gene expression levels normalized to β-actin as the internal control. The method for calculating relative gene expression was based on techniques detailed in our prior research [20]. Primer sequences for these analyses are provided in Table S3 and S4.

For protein analysis, colonic mucosa samples were used to extract total proteins, which were then quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). WB assays were conducted following methodologies described in our earlier studies [20]. Detection of protein signals was achieved using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Bio-Rad, CA, USA), and images were captured using the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS imaging System (Bio-Rad). The main antibodies employed were obtained from the subsequent providers: anti-ACTB (Sangon Biotech, #D110001), Claudin-1 (Bioss, #bs-10008R), Occludin (Sangon Biotech, #D110001), Mucin2 (Bioss, #bsm-60016R), ZO-1 (Proteintech, #21,773–1-AP), IL-10 (Abcam, #ab9969), IL-17A (Beyotime, #AF7260), RORγt (Bioss, #bs-10647R), NLRP3 (Beyotime, #AF2155), FOXP3 (Beyotime, #AF6927). The density of the target protein band was quantified and normalized to actin using Image J v1.8.0 software.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Colon tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, subjected to dehydration, and then embedded in paraffin. Sections were laterally sliced to a thickness of 5 μm, dewaxed in xylene, and sequentially rehydrated using a series of ethanol and distilled water gradients (100%, 95%, 90%, 80%, 70%, and 50%). For immunofluorescence staining of Occludin, ZO-1, Claudin-4, IL-1β, IL-17A, NLRP3, and FOXP3, the initial step involved heating the sections in 0.1 M citrate buffer at 121 °C for 15 min. The sections were then blocked with a solution containing PBS, 1% goat serum, and 0.1% Triton-100 for 30 min, followed by incubation with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. On the following day, after washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with the secondary antibody in a dark chamber at 37 ℃ for 30 min. Post-secondary antibody incubation, slides were again washed with PBS and the nuclei were stained using Hoechst stain (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA). For visualization, images were captured using a fluorescent confocal microscope at a magnification 10 times greater than standard settings. The primary antibodies for colon immunofluorescence staining are as follows: Occludin (Sangon Biotech, #D110001), ZO-1 (Proteintech, #21,773–1-AP), Claudin-4 (Invitrogen, #32–9400), IL-1β (Bioss, #bs-0812R), IL-17A (Beyotime, #AF7260), NLRP3 (Beyotime, #AF2155), FOXP3 (Beyotime, #AF6927). Secondary antibodies for immunofluorescence staining were Cy3-labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (Beyotime, #A0516).

16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis

Microbial DNA from the ileum, colon, and cecum contents of piglets and mice were extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® soil DNA Kit (Omega Biotek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and concentration of DNA were determined by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and kept at − 80 ℃ prior to further use. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified using the primer pairs 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) in an ABI Gene Amp® 9700 PCR thermocycler (ABI, CA, USA). The PCR product was extracted from 2% agarose gel and purified using the PCR Clean-Up Kit (YuHua, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and quantified using Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Libraries were constructed using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit with purified PCR products. The amplicons were then sequenced on the Illumina platform through MiSeq PE300 technology. Raw FASTQ files were de-multiplexed using an in-house perl script, and then quality-filtered by fastp version 0.19.6 (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) and merged by FLASH version 1.2.7(http://www.cbcb.umd.edu/software/flash). Then the optimized sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using UPARSE 7.1(http://drive5.com/uparse/) with a 97% sequence similarity level. The most abundant sequence for each OTU was selected as a representative sequence. To minimize the effects of sequencing depth on alpha and beta diversity measures, the number of 16S rRNA gene sequences from each sample were rarefied to 20,000, which still yielded an average Good’s coverage of 99.09%, respectively. The taxonomy of each OTU representative sequence was analyzed by RDP Classifier version 2.2 against the 16S rRNA gene database Silva v138 using a confidence threshold of 0.7. Data analysis was conducted on the Majorbio I-Sanger Cloud Platform (www.i-sanger.com, Majorbio, Shanghai, China). Alpha diversity indices such as Ace, Sobs, and Chao1 were calculated using Mothur v1.30.1 (http://www.mothur.org/wiki/Calculators), and intergroup differences in alpha diversity were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The similarity among the microbial communities in different samples was determined by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Unweighted-unifrac dissimilarity using Vegan v2.5–3 package. The PERMANOVA test was used to assess the percentage of variation explained by the treatment along with its statistical significance using Vegan v2.5–3 package. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to analyze significant differences in the mean relative abundance of the same species across different groups, and the FDR method was applied to correct the p-values. The raw reads were uploaded to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive database under the accession numbers PRJNA1113544 and PRJNA1113564.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Prior to analysis, data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test. Data that met both assumptions were analyzed using parametric tests; otherwise, non-parametric tests were applied. In the piglet experiment, comparisons between the two groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test. In the mouse experiment, comparisons across multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by the least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Microbial data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal–Wallis test for multi-group comparisons. These non-parametric tests were employed to evaluate differences in alpha-diversity indices (Ace, Sobs, and Chao1) and microbial composition. Multiple testing was corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, denoted as *P < 0.05, ** means P < 0.01. A p-value between 0.05 and 0.10 was considered indicative of a statistical trend.
