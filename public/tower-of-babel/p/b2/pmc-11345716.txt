Reconfigurable Optical Sensor for Metal-Ion-Mediated Label-Free Recognition of Different Biomolecular Targets
Authors: Tiziano Di Giulio, Martina Corsi, Francesco Gagliani, Giuseppe De Benedetto, Cosimino Malitesta, Aurora Mazzei, Amilcare Barca, Tiziano Verri, Giuseppe Barillaro, Elisabetta Mazzotta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractReconfiguration of chemical sensors, intended as the capacity of the sensor to adapt to novel operational scenarios, e.g., new target analytes, is potentially game changing and would enable rapid and cost-effective reaction to dynamic changes occurring at healthcare, environmental, and industrial levels. Yet, it is still a challenge, and rare examples of sensor reconfiguration have been reported to date. Here, we report on a reconfigurable label-free optical sensor leveraging the versatile immobilization of metal ions through a chelating agent on a nanostructured porous silica (PSiO2) optical transducer for the detection of different biomolecules. First, we show the reversible grafting of different metal ions on the PSiO2 surface, namely, Ni2+, Cu2+, Zn2+, and Fe3+, which can mediate the interaction with different biomolecules and be switched under mild conditions. Then, we demonstrate reconfiguration of the sensor at two levels: 1) switching of the metal ions on the PSiO2 surface from Cu2+ to Zn2+ and testing the ability of Cu2+-functionalized and Zn2+-reconfigured devices for the sensing of the dipeptide carnosine (CAR), leveraging the well-known chelating ability of CAR toward divalent metal ions; and 2) reconfiguration of the Cu2+-functionalized PSiO2 sensor for a different target analyte, namely, the nucleotide adenosine triphosphate (ATP), switching Cu2+ with Fe3+ ions to exploit the interaction with ATP through phosphate groups. The Cu2+-functionalized and Zn2+-reconfigured sensors show effective sensing performance in CAR detection, also evaluated in tissue samples from murine brain, and so does the Fe3+-reconfigured sensor toward ATP, thus demonstrating effective reconfiguration of the sensor with the proposed surface chemistry.Keywords: reconfiguration, regeneration, porous silicon, metal-ion functionalization, optical sensor, label-free, carnosine, peptides

Introduction

The requirements on sensing systems are continuously being shifted toward higher flexibility and adaptability.1−3 These are commonly achieved at software and system levels by leveraging intelligent algorithms and hardware redundancy (i.e., incorporating extra hardware into the system), respectively, to enable reconfiguration of the system that adapts to the new operational conditions, to some extent at least. Despite the rapid technological progress in sensor devices, reconfiguration at the sensor level is still regarded as a major challenge. Reconfiguration, intended as the capacity of the sensor to adapt to novel operational conditions, e.g., new target molecules, is a critical aspect to be envisioned already at the level of sensor design. However, research into reconfiguration of the sensor is rare, as it requires breakthrough modifications of existing sensor architectures and/or protocols.

With the increasing challenges facing the biosensor field after the latest pandemic event, interest in sensor reconfiguration has grown, even if this implies the introduction of novel materials, architectures, functionalization schemes, and receptors. This next generation of reconfigurable (bio)sensors envisions a rapid and cost-effective reaction to dynamic changes of the healthcare, environmental, and industrial fields, enabling us to timely face new challenges. Further, it will be beneficial in terms of waste and cost reduction, allowing one to switch between different use cases.

Only a few examples of reconfigurable biosensors have been reported so far.4−7 Buranachai et al.5 recently reported on a fluorescence sensor leveraging a DNA-based reconfigurable molecular aptamer beacon. The sensor consists of a variable part including an aptamer strand and its complementary strand and a constant part including an oligonucleotide doubly labeled with a Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) pair. The two parts are joined via DNA hybridization. The sensor can be reconfigured by washing away the aptamer and the complementary strand, keeping the expensive labeled part. As a proof of concept, the sensor was first designed to bind the enzyme thrombin, an analyte with strong binding, and then reconfigured to bind adenosine, an analyte with weak binding. Xu et al.4 reported on a conductometric biosensor leveraging distinct DNA-wrapped single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) immobilized from solution onto different prepatterned electrodes on the same chip, via a low-cost di-electrophoresis (DEP) method. As proof-of-concept application, the biosensor was used for the selective detection of ss-DNA (i.e., hybridization event) and then, after reconfiguration with suitable aptamers, for the label-free multiplexed sensing of cortisol, neuropeptide Y (NPY), and dehydroepian-drosterone-sulfate (DHEAS). The reconfiguration was done by exposing the chip to formamide to denature the ds-DNA, without affecting the electrical properties of the SWCNT–aptamer hybrids. Despite their versatility and accuracy, reconfigurable aptamer beacon sensors have limitations, including nucleic acid degradation in real matrices, potential false positives from endogenous sequences, and the labor-intensive systematic evolution of ligands by the exponential enrichment (SELEX) design process.8−10

Artificial receptors offer significant advantages for sensor development, including high stability, easy customization for specific analytes, and simpler, faster synthesis compared to traditional bioreceptors.11 Iminodiacetic acid (IDA) is a metal-ion chelator. IDA’s ability to interact with metal ions has been largely used in silica-based metal chelate affinity sorbents for chromatographic purposes.12−17 El Rassi et al.12 pioneered the functionalization of silicon-based materials with IDA using glycidoxypropyltrimethoxysilane (GLYMO) as linker, later readapted by several authors,13,14 for chromatography of amino acids and proteins. Further, IDA-functionalized silica-based materials have been also used in environmental applications.18,19 For example, mesoporous silicon particles were functionalized with IDA to chelate Cr3+ ions, then used for the preparation of tetraethoxysilane (TEOS)-based molecular imprinted polymers (MIPs) for removal of Cr3+ ions from aqueous matrices.18 In another work,19 mesoporous silicon nanoparticles were functionalized with IDA to chelate Zn2+ and Cu2+ ions, which were next converted to metal oxides by calcination and used in antimicrobial applications. Rarely, the binding event of IDA–metal ion complexes with biomolecules has been exploited for sensing purposes. Hutschenreiter et al.20 proposed the use of complex building block molecules bearing fluorophore groups and IDA functionalities to detect metal ions and histidine (his)-tagged molecules, exploiting fluorescence quenching as the analytical signal.

Here, we report on a reconfigurable optical sensor for the label-free detection of different biomolecules leveraging a versatile immobilization chemistry of IDA–metal complexes on a nanostructured porous silica (PSiO2) scaffold used as the transducer.11,21−26 The proposed chemistry allows reversibly grafting a large assortment of metal ions on the PSiO2 surface, namely, Ni2+, Cu2+, Zn2+, and Fe3+, which are used as receptors and can be switched at room temperature under mild conditions to tune the binding ability toward a specific target molecule. As a proof of concept, we designed the sensor to detect a dipeptide, namely, carnosine (β-ala-l-his, CAR), leveraging CAR interaction with divalent metal ions through histidine moieties. We first tested Cu2+-functionalized PSiO2 scaffolds for CAR detection, achieving satisfactory sensing performance in terms of limit of detection, selectivity, sensitivity, and ability to operate in biological samples such as nervous tissue extracts. We then demonstrated the sensor reconfiguration by removing the bound target and switching Cu2+ with Zn2+ ions, achieving a comparable sensing performance toward CAR detection. Eventually, we switched Cu2+ with Fe3+ ions on the PSiO2 surface and demonstrated reconfiguration of the sensor for a different analytical target, namely, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), used herein as a model nucleotide, exploiting Fe3+-chelate binding ability toward ATP via its phosphate group.

Results and Discussion

Silanization of the PSiO2 scaffold is investigated at different times, namely, 30 min and 1 and 2 h, and monitored through UV–vis reflectance spectroscopy. Reflectance spectra before and after silanization are shown in Figure S1. A red-shift of the reflectance spectra that increases with the silanization time is observed, providing indirect evidence of the successful grafting of GLYMO-IDA. Quantitative monitoring of GLYMO-IDA grafted to the PSiO2 surface is performed measuring changes in the effective optical thickness (EOT = 2nL, with n effective refractive index and L thickness of the PSiO2 scaffold) versus silanization time, using the EOT value of bare PSiO2 as reference (Figure 1E). A steady increase of the EOT value vs time is observed, with improved sample-to-sample reproducibility after 1 h (RSD% of about 12%, against ∼17% and ∼22% for 30 min and 2 h, respectively). This is consistent with the increased amount of silane anchored to the PSiO2 surface with time and possible hypersilanization occurring at times >1 h lowering reproducibility. A silanization time of 1 h is thus used for further experiments.

We next studied the chelation performance of a GLYMO-IDA-modified PSiO2 scaffold toward different metal ions, namely, Cu2+, Ni2+, Zn2+, and Fe3+. Reflectance spectra of PSiO2 scaffolds were acquired before and after exposure to metal-ion solutions and EOT values calculated (Figures 1F and S2). Remarkably, a metal-dependent variation of the EOT signal is observed (Figure 1F), which is in agreement with the refractive index of the metal species that can be smaller or larger than 1 and is wavelength dependent.25,29 Specifically, under the tested conditions, the refractive index of copper varies from 0.26 to 1.15 RIU, iron from 1.9 to 3 RIU, and nickel from 1.5 to 2.1 RIU. Consistently, the EOT value decreases after anchoring of Cu2+ (−60 ± 20 nm) and Zn2+ (−10 ± 3 nm) ions, increases for Fe3+ (+100 ± 10 nm), and does not change significantly for Ni2+. Notice that the effective refractive index (neff) of PSiO2 is ∼1.3 RIU.21

XPS analysis of the so-processed samples further corroborates the successful anchoring of metal ions to the PSiO2 scaffold (Figure 1G–L). The signal of each metal ion is clearly visible in the XPS spectra at the expected binding energy, coherently with the metal oxidation state. Interestingly, the N 1s signal ascribed to the IDA iminic group exhibits a significant modification upon metal-ion binding, as reported in Figure S3 and Table S1, which summarizes binding energy (BE) values and relative area of nitrogen components for each analyzed sample. The appearance of a component at higher binding energy is observed, supporting the occurrence of metal complexation possibly attributed to the formation of charged nitrogen species.27

Next, we investigated the detection of CAR with a PSiO2 scaffold functionalized with Cu2+ ions exploiting copper chelation from the CAR histidine moiety; then, we demonstrated a first level of reconfiguration of the PSiO2 scaffold replacing Cu2+ with Zn2+ ions for the detection of CAR (Figure 2A). The dipeptide CAR is selected as the molecular target due to its high biological relevance acting as intra-/extracellular nonenzymatic free radical scavenger,30 antioxidant, divalent metal ion chelating agent,31,32 and proton buffering system.32,33 Also, CAR is investigated as a specific metabolic tool against neurotoxic effects34−36 in patients affected by neurodegenerative pathologies, such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease.35 In addition, considering CAR species-specific levels in muscle tissues of vertebrates, monitoring its concentration allows the investigation of adulteration/counterfeiting of meat-based food and feed products.37,38

We preliminarily investigated the influence of the metal-ion concentration on the PSiO2 surface on CAR binding. Specifically, after functionalization with GLYMO-IDA, PSiO2 scaffolds were exposed to solutions containing different Cu2+ concentrations, namely, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2 M. After anchoring the metal ions, the sensors were tested for CAR detection at the same concentration of 1 mM. The results are summarized in Figure S4A. The sensor response increases with the concentration of metal ions anchored on the PSiO2 surface up to 0.1 M Cu2+ and then tends to saturate. Therefore, a Cu2+ solution with a concentration of 0.1 M was used for the next sensing experiments. Notably, the sensor can be reconfigured by adjusting the concentration of metal-ion receptors on the PSiO2 surface through HCl/EDTA treatment and then successfully used for CAR detection (Figure S4B).

Figure 2B shows EOT signals recorded on the Cu2+-functionalized PSiO2 scaffold with 0.1 M Cu2+ solution upon CAR binding, after removal of CAR and Cu2+ ions, and after functionalization with Zn2+ and binding of CAR. The EOT signal increases after incubation of the Cu2+-modified scaffold with 1 mM CAR solution when compared to the EOT value before incubation, which indicates binding of CAR with copper ions. CAR–copper binding is also confirmed by XPS spectra, which show an increase of the N 1s signal (Figure S5A), ascribable to amine groups of CAR, and a concomitant decrease of the Cu 2p signal (Figure S5B), possibly due to a “shielding” effect from CAR molecules bound to copper ions. These results are corroborated by N/Si and Cu/Si atomic ratio values, which change from 0.06 to 0.19 and 0.07 to 0.04 upon CAR binding, respectively.

Reconfiguration of the sensor with Zn2+ ions is performed firstly removing CAR molecules bound to Cu2+ in HCl solution, then Cu2+ ions in EDTA solution, promoted by the higher complexation ability of EDTA compared to IDA, revealed by the difference between formation constants of Cu2+/EDTA and Cu2+/IDA.39,40 Eventually, the IDA-terminated PSiO2 scaffold is immersed in a ZnCl2 solution for the chelation of Zn2+ ions. The EOT value decreases after removal of CAR, going back to the value recorded before exposure to the target molecule, suggesting that Cu2+ ions bound to IDA chelating groups are not affected by the acidic treatment eluting the dipeptide (Figure 2B). The presence of Cu2+ ions on the PSiO2 surface is also confirmed by XPS analysis, clearly showing the presence of a Cu 2p signal after HCl washing (Figure 2C). Thus, the Cu2+-terminated scaffold can be regenerated and reused multiple times to rebind CAR. After EDTA treatment, the EOT returns to a value comparable with that achieved after GLYMO-IDA functionalization, supporting the successful removal of Cu2+ ions (Figure 2B). This is confirmed by the XPS analysis in Figure 2C, which shows that the Cu 2p signal disappears upon exposure of the Cu2+-terminated scaffold to EDTA solution, while C 1s, O 1s, N 1s, and Si 2p signals are still detectable.

Eventually, the EOT value decreases upon Zn2+ chelation, as expected, and then EOT increases again after CAR binding on the Zn2+-functionalized scaffold, nicely confirming sensor reconfiguration (Figure 2B). The XPS spectrum in Figure 2C acquired after the incubation with Zn2+ clearly reveals the presence of the Zn 2p double peak on the PSiO2 surface.

We next tested the Cu2+-functionalized and Zn2+-reconfigured PSiO2 scaffolds for the detection of CAR at concentrations in the range of 0.1–1 mM (Figures 2D and S6). The concentration range is selected considering that CAR levels in skeletal muscle and nervous tissues of animals, while species-specific, can generally reach the order of mM.41Figure 2D shows the EOT values of the sensor functionalized with Cu2+ and Zn2+ ions, using the EOT0 in clean buffer as a reference, which increase with CAR concentration, demonstrating successful reconfiguration of the sensor. The two sensor configurations show a similar behavior toward CAR detection, as expected given the similar affinity of CAR for Cu2+ and Zn2+ ions. As a negative control, we tested the PSiO2 scaffold functionalized with GLYMO-IDA only, i.e., without metal ions, in CAR detection at 1 mM. No significant change in EOT signal is observed after exposure to CAR, demonstrating the key role of metal ions in mediating the interaction with the target analyte.

We then selected the Cu2+-functionalized PSiO2 scaffolds for a complete analytical evaluation of the sensing performance in CAR detection. Figure 2E shows the calibration curve within the concentration range 0.1–2 mM.42 A good linearity (R2 = 0.9951) over the whole concentration range is achieved, with a limit of detection (LOD) of 25 μM and sensitivity of 37.4 nm/mM. A satisfactory sample-to-sample reproducibility is obtained, with an RSD% of ∼15%, which highlights good control of the functionalization process of PSiO2 scaffolds.

We also evaluated specificity of the interaction between Cu2+-modified PSiO2 scaffold and CAR using three interference molecules, namely, β-alanine, which is one of the two amino acid components of CAR dipeptide, anserine (β-alanyl-3-methylhistidine), a natural CAR-like derivative,33,43 and glycyl-proline, as structural dipeptide analogues of CAR. Figure 2F summarizes the results of the experiments. In all cases the presence of interfering molecules does not dramatically affect the sensor response to CAR, with a maximum response of about 20% measured for anserine, reasonably ascribed to the copper chelating ability of this molecule33,44 that differs from CAR only for the methyl group in position 1 of the imidazole ring of the histidine moiety.45 The binding of Cu2+ with the histidine group is confirmed by the sensor response to l-histidine, which results in a sensor response comparable to that of CAR. CAR detection tests in real solute extracts from the mouse brain were performed using Cu2+-functionalized PSiO2 scaffolds, validating the results by comparison with HPLC analysis. After the sensors’ exposure to extracts, the resulting signal was used to extrapolate the concentration of CAR, which was found to be equal to 255 ± 35 μM, in good agreement with the concentration detected by HPLC analysis (222 ± 30 μM).

Further, we tested the regeneration/reuse of the Cu2+-modified PSiO2 sensor for CAR detection over multiple measurements on the same day, repeated over one month. Changes in the EOT value are monitored after incubation of the sensor with 1 mM CAR solution, and then CAR is removed from the PSiO2 scaffold by washing the sensor in HCl solution. Results are summarized in Figures 2G and S7. An excellent repeatability is observed for measurements carried out on the same day, with an RSD% of 2.5%, as well as over 30 days, with an average variability of only 3%.

Eventually, we demonstrated the reconfiguration of the sensor for a different analytical target, namely, the nucleotide ATP, switching Cu2+ with Fe3+ ions on the surface of the PSiO2 scaffold, exploiting the well-known46 interaction between phosphate and adenine moieties of ATP with the Fe3+ ion. Figure 3A sketches the main steps taken to reconfigure the sensor from CAR to ATP detection, namely, 1) removal of Cu2+ ions in EDTA solution, 2) subsequent chelation of Fe3+ ions, and 3) detection of ATP. Figure 3B shows consistent changes in the EOT value of PSiO2 scaffolds after chelation of Fe3+ ions, detection of ATP 1 mM in buffer solution, removal of the target through HCl washing, and further detection of ATP 1 mM, using the EOTPSiO2 value of bare PSiO2 as a reference. Remarkably, besides reconfiguration of the sensor for ATP detection, regeneration of the sensor is also demonstrated by removal of the target analyte in HCl and subsequent ATP binding.

We then exposed the sensor to ATP concentrations within the range 0.1 to 2 mM of clinical interest47,48 monitoring the reflectance spectra (Figure S8) and plotting the calibration curve of the sensor, using the EOT0 value in clean buffer as reference (Figure 3C). The Fe3+-functionalized sensor shows high linearity over the measured range, good sample-to-sample reproducibility (RSD% = 6.5%), and sensitivity and LOD of 43.5 nm/mM and 30 μM, respectively (Figure 3D).

Conclusions

Here we reported on an easy and robust sensor functionalization strategy that allows the reconfiguration of a sensor at different levels, namely, from regeneration of the sensor to be reused multiple times for the same target analyte, to change of the receptor on the sensor surface to enable the targeting of a new analyte. We leveraged the chelating properties of IDA to anchor different metal ions, namely, Ni2+, Cu2+, Zn2+, and Fe3+, on the inner surface of a nanoporous PSiO2 scaffold used as the optical transducer. As a case study, the dipeptide CAR was first detected on a Cu2+-functionalized device, leveraging the well-known interaction of CAR with copper ions, and interesting results were obtained, also in real biological samples deriving from nervous tissue extracts. A proof-of-concept of reconfigurability was then given at two different levels: 1) first, we switched the metal ions on the PSiO2 surface from Cu2+ to Zn2+ and tested also the ability of Zn2+-reconfigured devices for the sensing of the dipeptide CAR. The Cu2+- and Zn2+-based sensors showed similar sensing performance, as expected due to the similar affinity of CAR with these two divalent ions. 2) Second, we reconfigured the Cu2+-functionalized PSiO2 sensor for a different target analyte, namely, the nucleotide ATP, switching Cu2+ with Fe3+ ions to leverage Fe3+ interaction with phosphate groups. The Fe3+-reconfigured sensor was effective in ATP sensing.

The proposed strategy is highly flexible and effective, enabling the reconfiguration of the sensor for different biomolecules beyond those discussed in this work. This is achieved by choosing the most suitable metal receptor, while keeping the IDA-functionalized optical transducer unchanged. Additionally, the strategy can be readily extended to other transducers beyond the nanoporous silica scaffold used here.

Building on these results, we envisage a novel family of chemical sensors that can be reconfigured with limited cost, time, and effort to target different analytes.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

All chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received. Ultrapure water (conductivity <0.1 μS/cm) was used.

The chemical reagents used included glycidoxypropyltrimethoxysilane (GLYMO, 99%), iron(III) chloride anhydrous (FeCl3, powder, ≥99.99%), copper(II) sulfate anhydrous (CuSO4, powder, ≥99.99%), zinc chloride anhydrous (ZnCl2, powder, ≥99.99%), nickel(II) sulfate anhydrous (NiSO4, powder ≥99.99%), sodium hydroxide anhydrous (NaOH, pellets, ≥98%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36.5–38.0%), monosodium phosphate (MSP, NaH2PO4), and disodium phosphate (DSP, Na2HPO4) and were provided from Honeywell Fluka (College Park, GA, USA).

All solutions were prepared in ultrapure water. Phosphate buffer (PBS) solutions (50 mM, pH 7.4) were prepared using commercial DSP and MPS in appropriate proportions, adding 10 M NaOH to adjust the final pH.

A stock solution of carnosine (2 mM) was freshly prepared in PBS before use. From this, carnosine standard solutions at different concentrations (from 0.1 to 2 mM) were obtained.

Single-side-polished silicon boron-doped wafers (p++ type) with a resistivity of 0.8–1.2 mΩ × cm, orientation ⟨100⟩, were purchased from Siltronix Silicon Technologies (France). Aqueous hydrofluoric acid (HF, 48%) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 98%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany). Absolute ethanol (EtOH, 99.9%), isopropyl alcohol (IPA, 99.5%), diethyl ether (Et2O, >99%), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 96%), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%) were purchased from Carlo Erba Reagents (Italy).

Porous Silica Scaffold Preparation

The preparation of the porous silica scaffold was carried out according to the procedure detailed in our former works and briefly described here.49−51 An anodic etching of a polished silicon wafer (1.5 × 1.5 cm) was performed using a solution of HF (48%):EtOH, 3:1 v/v at RT. A two-electrode Teflon cell equipped with a platinum wire cathode and an aluminum flat anode was employed for the etching of the silicon samples over a circular area of 0.567 cm2. A Keithley 2602A sourcemeter was used to set the etching current density and measure the etching voltage. A first PSi sacrificial layer was etched at 350 mA cm–2 for 20 s and dissolved by alkaline dissolution with a solution of NaOH (1 M)/EtOH, 9:1 v/v, to avoid the formation of a top parasitic layer. The silicon samples were rinsed with DIW and ethanol and used to etch a second PSi layer at 350 mA cm–2 for 35 s. The sample was rinsed in EtOH for 120 s to remove residual HF and in Et2O for 120 s to obtain a crack-free PSi layer. Thermal oxidation of the PSi scaffold was carried out in a muffle furnace (ZB 1, ASAL, Italy) at 750 °C for 1 h (ramp-up/ramp-down 12 °C min–1) to obtain PSiO2 samples.

Optical Reflectance Spectroscopy

Reflectance spectra of the PSiO2 scaffold were acquired in air (both before and after oxidation) in the wavelength range 400–1000 nm using an optical setup consisting of a UV–vis spectrometer (SM242 SP) provided by Spectral Products, a bifurcated fiber-optic probe (QR200-7-VIS-BX), and a halogen lamp source (HL-2000) purchased from Ocean Optics (USA). Light exiting the halogen lamp source is fed orthogonally onto the PSi surface through one arm of the fiber-optic probe; the light reflected from the PSi layer is collected into a UV–vis spectrometer through the other arm of the fiber-optic probe. Acquisition parameters for reflection spectra were as follows: integration time 50 ms, average scan number 15, boxcar width 5, with the spectrometer working in photon counts mode. Porosity of the as-prepared PSi scaffold was estimated by best-fitting of the reflectance spectra of PSi layers acquired before oxidation, using homemade software (Matlab, MathWorks, USA).

FFT of the reflectance spectra of PSiO2 interferometers was performed to calculate the EOT values, namely, 2nL, where n = effective refractive index and L = thickness of the PSi layer, using homemade software (MatLab, MathWorks, USA). The wavelength axis of the reflectance spectrum was first inverted (x axis changed from wavelength to 1/wavelength) to obtain a wavenumber axis. A cubic-spline interpolation of reflectance data was then carried out to obtain a data set (reflection, wavenumber) spaced evenly (sample-to-sample distance 8.57 × 10–7 nm–1). A Hanning window was applied to the reflectance spectrum, which was zero padded to 224. Eventually, application of the FFT algorithm to the zero-padded reflectance spectrum yielded the Fourier transform amplitude and phase (y axis in the Fourier transform domain) as a function of 1/wavenumber (x axis in the Fourier transform domain), with a spatial resolution of about 0.07 nm. The EOT value is obtained as the value of the 1/wavenumber axis (x axis) in the Fourier transform domain for which the main peak in the Fourier transform amplitude (y axis) occurs.52

XPS Characterization

XPS measurements, performed before and after the PSiO2 samples’ functionalization with both GLYMO-IDA and metal ions (namely, nickel, iron, copper, and zinc ions), were recorded with an AXIS ULTRA DLD (Kratos Analytical) photoelectron spectrometer using a monochromatic source (1486.6 eV) at 150 W (10 kV, 15 mA) and 5.3 × 10–9 Torr as base pressure in the analysis chamber. The survey scan spectra were recorded with a pass energy of 160 eV and a 1 eV step, while the high-resolution spectra were obtained with a pass energy of 20 eV and a 0.1 eV step. In both cases, the analysis area was about 700 μm × 300 μm. The spectra processing was carried out with CasaXPS release 2.3.16 software, where the binding energy scale was referenced to the Au 4f7/2 peak at 84.0 eV. During the analysis of the high-resolution spectra, all the peaks were fitted with Shirley background and GL(30) lineshape (Gaussian 70%, Lorentzian 30%). For a quantitative analysis, the relative sensitivity factors from the CasaXPS library were used for the signal areas. The surface charging was rectified by considering adventitious C 1s (BE = 285 eV).

GLYMO-IDA Synthesis

The GLYMO-IDA silane was prepared following a literature protocol with slight modifications.13 A 4.25 g amount of IDA was dissolved in 50 mL of ultrapure water, and the pH was adjusted to 11 using 10 M NaOH. Later, the solution was transferred into an ice-bath at 0 °C, and 1.4 mL of GLYMO was slowly added under stirring. Then the mixture was heated to 65 °C for 3 h under stirring. Subsequently, it was again placed into the ice bath, and 1.6 mL of GLYMO was slowly added under stirring. After this step, the solution was heated to 65 °C for 3 h under stirring. Finally, the mixture was placed into an ice-bath and 1.7 mL of GLYMO was added under stirring; then it was heated overnight at 65 °C. The pH of the final solution was adjusted to 6 using 5 M HCl.

Functionalization of Porous Silica Scaffolds

PSiO2 samples were firstly treated with piranha solution (H2SO4:H2O2, 3:1, v/v) at 40 °C for 30 min. Then the samples were washed with ultrapure water for 2 min, rinsed with ethanol, and dried with a gentle stream of nitrogen. Afterwards, they were placed in a GLYMO-IDA solution for 1 h at 65 °C, to promote the silanization process. A washing with ultrapure water under stirring (200 rpm) was then performed to remove not-specifically adsorbed silane molecules. Later, the substrates were placed in 100 mM metal ion aqueous solutions, namely, CuSO4, ZnCl2, NiSO4, and FeCl3, for 2 h and then were washed again (5 min) in deionized water under stirring (200 rpm).

Preparation of Extracts Containing CAR from Mouse Brain Tissue Samples

Carnosine was extracted by adapting a previously described standard method.36,53 In brief, total mouse brain tissue was lysed at 4 °C in nanopure water with a KINEMATICA Polytron homogenizer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rodano, MI, Italy) and further homogenized by 10 passages through syringe needles. Then, the homogenate was centrifuged at 20000g for 30 min at 4 °C, and then the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter. The water-extracted filtrate was subjected to heat treatment at 80 °C for 15 min, subsequently cooled in an ice bath, and then centrifuged at 6000g for 20 min at 25 °C to remove precipitated proteins. For final deproteinization, the supernatant was mixed with 1 volume of 0.4 M perchloric acid by vortex, and the mixed samples were boiled for 10 min before centrifugation at 5000g for 5 min at 4 °C. The final deproteinized samples were collected and stored at −20 °C for the subsequent analyses.

Mouse Brain Tissue Sampling and Ethical Statement

In full compliance with the 3 Rs principle (replacement, reduction, and refinement), for the preparation of extracts from biological matrices containing CAR, mouse brain samples were obtained from a mouse tissue collection stored in liquid nitrogen at the Applied Physiology lab of the University of Salento, derived from previous experimental research. In brief, brains were obtained from inbred BALB/cByJ male mice (Harlan, Milan, Italy; 12–16 weeks old) maintained under standard laboratory conditions (12:12 h light–dark cycle, temperature 21 ± 1°C), receiving food and water ad libitum. All of the animal procedures were conducted upon review and ethical approval by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Department of Biological and Environmental Sciences and Technologies (University of Salento), according to the guidelines of the European Union Council’s directive for the ethical treatment of animals (Directive 2010/63/EU). Mice were housed according to international standards for health monitoring and animal welfare supervision. The experimental procedures were previously authorized by Decree 109/2014-B of the Italian Ministry of Health after review by the competent organs of the Veterinary Health Department. All of the procedures for animal housing and euthanasia were checked and reviewed by veterinary personnel.

HPLC Conditions for Real Sample Analysis

Chromatography was performed using an HPLC Agilent 1100 series system (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with a vacuum degasser (G1379A), a binary pump (G1312A), an autosampler (G1313A), and a G1315A diode array detector, which was used in the range 200–500 nm. Five microliters of standards or sample was injected into the HPLC system. Carnosine was separated on an Accucore HILIC silica column (2.1 × 100 mm, 2.7 μm) from Thermo at a temperature of 40 °C and a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. Mobile phases consisted of 10 mM formic acid, pH 3, in water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B). The solvents were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter and degassed prior to use. The separation was achieved through a gradient as follows: after a 1 min isocratic elution with 80% B, a 24 min gradient to 60% B was applied followed by 3 min of a washing step with 60% B and returning to the initial conditions in 1 min and then equilibrating the column for 5 min before the next injection. The separation was monitored by using a diode array detector at a wavelength of 214 nm. Peak areas were used to build the relevant calibration curve (Figure S9) and calculate carnosine concentration of the sample (average of 3 measurements).

Sensor Reconfiguration. Carnosine and ATP Detection Tests

Silanized PSiO2 samples functionalized with Cu2+ ions were exposed to carnosine by drop casting (500 μL) increasing concentrations of carnosine (0.1–2 mM) for 1 h at room temperature. After incubation with the dipeptide solution, the substrates were rinsed with water (5 min) under stirring (200 rpm), rinsed with ethanol, and then dried with a gentle stream of nitrogen.

To regenerate the artificial receptor (Cu2+/GLYMO-IDA), it was washed with 0.1 M HCl for 15 min before exposure to different carnosine concentrations. Reflectivity spectra were recorded after each binding with the carnosine experiment. Alternatively, the sensor was exposed to increasing concentrations of interfering molecules, specifically anserine, l-histidine, glycyl-l-proline, and β-alanine, recording the reflectivity spectra after each concentration tested.

For sensor reconfiguration, the metal ion Cu2+ was removed from the artificial receptor (Cu2+/GLYMO-IDA) by a washing procedure with a solution of 3.5 mM EDTA in water with stirring (200 rpm). Subsequently, the anchoring of a different metal ion (namely, Zn2+ or Fe3+) was performed by exposing the as-washed PSiO2 samples to a metal-ion aqueous solution (100 mM).

For ATP detection, Fe3+/GLYMO-IDA-functionalized PSiO2 samples were exposed to ATP solutions (0.1–2 mM) for 1 h at room temperature, and the reflectivity spectra were recorded after each concentration tested. Again, for sensor regeneration a mild washing in 0.1 M HCl aqueous solution (15 min), under gentle agitation (200 rpm), is sufficient. All tests were performed in triplicate.
