Speciation of European bullheads ( Cottus spp.): The next problem for taxonomy of northern freshwater fishes
Authors: David S. Murray, Vladimír Kováč, David Wright, Gordon H. Copp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh morphological and ecological diversity displayed by European bullheads led Freyhof et al. (2005) to describe 15 nominal species. However, the basis for declaring these nominal species is contentious due to a lack of rigorous statistical analysis of morphological variations among populations, limitations in the inferences made from previous generations of molecular studies, a dearth of next‐generation sequencing tools for European bullheads and an underestimation of the complexity of evolutionary processes. Here, we argue that the current evidence is insufficient for accurate speciation of European bullhead into the nominal species proposed by Freyhof et al. (2005). We suggest that future studies incorporate analytical approaches such as chromosome‐level reference genomes, integration of multiple biological data sources into multispecies coalescent models and rigorous statistical analysis of the full range of morphological characters, both meristic and mensural. Furthermore, we highlight the necessity of incorporating a standardised conceptual framework while considering bullhead speciation, as well as some practical suggestions of what such a process would look like.

INTRODUCTION

Approximately two decades ago, Freyhof et al. (2005) described, based on a limited number of morphometric characters and phylogeographic inferences, a complete taxonomic reorganisation of the European bullhead Cottus gobio; this created 14 species from this single, historically recognised species (see Table S1). Subsequent debate regarding European bullheads speciation has been limited, with some researchers not recognising these nominal new species without rigorous, statistically supported testing (Grabowska et al., 2016; Kakareko et al., 2016; Števove et al., 2019), whereas others applied the new names without challenge or scrutiny of the underlying evidence (e.g., Kobler et al., 2012; Nagelkerke et al., 2018; Ovidio et al., 2009; Stoffers et al., 2022). This lack of scrutiny has led to these nominal species being added to easily accessible scientific and conservation databases (e.g., FishBase, IUCN Red List etc). However, in the first study to examine the C. gobio clade more closely, with relevance to C. gobio introduced to Scotland, McLeish et al. (2020) concluded (p. 628) that ‘the Cottus specimens from [their] study as well as the ostensible European species C. duranii, C. hispaniolensis, C. microstomus, C. perifretum, C. rondeleti, C. rhenanus and C. scaturigo, with the potential exception of C. aturi, should be referred to as C. gobio’. This emphasises the urgent need for Cottus taxonomy to be re‐examined and clarified, thereby eliminating any confusion in ‘future publications, legislation and management practices relating to bullheads throughout the UK and Europe’ (p. 617, ibid). For example, C. gobio is protected under EU legislation but the nominal species are not (Habitat Directive 92/43 EEC, i.e., Annex II, Article 17); therefore no legislative obligation exists to protect the nominal species as endemics should they prove to be valid. This paper aims to examine the evidence behind the Freyhof et al.'s (2005) article and to discuss how new theoretical concepts, methods and molecular research could provide the necessary evidence to support or to refute C. gobio delimitation.

WHAT IS A SPECIES?

The ‘species’ concept is highly debated in biology, with scientists establishing a minor industry devoted to the production of new definitions for the term ‘species’ (De Queiroz, 2007). The problem of defining species categories and delimiting species in an objective, consistent and biologically meaningful manner across all living organisms is one of the most problematic issues in biology (Adams & Maitland, 2007). Universal acceptance of a single, clear definition of a species has been elusive, resulting in as many as 34 different species concepts (Zachos, 2018). Although fundamental to European bullhead taxonomy, discussions on each theoretical and philosophical concept for defining a ‘species’ (e.g., Morrison, 2011; Zachos, 2016) fall outside the scope of this paper. However, because Freyhof et al. (2005) used the evolutionary species concept (ESC) to create the largest single taxonomic reorganisation of European Cottus sp. in literature, the ESC concept requires explanation.

Under the ESC, an evolutionary species can be defined as ‘a single lineage of ancestor‐descendant populations of organisms which maintains its identity from other such lineages (in space and time) and which has its own evolutionary tendencies and historical fate’ (Wiley, 1981). The ESC concept was considered by some to be the most flexible species framework, with Mayden (2002) arguing that ‘species in nature are individuals and form lineages. As individuals, they cannot be defined but can only be diagnosed in time’. Furthermore, before additional concepts proliferated (see Zachos, 2018), most definitions agreed that species are evolutionary lineages united by gene flow in sexual organisms, providing credence to the idea that lineages were the key to parsing species (Vinet & Zhedanov, 2011). However, the ESC requires an accurate knowledge of unbroken lineages, which are often analysed from fossil records or, in analysing the evolutionary relationship of freshwater fish species, from DNA‐based studies. The assumed accuracy of these methods is the foundation in which the ESC delimits species, but if the accuracy of these methods to distinguish evolutionary trajectories is questionable, then the ESC prejudices evolutionary distances between progenitor and incipient species (e.g., Forsman, 2015; Wund, 2012). This accuracy is particularly important with potentially cryptic species such as European bullheads, where physical similarities among closely related organisms necessitate genetic lineage sorting.

LINKS BETWEEN GENETICS AND MORPHOLOGY

Meristic and mensural traits have arguably contributed the most evidence to cottid species delineation in bullhead speciation debates (Bacescu & Bacescu‐Mester, 1964; Freyhof et al., 2005; Koli, 1969a, 1969b). However, morphological traits with which species may be delineated are often discretional (Galtier, 2019), and some groups of organisms have proven particularly difficult to assign to species under the various concepts (summarised in Zachos, 2018). A pertinent example for bullheads, which demonstrate a high degree of morphological and life‐history variation (Koli, 1969a; Stemshorn et al., 2011), are the multiple intersecting phenotypes that are recorded consistently among other species of northern freshwater fishes (Guinand et al., 2021; Reist et al., 2013). Systematists, macroecologists, conservation and evolutionary biologists have all struggled to classify species such as brown trout Salmo trutta, Arctic charr Salvelinus alpinus and European whitefish Coregonus lavaretus (Adams & Maitland, 2007; Guinand et al., 2021; Mee et al., 2015). Much of the variation within these species appears to represent the legacy of Pleistocene glaciations, that is, arising from evolutionary‐scale events and allopatric differentiation (Reist et al., 2013). Evolutionary phenomena associated with habitat diversity, resource availability, climate change and genotype–phenotype interactions have created a broad spectrum of morphological and life‐history variations that maintains species viability but complicates these species' taxonomy (Guinand et al., 2021; Reist et al., 2013).

This phenotypic variation has challenged systematists for many decades, resulting in historic species names like ‘inconstans’, ‘perplexus’, ‘variabile’ and ‘varabilis’ (Robinson & Parsons, 2011). For example, the so‐called ‘char problem’ refers to the difficulty taxonomists encountered in classifying the exceptional phenotypic variation within and among Arctic charr populations (Adams & Maitland, 2007; Klemetsen, 2010; Reist et al., 2013). Considerable phenotypic variation is apparent within whitefishes (subfamily Coregonines), which can rapidly change features, for example, gill raker number, in response to interspecific competitors (Lindsey, 1981; Robinson & Parsons, 2011). Owing to this phenotypic plasticity, some whitefish stocks have been referred to as chameleons (Lindsey, 1981). In a similar circumstance to bullhead, Kottelat and Freyhof (2007) claimed to have clarified the uncertainty in the systematics of the Coregonidae. Using meristic traits, the authors recognised 59 separate species of coregonids across Europe, including splitting the seven native UK populations of C. lavaretus into three putative species (Kottelat & Freyhof, 2007). Etheridge et al. (2012) tested the Coregonus meristic key produced by Kottelat and Freyhof (2007) on UK populations and observed that most individuals (73%) could not be identified to any of the three putative species. The authors concluded that the key proposed by Kottelat and Freyhof (2007) was highly unreliable for allocating coregonids to putative species, and that, instead of three species, the variability observed was best explained by phenotypic variation (Etheridge et al., 2012).

Initial studies on European bullhead taxonomy ignored the concept of phenotypic plasticity, defined as ‘the ability of individual genotypes to produce different phenotypes when exposed to different environmental conditions’ (Pigliucci et al., 2006). However, this conceptual ambivalence was not limited to European bullhead taxonomy, being at the centre of the age‐old question of nature versus nurture (Pigliucci, 2005). Phenotypic plasticity has historically been considered a nuisance, from the rediscovery of Mendel's laws through to the early 1980s, when it became an integral part of our understanding of how organisms develop and interact with their environment (Pigliucci, 2005). Now, thanks to several landmark review papers, phenotypic plasticity is acknowledged as an important concept in modern evolutionary thinking (e.g., Pigliucci, 2005; Schneider & Meyer, 2017; Stearns, 1989; West‐Eberhard, 1989), this being the extended modern synthesis (e.g., Fábregas‐Tejeda & Vergara‐Silva, 2018) within which we must assess morphological variations in European bullheads.

European bullheads display similar levels of intraspecific phenotypical variation to the species mentioned above, with Freyhof et al. (2005) stating that this variability was ‘often difficult to describe with words’. Commonly noted examples include so‐called prickling (i.e, calcified spicules embedded in the abdomen), fin rays, genital papilla, head pores, opercular spines and colour patterns (Freyhof et al., 2005). The presence within cottids of a broad spectrum of skin prickling phenotypes among populations has been studied to test the plasticity of this morphological trait (Cheng et al., 2015; Freyhof et al., 2005). Studies of phylogenetic relationships in European bullheads (Cheng et al., 2015; Kinziger et al., 2005) failed to group species by the presence or absence of prickles, suggesting that in this particular instance prickling may not be a fixed, heritable trait. Using hybrids of the nominal species Cottus rhenanus and Cottus perifretum, Cheng et al. (2015) confirmed that the formation of abdominal prickles was controlled by ectodysplasin receptors. Complementary studies have highlighted that these genes are linked to rapid morphological alterations to environmental conditions within other freshwater fishes (e.g., Schluter et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2018). Evidence of environmental stimuli acting on genes to alter morphology questions the validity of using traits like prickles to delineate European bullheads.

Arguably, morphological diversity is the very foundation upon which European bullheads have historically been delimited into species. Koli (1969a) used prickle presence and lateral‐line pore counts to distinguish regional ecotypes within Northern Europe, whereas Kottelat (1997) used predominately meristic and mensural traits to define four species of Cottidae (Cottus ferrugineus, Heckel & Kner, 1858; C. gobio, Linnaeus, 1758; Cottus petiti, Băcescu and Băcescu‐Meșter 1964; Cottus poecilopus, Heckel 1837). However, the taxonomic delineation of C. gobio by Freyhof et al. (2005) used length‐based measurements and counts of a limited number of morphological traits, with no attempts made to conduct sophisticated statistical analysis on these physical traits (p. 112, ibid.). Additionally, for some nominal species, only a single ‘population’ was sampled within the presumptive distribution area. This lack of objective statistical framework combined with the growing evidence of morphological plasticity in response to environmental stimuli renders dubious the validity of the proposed 15 nominal Cottus ‘species’: Cottus aturi, Cottus duranii, C. gobio, Cottus haemusi, Cottus hispaniolensis, Cottus koshewnikowi, Cottus metae, Cottus microstomus, C. perifretum, C. petiti, Cottus poecilopus, C. rhenanus, Cottus rondeleti, Cottus scaturigo, Cottus transsilvaniae.

In fish taxonomy, relying solely on morphological traits for species identification can be problematic, particularly if there is no comprehensive evidence of heritability for those traits. The bewildering speed with which northern freshwater fishes can alter their morphological traits in response to environmental conditions calls into question the usefulness of meristic and mensural traits to speciate phenotypically plastic organisms. For example, coastrange sculpin Cottus aleuticus, a postglacial coloniser of freshwater systems, displays camouflaged pigmentation changes in response to substratum colour changes within a single generation (Whiteley et al., 2009). Environmentally induced variations in meristic and mensural traits have been reported in other fish species, such as three‐spined stickleback. A plated marine morphotype, which had colonised an Alaskan lake devoid of the non‐plated freshwater morphotype, underwent a decrease (from 96% to 11%) in the proportion of fully plated individuals between 1990 and 2001, with most of the decline occurring in the first 3 years (Bell et al., 2004). Additionally, comparative studies on native and repatriated pumpkinseed Lepomis gibbosus, reared under identical environmental conditions, found significant differences in body dimensions, with morphological divergence found not to be associated with genetic factors (Yavno et al., 2014). Without clear evidence of genetic heritability, meristic and mensural traits used to identify nominal European bullhead species have limited functionality.

MOLECULAR ASPECTS OF EUROPEAN BULLHEAD SPECIATION: FROM ALLOZYMES TO SINGLE NUCLEOTIDE POLYMORPHISMS

The advent of molecular techniques in the 1960s (Sick, 1961) has made it possible to look beyond morphological attributes and infer intra‐ and interspecific population structures in ecology and conservation (Allendorf, 2017). Technological advances within molecular sciences, such as next‐generation sequencing (NGS), have provided unparalleled evidence on the evolutionary history and relationships within and among groups of organisms. For species such as Arctic charr, brown trout and European whitefish, genetic data have arguably contributed substantially to discussions aimed at resolving their respective taxonomy, and thus conservation issues (Crotti et al., 2020; Fenton et al., 2026; Segherloo et al., 2021).

For the ESC, the evolutionary history of European bullheads is of prime importance to determine whether they are a single or multiple species. Based on the presence or absence of similar alleles, phylogenetic assessments have been used to measure genetic distances between populations (Stankowski & Ravinet, 2021) such as ‘D’ (Nei, 1972), with the genetic differentiation indices ‘FST’ (Wright, 1951) and RST (Slatkin, 1995) employed to determine a significant level of genetic divergence, which serves as the basis for delineating either populations or species (e.g., Barraclough & Nee, 2001; Harrison, 1991; Templeton, 1981). Regarding European bullhead taxonomy, Freyhof et al. (2005) made inferences to allozyme, mitochondrial DNA and microsatellite analyses from previous studies to support their description of 15 nominal species based on morphological traits. Here, we critique the research associated with each of these primary molecular approaches.

Allozyme

Initial molecular European bullhead research used allozymes and focused on populations within commercially and ecologically important systems such as the rivers Danube, Elbe, Rhine and Rhone. Riffel and Schreiber (1995) collected 206 European bullheads from nine rivers and examined their genetic diversity using 29 allozyme loci. An additional 20 sites were sampled, but small sample sizes (minimum = 2; maximum = 6) made comparisons difficult for the purposes of our review. Genetic distances between catchments led Riffel and Schreiber (1995) to suggest that bullhead populations from the rivers Doubs and Neckar were new cottid species. A similar geographical‐variation study of 16 bullhead populations from the rivers Rhine, Elbe and Danube by Hänfling and Brandl (1998) found five polymorphic loci were fixed for different alleles between Rhine and Danube populations, three between the Rhine and Elbe populations and one between the Rhine and Elbe populations. Two populations were recognised as separate taxa, one from the rivers Danube and Elbe (at least the Bavarian part) and one from the upper and higher Rhine, with Hänfling and Brandl (1998) concluding that ‘whether these taxa are classified as subspecies or as sibling species is a matter of opinion’.

Allozymes have also been used to facilitate studies that question species classifications based purely on meristic and mensural traits. For example, based on the external morphological characters of 17 specimens, Bacescu and Bacescu‐Mester (1964) delimited Lez bullhead C. petiti (from C. gobio) and declared it endemic to the coastal River Lez (France). This was contested in subsequent studies (Koli, 1969a, 1969b), leading Eppe et al. (1999) to use allozymes to compare the genetics of C. petiti with C. gobio populations from the rivers Garonne, Loire and Rhône. Eppe et al. (1999) stated that genetic distances obtained between some of the sampled bullhead populations could be characterised as subspecies or species; but they also noted that bullheads were intermediately differentiated from their closest geographical neighbours, and differences in genetic distances became more apparent only when compared to geographically disparate populations.

Traditionally, allozyme polymorphisms were assumed to be selectively neutral, suggesting that loci conferred no fitness advantage (Allendorf, 2017), whereas subsequent studies found that some allozyme polymorphisms are in fact under selection (Allendorf & Seeb, 2000; Karl & Avise, 1992; Latta & Mitton, 1997; Pogson et al., 1995; Scribner et al., 2011). Such allozymes are more likely to be subject to local selection and consequently detected as outliers in comparison to known neutral markers such as mitochondrial control regions (Olsen et al., 2014). Indeed outlier loci are adaptive, with the different electrophoretic profiles of allozymes developed due to non‐synonymous nucleotide substitutions within loci (Olsen et al., 2014). These substitutions are heritable and after generations would allow the inter and intraspecific genetic variations used above to infer European bullhead speciation. However, environmentally driven, cis‐regulatory processes, such as alternative splicing of messenger RNA and/or post‐translational modifications, may yield similar allozyme variation (reviewed by Wittkopp & Kalay, 2012). Unfortunately, this was never tested on diagnostic allozyme markers in European bullhead populations.

mtDNA

Phylogenetic studies of European bullhead, using mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), have not linked lineages to speciation processes, and no attempt has been made by researchers to suggest alterations to European bullhead taxonomy. Despite this, Freyhof et al. (2005) used mtDNA studies, combined with morphological measurements and geographical proximity, as rationale to delineate their nominal species.

In the first pan‐European study of bullhead genetic diversity, studying historical colonisation routes throughout central Europe, Englbrecht et al. (2000) used 407 specimens from 56 sites (N min = 1; N max = 47) and mitochondrial D loop sequencing between the Pro‐tRNA gene and the end of the central conserved region of the mtDNA. Nucleotide diversity indices and network analysis were used to reveal six genetically related lineages, which were defined by their relative position within the network and their geographical locations. Of these, group 1 was the largest and the most heterogenous group, occurring in the upper and lower sections of the rivers Danube, Main, Elbe, as well as in Finland, highlighting the tremendous geographical scope of this lineage (Englbrecht et al., 2000). Despite evidence of complementary haplotypes within this region, Freyhof et al. (2005) stated that group 1 bullheads included the following nominal species, Cottus affinis, C. ferrugineus, C. gobio, Cottus pellegrini and C. petiti. However, a complementary study using mtDNA on the Mediterranean/Adriatic group 1 haplotype bullheads (Šlechtová et al., 2004) found no significant genetic differences or geographical sorting to support nominal Cottus species proposed by Freyhof et al. (2005).

The other five groups showed a narrower geographic range (Englbrecht et al., 2000): group 2 encompassed southern tributaries of the Baltic Sea and the rivers Odra and Vistula, which most likely extend all the way east to Estonia and, according to Freyhof et al. (2005), including the nominal species C. koshewnikowi and C. microstomus. Genetic analyses, using allozymes and mtDNA data, highlighted evidence of thorough mixing of various bullhead lineages in and around the Baltic, leading researchers to refute the species status of C. koshewnikowi and suggest that evolutionary significant units (ESU) would be a more appropriate designation for bullheads within these regions (Kontula & Väinölä, 2001). The remaining lineage groups (3–6) displayed a distinct westerly distribution, with group 3 and group 4 occupying sections of the lower River Rhine and its tributaries (Englbrecht et al., 2000), that is putatively assigned by Freyhof et al. (2005) as C. rhenanus (group 3) and C. perifretum (group 4). Subsequent, examination of mtDNA haplotypes by Knapen et al. (2003) also revealed the presence of group 1 genetic sequences (nominal C. gobio) within these populations. Volckaert et al. (2002) expanded on the mtDNA data gathered by Englbrecht et al. (2000), enabling the authors to assign English and Welsh bullhead populations to group 4, which Freyhof et al. (2005) defined as C. perifretum. Recently, cytochrome oxidase 1 (COI) mitochondrial analysis of non‐native bullheads in Scotland confirmed their close link to English populations (McLeish et al., 2020). This latter study referred to bullheads as both C. gobio and C. perifretum but highlighted this latter attribution as being a nominal designation (McLeish et al., 2020). The final two lineage groups observed by Englbrecht et al. (2000) were located within the Seine (group 5) and Adour drainages (group 6), which Freyhof et al. (2005) defined as C. perifretum and C. aturi, respectively.

Although mtDNA has been used to examine phylogenies and create an unparalleled mitochondrial marker database for European bullheads (Englbrecht et al., 2000), this significant genetic resource was rapidly used to examine lineages further. Using the Englbrecht et al. (2000) dataset, Volckaert et al. (2002) combined their mitochondrial control region data to identify a new phylogenetically distinct lineage (group 7) that encompassed bullheads inhabiting the River Scorff (Bretagne, France), though related to bullheads within the Loire basin (assessed by Eppe et al., 1999) and therefore nominally assigned as C. duranii (Freyhof et al., 2005). However, in combining the Englbrecht et al. (2000) control region mtDNA data with their own COI mtDNA data, McLeish et al. (2020) acquired evidence for only three distinct clades: one containing C. gobio (group 1 of Englbrecht et al., 2000), one containing C. duranii (clade 7 of Volckaert et al., 2002) and one containing C. perifretum (group 4 of Englbrecht et al., 2000). Genetic analysis of the mtDNA lumped together the Freyhof et al. (2005) purported species C. hispaniolensis with C. duranii, and C. aturi with C. gobio, with the other nominal species (C. microstomus, C. rhenanus, C. rondeleti and C. scaturigo) grouped within European bullheads but did not form significant associations with other clades (McLeish et al., 2020).

The lack of recombination, which makes mtDNA especially valuable to reconstruct phylogenies, reduces its accuracy for describing patterns of gene flow, with the entire mtDNA genome acting as a single locus (Allendorf, 2017). Additionally, mtDNA's use to capture geographical structuring can result in discordance between nuclear and mitochondrial genomes (Toews & Brelsford, 2012). Discordance is to be expected in these situations because the mitochondrial genome is haploid and uniparentally inherited in most animals, including European bullheads (Hudson & Turelli, 2003; Toews & Brelsford, 2012). The uncertainty of using mtDNA to delimit population lineages led to some journals (e.g., Molecular Ecology) to enact policies requiring mtDNA phylogeographical studies to base their inferences on multiple loci and additional genetic markers (Allendorf, 2017). If mtDNA analysis is unsuitable for determining population associations, then it is arguably unsuitable to support the ESC as employed by Freyhof et al. (2005) to speciate European bullheads, because the ESC relies on the fundamental assumption of accurately ascertaining historical lineages, which mtDNA does not necessarily provide.

Microsatellites

Relative to allozymes and mtDNA analyses, few studies have used microsatellites to parse European bullhead lineages. Similar to mtDNA data, the published microsatellite applications to bullhead populations have refrained from interpreting genetic differences among populations as proof of speciation. Building on previous warnings regarding the use of singular molecular methods for accurately determining genetic differentiation, most studies using microsatellites have also incorporated one or more additional genetic markers within their investigations.

Using mtDNA to assign fish to the Englbrecht et al. (2000) haplotype groups and 10 highly polymorphic microsatellites to assess genetic variability between these groups, Hänfling et al. (2002) collected tissue samples from 44 distinct European bullhead populations across Europe. A high degree of differentiation was observed among populations (FSTmin = 0.04, FSTmax = 0.87), and in all cases pair‐wise values were statistically significant (Hänfling et al., 2002). Within group 1 lineages, neighbour‐joining tree‐based distances of microsatellite data revealed a clear distinction between populations from drainages of the lower and upper Danube, Elbe and Rhine/Main (Hänfling et al., 2002). Freyhof et al. (2005) suggested that these results were consistent with hybridisation events between Danube and Elbe C. gobio, and nominal C. rhenanus from the River Main. Similarly, the Danube populations were observed to display a higher level of genetic differentiation than conspecifics from the Elbe and Main sites (Hänfling et al., 2002). According to Freyhof et al. (2005), C gobio and the nominal species C. haemusi, C. matae, C. scaturigo and C. transsilvaniae are all endemic to the Danube, highlighting the ‘remarkable molecular diversity’ of Danube bullheads. However, unlike previous mtDNA data, the inferences made by Freyhof et al. (2005), based on other authors’ genetic research, purported that northern English and Welsh bullhead populations were more genetically dissimilar than those collected from southern England and the Scheldt (Hänfling et al., 2002). The apparent suggestion that more than one species occurs within Great Britain (Freyhof et al., 2005) merits further investigation.

To examine European bullhead populations at finer spatial scales, Knapen et al. (2003) combined mtDNA from Englbrecht et al. (2000) and Volckaert et al. (2002) with seven polymorphic microsatellite loci to delineate ‘evolutionary significant units’ (sic) of European bullheads in two adjacent Flemish rivers, Meuse and Scheldt. Knapen et al.'s (2003) study perfectly captures the difficulty of using genetic distances to parse species, as the calculated FST, RST and Dθ values of bullhead populations within the Sheldt basin alone could, based on genetic distances and inferences raised by previous authors (Hänfling & Brandl, 1998; Riffel & Schreiber, 1995), be used to delineate eight separate bullhead species within a single basin (Fstmin = 0.268, Fstmax = 0.688) (Knapen et al., 2003).

Local, fine‐scale population structure in European bullhead populations was examined further by Hänfling and Weetman (2006) using seven highly polymorphic microsatellite markers in fish (nominal C. perifretum according to Freyhof et al., 2005) at 24 sites within the River Rye catchment (northeast England). Marked genetic differentiation was observed across most bullhead sample sites (pair‐wise FSTmin = 0.00, FSTmax = 0.67), and genetic differentiation was near universal among sampling locations, with only four of 276 pair‐wise comparisons not significant. The genetic variation within narrow geographical ranges was exemplified by bullheads from two sites; these were significantly genetically differentiated using microsatellite markers (FST = 0.07, p < 0.001) despite their very close proximity (Hänfling & Weetman, 2006). Significant correlations between genetic diversity and locations were also observed (Hänfling & Weetman, 2006), whereby upstream‐dwelling bullheads exhibited significantly lower levels of genetic diversity possibly due to fewer fish immigrating upstream than downstream (Hänfling & Weetman, 2006). A strong relationship between upstream size and allelic richness was also observed when comparing bullheads from distinct river systems across Europe (Hänfling et al., 2002). This suggests that genetic differentiation, which led to the formation of groups 1–6 and contributed to delimitation of European bullheads proposed by Freyhof et al. (2005), may not be solely attributed to long‐diverged genetic lineages but could also be in response to environmental factors, and/or simply a result of purely stochastic non‐directional microevolutionary processes.

Since their development, the microsatellite loci generated by Englbrecht et al. (2000) and used to parse bullhead populations, and thus used in part to infer cottid speciation by Freyhof et al. (2005), have been used in the majority of bullhead population genetics research (Hänfling et al., 2002; Hänfling & Weetman, 2006; Knapen et al., 2003). Multiple analyses, including information continuum model, stepwise mutation model and coalescent models using Markov‐chain Monte‐Carlo approaches, have been used in conjunction with these microsatellites to parse bullhead population data (Hänfling et al., 2002; Hänfling & Weetman, 2006; Knapen et al., 2003). However, the compatibility of these models with Englbrecht et al.'s (2000) microsatellites has not yet been validated. Without simulation testing, microsatellites may lead to the underestimation or overestimation of gene flow among populations (Putman & Carbone, 2014), either way creating ambiguous interpretations of population structures within and among species. This appears to be the case with the nominal Cottus species proposed by Freyhof et al. (2005).

Single nucleotide polymorphisms

Unlike Salmo species (Segherloo et al., 2021), whole‐genome single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) have not yet been used to delineate European bullhead species. Instead, SNPs have been incorporated into investigations that examine the early evolutionary processes of bullhead populations (Cheng et al., 2013; Lucek et al., 2018; Nolte et al., 2005; Stemshorn et al., 2011). For example, the presence or absence of six diagnostic SNPs indicated that man‐made alterations to the rivers Rhine and Scheldt have connected two previously isolated populations, thus fostering hybrids of the nominal species C. rhenanus and C. perifretum (Freyhof et al., 2005; Nolte et al., 2005).

Cheng et al. (2013) generated a genetic map of nominal C. rhenanus × C. perifretum hybrids using 1302 SNPs. Genetic (chromosome) maps illustrate the relative locations of genes and other important features, allowing the identification of gene linkage within chromosomes and thus the probability that these genes will be inherited together (Gienapp, 2020). Mapping, which also identifies variable SNPs within the genome of bullhead populations, is an important initial step in underlining the inherent genetic variability within specific nominal bullhead species (Cheng et al., 2013). Similar genomic maps for other nominal bullhead species proposed by Freyhof et al. (2005), which would provide additional data on pan‐continental genetic variation and greatly benefit European bullhead speciation debates, are currently unavailable.

Lucek et al. (2018) highlighted the power of genome‐wide SNPs for analysing bullhead genomic variation using 8047 and 13,182 SNPs to identify cottid phylogenies of central European bullhead populations inhabiting lakes and streams. DNA sequences from bullheads were aligned against the nominal C. rhenanus assembly (Smolka et al., 2015), and the combined phylogenetic and population genomic analysis suggested the existence of three major lineages: Aare/Rhine, Doubs and Adriatic (Lucek et al., 2018). Within the Aare/Rhine catchment, evidence of complex genetic patterns was observed between lake and stream populations (Lucek et al., 2018). Firstly, network analysis of gene flow revealed that most lake populations of bullheads cluster closely together despite being geographically isolated (e.g., Lake Thun vs. Lake Zurich pair‐wise F ST = 0.135, p < 0.001). Secondly, bullhead populations between hydrologically connected rivers and lakes were more genetically dissimilar than the aforementioned lacustrine interactions. For example, Lake Thun bullheads were significantly differentiated from those of its tributary, the upper River Aare (F ST = 0.288, p < 0.001). Adding to this genetic complexity, Lucek et al. (2018) observed weak, yet significant, evidence of genetic differentiation between littoral and profundal dwelling bullheads within Lake Walen (F ST = 0.004, p = 0.025). Theoretically, biased directional gene flow among bullhead populations, either from head waters to downstream or from streams to lakes without reciprocal exchanges, has created genetically distinct populations at narrower geographical scales (Hänfling & Weetman, 2006; Junker et al., 2012; Lucek et al., 2018). However, it is more likely that these intra‐lacustrine genomic patterns reflect environmentally driven plastic responses to differences, such as thermal stability and hydrodynamic flow rates or other ecological conditions that distinguish streams from lakes (Fusco & Minelli, 2010; Pigliucci, 2005; Velotta & Cheviron, 2018). These results highlight the inherent complexity of European bullhead genetics and the importance of environmental influences on population structures when considering bullhead systematics.

THE WAY FORWARD FOR EUROPEAN BULLHEAD – ONE SPECIES, MULTIPLE SPECIES OR A SPECIES COMPLEX?

Like all species delineation debates, the question regarding European bullhead taxonomy is complex and is unlikely to be resolved by further, one‐by‐one molecular comparisons of contentious species against C. gobio. Decades of morphometric analysis, environmentally variable allozymes, uniparentally inherited mtDNA markers or reduced nuclear DNA markers have resulted in a chaotic nomenclature. Comparatively, European bullheads lack the commercial or evolutionary importance of Salmonidae (Crotti et al., 2020; Jacobs & Elmer, 2021; Murray et al., 2016, Murray et al., 2022) for which evidence on the ‘lumping’ or ‘splitting’ of these aforementioned species has proliferated (e.g., Klemetsen, 2010; Jacobs & Elmer, 2021; Segherloo et al., 2021).

Despite the lack of progress, avenues of research remain to complement existing bullhead taxonomic data. Genetically, ‘going large’ refers to NGS technologies, which provide access to thousands of SNPs to delineate genealogies and comprehensive phylogenomic analyses. This technology has assisted in modernising our understanding of patterns and mechanisms underpinning lineage diversification and species delimitation (e.g., Chan et al., 2020; Guinand et al., 2021; Hughes et al., 2020). Several studies have employed SNPs to answer bullhead phylogenetic questions (Cheng et al., 2013; Lucek et al., 2018; Nolte et al., 2005; Stemshorn et al., 2011), but all have employed either a limited number of markers or sampled at narrow geographical scales, making it difficult to parse the entire bullhead taxonomic classifications on these data alone. To achieve genomic data levels equalling those used to delineate trout (Segherloo et al., 2021) and stickleback species (Guo et al., 2019), bullhead genomic studies must use a large selection of continental SNPs to encompass multiple populations from a variety of intra‐catchment habitats.

The current limited number of reference genomes per nominal bullhead species prevents this, thereby hindering inferences made regarding evolutionary processes such as connectivity (i.e., how contemporary observed connectivity and diversity reflect long‐term selective/adaptive patterns), demographic history and patterns of genetic admixture and speciation (Gagnaire, 2020; Guinand et al., 2021). Only a partially assembled genome is currently available for nominal C. rhenanus (Smolka et al., 2015; GenBank: GCA_0014555555.1). The next step must be to assemble as many genomes as possible of all the nominal bullhead species to capture better the variation present and choose appropriate SNP markers for species delineation purposes (Davey et al., 2011). The rapidly decreasing cost of de novo genome assembly and NGS should no longer constrain bullhead research, and pan‐continental genomic analyses may help clarify European bullhead taxonomy.

Advances in statistical methods, which cope with more comprehensive genetic datasets, will provide additional transparency for complex speciation questions (Blair & Ané, 2020). Based on coalescent theory, multispecies coalescence (MSC) is a statistical framework that models alleles within populations backwards through time until lineages coalesce (see Degnan & Rosenberg, 2009). Using neutral loci, these methods address the timing of divergence, leading to testable theories about species' origins (Blair & Ané, 2020). When additional parameters are added to basic coalescent models (e.g., migration rates between lineages, genetic markers highlighting departures from neutrality), then these are species‐specific ‘MSC models’ (Rannala & Yang, 2017). Initially, MSC modelling explicitly accounted for gene‐tree discordance due to incomplete lineage sorting and was devoted to identify ‘species’ as entities between which genetic exchanges have been negligible (Guinand et al., 2021). However, recent advances have allowed more complex demographic modelling of speciation to take place, such as using the software package BPP (Bayesian Phylogenetics and Phylogeography) (Leaché et al., 2019; Yang & Rannala, 2010), complementing MSC models by implementing empirical criteria for determining species status among allopatric populations.

Modelling of potential speciation events has proven fertile ground for cutting‐edge theoretical research, with guidelines and tools being regularly updated (e.g., Carstens et al., 2013; Kapli et al., 2020; Leaché et al., 2019; Rannala & Yang, 2017), which could benefit bullhead systematics. For example, the speciation‐based delimitation framework of Sukumaran et al. (2021) distinguishes species from population boundaries using full probabilistic models. These analyses incorporate an explicit model of an extended speciation process into the delimitation analysis. Thus, the formation of population lineages and their subsequent development into independent species are decoupled and modelled separately (Sukumaran et al., 2021). For European bullheads for which systematic information is available, this framework would incorporate existing morphological‐ and molecular‐based species data to identify population lineages and subsets, thereby generating more reliable and accurate bullhead delineations for unknown or uncertain species identities (Sukumaran et al., 2021). This approach implicitly captures whichever species concept is used by the investigator or considered by the investigator to be appropriate for a particular system, rather than enforcing any particular predefined speciation concept (Sukumaran et al., 2021). Consequently, this approach would also allow comparisons among species concepts while using the same data, facilitating a more rigorous discussion regarding where species boundaries lay.

Our understanding of European bullhead systematics can advance further, thanks to the proliferation of genomic tools, which provide a comprehensive phylogenetic history of complex taxon, and the advent of statistical packages capable of accurately parsing entire genomes. However, will such advances build a consensus regarding bullhead species? Unfortunately, the answer is probably no and will likely remain this way, whereas there are multiple concepts for defining ‘species’. The theoretical description of what constitutes a species is the very foundation of how we define taxonomic trees, with 34 definitions currently in circulation (Zachos, 2018), making it possible for scientists to ‘lump’ or ‘split’ bullheads into a variety of different clades, taxa, species, subspecies etc., depending on the chosen concept.

The bullhead problem also illustrates a general problem in current taxonomy: mixing incompatible criteria with each other because they are derived from incompatible species concepts. It is methodologically wrong to take a taxon described on the basis of one species concept (such as a classical Linnean concept of species, e.g., C. gobio) and then to confound it by applying another, incompatible species concept (such as the ESC employed by Freyhof et al., 2005). The Linnaean system focuses on observable physical characteristics to group organisms into hierarchical classification based on the degree of similarity but does not account for evolutionary mechanisms of change (Kuntner & Agnarsson, 2006). Alternatively, ESC defines species as lineages over time and accounts for evolutionary change (Wiley, 1978). Thus, the Linnaean and ESC represent fundamentally different approaches to define species, and applying multiple incompatible species concepts on the same organism will always lead to confusing classification systems.

PRAGMATIC APPROACHES TO EUROPEAN BULLHEAD TAXONOMY FOR THE SAKE OF CONSERVATION

Splitting and lumping European bullheads based on morphology and incomplete genetic data could have adverse effects on the conservation of certain populations by limiting potential rescue efforts, as there may be no alternative genetically similar source populations to replace endangered populations (Frankham et al., 2012). This would explicitly be the case for the Lez sculpin (nominally C. petiti), which is restricted to a solitary river system in France (Freyhof et al., 2005). Alternatively, considering many bullhead populations as members of one species has its own risks, as transfer of individuals among highly divergent populations could mix gene pools at different evolutionary scales, potentially resulting in outbreeding depression and loss of genetic identity (Frankham et al., 2012).

If delineation of European bullheads is required to provide accurate conservation measures for each nominal species, as Freyhof et al. (2005) suggest, then perhaps we should stop conflating both issues and acknowledge an alternative framework, as spearheaded by systematic studies of brown trout (Guinand et al., 2021; Segherloo et al., 2021), Arctic charr (Adams & Maitland, 2007; Klemetsen, 2010; Fenton et al., 2026; Reist et al., 2013) and European whitefish (Crotti et al., 2020; Mee et al., 2015). Instead, conservation considerations should perhaps be isolated from taxonomic criteria, that is, pursue management at the population level because European bullhead taxonomy remains uncertain (Galtier, 2019; Mayden, 2002; Zachos, 2018). By rigidly adhering to ‘names’, we may fail to take into account context‐dependant variables (e.g., biological characteristics, ecological/evolutionary potential) inherent in local metapopulations and the impact of pressures that requires conservation action (e.g., Galtier, 2019; Roux et al., 2016).

However, ‘species’ is the only recognised unit used to frame conservation policies and legislation. Indeed, the European Council ‘Habitats Directive’ (European Commission 1992) lists C. gobio for protection but not the other nominal species (https://eunis.eea.europa.eu/index.jsp), leaving them unprotected. Thus, both taxonomy and conservation will unfortunately remain intertwined, putting the species status at the forefront of conservation methods (Galtier, 2019). How can a standard definition for species be obtained when: (a) scientists disagree on the definition, (b) speciation can occur via a spectrum of evolutionary mechanisms and (c) the size and nature of available data vary considerably in time and among taxa?

Recent research has highlighted the relevance of comparable studies among distantly related taxa in relation to speciation (e.g., Luo et al., 2018; Roux et al., 2016). Galtier's (2019) suggestion is to use reference systems to identify taxa for which large amounts of data are available and species boundaries that are widely accepted by the scientific community. By deconstructing the variables that led to the consensual delineation within the reference species, taxa with disputed systematics could be assessed using these thresholds as a basis for a standardised speciation methodology (Galtier, 2019). One strong feature of this approach is its versatility, being adaptable to a range of data types, provided similar levels of data are available within whatever reference species is chosen. Taxonomists would therefore state that ‘Taxon X1 and Taxon X2 were lumped into Taxon X because X1 and X2 are more similar than Reference‐Taxon 1 and Reference‐Taxon 2, according to criterion C’ (Galtier, 2019). This may seem an overly simplistic example, but such a system would offer an objective approach to the problem of species delineation and could help converge bullhead systematics, as well as other complex species delineations, towards a more standardised taxonomy.

When deciding which reference taxa to use, ‘many’ is probably the correct answer (Galtier, 2019; Loire & Galtier, 2021). However, an obvious initial candidate would be salmonids, specifically Salmo salar and its close relatives, for which a very large amount of morphological, ecological, genetic and genomic data is available (Crespi & Fulton, 2004; Klemetsen et al., 2003; Segherloo et al., 2021). Importantly, species boundaries between S. salar and extant lineages are universally accepted, with consensus that all S. salar populations belong to the same species, despite well‐documented morphological, genetic and life‐history trait differences among populations. Crucially, there is consensus that S. trutta and Salmo ferox are distinct species from S. salar within the Salmonidae family. Clearly, how divergence/differentiation is measured is crucial, and the optimal answer would be to incorporate multiple data streams (genetic/genomic, morphological, ontogenetic, phenotypic, ecological and geographical distributions), thereby improving significantly the power of statistical models used to compare species for delineation.

The practical application of this reference system for conservation purposes could be used alongside additional genetic resources, including a European bullhead pangenome, that is, a collection of common and unique reference genomes. Pangenomic data from across the nominal species distribution areas could be used to identify SNPs and calculate genetic distance measures (e.g., FST); such genomic data and thus the ability to calculate genetic distances are also commonly available among both Atlantic salmon and brown trout populations. Therefore, the simplest application of the proposed reference system could involve calculating FST scores among nominal European bullhead species: FST scores less than those calculated between Atlantic salmon and brown trout would be considered the same species for conservation purposes, whereas similar or higher Fst scores could be used to distinguish different species of European bullheads. This type of genetic data could also be aggregated with complementary biological and ecological evidence to build a robust and comprehensive analytical assessment of European bullhead species, supporting advice and management.

In general, until additional data, genetic or otherwise, are available and analyses are performed, all European bullhead taxa except C. gobio Linnaeus, 1758, in particular the nominal species proposed by Freyhof et al. (2005), should be viewed with caution.
