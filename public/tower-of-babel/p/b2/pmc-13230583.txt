Real-world benchmarking and validation of foundation model transformers for endometrial cancer subtyping from histopathology
Authors: Vincent M Wagner, Casey M Cosgrove, Stephanie J Chen, Daniel T Griffin, Megan I Samuelson, Michael J Goodheart, Jesus Gonzalez-Bosquet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe benchmarked histopathology foundation encoders paired with attention-based multiple instance learning (MIL) against convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to assess their robustness for endometrial cancer molecular classification (MMR-deficient, p53 aberrant, POLE pathogenic mutation, and no specific molecular profile) from whole-slide images (WSIs) in a real-world cohort. A public cohort of 815 patients (1195 WSIs) was assembled for model development. Generalizability was evaluated using an external cohort of 720 patients (1357 WSIs). Models were trained using five-fold cross-validation and tested on the external cohort. Performance was summarized using macro-area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), macro-F1 score, and balanced accuracy. In cross-validation, foundation encoder models outperformed CNNs (macro-AUC 0.799–0.860 vs 0.715–0.829). The best configuration (Virchow2 with CLAM MIL) achieved macro-AUC 0.860, macro-F1 score 0.607, and balanced accuracy 0.647. On external validation, CNN performance degraded substantially, whereas foundation models retained higher discrimination. UNI2 with CLAM MIL achieved the highest external macro-AUC 0.780 with a macro-F1 score of 0.416 and balanced accuracy of 0.507. Subtype-level performance was highest for p53abn (AUC 0.851). When evaluated within a benchmarking framework, foundation encoders paired with attention-based MIL demonstrate improved generalization for endometrial cancer molecular subtyping from WSIs compared with CNNs, supporting their potential for subtype inference.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is the most frequently diagnosed gynecologic cancer in the United States and, unlike many other solid tumors, its incidence and mortality are on the rise1,2. This trend represents a growing clinical and societal burden. While histology and tumor stage have traditionally guided treatment decisions3, they offer limited utility in the context of personalized or molecularly targeted therapies4. In 2013, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) introduced a landmark classification system for EC, identifying four prognostically distinct molecular subtypes: polymerase ε (POLE) ultra-mutated, high microsatellite instability hypermutated (MSI-high), copy-number low (CNV-L), and copy-number high (CNV-H)5. These molecular subtypes have since been validated as a biological reference standard across multiple independent cohorts using clinically accessible testing strategies6–8.

To facilitate clinical adoption, surrogate molecular classifiers were developed to approximate the TCGA subtypes using routinely available assays. Combining mismatch repair (MMR) and p53 protein immunohistochemistry (IHC), together with targeted TP53 and POLE sequencing yields four clinically applied categories: MMR-deficient (dMMR), p53 aberrant (p53abn), POLE pathogenic mutation (POLE) and no specific molecular profile (NSMP)7,9. These categories correspond to MSI-high, CNV-H, POLE, and CNV-L tumors, respectively7,9. This clinical-based classification has been incorporated into international treatment guidelines, staging systems and clinical trial designs10–12. However, concordance with TCGA-defined subtypes is imperfect, reflecting known technical limitations of IHC, biologic heterogeneity, and tumors with overlapping molecular features7,9. Furthermore, comprehensive molecular classification has not yet been universally implemented in routine practice, limited by cost, complexity, and turnaround time13,14.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slides, produced for every patient at diagnosis, contain histologic features that reflect these molecular subtypes. While expert pathologists can identify some morphologic correlates, deep learning (DL) and artificial intelligence (AI) methods can uncover subtle, more complex morphologic and spatial signatures that are beyond human perception15. Studies have demonstrated the potential of AI to extract prognostic and genomic information from H&E whole-slide images (WSIs) across a range of cancer types16.

Several groups have explored AI-based molecular classification in EC using a range of deep learning architectures and datasets, demonstrating the feasibility of subtype prediction from H&E WSIs17–22. However, many prior studies are limited by one or more factors that impede generalizability and reproducibility. Limitations include reliance on proprietary or institution-specific datasets, use of custom or non-public model architectures, and evaluation restricted to internal or weakly independent validation cohorts. In addition, existing studies typically focus on a single modeling approach without systematic comparisons within a unified framework. As a result, the relative strengths and limitations of contemporary architectures for EC molecular subtyping, particularly under real-world deployment conditions, remain incompletely characterized.

State-of-the-art histopathology foundation models offer a powerful alternative to fully in-house trained models. These general purpose histology feature extractors are trained on millions of histology tiles using self-supervised learning and can be repurposed for downstream tasks without full retraining23. These encoders require the WSIs to be split into tiles for embedding, which then need to be aggregated for whole slide prediction. To achieve this under weak supervision (i.e. without tile-level labels), multiple-instance-learning (MIL) is used. Among the leading open-source MIL strategies are TransMIL, a transformer-based aggregator using inter-tile self-attention24, and CLAM, which applies class-specific attention pooling and instance-level supervision25. Both approaches have demonstrated high performance in histopathology tasks while offering architectural flexibility and interpretability. The recently introduced STAMP (Solid-Tumour Associative Modelling in Pathology) framework offers an open-source pipeline that combines tiling, feature extraction using foundation models, and MIL-based prediction26. This enables rigorous benchmarking of AI pipelines using community-shared encoders and datasets.

The objective of this study was to systematically benchmark six foundation encoders, paired with two MIL aggregation methods (TransMIL and CLAM) against standard CNNs, using the STAMP pipeline, Fig. 1. We trained and evaluated these models on a public discovery cohort using TCGA and Clinical and Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) and tested generalizability on an independent, real-world cohort of over 700 patients (Table 1). We hypothesized that foundation encoder-based pipelines could deliver accurate molecular classification directly from H&E slides, potentially enabling scalable, point-of-care precision oncology for patients with endometrial cancer.

Results

Internal cross-validation reveals progressive performance gains with foundation encoders and MIL aggregation

During five-fold cross-validation every architecture achieved a macro-AUC above 0.71, confirming discriminative learning, Fig. 2 (light bars), Supplemental Table S1. Among the CNNs, EfficientNet was clearly the weakest with macro-AUC 0.715 (95%CI 0.675–0.754), macro-F1 score 0.404 (95%CI 0.353–0.455), balanced accuracy 0.420 (95%CI 0.372–0.468), while ResNet-18, ResNet-50, and DenseNet clustered at macro-AUC 0.81–0.83 (95%CI width≈0.05) with macro-F1 ≈ 0.58 and balanced accuracy≈0.58. The baseline ImageNet trained transformer model with TransMIL aggregation out-performed EfficientNet (macro-AUC = 0.760, 95%CI 0.706–0.814) yet still trailed the stronger CNNs, particularly in class-balanced metrics (macro-F1 0.465 (95%CI 0.373–0.557), balanced accuracy 0.497 (95%CI 0.426–0.569)).

Model macro-metrics in cross-validation and external validation.

Comparison of (a) macro–area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (Macro-AUC), b macro–F1 score, and c balanced accuracy for CNN, TransMIL, and CLAM pipelines across foundation model feature extractors. Light bars represent mean performance across cross-validation folds and dark bars represent mean performance on external validation; error bars show standard deviation.

Replacing the baseline ViT feature extractor with foundation encoders yielded a consistent improvement in performance. CTransPath, Prov-GigaPath, H-Optimus-0, UNI2, and Virchow2 with TransMIL aggregation all achieved higher macro-AUCs. Values ranged from 0.799 (95%CI 0.778–0.820) for CTransPath to 0.858 (95%CI 0.839–0.876) for Virchow2. Balanced accuracy climbed to 0.624 for Virchow2.

When the aggregation strategy was switched to the CLAM architecture, further improvements were observed for every backbone except baseline ViT. Prov-GigaPath, H-Optimus-0, and UNI2 saw the biggest improvements with CLAM over TransMIL (macro-AUC TransMIL to CLAM: 0.807–0.845, 0.820–0.844, and 0.830–0.858, respectively) with similar improvements in macro-F1 and balanced accuracy. Despite a lower absolute gain with the CLAM vs TransMIL architecture, the highest internal performance on cross-validation was Virchow2 with CLAM, which achieved a macro-AUC of 0.860 (95%CI 0.839–0.880), macro-F1 of 0.607 (95%CI 0.565–0.648,) and a balanced accuracy of 0.647 (95%CI 0.623–0.670).

External validation exposes poor generalization of CNNs and robustness of foundation models

External validation on the independent cohort revealed a marked divergence between model architectures, Fig. 2 (dark bars), Supplementary Table S3. All CNNs experienced pronounced degradation in performance when compared to cross-validation: EfficientNet, DenseNet, and both ResNets fell to macro-AUC of 0.528-0.588, macro-F1 below 0.252, and balanced accuracy less than 0.286.

The baseline ViT performed marginally better than CNNs with macro-AUC 0.626, this improvement was statistically significant in all comparisons except to DenseNet (p = 0.130, Supplementary Table S5). Foundation-model transformers retained substantially higher discrimination with TransMIL implementations producing macro-AUCs from 0.712-0.761, macro-F1s from 0.311 to 0.424, and balanced accuracies from 0.433-0.519. These were statistically superior to all CNNs and to the baseline ViT with large effects sizes (Cohen’s dz 1.31–2.93, Supplementary Table S5).

The CLAM tile aggregation architecture again conferred an incremental benefit for every backbone with the exception of ViT and CTransPath, this difference was statistically significant with large effect size in Prov-GigaPath and UNI2 (Cohen dz 2.55 and 2.52, respectively, Supplementary Table S5). In the external set, Prov-GigaPath with CLAM outperformed Prov-GigaPath with TransMIL by 0.046 macro-AUC points (0.777 vs 0.731) and achieved the highest macro-F1 of any model (0.438, 95%CI 0.402–0.474). UNI2 with CLAM yielded the top macro-AUC overall (0.780, 95%CI 0.750–0.810) with macro-F1 0.416 (95%CI 0.339–0.493) and balanced accuracy 0.507 (95%CI 0.445–0.570). Because POLE tumors were rare in the external validation cohort (n = 13 patients), we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding POLE from macro-AUC calculations. Across all five folds, AUC values were unchanged or modestly higher when POLE was excluded (macro-AUC excluding POLE = 0.794, 95%CI 0.778–0.810), indicating that overall performance estimates are not driven by instability in the POLE subgroup.

Subtype-specific discrimination highlights differential gains across molecular classes

Across all four molecular subtypes, conventional CNN backbones performed reasonably well in cross-validation with subtype-specific AUCs over 90% for p53abn and slightly lower for other subtypes, Fig. 3, Supplementary Table S2. However, in external validation, the CNNs had a dramatic fall off across all subtypes with AUCs seldom surpassing 0.60, Supplementary Table S4. p53abn predictions were the highest with dMMR and POLE having very poor discrimination ( ~ 50%). The baseline ViT models had a similar trend with slightly better performance in external validation.

In contrast, models using foundation encoders showed consistently superior discrimination, Fig. 3 (light bars), Supplemental Table S2. The choice of tile-aggregation strategy further had an effect performance. During cross-validation, UNI2 with CLAM had the highest performance for both p53abn and dMMR subtypes (AUC 0.942 (95%CI 0.933–0.951) and 0.792 (95%CI 0.757–0.827), respectively). In external validation, UNI2 with CLAM had the highest AUC for all subtypes except POLE, Fig. 3 (dark bars), Supplemental Table S4. p53abn had the highest performance with an AUC of 0.851 (95%CI 0.837–0.865). NSMP and dMMR had AUC 0.770 (95%CI 0.746–0.794) and 0.759 (95%CI 0.718–0.800), respectively. Virchow2 with TransMIL had the highest prediction performance by AUC for POLE at 0.798 (95%CI 0.752–0.844). In most other cases, CLAM improved performance across subtypes when using foundation models.

Error structure and subtype confusion in external validation

Examination of the normalized confusion matrix, Fig. 4, for the best-performing model (UNI2 with CLAM) demonstrated structured and non-random misclassification patterns in the external validation cohort. NSMP tumors showed the highest classification fidelity (per-class recall of 0.72), followed by p53abn (0.63) and POLE (0.54), while dMMR showed low recall (0.14). NSMP tumors were most frequently misclassified as dMMR. p53abn was most frequently misclassified as POLE. POLE tumors were confused between p53abn and NSMP. dMMR tumors were frequently confused between NSMP and POLE. These misclassification patterns were consistent across foundation encoder–based pipelines (Supplementary Figs. S2,S3). In contrast, CNN-based models exhibited highly imbalanced confusion patterns, with predictions frequently collapsing into one or two dominant subtypes and minimal correct identification of POLE tumors (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Qualitative histopathologic correlates align model attention with known subtype morphology

Review of attention maps revealed all models focused on tumor containing regions of the WSIs and largely ignored non-tumor regions, Fig. 5. Expert pathologist review of high-scoring tiles revealed distinct morphologic correlates for each subtype.

Whole slide image (WSI), tiles, and attention maps.

Representative WSIs from each molecular subtype (rows) showing: (left to right) original WSI, tile-level subtype predictions, attention heatmaps, and the top tiles most predictive for that final predicted subtype. Tile prediction maps are color-coded by predicted class as seen along the left side; attention maps highlight regions contributing most to the slide-level prediction with darker areas receiving more attention.

p53abn: High nuclear-to-cytoplasmic (N/C) ratio with pleomorphic, hyperchromatic nuclei, coarse chromatin, prominent nucleoli, and frequent mitoses. Growth is papillary to micropapillary with irregular luminal contours and minimal inflammation.

NSMP: Low N/C ratio with bland, round nuclei and tubular glands, often with morular or tubal metaplasia. Stroma separates glands, luminal borders are smooth, and inflammation is variable but limited.

dMMR: Fused or cribriform glands with lumens, moderate N/C ratio, smooth luminal borders, and mild intra- and peritumoral lymphocytic inflammation without stromal desmoplasia.

POLE: Abundant peri- and intratumoral lymphoplasmacytic inflammation fragmenting tumor nests. Tumor shows low N/C ratio, smooth luminal borders when present, basal to pseudostratified nuclei, and prominent nucleoli but minimal atypia.

Overall, the models emphasized morphologic features consistent with expected subtype phenotypes: papillary/pleomorphic for p53abn, bland glandular for NSMP, intermediate glandular with mild inflammation for dMMR, and inflammation-dominant for POLE.

Quantitative cell-type composition differs systematically by molecular subtype

Based on quantitative evaluation using HoVerNet (Fig. 6, Supplementary Table S6), p53abn high prediction tiles are overwhelmingly tumor‑cell–dominant: neoplastic 82% (highly statistically significant compared to all other subtypes, Supplementary Tables S7, S8), with very little inflammatory ( < 2%), low stroma ( < 10%), and minimal necrosis ( < 2%). NSMP and POLE are markedly more stromal, each with >30% connective tissue and a lower tumor fraction (NSMP 60%, POLE 38%). dMMR sits between these extremes (neoplastic 73%, inflammatory 4.4%, connective 18%, dead <2%). dMMR tiles have a longer tail towards more inflammatory cells. POLE shows the most immune cells (16% inflammatory, highly statistically significant, Supplementary Tables S7, S8) and the greatest necrosis (7.4% dead). These quantitative findings are consistent with and strengthen the expert pathology review and established descriptions27,28.

Distinct nuclear morphometric signatures underpin subtype-specific predictions

Significant differences were identified in all nuclear morphometric metrics across the four molecular subtypes (Supplementary Table S9, Supplementary Fig. S1A–S1E). Post-hoc Dunn’s tests with Holm correction revealed that mean nuclear area showed the most pronounced separation, with p53abn nuclei significantly larger than all other subtypes (Supplementary Table S10). Circularity irregularity also demonstrated broad separation, with p53abn differing significantly from NSMP, dMMR, and POLE. Size dispersion and pleomorphism index primarily distinguished POLE from other subtypes. These findings suggest that both nuclear size and shape irregularity contribute to the morphological distinctiveness of certain subtypes, particularly p53abn. These quantitative findings align with our expert pathology review and established histopathologic descriptions of endometrial cancer molecular subtypes27,28.

Discussion

In this study of 1535 patients and 2552 diagnostic H&E WSIs, we demonstrate that transformer-based pipelines built on open histopathology foundation encoders and trained on open-sourced data with attention-based MIL achieve state-of-the-art discrimination of molecular subtypes of endometrial cancer. The best configuration (UNI2 with CLAM) reached a macro-AUC of 0.86 in five-fold cross-validation and, critically, retained 0.78 macro-AUC on an independent patient cohort of real-world patients. Conventional ImageNet-pre-trained CNNs and an ImageNet-pre-trained ViT model suffered marked performance collapse on the same independent set. The divergence between CNNs and foundation encoder models underscore the importance of domain-specific pre-training that define the foundation models. Due to this pre-training, foundation encoders capture histologic patterns that are missed by ImageNet-pretrained models. The foundation encoders also allow more data efficiency when training so the MIL head can converge with hundreds rather than tens of thousands (or millions) of labeled slides.

Furthermore, although all pipelines shared identical frozen foundation encoders, swapping TransMIL for the CLAM aggregator raised macro-AUC by 0.03-0.05 and boosted balanced accuracy by up to six percentage points on the independent cohort. We attribute this to CLAM-MB’s class-specific, instance-level attention. This mechanism explicitly compels the network to identify a small subset of diagnostically relevant tiles which is an advantage with four distinct subtypes. This mechanism is also advantageous when endometrial-cancer slides contain extensive benign tissue. In contrast, TransMIL’s global self-attention, while having high representational capacity, appeared more prone to over-fitting. These findings highlight the importance of the aggregation architecture when translating AI models from training to real-world deployment.

p53abn consistently had the highest suptype-specific AUC across configurations, this is most likely due to its overtly high-grade, tumor-dense morphology as noted in both qualitative and quantitative interpretation. It maintained high recall in external validation. NSMP also had consistently high performance across configurations and as the most common subtype, had the highest overall recall and accuracy. Although subtype-specific AUC for dMMR was reasonably high in the best-performing models, it exhibited the lowest recall across models. This apparent discrepancy highlights the distinction between discrimination metrics and classification performance. AUC reflects the model’s ability to rank cases across decision thresholds, whereas recall reflects classification accuracy at the final subtype classification. In this setting, many dMMR tumors received intermediate prediction probabilities but were ultimately classified incorrectly as NSMP or POLE, resulting in preserved discrimination but low recall. Notably, similar confusion patterns were observed across multiple foundation encoder-based pipelines (Supplementary Figs. S2–S3), suggesting that this behavior reflects an intrinsic challenge of dMMR morphology rather than a model-specific failure. Histologically, dMMR tumors often display glandular architecture similar to NSMP tumors while simultaneously exhibiting inflammatory features reminiscent of POLE tumors. This intermediate phenotype was reflected in our quantitative analyses of cellular composition and nuclear morphometrics.

POLE maintained reasonably high AUC with moderate recall. POLE was most often incorrectly classified as p53abn or NSMP, consistent with its combination of pronounced heterogeneity on tile classification and wide variation of cellular content and nuclear morphometrics. The POLE subtype was underrepresented in the external validation cohort, reflecting its low real-world prevalence. As a result, subtype-specific performance estimates for POLE should be interpreted cautiously. Importantly, sensitivity analyses excluding POLE demonstrated stable overall performance, confirming that the study’s primary conclusions are not dependent on this subgroup. POLE tumors are also associated with exceptionally favorable prognosis and are increasingly considered for treatment de-escalation rather than aggressive therapy, which further contextualizes the clinical implications of misclassification in this group.

Several groups have previously published AI models for molecular classification in EC, Supplemental Table 11. Panoptes, a multi-resolution CNN based model, predicted driver mutations but lacked full subtyping validation and required multiple models to achieve accurate predictions17. im4MEC, developed by the PORTEC consortium, represents the most comprehensive prior effort toward full molecular subtyping, leveraging a custom transformer-based model trained on well-annotated trial and registry cohorts. While im4MEC demonstrated strong performance across subtypes, both training and testing were conducted within tightly controlled clinical trial settings using harmonized protocols and scanner environments, limiting assessment of real-world domain variability18. Other models, such as TR-MAMIL and MMRNet, focus on specific molecular features such as MMR status, MSI, p53, or tumor mutational burden rather than full subtype classification, often relying on internal splits or limited external testing19,20,22. hi-UNI, developed by Cui et al, introduced a hierarchical ViT backbone that leverages the foundation encoder UNI for subtype prediction; however, evaluation was restricted to cross-validation within a single-institution cohort, without independent external validation21.

A distinctive advantage of our work is that every component of model development, from training WSIs and matched molecular labels to training pipeline and pre-trained encoders, is openly accessible. The discovery cohort was assembled exclusively from TCGA and CPTAC projects. All pre-processing and training code is released under an MIT license, and the foundation encoders are available for use (some with permission from their developers). Open data and pipelines provide concrete benefits for this type of prediction model development. First, they enable external investigators to benchmark new models, encoders and aggregation schemes or fine-tune for rarer entities without starting from scratch. Second, regulators now expect traceability for AI-based diagnostics; open source shortens the path to FDA clearance and adoption. Finally, transparent resources foster multicenter collaboration which is especially important for rare subtypes. Because we used the publicly available data for training, we were able to add a more stringent external validation, rather than validating on TCGA, the performance of our model was measured on a real-world 720 patient cohort providing a realistic gauge of deployment readiness.

Additional strengths include our comprehensive interpretability analysis that combines qualitative review with quantitative metrics. Qualitative assessment provides face validity for pathologists by highlighting histologic features consistent with known molecular phenotypes, while quantitative analyses offer reproducible evidence aligned with established biology. Together, this dual approach supports both clinical trust and objective benchmarking while reducing reliance on subjective visual interpretation alone. Model evaluation followed a three-tiered framework (development, cross-validation, external validation) consistent with TRIPOD guidelines, using rigorous statistics to minimize bias29. Nonetheless, slide-level splitting during cross-validation may introduce residual optimism due to shared patient-level characteristics, underscoring the importance of the independent external cohort for unbiased performance assessment.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, all cohorts were retrospective and variability in staining protocols and scanner hardware represents a potential source of domain shift, only partially mitigated by the external cohort. These factors may impact performance in unseen deployment environments. Stain normalization was intentionally omitted in the present study because benchmarking work on histopathology feature extractors has suggested that aggressive color normalization can disrupt stain-morphology relationships learned during large-scale self-supervised pretraining, potentially degrading downstream performance30. Nevertheless, staining variability remains an important practical challenge for real-world deployment. Prior work in computational pathology has shown that moderate stain normalization or stain-augmentation strategies may improve cross-site robustness in some settings. Future studies could therefore systematically evaluate how stain normalization interacts with foundation encoders and whether selective normalization or augmentation strategies improve stability across heterogeneous clinical environments.

Second, molecular subtype imbalance, particularly for rare subtypes such as POLE, reflects real-world prevalence but may influence per-class performance estimates. Subtype distributions differed across cohorts, and difficult to classify subtypes, such as dMMR, could also benefit from strategies that may improve classification, including class-balanced training approaches, targeted sampling of regions-of-interest, or integration of complementary modalities such as immunohistochemistry. Until such improvements are demonstrated, subtype-specific predictions, particularly for dMMR and POLE, should be interpreted cautiously in clinical contexts, and AI-based models may be better positioned as triage tools to guide reflex molecular testing rather than as standalone diagnostic classifiers.

Additionally, preprocessing was intentionally standardized across encoders to enable fair comparison rather than optimize to each model’s performance (e.g., model card specifies 0.5 µm/pixel for H-optimus-0). Accordingly, results should be interpreted as comparative performance under a common pipeline rather than each model’s maximum achievable performance. Differences in scanning magnification and effective spatial resolution may influence the types of morphologic features captured by the models. Higher-resolution inputs may improve representation of nuclear-level features, whereas lower effective magnifications may emphasize broader tissue architectural patterns. Increasing resolution also substantially increases the number of extracted tiles and computational burden without necessarily providing proportional performance gains once diagnostically relevant structures are adequately resolved. Future work could explore encoder-specific resolution optimization or multi-scale strategies that integrate both architectural and nuclear morphologic information.

Finally, attention maps should not be interpreted as causal explanations; however, pairing them with quantitative analyses reduces over-interpretation and strengthens biological validity. While our external cohort supports generalizability, prospective, multi-center validation will be required prior to clinical deployment.

With the increasing adoption of molecular classification in the clinical management of EC, limitations of current methods highlight the need for scalable, high-throughput alternatives. Our findings demonstrate that transformer-based AI models applied to routine H&E slides can accurately predict molecular subtypes of endometrial cancer, providing further evidence that an AI-driven platform can serve as a surrogate for molecular classification. Such an approach has the potential to function as a clinical decision-support tool, either by prompting reflex confirmatory testing or by serving as a practical alternative in low-resource settings. Ultimately, this strategy could broaden access to molecularly informed care and enable more equitable implementation of precision oncology for EC worldwide.

Methods

Pipeline overview (Fig. 1)

Using the STAMP framework, WSIs are tessellated into tiles after standardized preprocessing and tile-level quality control. Depending on the modeling strategy, tiles are either passed through an end-to-end CNN classifier or embedded using a frozen feature extractor, including conventional vision transformers or large histopathology foundation models. Tile-level outputs or embeddings are then aggregated into a slide-level representation using simple mean pooling (CNN pipeline) or attention-based MIL approaches, including TransMIL or CLAM. Finally, the aggregated slide-level representation is passed to a classifier that produces probabilistic predictions for the four molecular subtypes (p53abn, NSMP, dMMR, and POLE). This modular design enables systematic benchmarking of architectures, encoders, and aggregation strategies within a unified and reproducible framework.

Public discovery cohort: TCGA and CPTAC

WSIs and matched molecular data were obtained from TCGA and CPTAC; TCGA-UCEC (n = 523), TCGA-UCS (n = 52), and CPTAC-UCEC (n = 240) projects. All images and data from the TCGA and CPTAC are publicly available at https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov and https://www.cancerimagingarchive.net. After compiling, 815 unique patients were identified. These individuals contributed 1195 diagnostic H&E WSIs (median 1 slide per patient, IQR 1–2), Table 1. Molecular ground-truth labels were derived from molecular annotations on each platform and mapped to their corresponding clinical surrogate categories (p53abn, NSMP, dMMR, POLE) to ensure consistency with the external validation cohort.

Independent external cohort for deployment-style validation

An independent cohort was assembled under IRB approval and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The cohort included 720 patients with endometrial cancer between 2014 and 201731, yielding 1357 WSIs with confirmed molecular labels, Table 1. Molecular subtype was determined using IHC for MMR and NGS for TP53 and POLE31 (only known pathogenic mutations were labeled positive32). No slides or patients from this cohort were used during model development; they were reserved exclusively for held-out deployment testing to evaluate generalization performance of the models from cross-validation.

Whole-slide image preprocessing and tile extraction

All WSIs were processed with a modified HIA33 and STAMP pipeline26. Slides were not manually annotated or segmented for tumor or region of interest. Stain normalization was intentionally omitted, as recent work has shown that color normalization may impair generalization of foundation models by altering biologically meaningful stain–morphology correlations learned during large-scale pretraining30.

To ensure consistent morphologic context across whole-slide images with variable native magnification, WSIs were resampled to a target microns-per-pixel (MPP) such that each extracted tile corresponded to a fixed physical field-of-view (MPP=tile µm/pixel). Specifically, tiles corresponded to 256 × 256 µm of tissue and were extracted as 224 × 224 pixel images after rescaling each WSI to a target MPP of approximately 1.14 µm/pixel per STAMP protocol26,33. This ensured consistent tissue area per tile across WSIs. We did not apply encoder-specific resolution tuning because the study objective was to benchmark encoders under a unified preprocessing protocol.

All quality control was performed at the tile level. Extracted tiles underwent two predefined quality-control steps. First, a brightness-based background filter excluded tiles with mean grayscale intensity ≥240 on an 8-bit scale (0–255), removing whitespace-dominated regions. Second, a texture-based filter using Canny edge detection (thresholds 40–100) excluded tiles with low edge density ( < 2%), effectively removing low-texture or out-of-focus regions. Across slides, a median of 44% of tiles were filtered (IQR 37–52%). These procedures were designed to remove background and artifact tiles while preserving representative tissue content and did not result in exclusion of any WSI.

Baseline CNN encoders for tile-level feature learning

We evaluated four CNN architectures with ImageNet34-pretrained weights (EfficientNet-B735, ResNet-1836, ResNet-5036, and DenseNet37) for tile-level classification. Architectures were selected to span a range of depth, connectivity, and computational complexity. CNNs were implemented within the HIA framework with pre-specified configurations2, a fixed proportion of network layers was frozen (freeze ratio = 0.5) with the remaining layers fine-tuned to balance feature reuse with task-specific adaption33. Models were optimized using Adam with a learning rate of 2 × 10⁻⁴, batch size of 256, and early stopping based on validation loss (minimum 5 epochs, maximum 20 epochs, patience = 3), using five-fold cross-validation with slide-level splitting. Slide-level predictions were generated by mean-pooling tile outputs. Top-scoring tiles and slides were retained for interpretability analysis. Complete architecture specifications, hyperparameters, and training configurations are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Foundation model encoders with multiple instance learning aggregation

For the transformer pipeline, tiles were embedded within the STAMP framework using frozen (weights kept constant) encoders/feature extractors (ViT-B/1638, CTransPath39, Prov-GigaPath40, H-Optimus-041, UNI-242, Virchow243) which were downloaded through https://huggingface.co/. Model-specific normalization transforms provided by the corresponding model cards were applied. Embeddings were aggregated into slide-level predictions using either a transformer-based MIL aggregator (TransMIL) or an attention-based pooling model with instance-level supervision (CLAM-MB). Models were optimized using AdamW with a learning rate of 1×10⁻⁴, fixed bag size of 512 embeddings, batch size of 8 bags, and early stopping based on validation loss (minimum 5 epochs, maximum 20 epochs, patience = 5), using five-fold cross-validation with slide-level splitting. Full model configurations and hyperparameters in Supplementary Material 2. All training was performed on a cluster of 4 NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs.

Primary performance metrics and statistical comparison of models

Model performance was evaluated separately for internal cross-validation and independent external validation. Within the public discovery cohort, five-fold cross-validation was performed with slide-level splitting. For each iteration, models were trained on four folds with a validation split for early stopping and evaluated on the held-out fold, yielding five held-out performance estimates per configuration. For external validation, each of the five models trained during cross-validation was applied without retraining to the independent external cohort. Performance metrics reported for external validation therefore represent the distribution of results obtained by applying these five independently trained models to the same held-out real-world dataset.

The primary outcome metric was the unweighted mean of the per-subtype AUC (macro-AUC), ensuring equal contribution from each molecular subtype regardless of prevalence. Secondary metrics included macro-F1 score (the average F1 score across subtypes, balancing precision and recall) and balanced accuracy (the mean recall across subtypes), both of which account for class imbalance by ensuring minority subtypes are weighted equally. In addition, per-subtype AUCs were reported to assess subtype-specific discrimination performance. As a sensitivity analysis, we calculated macro-AUC excluding the POLE subtype to assess the influence of its low prevalence on overall performance estimates.

Performance metrics are reported as means with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) calculated across the five models. To assess whether observed differences in macro-AUC were statistically significant, we performed two-sided paired t-tests on the fold-level metric vectors. Normality of fold-wise deltas was verified with the Shapiro–Wilk test (P > 0.10 for all comparisons). Resulting p values were adjusted for multiple testing with Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate (FDR) (α = 0.05). Effect sizes are presented as ΔAUC with 95%CI. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s dz (mean fold-wise ΔAUC divided by the standard deviation of ΔAUC).

Histopathologic interpretability and quantitative morphometric validation

To ensure biological plausibility and clinical relevance, we included interpretability analyses including a qualitative review of attention maps/tiles and a quantitative evaluation of cell composition and nuclear morphometrics. The qualitative review verifies models focus on expected histology. The quantitative analyses provide objective, reproducible measures. We selected the 10 highest predicted WSI from each subtype and then identified the 8 highest scoring tiles from each WSI using attention heatmaps for the correctly-predicted subtype, yielding 80 tiles per subtype for interpretability analysis. Tiles were analyzed by trained pathologists. Nuclei were then segmented with TIAToolbox44 using the HoVer‑Net45 with PanNuke46 schema and nuclear morphometric features were extracted from neoplastic epithelial cells to quantify size variability, pleomorphism, eccentricity and circularity. Differences among subtypes were evaluated with the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc comparisons with Holm correction. Full feature definitions, computational settings, and visualization methods in Supplementary Material 2.
