Family caregivers' needs at 6 months after pancreatic cancer surgery: A latent profile analysis
Authors: Linglong Liu, Xiaoping Fang, Xinbo Wang, Xiaoyan Wang, Yehua Xie, Yaoyao Cai, Lei Cui, Tianying Yao, Xiaoxuan Li, Qian Li, Mingxia Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFamily caregivers ('carers') bear the highest care burden during the postoperative survivorship period of pancreatic cancer, given its poor prognosis. Most carers report unmet needs when taking on caregiving responsibilities during this period. Thoroughly investigating carers' needs is essential for helping families address practical care challenges. However, this important topic remains underexplored.ObjectiveTo assess the need levels and identify need subgroups among carers of patients with pancreatic cancer 6 months after surgery and demographic predictors contributing to heterogeneity.DesignCross-sectional study.Setting(s)Participants were recruited from the pancreas centres of four tertiary A-level comprehensive hospitals in Jiangsu Province, China.Participants240 patients with pancreatic cancer and their carers ('dyads') participated in the survey.MethodsCarers completed the Comprehensive Needs Assessment Tool in Cancer for Carers, the Activities of Daily Living Scale for patients, and the General Demographic Information Questionnaire for dyads. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was used to categorise carers' needs. Non-parametric and chi-square tests were used to examine differences in need scores and sociodemographic characteristics among subgroups. Multiple logistic regression (MLR) was used to analyse sociodemographic impacts.ResultsSix months post-surgery, the total carers' need score was 41.83 ± 22.65 points, indicating a moderate level, with the highest needs reported for healthcare personnel, information and knowledge, and facilities and services. The LPA results revealed that carers were divided into five distinct subgroups based on differing levels of need across the domains assessed by the Comprehensive Needs Assessment Tool in Cancer for Carers, with proportions of 8.8 %, 22.5 %, 8.3 %, 55 %, and 5.4 %. Subgroup membership was predicted by four factors: carers' sex (odds ratio [OR]: 11.08, 95 % confidence interval [CI]: 1.64, 74.99, p = 0.01), carers' education levels (OR: 3.92, 95 % CI: 1.04, 14.72, p = 0.04), patients' treatment after surgery (OR: 0.10, 95 % CI: 0.01, 0.84, p = 0.04) and activities of daily living levels (OR: 14.63, 95 % CI: 1.97, 108.77, p < 0.01).ConclusionsWe have highlighted the complex individualised needs of carers of patients with pancreatic cancer. Through LPA and MLR, we identified distinct need subgroups and their predictors. Healthcare professionals may be able to improve dyads’ health by tailoring support to each subgroup’s specific needs and issues.RegistrationRegistration number: ChiCTR2400079415, registered 03/01/2024, first recruitment 04/02/2024.

Background

Pancreatic cancer is a major cancer type in China, reported with 118,672 new cases and 106,695 deaths in 2022, accounting for half of the total cases in Asia (Bray et al., 2024). There is an exceedingly poor prognosis for pancreatic cancer, with a 5-year survival rate of only approximately 7 % in China (Zhao et al., 2019a). The sole opportunity for patients with pancreatic cancer to extend their lives remains early radical surgical treatment. The survival rate of patients who have undergone early radical resection of pancreatic cancer has increased from 6 months to 26.5 months (Chinese Pancreatic Surgery Association, 2021). Consequently, as survivorship is extended, healthcare professionals must address the new challenges faced by families during this period (Cai et al., 2021).

Oncology healthcare professionals are notably scarce in China due to its large population and uneven regional distribution of medical resources (Wang et al., 2023). Outside the hospital, professional care rarely covers the full scope of survivorship, leaving patients to rely heavily on family carers for continuous cancer treatment and support (Bauer et al., 2018). In this context, family carers ('carers') refer to those who often provide substantial care to people affected by illness, have a kinship or marital relationship with them, and care for them for the longest period (Chong et al., 2022). Moreover, patients in China generally favour receiving cancer care from family carers instead of outsider carers. This predilection is intricately connected to the Chinese cultural prioritisation of familial bonds and patients' emotional reliance on their family carers (Ng et al., 2016). Carers thus play a crucial role in meeting the patient's physical and emotional needs and managing treatment side effects (Zhang et al., 2023); however, they are also vulnerable to significant physical and emotional strain. The high expectations placed on carers frequently surpass their ability to fulfil them, which means that the unmet needs corresponding to caregiver responsibilities and work are overwhelming (Wang et al., 2018). Carers' needs are defined as the resources required for addressing the carers’ health status and care-related problems (Fitch, 2008). To understand these complexities, researchers conducting a systematic review have highlighted that many family carers, especially of patients with advanced pancreatic cancer, face unmet needs related to information, financial support, and psychological wellbeing, often prioritising the patient's needs over their own (Liu et al., 2020). However, such evidence is primarily qualitative, and reliable quantitative data on carers' needs for pancreatic cancer remain limited.

Radical resection combined with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy is the recommended strategy for prolonging survival in pancreatic cancer (Chinese Pancreatic Surgery Association, 2021). Most patients commence regular chemotherapy about 1 month after surgery, provided there are no significant blood glucose abnormalities or major postoperative complications, including gastroparesis or diarrhea (Abe et al., 2025). Both postoperative recovery and chemotherapy require specific home care, leading to a range of new unmet carers' needs beyond the scope of professional healthcare services, particularly at 6 months after surgery. The 6-month postoperative mark represents a critical turning point for pancreatic cancer families as this period is associated with a high risk of recurrence and metastasis (Tomimaru et al., 2023). This risk is due to pancreatic cancer’s immune escape mechanisms and the potential for incomplete tumour removal, especially in those who do not achieve an R0 resection, defined as complete tumour removal with negative margins (Chinese Pancreatic Surgery Association, 2021). Thus, 6 months post-surgery is not only a key milestone for assessing treatment efficacy but also a peak period for caregiver burden and psychological distress (Chong et al., 2022; Elberg Dengso et al., 2023). Historically, limited treatment technologies meant that few patients survived beyond 6 months, and studies on carers' needs during this survivorship period have been scarce (He et al., 2022). With increasing survivorship in China, there is an urgent need to address carers' unmet needs at this pivotal time.

Another unexplored issue is the individual characteristics of need levels when formulating tailored family support plans. The mean needs scores reflect only the sample group's general level and may conceal meaningful variations across individuals, thereby offering limited value for informing targeted clinical support strategies. In clinical practice, carers often report widely-varying levels of unmet needs (Huynh et al., 2023; Grivel et al., 2023), indicating the necessity of recognising underlying heterogeneity. Person-centred management and support strategies may be more effective when these differences are taken into account. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA), a mixed-model-based group discovery method, identifies different profiles—latent subgroups with similar patterns—and examines their heterogeneity across multiple variables (Von and Bergman, 2003). Unlike traditional clustering, LPA uses probabilistic classification to estimate the likelihood of individual membership in each latent class without imposing rigid assumptions (Berlin et al., 2014). This approach effectively reveals hidden group structures in the data and emphasises the value of subgroup identification in clinical practice. A further strategy for advancing person-centred care is to explore the sociodemographic correlations of latent profile groupings of carers' needs. In addition to carer-related sociodemographic factors already shown to be relevant, such as sex, age, education level, type of health insurance, relationship to the patient, and duration of caregiving (Zhao et al., 2019b; Zhao et al., 2023), patient-related characteristics, including sociodemographic information, clinical status, and postoperative self-care ability, may also be important contributors to individual differences in carers' needs (Chong et al., 2022; Mollica et al., 2020).

Accordingly, we aimed to assess need levels, identify latent subgroups of carers' needs for patients with pancreatic cancer using LPA, and examine their sociodemographic correlations. These findings may serve as an evidence base for developing future support strategies tailored to carers’ specific needs during the pancreatic cancer survivorship period.

Methods

Study design

This study was a cross-sectional analysis based on a prospective investigation of the needs of family carers of patients following pancreatic cancer surgery. The study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, with registration number ChiCTR2400079415. This study was reported following the STROBE statement checklist for cross-sectional studies (Battegay, 2008). This checklist is attached as Supplementary Material Table S1.

Setting

This study was conducted in four pancreatic cancer centres of four authoritative tertiary A-level comprehensive hospitals in Nanjing, Jiangsu Province, China.

Participants

According to the rule of thumb, a minimum of 200 cases is required for latent variable models (Kim, 2014). This study was a sub-series of a prospective follow-up survey, with 300 participants initially included at baseline and 240 participants completing all surveys (at 6 months post-surgery), thus meeting the minimum sample size requirement. Participants were adult primary carers and their relatives with newly diagnosed pancreatic cancer who were about to be discharged after initial surgery in China. Individuals with pancreatic cancer were also recruited as participants to enable data to be collected about their carers' needs. Data were collected from February to November 2024 using convenience sampling. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown below.

Inclusion criteria

We included the primary carers of patients undergoing pancreatic cancer resection for the first time, aged ≥18 years, with a kinship or marital relationship with the patient, and possessing the ability to read and communicate independently.

We included patients diagnosed with pancreatic cancer who had undergone resection for the first time and had a life expectancy of >6 months, were ≥ 18 years, and could read and communicate independently to complete the questionnaire. Expected survival time was based on the treating physician's clinical judgement, incorporating factors such as cancer stage, operative findings, postoperative recovery, and overall self-care functional status.

Exclusion criteria

Carers responsible for individuals with pancreatic cancer that has already metastasised at the time of recruitment, those paid for their caregiving work, or those with severe health conditions, known as serious physical or mental illnesses that would substantially interfere with a carer participant's ability to complete study procedures, potentially leading to inaccurate survey results or increasing the psychological burden on participants, were excluded. Examples included, but were not limited to, advanced heart failure, end-stage renal disease, severe respiratory diseases, active cancer under treatment, and severe psychiatric disorders (such as schizophrenia or bipolar disorder).

Patients with metastatic pancreatic cancer at the time of recruitment or those with severe health conditions that might hinder survey participation and affect data accuracy were excluded. The definition of severe health conditions was consistent with the exclusion criteria for carer participants and was determined based on clinical diagnosis and a review of medical records.

Data collection tools

Self-designed Demographic Questionnaires for Dyads

For patients with pancreatic cancer after surgery, the survey included questions about age, sex, highest education level, marital status, religious belief, and types of health insurance. Data were collected from patients' medical records, including clinical diagnoses, surgical types, the number of chronic diseases, smoking history, and post-surgical treatment.

For carers, questions in the survey included age, sex, highest education level, marital status, care setting, work status, religious belief, relationship with patients, hours of daily care provided to patients, past caregiving experience, and health insurance.

The Chinese Version of the Activities of Daily Living Scale for Patients

Eating, dressing and undressing, grooming, walking, bathing, toileting, shopping, ability to use the telephone, food preparation, housekeeping, laundry, responsibility for one's medications, mode of transportation, and ability to handle finances are items of basic activities of daily living. The instrument utilised in this study was created by Lawton and Brody (1969) in the United States. The assessment comprises the Physical Self-Maintenance Scale and the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale (Lawton and Brody, 1969). The collaborative research group indicated that the scale was comparable to a revised version for the Chinese population. The Chinese version has demonstrated good psychometric properties. Inter-rater and test–retest reliability, assessed via intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), were 0.99 and 0.90, respectively. The internal consistency of the scale, measured by Cronbach's alpha, was 0.86 (Tong and Man, 2002). The scale utilises a 4-point Likert grading system, ranging from total self-sufficiency to complete incapacity, with scores from 1 to 4. The overall score ranges from 14 to 56 points, with 14 points indicating normal functioning, 15 to 21 points reflecting modest impairment, and scores of 22 or above indicating significant impairment (Lawton and Brody, 1969).

The Chinese Version of the Comprehensive Needs Assessment Tool in Cancer for Carers

The scale was developed by Shin, a Korean scholar, in 2011 (Shin et al., 2011). It measures the needs of carers of patients with cancer in different settings, including health and psychological, family and social support, healthcare personnel, knowledge and information, spiritual support, facilities and services, and practical support needs. In 2014, Zhao Xinshuang and colleagues translated and adapted the original questionnaire into Chinese. The findings indicated the scale's reliability and validity, with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.94 (Zhao et al., 2019b). This instrument comprises 41 items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 123 points. Raw scores represent the total need scores. Total standardised scores are calculated as the sum of scores from the 41 items multiplied by 100, divided by 41, and multiplied by 3 to facilitate comparison across different need domains. Each item is scored from 0 to 3, with 0 indicating no need, and 1 to 3 representing low, moderate, and high need, respectively (Zhao et al., 2019b).

Data collection

This study represents a cross-sectional analysis conducted within a longitudinal follow-up study. Of the 310 eligible dyads invited, 10 dyads declined participation, 300 were enrolled at baseline, and 240 completed the final follow-up at 6 months post-surgery and were included in the final analysis. Participant flow is detailed in Fig. 1. Pancreatic cancer dyads received paper copies of self-designed demographic questionnaires to complete independently on the discharge date after initial curative surgery. Patients' disease-related information was also collected by investigators on the discharge date from electronic medical records with consent. Postoperative treatment, patients' activities of daily living and carers' needs were assessed at 6 months after surgery via on-site surveys (n = 202) or telephone interviews (n = 38). All participants provided written informed consent. For carers unable to attend in person, trained investigators collected data by telephone. The data collection process followed ethical guidelines, with participant anonymity maintained via coding procedures. Two researchers reviewed the paper questionnaires to identify and resolve missing or inconsistent responses. All data were double-entered and cleaned before analysis. Missing values were not imputed because the primary outcome—need—was self-reported by carers, making it a subjective variable, rather than a randomly distributed one. To avoid introducing additional bias, cases involving voluntary withdrawal or those meeting exclusion criteria during follow-up surveys were automatically excluded from the analysis sample.Fig. 1Flowchart of the Study.Fig. 1

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics summarised participants' demographics, clinical features, and scale scores. The normality of continuous variables was assessed to determine appropriate statistical tests. The LPA was used to identify subgroups of carers based on their reported needs (Muthén and Muthén, 2010). Model selection was guided by fit indices, including Log-Likelihood (LL), Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), modified Bayesian information criterion (aBIC), Lo-Mendell-Rubin test (LMRT), bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), entropy and clinical interpretability (Tofighi and Enders, 2008). The proportion of each subgroup should be at least 5 % (Asparouhov and Muthén, 2009). Additional details on model fit criteria are provided in Supplementary Materials. Between-group differences were tested using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), non-parametric tests, and chi-square tests as appropriate. Variables with p < 0.25 were included in multiple logistic regression (MLR) to examine predictors of subgroup membership (Hosmer, 2000). Odds ratios (ORs) and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, using Class 5 as the reference group. If the number of cases in each subgroup used for MLR analysis was too small after classification, the groups were merged or Fisher's exact test was used. The variables were entered into the model using the forward enter method. All analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Software, version 27.0 and Mplus Software, version 8.0. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically-significant.

Pancreatic cancer carers' needs

As shown in Supplementary Material Table S2, at 6 months post-surgery, carers of patients with pancreatic cancer reported a moderate overall level of need, with the greatest needs in healthcare personnel, information and knowledge, and facilities and services, and the least needs in health/psychological support, family and social support, and spiritual support.

The LPA model results

LPA models with one to six classes were constructed using the mean scores of the seven need domains at 6 months post-surgery as observed indicators. The model fit indices for each class are presented in Table 2. The AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased progressively from the 1-class to the 6-class model, indicating improved model fit. However, the LMR test for the 3-, 4-, and 6-class models was insignificant, suggesting that these models did not fit better than the 2-class and 5-class models and were therefore not considered. When comparing the 2- and 5-class models, both showed statistically significant LMRT and BLRT results (p < 0.05). However, the 5-class model had lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values than the 2-class model, indicating a superior fit. Additionally, the 5-class model had the highest entropy value (0.95), which approached 1, signifying the highest classification accuracy. The proportion of the 5-class model was at least 5 %. Each profile exhibited a high likelihood of being classified into its respective category, with assignment probabilities ranging from 0.94 to 0.99. The probability of misclassification into other profiles was extremely low, indicating that the latent profiles were clear and well-defined among the 240 carers.

Notably, the level of spiritual support needs was moderate-high, distinguishing this group from the other four profiles as the highest domain in Class 1. Class 2 (22.5 %, n = 54) showed very low scores in spiritual support needs and low scores in health and psychological needs, as well as family and social support needs among carers of patients with pancreatic cancer. Carers in Class 2 had moderately low scores in information and knowledge needs, facilities and services needs, and practical support needs. Notably, facilities and services needs were ranked second in Class 2, differing from the other four classes, where this domain was ranked after the information and knowledge domain. Healthcare personnel's needs had the highest score in Class 2, consistent with Classes 3 to 5. Class 3 (8.3 %, n = 20) had low scores in spiritual support needs and moderately low scores in family and social support needs, facilities and services needs, and practical support needs. Notably, health and psychological needs, with moderate scores, ranked third, standing out as the highest ranking for this domain among all five classes. The healthcare personnel needs scored the highest in Class 3 and were also the highest across all profiles. Meanwhile, the information and knowledge domain, which ranked second in Class 3, was at a moderately high level. Class 4 had the largest proportion of carers, accounting for 55.0 % (n = 132). The healthcare personnel needs remained the highest-ranked domain, followed by information and knowledge needs and facilities and services needs. A defining characteristic of this class was that most carers reported consistently low needs across all seven domains, distinguishing it from the other four classes. Class 5 had the fewest carers among the five classes, accounting for 5.4 % of the total carers (n = 13). Compared to the other four classes, its distinguishing feature was that nearly all domains scored at or near the moderate level, exceeding the corresponding scores of the same domains (information and knowledge, spiritual support, facilities and services and practical support) among the other four classes. In Class 5, the healthcare personnel domain had the highest score, ranking first. The domains of information and knowledge needs, spiritual support needs, and facilities and services needs ranked second to fourth in Class 5, all falling within the moderately high need range. The MLR model results

An MLR analysis was conducted, with the Class 5 group serving as the reference group. Variable coding and entry sequence are detailed in Supplementary Material Tables S6 & S7. Forward entry was used, and all included variables were statistically significant (p < 0.05). The final model showed a good fit (−2 Log Likelihood = 414.22). As shown in Supplementary Material Table S8, the model significantly improved over the intercept-only model (χ² = 108.7, df = 24, p < 0.001), indicating that the predictors reliably distinguished latent classes. The goodness-of-fit statistics (Supplementary Material Table S9) showed excellent fit (Pearson χ² = 586.18, df = 632, p = 0.90; deviance χ² = 361.06, df = 632, p = 1.00), with no indication of model-data mismatch. The Nagelkerke R² was 0.40, meaning the final model explains approximately 40 % of the variation in the dependent variable (moderate explanatory power).

The results of MLR, shown in Table 3, indicate that carers' sex, education levels, and patients' treatment after surgery and activities of daily living levels were essential factors determining the classification of carers' needs into five distinct profiles. Specifically, carers' sex significantly predicted membership in Class 3. Compared to Class 5, female carers were substantially more likely to be classified into Class 3 than male carers (OR: 11.08, 95 % CI: 1.64, 74.99, p = 0.01). For Class 4, several factors were significantly associated with class membership relative to Class 5. Patients who received postoperative treatment had significantly lower odds of being classified into Class 4 compared to those who did not (OR: 0.10, 95 % CI: 0.01, 0.84, p = 0.04). In contrast, patients with a moderately impaired level of activities of daily living had markedly increased odds of Class 4 membership (OR: 14.63, 95 % CI: 1.97, 108.77, p < 0.01), indicating that greater functional dependence increased the likelihood of being in this class. Additionally, carers with lower education levels were significantly more likely to belong to Class 4 than those with higher education (OR: 3.92, 95 % CI: 1.04, 14.72, p = 0.04). Key findings of this study

We assessed the specific needs of Chinese family carers of patients in the critical survivorship phase, 6 months after curative resection for pancreatic cancer. We found that carers experienced a moderate level of needs. At a broader level, carers of patients with pancreatic cancer reported the highest proportion of unmet needs in three key domains: healthcare personnel, information and knowledge, and facilities and services. When considering individual variability within the overall sample, carers' needs at 6 months post-surgery were categorised into five distinct classes, with key differences reflected in the level of specific need domains across subgroups. Furthermore, the key determinants distinguishing these five classes were the postoperative treatment and activities of daily living levels in patients and carers' sex and education levels. Notably, we have enhanced clinical awareness of carers' long-term needs and highlighted the importance of recognising individual variations for personalised support.

The overall level of needs among carers of patients with pancreatic cancer

Given the unique challenges faced by families affected by pancreatic cancer within the context of China's healthcare system and demographic context, the unmet needs of carers may require urgent attention. The 6-month postoperative period is a critical transition point in the cancer care trajectory, yet few researchers have explored carers' needs at this stage. Anderson et al. (2025), in a recent scoping review of the psychosocial impact of pancreatic cancer on carers, found a consistently high prevalence of unmet needs among family carers of individuals with pancreatic cancer. Two quantitative studies included in this scoping review, which used the Supportive Care Needs Survey for Partners and Carers, reported that 63 % of Australian and 93.7 % of French pancreatic cancer carers had at least one moderate-to-high unmet need, predominantly in the domains of healthcare personnel, medical services, and information (Huynh et al., 2023; Grivel et al., 2023). Although these two quantitative studies were conducted with relatively small samples (N < 100), their findings align with those from our survey, indicating that many pancreatic cancer carers encounter difficulties in accessing clear, timely, and understandable information, as well as direct support from healthcare professionals. Therefore, it may be essential for healthcare professionals to proactively provide relevant, easy-to-understand information and long-term support to carers, addressing these high-ranked unmet needs. Furthermore, Anderson et al. (2025), in their scoping review, highlighted that emotional and psychological support was another key area of carers' unmet needs. Some carers reported a lack of emotional support and significant psychological distress, which often went unaddressed by healthcare professionals (Anderson et al., 2025). Related qualitative studies further revealed that carers tended to prioritise the patients' needs over their own, often experiencing guilt and disregarding their own needs, which resulted in insufficient self-care awareness and action (Chong et al., 2022). Consistent with these findings, we also found that at 6 months post-surgery, carers' own psychological and emotional needs were ranked lower, suggesting a lack of attention to their self-care needs. This finding should not be overlooked in clinical practice, as carers' health concerns may affect their own wellbeing and are closely linked to that of their patients’ (Lin et al., 2020). Therefore, healthcare professionals should consider not only focusing on patient needs but also encouraging carers to express and attend to their own needs and incorporate carer health-related assessments into ongoing follow-up support.

While the discussion above addresses the overall level of carers' needs, significant individual variability exists in clinical settings. For example, researchers on the needs of families coping with other gastrointestinal cancers have revealed notable variations in reported priorities. Some carers emphasised a strong need for religious support (Keten Edis and Kurtgoz, 2024), while others did not (Zhao et al., 2023); similarly, some ranked information needs about diet, health, and emotional recovery as their top priority (Milliron et al., 2023), whereas others placed greater emphasis on access to healthcare professionals (Zhao et al., 2023). These diverse findings indicated individual differences in carers' needs, warranting further investigation by healthcare professionals (American Geriatrics Society Expert Panel on Person-Centered, 2016).

Differences in carers' latent needs

We identified five distinct needs subgroups among family carers of patients with pancreatic cancer, 6 months post-surgery, providing preliminary insights into subgroup-specific needs using LPA, aligning with the growing emphasis on person-centred care (Berlin et al., 2014). Classes 1, 3, and 5 accounted for 5 % to 10 % of the sample. Although these subgroups represented a smaller proportion of carers, their unique unmet need profiles warrant detailed discussion rather than being overlooked. A common feature across Classes 1, 3, and 5 was the consistently high ranking of healthcare personnel and information and knowledge needs, which ranked among the top three priorities in each group. Specifically, in Classes 3 and 5, these two domains ranked first and second, respectively, highlighting their critical role. However, each of these three classes also demonstrated distinctive characteristics: (1) Class 1: Prominent Religious Support Needs: Carers in this class exhibited a stronger need for religious support than other domains within Class 1, though to a lesser extent than those in Class 5. (2) Class 3: Emphasis on Self-Health and Psychological Wellbeing: Unlike carers in other classes, who predominantly prioritised the needs of their relatives with cancer over their own, those in Class 3 placed substantial emphasis on their personal health and psychological wellbeing. Apart from religious support (which remained low), all other domains scored within the moderate to high range. Notably, health and psychological needs, family and social support needs, and healthcare personnel needs were significantly higher in Class 3 than in the other four classes, with healthcare personnel needs ranking the highest. (3) Class 5: Comprehensive Moderate to High Needs: This class was characterised by consistently almost moderate to high levels of need across all six domains. Compared to the other four classes, Class 5 carers reported significantly higher scores for needs related to information, religious support, and facilities and services. However, while healthcare personnel's needs ranked first within Class 5, their scores were slightly lower than those observed in Class 3, making it the second highest across all five classes. In contrast, Classes 2 and 4 constituted the largest proportions of carers, representing 22.5 % and 55 % of the sample, respectively. Both groups exhibited the lowest engagement in religious support needs, with scores significantly lower than those observed in Classes 1, 3, and 5. Although Class 2 carers consistently reported slightly higher need scores across all seven domains compared to Class 4, a notable distinction was observed: in Class 2, facilities and services needs ranked second, whereas in Class 4, this domain ranked third, indicating a subtle but meaningful difference in priority ranking.

Across all five identified subgroups, the most reported need was the active involvement of healthcare professionals throughout the survivorship period. Additionally, many carers expressed a need for guidance from healthcare professionals in accessing, evaluating, and verifying medical information to ensure the reliability and accuracy of obtained resources (Zhao et al., 2023). Specifically, Class 1 may benefit from enhanced attention to spiritual or religious support, which could help carers find meaning in their caregiving experience. For Class 3, the pattern of reported needs suggests that carers may be at heightened risk for personal health issues, including anxiety, depression, chronic illness, and caregiving-related fatigue or somatic symptoms. These findings indicated the potential value of regular assessment and supportive strategies to resolve these risks. Carers in class 3 also reported greater reliance on family and social support networks and an exceptionally high need for healthcare professional assistance—areas that may warrant prioritisation in future support planning. For Class 5, the distinct need pattern, focused on caregiving information, religious support, healthcare services, and facility access, may reflect more complex challenges, highlighting the need to explore targeted, multidimensional support.

In contrast, religious support appeared to be a lower priority for carers in Classes 2 and 4, suggesting that support strategies for these groups may focus on other areas of need. Class 2 carers may have a greater need for access to medical resources and services, including dedicated consultation services, guidance in navigating hospital systems, home care visits, and peer support networks that facilitate the sharing of caregiving experiences. Although Class 4 accounted for the largest proportion of carers, their overall need scores across all seven domains were the lowest among the five classes. Nevertheless, this does not suggest an absence of need. The relatively low need scores may be influenced by the 6-month post-surgery period, which coincides with follow-up care and chemotherapy, during which carers are in regular contact with healthcare professionals (Gustavell et al., 2020). This engagement may help address some caregiving-related concerns through routine clinical interactions. Nonetheless, further research is necessary to identify persistent challenges and unmet needs within this subgroup, thereby enhancing their long-term caregiving and health outcomes.

In summary, we have highlighted the significant clinical value of LPA in scientifically identifying subgroups with distinct caregiving needs. Such stratification may help inform more efficient resource planning and support the development of needs-based and individualised strategies for carer support.

Analysis of factors influencing the latent profile of carers' needs

We have identified key factors that influenced the classification of carers' needs, including the patients' activities of daily living levels, post-surgery treatment, and the carers' sex and education levels.

We found that patients' postoperative activities of daily living levels were a crucial factor in explaining differences in carers' needs for individuals affected by pancreatic cancer, consistent with previous findings by Zavagli et al. 2022. A key innovation of our study was its stratification of activities of daily living into distinct 3-level categories, allowing for a more nuanced understanding of how carer needs may differ across functional levels. As postoperative recovery progresses, varied among individuals, the degree of self-care impairment likely influenced both the intensity and nature of caregiving required (Zavagli et al., 2022). In our study, most patients 6 months post-pancreatic cancer surgery experienced moderate impairment, meaning that although they retained some level of independence, they still required assistance with specific activities (such as cooking, outdoor activities, or medication management) (Chong et al., 2022). For patients experiencing ongoing challenges, carers may need to be equipped with knowledge and skills related to long-term recovery, including nutritional support, rehabilitation, and complication monitoring (Tang et al., 2018).

For patients with pancreatic cancer who had undergone curative surgery, adjuvant chemotherapy was generally recommended in the absence of significant contraindications and when the patient's physical condition had sufficiently recovered (Chinese Pancreatic Surgery Association, 2021). Standard regimens typically involve six to eight consecutive cycles (Altman et al., 2019). In our study, over 65 % of participants were still undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy at 6 months post-surgery, aiming to maintain disease control and prevent recurrence. The unique needs associated with chemotherapy care and the logistics of transporting patients between hospital and home likely contributed to the specific needs among carers. We have further confirmed that continued postoperative treatment was associated with differences in carers' needs. Previous qualitative researchers on the caregiving experience during chemotherapy for colorectal cancer revealed that carers developed additional needs for health professionals' guidance and psychosocial support (Cal et al., 2017). Many carers reported that patients became more irritable and impatient during chemotherapy, which negatively impacted carers' emotional wellbeing, at times leading to a desire to withdraw from caregiving responsibilities (Cal et al., 2017). Moreover, carers struggled to manage a range of chemotherapy-related complications, including pain, anorexia, nutritional deficiencies, diarrhea, fatigue, insomnia, and mucosal ulcers (Cal et al., 2017). They acknowledged their inability to effectively manage these complications, particularly the dietary risks associated with chemotherapy-induced appetite loss. Although these insights are drawn from qualitative rather than quantitative evidence and cannot directly determine whether chemotherapy itself is a statistically-significant predictor of carers' needs, they do substantiate the profound impact of chemotherapy on families affected by cancer, complementing the findings of our study. In the future, the influence of adjuvant chemotherapy on carers' needs, particularly in the context of prolonged survival, should not be underestimated. While our study has demonstrated the complex role of postoperative chemotherapy in shaping carers' needs, it remains unclear whether differences in chemotherapy regimens result in more pronounced variations. For instance, oral chemotherapy is increasingly recommended due to its convenience and reduced need for hospital visits. However, its absorption is subject to interindividual variability (Fenocchio et al., 2019). Whether such differences, compared to conventional intravenous administration, result in divergent need profiles among carers warrants further investigation.

Carers' demographic characteristics also appeared to shape the profiles of need subgroups, particularly sex and educational levels. Prior researchers have indicated that female carers tend to report greater unmet needs than males, especially in the domain of psychological needs (Zhao et al., 2019b). In our study, female carers were more likely to be classified into Class 3, a profile marked by high levels of health and psychological needs and a stronger reliance on healthcare professionals for informational and emotional support. This pattern may reflect traditional sociocultural expectations, particularly within the Chinese context, where Confucian values often position women as the primary carers (Zhan, 2004). Reflecting this cultural norm, most carers in our sample were women. As caregiving duration extends and illness progresses, female carers may encounter increasing challenges that intensify their needs for timely, professional information and support.

A longitudinal study examining the information needs of cancer carers over the 9 months following diagnosis identified that carers with lower educational attainment reported higher information needs (Ullrich et al., 2021). However, this finding partially contrasts with our results, which showed that a lower education level was associated with lower reported information needs. One possible explanation is that less-educated carers may have reduced awareness of the gap between their capabilities and the intricate needs of caregiving. Consequently, they might adopt a more passive stance, showing less initiative in seeking advanced caregiving knowledge or skills perceived as beyond their capacity (Sorensen et al., 2012). Carers’ educational background could influence their ability to understand and apply health-related information. Tailoring support based on education levels and continuously assessing carers’ comprehension may therefore enhance the effectiveness of family-centred care (Holden et al., 2021). Overall, the distinct impact of sex and education levels on carers' needs highlights the possible importance of early identification and classification of these demographic characteristics. While demographic attributes are inherently non-modifiable, their early recognition could help healthcare professionals design more targeted and timely support that alleviates caregiving burden and enhances carers’ adaptability.

Implications for nursing practice

Nurses play a critical role in identifying and addressing the unmet needs of carers, particularly during the key post-surgical period. We recommend that routine assessment of carers' psychological, informational, and practical support needs should be incorporated into the standard family care program. In addition, nurses should ensure that carers receive clear, understandable information and appropriate guidance, as well as support for their own needs, to assist them in managing the needs of long-term postoperative care. Awareness of factors such as carers’ sex and education levels, patients’ activities of daily living levels, and post-surgery treatment may help nurses identify carers who are more likely to belong to particular need subgroups, informing the provision of more tailored support during the postoperative survivorship period.

Limitations

This is the first quantitative study to assess the needs of Chinese carers of patients with pancreatic cancer while also considering the individualised factors that distinguish heterogeneous need subgroups and their predictors. This study has several limitations. First, it used convenience sampling, and the questionnaire relied on self-reports, which may have introduced selection and recall bias (Munnangi and Boktor, 2025). Second, although excluding participants with newly-diagnosed severe mental illness or cancer metastasis during follow-up may limit the generalisability of our findings, this approach also helped to minimise potential confounding effects from these comorbid conditions. It allowed researchers to focus more precisely on the needs and predictors specific to pancreatic cancer caregiving. Third, as this was a cross-sectional study, causal relationships could not be established from the observed associations, and all interpretations should be made with caution. Furthermore, participants were recruited from a single region in China, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Regional differences in healthcare resources, cultural practices, and support systems may influence the needs and experiences of carers. Therefore, caution should be exercised when applying these results to carers in other regions or countries. Fourth, 16 % of the total sample completed the survey by telephone. This method may increase the risk of social desirability bias or interviewer influence, potentially compromising the comparability of self-reported data. Finally, although the sample size met the minimum requirement, it was still limited for subgroup analysis. We identified five subgroups, and while each group had the minimum required number of participants for LPA, the sample size was relatively small for MLR analysis. As a result, the 95 % CIs for the identified influencing factors (patients' activities of daily living levels and carers' sex) were relatively wide, which could cause ambiguity regarding the reliability of the results. Despite this limitation, the primary aim of the study was to identify distinct subgroups with varying characteristics, thereby enabling an in-depth understanding of carers' needs. In this context, the findings retain their value for clinical application. Future studies should use larger and more diverse samples to enhance the robustness of subgroup classification and identify more associated factors (e.g., chemotherapy regimens).

Conclusions

We employed LPA to identify distinct category characteristics and key predictors regarding the needs of carers of patients with pancreatic cancer. The needs of these carers were classified into five distinct latent profiles. The results from the MLR model revealed that patients' activities of daily living levels, postoperative treatment, and carers' sex and education levels were significantly associated with the profile membership. Recognising the similarities and differences among the five need profiles and their associated factors could help clinical professionals in prioritising elements of support more effectively. The findings may also point to the potential value of developing personalised care strategies tailored to carers’ specific needs, to enhance care quality and promote the wellbeing of both patients and families.
