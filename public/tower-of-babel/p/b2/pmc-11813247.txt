Kurtosis Assessment of Cardiovascular Disease Risk Caused by Complex Noise in Coal Mines
Authors: Yuanzhen Du, Xinrong Tao, Fengjen Chu, Yuanjie Zou, Jia Wang, Yu Ding, Min Mu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objectives:This study aims to investigate the relationship between noise kurtosis and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk while exploring the potential of kurtosis assessment in evaluating CVD risk associated with complex noise exposure in coal mines.Methods:This cross-sectional study started in April 2021 and ended in November 2022. It involved 705 coal miners selected from 1045 participants. The participants underwent questionnaire surveys, physical examinations and assessment of individual noise exposure levels in the form of LAeq.8h. Individual CVD risk was evaluated by employing the Prediction for Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk in China (China-PAR). Logistic regression analyses were used to analyse the effect of noise kurtosis on the risk of developing CVD and adjust for confounders to determine independent effects. Stratified analyses were applied to examine the effect of different noise characteristics on risk.Results:In cardiovascular risk assessment using China-PAR, 637 and 68 individuals were classified as low and high risk, respectively. Notably, the group exposed to noise levels of 85–100 dB(A) exhibited a significantly higher risk than those exposed to noise levels of <85 dB(A). Regarding kurtosis (β), individuals with β < 3 had low cardiovascular risk, whereas those with β > 5 had high risk, with risk increasing alongside kurtosis. Correlation analysis indicated a strong association amongst noise kurtosis, cumulative noise exposure (CNE) and CVD risk after accounting for individual age and service duration. Logistic regression analysis identified kurtosis as the primary influencing factor for CVD risk (odds ratio = 3.46, 95% confidence interval: 1.68–7.13).Conclusions:Given the pervasive presence of complex noise in the coal mining industry, kurtosis can serve as a valuable supplementary parameter for adjusting CNE, thus facilitating the assessment of CVD risk associated with complex noise exposure in coal mines.

INTRODUCTION

Occupational noise, characterised by unwanted and monotonous sounds of varying frequencies and intensities generated during production and work processes, poses a considerable challenge in various industries, including coal mining.[1] In coal mines, sources of such noise encompass the operation of underground machinery and clamour resulting from coal and rock collapses. Compounded by confined working spaces within coal mines, the noise energy is not easy to dissipate. As a result the noise propagates over extended distances, leading to extensive areas of noise pollution. The effect of noise on individuals is intricate because high noise levels not only disrupt normal verbal communication, but also induce adverse effects, such as accelerated heart rate and central nervous system disorders, amongst miners.[2] The general findings from the national statistical survey of occupational hazards conducted by China’s Occupational Health Department in 2022 revealed a concerning scenario. According to the report, approximately 6,262,800 workers across industrial enterprises nationwide, accounting for a substantial 71.95% of the total workforce, are exposed to occupational noise hazards.[3]

Furthermore, 2019–2020 data from the Chinese Centre for Disease Control and Prevention’s Occupational Health and Poison Control Institute reported a rising trend in cases of noise-induced hearing loss primarily concentrated within the manufacturing and mining sectors.[4] This upward trajectory underscores the escalating health risks stemming from noise exposure in coal mines, necessitating increased attention and preventive measures.

Noise ranks as the second most common occupational disease hazard factor within the Chinese coal industry, with the prevalent noise type being non-Gaussian complex noise.[5] Notably, the noise landscape of coal mining features two prominent noise sources. These are high-decibel background noise generated by mining machinery operations and pulse noise originating from coal and rock collapses or equipment collisions. Researchers have long established that at similar noise exposure levels, impulse or complex noise with pulse components poses a greater danger to health than continuous steady-state noise.[6]

In addition to its detrimental effect on hearing, noise inflicts harm on the cardiovascular system. Studies have shown that noise increases the sensitivity of vascular smooth muscle to vasoconstrictive stimuli, resulting in heightened reactivity and subsequent blood vessel constriction, ultimately leading to elevated blood pressure.[7] Noise exerts its effects through auditory organs, influencing the cerebral cortex and central nervous system, triggering a series of responses that disrupt autonomic nervous system regulation and vascular control, hence causing disturbances in lipid metabolism.[8] Prolonged exposure to noise at certain intensities has been linked to pronounced abnormalities in serum glucose, total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels.[9] In research employing the Framingham cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk model, noise energy was found to be positively correlated with the risk of CVD.[10]

Zhang et al. indicated that assessing health damage solely on the basis of noise energy without considering the pulsatile nature of noise may underestimate the extent of health harm.[11] However, current noise standards primarily rely on continuous steady-state noise for evaluating health damage. Consequently, applying these existing standards to assess the health damage caused by complex noise in coal mining settings inevitably results in underestimations.

Kurtosis serves as a valuable tool for gauging the presence of high-amplitude sounds and the degree of pulsation in the realm of noise.[12] On the basis of kurtosis values, noise can be categorised into following two broad types: steady noise with a kurtosis less than 3 and complex noise with a kurtosis greater than 3. Given that noise kurtosis essentially expresses the time domain structure of noise energy, incorporating kurtosis as an auxiliary parameter alongside noise energy in noise assessment can greatly enhance our ability to evaluate the health risks associated with noise exposure.[13] In this study, a comprehensive database comprising 1045 workers hailing from a large coal mining enterprise was compiled. Detailed long-term noise records and comprehensive physical examination data were gathered from 705 individuals employed across seven distinct departments.

The main objective of this study is to investigate the feasibility of using the kurtosis index as an effective tool for assessing CVD risk due to exposure to complex noise, particularly health hazards associated with complex noise encountered in the coal mining industry.

Participants and methods

Selection of participants

Between April 2021 and November 2022, 1045 individuals were initially informed about the study’s purpose and invited to participate. A total of 705 patients were ultimately included in the study [Figure 1]. All participants provided informed consent by signing a consent form. This study was in line with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013).[14] For inclusion in the study, participants had to meet the following four criteria: (1) had clear work experience and no remarkable history of occupational noise exposure, (2) had worked in their current position for at least 1 year, (3) worked in an environment with noise present and complete noise monitoring data available and (4) had complete physical examination and questionnaire data.

Screening of study subjects.

Questionnaire survey

All participants were required to undergo an occupational health examination and complete a questionnaire. A trained occupational hygienist assisted participants in completing the physical examination and questionnaire interviews to ensure quality control. The occupational health examination included various information, such as general demographic details (e.g., height, weight, waist circumference and hipline), routine blood test results, routine urine test results, biochemical data, immunology data and other relevant factors. The questionnaire collected general demographic information (age and sex), lifestyle habits (smoking) and general health status (genetic history and diabetes situation). No interactions were detected between excess noise and smoking, alcohol consumption, exercise, sleep status, income status, education, marriage, or obesity in terms of CVD risk. Therefore, noise was used as an independent factor for the following studies.

Cardiovascular risk assessment

Each participant’s CVD risk was assessed by using the Prediction for Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk in China (China-PAR) model. The China-PAR model is a risk prediction model for CVD in Chinese individuals developed by the academician Gu Dongfeng in 2016. This model evaluates the risk of CVD comprehensively on the basis of factors, such as age, systolic blood pressure, TC, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and diabetes.[15] TC and HDL levels were determined by using standard laboratory methods. Blood pressure was measured after the patients had rested with their arm placed horizontally.[16] Smoking status and family history of CVD were self-reported through the questionnaire. Diabetes status was determined on the basis of fasting blood glucose levels (≥7.0 mmol/L) or the use of antisugar drug or insulin.[17] In accordance with the China-PAR model, a 10-year risk of ≥10.0% was classified as high risk, that of 5.0–9.9% was classified as medium risk and that of <5.0% was classified as low risk.[15] For the purpose of expanding the sample size, a 10-year risk of <5% was defined as a low risk of CVD, whereas a 10-year risk of ≥5% was considered a high risk of CVD.

Noise exposure measurement

An explosion-proof individual noise dosimeter (model: SV104-IS, manufacturer: SVANTEK, location: Poland) was employed to record the personal noise exposure of each participant throughout their shift. The dosimeter utilises a 1/2" microphone with a wide frequency response range of 20 Hz to 10 kHz. The measurement range extends from 53 to 143 dB(A) with a dynamic range of 100 dB. The dosimeter can operate continuously for more than 50 hours on full charge.

A meter (model: SV 34B, manufacturer: SVANTEK, location: Poland) was used to calibrate individual noise dosimeters before each experiment. During the measurement, continuous recordings were conducted by using three exchange recording steps and 2-second recording intervals. The recording duration ranged from 8 to 12 hours. The generated audio information file was imported into a computer by using Supervisor software (model: supervisor ver.1.9.7, manufacturer: SVANTEK, location: Poland) to analyse the collected data. This process was performed once for each participant. Before initiating the recordings, a hygienist confirmed with each participant that the recorded noise level corresponded to their typical daily exposure at work.

A dosimeter was placed on the shoulders of each participant at the beginning of the work shift, and data were collected at the end of the shift. Participants received proper training on how to correctly wear tape recorders.

Noise data categories

Valid operating noise data were intercepted for a fixed operating time length of 8 hours (8:00–16:00, 16:00–24:00 and 24:00–8:00) to ensure the consistency of the overall data. Window length was chosen by comparing the kurtosis derived from the 40- and 60-second time windows. The kurtosis of the 60-second time window expressed the impulses in the noise data well. Therefore, the average kurtosis per 60-second time window over an 8 hours working time is expressed as β.[21] Each worker was categorised into distinct groups on the basis of their β (β < 3, 3 ≤ β < 4, 4 ≤ β < 5 and β ≥ 5) and CNE (CNE < 90, 90 ≤ CNE <95, 95≤ CNE< 100, 100 ≤ CNE < 105, 105 ≤ CNE < 110 and CNE ≥ 110) values to assess the influence of complex noise in coal mines on changes in CVD risk. These kurtosis groups were meticulously designed to ensure that the CVD risk within each group significantly deviated from that in the other groups.

Statistics

Data were entered into Excel software, a database was established and statistical analysis was performed with SPSS (version: 27.0, International Business Machines Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Figures were drawn with GraphPad Prism (version: 9.5.1, GraphPad Software, Boston, USA). Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analysed by Student’s t-test. Nonnormally distributed continuous variables were expressed in the form of median (P25, P75) and analysed by Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described as percentage and analysed by chi-square test. Previous studies[22] have found that the atherosclerotic markers ankle-brachial index (ABI) and pulse wave velocity (PWV) are associated with the risk of CVD. Additionally, lifestyle factors, such as age, waist circumference, blood pressure and TC, triglyceride (TG), HDL and low-density lipoprotein levels, as well as smoking and drinking habits included in the China-PAR, are considered as risk factors for CVD.[23] Furthermore, exposure to dust and noise has been linked to an increased risk of CVD. All these aforementioned risk factors were incorporated into the variables for logistic regression analysis. Binary logistic regression was employed for univariate and multivariate analyses to ascertain the effect of noise kurtosis on the risk of CVD, with subsequent subgroup analyses conducted to elucidate the influence of noise kurtosis on CVD risk further. Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted with CVD risk class as the dependent variable and age, service length, LAeq.8h and noise kurtosis as independent variables. P < 0.05 indicated that a difference was statistically significant

Noise exposure and risk analysis of CVD

According to LAeq.8h stratification, the high-risk rates for noise levels of <85 dB(A) and >100 dB(A) were lower than those for the 85 dB(A) ≤ 100 dB(A) range, with the high-risk rate within the 85 dB(A) ≤ 100 dB(A) category consistently fluctuating by approximately 10%. The high-risk rate, which ultimately reached 100% when kurtosis exceeded 5, increased as kurtosis increased.

The relationship between CNE and the high-risk rate revealed that CNE within the range of 90 dB(A) · year ≤ 95 dB(A) · year and ≥110 dB(A) · year decreased but that in other ranges increased. During CNE stratification, the high-risk rate increased with increasing CNE. However, no apparent downwards trend was observed throughout the CNE’ entire process. The relationship between CVD risk and LAeq.8h revealed no significant difference in the high-risk detection rate amongst different noise intensity stratifications. However, the high-risk detection rate increased as kurtosis increased. The high-risk population was not detected when the noise kurtosis fell within the steady-state kurtosis stage (β < 3). As the sample transitioned to the low kurtosis stage (3 ≤ β < 4), the high-risk detection rate sharply increased. However, the increase in the detection rate of high-risk patients was relatively modest as the risk progressed from the low kurtosis to the middle kurtosis stage (4 ≤ β < 5). Once the high kurtosis stage was reached (β ≥ 5), all individuals were classified as high risk. At the same exposure level, an increase in noise kurtosis led to a corresponding increase in the high-risk detection rate until it reached saturation.

Subgroup analysis of noise kurtosis and CVD risk

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the relationship between noise kurtosis and CVD risk exhibited a direct variation across subgroups. The difference between noise kurtosis and CVD risk was statistically significant in those aged 30–40 years (odds ratio [OR] = 7.21, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.32–22.41, P < 0.001). The OR for seniority between 5 and 10 years was 2.84, with a 95% CI of 1.10–7.35. The difference observed in the population with a history of smoking and no CVD family history was statistically significant [Table 5]. The subgroup analyses of sex and history of diabetes were not performed due to the predominance of males in the selected population and absence of diabetes in the subgroup.

DISCUSSION

Researchers in the field of noise-induced health damage have extended their focus beyond noise energy alone to consider various influencing factors. Health damage in noise-exposed workers has been well established to aggravate with the prolongation of the duration of noise exposure. As a result, a novel approach has been proposed for assessing the risk of noise-induced diseases temporally and spatially by combining noise energy with exposure duration, resulting in the concept of CNE. Upon the recognition of the divergence between complex noise-induced health damage and steady-state noise, the concept of noise kurtosis was introduced as an important metric for the evaluation of complex noise. The distinction between steady and complex noise can be made with relative ease and effectiveness through the use of kurtosis. Kurtosis enables the transformation of noise pulse peak, duration and pulse interval distribution into simple variables, thereby greatly simplifying the classification of noise.

The results indicate that high-risk groups have a positive relationship with noise kurtosis and CNE. Notably, a peak in noise kurtosis corresponded to a 100% prevalence of high-risk groups, whereas the prevalence of high-risk groups declined before CNE peaked. Upon adjusting for kurtosis, the trend of CNE remained consistent with that of noise kurtosis primarily due to the dependence of CNE on noise intensity and duration. Populations exposed to occupational noise experience various scenarios, including high-intensity short-duration exposure and low-intensity long-duration exposure, which collectively affect the prevalence of high-risk individuals.

The correlation between noise kurtosis and high-frequency hearing loss (HFHL) is robust.[24] Additionally, a significant association exists between noise exposure and CVD risk.[25] By building upon prior investigations, the current study delves deeply into the correlation between noise kurtosis and CVD risk. The regression coefficient for noise kurtosis is 1.24 (OR = 3.46, 95% CI: 1.68–7.13, P < 0.001). The degree of noise kurtosis is a clear factor that affects CVD risk. This situation suggests that noise kurtosis can serve as a crucial indicator for evaluating CVD risk, emphasising the potential negative health implications of noise kurtosis.

In the study of the association between CNE and CNE’ with HFHL, the degree of hearing loss was observed to tend to increase with the increase in CNE and CNE’.[26] Furthermore, the increase in CNE’ was significantly higher than that in CNE. In the present study, we investigated the association of CNE and CNE’ with CVD risk. The trend of change found in this work was similar to that observed in Themann’s study,[26] except that CNE showed superior performance to CNE’. This disparity could be attributed to variations in noise characteristics between the coal mining sector and manufacturing industry, as well as potential constraints in sample size. Consequently, the further validation of the effectiveness of CNE and CNE’ in assessing CVD risk is required in future studies.

Kurtosis has been demonstrated to exhibit a significant correlation in studies investigating its relationship with CVD risk. Some studies have proposed that utilising different calculation time frames for kurtosis might influence its correlation with noise-induced health impairments.[27] Meanwhile, adjustment coefficients for kurtosis could also affect the outcomes of health risk assessments.[28] Consequently, conducting further research into how calculation time frames and adjustment coefficients for kurtosis influence the evaluation of noise-induced health hazards is imperative.

Kurtosis has emerged as an effective auxiliary parameter for assessing CVD risk. The use of kurtosis as an auxiliary parameter in conjunction with noise power indicators proves to be valuable in evaluating the risk of CVDs arising from complex noise. It has been found to be more effective than relying solely on noise energy indicators because it plays a pivotal role in assessing occupational health damage in complex noise environments, such as coal mines. Large-scale epidemiological studies through the establishment of databases with extensive samples are indispensable for further validating the role of kurtosis in evaluating the risk of CVD resulting from non-smooth noise exposure

CONCLUSIONS

This study revealed that the kurtosis of noise in the coal mining industry is notably lower than that in the general manufacturing and metal processing industries. Comprehensive noise measurements and meticulous kurtosis calculations demonstrated that the studied coal mining enterprise grapples with continuous exposure to a high-intensity complex noise environment. An in-depth analysis of the physical indicators and noise exposure data of 705 Chinese workers subjected to this complex noise environment during coal mining allowed us to examine the combined influence of noise exposure levels and kurtosis on the incidence of CVDs.
