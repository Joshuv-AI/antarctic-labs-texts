Targeted next-generation sequencing for direct drug-resistant tuberculosis detection in sputum samples in Indonesia: an implementation study (2024–25)
Authors: Bony Wiem Lestari, Rifky Waluyajati Rachman, Rahmah Aulia Zahra, Alamanda Larasmanah, Silvi Indriani, Almira Alifia, Sonya Alexandra, Azzania Fibriani, Yuvi Siti Marwah, Ryan B. Ristandi, Bachti Alisjahbana, Philip C. Hill, Arto Yuwono Soeroto, Reinout van Crevel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe availability of targeted Next Generation Sequencing (tNGS) for drug-resistant tuberculosis (DRTB) is a major advance in TB diagnostics, but its optimal implementation, yield, and cost in high TB-incidence settings remain unclear. We aimed to evaluate tNGS in relation to laboratory workflow, implementation cascade, turnaround time, and unit cost.MethodsWe prospectively evaluated tNGS implementation with Deeplex Myc-TB for sputum samples at the provincial reference laboratory in West Java, Indonesia, from 1 August 2024 to 10 October 2025. We included patients with Xpert-diagnosed rifampicin-resistant TB (RRTB) at 32 hospitals and 15 community health centres, collectively catering to 51.0% of RRTB patients in the province.FindingsFrom 1210 RRTB sputum samples collected, 812 (67.1%) samples completed sequencing, with 429/812 (52.8%) yielding complete and 164/812 (20.2%) partial reads. Success of tNGS depended on bacterial load, ranging from 88.8% for Xpert high to 0% for very low samples. Majority samples were confirmed as RR/MDR TB (89.2%), with pre-XDR and XDR TB recorded in 10.6% and 0.2%, respectively. Workflow optimisation improved sequencing performance, with final workflow reaching a laboratory success of 82.4% (201/244). The median turnaround time for tNGS from sample reception until sequencing results was eight days (IQR 6–10). The total tNGS unit cost per sample was $263.6 for iSeq and $239.6 for MiSeq.InterpretationtNGS performed well in those with high DNA sputum load with relatively rapid turnaround time. Its optimisation for wide adoption should consider DRTB burden, sample selection, sequencing platform, and costs.FundingGates Foundation and FIND.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We conducted a rapid literature review between June and July 2025 using the following electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane CENTRAL, and MedRxiv. The search strategy employed variations of the keywords: “tuberculosis”, “targeted next generation sequencing”, “implementation”, “high-burden”, and “LMIC”. Evidence suggests that targeted Next-Generation Sequencing (tNGS) is a feasible method for genotypic drug susceptibility testing (DST) in high-burden tuberculosis settings, capable of providing a broader resistance profile. Performance of tNGS implementation has mostly been laboratory-based, with diagnostic accuracy reported as the primary outcome. High sensitivity and specificity have been reported for tNGS testing, except for linezolid. The Xpert Ultra test can serve as a useful predictor of downstream tNGS success. Key implementation challenges include the complexity of tNGS laboratory procedures and results interpretation, procurement barriers, and laboratory readiness. Evidence on tNGS turnaround time and implementation costs remains scarce, with most findings restricted to laboratory-based evaluation of tNGS workflow. To date, no studies have provided detailed information on feasibility or operational experience in a programmatic setting, emphasising the need for real-world operational evaluation.

Added value of this study

This study reports a culture-free tNGS implementation evaluation conducted in a high TB-burden country like Indonesia, within routine care setting. Success of tNGS depended on bacterial load, ranging from 88.8% for Xpert high to 0% for very low samples. The optimised workflow using a modified pre-extraction procedure after three rounds of trials finally improved overall tNGS success reaching a laboratory success of 82.4% (201/244). This study also reported tNGS unit costs (per sample) were $285.4 for iSeq 100 and $261.4 for MiSeq i100 plus, costs related to the core tNGS workflow contributed to majority of tNGS cost.

Implications of all the available evidence

The findings of this study have implications for future tNGS adoption strategies in low-resource countries. It highlights that implementation of tNGS is feasible in high TB-burden countries, but its performance needs to be enhanced for samples with a low sputum bacterial burden and integration into programmatic settings will be resource-intensive. Given the complexity and high cost of this technology, further assessment of tNGS’s cost-effectiveness and clinical utility is needed and further technical improvements should be made to enhance its performance. Other sequencing platforms should be assessed in terms of local adaptability, yield, and costs.

Introduction

Rapid and comprehensive diagnosis of drug-resistant tuberculosis (DRTB) is challenging, particularly in high-burden setting. The current gold standard for DRTB diagnosis is phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (pDST), which is time-intensive, technically challenging, and dependent on adequate laboratory infrastructure.1, 2, 3 Molecular WHO-recommended rapid diagnostics (mWRDs) such as Xpert MTB/RIF (Xpert Ultra) and MTB/XDR can diagnose DRTB, but with limited drug coverage.1 Early enrolment in an optimised drug regimen based on a patient’s resistance profile improves treatment outcome, which calls for evaluation of newer tools to diagnose DRTB.

Targeted Next Generation Sequencing (tNGS) has been recommended by WHO for diagnosing DRTB, due to its potential for reliable detection of resistance to new and repurposed drugs, such as bedaquiline, linezolid, and clofazimine, that remain unassessed in other existing molecular tests.4 It could suit routine conditions better than whole genome sequencing (WGS), which consumes more time and resources, typically requiring culture of Mycobacterium tuberculosis from sputum samples.5,6 Three tNGS products were listed in the most recent WHO guidelines for TB diagnosis: Deeplex Myc-TB, AmPORE-TB, and TBseq.4 The recommendation is conditional as data are still limited for several drugs, and with respect to feasibility.4

Among TB high-burden countries, use of tNGS for TB has been reported in India, Namibia, Bangladesh, Eswatini, South Africa, and Zambia, with between 48 and 720 samples included.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 Only Eswatini integrated tNGS into its national DRTB diagnostic algorithm, noting that the country’s DRTB case numbers are very small.10 Local challenges might differ across different settings, and information on how tNGS is used by clinicians is limited.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 There remains an urgent need for context-based tNGS implementation studies to be reported from high-burden settings to better describe the feasibility of implementation.

Indonesia has the second-largest TB burden worldwide, with an estimated 1,092,000 TB patients in 2024, with a total of 12,512 DRTB patients and a 58.5% DRTB treatment success rate reported.13,14 We previously found that only 9.8% of estimated DRTB patients were diagnosed, although this has been increasing with the ongoing rollout of Xpert Ultra testing.15 Moreover, we found that pDST results were only available for half of the patients, requiring an average of two months to yield results.15 We therefore aimed to evaluate the implementation of tNGS rapid genotypic drug resistance profiling directly from sputum samples in Indonesia, tailored as closely as possible to the programmatic context, taking into account laboratory workflow implementation across the DRTB cascade, turnaround time, and unit costs.

Methods

This tNGS implementation study was conducted from 1 August 2024 to 10 October 2025 in West Java (population 50.8 million people), the province with the highest DRTB burden in Indonesia. Of 232,602 TB patients reported in 2024, 3988 were identified as DRTB.14 There are a total of 5252 health facilities that provide TB treatment and 284 Xpert Ultra machines for TB diagnosis, with an estimated 39.8% of TB confirmed by Xpert Ultra-testing.16 Management of DRTB is facilitated by 39 Programmatic Management of DRTB (PMDT) Hospitals, 30 Community Health Centres (CHCs) that offer DRTB treatment initiation, and three referral laboratories that perform culture and phenotypic drug-susceptibility testing (pDST).16 Diagnosis and treatment of DRTB patients operate under the National DRTB Technical Guidelines.17 Patients with Rifampicin-Resistant TB (RRTB) confirmed by Xpert Ultra are referred to DRTB hospitals for further testing, including molecular testing (Xpert MTB/XDR) and standardised pDST.17 Study sites were selected in consultation with the Provincial Health Office (PHO), taking into account the burden of DRTB, existing diagnostic and treatment referral networks, geographic coverage, and willingness to participate in the study (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Procedures

tNGS was performed on sputum samples from newly diagnosed Xpert Ultra pulmonary RRTB patients who provided written informed consent during DRTB baseline assessment in selected study sites. Training was conducted for clinic nurses and laboratory technicians for sample collection and packaging. Another series of training sessions was provided by GenoScreen trainers for laboratory technicians of the West Java Provincial Health Laboratory (WJPHL), a provincial-referral TB laboratory equipped with BSL 2+ facilities and experience in COVID-19 sequencing.

When samples were available for pickup, on-site healthcare workers (HCWs) notified the study team, which ensured collection within a day by a courier, using a standardised sample packaging procedure. All samples were sent to and processed in WJPHL as the only tNGS testing site of this study. At WJPHL, received samples were cross-checked, inspected visually for initial quality assessment, and decontaminated within 48 h before being processed for tNGS, using the standard NALC-NaOH method.17 DNA was manually extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany; Cat. No. 51306). We utilised the Deeplex Myc-TB (GenoScreen, France) as the targeted PCR assay and Illumina DNA prep (Illumina, USA) for library preparation. For purification, we utilised AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA) and quantified the DNA concentration post-extraction, clean-up, and library preparation using Qubit dsDNA HS assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For sequencing, we used the iSeq 100 and MiSeq i100 plus platforms, capable of processing 13 and 21 samples in a single run, respectively, using the 150 bp paired-end amplicon setup. The kit can detect resistance to 13 anti-TB drugs: rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol, streptomycin, amikacin, kanamycin, capreomycin, fluoroquinolones, ethionamide, linezolid, bedaquiline, and clofazimine.18 We uploaded the tNGS results to the Deeplex Myc-TB v3.0.1 platform to obtain detailed drug resistance profiles. Then, the tNGS results were interpreted, verified, and converted to an adapted local format test result sheet together with the manufacturer’s resistance profile results to be disseminated to the clinicians. The complete process is depicted in Appendix p.3, Supplementary Fig. S2.

Several steps were taken to optimise the laboratory workflow, guided by international laboratory consultants from Global Health Labs, Radboud University Medical Center, Gates Foundation, and FIND. In the first phase, based on repeated Xpert Ultra testing, we processed Xpert Ultra semi-quantitative ‘high’, ‘medium’, ‘low’, and ‘very low’ samples, excluding ‘trace’ or negative results. In the second phase, because of insufficient yield, Xpert Ultra ‘low’ and ‘very low’ samples were first cultured before applying tNGS. In the third phase, pre-extraction bead-beating was added to facilitate direct sequencing of Xpert Ultra ‘low’ samples. In the final phase, additional centrifugation was done during extraction. Direct sputum processing was applied to ‘high’, ‘medium’, and ‘low’ Xpert Ultra results, while ‘very low’ results were later excluded from tNGS. Detailed laboratory optimisation procedures are described in Appendix p.4 Material S3 and the final workflow is shown in Appendix p.5 Supplementary Fig. S4.

Patient-related information, including identity number, informed consent, initial Xpert Ultra results, acid-fast bacilli (AFB) status, and clinical data, was obtained from medical records and health facilities’ reports and the national TB programmatic form, cross-verified through the Indonesian TB Information System and entered into the study’s Clinical Case Report Form (CRF). tNGS procedures were recorded in the Laboratory CRF, which includes the date and all laboratory process results. The number of received samples was obtained from the health facilities’ notification and laboratory team confirmation. All samples were labelled with the study lab identification number. Microscopic examination for AFB was conducted using the Ziehl-Neilsen staining method.19 Semi-quantitative Xpert results were obtained from Xpert Ultra in portable document format (PDF). The laboratory technician then recorded the results category, which was used to determine if samples could continue for tNGS. After tNGS results were retrieved, the sequencing acceptability status and detailed resistance profile were extracted from the Deeplex Myc-TB v3.0.1 extended catalogue web platform. Because tNGS had not been adopted by the National Tuberculosis Program (NTP), the results could not be uploaded through the Indonesian TB Information System.

We collected data on the tNGS unit cost using Microsoft Excel™ provided by WHO’s “Genomic Costing Tools” (GCT), comprising all item costs allocated to each tNGS workflow step, including reagents and consumables, equipment, personnel and training, facility and transport, and incidentals.20 We recorded the quantity used and the number of samples processed for each workflow step, including sample screening with Xpert Ultra. Costs related to isolated sample processing and bead-beating were excluded. We conducted interviews with the biomolecular laboratory manager and four laboratory technicians, separately for sequencing with the iSeq (September 2024) and the MiSeq (September 2025) machines. Additional cost-related information was compiled from the 2024 local procurement catalogue and purchase receipts. More detailed descriptions of tNGS cost data collection are shown in Appendix p.6 Supplementary Table S5. All collected data were entered into the study’s database, REDCap version 14.5.36.

We established a tNGS implementation cascade for fresh sputum samples, comprising indicators for five key steps with respect to numbers of samples: (1) received by the provincial lab that were earmarked for tNGS; (2) screened with semi-quantitative Xpert Ultra at the provincial lab; (3) continued for tNGS (Xpert Ultra ‘high’, ‘medium’, ‘low’, or ‘very low’); (4) completed sequencing; and (5) with interpretable sequencing results of at least one resistance-associated position. Results were classified as ‘complete’, ‘partial’, or ‘not detected’. We defined sequencing success as ‘complete’ or ‘partial’ reads. Reads were classified as ‘complete’ if all resistance-associated positions met Deeplex’s minimum coverage and had the quality score of ‘+/++/+++’, and as ‘partial’ when they had suboptimal coverage and had the quality score of ‘−’.18 Samples for which reads failed to identify M. tuberculosis were classified as ‘not detected’ (ND) and considered as unsuccessful sequencing results.18 Laboratory success was measured based on the proportion of samples yielding complete or partial reads over the number of samples completed for sequencing. Only direct sputum samples were included in the final performance evaluation. We also measured turnaround time (TAT) for each step in days, excluding weekends and national public holidays.

We calculated tNGS unit costs using a micro-costing approach with a breakdown per laboratory step. Costs were recorded and calculated in Indonesian Rupiah (IDR), and then converted to US Dollars (USD) applying the World Bank 2024 period average of 15,855 IDR per USD.21

Statistical analysis

With respect to sample size, we expected that from an estimated 2300 RRTB cases in West Java over a 15-month period, we would process approximately 1000 samples with tNGS. All analyses done in this study were primarily descriptive, and hence no formal statistical comparisons were conducted. Cascade indicators were summarised as percentages (%), with 95% CI calculated using the Wilson score method. Bacterial load parameters (Xpert Ultra and AFB) and sequencing success were analysed using logistic regression. tNGS success rates were calculated separately for each workflow to compare the effect of workflow optimisation using logistic regression. All analyses were done using R and RStudio version 2025.05.1 + 513.

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee at the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Padjadjaran (39/UN6.KEP/EC/2025). Each participant was provided with written and verbal explanations about the study prior to sample collection. Only subjects who had provided written consent were included in this study.

Role of the funding source

The funders of this project had no role in the development of the study design, data collection, analysis, interpretation, or the writing of the report.

Substantial losses were noted across the cascade, with approximately half of the samples ultimately having interpretable results, either complete or partial reads (Fig. 1). Approximately one in five samples either had insufficient volume or quality upon visual inspection or were Xpert Ultra negative or ‘trace’. From the remaining 975 samples, sequencing results could be generated for 83.3% (95% CI 80.8–85.5). A total of 163 samples could not be sequenced directly from sputum due to workflow-specific direct processing eligibility criteria (Fig. 2 and Appendix p.4, Material S3). Of these, 143 samples underwent culture processing in the second and third workflow, and 20 samples were excluded in workflow four due to ‘very low’ Xpert Ultra results. From those directly sequenced from sputum, 219 samples (27.0%; 95% CI 24.0–30.1) yielded a not detected sequencing result, 429 (52.8%; 95% CI 49.4–56.2) complete reads and 164 (20.2%; 95% CI 17.6–23.1) partial reads. Among those with interpretable Deeplex results, 39.5% (234 of 593) were classified as RRTB, 49.7% as MDR, 10.6% as Pre-XDR, and 0.2% as XDR. Incomplete sequencing results were most frequent for linezolid (120 [20.2%] of 593) and second-line injectable drugs Capreomycin (8.9%), Amikacin (7.6%), and Kanamycin (7.4%). Completion of sequencing improved with optimisation of the workflow (Fig. 2), with the final workflow yielding 92.4% (244 of 264; 95% CI 88.6–95.0) of samples completed sequencing, and 76.1% (201 of 264; 95% CI 70.6–80.9) having complete or partial reads, resulting in a laboratory success of 82.4% (201 of 244; 95% CI 77.1–86.7). Across the workflows, no notable increase was observed in the proportion of samples with complete reads (Appendix p.7, Supplementary Fig. S6). However, the proportion of samples that continued and yielded partial reads rose substantially from 10.3% (29 of 281; 95% CI 7.2–14.4) in the first to 33.0% (87 of 264; 95% CI 27.6–58.9) in the final workflow (Appendix p.7, Supplementary Fig. S6). Cumulatively, the fourth workflow resulted in a significantly higher proportion of samples with interpretable results compared to the other workflows (p < 0.001; Fig. 2), and was therefore considered the most optimal. The median TAT from sputum samples received at WJPHL until tNGS results become available was eight working days (IQR 6–10) (Fig. 1; Appendix p.8, Supplementary Fig. S7). Meanwhile, it took an additional median of 3 days (IQR 1–5) for clinicians to receive tNGS results. Throughout the implementation, 620 (76.4%) samples were sequenced on the iSeq, with an average of two batches run per week, while the remaining 192 (23.6%) samples were processed on the MiSeq i100 plus platform. Discussion

We evaluated tNGS on 1210 Xpert Ultra rifampicin-resistant sputum samples received in a provincial reference laboratory from 47 different clinical sites across West Java. After optimisation, two-thirds (67.1%) of samples completed sequencing under these programmatic conditions, only half (52.8%) yielded complete, and one-fifth (20.2%) yielded partial reads. The success of tNGS was highly dependent on the sputum bacterial load. Laboratory optimisation was necessary to reduce sequencing failure. Results were available after a median of eight days, while the tNGS unit cost was relatively high.

Although several refinements and optimisations of laboratory workflow were performed during the study period, the tNGS performance improvement was primarily reflected only in the increased number of samples yielding partial reads. The technical complexity of the laboratory workflow might hamper tNGS adoption for national TB programs in high-burden TB setting. Partial reads compromise tNGS interpretation and hinder clinical decision-making, particularly when tNGS fails to detect resistance to critical drugs such as linezolid.12,22 The complexity of this technology has been highlighted by previous studies, requiring trained laboratory technicians and a high level of troubleshooting competency.7,23 Furthermore, intricate protocols and procedures, together with advanced and unstandardised data management, analysis, and interpretation, have also been reported as tNGS programmatic challenges, underscoring the importance of local protocol validation.8 Nevertheless, the optimisation efforts conducted in this study represent a notable improvement from the default workflow, enabling us to successfully sequence ‘low’ Xpert Ultra samples directly from sputum.

Our findings showed lower laboratory success (73.0%) compared to other studies, which ranged from 91.0% to 94.8%.9,12 This difference may be influenced by variability in the study population, sample size, and sample selection criteria for tNGS processing. Additionally, it may also be affected by the variation of laboratory workflow applied in our study and a higher proportion of poor-quality samples processed for tNGS. Under programmatic conditions, more than a quarter (27.7%) of the samples we processed had ‘low’ and ‘very low’ Xpert Ultra results, which is about ten percentage points higher than the proportion of poor-quality samples reported in other studies.9,12 Our findings on low bacterial load and tNGS yield are consistent with other studies on tNGS, reinforcing the importance of sputum collection education by HCWs to improve sample quality.9,12,24 This also shows the limitation of tNGS itself, which seems to require samples with better quality and higher bacterial load to provide successful results compared to more widely used mWRDs like Xpert Ultra. To note, the Deeplex Myc-TB has a limit of detection (LoD) of 100–1000 genomes per test, provided a minimum 200 μL initial clinical sample, while Xpert Ultra claims to have LoD of as low as 12 CFU/mL for sputum samples and 11 CFU/mL for sputum sediment samples.18,25

In our study, tNGS results were available within a median of eight days after receipt. Currently, limited studies have provided information on end-to-end tNGS turnaround time from sample receipt to result. Our tNGS turnaround time closely aligns with the recommended clinical standard practice for NGS-based DST of seven days.26 One study from India reported a 24-day turnaround time for sputum samples.7 With regard to laboratory turnaround time (from extraction to result), our study is comparable with previous findings that reported durations ranging from 4 to 7 days.7,9 It is important to note that turnaround time would depend on the volume received and different batching strategies applied.

The cost of tNGS presents another constraint for programmatic use. We found that the largest component of the cost of tNGS is incurred from extraction to sequencing, meaning that the main tNGS cost driver is directly related to the consumables. Our unit costs were slightly higher than those reported in a previous study, with an estimated $230 per sample for the MiSeq platform.7 This higher cost could relate to varying tax regulations and supply chain systems across different settings.8,27,28 The high tNGS cost might also be seen as a trade-off with the offered low turnaround time and comprehensive resistance profile. Batching and optimisation of DRTB diagnostic algorithms incorporating tNGS could be done to reduce costs, but advocacy to manufacturers to bring down tNGS kit prices is as important.8,27,28

Our study examined the full tNGS performance cascade from a programmatic perspective. There are limited published literature on implementation studies that address tNGS performance evaluation. Our study has several limitations. It was performed in the West Java TB reference laboratory which may not be representative of capacity in other provincial laboratories. The tNGS cascade in this study reported the overall performance regardless of the type of laboratory workflow, consequently, there is a possible underestimation of tNGS success as the best tNGS performance was only achieved with the final workflow. However, reporting the overall performance of all workflows was most suitable, as it reflected the optimisation processes needed in order to implement tNGS in practice. There were also several high-burden sites within West Java that could not be included due to changes in local regulations, which may limit sample coverage and generalisability of the findings. However, we included multiple high-burden sites and processed 51.0% of West Java samples, ensuring that our findings are still highly relevant. The sample coverage could have been higher, but sample collection experienced a halt due to occasional Xpert Ultra stockout. Our recent assessment of Xpert implementation in multiple CHCs across West Java, showed that cartridge stockouts lasting a median of 30 days.29

Our study suggests that wider implementation of tNGS for programmatic DRTB detection from sputum requires careful consideration. Currently tNGS is feasible only in referral laboratories due to its high-level infrastructure and skilled personnel requirements. It is necessary to develop locally validated workflows in accordance with laboratory capacity, along with continuous support and training for sample collection and sequencing. Sequencing for drug resistance starts with optimal Xpert Ultra-based diagnosis of tuberculosis. Our recent evaluation of the operational performance of Xpert Ultra in West Java identified significant gaps and multi-sectoral challenges.29 Targeted interventions to maximise Xpert Ultra performance or introduction of an equivalent alternative test remain a first and crucial step for subsequent and more complete susceptibility testing. Furthermore, to ensure tNGS optimal utilisation, it is crucial to define the position and testing criteria of tNGS within national DRTB diagnostic algorithms. Embedding tNGS into national TB information system and DRTB treatment guidelines would allow a national comprehensive evaluation of its clinical utility and cost-effectiveness for sustainability, with attention to drug resistance burden, referral network, sample type, sample load, as well as infrastructure-related costs. Considering the relatively high unit cost of tNGS, there is also a need to explore other platforms and financing schemes that might contribute towards reducing cost.
