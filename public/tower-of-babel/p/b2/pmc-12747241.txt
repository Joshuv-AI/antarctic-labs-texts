Xanthogranulomatous cystitis presenting as a large pelvic mass invading bladder and causing obstructive uropathy: A rare case report
Authors: Victor Sandoval, Natalie Brossard, Matthew Steidle, Rodolfo Alpizar-Rivas, Hiroshi Miyamoto, Nitin Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a unique case of a 9 cm xanthogranulomatous cystitis pelvic mass with intravesical and extravesical component. Patient had a past medical history of a right orbital pseudotumor 15 years back, diagnosed on imaging with conservative treatment with steroids. Since xanthogranulomatosis is associated with immunologic or chronic inflammatory disorders, we suspect this patient might have baseline chronic inflammation that makes him prone to inflammatory masses.

Introduction

Xanthogranulomatous cystitis (XC) is a benign chronic inflammatory disease without a clearly defined etiology, first described in 1932.1 Xanthogranulomatosis can present along the urinary tract, the most common place is the bladder. Theories on etiology highlight immunologic disorders, unusual lipid metabolism, local allergies caused by tumors and urothelial metaplasia resulting from an initial inflammatory insult such as infection.2 38 cases of XC have been reported in the literature, in which 53 % of lesions are located in the bladder dome and 70 % are related to urachal anomalies.3 All reported cases are small intravesical tumors, there is one case of a colovesical fistula secondary to XC.4 Symptoms are non-specific. On imaging, XC resembles bladder cancer. (BC) Diagnosis of XC is mostly made on pathological examination of the specimen obtained by cystosocpic biopsy. Most common presentation of XC is incidentally bladder lesion found on Cystoscopy. The surgical treatment for XC depends on the size and degree of invasion,5 and is commonly treated by transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) or rarely partial cystectomy.2

Case presentation

A 52-year-old male with past medical history (PMH) for alcohol and substance abuse and a past diagnosis of right orbital pseudotumor (non-specific orbital inflammation) made 15 years ago treated conservatively with steroids. However, compression of the tumor caused right optic nerve atrophy. Patient had high serum IgG4. Patient now presented with a left 3 cm left maxillary sinus mass, extending into the contralateral infraorbital space. Histopathology was suggestive for chronic fibroinflammatory process, overall histologic features suggest the possibility of IgG4 related disease. He also had hematuria and bilateral severe hydroureteronephrosis. Abdominal imaging (Fig. 1) showed a 9 × 6 cm pelvic mass with intravesical and extravesical component, causing obstructive uropathy, requiring bilateral nephrostomy tubes. Urine cytology showed atypical urothelial cells. On cystoscopy, there was an 8 cm smooth solid bladder mass overlying the left lateral wall and the trigone. TURBT was done to achieve complete resection of intravesical component and obtain tissue diagnosis. Histopathology showed non-neoplastic urothelium with reactive epithelial changes and underlying submucosa with abundant lipid-laden macrophages and plasma cells mixed with neutrophils and lymphocytes, compatible with xanthogranulomatous cystitis (Fig. 2). Michaelis-Gutmann bodies, basophilic intracytoplasmic inclusions characteristic of malakoplakia, were not identified. CT Guided biopsy of the large extravesical component of the pelvic mass was also done and same biopsy was obtained. Though literature suggests that XC can be treated with anti-inflammatory agents, due to the large size of the pelvic mass causing obstructive uropathy, the patient is planned for robotic excision of mass with partial cystectomy and ureteral reimplant.

Discussion

In contemporary literature, there are only 38 cases of XC.3 Most of these are small tumors located intravesical. We present a unique case of 9 × 6 cm pelvic xanthogranulomatous mass, with intra and extravesical component blocking both ureters. Since XC mimics BC on imaging, the diagnosis is done by histopathological assessment. Though XC is a benign tumor, a large infiltrative XC can involve bladder, ureters and other pelvic organs mimicking a malignant tumor. In literature there is no report of metastasis or recurrence, but our patient has a synchronous benign inflammatory tumor in right maxillary sinus with similar pathology. Commonly seen small XC are treated with endoscopic resection, but a large XC may invariably need surgical excision. Surgical treatment for large XC is thought to be curative as there are no recurrences reported in the literature.3 The physiopathology proposed describes an overreaction of the immune system to an insult which often is attributed to an infection with a continuous growth of tissue and persistent chemotaxis long after the infection has resolved. This is exemplified by the numerous inflammatory cells seen in pathology. Therefore, treatment with antibiotics has little to no effect as it appears to be a delayed chronic response. Interestingly, with the information we have now confirming a diagnosis of xanthogranulomatosis, it is plausible that his history of right orbital pseudotumor was another manifestation of his disease. Adult-onset xanthogranuloma is a rare entity and may present as a solitary xanthogranulomatous eyelid or orbital lesion. His right optic nerve atrophy now was likely caused by compression by the orbital inflammation at the time of presentation 15 years back.

Conclusion

XC is a benign inflammatory tumor whose etiology is not clearly defined; however, it's believed to be secondary to a chronic immunologic or infectious disorder. Large XC mimicking as a pelvic mass invading bladder and ureters are very rare and have not been reported in the literature. As these are benign masses, treatment of large bladder XC should be surgical excision of mass with possible preservation of pelvic organs.
