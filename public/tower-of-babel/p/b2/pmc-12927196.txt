Milky cerebrospinal fluid without infection during endoscopic hematoma removal: illustrative case
Authors: Koji Saito, Hirokazu Koseki, Tatsuya Hirotsu, Ryosuke Mori, Yasuharu Akasaki, Yuichi Murayama

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is caused by multiple factors. Common contributors include chronic conditions, such as hypertension, elevated lipid levels, and hyperglycemia, which can promote arteriosclerosis. Structural vascular abnormalities, including intracranial aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, and moyamoya disease, have also been recognized as causes of ICH. Hypertension and hyperlipidemia are often regarded as the most influential risk factors.1

CSF is typically clear and colorless, and turbidity often suggests underlying pathological processes, such as infection, hemorrhage, or malignancy. In rare instances, metabolic conditions may cause the CSF to appear cloudy or milky. One such condition is severe hypertriglyceridemia, which has been reported to cause serum lipemia and, in exceptional cases, may be associated with CSF turbidity. However, its underlying pathophysiology remains unclear. Here, we present an illustrative case of milky CSF associated with marked hypertriglyceridemia and review previously reported cases in the literature to contextualize this rare but important finding.

Illustrative Case

A 58-year-old man was brought to the emergency department with sudden numbness in his left extremities. Although he had previously received treatment for tongue carcinoma, he had no recurrence for 8 years. The patient works as a company manager and has been independent in his daily activities. However, he had not undergone any medical examination prior to admission. On admission, his Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score on arrival was E4V4M5, his body temperature was 35.9°C, and his blood pressure was 235/150 mmHg. However, his consciousness deteriorated during the initial examination, and his GCS score decreased to E1V2M4. CT demonstrated a 13-mL hematoma in the right thalamus, followed by ventricular penetration forming a cast from the lateral ventricle to the foramina of Magendie and Luschka (Fig. 1A). MR angiography (Fig. 1B) and contrast-enhanced CT (not shown) revealed no vascular abnormalities, confirming the diagnosis of spontaneous ICH. Emergency endoscopic intraventricular hematoma removal was performed for acute hydrocephalus associated with ventricular penetration of a thalamic hemorrhage.

A: Simple head CT scans showing a 13-mL hematoma in the right thalamus with perforation into the ventricles. B: MR angiogram revealing no obvious aneurysms or vascular malformations.

A 1.5-cm-radius burr hole was created at Kocher’s point, and a transparent sheath (Neuroport, Olympus) was guided to the anterior horn of the right lateral ventricle. After the initial aspiration of the hematoma, the foramen of Monro was exposed, and milky CSF suddenly erupted from the third ventricle (Fig. 2A–C, Video 1). After perforation of the septum pellucidum, the hematoma could be removed from the contralateral anterior horn to the foramen of Monro (Fig. 2D and E).

A: A Neuroport sheath was guided to the anterior horn of the right ventricle, and the hematoma was aspirated. B and C: When the foramen of Monro could be seen, a jet of milky spinal fluid was observed from the third ventricle. D and E: Since it was difficult to see the whole area, the hematoma in the third ventricle was further aspirated and removed after sufficient irrigation.

The procedure was terminated after placement of a ventricular drain. The submitted CSF cytology was classified as class 2, and the culture examination results were negative. Blood test results showed that the blood was cloudy in appearance, with abnormally high levels of triglycerides (TGs): 2445 mg/dL. The low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels were measured at 98 mg/dL and 46 mg/dL, respectively, while the white blood cell count and C-reactive protein levels were measured at 5300/μL and 0.16 mg/dL, respectively. No signs of infection were observed during blood sampling.

The patient was extubated 2 days postoperatively, and the ventricular drain was removed 5 days postoperatively. The patient was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital with a modified Rankin Scale score of 4 on postoperative day 60.

Discussion

Observations

In general, milky CSF suggests a bacterial infection as the most likely diagnosis. However, in this case, bacterial infection was ruled out based on physical and laboratory findings. There have been a few reports of CSF turbidity without any evidence of infection. Table 1 highlights the literature review of milky CSF.2–8 Hypertriglyceridemia was observed in all the reported cases. TG levels in the CSF were elevated in all previously reported cases. In the present case, blood tests revealed a high TG level of 2445 mg/dL, and a CSF opacity of dyslipidemia origin was considered. Taken together with previous reports, these findings suggest that in cases of milky CSF without infection, assessment of a lipid panel may be important.

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) is generally considered impassable to substances with a molecular weight greater than 500 g/mol, including TG.9 Accordingly, it is believed that there is little or no transfer of fatty acids from the blood to the CSF. Koch et al. collected blood and CSF samples from lumbar punctures obtained from 216 patients without brain disease and compared the fatty acid concentrations between the two sample types. They reported that CSF concentrations were 0.54% of blood concentrations.10 Similarly, Banks et al. collected blood and CSF samples from lumbar punctures obtained from 39 healthy subjects and compared the concentrations of fatty acids in the spinal fluid to those in the blood. They reported that the CSF concentration was 0.6% of the blood concentration.11 Meanwhile, Hanson et al. reported that TG infusion led to an increase in diacylglycerol levels without increasing the concentrations of free fatty acids or TGs in the CSF. This finding suggests that specific fatty acids composing the TG may have crossed the BBB and been metabolized, or that the metabolized diacylglycerol may traverse the BBB.12

There is currently no universally accepted definition of the TG concentration that constitutes lipemic appearance, and the reported thresholds vary depending on the type of specimen. Fujimoto suggested that TG concentrations of 250 mg/dL or higher are indicative of gross emaciation,13 while Dababneh and Mousa reported that TG levels of 200 mg/dL or higher in ascitic fluid could serve as a diagnostic indicator of this condition.14 In contrast, previous studies have shown that the serum typically appears grossly lipemic when TG concentrations exceed 1000 mg/dL. Pillai described a case in which markedly milky and opaque serum was observed at TG levels that exceeded this threshold, highlighting the visual and clinical significance of severe hypertriglyceridemia in the diagnostic assessment.15

In the 8 cases reported here (Table 1), the TG ratio of the blood and CSF levels was higher than that previously reported in 3 cases (6.0%, 54.9%, and 13.0%, respectively). In contrast, serum TG levels were well above 250 mg/dL in all 8 cases, including the present case.

In summary, these two hypotheses can be considered as causes of milky CSF. First, the intraventricular hemorrhage may have caused an influx of milky blood into the CSF. In this case, a comparison of CSF TG levels measured via lumbar puncture before and after surgery may support this hypothesis. However, Wang et al. reported a case of ICH accompanied by massive milk-like serous fluid associated with hypertriglyceridemia, which was informative for our considerations.1 Second, the BBB was disrupted, and Barabási et al. reported that hyperlipidemia, as well as interleukin-6 (IL-6), contributes to the permeability of the BBB.16 Elevated IL-6 levels have been reported in patients with cerebral hemorrhage, and these interactions may increase TG levels in the CSF. Since IL-6 was not measured in all the reviewed cases, including ours, IL-6 measurement may be helpful in similar cases.

Lessons

In this study, milky CSF was unexpectedly encountered during endoscopic hematoma removal and aspirated as a specimen. Although only a small amount was available, measurement of TG levels in the CSF would have been feasible. Based on previous reports, and in the absence of infection, milky CSF is suspected to result from high concentrations of TGs eluted from the hematoma. In such a case, performing a lipid panel in blood together with lipid analysis in CSF is important for evaluation.
