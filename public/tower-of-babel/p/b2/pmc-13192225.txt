Impact of different vitamin D supplementation doses on vitamin D status and bone metabolism in preterm infants
Authors: Dilek Kurnaz, Burcu Cebeci, Hakan Cakir, Berker Okay, Derya Büyükkayhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreterm infants are at high risk for vitamin D deficiency due to limited transplacental transfer and increased postnatal requirements. Optimal vitamin D supplementation dosing in this population remains controversial.ObjectiveTo compare the effects of 400 IU/day versus 800 IU/day vitamin D supplementation on serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] levels, mineral metabolism, renal outcomes, and neonatal morbidities in preterm infants.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, 66 preterm infants born at ≤ 34 weeks’ gestation and admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) were followed. Infants were categorized into two groups according to the documented daily vitamin D supplementation regimen used in routine clinical practice (400 IU/day or 800 IU/day). Serum 25(OH)D, calcium, phosphorus, and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels were measured at admission and at 1 and 2 months postnatally. Renal ultrasonography and urinary calcium-to-creatinine (Ca/Cr) ratios were evaluated at 2 months. Neonatal morbidities, including bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), were recorded.ResultsAt admission, vitamin D deficiency or insufficiency (< 20 ng/mL) was highly prevalent in both groups. By postnatal 1 month, more than 90% of infants achieved vitamin D sufficiency, and all infants were sufficient by 2 months. The increase in serum 25(OH)D from admission to 2 months was significantly greater in the 800 IU/day group (p = 0.040). Calcium, phosphorus, and ALP levels remained within physiological ranges in both groups. No cases of vitamin D toxicity were observed, and renal ultrasonography findings were comparable between groups. In the unadjusted analysis, the incidence of BPD was lower in infants receiving 800 IU/day compared with those receiving 400 IU/day.ConclusionVitamin D supplementation with 800 IU/day appeared metabolically safe in preterm infants and resulted in greater increases in serum 25(OH)D levels compared with 400 IU/day. A lower incidence of BPD was observed in the higher-dose group, although randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings.Clinical Trial RegistrationNot applicable.

Introduction

Vitamin D deficiency is highly prevalent among preterm infants because of limited transplacental transfer during the third trimester, insufficient endogenous synthesis, and difficulty achieving adequate enteral nutrition during early hospitalization [1, 2]. These factors are particularly pronounced in infants born at lower gestational ages, who miss the critical period of maternal-fetal vitamin D accretion and therefore begin postnatal life with reduced vitamin D stores. In addition, prolonged hospitalization and minimal sunlight exposure further increase the risk of vitamin D deficiency in this vulnerable population [3].

Vitamin D plays a central role in calcium and phosphorus homeostasis, skeletal mineralization, and bone growth. In preterm infants, inadequate vitamin D status has been linked to disturbances in mineral metabolism and an increased risk of metabolic bone disease of prematurity [4, 5]. Beyond skeletal health, vitamin D has also been implicated in immune regulation, lung development, and neurodevelopment, suggesting a broader impact on early-life outcomes [6, 7]. Despite these recognized biological roles, the optimal strategy for vitamin D supplementation in preterm infants remains controversial.

Current recommendations for vitamin D supplementation in preterm infants vary widely across international guidelines. Recent European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) guidelines recommend higher daily vitamin D intakes, generally 800 to 1000 IU/day, to prevent deficiency in preterm infants [2]. In contrast, recommendations in several Western countries, including those influenced by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), continue to support lower daily doses of approximately 400 IU/day [1, 8]. This discrepancy reflects uncertainty about the balance between achieving adequate serum 25(OH)D concentrations and concerns about potential adverse effects, such as hypercalcemia and nephrocalcinosis [9].

Serum 25(OH)D concentrations measured in the first days of life in preterm infants often fall within the deficient or insufficient range, with substantial variability reported across populations and geographic regions [10, 11. Although higher supplementation doses have consistently increased serum 25(OH)D levels, evidence regarding their effects on calcium–phosphorus balance, ALP activity, and short-term neonatal morbidities remains inconsistent [8, 12, 13]. Moreover, limited data are available on the clinical significance of admission vitamin D status in relation to subsequent outcomes, while accounting for differences in supplementation dose and mineral metabolism markers.

Therefore, the present study aimed to compare the effects of two commonly used vitamin D supplementation regimens, 400 IU/day and 800 IU/day, on serum 25(OH)D concentrations and markers of bone mineral metabolism in preterm infants. A secondary objective was to evaluate the association between admission vitamin D status and neonatal clinical outcomes, with particular emphasis on safety and the absence of vitamin D toxicity. By addressing these questions in a real-world clinical setting, this study seeks to contribute to the ongoing discussion about optimal vitamin D supplementation strategies in preterm infants.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This prospective cohort study was conducted in the NICU of Haseki Training and Research Hospital. Preterm infants born at ≤ 34 weeks’ gestation and admitted to our NICU between January 2024 and October 2025 were prospectively screened for eligibility. Infants were included if they had available serum 25(OH)D measurements at hospital admission and at 1 month of postnatal age, and if they received routine vitamin D supplementation according to the unit protocol. Only infants who remained hospitalized until the first follow-up assessment were enrolled. Exclusion criteria were the presence of major congenital malformations, chromosomal abnormalities, inborn errors of metabolism, severe renal or hepatic disease, or conditions affecting calcium–phosphorus metabolism. Infants who died before the first follow-up, were transferred to another center, had feeding intolerance preventing enteral vitamin D administration, or had incomplete biochemical data were also excluded.

Vitamin D supplementation

Vitamin D supplementation was initiated after discontinuation of total parenteral nutrition (TPN) and once full enteral feeding was established. In this study, preterm infants were categorized into two groups according to the daily vitamin D dose they had received during hospitalization (400 IU/day or 800 IU/day), as documented in their medical records. Group allocation was based solely on the supplementation regimen recorded in the patient files. Oral supplementation was administered in the form of cholecalciferol drops (Devit-3 Oral Drop, Deva Company, Turkey), in which three drops contain 400 IU of vitamin D. The assigned dose was given daily by nursing staff and continued throughout the follow-up period. During the period of TPN administration, all infants received standard parenteral multivitamin supplementation (Cernevit®), which provides 220 IU of vitamin D per 5 mL. At the routine dose of 1 mL/kg/day, this corresponded to approximately 44 IU/kg/day of vitamin D. After the transition to full enteral feeding, which occurred at a mean postnatal age of 17.9 ± 9.7 days (median: 15.5 days), parenteral vitamin supplementation was discontinued and only the assigned oral vitamin D regimen was continued. Adherence to supplementation was monitored by daily medication records, and no additional vitamin D-containing preparations were administered during the study period.

Nutritional management

Enteral feeding was initiated within 1–3 days after birth as trophic feeding and advanced by 10–20 mL/kg/day according to a standardized NICU protocol. Feeding tolerance and volume advancement were assessed daily by neonatologists. Enteral nutrition consisted of preterm infant formula and/or the mother’s own breast milk when available. Once enteral intake reached 100 mL/kg/day, breast milk was fortified with a human milk fortifier providing approximately 220 IU vitamin D, 69 mg of calcium and 38 mg of phosphorus per 100 mL.

Laboratory assessments

Serum biochemical markers of mineral metabolism were measured at admission and during follow-up, including corrected serum calcium, serum phosphorus, and serum ALP. Serum 25(OH)D levels were measured at admission and at 1 and 2 months postnatally. These tests were performed as part of standard practice for preterm neonates. Urinary Ca/Cr and renal ultrasonography findings were also reviewed at 2 months postnatally to assess for nephrocalcinosis and other renal abnormalities. Laboratory findings suggestive of metabolic bone disease of prematurity were defined as serum phosphorus < 4.5 mg/dL, ALP > 450 U/L, corrected serum calcium < 8.5 mg/dL, and serum 25(OH)D < 20 ng/mL. Vitamin D status was classified according to national guidelines as deficiency (< 12 ng/mL), insufficiency (12-<20 ng/mL), sufficiency (20–100 ng/mL), and toxicity (> 100 ng/mL).

Clinical variables and morbidities

Demographic and clinical data collected included gestational age, birth weight, sex, maternal age and comorbidities, length of hospitalization, time to initiation of enteral feeding, time to full enteral feeding, and duration of TPN. Prematurity-related morbidities assessed were respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC ≥ Bell stage II), BPD, retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), and intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH ≥ grade II). BPD was defined by the need for supplemental oxygen at 28 days of life and at 36 weeks’ postmenstrual age. Intraventricular hemorrhage was classified according to Volpe’s criteria, and necrotizing enterocolitis was diagnosed using the Bell staging system.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, including mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum, frequency, and percentage values, as appropriate. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Changes in biochemical parameters over time were evaluated by calculating the difference between baseline and follow-up measurements.

The association between vitamin D dose (400 IU vs. 800 IU) and BPD was initially evaluated using the Pearson chi-square test. Logistic regression analyses were subsequently performed to determine whether the relationship between vitamin D dose and BPD persisted after adjustment for potential confounders. A univariable model was first constructed, followed by multivariable models in which gestational age and birth weight were entered sequentially, together with relevant clinical variables. Additional multivariable models including RDS, PDA, duration of TPN, and small-for-gestational-age (SGA) status were also analyzed to further assess potential clinical confounding. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) or adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and a two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For comparisons of independent continuous variables with a normal distribution, the independent samples t-test was used. When the distribution was non-normal, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Paired continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

Because the sample size was determined by the available cohort, a post hoc power analysis was performed. Using a two-sided α of 0.05 for comparison of two independent proportions, the observed difference in BPD incidence between groups (75.0% vs. 50.0%) corresponded to an achieved power of approximately 56–58%. Based on the same assumptions, a total sample size of approximately 116 infants (about 58 per group) would be required to achieve 80% power to detect a 25% absolute risk difference.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were generally comparable between infants receiving 400 IU/day and 800 IU/day of vitamin D (Table 1). Maternal factors, gestational age, birth weight, and most neonatal morbidities did not differ between groups. However, in the unadjusted analysis, the incidence of BPD was higher in the 400 IU/day group than in the 800 IU/day group (75.0% vs. 50.0%, p = 0.036). Rates of RDS, PDA, NEC (≥ stage 2), ROP (≥ stage 2), and IVH (≥ grade 2) were similar between groups.

Renal ultrasonography

At the second-month ultrasonographic evaluation, renal calcification was detected in both supplementation groups (Table 1). In the 400 IU/day group (n = 36), two infants had renal calculi and two had nephrocalcinosis. In the 800 IU/day group (n = 30), renal calculi were observed in two infants and nephrocalcinosis in four.

Vitamin D levels and renal safety

All infants with renal calculi or nephrocalcinosis had serum 25(OH)D levels ≥ 70 ng/mL, although values remained below toxic thresholds. No cases of hypercalcemia or hypercalciuria were observed, and at the second-month evaluation, the urinary Ca/Cr ratio was similar between groups (0.7 ± 0.4) (Table 1).

Vitamin D status

At admission, vitamin D deficiency or insufficiency (< 20 ng/mL) was highly prevalent. All infants in the 800 IU/day group and 83.3% in the 400 IU/day group had low 25(OH)D levels (p < 0.001). By postnatal month 1, most infants in both groups achieved vitamin D sufficiency, with no between-group difference (p = 0.652). No vitamin D toxicity was observed (Table 2).

Biochemical outcomes

Serum 25(OH)D levels at admission were significantly lower in the 800 IU/day group than in the 400 IU/day group (p < 0.001). However, levels at postnatal months 1 and 2 were comparable. The increase in 25(OH)D from admission to month 2 was greater in the 800 IU/day group (p = 0.040).

Serum calcium and phosphorus concentrations at admission and month 1 were similar between groups. At month 2, calcium levels were slightly higher in the 800 IU/day group (p = 0.04). Phosphorus increased more from admission to month 1 in the 400 IU/day group (p = 0.03). ALP levels did not differ between groups at any time point (Table 3).

Multivariable analysis of BPD

To evaluate whether the association between vitamin D dose and BPD was independent of baseline clinical differences, logistic regression analyses were performed. In the univariable model, supplementation with 800 IU/day was associated with lower odds of BPD (OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.12–0.94; p = 0.038). After adjustment for gestational age and birth weight, the association remained statistically significant (aOR 0.24, 95% CI 0.07–0.86; p = 0.028). However, when additional clinical variables reflecting disease severity (SGA, RDS, duration of TPN, and PDA) were included simultaneously in the fully adjusted model, the association remained in a protective direction but was no longer statistically significant (aOR 0.26, 95% CI 0.05–1.25; p = 0.093). Discussion

In this study, vitamin D supplementation with 800 IU/day was associated with a lower incidence of BPD compared with 400 IU/day in the unadjusted analysis, while baseline characteristics and most neonatal morbidities were generally similar between groups. Importantly, no biochemical or clinical evidence of vitamin D toxicity was observed with either supplementation regimen.

To account for potential baseline clinical differences between groups, additional multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. After adjustment for key indicators of prematurity severity, including gestational age and birth weight, the association between the higher vitamin D dose and lower odds of BPD remained statistically significant. However, when clinical variables reflecting disease severity, including SGA, RDS, duration of TPN, and PDA treatment, were included simultaneously in the fully adjusted model, the association remained in a protective direction but was no longer statistically significant. These findings suggest that the observed relationship between higher vitamin D supplementation and lower BPD incidence may be partly influenced by baseline clinical differences and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Vitamin D deficiency is highly prevalent in preterm infants due to limited transplacental transfer during the third trimester and increased postnatal requirements. International guidelines differ substantially regarding optimal supplementation doses, with ESPGHAN recommending 800–1000 IU/day, while several Western guidelines continue to support 400 IU/day because of safety concerns [1, 2]. In this context, our findings suggest that supplementation with 800 IU/day appears metabolically safe in preterm infants.

The observed association between higher vitamin D supplementation and a lower incidence of BPD is biologically plausible. Vitamin D plays an important role in lung development through its effects on alveolarization, regulation of lung growth, and modulation of pulmonary inflammation [14–19]. Vitamin D receptors are expressed in developing lung tissue, and vitamin D signaling has been shown to regulate alveolar epithelial cell proliferation, promote alveolarization, and support pulmonary vascular development. In addition, vitamin D exerts important immunomodulatory effects by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production and oxidative stress, both of which are key contributors to bronchopulmonary dysplasia pathogenesis in preterm infants. Experimental and clinical studies have suggested that vitamin D deficiency may impair lung maturation and increase susceptibility to inflammatory lung injury in preterm infants [17–19]. These biological mechanisms provide a plausible explanation for how adequate vitamin D status may contribute to improved lung maturation and potentially reduce susceptibility to bronchopulmonary dysplasia in preterm infants.

Nevertheless, previous observational studies and meta-analyses have reported inconsistent associations between vitamin D status and BPD [20, 21]. Therefore, although a greater increase in 25(OH)D levels was observed in the higher-dose group, the observational study design does not allow causal inference.

In contrast, higher vitamin D supplementation was not associated with significant differences in other prematurity-related morbidities such as NEC, PDA, RDS, IVH, or ROP, which is consistent with previous meta-analyses [22]. These conditions are multifactorial, and vitamin D status alone is unlikely to play a dominant role in their pathogenesis.

Renal calcification was observed in infants receiving both supplementation regimens, with nephrocalcinosis occurring more frequently in the 800 IU/day group. All affected infants had serum 25(OH)D concentrations ≥ 70 ng/mL; however, none showed biochemical evidence of vitamin D toxicity, including hypercalcemia or hypercalciuria. Similar Ca/Cr ratios between groups suggest that renal calcification may develop independently of urinary calcium excretion. Previous studies indicate that nephrocalcinosis in preterm infants is a multifactorial condition related to renal tubular immaturity and early postnatal exposures rather than vitamin D dose alone [23, 24]. Elevated serum 25(OH)D levels without metabolic derangements have also been reported in association with renal calcifications [25].

At admission, vitamin D deficiency or insufficiency was highly prevalent in both groups, consistent with impaired maternal-fetal transfer in preterm infants. According to the Institute of Medicine, AAP, and ESPGHAN, serum 25(OH)D concentrations ≥ 20 ng/mL are considered sufficient for infants [2, 26, 27]. Following supplementation, more than 90% of infants achieved vitamin D sufficiency by postnatal 1 month, and all infants were sufficient by 2 months. No cases of vitamin D toxicity (> 100 ng/mL) were observed, in line with previous studies [22, 28].

In the present study, the proportion of infants with vitamin D deficiency at admission was lower in the 400 IU/day group than in the 800 IU/day group. This difference most likely reflects baseline variability between the groups rather than an effect of vitamin D supplementation, as group allocation followed routine clinical practice rather than a formal randomization process. Therefore, the groups were not fully comparable at baseline. Despite this imbalance, serum 25(OH)D levels increased substantially in both groups during follow-up, and the magnitude of increase appeared greater in infants receiving the higher supplementation dose. Thus, the statement regarding a possible dose-related difference refers to the greater improvement in vitamin D status observed during follow-up rather than the baseline vitamin D distribution.

Assessment of mineral metabolism showed that both supplementation regimens improved vitamin D status without clinically relevant disturbances in calcium–phosphorus homeostasis. Although infants in the 800 IU/day group had lower baseline 25(OH)D levels, follow-up concentrations were comparable between groups, with a greater increase from admission to 2 months in the higher-dose group. This enhanced response was not accompanied by significant changes in serum calcium, phosphorus, or ALP levels [29, 30]. ALP levels increased during follow-up in both groups but remained within normal limits, suggesting physiological skeletal adaptation rather than pathological mineral imbalance [31–34].

Overall, these findings support the metabolic safety of higher-dose vitamin D supplementation in preterm infants and suggest a potential association with improved respiratory outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, group allocation was based on the vitamin D supplementation regimen used in routine clinical practice rather than a formal randomization process. In addition, the study was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size, which may limit generalizability and reduce statistical power for less frequent outcomes such as NEC, IVH, or severe ROP. Because the sample size was determined by the available cohort, an a priori sample size calculation was not performed. A post hoc power analysis suggested that the study had moderate statistical power (approximately 56–58%) to detect the observed difference in BPD incidence, indicating that larger studies would be required to confirm these findings.

Second, the follow-up period was limited to the first two postnatal months. Therefore, long-term effects on bone health, renal function, growth, and neurodevelopment could not be assessed. The persistence and clinical relevance of renal calcifications also remain uncertain.

Third, serum 25(OH)D levels were measured at predefined time points rather than continuously, limiting evaluation of peak concentrations and short-term fluctuations. Maternal vitamin D status was not available and may have influenced neonatal baseline levels. Feeding type, including the proportion of breast milk versus formula feeding, may influence vitamin D absorption and metabolism. Although feeding practices were comparable between groups in our cohort, this factor could still represent a potential confounder.

Finally, while the study suggests an association between higher vitamin D supplementation and reduced BPD incidence, causal inference cannot be established due to the observational study design. Larger, multicenter randomized trials with longer follow-up are needed to clarify whether higher vitamin D supplementation has a direct protective effect on BPD development in preterm infants.

Conclusion

In preterm infants born at ≤ 34 weeks’ gestation, vitamin D supplementation with 800 IU/day appeared to be metabolically safe and resulted in a greater increase in serum 25(OH)D levels compared with 400 IU/day. Although a lower incidence of BPD was observed in infants receiving the higher supplementation dose in unadjusted analyses, this association should be interpreted cautiously due to potential confounding factors. No evidence of vitamin D toxicity or clinically relevant disturbances in mineral metabolism was identified. Further randomized controlled studies with longer follow-up are needed to confirm these findings and to optimize vitamin D supplementation strategies in this population.
