Cutting Through the Noise: Radiation Oncology Applicant and Program Director Response to Signaling During Match 2025
Authors: Mary T Mahoney, Stephanie O Dudzinski, Jordan D Fredette, Sylvia Choo, James Murphy, Steve E Braunstein, Stephanie E Weiss, Jillian R Gunther, Jeremy G Price, Radiation Oncology Education Collaborative Study Group (ROECSG) Undergraduate Medical Education Working Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGeographic and program signals allow applicants to denote preferred regions and institutions for residency training. For the 2025 Match, program signals were introduced for radiation oncology (RO). We describe the experiences of RO applicants and program directors (PDs) with these novel program signals and geographic signals.Methods and MaterialsAnonymous REDCap surveys were distributed to RO applicants and PDs after Match Day 2025. Consented participants answered questions on their perspectives and behaviors surrounding signals.ResultsAll surveyed applicants (71, 100%) used the newly offered program signals. Many applicants (46, 65%) and PDs (25, 63%) were aware of the RO professional society guidelines regarding signaling home and away rotations. Most informed applicants did not signal their home (16/23, 70%) or all away (21/37, 57%) rotations, despite awareness of guidelines. Likewise, informed PDs advised internally (17/24, 71%) and externally rotating applicants to not signal (14/24, 58%), also against guideline recommendations. Geographic signals continued to be widely used by RO applicants (59/71, 83%), and most PDs reported using geographic signals during the selection process (30/40, 75%). Interview invitation (P = .16) and matching (P = .26) to a program within a division of a geographic signal did not differ by applicant type.ConclusionsProgram signals were universally used by surveyed RO applicants for the Match 2025, with most employing all tokens. Surveyed RO PDs reported signals influenced stages of residency recruitment, including interview invitations and ranking. Our data show that program and geographic signals may influence interview invites, but their impact on final match outcomes remains unclear. There was notably poor alignment of applicant and RO PD behaviors with the best practices regarding program signals as described by governing bodies, which should be addressed in future match cycles.

Purpose

Geographic and program signals allow applicants to denote their preferred regions and institutions, respectively, for their residency training.1 These signals were designed as one of many factors for programs to consider during the interview offer phase.2 These features were voluntarily introduced by the Electronic Residency Application Service (ERAS) for several medical specialties participating in the National Match Residency Program Main Residency Match to mitigate increased application numbers and interview invite maldistribution in the setting of the COVID-19 pandemic and step 1 pass/fail transition.3,4 Since the inception of program signals, there are emerging data that program signals decrease application numbers5 and redistribute interview invitations more evenly among increasingly competitive applicant pools.6 Likewise, there are positive impacts on interview offers and matching odds for applicants across specialties.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13

Geographic signals allow applicants to elect up to 3 United States census divisions as preferred regions.1,2 Residency programs located within an applicant’s signaled division are informed of the preference, while all other programs outside of the signaled division remain blinded.2 Alternatively, applicants can elect “no preference,” to which all programs will be informed.2 Program signals voluntarily allow applicants to alert a predefined number of institutions as preferred training programs.1 Similar to geographic signals, program signals are only visible to the programs receiving a signal, while all other programs remain blinded.2

The number and weight of the program signals can be categorized into “small signal,” “large signal,” and “2-tiered” (ie, gold or silver).5,14 “Small” and “large” signals are equally weighted and only differ in quantity offered, often <10 for “small” and >20 for “large.”5 In contrast, “2-tiered” signals offer differently weighted signals—“gold” with the highest weight and smallest allotment, and “silver” with lower weight but larger allotment.5 For the radiation oncology (RO) Match 2025, program signals were introduced and offered in limited quantities as a “small signal” specialty with 4 equally weighted signals.14

There are distinct trade-offs to these 3 signal strategies. “Small” signal specialties tend to have the highest concentration of signals to the top 10% of programs,5 while “large” signal and “2-tiered” signal specialties “filter out” applicants on review by residency programs.13 Given the potential gamification, ahead of submission of residency applications, the Association of Directors of Radiation Oncology Programs (ADROP) and the Association of Residents in Radiation Oncology published the best practice guidelines that encouraged applicants to signal home and away rotations as an effort to promote fairness.15 Home and/or away rotation(s) are not uniformly accessible for all applicants, and, as such, applicants with access should not be entitled to “save” a signal. For example, if an applicant attends an institution that has an RO residency and can accommodate 3 away rotations, then that applicant would effectively have 8 signals–double the 4 allotted.

This work captures RO applicants’ perceptions and behaviors regarding program signals. Likewise, we investigated engagement with program signals by program directors (PDs) through the recruitment process. Of particular interest was detecting adherence to society-level best practices.15

Methodology

This study was exempt from review by the Fox Chase Cancer Center IRB. Anonymous, voluntary RedCap surveys were distributed to RO applicants (Appendix E1) and PDs (Appendix E2) following Match Day 2025. Applicants were recruited through a single institutional applicant email list (n = 172) and supplemented by social media advertising on X. The total applicant pool (n = 313) was derived from ERAS 2025 data timestamped before the Supplemental Offer and Acceptance Program.16 The email list represented 55% (172/313) of the 2025 RO applicant pool. Email returned 62 completed surveys with a 36% response rate (62/172). Social media yielded an additional 11 responses; as such, there were 71 completed responses, representing 23% of the total applicant pool (n = 313). Only fully completed returned surveys were included for analysis. Response rates for multiple-choice questions were reported with frequencies and proportions. Demographic variables such as gender, race, and applicant type were compared between groups using Fisher’s Exact tests.

Results

Utilization of program signals

All surveyed RO applicants (71, 100%) used the program signals, most using all 4 signals (69, 97%). This behavior did not differ by applicant type (P = .55), gender (P = .52), or race (P = 1.0). Most programs received a program signal from at least 1 applicant (34, 85%), and PDs tended to consider this information important during the application review process (31, 78%).

Why did applicants program signal?

When asked to select up to 5 predetermined factors to signal a particular program (Appendix E1), RO applicants cited geographic location of a program (75%), strength of a program’s clinical training (61%), alignment of the program’s strength with career interests (55%), close to friends and family (54%), and program reputation/prestige (48%) (Table 2).

Interview invitations and acceptances

Most surveyed applicants who used all 4 signals received at least 1 interview from a signaled program (62, 90%). However, applicant type (United States Allopathic Medical Doctor (USMD), International Medical Graduate (IMG), Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine (DO)) was associated with interview offers, with IMGs being less likely than other applicant groups to receive an interview invite from a signaled program (69% for IMGs vs 94% for MDs vs 100% for DOs, P = .03]. Applicants agreed that they were more likely to accept an interview offer from a program that they program (46, 65%) and geographically signaled (41, 69%). PDs agreed that applicants were more likely to accept an interview offer if they program (20, 71%) and geographically (19, 48%) signaled that program.

Many PDs reported using program signals to identify an applicant whom they might otherwise overlook (11, 39%) and as a tiebreaker for interview invites (23, 70%). This behavior is appropriate per ERAS and ADROP guidance2,15; however, the possible differential application by applicant type is concerning and should be investigated further.

Perceptions regarding number of allotted signals

Surveyed RO applicants (45% for and 48% against) and PDs (55% for and 20% against) were of mixed opinion as to whether 4 signals are appropriate. Many applicants believed that 4 signals were too few (34, 48%), an opinion which was not shared by PDs (3, 8%).

Program signaling home and away rotations

A majority of surveyed applicants (46, 65%) and PDs (25, 63%) were aware of the society-level guidelines regarding home and away rotation(s). When stratified for applicants who were aware of the guidelines and could provide a home signal, a majority (16, 70%) did not signal their home program. Most informed PDs of home programs likewise advised internal applicants not to signal (17, 71%). Similar behavioral patterns arose with signaling away rotations. Of informed applicants who attended at least 1 away rotation, 19% (n = 7) did not signal any away rotations, and 38% (n = 14) only signaled some. Many informed PDs also advised rotating applicants not to signal (14, 58%).

Program signals and matching outcomes

About half of applicants reported matching at an RO residency that the program signaled (29, 41%), which, unlike interview invitations, did not differ by applicant type (MD 40%, DO 70%, IMG 23%, P = .08) (Table 3).

We detected that 55% (17) of RO PDs endorsed using these signals during rank order list discussions, which is explicitly discouraged by American Association of Medical Colleges as acknowledgment that an applicant’s program preference may be changed following the interview.2

Geographic signaling trends

This was the second Match cycle where geographic signals were available to RO applicants, with results from the initial Match 2024 cycle described elsewhere.18 Geographic signals continued to be widely used by RO applicants (59, 83%), and most PDs report using geographic signaling information (30, 75%). Most applicants received an interview invite (55, 93%) and matched (44, 62%) into a program within a region they geographically signaled. Interview invitation (P = .16) and matching (P = .26) to a program within a division of a geographic signal did not differ by applicant type. Of RO applicants who both program and geographically signaled, 39% (n = 22) matched at a program that they both program and geographically signaled, which did not differ by applicant type (P = .06).

RO PDs continued to use this signal information (30, 75%) throughout the application process, and some agreed that geographic signals helped them identify an applicant they would have otherwise overlooked (13, 43%). When asked how the program interpreted program and geographic signals, some RO PDs (10, 36%) weighted program signals more than geographic signals, which is in accordance with ADROP and Association of Residents in Radiation Oncology guidance.15

Limitations

Our survey was intentionally released following Match Day 2025 to maximize applicant participation, which introduces recall bias and survey fatigue for both applicants and PDs. The use of a single institutional applicant list limits generalizability, and a <50% response rate may introduce selection bias toward motivated applicants. The augmentation with social media introduces the theoretical possibility that a single applicant could complete the survey multiple times. There remains uncertainty on the true characteristics of the RO applicant pool. For example, our survey reported that almost 50% of applicants lack a home RO program, while another survey reports a more conservative finding of 1 in 3 applicants.19 Finally, we lack other critical elements of the application, including letters of recommendations, personal statements, board examination scores, and grades, which also influence applicants’ interview invites and matching outcomes.

Conclusions

Program signals were universally used within the surveyed RO applicant cohort for the Match 2025, with a supermajority employing all 4 newly available signals. Most applicants matched at a program they program signaled, and two-thirds matched at a program in a region they geographically signaled. Most surveyed RO PDs received at least 1 signal, which influenced both selection stages of the residency recruitment process, including both interview invitations and ranking. We detected significant deviation from society's published best practices regarding the signaling of home programs and away rotations. Further work in revisiting or communicating expectations regarding program signaling for home and away programs is warranted. The influence of both program and geographic signals on RO interview yield and matching outcomes is still premature and may continue to evolve given the dynamic landscape.
