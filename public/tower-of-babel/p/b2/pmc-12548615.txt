Ketogenic diet strategy in patients with sepsis: a multicentre prospective randomised interventional trial protocol

Introduction

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection.1 It has a high incidence and mortality rate worldwide, impacting an estimated 30 million individuals annually and potentially leading to around 6 million fatalities.2 Despite the advanced concepts in intensive care, the mortality rate for sepsis remains as high as 30%–50% over the past decade.3 Current treatment for sepsis primarily includes early initiation of antibiotic therapy, haemodynamic management, mechanical ventilation (MV) and renal replacement therapy as supportive measures.4 Nutritional intervention is traditionally viewed as life sustaining; however, it has garnered increasing attention as a therapeutic strategy in recent years.5 6 Nutritional intervention, being a cost-effective and easily applicable approach, can modulate the energy metabolism of patients, which plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis and progression of various diseases.79

The ketogenic diet is characterised by high fat and low carbohydrate intake, inducing systemic ketosis by elevating plasma ketone body levels.10 Ketone bodies serve as an alternative substrate for energy production in critically ill patients,5 enhancing ATP generation and reducing muscle protein breakdown during states of stress.11 Ketone bodies include acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) and acetone, with BHB being the predominant component, accounting for approximately 70% of the total ketone bodies.12 A clear correlation has been found between BHB levels and survival in human sepsis. The BHB levels in sepsis survivors are significantly higher than those in non-survivors.13 Ketone metabolism mitigates oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes by preventing the excessive generation and accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibiting lipid peroxidation, enhancing protein antioxidant capacity and improving mitochondrial function, thereby promoting ATP synthesis.14 Additionally, BHB exerts its anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, thereby preventing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β and interleukin-18.15 Recent studies have shown that exogenous supplementation of ketone esters can alleviate sepsis-induced inflammation in the heart, kidneys and liver.16

The ketogenic diet was originally developed as a treatment for epilepsy and is recognised as an effective clinical tool for reducing seizure frequency with good tolerability in patients.17 18 In recent years, the application of the ketogenic diet has expanded to include the treatment of other conditions, such as cancer,19 20 heart failure21 and ischaemia-reperfusion injury.22 Additionally, recent studies have confirmed the role of the ketogenic diet in inflammatory diseases. Sukkar et al study indicated that in COVID-19 patients, there was a notable difference in interleukin-6 levels between the Td0 and Td7 in the ketogenic diet treatment group.6 Animal studies further corroborate these findings, demonstrating that supplementing BHB in mice with severe COVID-19 decreased the mortality rate from 70% to 30%.23 Furthermore, McNelly’s research confirmed that inducing a ketogenic state in patients experiencing physiological instability is both feasible and safe.5 A single-centre randomised controlled trial by Rahmel et al3 demonstrated that safely achieving a stable ketogenic state in patients with sepsis is possible. Additionally, patients in the ketogenic diet group had a longer duration without MV, vasopressors and dialysis and fewer days in the intensive care unit (ICU) compared with the control group. However, these studies did not report any protective effects of the ketogenic diet on organ function.

In the prospective multicentre randomised controlled trial, we aim to increase the sample size to assess the impact of a ketogenic diet on sepsis patients. Furthermore, we will explore the protective effects of the ketogenic diet against sepsis-induced multiple-organ dysfunction.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This prospective, multicentre, randomised, open-label trial is planned to be conducted in the ICUs of five centres: Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital Affiliated to Yangzhou University, Jiangdu People’s Hospital, Yizheng People’s Hospital, Gaoyou People’s Hospital and Jingjiang People’s Hospital. It will compare an intervention group receiving a ketogenic diet with a control group receiving standard enteral nutrition. This study aims to investigate the protective effects of a ketogenic diet on organ function in critically ill patients with sepsis. This study is expected to start in January 2025 and conclude in June 2026. The study duration for each enrolled patient is 7 days, including randomisation, intervention and data collection. The study concludes on day 7, or death or discharge from ICU, whichever occurs first. This randomised controlled trial protocol follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2013 statement (online supplemental file 1).

Study population

The inclusion criteria are as follows: age ≥18 years, meeting the diagnostic criteria for sepsis 3.0, diagnosis of sepsis not more than 72 hours at the time of ICU admission, enteral nutrition is feasible within 48 hours of ICU admission, and obtaining informed consent from the patient or legal guardian.

The exclusion criteria are as follows: pregnancy or lactation, autoimmune diseases, severe liver dysfunction or liver failure (Child C), refractory metabolic acidosis (pH <7.0 persisting for more than 2 hours and difficult to correct), presence of contraindications to enteral nutrition, and allergy to any components of the enteral nutrition formulation. The discontinuation criteria are as follows: cases in which the patient’s condition worsens or other circumstances arise that may lead the principal investigator to deem continued participation in the study inappropriate; cases where participation is discontinued at the request of the investigator; cases found to be in severe violation of eligibility criteria after enrolment; or cases where the investigator deems continued participation inappropriate, or the study is terminated. Participants will show their comprehension, agreement and readiness to take part in the study by signing the informed consent document (refer to online supplemental file 2).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the formulation of the research questions, outcome measures or study design.

Randomisation

Block-balanced randomisation, in a 1:1 ratio (n=133 intervention group; n=133 control group), is computer generated by an independent statistician who is not involved in patient recruitment or clinical management with random block sizes between n=8 and n=16, additionally using random permutations of treatments within each block. The researchers will remain blinded to the allocation before patient enrolment.

Intervention and study procedures

Following inclusion and randomisation, patients in the two groups received different enteral nutrition formulas. Patients in the intervention group received a ketogenic formulation (SHENZHEN ZENECA BIOTECHNOLOGY, Shenzhen, China) with 8 g of carbohydrates per 100 g. The control group received Peptisorb enteral nutrition solution (NUTRICIA, Jiangsu, China) with 17.6 g of carbohydrates per 100 mL.

The nutritional energy target calculations were based on weight according to guidelines.24 The attending physician is responsible for ensuring the achievement of energy targets. The initial infusion rate of the enteral nutrition formula in the control group was set at 20 mL/h. If no significant gastric retention (defined as ≥500 mL) was observed, the rate was increased by 20 mL/h every 6 hours. In the intervention group, the nutritional powder was dissolved in warm water (60–80 mL per packet) and administered via intermittent bolus feeding through a gastric tube. In cases of significant gastric retention, metoclopramide and/or erythromycin could be administered to promote gastric motility. As soon as the patients are capable of consuming oral food, the intervention group receives an oral ketogenic drink solution, whereas the control group is provided with a standardised healthy diet. Furthermore, the physician guided patient treatment following sepsis guidelines,4 including fluid resuscitation, early antibiotic administration, haemodynamic monitoring, MV, anticoagulation, sedation and analgesia, and other multimodal integrated therapies. On day 1 (study inclusion), day 3, day 5 and day 7, or at the end of the ketogenic diet, clinical parameters were collected from the patients to evaluate efficacy indicators such as inflammatory cytokine levels and ketone body levels.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined according to the proportion of patients who encountered adverse outcomes (30-day mortality rate).3 Assuming a 40% risk of adverse outcomes in the control group,3 the sample size was determined accordingly. Considering a 20% dropout rate,25 a total sample size of n=266 (n=133 per group) is necessary to achieve a power of 95%. To confirm that this sample size was also sufficient for the primary outcome (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score), a validation calculation was performed. Based on published literature indicating a clinically relevant mean difference of 2.5 points in SOFA scores,3 and setting a significance level (α) of 0.05 with 90% power, a minimum sample size of 110 was calculated (including dropout allowance). The final sample size of 266 was therefore adopted; it ensures robust power for the primary SOFA score analysis while adequately powering the mortality endpoint.

Data collection

Clinical and demographic data of enrolled patients will be collected from the medical record system, including sources of infection, comorbidities, length of stay in ICU and hospital, etc. Data from routine measurements will be recorded and organised during the study period. Data will be collected in paper case report forms and then transferred anonymously to a secure database. Access to all research materials and data is restricted to the members of the research team. All study data will be permanently destroyed 5 years after the completion of the study.

Monitoring and adverse events (AEs) management

The monitoring committee is composed of an investigator from Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital due to no sponsor. This committee will conduct audits via monthly online meetings and semi-annual site visits. Adverse events (AEs) refer to any unintended or harmful clinical manifestations occurring after treatment. Investigators will gather information on abnormal reactions at 60-day intervals. All details including the time of occurrence, severity, duration, suspected causes, measures taken and outcomes will be documented on paper case report forms. In the event of an AE, the attending physician will implement emergency measures immediately and report the incident to the principal investigators and ethics committee within 24 hours.

Statistics analysis

The baseline characteristics of patients will be outlined for each group. Categorical variables will be presented using frequency and percentage, while continuous variables will undergo normality testing. If they follow a normal distribution, they will be expressed as mean±SD; if not, they will be represented as median and IQR. Categorical variables will be compared between groups using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, while continuous variables will be compared using Student’s t-test or non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. A p value of <0.05 will be regarded as statistically significant.

Ethics and dissemination

It has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital Affiliated to Yangzhou University (No. 2024ky290-2). The project was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry in December 2024 (Registration Number: ChiCTR2400093805). The trial will follow the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant Chinese laws and regulations. Study results will be disseminated via scientific conferences and peer-reviewed publications.

Discussion

This trial will evaluate the role of a ketogenic diet in organ function protection in patients with sepsis. The results of this trial will contribute to providing new treatment strategies for sepsis patients, improving organ dysfunction and ultimately enhancing the prognosis of these patients.

Sepsis frequently results in systemic multiple-organ dysfunction. Excessive inflammation and the generation of ROS are key maladaptive responses in sepsis that are associated with organ dysfunction and unfavourable outcomes.25 The incidence of myocardial injury among sepsis patients ranges from 13.8% to 40%, with mortality rates soaring between 70% and 90%.26 In addition, sepsis is associated with acute kidney injury in up to 50% of cases, with as many as 60% of sepsis patients experiencing acute kidney injury.27 Protecting organ function in sepsis is essential for improving the prognosis of these patients.

Preliminary studies suggest that ketone bodies may have potential protective effects in both the heart and kidneys. In the early stages of structural heart disease, insufficient energy production from glycolysis fails to meet the heart’s demands for maintaining normal physiological functions, thereby exacerbating the progression of heart failure. At this stage, ketone bodies, recognised as the second most efficient energy substrate after glucose, are increasingly used by cardiomyocytes. Exogenous supplementation of ketone bodies may enhance the prognosis of heart failure, while a ketogenic diet may prevent or even reverse ventricular remodelling.28 29 Moreover, the ketogenic diet can enhance myocardial contractility and improve cardiac function by reducing fatty acid oxidation and oxygen consumption, ultimately decreasing the incidence of cardiovascular events.30 Torres et al have found that dietary interventions or ketone body supplementation can prevent renal fibrosis.31 Furthermore, a study on preventive ketogenic diet feeding in rats demonstrated that a 3-day ketogenic diet can alleviate acute renal ischaemic injury, improve renal function and prevent renal fibrosis.32

In addition to the ketogenic diet, exogenous ketone levels can also be elevated through direct supplementation with ketone salts and ketone esters. A study investigating ketone salt dosages found that a 40 mmol/kg/day dose is near the toxic threshold.33 Indeed, increasing the dose by 20% daily led to increased organ damage and mortality due to excessive Na+ intake.34 This limitation restricts the use of ketone salts in critically ill patients. Ketone esters represent another approach to increasing ketone body levels. A study investigating ketone ester infusion to prevent sepsis-induced muscle weakness found that, following bolus injection, septic mice had impaired clearance of 3-hydroxybutyrate (3-HB).34 In critically ill patients, impaired mitochondrial dysfunction may affect the metabolism of 3HB, potentially leading to fatal metabolic acidosis.35 Although continuous ketone ester infusion alleviated this toxic side effect,34 critically ill patients are at an increased risk due to the coexistence of metabolic and electrolyte imbalances, as well as concurrent organ failure.

The beneficial effects of ketogenic diets in chronic cardiac and renal diseases have been established. Furthermore, the safety and efficacy of inducing a stable ketogenic state through a ketogenic diet in sepsis patients have been demonstrated. However, research on the organ-protective effects of a ketogenic diet in sepsis is currently lacking. This study aims to assess the role of a ketogenic diet in protecting organ function in patients with sepsis.

This study has several limitations. First, the open-label nature introduces the potential for performance and detection bias. Second, implementing a complex dietary intervention uniformly across multiple centres presents a challenge. Finally, the generalisability of our findings may be limited to ICU patients who meet our specific eligibility criteria and are treated in settings similar to the participating centres.

The project is currently ongoing, and recruitment of participants began in January 2025.
