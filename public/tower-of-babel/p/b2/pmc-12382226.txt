Development and validation of a deep learning model for early detection and screening of diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Feifei Cao, Xitong Guo, Meng Li, ShuJu Li, Xin Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly diagnosis and screening of diabetic retinopathy (DR) are crucial for reducing medical burdens and conserving healthcare resources. This study introduces an advanced AI-assisted recognition system designed to enhance the detection of DR lesions through innovative automatic learning methods. Central to our approach are agnostic text instruction templates, which facilitate zero-shot DR detection by integrating text embeddings with visual information. Our system performs comprehensive lesion detection by leveraging similarity mapping at both the image and patch levels, enabling it to identify a wide range of diabetic retinopathy (DR) lesions without the need for extensive annotated data. This AI-assisted system distinguishes itself from traditional fully supervised models and few-shot learning approaches by addressing the complexities of DR image annotation and safeguarding patient privacy. To validate the system’s effectiveness, we conducted extensive experiments across five internal and publicly available test sets, as well as an external test set captured using smartphone devices. Our evaluation involved performance analysis of various pre-training methods, including detailed patch-level visualizations and t-SNE clustering techniques to assess the quality of feature embeddings. The results of our zero-shot experiments reveal that our system outperforms conventional transfer learning-based DR detection methods. This superiority is evident in both the pre-training and testing phases, showcasing the system’s ability to deliver accurate and reliable DR lesion detection while circumventing the limitations of traditional approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03117-1.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) remains one of the leading causes of preventable blindness worldwide, with profound implications for both patient quality of life and the strain it places on healthcare systems [42]. The early detection and diagnosis of DR are crucial in mitigating this burden, as timely intervention can prevent irreversible vision loss [11]. In light of this, developing advanced lesion detection systems for DR holds great potential for improving clinical outcomes by facilitating early diagnosis and treatment.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has shown substantial promise in augmenting DR detection capabilities [20]. However, traditional AI models face several challenges due to their reliance on large, labeled datasets for training. These challenges include the labor-intensive process of data annotation, the high costs associated with accurate labeling, and the difficulty in generalizing these models to new diagnostic scenarios due to overdependence on specific training data [12]. Furthermore, medical image annotation requires expert knowledge, and privacy concerns surrounding patient data present additional obstacles in developing effective AI-based solutions.

To address these limitations, zero-shot learning (ZSL) has emerged as a promising approach in medical image analysis. ZSL enables AI models to detect and classify lesions without requiring disease-specific labeled datasets by leveraging prior knowledge and identifying generalizable patterns. This method enhances the adaptability of AI models across different diagnostic scenarios, improving their ability to recognize DR lesions in varied clinical settings without extensive labeled data [43].

Recent advancements in the field have further demonstrated the effectiveness of AI in DR detection. For instance, researchers have successfully applied transfer learning with optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging to enhance DR diagnosis, overcoming challenges posed by small and medium-sized datasets using pre-trained models [17]. Similarly [5], has employed deep learning algorithms on color fundus photography (CFP) images for DR detection, showcasing the adaptability of these models across various ethnic groups. Additionally, Google DeepMind achieved a 95% accuracy rate in DR detection using a two-stage deep neural network for both lesion segmentation and grade classification on OCT images. This underscores the robustness of their approach across different imaging modalities [16]. Despite these advances, however, these methodologies share a common reliance on large-scale, manually annotated datasets, which are costly and time-consuming to produce.

The reliance on human-annotated data introduces additional complications. Labeling quality may vary due to the subjective nature of medical image interpretation, and handling medical data raises significant privacy concerns, necessitating stringent safeguards to protect patient confidentiality [36]. In response to these challenges, our study proposes a zero-shot learning-based approach for DR detection, which minimizes reliance on manually labeled data while addressing concerns about privacy. By leveraging the ZSL framework, our method provides a promising solution for enhancing DR lesion detection without requiring extensive, annotated datasets.

Most existing deep learning (DL) approaches focus on detecting moderate non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) or more severe stages of DR, which are more likely to cause vision-threatening conditions [12]. However, early-stage DR, particularly mild NPDR, is often underrepresented in screening protocols. Given the progressive and irreversible nature of DR, early detection is critical for preventing disease advancement, which significantly increases the risk of blindness if left untreated [29]. Research indicates that timely intervention, alongside effective management of key metabolic factors such as blood glucose levels, blood pressure, and lipid profiles, can prevent or even reverse the progression of mild NPDR [4].

Despite the recognized benefits of early detection, current supervised and transfer learning models remain constrained by the high costs and privacy concerns associated with medical data labeling. To overcome these challenges, we introduce an innovative AI framework inspired by advances in contrastive learning and large visual language models, such as those trained on datasets like ImageNet. Our model harnesses paired text-image medical data to pre-train both text and image encoders, using custom-designed text instruction templates specifically tailored for DR detection tasks. This framework enables zero-shot transfer learning, allowing the system to generalize across diverse diagnostic scenarios with minimal reliance on disease-specific annotations.

In this study, we present a novel AI system employing object-agnostic prompt learning for DR detection. This system uses universal prompts to bypass the need for extensive, manually annotated datasets, allowing for more efficient and scalable detection capabilities. The zero-shot learning framework enhances the model’s ability to generalize across different DR detection tasks, reducing the need for specialized annotations for each dataset. By incurporating prompt learning and minimizing dependence on large-scale labeled data, this system addresses the critical challenges of medical data annotation and privacy concerns while advancing the field of AI-driven DR detection. Furthermore, the system’s ability to accurately detect early-stage DR lesions, particularly Microaneurysms, marks a significant step forward in preventing DR progression and improving patient outcomes through early intervention.

Research background

DR detection based on deep learning Deep learning (DL) models have gained substantial attention in medical image analysis due to their ability to extract intricate, hierarchical features from complex medical images. In the context of diabetic retinopathy (DR) detection, DL models play a pivotal role in automating lesion identification and diagnosis, thus reducing the diagnostic burden on healthcare professionals. The current state-of-the-art approaches for DR detection predominantly focus on lesion area segmentation and transfer learning techniques, both of which have shown promising results in detecting retinal abnormalities. Lesion area segmentation models can be broadly categorized into two types: encoder-decoder architectures and Atrous convolution-based models. Encoder-decoder structures, such as those used in [10, 37], encode the input image into a latent feature space and then decode it back into a segmentation map, highlighting critical lesion areas. This layered approach is effective in locating regions affected by DR. In contrast, Atrous convolution models, as demonstrated in [6], use dilated convolutions to capture multi-scale contextual information while maintaining computational efficiency. This technique is particularly useful for detecting small but clinically significant lesions, such as Microaneurysms, which are early indicators of DR progression.

To improve model robustness, researchers have also experimented with data augmentation techniques to diversify the training dataset. Common methods include image rotation, flipping, and adding noise [43]. While these techniques introduce variability, they often fail to generate realistic samples that reflect the full complexity of DR lesions, thereby limiting their effectiveness in improving model performance. Transfer learning is another widely adopted strategy in DR detection, particularly in addressing the scarcity of labeled medical data. For example [12], applied transfer learning by pre-training a neural network on large-scale datasets like ImageNet and then fine-tuning it on DR-specific data. This approach leverages feature representations learned in the source domain to improve DR detection in the target domain.

Two additional techniques within the domain of transfer learning are gaining traction: semi-supervised transfer learning and cross-domain adaptation. Semi-supervised transfer learning addresses the challenge of limited labeled data by leveraging large amounts of unlabeled data to enhance model training [39]. Cross-domain adaptation transfers knowledge from a well-annotated domain (e.g., OCT imaging) to a less annotated domain (e.g., fundus photographs), which allows the model to generalize across imaging modalities [31]. However, both methods face challenges, particularly when there is a significant domain gap between the source and target datasets, leading to suboptimal feature transfer. This gap, combined with heterogeneous data distributions, poses a significant challenge for transfer learning applications in medical imaging [20].

Text-driven contrastive visual learning

Contrastive learning, which involves training models to minimize the distance between similar samples while maximizing the distance between dissimilar ones, has shown great potential in various computer vision tasks [40]. In unsupervised representation learning, this method is particularly effective, enabling models to learn rich, discriminative feature representations without the need for extensive labeled datasets. In medical imaging, contrastive learning has been applied to visual language models (VLMs), which incorporate text instruction learning to enhance both the interpretability and precision of DR detection. For example, models like CONVIRT and CONVIRT-AC [43] utilize text instructions that capture target semantics but struggle to fully capture the complexity of abnormal lesion semantics. This limitation results in suboptimal detection performance. More recent models, such as WinCLIP [19] and VAND [9], introduce manually defined text instructions, enhancing the identification of specific anomalies but lacking flexibility across different lesion types.

CoOp [44] addresses some of these shortcomings by employing learnable text instructions, which enable the model to generalize better by capturing global exception semantics. However, it still struggles with capturing local exception semantics, which is essential for fine-grained anomaly detection. ANOMALYCLIP [45] further advances the field by learning text instructions specifically designed for identifying unknown objects or abnormalities, achieving superior performance in zero-shot anomaly detection and segmentation tasks. This method excels at identifying abnormal regions in medical images, even when the model has not previously encountered those anomalies during training [38].

Zero-shot anomaly detection (ZSAD) ZSAD is a state-of-the-art technique developed to identify anomalies that fall outside of the categories included in the training dataset [2]. Unlike traditional supervised learning, which relies on extensively annotated datasets, ZSAD detects anomalies based on patterns learned from normal samples or by leveraging prior knowledge. This approach is especially valuable in medical applications, where abnormal cases may be rare or difficult to annotate comprehensively. Recent advancements in transfer learning have significantly enhanced the performance of ZSAD systems. For instance, models pre-trained on large-scale datasets like BERT and Vision Transformer have demonstrated strong capabilities in creating generalizable feature representations that can be applied to new, unseen data for anomaly detection [27]. These models rely on learning from vast amounts of data, enabling them to detect deviations from normative patterns that signal the presence of anomalies. In this study, zero-shot learning (ZSL) aims to leverage pre-training on semantically annotated medical imaging datasets (e.g., chest radiographs) to establish semantic relationships between images and textual descriptors. This acquired knowledge is then transferred to diabetic retinopathy (DR) fundus image analysis, enabling the model to predict novel categories despite fundus images being entirely absent from the training data - thus constituting a zero-shot learning paradigm.

CLIP, originally developed for zero-shot image recognition, has proven highly effective in pre-training for ZSAD by adapting to diverse target datasets through its integration of auxiliary data [32]. Building on CLIP’s success, text instruction learning modules have been refined to improve ZSAD performance, particularly in medical image analysis. However, the manual annotation of text prompts for zero-shot image retrieval tasks can be inefficient and often results in low detection accuracy [14]. To address this, learnable linear projection techniques have been introduced, enhancing the local semantics of image visualizations and improving efficiency in anomaly detection tasks [15].

This study introduces a zero-shot learning (ZSL) framework specifically designed for diabetic retinopathy (DR) detection, aiming to improve diagnostic accuracy while minimizing dependence on large, annotated datasets. By applying ZSAD techniques and text-driven contrastive learning, the proposed system seeks to enhance the detection of DR lesions—particularly early-stage lesions such as Microaneurysms—thereby enabling earlier interventions and improving patient outcomes.

Methodology

Comparative pre-training of text and image encoders

The proposed methodology begins with comparative pre-training using a dual encoder framework consisting of a text encoder and an image encoder (Radford et al., [32], both based on state-of-the-art neural network architectures. The image encoder utilizes the Vision Transformer (ViT), known for its superior performance in image classification tasks, while the text encoder employs BERT (Devlin [13], ), optimized for natural language processing tasks. In this application, the text encoder is pre-trained using ClinicalBERT, (Alsentzer et al., [1] a domain-specific variant of BERT tailored for processing clinical text. The image encoder leverages ResNet50, a deep residual network architecture widely used in medical image analysis [18].

Given the highly specialized nature of medical text and the intra-class similarity of medical images, we adopt the CLIP framework [32], which maximizes cosine similarity between text and image embeddings. This ensures a closer alignment between textual descriptions of diabetic retinopathy (DR) lesions and their corresponding visual features. Both paired and unpaired data consisting of medical images and their associated clinical texts are utilized during training to enhance the model’s ability to detect DR. For training efficiency, we adopt a selective fine-tuning approach: the text encoder’s initial layers are frozen, and only the final six layers are fine-tuned, tailoring the encoder to DR detection while avoiding overfitting to early-stage features. Construction of text prompts for DR detection

To improve the classification of different DR lesions, we developed a text-based instruction template specifically designed to optimize zero-shot detection. This approach is based on the principle that text instructions can help a model recognize unseen classes [34]. The text instruction template integrates categorical labels such as “Microaneurysm,” which are embedded by the text encoder to enhance classification accuracy [24].

Existing templates, such as the general “A photo of a [cls]” template, have been effective in aligning text and image embeddings [25]. However, these templates often fail to capture critical abnormal visual features, which are essential for medical anomaly detection. To address this, we created agnostic text instruction templates that focus on detecting salient features of anomalies without being limited to specific categories [45]. These templates enable the model to detect abnormal features across multiple DR lesion types while maintaining generalizability to other medical conditions, thereby enhancing cross-domain zero-shot anomaly detection (ZSAD).

We further improved the DR detection performance by incorporating specific abnormal text information into the instruction template. For example, a lesion such as a Microaneurysm or hemorrhage might be described as “A photo of a DR with a Microaneurysm.” However, such templates are limited in their ability to capture general abnormal features. Refinement of local visualization regions

Detecting fine-grained anomalies in medical images is challenging due to the subtle visual differences between normal and abnormal tissues. To address this, we implemented the Diagonally Prominent Attention Map (DPAM) technique, which enhances the traditional attention mechanisms used in image encoders [45].In standard attention mechanisms, the Query-Key (Q-K) attention process is central. However, we replaced it with a more refined framework that includes Query-Query (Q-Q), Key-Key (K-K), and Value-Value (V-V) self-attention mechanisms. The V-V attention mechanism was found to be particularly effective in capturing local visual features critical for distinguishing subtle abnormalities [15]. By focusing on the relationships between value representations, this method enhances the model’s ability to localize and distinguish subtle anomalies, leading to more accurate anomaly detection in medical images.

Global and local instruction optimization

To maximize DR detection performance, we propose a joint optimization strategy that enhances both global and local text-visual alignment. The global context optimization aligns text embeddings related to unknown target anomalies with visual embeddings across all relevant regions of an image. This ensures that the model captures the overall image context, improving its ability to detect both normal and abnormal areas. Local context optimization focuses on refining text instructions to better identify localized abnormal regions, even when these regions are rare or small. We employ focal loss, which emphasizes difficult-to-classify cases while reducing the impact of well-classified examples, effectively mitigating class imbalance—a common issue in medical anomaly detection where abnormal cases are rare compared to normal ones.

To further enhance the accuracy of segmentation, we incorporate Dice loss, a measure of the overlap between predicted abnormal regions and ground truth annotations. Dice loss is well-suited for medical image analysis, as it evaluates the model’s ability to capture fine details like lesion boundaries, improving anomaly segmentation performance. Experiment

Experiment setup

Datasets During the pre-training phase, we utilized two key datasets: the MIMIC-CXR dataset [21] and the CBIS-DDSM dataset. Both datasets include paired text descriptions, which aid in learning the associations between medical images and their corresponding textual information. For the fine-tuning phase, we employed the MURA-1.1, which is specifically tailored for diabetic retinopathy (DR) detection. The dataset was divided into training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio to ensure robust model evaluation and to optimize the performance of the DR detection system.

To assess the model’s zero-shot performance, we conducted comprehensive evaluations using both internal and external test datasets. Internal Tests: Several public datasets from the DR domain were used, including DRIVE, ODIR-5k, FIRE, STARE, and CHASE-DB1. These datasets are widely recognized in DR research and provide a broad range of DR images, which is essential for evaluating model accuracy and robustness. External Test: For external validation, we utilized the IDRiD. This dataset was obtained via smartphone imaging technology and consists of color fundus images (CFP). It does not include manual annotations, making it ideal for testing the model’s performance in a real-world, less controlled setting. This enhances the generalizability of our findings. This comprehensive evaluation across diverse datasets ensures that the model’s performance is thoroughly assessed under a variety of conditions and data types.

Data preprocessing and evaluation metrics To ensure the quality and consistency of the input data, several preprocessing steps and evaluation metrics were employed: Size Normalization: All input images are resized to 336 × 336 pixels to maintain consistency across the dataset. This standardization is crucial for uniform data processing and model training, ensuring that all images conform to the same resolution specifications. Image Enhancement: Various image enhancement techniques are applied to improve the visibility of pathological features and mitigate variations in lighting and color arising from different imaging devices. Specifically, Contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) [30] is used to enhance contrast and reveal fine details in the images. Color normalization [26] is applied to standardize color variations across images, reducing discrepancies that could negatively impact the performance of deep learning algorithms. Sensitivity and Specificity are key metrics for evaluating the model’s performance. Sensitivity measures the model’s ability to correctly identify positive cases (true positives), while specificity measures its accuracy in identifying negative cases (true negatives).Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC): AUROC provides a comprehensive evaluation of the model’s ability to distinguish between positive and negative cases across various thresholds. Average Precision (AP) assesses the model’s performance at the image level, with a focus on the precision-recall trade-off.AUPRO: used for evaluating image segmentation tasks, measuring the area under the precision-recall curve for segmented regions.

Implementation details

For the text encoder, we employed BERT [41] for pre-training, with ClinicalBERT [1] used to initialize the weights. ClinicalBERT, which is optimized for clinical language processing, is well-suited for handling medical text data. The image encoder is pre-trained using ResNet50 [18], an architecture known for its ability to effectively extract significant features from medical images, particularly in identifying abnormal characteristics [8].

During the detection phase, a similarity score is used to evaluate the anomaly score. This score approaches 1 when the text embedding for anomalies is well-aligned with the global visual embedding, indicating a high correspondence between the identified anomaly and the visual features of the image. To achieve fine-grained detection, both normal and abnormal segments are integrated from all intermediate layers of the network. This approach facilitates a comprehensive analysis of image features across various levels of abstraction, leading to more accurate anomaly detection. To further enhance the model’s ability to capture detailed image information, we employed the Diagonally Prominent Attention Map (DPAM) module [45]. The DPAM module refines visual information by incorporating diagonal attention mechanisms, which improve the detection of subtle and fine-grained anomalies. The text instruction length is set to 14 tokens, optimizing the balance between capturing sufficient semantic detail and maintaining computational efficiency. The depth of the learned text embedding is set to 9 layers, providing a robust representation of text instructions. Additionally, the visual encoder comprises 4 layers, which are configured to effectively process and integrate image features, ensuring accurate anomaly detection [45].

To evaluate the zero-shot detection capability of the proposed DR_OAPL method, we conducted experiments using five publicly available internal diabetic retinopathy (DR) test sets and one external test set. The results of these experiments are summarized in Table 1. The DR_OAPL method exhibited competitive performance across the internal test sets. Comparison with transfer model detection performance

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) detection methods are varied, but mainstream approaches typically follow a standard process. Initially, these methods utilize convolutional neural networks (CNNs) pre-trained on ImageNet to extract generic features. These features are then adapted for DR lesion detection. Notable transfer learning models that have demonstrated significant performance include Fully Convolutional Networks (FCN) [28], SegNet [3], U-Net [33], DeepLabV3 [7], and DeepDR [12].

We conducted a comparative analysis between these transfer learning models and the DR_OAPL method. The results of this comparison are detailed in Table 2, encompassing both the fine-tuning and testing phases of detection. Our findings indicate that DR_OAPL delivers superior performance across both phases. Although transfer learning models generally excel during the fine-tuning phase, they experience notable performance degradation during testing. This degradation is likely due to the models being primarily trained on the ImageNet dataset, which may not adequately capture the specific characteristics of DR data. Medical imaging, particularly for DR, often involves unique features that are not well-represented by general pre-training datasets. In contrast, DR_OAPL maintains robust performance throughout the testing phase. This enhanced performance is attributed to the incorporation of an unknown object text learning module and contrastive learning techniques. These components enable DR_OAPL to effectively capture significant abnormal features across various lesions, leading to improved accuracy in regional identification and overall detection performance.

Performance of models on different lesions

The detection of different lesions in diabetic retinopathy (DR) is particularly challenging due to the high similarity in features among various types. To assess the diagnostic performance of each model in detail, we visualized the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves for different lesion types, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The results reveal that DR_OAPL excels in detecting Microaneurysms, hemorrhages, and hard exudates—critical indicators in the early stages of DR. Notably, Microaneurysms, which are often difficult to detect, achieved an Area Under the ROC Curve (AUROC) of 95.19% with DR_OAPL, while hard exudates reached an AUROC of 96.25%. This high performance can be attributed to the use of a patch-level cutting technique, which allows the model to concentrate on local features of abnormalities, thus improving detection accuracy. In comparison, the DeepDR model also showed strong results in detecting Microaneurysms and hard exudates, with AUROC values of 94.04% and 95.07%, respectively. However, DeepDR’s reliance on the ImageNet dataset for training and its use of three subnetworks for feature extraction contribute to a relatively high training cost. This makes DR_OAPL not only more effective in specific lesion detection but also more efficient in terms of training resources.

Comparison of clustering effects of different pre-training methods

Current mainstream diabetic retinopathy (DR) detection methods generally utilize image encoders that are pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset, which are subsequently adapted for DR-specific tasks. However, emerging research [43, 45] indicates that pre-training on medical image datasets may provide more accurate detection outcomes. To evaluate this hypothesis, we conducted a t-SNE clustering analysis comparing pre-training on ImageNet with pre-training on medical image datasets. The results of this analysis are illustrated in Fig. 5. In our study, both ConVIRT [43] and DR_OAPL were pre-trained using the MIMIC-CXR chest radiograph dataset [21] and the CBIS-DDSM. A notable difference between these methods is that DR_OAPL incorporates an Object-Agnostic Prompt Tuning (OAPT) module. The t-SNE clustering analysis demonstrates that in-domain pre-training methods, such as those used by ConVIRT and DR_OAPL, achieve more effective clustering of diseases compared to ImageNet-based methods. Specifically, in-domain methods exhibit better clustering performance for diseases like Microaneurysms, which are often challenging to identify using general pre-training approaches. These results corroborate the findings of previous studies, underscoring the advantages of domain-specific pre-training for DR detection.

Although the primary focus of DR_OAPL is its zero-shot transfer capability, its performance in few-shot learning scenarios is also significant, especially given the challenges of acquiring limited labeled data in the medical field. To evaluate this, we examined the impact of various few-shot settings on the model’s detection performance during the fine-tuning stage and compared it to other transfer learning models. The specific few-shot settings included 1, 2, and 4 labeled samples, with results detailed in Table 4. The findings reveal that DR_OAPL retains strong lesion recognition ability even with limited labeled data. Ablation study

Impact of different modules on performance

To evaluate the impact of each module on diagnostic performance, we conducted ablation experiments, with results illustrated in Fig. 7. The base model (Base) serves as the reference for comparison. Module M1 incorporates the CONVIRT framework for comparison pre-training into the base model. Module M2 adds the Diagonally Prominent Attention Map (DPAM) to M1, while Module M3 introduces the Object-Agnostic Pre-Training (OAPT) based on M2. The results demonstrate a substantial improvement in DR_OAPL’s performance with the integration of these modules, though the contribution of each varies. Adding Module M1 led to a 10.6% increase in the patch-level AUROC score and a 4% increase in the image-level AUROC score. This improvement underscores the effectiveness of contrastive learning in capturing the alignment between local image features and text descriptions. The addition of Module M2 and subsequently Module M3 further enhanced performance. Specifically, incorporating the M3 module resulted in an increase of 12.2% in the patch-level AUROC score and 11.7% in the image-level AUROC score. This highlights the significant role of the OAPT module in improving diagnostic accuracy by enhancing lesion identification capabilities.

The impact of different context optimizations on performance The Object-Agnostic Pre-Training (OAPT) module is pivotal in our zero-shot detection experiments. This module enhances visual embeddings at both the patch and image levels by learning text embeddings, optimizing cosine similarity loss, and thereby capturing a range of lesion characteristics. This enhancement significantly improves the recognition capabilities of DR_OAPL. In designing the text instruction templates, we drew from previous research [19, 24] and tested various terms such as “Anomalous,” “Damaged,” and “Lesions.” Our experiments revealed that the term “Lesions” yielded the most effective performance. The impact of global and local context optimization on DR_OAPL’s performance was evaluated through an ablation study, as depicted in Fig. 8. In this study: “G” denotes global text optimization, “L” signifies local text optimization, “No-L&G” indicates the absence of both optimizers, “Only-L” represents local optimization alone, “Only-G” refers to global optimization alone, and “L&G” represents the integration of both optimizations. The results illustrate that incorporating both global and local context optimizations significantly enhances DR_OAPL’s performance. Global optimization improves image-level semantic understanding, while local optimization refines patch-level details. The combination of these optimizations produces the most effective diagnostic results, highlighting the value of integrating both approaches in the model.

Hyperparameter ablation We conducted an analysis of two key hyperparameters: the learnable text instruction length (L) and the learnable text instruction depth (D). The findings are illustrated in Fig. 9. For fine-tuning, we utilized the MURA-1.1 and CHASE-DB1 datasets to ensure robust parameter selection. Figure 9 presents the AUROC results at the image level in the first row and the AUPRO results at the patch level in the second row. The analysis revealed that the optimal values for both hyperparameters, across both datasets, are 14 for L and 9 for D. These values consistently yielded the best performance at both the patch and image levels. Consequently, we adopted L = 14 and D = 9 for all subsequent experiments.

Conclusion

To effectively locate and identify various types of diabetic retinopathy (DR), we developed an auxiliary diagnostic system, DR_OAPL. This system integrates a text instruction learning framework tailored for unknown targets through comparative pre-training and utilizes auxiliary data from the field to achieve zero-shot migration. DR_OAPL leverages both local (patch-embedding) and global (image-embedding) context information to enhance feature extraction within the visualization field. It employs contrastive learning to capture detailed feature representations of each lesion, which results in exceptional performance in DR detection. Compared to mainstream transfer learning methods, DR_OAPL offers significant improvements in detection performance and shows strong results in both zero-shot and few-shot tasks. This capability not only addresses privacy concerns related to medical data but also mitigates the challenges associated with costly and complex data annotation. Furthermore, DR_OAPL is particularly effective in detecting Microaneurysms, a critical early indicator of DR. Accurate detection of Microaneurysms is crucial for timely intervention and effective management of DR. Given the multimodality of medical data, applying DR_OAPL to multimodal data detection presents a promising avenue for future research. This study aims to provide valuable insights into related fields and contribute to advancements in medical imaging and diagnostics.
