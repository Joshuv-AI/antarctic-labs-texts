Arboreal networks and their underlying trees
Authors: K T Huber, D Overman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHorizontal gene transfer (HGT) is an important process in bacterial evolution. Current phylogeny-based approaches to capture it cannot however appropriately account for the fact that HGT can occur between bacteria living in different ecological niches. Due to the fact that arboreal networks are a type of multiple-rooted phylogenetic network that can be thought of as a forest of rooted phylogenetic trees along with a set of additional arcs each joining two different trees in the forest, understanding the combinatorial structure of such networks might therefore pave the way to extending current phylogeny-based HGT-inference methods in this direction. A central question in this context is, how can we construct an arboreal network? Answering this question is strongly informed by finding ways to encode an arboreal network, that is, breaking up the network into simpler combinatorial structures that, in a well defined sense uniquely determine the network. In the form of triplets, trinets and quarnets such encodings are known for certain types of single-rooted phylogenetic networks. By studying the underlying tree of an arboreal network, we complement them here with an answer for arboreal networks.Keywords: Arboreal network, Horizontal gene transfer, Enhanced quartet tree system, Augmented tree, Phylogenetic tree, Multiple-rooted network

Introduction

Evidence suggests that bacteria living in distinct ecological niches such as the human gut and the human skin can exchange genetic material via horizontal gene transfer (Jeong et al. 2019). Until now, the standard approach in phylogenetics to model this phenomenon has been to assume a common phylogenetic tree for the species of interest called a species tree and to then explain the evolutionary signal conveyed by a gene tree within the assumed species tree (Bansal et al. 2012; Jeong et al. 2019). To do this, a cost is generally assigned to permissible evolutionary events such as speciation, duplication, and loss and the task is then to find an embedding of the gene tree within the species tree that is optimal in terms of a cost function. Although much research has gone into extending and refining this approach by, for example, assuming additional evolutionary events such as incomplete lineage sorting, not much is known for the case where a species tree cannot be found with high enough confidence or where the information that the exchange of genetic material between niches is important and that this information should therefore be preserved.

To start filling this gap, multiple-rooted phylogenetic networks such as the one depicted in Figure 1(i) have been introduced and studied in the literature in the form of Overlaid Species Forests (Huber et al. 2022a), forest-based networks (Huber et al. 2022b, 2025; Lafond and Moulton 2025) and arboreal networks (Huber et al. 2024a). Although distinct combinatorial objects all of whose set of leaves is a pre-given set X of organisms, they all can be thought of as a forest S of rooted phylogenetic trees (each representing, for example, the evolutionary past of a set of bacteria inhabiting an ecological niche) along with a set A of additional arcs (each representing, for example, a horizontal gene transfer event) such that each arc in A joins two distinct trees in S.

Arboreal networks N are at the centre of this paper and, in addition to being multiple-rooted, enjoy the property that their underlying graph i.e. the graph obtained from N by ignoring directions and suppressing all roots (i.e. vertices with indegree zero and outdegree two or more) that have become vertices of degree two are unrooted phylogenetic trees in the usual sense (see Sections 6 and 2 for formal definitions, respectively). Although relatively simple looking structures, arboreal networks enjoy interesting combinatorial properties such as being Ptolemaic (Huber et al. 2024a). Furthermore, they naturally generalize so called tree-based single-rooted phylogenetic networks (Francis and Steel 2015) which have attracted a considerable amount of attention in the literature (Fischer and Francis 2020; Francis et al. 2018; Hayamizu 2016; Suzuki et al. 2024; Zhang 2016). However in contrast to certain single-rooted phylogenetic networks not much is known about them in terms of encodings whereby we mean that the network can be broken up into simpler combinatorial structures that, in a well-defined sense, uniquely determine the network (see e.g. Gambette and Huber 2012; Huber and Moulton 2013; Van Iersel and Moulton 2014 for such encodings). Arguably, the main attraction of them is that they not only naturally lend themselves as a first port of call for developing reconstruction algorithms for arboreal networks so that their power can be exploited by evolutionary biologists, but also that they are generally easier to infer from real biological data.

As suggested by the example in Figure 1, any answer to the question of what can be said about encodings for arboreal networks N must in one way or another incorporate the roots of N and also its reticulation vertices that is, the vertices of indegree two or more and outdegree one. Motivated by the fact that a rooted phylogenetic tree is sometimes constructed from an unrooted phylogenetic tree by including an outgroup for rooting purposes, we address the former by associating to an arboreal network N with leaf set X its planted version Np by adding for each of the roots r of N a new vertex r^ and an arc (r^,r). Calling the vertices r^ the roots of Np and denoting the set of newly added vertices by R(N) then it is immediately clear that the underlying graph U(Np) of Np is an unrooted phylogenetic tree with leaf set X∪R(N). Writing U(N) for U(Np) to keep notation at bay, we address the latter by recording the reticulation vertices of N in terms of a map νN from the vertex set of U(N) into a set {∙,∘} where a vertex is assigned ∙ under νN if, in N, it is a reticulation vertex of N and ∘ otherwise. For the convenience of the reader, we depict for the arboreal network N in Figure 1(i) the planting Np of N and the augmented tree U(N)=(U(N),νN) associated to N in Figures 1(ii) and (iii), respectively. Note that, from a conceptual level, such trees are unrooted phylogenetic trees T along with a symbolic map from the vertex set of T into a set of symbols which, in our case, are ∙ and ∘ (see e.g. Böcker and Dress 1998; Hellmuth et al. 2013; Huber et al. 2019, 2024b and (Semple et al. (2003), Section 7.6) for other cases where such maps have been used in phylogenetics). Throughout the paper, we will refer to them as augmented trees.

As is well-known, unrooted phylogenetic trees are encoded by collections of unrooted phylogenetic trees on 4 leaves called quartet trees that satisfy certain properties (see e.g. Colonius and Schulze 1981; Dress and Erdös 2003; Grünewald et al. 2008; Semple et al. 2003). Using the novel concept of an enhanced quartet tree system which we introduce here, we present in Theorem 1 an encoding of augmented trees in terms of enhanced quartet tree systems that satisfy six non-trivial mutually independent properties. As the forbidden configuration for augmented trees in Figure 8 suggests, this characterization of augmented trees can however not easily be used as a starting point for finding a characterization of arboreal network in terms of such quartet tree systems. We therefore restrict our attention in the last-but-one section of the paper to binary augmented trees Tand make the additional assumption that the set of roots of an arboreal network N that is potentially obtainable from T is known. In particular, denoting the set of leaves of T by R∪X where we view R as the set of roots of N and X is the set of leaves of N then we present a O(|X∪R|2) time algorithm that either constructs such an arboreal network from T, X and R or returns the statement that no such arboreal network exists (see also Theorem 2).

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we present formal definitions of the concepts that allow us to establish Theorem 1. This also includes the definition of the enhanced quartet tree system (γT,νT) induced by an augmented tree T=(T,ν). In Section 3, we introduce our aforementioned six properties for enhanced quartet tree systems and show that they are independent of each other. Subsequent to that section, we then establish some basic results about enhanced quartet tree systems which will allow us to prove Theorem 1 in Section 5. Preliminaries

From now on, |X|≥4. For a map f:A→B from a set A into a set B of integers, we define the support supp(f) of f to be the set {a∈A:f(a)≥0}.

Suppose for the following that G is a simple graph. Then we denote the vertex set of G by V(G) and the set of edges of G by E(G). Furthermore, we denote an edge between two distinct vertices u and v of G by {u,v}. We call a vertex of G of degree one a leaf of G and denote the set of leaves of G by L(G).

Suppose that v∈V(G). Then we call v an interior vertex of G if v is not a leaf of G. If u is a further interior vertex of G such that e={u,v}∈E(G) then we call e an interior edge of G. If the degree of v is two then we call v a subdivision point of G. For a subdivision point v with adjacent vertices w and w′ we refer to the deletion of v and its two incident edges and addition of the edge {w,w′} as suppressing of v. If |V(G)|≥3 and x, y, and z are three vertices of G such that v is the unique vertex that simultaneously lies on the path from x to y, on the path from x to z and on the path from z to y, then we call v the median of x, y, and z. In this case, we also write medG(x,y,z) for v where the order of x, y and z is of no relevance. Note that a graph might contain three vertices that do not have a median – see e. g. Bandelt and Van De Vel (1999); Bruckmann et al. (2022); Choe et al. (2008); Hellmuth et al. (2023); Klavzar and Mulder (1999); Mulder (2011, 1978) for more on medians in graphs.

Phylogenetic trees

Suppose that T is an unrooted tree with leaf set X. Then we say that T is partially subdivided if T only has finitely many subdivision points and there is no leaf that is adjacent with a subdivision point of T. If T does not have a subdivision point then T is called a phylogenetic tree (on X).

Suppose for the following that T is a phylogenetic tree on X. If T is such that every interior vertex of T has degree three, then we call T a binary phylogenetic tree and if T has a single interior vertex then we call T a star tree. We refer to a set L of leaves of T with at least two elements as a multi-cherry of T if there exists a unique vertex in T that is adjacent with every leaf in L. For a phylogenetic tree T on X and a non-empty subset Y⊆X with at least four elements, we call the minimal connected subgraph of T that joins the elements in Y the span of Y, denoted by T(Y). Furthermore, we denote by T|Y the phylogenetic tree on Y obtained by suppressing all vertices of degree two in T(Y). We say that two phylogenetic trees T and T′ on X are equivalent if there exists a bijection ψ:V(T)→V(T′) that gives rise to a graph isomorphism between T and T′ that is the identity on X. If T is binary and has four leaves then we call T a quartet tree. We denote the set of all quartet trees whose leaf set is contained in X by Q(X). We call a non-empty subset Q′⊆Q(X) of quartets trees a quartet tree system (on ⋃q∈Q′L(q)).

To be able to link a quartet tree system Q with the set S of partially subdivided quartet trees obtained from Q by adding kq≥0 subdivision points to the interior edge of a quartet tree q in Q, we next associate a map γ:Q(X)→Z≥-1={n∈Z|n≥-1} to Q defined, for all q∈Q(X), as follows. If q∉Q, then we put γ(q)=-1 and, otherwise, we put γ(q)=kq. We call γ a subdivision map for Q and also write Qγ for S. Furthermore, we call Qγ a system of partially subdivided quartet trees. By abuse of terminology, we sometimes refer to the pair (q,γ(q)) with q∈Q as a partially subdivided quartet tree in Qγ and to a partially subivided quartet tree s∈Qγ with ks≥0 subdivision points as a pair (qs,ks) where qs is the quartet tree obtained from s by removing all its subdivision points.

Suppose for the following that q is a quartet tree in Q(X) with leaf set {a,b,c,d}. Let s and t denote the two interior vertices of q. If the path from a to b crosses s but not t (and therefore the path from c to d crosses t but not s) then we also denote q by ab|cd or, alternatively, by cd|ab where, in each case, the order of a and b and c and d does not matter. Suppose that q is a quartet tree in Q(X) and that T is a phylogenetic tree on X. Then we say that q is displayed by T if q is equivalent with T|L(q). We denote the set of all quartet trees displayed by T by Q(T).

For X:={a,b,c,d,z} and two quartet trees q′:=ab|dz and q′′:=zb|dc, we denote the unique binary phylogenetic tree on X that simultaneously displays q′ and q′′ by T(q′,q′′) – see Figure 2 for an illustration. Note that the five quartet trees that make up the system Q(T(q′,q′′)) of quartet trees displayed by T(q′,q′′) can be obtained by taking, for example, the so-called semi-dyadic closure scl2(q′,q′′) of q′ and q′′ – see Colonius and Schulze (1981) and Semple et al. (2003) for more on the semi-dyadic closure of a quartet tree system. The map γ:Q(T(q′,q′′))→Z≥-1 that assigns the value 1 to the quartet tree ab|cd, the value 0 to all other quartet trees in Q(T(q′,q′′)), and the value -1 to the remaining quartet trees in Q(X) is a subdivision map for Q(T(q′,q′)).

Phylogenetic trees and splits

A partial split σ (on X) is a set {A,B} where A and B are two non-empty disjoint subsets of X. If A∪B=X, then we call σ a split of X. In either case, we sometimes also write A|B (or, equivalently, B|A) for σ and call A and B the parts of σ.

If one of the parts of a partial split σ on X has size one then we call σ a trivial partial split of X. For a subset Y⊆X and a split σ:=A|B on X such that B′:=Y∩B≠∅ and A′:=Y∩A≠∅, we call the split A′|B′ on Y the restriction of σ to Y. We refer to a set Σ of partial splits on X as a system of partial splits (on X), and as a split system (on X) if all elements of Σ are splits on X. We say that a split σ on X displays a partial split σ′ on X if there exists a subset Y⊆X of X such that σ′ is the restriction of σ to Y.

Clearly, deleting from a phylogenetic tree T on X an edge e induces a split σe on X given by taking the leaf set of one of the resulting connected components to be one part of σe and the leaf set of the other to be the other part of σe. We denote the split system obtained from T by taking all splits σe, e∈E(T), by Σ(T). We say that a split σ is displayed by a phylogenetic tree T if σ∈Σ(T). In case T is a quartet tree, then we call the split induced by deleting the interior edge of T the quartet split of T. We say that a split σ of X displays a quartet tree q∈Q(X) if the quartet split associated to q is displayed by σ. Finally, for a non-trivial split σ of X, we denote by Q(σ) the set of quartet trees in Q(X) that are displayed by σ.

Intriguingly, a phylogenetic tree can be reconstructed from its induced set of quartet trees in polynomial time. To make this more precise, we follow Semple et al. (2003) and denote for a quartet tree system Q on X the split system obtained by taking all splits A|B of X such that, for all pairwise distinct a1,a2∈A and b1,b2∈B, we have a1a2|b1b2∈Q by Σ(Q). By (Semple et al. (2003), Theorem 6.3.7), it follows for any phylogenetic tree T on X that the split system Σ(Q(T)) associated to the set Q(T) of quartet trees displayed by T is the set of non-trivial splits displayed by T. Furthermore, Σ(Q(T)) and therefore also T can be constructed from Q(T) in polynomial time - see (Dress et al. (2012), Chaptrer 8) for more on the relationship between quartet trees, quartet splits, and split systems.

Augmented trees and enhanced quartet tree systems

Suppose for the following that T is a partially subdivided tree with leaf set X. Then we call a map ν:V(T)→{∘,∙} an augmentation map (for T) if, for all v∈V(T), we have that ν(v)∈{∘,∙} if v is an interior vertex of T that is not a subdivision point of T, ν(v)=∙ if v is a subdivision point of T, and, otherwise, ν(v)=∘ holds.

Suppose that ν is an augmentation map for T. Then we call a subdivision point v of T an augmentation point and a non-subdivision point v of T with ν(v)=∙ an augmentation vertex of T under ν. We denote the set of augmentation vertices of T by A(T,ν), or AT, for short. We call a pair T=(T,ν) an augmented, partially subdivided tree and refer to T as the underlying tree of T. If T is a phylogenetic tree, then we refer to T as an augmented (phylogenetic) tree (on X). Note that a phylogenetic tree is an augmented tree without any augmentation vertices. Note also that in the context of reconstructing an arboreal network N from an augmented tree (T,ν) only limited information is provided by the augmentation map ν as to where to place the roots of N - see Figure 1 and also Section 6 for more on this.

Extending the notion of an augmentation map for a partially subdivided tree to a system S of partially subdivided quartet trees, we call a map ν:V(S):=⋃q∈SV(q)→{∙,∘} an augmentation map for S if, for all v∈V(S), we have that ν(v)=∘ if v is a leaf of at least one partially subdivided quartet tree in S, ν(v)=∙ if v is a subdivision point of at least one such tree in S, and, otherwise, ν(v)∈{∙,∘}. We sometimes refer to a set of augmented partially subdivided quartet trees as a system of augmented, partially subdivided quartet trees.

Six useful properties and their independence

In this section, we introduce six useful properties called (A1)-(A6) for enhanced quartet tree systems. As we shall see in Section 5, they will allow us to characterize augmented trees in terms of such systems because the enhanced quartet tree system (γT,νT) induced by an augmented tree T=(T,ν) satisfies them. To be able to state Condition (A4), we require further concepts which we introduce next.

Before we start our investigation of Properties (A1)-(A6) with establishing independence relationships, we remark that if (γ,ν) is an enhanced quartet tree system such that ν assigns the value ∘ to every vertex in V(supp(γ)γ), then γ is the all-zero map and (A3) and (A6) always hold. Furthermore, Properties (A4) and (A5) hold vacuously and Properties (A1) and (A2) are similar to the antisymmetry, symmetry and substitution properties used in (Bandelt and Dress (1986), Propositon 2) to characterize quaternary relations on X that are tree-like in the sense that there exists a potentially unresolved X-tree that induces that relation on X. The independence of each of the Properties (A1) - (A3) from the other five

To see that Property (A1) is independent of (A2) - (A6), consider the set X:={a,b,c,d} and the map γ:Q(X)→Z≥-1 given by putting γ(q)=0, for all q∈Q(X). Characterizing augmented trees

One of the fundamental results in phylogenetics is the Split-Equivalence Theorem (Semple et al. (2003), Theorem 3.1.4) – see also Buneman (1971) which may be stated as follows. Saying that a split system Σ is compatible if for any two distinct splits σ and σ′ in Σ there exists a part A∈σ and a part A′∈σ′ such that A∩A′=∅ then that theorem guarantees that for any split system Σ on X that contains all trivial splits on X, there is a phylogenetic tree T on X with Σ=Σ(T) if and only if Σ is compatible. Moreover, if such a phylogenetic tree T exists, then, up to equivalence, T is unique. Our first result (Theorem 1) may be viewed as its analogue with the enhanced quartet tree system (γT,νT) induced by an augmented tree T=(T,ν) introduced at the end of Section 2.3 playing a key role. To be able to state it, we require further concepts. We say that two augmented trees (T,ν) and (T′,ν′) are augmentation-equivalent if T and T′ are equivalent as phylogenetic trees and, with ψ:V(T)→V(T′) denoting the underlying bijection, we have for all v∈V(T) that ν(v)=ν′(ψ(v)).

Theorem 1

Suppose that |X|≥4 and that (γ,ν) is an enhanced quartet tree system on X. Then there exists an augmented tree T=(T,ν) such that T is not the star tree, γ=γT and ν=νT if and only if (γ,ν) is an enhanced quartet tree system on X that satisfies Properties (A1)-(A6). Moreover, if such an augmented tree T exist then, up to augmentation-equivalence, T is unique.

Proof

First suppose that T is an augmented tree on X such that its underlying tree of T is not a star tree. Then, the pair (γT,νT) introduced in Section 2.3 is an enhanced quartet tree system on X. We need to show that (γT,νT) satisfies Properties (A1) - (A6).

To improve the clarity of our arguments, we first assume that |X|=4. Then T is a quartet tree and γT(T)=0 since an augmented tree cannot contain augmentation points. Since supp(γT)⊆Q(T)={T} it follows that T is the sole element in supp(γT). Thus Property (A1) must hold. Since the remaining five properties require X to have at least five elements, it follows that they hold vacuously.

Assume for the remainder of this direction of the proof that |X|≥5. To see that (γT,νT) satisfies Property (A1), suppose that a, b, c, and d are pairwise distinct elements in X. Since a phylogenetic tree on X can display at most one quartet tree on Y={a,b,c,d}, it follows that at most one quartet tree on Y can be assigned a non-negative value under γT. Thus, (γT,νT) satisfies (A1).

To see that (γT,νT) satisfies Property (A2), suppose that a,b,c,d∈X are such that ab|cd is a quartet tree in supp(γT). Then Σ(T) contains a split σ=A|B that displays ab|cd. Without loss of generality, we may assume that a,b∈A and c,d∈B. Let x∈X-{a,b,c,d}. Then either x∈A or x∈B. If x∈A, then σ displays the quartet trees ax|cd and bx|cd. Hence, ax|cd∈supp(γT) and bx|cd∈supp(γT). Since similar arguments apply if x∈B, it follows that (γT,νT) satisfies (A2).

To see that (γT,νT) satisfies Property (A3), suppose that q and q′ are quartet trees in supp(γT) that share three pairwise distinct leaves x, y, and z. Then the medians medq(x,y,z) and medq′(x,y,z) in q and q′, respectively, result from the median medT(x,y,z) in T when restricting T to the leaf sets of q and q′, respectively. Hence, νT(medq(x,y,z))=νT(medq′(x,y,z)). Thus, (γT,νT) satisfies (A3).

To see that (γT,νT) satisfies Property (A4), suppose that s is an augmentation point of a partially subdivided quartet tree (q,γ(q)) where q:=cd|az is a quartet tree in supp(γT). Then s must be an augmentation vertex of T. Since an augmentation point of a partially subdivided quartet tree can, in T, only be adjacent to at most one leaf of T and |X|≥5, there must exist interior vertices v and v′ of T such that v lies on the path joining s and c and v′ lies on the path joining s and a. Thus, e:={s,v} and e′:={s,v′} are interior edges of T that lie on the path joining c and a. Choose a leaf b of T such that the path from s to b neither crosses v nor v′. Let σe and σe′ denote the splits in Σ(T) obtained by deleting the edges e and e′, respectively. Then since q′:=cd|bz is displayed by σe and q′′:=bc|az by σe′, it follows that q′ and q′′ are contained in Q(T). Thus, q′ and q′′ are contained in Q(X) and γT(q′),γT(q′′)≥0 holds. Since the restriction T|Y of T to Y:={a,b,c,d,z} is equivalent with the phylogenetic tree T(q′,q′′), it follows that the median medT(b,c,z) in T results in the median s′=medq′(b,c,z) in q′ when restricting T to {b,c,d,z} and the median s′′=medq′′(b,c,z) in q′′ when restricting T to {a,b,c,z}. Since s is an augmentation vertex of T, it follows that νT(s′)=ν(s′′)=∙. Hence, s is supported by supp(γT). Thus, (γT,νT) satisfies (A4).

To show that (γT,νT) satisfies Property (A5), suppose a,b,c,d,e∈X are such that the quartet trees ab|cd and q:=ab|ce are both contained in supp(γT) and that γT(ab|cd)>γT(q)≥0. Let q′:=ae|cd. Then the restriction T|Y of T to Y:={a,b,c,d,e} must be equivalent with T(q,q′) since otherwise ab|ce∉Q(T) or γT(ab|cd)≤γT(q) would hold which is impossible. It follows that the median v:=medq(a,c,e) in q results from the median medT(q,q′)(a,c,e) in T(q,q′) which itself results from the median w=medT(a,c,e) in T. Similarly, the median v′:=medq′(a,c,e) in q′ results from w this way. Thus, if w is not an augmentation vertex of T then νT(v)=νT(v′)=∘ and if w is an augmentation vertex of T then νT(v)=νT(v′)=∙. Since w is an interior vertex on the path in T that joins the path from a to b in T and the path from c to d in T, Equation (1) follows if νT(v)=∙. And if νT(v)=∘ then Equation (2) follows. Thus, (γT,νT) satisfies (A5).

Finally, to see that (γT,νT) satisfies Property (A6), suppose that there exist elements a,b,c,d,e∈X such that the quartet trees q:=ab|cd and q′:=bc|de are both contained in supp(γT). Then there exists splits σ:=A|B and σ′∈Σ(T) such that σ displays q and σ′ displays q′. Without loss of generality, we may assume that a,b∈A and c,d∈B. Note that e∉A because otherwise q′∈supp(γT) would not hold. Hence, e∈B. In particular, the number of augmentation vertices in the interior of the path in T separating the path from a to b from the path from e to d equals γT(q)+γT(q′)+1 if w:=medT(b,c,d)∈AT as in this case νT(medq(b,c,d))=∙=νT(medq′(b,c,d)). If w∉AT then γT(q)+γT(q′) follows because in this case νT(medq(b,c,d))=∘=νT(medq′(b,c,d)). Hence (γT,νT) satisfies (A6).

In summary, it follows that (γT,νT) is an enhanced quartet tree system on X that satisfies Properties (A1) - (A6).

To prove the converse, suppose that (γ,ν) is an enhanced quartet tree system on X that satisfies Properties (A1) - (A6). We perform induction on |supp(γ)|. Since supp(γ)≠∅ by the definition of an enhanced quartet tree system, the base case is |supp(γ)|=1. Let q denote the sole quartet tree in supp(γ). Then γ(q)=0 must hold since otherwise (q,γ(q)) contains an augmentation vertex which, in view of Property (A4) holding, must be supported by supp(γ). But then |supp(γ)|≥3 which is impossible as we are in the base case of the induction. Thus, (q,ν) is an augmented tree on X for which γ=γq, and ν=νq holds. Thus, the base case holds.

Assume for the remainder that |supp(γ)|≥2 and that the induction hypothesis holds for all enhanced quartet tree systems (γ′,ν′) on X with |supp(γ′)|<|supp(γ)|. Let p:=a1a2|b1b2 be a quartet tree in supp(γ) such that γ(p) is minimum. In view of (A4), it follows that γ(p)=0. Let A and B be two disjoint subsets of X such that a1,a2∈A, b1,b2∈B, Q(A|B)⊆supp(γ) and |A|+|B| is maximum. Note that such subsets must exist since p∈supp(γ). By Lemma 2, σ:=A|B is a split of X. Augmented trees and arboreal networks

We now turn our attention to arboreal networks which we already described briefly in the introduction. Formally speaking, an arboreal network N (on X) is a multiple-rooted directed acyclic graph with leaf set X such that every vertex with indegree at least two has outdegree one and planting N and ignoring directions results in a phylogenetic tree on X∪R(N). In this context, R(N) denotes the set of roots r^ of the planted version of N that were attached via incoming arcs (r^,r) to the roots r of N. Note that, by construction, R(N)≠∅. Clearly, every planted arboreal network N can be transformed back into the arboreal network that gave rise to it by reversing the planting process described in Section 1, that is, removing for every root r of N the newly added vertex r^ and the arc (r^,r). Although, for every arboreal network N its planted version Np gives rise to an augmented tree U(N):=U(Np) as described in the introduction, not every augmented tree gives rise to a planted arboreal network and, therefore, to an arboreal network. For example, since an augmentation vertex in U(N) is a reticulation vertex in N and a reticulation vertex in an arboreal network has outdegree one and neither one of its parents can be a root of the network, no augmentation vertex in U(N) can be adjacent with more than one leaf.

Even though this insight is undoubtedly useful, from the point of view of reconstructing an arboreal network N from an augmented tree T=(T,ν) such that T is augmentation-equivalent with the augmented tree U(N) associated to N it is however not sufficient, even if T is binary and does not contain augmentation vertices that are adjacent with each other. One of the reasons for this is the configuration for T depicted in Figure 8(i) where the vertices indicated by "∘" are leaves of T and, for some k≥3, the grey disk marked T′ is an augmented tree on the set of vertices of T that are adjacent with one of the augmentation vertices wi of T but are not contained in Bi, 3≤i≤k, or a leaf. For all 3≤i≤k, some integer k≥3, the disk marked Bi is the augmented tree obtained by (i) attaching a new leaf lvi to the vertex vi in the underlying tree of Bi that is adjacent with wi. (ii) assigning the value ∘ to lvi and preserving all other values under ν, and (iii) deleting the edge {vi,wi} from T.

More precisely, assume that T is a binary augmented trees such that no two augmentation vertices are adjacent and that is of the form as described in that figure. Then since T is binary and a reticulation vertex in an arboreal network has oudegree one, it is straightforward to see that, once oriented, T′ must contain a vertex that has outdegree three. Indeed, continuing with the notation in Figure 8(i), each edge incident with a vertex in T′ and a vertex wi, 1≤i≤k, some k≥3 must be directed towards wi because wi is an augmentation vertex of T that is adjacent with a leaf. Since k≥3 and T′ does not contain a leaf and therefore no leaf that could serve as a root in an arboreal network it follows that, once oriented, T′ has a vertex of outdegree three, as required. Thus, there does not exist an arboreal network N such that U(N) is augmentation-equivalent with T.

On the other hand, if T is such that one of the leaves in the configuration in Figure 8(i) is an augmented tree with at least two vertices all of which are non-augmentation vertices then it is not difficult to to see that there exists an arboreal network N such that U(N) and T are augmentation equivalent because this extra leaf introduces a level of flexibility for directing edges. However even with this requirement for two leaves there does not exist, in general, an arboreal network N such that U(N) and T are augmentation-equivalent if T′ contains at least one augmentation vertex.

We conclude this section with presenting an efficient algorithm called Check which, in case the set of roots of an arboreal networks (or more precisely the set R of roots of its planted version) is known, will construct an arboreal network N with leaf set X from an augmented tree T:=(T,ν) on X∪R such that R is the root set of Np and T and U(N) are augmentation-equivalent. To this end, we construct an augmented tree Tρ:=(Tρ,νρ:=ν|V(Tρ)) on R∪LAT as follows were LAT denotes the set of leaves of Tρ that are adjacent with an augmentation vertex of Tρ. We first make a copy of T which we call Tc. We then choose an element ρ∈R and, starting at ρ, use a depth-first search to traverse Tc to make the following modifications to the underlying tree Tc of Tc and T which is based on the fact that interior vertices of Tc are visited at least twice by our search. For each vertex w of Tc not in AT∪R such that between visits i and i+1 of w, some i≥1, only vertices not in AT∪R are encountered, we remove all encountered vertices (and their incident edges!) from Tc and suppress w in Tc. For each such w, we mirror this modification of Tc in T by directing all removed edges towards the removed leaves. The augmented tree that results once the traversal of Tc is complete is Tρ. For example, for the augmented tree T in Figure 9(i) and R={1^,5^,7^,15^,11^,14^}, ignoring the orientations of the edges for the moment, the augmented tree Tρ on R∪LAT is depicted in Figure 9(ii) where AT={v1,…v5} and LAT={3,4,12,16}.

Theorem 2

Suppose that X and R are two finite non-empty sets and that T is a binary augmented tree on X∪R. Then algorithm Check can be used to check in O(|X∪R|2) time if there exists an arboreal network N on X such that R is the root set of the planted version of N and U(N) is augmentation-equivalent with T.

Proof

By construction, algorithm Check is correct. Since the running time complexity of a depth-first-search of a tree is O(m) where m is the number of edges of that tree it follows that the running time complexity of constructing Tρ, the partial orientation of the underlying tree of T, and the look-up table τT is O(|X∪R|) since a binary unrooted phylogenetic tree with m leaves has 2m-3 edges (Semple et al. (2003), Proposition 2.1.3). Furthermore, the split system Σ(Tρ) can be computed in O(|Y|2) time where Y:=R∪LAT since we can choose an element x∈Y, direct all edges of Tρ away from x, delete all arcs, one at a time, in the resulting orientation of Tρ to obtain its associated cluster system C, and then associate to each cluster C∈C the split C|Y-C. Using again the fact that a binary unrooted phylogenetic tree on m leaves has 2m-3 edges it follows that the loop starting in Line 11 can be carried out in O(|Y|) time and that therefore the loop starting in Line 5 can be carried out in O(|Y|2) time. Similarly, the loop starting in Line 15 can be carried out in O(|Y|) time since the leaf set of Tρ is Y. Finally, the loop starting in Line 17 can be carried out in O(|R|) time. Since |LAT|≤|X| the theorem follows. □

Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the question of what can be said about encodings of arboreal networks, a certain kind of multiple rooted directed acyclic graph that might prove useful for extending phylogeny-based HGT-inference methods to the case where an overall species tree might be uncertain or unavailable or the information that the transfer happened between bacteria inhabiting different ecological niches is important and therefore should be preserved. Our results are combinatorial (Theorems 1 and 2) as well as algorithmical (Algorithm 1) in nature and also include a forbidden configuration that prevents a binary augmented tree T from giving rise to an arboreal network.

Numerous questions however remain that might warrant further study. These include characterizing augmented trees that cannot be transformed, in the above sense, into an arboreal network in terms of forbidden configurations. From a more algorithmical point of view it might also be interesting to find an efficient algorithm for checking if an enhanced quartet tree system satisfies properties (A1) - (A6) and, if so, constructs an augmented tree from that gave rise to it in the sense of Theorem 1. Finally, and as a first step towards reconstructing augmented trees from real biological data and, in the long run, arboreal networks, it might be interesting to develop ways to estimate augmentation maps for quartet trees from data.
