Comprehensive genomic analysis of Brevibacillus brevis BF19 reveals its biocontrol potential against bitter gourd wilt
Authors: Luyang Song, Yue Shen, Huihao Zhang, Han Zhang, Yuanyuan Zhang, Mengjiao Wang, Mingyue Zhang, Fei Wang, Lin Zhou, Caiyi Wen, Ying Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Bitter gourd wilt, a severe vascular disease triggered by the soilborne pathogen Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. momordicae (FOM), markedly constrains bitter gourd yield. In this study, a novel strain BF19 of Brevibacillus brevis was isolated and identified, exhibiting strong antimicrobial activity against FOM through in vivo and in vitro experiments. To comprehensively assess the biocontrol potential of strain BF19, we conducted phenotypic, phylogenetic, and comparative genomics analyses. Phenotypic analysis revealed that BF19 exhibited 53.33% biocontrol efficacy and significantly increased the average plant height, root fresh weight, and dry weight. Whole-genome sequencing and comparative genomic analysis revealed numerous potential genes associated with biocontrol mechanisms in BF19. Importantly, the integration of metabolic cluster prediction with liquid chromatography‒tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS) revealed the presence of a macrobrevin antibiotic, a product of polyketide synthases (PKSs), predominantly in BF19 fermentation products. The effectiveness of the Br. brevis strain BF19 and its crude extract against bitter gourd wilt has also been confirmed. This study provides a genetic framework for future investigations on PKSs and establishes a scientific basis for optimizing field applications of microbial biopesticides derived from Br. brevis BF19.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03519-7.

Background

Bitter melon, scientifically known as Momordica charantia L., belongs to the Cucurbitaceae family, is a cultivated annual plant valued both as a vegetable and for its medicinal properties. Renowned for its high content of bioactive compounds, bitter melon holds substantial biomedical and economic value [1]. Among plant diseases, 70–80% are attributed to Fusarium, a pathogen capable of infecting a wide range of crops [2]. Bitter gourd wilt, a devastating vascular disease induced by the soilborne pathogen FOM, can lead to complete yield loss in fields subjected to continuous cropping [3]. To date, various strategies have been implemented to mitigate agricultural losses attributed to F. oxysporum, encompassing integrated disease management (IDM) approaches [4], chemical treatments and biological control measures.

In addition to the potential issues associated with chemical control, such as ecological damage, environmental pollution, pesticide residues, and pathogen resistance, biocontrol offers an alternative and environmentally friendly approach to suppress soil-borne plant pathogens. This is achieved through various mechanisms, including parasitism, antagonism, competition, and induced systemic resistance (ISR), in host plants against pathogens [5]. At present, a range of biocontrol agents, including Streptomyces spp., Talaromyces spp., Pseudomonas spp. and Bacillus spp., are employed to manage Fusarium wilt across multiple crops [6–9]. The control efficacy of Paenibacillus polymyxa HX-140 in reducing the incidence of Fusarium wilt in cucumber seedlings is greater than 50.9% when it is inoculated with 108 colony-forming units (CFUs)/mL of bacterial suspension [10]. Bacillus subtilis, an important biocontrol microorganism, is well known for its ability to produce a diverse array of antimicrobial agents. For example, Bacillus subtilis CAS15, a bacterium known for its production of siderophores, has been shown to reduce the incidence of Fusarium wilt by 12.5–56.9% and increase the average yield per pepper plant by 49.68% [11]. Bacillus velezensis F21 can induce systemic resistance in watermelon against F. oxysporum. In greenhouse trials, the biocontrol efficiency reached 80.35%, whereas under field conditions, it reached 65.81% [12]. Streptomyces, a member of the actinomycetes, has been extensively studied as a potential biological control agent (BCA) against phytopathogenic fungi [13]. Rice seedlings treated with Streptomyces chilikensis RC1830 significantly inhibited F. oxysporum wilting and root rot disease, resulting in a remarkable 80.51% reduction in the disease severity index [14].

Variations in the utilization patterns of mannose and mannitol were observed among previously identified strains classified as Bacillus brevis [15]. Consequently, these strains were reclassified as novel species within the genus Brevibacillus on the basis of polyphasic taxonomy [16]. There are 20 species within this genus, among which are 10 Bacillus species identified previously, known as Br. brevis cluster. Brevibacillus species are considered plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPRs) and are commonly found in soil and sediment. They have been widely researched and applied for their advantageous effects in agriculture and environmental remediation due to their diverse functional capabilities. Numerous researchers have explored the application of this bacterium as a PGPR in various crops, including tomato, cotton, maize, tea, etc [17–19].

As biocontrol bacteria, Brevibacillus strains are capable of producing several inhibitory metabolites, such as chitinase and HCN, which exhibit potential antifungal properties. The strains Br. formosus DSM 9885 and Br. brevis NBRC 15304 have been reported to inhibit linear mycelial growth and spore germination in Alternaria alternata, thereby reducing the incidence of brown leaf spot disease in potato [20]. Brevibacillus spp. are crucial in managing Fusarium wilt caused by Fusarium oxysporum. For example, Brevibacillus brevis acts as a potential biocontrol agent against F. oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici in tomatoes [21]. Additionally, Brevibacillus fortis NRS-1210 produces edeines, which inhibit the growth of F. oxysporum f. sp. cepae (FOC) conidia, thereby reducing basal rot in onions caused by FOC [22]. To date, the application of Brevibacillus spp. in the management of bitter gourd wilt has not been reported. Therefore, the screening of Brevibacillus spp. for controlling bitter gourd wilt is highly important for advancing the exploration and utilization of microbial germplasm resources.

In this study, we identified an efficient PGPR strain, Br. brevis BF19, which can be utilized to control FOM in bitter gourd. To gain comprehensive insights into the biocontrol potential of strain BF19, we sequenced its genome. A comparative genomic analysis was subsequently conducted to elucidate the structure of this biocontrol bacterium, alongside three other closely related Brevibacillus spp. genomes, all demonstrating high biocontrol potential. By integrating metabolic cluster prediction and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS), a macrobrevin antibiotic belonging to the polyketide synthases class was found to predominantly exist in the fermentation products of BF19, which may be associated with the FOM antagonistic ability of Br. brevis BF19. This study provides a genetic framework for future research into PKSs and establishes a scientific foundation for improving the field applications of microbial biopesticides derived from Br. brevis BF19.

Methods

Isolation and taxonomy of strain BF19

Strain BF19 was isolated using the serial dilution method [23] from Tongbai Mountain in Nanyang, Henan Province, North China. The antifungal efficacy of this isolate against pathogenic fungi was evaluated through a dual culture assay [24]. The characteristics of the cultures were examined via potato dextrose agar (PDA). Morphological traits, including aerial hyphae, colony appearance, pigmentation, and substrate mycelium, were assessed following 5–7 days of incubation at 28 °C. The mycelial morphology under dual culture conditions was observed via optical microscopy (Zeiss, Germany).

The genomic DNA of the antifungal strain BF19 was extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. Partial sequences of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified using the following primers: reverse primer 1492R (5’-TACGGYTACCTTGTTAYGACTT-3’) and forward primer 27 F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3’). The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted under these conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min; 29 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 59 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1.5 min; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The amplified product was subsequently cloned and inserted into a pEASY-Blunt vector and transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells. The plasmid DNA harboring the 16S rRNA gene was sequenced with a primer set (M13F/M13R) by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

The sequence data were initially compared via the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) available in GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST). Phylogenetic and multiple sequence alignments of the 16S rRNA gene were conducted using MEGA X software. A phylogenetic tree was then constructed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates [25]. The bacterial genome phylogeny was constructed via the use of 120 single-copy genes extracted from 11 bacterial genomes via GTDB-TK v2.4.0, employing the ‘infer’ command within the software. Additionally, the FastANI algorithm was employed to calculate the genome average nucleotide identity (ANI) and average amino acid identity (AAI) [26]. It is advisable to employ an ANI value threshold of 96% for species differentiation [27].

Genome sequencing

The genome of Br. brevis BF19 was sequenced using a whole-genome shotgun (WGS) approach, which involves the construction of libraries with different insert sizes. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology, specifically the Illumina NovaSeq sequencing platform, was utilized for sequencing. Additionally, the libraries were sequenced separately via third-generation single-molecule sequencing technology on the Oxford Nanopore ONT sequencing platform.

Illumina sequencing libraries were prepared from fragmented DNA (400–500 bp) using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit. The 5’ ends were end-repaired and phosphorylated, followed by A-tailing of the 3’ ends and ligation of sequencing adapters. The adapter-ligated products were enriched through PCR amplification and then sequenced (2 × 150 bp) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

For third-generation nanopore sequencing, DNA fragments were repaired, purified, and attached to sequencing adapters using the SQK-LSK109 kit 1D (PM). The library was then sequenced on the Oxford Nanopore PromethION platform (Oxford Nanopore, Oxford, UK).

Genome assembly and annotation

The data obtained from both the Nanopore and Illumina platforms were used for subsequent bioinformatics analysis [28]. The 16S rRNA gene sequence and complete genome of BF19 have been deposited in the NCBI GenBank database (accession number PRJNA1138495). All analyses were conducted via the freely accessible Majorbio Cloud Platform (http://cloud.majorbio.com).

The raw reads from the Illumina paired-end library were quality-filtered using fastp v0.23.0, while the Nanopore reads from third-generation sequencing were processed with a minimum Q score of 7. Clean short and long reads were assembled into complete genomes via Unicycler v0.4.8 [29]. The assembly was polished via short-read alignment with Pilon v1.22, reducing minor errors. Coding sequences (CDSs) for chromosomes and plasmids were predicted via Glimmer or Prodigal v2.6.3 [30] and GeneMarkS [31]. tRNA and rRNA were predicted via tRNA-scan-SE (v 2.0) [32] and Barrnap v0.9. Each protein set underwent alignment with the databases, and genes were annotated based on the highest-scoring matches (e-value < 10− 5). Secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) were identified using antiSMASH v5.1.2 [33].

Comparative genomic analysis

The comparison of chromosome features and the construction of circular maps were conducted via Proksee [34]. Prophages were predicted and identified using the PHAge Search Tool—Enhanced Release (PHASTER) [35]. The BF19 chromosome was analyzed for the identification of horizontally transferred genes via HGTector [36]. Syntenic analysis was performed with ProgressiveMauve [37], which helped identify locally collinear blocks (LCBs) via default parameters. IslandViewer 4 was used for the identification of genomic islands [38]. For the analysis of shared and unique genes among BF19, Br. formosus NRRL NRS-863, Br. brevis NBRC 100599, and Br. brevis HNCS-1, we initially annotated their genomes via the default parameters of Prokka [39]. Subsequently, Venn diagrams were constructed via an online tool (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/). For the analysis of BF19’s unique genes in terms of Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COGs) categories, we conducted analysis via the default parameters of eggNOG-mapper v2 [40].

Assessment of the antifungal activity of strain BF19 fermentation against FOM

Strain BF19 was streaked and cultured on LB plates at 28 °C for 24 h. To obtain BF19 fermentation, we cultured single colonies in 5 mL of LB liquid medium at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 24 h. Next, we inoculated 2 mL of the bacterial suspension into 200 mL of LB liquid medium and incubated it under the same conditions for an additional 24 h. After fermentation, we centrifuged the culture at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and filtered the resulting supernatant through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove bacteria. The antifungal activity of BF19 fermentation was subsequently assessed. The sterile BF19 fermentation filtrate was added to PDA medium to prepare plates with final concentrations of 20%, 10%, and 5% (v/v) (Fig. S4). Disks of FOM mycelium (d = 5 mm) were then inoculated onto the center of each PDA plate using a sterile inoculating needle. The plates were inverted and incubated in an incubator at 28 °C. PDA plates containing mycelium alone served as blank controls, and the experiment was conducted in triplicate.

To evaluate the antifungal properties of the crude extract, a small amount was dissolved in methanol to create a stock solution at a concentration of 10 mg/mL. A 200 µg/mL working solution was obtained by diluting the stock solution with sterile water. The samples were subsequently filtered through a sterile 0.22 μm filter. The antifungal activity of the crude extract was assessed using the Oxford Cup method [41], where 100 µL of the crude extract was added to small round wells. The plates were photographed, and the biocontrol efficacy was determined until the PDA plate became overgrown with mycelium in the blank control group.

Analysis of the active component in crude extracts of strain BF19 fermentation

The active substances in the crude extract of strain BF19 fermentation were analyzed via LC‒MS/MS on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, USA), an ultra high-resolution liquid mass spectrometer.

Liquid chromatography was carried out using an Ultimate 3000 system with an Agilent SB-AQ C18 column (4.6 × 10 mm, 0.17 μm). The mobile phases consisted of acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (Phase A) and water with 0.1% formic acid (Phase B). The flow rate was maintained at 0.2 mL/min under a gradient program: 0 to 2 min at 0% A, 2 to 9 min from 0 to 100% A, and 9 to 11 min at 100% A. In our study, we employed a UV wavelength of 280 nm for detecting compounds, and we kept the column temperature at 30 °C.

Mass spectrometry was conducted with the following parameters: electrospray ionization (ESI) mode, spray voltages set at 3500 V (+) and 3000 V (-), a heater temperature of 320 °C, a vaporizer temperature of 270 °C, a sheath gas flow rate of 35 L/min, an auxiliary gas nitrogen flow rate of 10 L/min, a blowback gas nitrogen flow rate of 2 L/min, and a mass-to-charge (M/Z) range of 200–1500.

Pot experiments

Bitter gourd seedlings with 2–3 true leaves under identical growth conditions were selected. To obtain spores, FOM strains were cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates at 28 °C for 4–5 days in the dark. Three agar pieces from the PDA plates were moved to 100 mL of minimal media (comprising 1% KNO₃, 3% sucrose, and 0.17% yeast nitrogen base without amino acids and ammonia) and incubated for 4 to 7 days at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm. After incubation, the spores were filtered through a Miracloth filter (Millipore), washed with sterile water, and adjusted to a concentration of 106 spores/mL. The roots were immersed in a spore suspension of F. oxysporum at a concentration of 106 CFU/mL for 30 min, followed by transplantation into pots. Thirty minutes later, the seedlings were treated with 60 mL of biocontrol bacteria fermentation per plant via root irrigation, while the seedlings treated with 60 mL of PDB served as the control. Five seedlings were used for each treatment and cultivated in a greenhouse at 28 °C for 3 weeks. Disease severity was assessed, and biocontrol efficacy and disease indices were calculated. The experiment was replicated three times.

In the pot experiment evaluating the growth-promoting effect of strain BF19 on bitter melon, the plants were grown until at least two true leaves were present. Bitter melon seedlings were then inoculated with strain BF19 fermentation at 10 mL per plant via root irrigation, with bitter melons inoculated with LB culture medium serving as controls. Each treatment was replicated five times, and all the plants were grown in a greenhouse at 28 °C for 25 days. Images of the plants were taken, and measurements were recorded for growth parameters including plant height, fresh and dry weights of the aerial parts and roots, as well as root length. Each experiment was conducted three times.

Statistical analysis

The data are shown as means ± standard deviations (SDs). Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS Statistics 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), employing t-tests to compare differences between two groups. The significance levels are indicated as follows: *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001.

Results

Biocontrol potential of strain BF19

A total of 281 strains were isolated from soils collected in the scenic areas of Tongbai Mountain in Nanyang, Henan Province, and Bosan in Zibo, Shandong Province. Using the plate confrontation method, 38 bacterial strains exhibiting antagonistic effects against the pathogen causing bitter gourd wilt disease were identified. Subsequent rescreening, outlined in Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table S1, highlighted that strain BF19 exhibited the most pronounced antagonistic effect against the pathogen causing bitter gourd wilt disease. After the cocultivation of strain BF19 with the wilt pathogen, some mycelia exhibited bending, deformation, and dissolution phenomena (observed under a 40× objective lens) (Fig. 1A-d). In the control group, the mycelia of the wilt pathogen appeared smooth and straight and presented no abnormal phenomena (Fig. 1A-c). On the basis of these results, BF19 potentially suppressed vascular wilt in bitter gourd. This hypothesis was tested through greenhouse pot experiments, which confirmed the effectiveness of BF19 in controlling this disease. The wilt symptoms in bitter gourd treated with BF19 were notably milder than those in the control gourd (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, the disease index of wilt in the BF19-treated bitter gourd was significantly lower (31.85%) than that in the control treatment (85.18%) (Fig. 1C).

Furthermore, the plant growth-promoting capacity of BF19 was assessed through pot experiments. As shown in Fig. 2, the growth parameters (including root length, plant height, and fresh and dry weights of both aboveground parts and roots) were markedly greater in the bitter gourd plants subjected to the BF19 treatment than in those in the control group. Compared with those in the control group, the average plant height increased by 1.32 times after BF19 treatment (Student’s t test, p = 5.31 × 10− 5), whereas both root fresh weight and dry weight substantially increased by 2.24 (Student’s t test, p = 2.15 × 10− 5) and 2.18 times (Student’s t test, p = 1.40 × 10− 2), respectively. These results suggest that strain BF19 possesses characteristics of PGPR.

Identification of strain BF19

The nearly complete 16S rRNA gene (1500 bp) of strain BF19 was amplified and compared for similarity with the unified 16S rRNA database in NCBI. Sequence analysis of the 16S rRNA revealed that BF19 grouped within the genus Brevibacillus and showed the closest match (99.52% similarity) to Brevibacillus brevis strain NBRC 15304 (NR 041524.1) (Fig. S1). Nevertheless, analysis of the 16S rRNA gene sequence alone was found to be insufficient for species-level discrimination [42, 43]. Therefore, the complete genome of BF19 was sequenced to comprehensively assess its taxonomic position within the Brevibacillus genus. We chosed 11 publicly accessible reference genomes of related Brevibacillus species (Supplementary Table S2) for further analysis. The chromosome of BF19 exhibited an average nucleotide identity (ANI) of 98.03% and an average amino acid identity (AAI) of 98.35% with Br. brevis NBRC 100599 (Fig. 3). Therefore, the integration of 16S rRNA and core genome phylogenetic analyses, coupled with genomic properties, supports the classification of BF19 as a novel member within Br. brevis species. Consequently, it is proposed to designate this strain as Br. brevis BF19.

General genomic characteristics of BF19

The genomic features of BF19 were elucidated through comprehensive analysis, integrating insights from the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes Genomes (KEGG) databases. Genomic sequencing has provided comprehensive insight into an organism’s genetic makeup, revealing a genome size of approximately 6,426,658 bp, which is organized across 1 contig/scaffold (Fig. 4). The G + C content was 47.2%, providing valuable information about the nucleotide composition. Gene annotation efforts have identified 6017 coding sequences (CDSs), 44 rRNA genes, and 127 tRNA genes, contributing to the overall genetic landscape (Supplementary Table S3).

Using the COG (Clusters of Orthologous Genes) database, our analysis classified 85.1% of the total coding sequences (CDSs), equivalent to 5121 CDSs, into 21 COG categories. With the exception of the ‘function unknown’ category, the largest represented group was the ‘Transcription’ category, encompassing 478 genes, constituting 7.94% of all CDSs, followed closely by ‘Amino acid transport and metabolism’, with 450 genes, accounting for 7.47% of all CDSs (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table S4). In the context of PGPRs, the intricate processes associated with inorganic ion transport and metabolism assume a pivotal role in shaping their overall functionality and interactions with plants. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the ‘inorganic ion transport and metabolism’ category comprises 302 genes, constituting 5.01% of all CDSs, and is among the most significant functional categories within the BF19 genome. A significant portion of CDSs (40.6%) were poorly characterized, with 1203 genes classified as ‘S: Functional unknown’ and 938 genes having ‘no homologs identified’. KEGG pathway analysis revealed an array of metabolic pathways, highlighting the functional diversity encoded within the genome. On the basis of KEGG annotation, 4830 genes, constituting 80.2% of all coding sequences (CDSs), were assigned to 50 KEGG pathways. The majority of the identified genes were categorized into pathways within the ‘Brite Hierarchies’. Notably, the most prominently represented pathways were those associated with ‘signaling and cellular processes’, encompassing 649 genes (12.89% of all CDSs). Pathways closely related to ‘genetic information processing’ followed (Fig. S2).

Using the genome of the model strain B. subtilis 168 as a reference sequence, we also conducted an analysis of genes in the BF19 genome related to colonization and plant growth promotion. The results revealed that 61 putative coding sequences associated with colonization, 20 putative coding sequences related to plant growth promotion, and 15 putative coding sequences associated with inducing plant resistance were identified from the BF19 genome (Supplementary Table S7).

Comparative genomic analysis of BF19 with closely related species revealed its evolution and speciation

Comparative genomics with closely related strains illuminates conserved and unique genomic elements, providing insights into evolutionary relationships and adaptive strategies. We conducted a comparative genome analysis of BF19 using three strains with high genomic similarity—Br. formosus NRRL NRS-863, Br. brevis NBRC 100599, and Br. brevis HNCS-1—as reference genomes. Figure 6 reveals additional gene features, such as adjacent tRNAs and ISs, along with remnants of GIs and prophages, suggesting the occurrence of HGTs. These HGTs likely played a significant role in the evolution and speciation of BF19, enhancing bacterium‒plant interactions.

The BF19-specific gene repertoire was characterized, identifying a total of 591 BF19-specific genes present in the BF19 chromosome that are absent in the other three closely related species (Fig. 7A; also refer to Supplementary Table S5). COG classification revealed that 41, 11, and 22 genes unique to BF19 were enriched in ‘K: Transcription’, ‘E: Amino acid transport and metabolism’, and ‘P: Inorganic ion transport and metabolism’, respectively (Fig. 7B). This finding indicates that BF19 has a heightened potential for acquiring and utilizing essential nutrients and adapting to various environmental ions. Notably, 10 genes were enriched in ‘Q: secondary metabolite biosynthesis, transport, and catabolism’. These findings suggest that BF19 may respond to environmental pressures, such as resource competition or defense against predators or pathogens.

To better understand the evolutionary dynamics of BF19 and the other three strains, their genomes were compared. This comparison, involving genome rearrangement and synteny analyses, shed light on their evolutionary relationships. Synteny blocks, or locally collinear blocks, denote genomic regions with orthologous and co-arranged genes relative to the reference genome [44]. Synteny analysis revealed numerous synteny blocks in BF19 and Br. formosus NRRL NRS-863. As illustrated in Fig. S3, these synteny blocks were notably fragmented, with the majority being relatively short. Conversely, the presence of syntenic blocks with two other Br. brevis species was limited. Our findings suggest that significant genome rearrangements occurred in BF19 and Br. formosus NRRL NRS-863 following their divergence from their most recent common ancestor.

Identification and distribution of gene clusters in BF19

A total of 27 gene clusters were identified within the three selected strains (refer to the Supplemental Table S6). Among these strains, NRPSs were the most abundant, followed by PKSs. Among these clusters, 13 displayed homologous sequences in the MIBiG database with a similarity exceeding 50% (refer to Fig. 8A and Supplementary Table S6), primarily comprising petrobactin, tyrocidine, bacillopaline, macrobrevin, and gramicidin. Notably, only three gene clusters presented homologous sequences with 100% similarity, including bacillopaline and gramicidin, which are present in all three strains, and macrobrevin (BGC-11), which is exclusively found in BF19.

The structural analysis of the biosynthesis-related gene cluster (BGC-11), which comprises 11 core biosynthetic genes spanning a total length of 90,771 nucleotides (nt), is presented in Fig. 8B. Within BGC-11, both trans-AT-PKS (transacyltransferase polyketide synthases) and PKS-like (polyketide synthase) domains were identified, suggesting that BGC-11 is a large hybrid enzyme with multiple functional domains involved in polyketide compound synthesis. Polyketides (PKs) and nonribosomal peptides (NRPs) are secondary metabolites synthesized by microorganisms, facilitating their adaptation to environmental conditions and providing resistance to various stressors. Additionally, a total of 14 coding sequences within the BGC corresponded to the structural domains of Brevibacillus sp. Leaf182. The products encoded by these sequences exhibit remarkable 100% similarity to macrobrevin (Fig. 8C), which was originally isolated from the mesophilic bacterium Brevibacillus sp. Leaf182 and shows promising antibacterial properties against nosocomial pathogens. These findings suggest that macrobrevin may demonstrate strong antagonistic activities against bitter gourd wilt caused by FOM.

Preliminary characterization of the active metabolites of BF19

To determine whether the fungistatic activity of strain BF19 was caused by BF19 itself or secondary fungistatic metabolites produced by BF19. The fungistatic effects of fermentation with strain BF19 were explored. The results revealed that the inhibition rates of fermentation with strain BF19 diluted 5, 10 and 20 times against F. oxysporum were 77.71%, 73.84% and 54.46%, respectively (Fig. S4), which indicated that the secondary metabolites produced by strain BF19 exhibit antagonistic activity against F. oxysporum. Identifying the antifungal metabolites of BF19 can be helpful for its application in biocontrol. The crude extract of the fermentation agent of strain BF19 was subsequently collected. A total of 1109.7 mg of crude extract was obtained from 2 L of fermentation broth. The crude extract of BF19 was dissolved in sterile water to achieve a concentration of 10 mg/mL and then stored at 4 °C. The antifungal activity of the crude extract against FOM is illustrated in Fig. 9A. In comparison to the antagonistic activity of the supernatant from strain BF19, the crude extract at a concentration of 500 µg/mL demonstrated superior inhibitory activity against FOM, achieving an inhibition rate of 58.33%.

These antimicrobial agents produced by antagonistic bacteria primarily emanate from two major pathways: ribosomal synthesis and nonribosomal synthesis. Presently, the secondary metabolites with antibacterial efficacy from Bacillus species that have been isolated and characterized predominantly include antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) and nonribosomal peptides (NRPs). Quantitatively, the primary antimicrobial compounds synthesized by Bacillus species are nonribosomal peptide antibiotics, with conventional antimicrobial peptides following closely in abundance. The crude extract of strain BF19 was subjected to active compound analysis via ultrahigh-performance LC‒MS/MS. Discussion

In the present study, among 38 isolated candidate antagonistic strains, strain BF19 exhibited the most significant inhibitory effect on FOM under both plate and greenhouse conditions. Moreover, Br. brevis BF19 not only demonstrated a 53.33% biocontrol efficacy in the greenhouse pots (Fig. 1) but also significantly increased the average plant height, root fresh weight, and dry weight by 1.32, 2.24, and 2.18 times, respectively (Fig. 2). The antimicrobial and plant growth-promoting abilities of BF19 were significantly greater than those reported for other strains of Bacillus brevis with biocontrol properties [17, 46]. However, the control efficiency of biocontrol strains is subject to various influencing factors, such as colonization ability, inoculation method, environmental conditions, and the production of antifungal compounds [47]. For example, colonization ability can be an indicator of enhanced biocontrol capacity [48]. BF19 effectively and stably colonized the roots, stems, and leaves of bitter gourd. Notably, after 28 days post-inoculation, the colonization density in the roots reached 1 × 103 CFU/g (Fig. S7). The presence of numerous colonization-associated gene sequences in the genome (Supplementary Table S7) further confirms the stable and effective colonization ability of strain BF19 on bitter gourd tissues.

Given the complex diversity within the Brevibacillus genus and their limited response to conventional biochemical identification tests, along with the similarity of 16S rRNA gene sequences among many Bacillus and Brevibacillus strains, traditional differentiation methods pose a challenge [49]. As sequencing techniques have advanced, more Brevibacillus genome sequences are being published. Building a species tree from these sequences aids in refining strain taxonomy. For example, strains CECT 8237 and CECT 8238 were initially classified as B. subtilis on the basis of 16S rDNA and metabolic profiles. However, conserved protein sequences identified these biocontrol strains as part of the B. amyloliquefaciens group [50]. ANI and AAI cutoffs of 94–96% are increasingly recognized as the gold standard for delineating microbial species on the basis of genomic analysis [51, 52]. In this study, we identified strain BF19 as belonging to Br. brevis on the basis of analysis of its 16S rRNA and core genome sequences (Fig. S1 and Fig. 3). The BF19 chromosome presented a high level of similarity, with 98.03% ANI and 98.35% AAI shared with Br. brevis NBRC 100599, significantly surpassing the recommended threshold of 96% for species delineation. Our findings not only accurately classify BF19 but also offer genomic resources for enhanced identification of related Brevibacillus species in future studies.

Induced resistance by PGPR, such as B. pumulus, Pseudomonas putida, Flavomonas oryzihabitans, and Serratia marcescens, enhances the defensive capacity of plants against pathogens through induced systemic resistance (ISR) [53]. Bacterial determinants such as siderophores, pyoverdin, salicylic acid, and flagellins [54] contribute to the development of resistance. B. subtilis strain 168 [55] is commonly used as the reference genome for gene coordinates in the majority of experimental studies involving biocontrol bacteria [56]. Compared with the genome of B. subtilis 168, we identified numerous coding sequences in the BF19 genome associated with induced plant resistance. These include flagellar proteins (HAG, HAP1, HAP2), as well as genes involved in teichoic acid biosynthesis (tuaC, tuaD) and lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis protein (tuaB) (Supplementary Table S7). Additionally, there are abundant coding sequences related to plant growth promotion, such as indole-3-acetic acid (ysnE, yhcX, and dhaS), 2,3-butanediol and ethanolamine (alsD, alsS, alsR, and bdhA), and nutrient uptake promotion (yusV), among others (Supplementary Table S7). However, which specific genes directly and indirectly impact plant disease resistance and growth promotion and how they do so are crucial questions that require further in-depth investigation.

To identify specific traits of the BF19 strain, we selected three closely related Brevibacillus strains for comparative genome analysis. We identified a total of 591 BF19-specific genes present in the BF19 chromosome but absent in the other three strains. Furthermore, analysis of the COG database revealed that these genes are involved in inorganic ion transport and secondary metabolite biosynthesis (Fig. 5). In summary, our results indicate an enrichment of functional genes, especially those linked to secondary metabolism, across various species and strains from diverse origins. Secondary metabolism is crucial in plant–bacteria interactions. In this study, comparative genomics analysis and prediction revealed the presence of a gene cluster in the BF19 genome capable of synthesizing macrobrevin, which is absent in the other two strains (Fig. 8). Importantly, we further confirmed the abundant presence of macrobrevin in fermentation products of BF19 through LC/MS-MS analysis. Macrobrevin is a trans-acyltransferase polyketide synthase-derived antibiotic first isolated from the Arabidopsis phyllosphere bacteria Brevibacillus sp. Leaf182 [57]. It has demonstrated potent antagonistic activities against Staphylococcus aureus, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Streptococcus pyogenes, among others [45]. However, whether macrobrevin produced by BF19 exerts a growth-promoting effect on host plants requires further investigation. Additionally, further research should focus on whether the macrobrevin gene cluster of strain BF19 has an effect on bitter gourd wilt.

Conclusion

The newly identified PGPR strain BF19 of Br. brevis, which exhibits efficient colonization ability, was confirmed to possess strong antibacterial activity against the bitter gourd wilt pathogen FOM through both in vivo and in vitro experiments. Whole-genome sequencing and comparative genomic analysis revealed that it harbors numerous potential genes responsible for plant biocontrol mechanisms. Moreover, the effectiveness of the Br. brevis strain BF19 and its crude extract against bitter gourd wilt has also been confirmed. Overall, we propose that strain BF19 could represent a promising candidate for the development of biological agents targeting the control of bitter gourd wilt.
