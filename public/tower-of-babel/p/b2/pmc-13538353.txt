How game score shapes anticipatory swing decisions in baseball players: a functional MRI study
Authors: Yin-Hua Chen, Ya-Ling Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Contextual information influences action anticipation in sport, but how game-score cues shape perceptual processing and neural correlates remains unclear. This study used functional MRI to examine behavioral and neural responses to score context in baseball batters of varying skills. Skilled and intermediate batters viewed far-ahead or even score scenarios followed by a pitcher’s delivery and predicted whether to swing. A no-score control condition was included. Participants rated perceived stress and confidence after each condition. Score cues did not affect anticipation accuracy in either group, but confidence was higher in far-ahead than even-score conditions. Skilled batters reported lower perceived stress than intermediates in the even-score condition, reflecting more adaptive responses. During score presentation, skilled batters showed lower right superior temporal gyrus activity than intermediates, suggesting more efficient integration of contextual cues. Group-by-score interactions revealed distinct neural patterns: skilled batters engaged left angular gyrus and posterior middle temporal gyrus for even-score information, reflecting semantic and symbolic processing. Conversely, intermediates recruited left middle to posterior cingulate and bilateral middle frontal gyri under far-ahead scores, indicating attentional control. During anticipation, skilled batters showed reduced caudate activity in the even-score condition, reflecting decreased cognitive engagement, while intermediates showed increased dorsal attention network activity in the no-score condition, reflecting compensatory attentional recruitment under uncertainty. These findings demonstrate skill-dependent neural reallocation between score processing and anticipation stages, highlighting how expertise shapes the integration of contextual and kinematic information to guide adaptive anticipatory decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-46951-7.

Introduction

Skilled athletes consistently outperform less-skilled counterparts in anticipating an opponent’s action while observing incomplete action sequences. This advantage is often attributed to their enhanced ability to detect and utilize key kinematic cues, refined through extensive perceptual-motor experience1–3. For instance, skilled baseball batters anticipate pitch location or type more accurately than intermediates or non-players by extracting critical information embedded in the pitcher’s motion and early ball trajectory4–7, and they report higher confidence in their anticipatory decisions5.

Beyond kinematics, skilled players integrate contextual information such as pitch count, field positions, opponent’s action tendencies, and game score to reduce uncertainty4,6,8–13. Congruent or valid contextual cues can enhance anticipatory accuracy9–11,13, though some studies reported limited effects14. Despite their potential importance, game-score cues are rarely examined in isolation, making it difficult to determine their specific influence on anticipation (e.g.,11–14). Clarifying this is critical, as score differentials represent a central source of situational stress, which can disrupt performance15,16. Athletes consistently report elevated stress and reduced confidence before competition compared to rest or training (reviewed by 17,18).

Anticipating others’ actions engages a distributed neural system including the action observation network (AON19,20), subcortical motor structures such as caudate, putamen, and the cerebellum4,5,21. Core AON nodes, such as the inferior frontal gyrus/ventral premotor cortex (IFG/vPMC) and superior and inferior parietal lobules/intraparietal sulcus (SPL/IPL/IPS), exhibit mirror properties, activating both during action execution and observation22,23. These overlapping activations support common-coding theory, which posits that perception and production of actions mutually facilitate each other24,25. Individuals with greater motor experience typically show stronger engagement of the mirror neuron system and more accurate perception of observed actions5,26–35.

Baseball provides a unique context for dissociating perceptual and motor experience. Pitchers possess extensive motor experience with pitching mechanics, whereas batters accumulate substantial perceptual experience observing and anticipating these actions5. Correspondingly, skilled batters displayed heightened activation in visual motion-sensitive regions (extrastriate cortex) than non-players5and reduced activation in biological-motion-sensitive region (the posterior middle temporal gyrus/superior temporal sulcus, pMTG/pSTS36,37); than intermediates4. These neural patterns do not necessarily change linearly with expertise4, underscoring the complexity of expertise-related neural adaptations.

Only a few studies have investigated neural responses to game-score cues. Simonet et al.14reported that skilled cricket batters showed stronger occipital alpha event-related desynchronization (ERD) compared to less-skilled cricket batters when contextual cues (runs scored, overs bowled, wickets taken) were available, suggesting greater task engagement or increased reliance on semantic and episodic memory (e.g.,38). However, isolated game-score effects and confidence were not examined. Thus, the neural and behavioral mechanisms by which game-score information influences anticipation remain unclear, particularly across different skill levels.

To address this gap, the present study examined how game-score cues influence anticipatory behavior in baseball players of varying expertise. Skilled and intermediate batters viewed score scenarios followed by a video of a pitcher delivering a pitch while undergoing fMRI and indicated whether they would swing. This study employed an event-related fMRI study with a mixed factorial structure, including group (experts vs. intermediates) as a between-participants factor and game score condition as a within-participants factor. Subjective ratings of perceived stress and confidence were also collected. We hypothesized that (1) expertise-related differences in anticipatory accuracy, confidence, and perceived stress would be amplified when score cues were provided, and (2) these behavioral patterns would correspond with distinct perceptual-neural processing within the AON and visual and biological-motion-sensitive regions. This multilevel approach provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how expertise shapes the integration of contextual and kinematic information during anticipation and informs how athletes make adaptive decisions in sport.

Materials & methods

Participants

We recruited 18 skilled male collegiate baseball batters (SBs; 20.19 ± 1.0 years, 176.4 ± 5.0 cm) from highly ranked Taiwanese university baseball teams. These players had extensive baseball experience (10.5 ± 2.6 years, starting at 10.2 ± 1.7 years of age), engaged in intensive weekly training (20.1 ± 8.0 h), and actively participated in national- and/or international-level competitions at the time of experiment. For comparison, 21 intermediate batters (IBs; 21.1 ± 2.7 years, 175.0 ± 6.6 cm) with moderate experience (4.5 ± 3.0 years, starting at age 17.1 ± 2.8 years) and lower weekly training loads (6.7 ± 4.5 h) were recruited. These participants had limited competition experience, participating at most twice per year in departmental competitions within their universities. An a priori power analysis was conducted using MorePower 6.0.439 to determine the appropriate sample size for our 3-way (2 groups x 3 game score conditions x 2 pitch types) mixed-design analyses of variance (ANOVA). The analysis indicated that a sample of 32 participants would be sufficient to achieve 80% power at an alpha level of 0.05, assuming a large effect size (ηp2 = 0.1379) based on Runswick et al.11 with a similar (3-way) experiment design and overall effect size. All participants were right-handed, with normal or corrected-to-normal eyesight and had no neurological disorders or contraindications to MRI. Written informed consent was obtained in accordance with procedures approved by the research committee of Fu-Jen Catholic University (protocol code: C109096; approval date: 9 April 2021) and the 1964 Helsinki declaration and amendments. Participants received a participation fee upon completing the experiment.

Manipulation of game score

To determine the game-score conditions used in this study, we conducted a pilot test with a separate group of skilled male collegiate baseball players (n = 52), who rated their perceived stress level (1—9 scale) under three game score scenarios: far-ahead and far-behind (score differences > 3 runs), and even/slightly-behind/ahead (0- or 1-run difference, hereafter referred to even) conditions. A repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of game score, F(2,102) = 85.809, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.627, CI [0.526, 0.690], with even scores evoking the highest stress level, followed by far-behind and far-ahead conditions (mean values of 6.333, 3.486, and 2.668, respectively). All pairwise comparisons were significant (p values < 0.05). Based on these results, we selected the even and far-ahead conditions to represent two extremes for the experiment. A control condition with no score information was also included. To enhance ecological validity, realistic game ambience was provided by playing four 5-minutes audio recordings captured from the dugout (the bench area where players and coaches sit during games) to simulate realistic game atmosphere.

Stimuli

Two pitchers, one left-handed (20.8 years old, 10 years of experience) and one right-handed (21.5 years old, 11 years of experience), were recorded throwing strikes (four-seam fastballs at 120–130 km/hr) from the pitcher’s mound to an imaginary strike zone divided into a 3 × 3 grid, simulating the perspective of a 175–180 cm right-handed batter. All recording and video editing procedures followed those described in Chen et al.30, except that the videos in the present study were captured from a right-handed batter’s perspective rather than an umpire’s perspective. For each pitcher, 36 pitching videos were selected, comprising 18 strikes and 18 balls. To increase stimulus variability, the videos were truncated at 33, 67, or 100 ms after ball release. The average video duration was 3.33 s (SD = 0.47). Additionally, four videos per pitcher depicting warm-up movements were included as control stimuli.

Task

As shown in Fig. 1, in each trial participants were presented with game score information (far-ahead, even, or none), followed by a pitching video depicting either a strike or a ball. They were asked to predict their swing decision by pressing one of the two buttons, “swing” or “no swing”, with their right index or middle finger, counterbalanced across participants. When uncertain, they could select “I don’t know” using the right ring finger. For warm-up videos, participants pressed a separate button with their right little finger. After completing all the trials within each condition, participants rated their perceived stress and confidence in their decisions on a 1–4 scale (low to high).

Procedure

Participants first completed a safety screening, received a briefing, and provided informed consent. They then watched full-length sequences of the pitching videos and completed several practice trials to become familiar with the task. Afterward, they were escorted to the scanner room for data acquisition. Participants lay in a supine position with their head stabilized in the coil. MRI data acquisition began with a 6-minute resting-state scan (eyes open), followed by functional scanning during the anticipation task.

Each trial started with a 1-second fixation cross, followed by a 2-second game score display, and then a 3–4 s pitching video. Participant had 2.5 s to respond; missed responses were marked as errors. The inter-trial interval (ITI) was jittered between 2 and 5 s. Each run consisted of 40 trials, including 36 pitching videos and 4 warm-up videos from the same pitcher. In total, participants completed 6 runs (2 pitchers x 3 game score conditions). The sequence of runs was arranged so that all three game score conditions were tested separately, and the order of game score conditions counterbalanced across participants. Within each score condition, the assignment of left- and right-handed pitchers, as well as trial order within each run, were randomized. Brief breaks were given between runs to prevent fatigue. After completing the task runs, participants underwent structural scanning, including 12 min of diffusion-tensor imaging and 8 min of T1-weighted imaging. Diffusion-tensor and resting-state data were collected but not included in the current analyses. The entire session lasted approximately 1.5 h. Task execution and stimuli presentation were managed using MATLAB (R2016b, MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA). Behavioral responses, including swing decisions, reaction times, and ratings of stress and confidence, were recorded alongside simultaneous brain imaging data.

Data acquisition

MRI images were collected using a 64-channel head coil in a 3T scanner (Skyra, Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany). A T2*-weighted gradient-echo echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence was used for functional scanning, with 3 mm slice thickness, 90-degree flip angle, 41 slices, 2000 ms repetition time (TR), and 25 ms echo time (TE), on a 64 × 64 matrix in a 216 × 216 mm2 field of view (FOV). The anatomical, T1- weighted high-resolution image (1 × 1 × 1 mm3) was acquired using a standard MPRAGE sequence, with 7-degree flip angle, 3500 ms TR, 2.26 ms TE, and 1100 ms inversion time (TI), on a 256 × 256 matrix in a 256 × 256 mm2 FOV.

Data analysis & statistics

Behavioral data

Accuracy was analyzed using a three-way (2 groups x 3 game score conditions x 2 pitch types) mixed-model ANOVAs. Perceived stress and confidence were analyzed with separate two-way (2 groups x 3 game score conditions) mixed-model ANOVAs. Statistical analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS 27.0, with α set at 0.05. In situations in which sphericity is violated, Greenhouse Geisser correction was employed. Bonferroni’s corrections were applied for post hoc comparisons. Effect sizes (ηp2 and Cohen’s d) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported.

fMRI data

Preprocessing and statistical analysis of brain images were conducted using SPM12 (Wellcome Trust Center for Neuroimaging, London, UK). Functional images underwent slice timing and head motion correction. Volumes with frame-wise displacement exceeding 0.5 mm or derivative variance greater than 0.5% of global BOLD signal were excluded. Images were co-register to each participant’s segmented gray matter, normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, and spatially smoothed using an 8 mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel. Statistical analysis was performed at individual and group levels using the general linear model (GLM). At the individual level, data were modelled with all task events, including fixation, game score presentation, video display, response choice, jittered ITI, and stress and confidence ratings periods, with their onsets and durations explicitly specified. Events of game score presentation were modeled as categorical conditions with two levels (far-ahead and even). Events of video display were organized according to the preceding game score condition (far-ahead, even, and no-score) and further categorized by participant responses (swinging at strikes, not swinging at strikes, swinging at balls, not swinging at balls, no responses, and identifying warm-up videos). Nuisance regressors included (1) motor components associated with button press during response and stress/confidence rating periods, and (2) six motion-correction parameters derived from realignment (three translations: x, y, z; and three rotations: pitch, roll, yaw) to account for head movement in each run. Each regressor was convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF) to model the expected BOLD signal, without including temporal and dispersion derivatives.

At the group level, two sets of analyses were performed. The first set focused on brain images acquired during the presentation of game score information. A 2 (group) x 2 (game score condition: even, far-ahead) mixed-design full factorial analysis was used to examine the neural correlates of game score cues across the two groups. The second set of analysis focused on brain images acquired during video presentation. For each group separately, a within-participants full factorial analyses were performed using a 3 (game score) x 2 (pitch type) x 2 (swing decision) design to assess anticipation-related neural activity. For these two sets of analyses, group was treated as a between-participants factor, while all other variables were within-participants factors analyzed using random effects. For the second set of analysis, main effects unrelated to game score (i.e., pitch type and swing decision) are reported in Supplementary Material. Statistical maps were thresholded at p < 0.005 (uncorrected), with multiple comparison corrections at p < 0.01 using a Monte Carlo-determined cluster extent across the whole brain. Significant F-tests were followed by t-contrasts to determine the direction of effects.

Results

Behavior results

Accuracy

The main effect of pitch type yielded significance, F(1,37) = 25.227, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.405, CI [0.197, 0.549], with batters showing higher accuracy for strikes compared to balls (mean values of 58.4% and 43.9% for strikes and balls, respectively). Furthermore, it interacted with the main effect of group, F(1,37) = 5.349, p = 0.026, ηp2 = 0.126, CI [0.008, 0.291] (Fig. 2A). It was because SBs showed comparatively lower accuracy than IBs for balls (p = 0.004; mean values of 38.7% and 49.0% for SBs and IBs, respectively), but there were no group differences for strikes (p = 0.386; mean values of 59.9% and 56.9% for SBs and IBs, respectively). On the other hand, accuracy was higher for strikes than balls for SBs (p < 0.001), whereas the differences were marginally significant for IBs (p = 0.054). The main effect of pitch type also interacted with the main effect of game score, F(2, 74) = 3.205, p = 0.046, ηp2 = 0.080, CI [0.001, 0.174] (Fig. 2B). Post-hoc analyses indicated that there were no differences among the three game score conditions when participants anticipated their swing decisions for strikes (p values > 0.537 for each pairwise comparisons; mean values of 56.7%, 58.7%, and 59.8% for even, far-ahead, and no-score conditions, respectively). But when they anticipated their swing decisions for balls, they demonstrated the highest accuracy for even condition, followed by far-ahead and then no-score conditions (mean values of 46.5%, 42.7%, and 42.3% for even, far-ahead, and no-score conditions, respectively), with differences between even and far-ahead conditions most prominent (p = 0.084) and the other pairwise comparisons not reaching statistical differences (p values > 0.159). On the other hand, participants always showed higher accuracy for strikes than balls no matter in which game score conditions (p values < 0.002). Conversely, the other main and interaction effects were not significant: F(2,74) = 0.294, p = 0.746, ηp2 = 0.008, CI [< 0.001, 0.047] for game score; F(1,37) = 3.930, p = 0.055, ηp2 = 0.096, CI [< 0.001, 0.255] for group; F(2,74) = 0.471, p = 0.626, ηp2 = 0.013, CI [< 0.001, 0.062] for game score-by-group; F(2,74) = 1.700, p = 0.190, ηp2= 0.044, CI [< 0.001, 0.124] for the 3-way interaction effects.

Perceived stress level

The main effect of game score was significant, F(2,74) = 21.462, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.367, CI [0.213, 0.474], with participants showing the highest stress level for even condition, followed by far-ahead and no-score conditions (mean values of 2.163, 1.556, and 1.562 for even, far-ahead, and no-score conditions, respectively). The pairwise comparisons between conditions were all significant (p values < 0.001), except for the one between far-ahead and no-score conditions (p = 1.000). Furthermore, there was a group-by-game score interaction effect, F (2,74) = 3.439, p = 0.037, ηp2 = 0.085, CI [0.003, 0.182] (Fig. 2C). Post-hoc analyses indicated both groups showed this aforementioned pattern (mean values of 1.944, 1.611, and 1.528 for respective conditions for SBs, and mean values of 2.381, 1.500, and 1.595 for IBs) and the differences were significant between even and no-score conditions for both groups (p values < 0.039). Whereas the differences between even and far-ahead conditions remained only for IBs (p < 0.001) but not for SBs (p = 0.105). One other hand, SBs showed lower stress level compared to IBs specifically for even condition (p = 0.027), whereas they were not different from IBs for the other conditions (p values > 0.505). The main effect of group was not significant, F(1,37) = 0.902, p = 0.348, ηp2 = 0.024, CI [< 0.001, 0.147].

Confidence level

The two-way ANOVA detected a significant main effect of game score, F(2,74) = 4.883, p = 0.010, ηp2 = 0.117, CI [0.017, 0.222], with both groups being more certain of their decisions for far-ahead condition compared to even condition, p = 0.013 (mean values of 2.865, 3.167, and 3.062 for even, far-ahead, and no-score conditions, respectively; no differences were found for other pairwise comparisons, p values > 0.143). The main effect of group, F(1,37) = 0.528, p = 0.472, ηp2 = 0.014, CI [< 0.001, 0.125], as well as the group-by-game score interactions, F(2,74) = 0.932, p = 0.398, ηp2 = 0.025, CI [< 0.001, 0.115], were not significant.

Imaging results

Due to a technical issue, fMRI data of one IB were not acquired. Additionally, 3 SBs were excluded due to extensive head motion. As a result, the final fMRI analyses included data from 15 SBs and 20 IBs.

Neural correlates of game-score information

As shown in Fig. 3A, the first set of fMRI analyses revealed widely spread activations across the superior/middle temporal, parietal and occipital cortices, extending bilaterally to the cerebellum and including the middle, inferior and orbitofrontal cortices for both groups. Game-score information elicited a group effect, with IBs showing greater activation than SBs in the right superior temporal gyrus (STG). An interaction effect was observed in the left dorsal posterior cingulate cortex extending to posterior middle cingulate cortex (dPCC/pMCC), the angular gyrus (AG) extending to the posterior MTG (pMTG), and the bilateral middle frontal gyri (MFG) (Fig. 3B; Table 1). Subsequent paired t-tests among conditions revealed that in the left AG/pMTG, greater activation in the even condition compared to the far-ahead condition was seen for SBs but not for IBs. In the other clusters (left dPCC/pMCC and bilateral MFG), IBs showed greater activation in the far-ahead condition than in the even condition, a pattern not observed in SBs. Activation maps for other contrasts (e.g., group differences in both even and far-ahead conditions) did not overlapped with the interaction effects map. Moreover, the inverse contrast between groups (SBs > IBs) and the main effect of the game score did not yield significant clusters.

Neural correlates of action anticipation followed by game-score information

Skilled batters (SBs)

As one SB chose to swing at all ball pitches, the absence of decisions involving not swinging excluded him from this data analysis. Consequently, only 14 SBs were included in this analysis. As shown in Fig. 4A, the action anticipation processes activated the AON and bilateral caudate. Specifically, there was a significant main effect of game score in the left caudate (see Fig. 4B; Table 2). Subsequent t-tests indicated this effect stemmed from the stronger activation during the even condition compared to far-ahead condition, with no-score condition also showing activation that marginally reached the threshold (voxels = 68). The main effect of decision was significant (see Supplementary Material), whereas the main effect of pitch type and all two-way and three-way interactions did not yield significant clusters.

Intermediate batters (IBs)

The action anticipation processes also activated the AON (see Fig. 4A). As shown in Fig. 5A, there was a significant main effect of game score in eight clusters, including the SFG, SPL and IOG bilaterally, as well as the lingual gyrus (LG) and the left MFG. Post-hoc t-tests revealed greater activation in the no-score condition compared to the far-ahead condition across all clusters. Notably, the left SPL cluster also showed greater activation in the even compared to far-ahead condition, while bilateral IOG clusters further showed greater activation in the no-score condition compared to even condition. A significant game score-by-pitch type interaction emerged in two clusters, including the left MFG and a cluster spanning the postcentral gyrus (PoCG) and extending to the precentral gyrus (PreCG; see Fig. 5B; Table 3). The left MFG cluster overlapped with no-score > far-ahead main effect. Post-hoc analyses indicated that the game score effect was stronger for ball pitches than for strike pitches, reflected in a larger (no-score > far-ahead) contrast. The contrast also elicited stronger activation in the PreCG and PoCG. The main effect of decision was significant (see Supplementary Material). Additionally, the 3-way interaction was significant in the mSFG bilaterally (see Fig. 5B). Subsequent t-tests showed stronger activations when IBs made correct swing decisions (swing at strike pitches, no swing at ball pitches) in the far-ahead condition compared to the other two conditions. The main effect of pitch type and other two-way interactions did not yield significant clusters.

Discussion

This study examined how game-score cues shape anticipatory decision-making in baseball players of varying expertise, using a multilevel approach across behavioral, neural and subjective levels. Although score cues were presented before the pitch sequence, rather than emerging naturally during play, they remain relevant sources of information players use to guide their perceptual process. We manipulated three conditions: an even or slightly ahead/behind score, a far-ahead score, and a control with no score, with realism enhanced by incorporating dugout audio from collegiate games.

Reduced non-swing rate for balls across score conditions reflect greater strategic utility or perceived swing action possibilities

The observed anticipation accuracy patterns generally align with Chen et al.4, particularly the higher accuracy for strikes than for balls. Our findings further indicate that score context modulates the response criterion for initiating a swing. In the even-score condition, the non-swing rate for balls (46.5%) was comparable to the 49% reported by Chen et al.4(t38 = -1.037, p = 0.291) under an explicitly high-stakes condition (tie game, full count, 2 outs, and bases loaded in the last inning). This similarity suggests that even-score condition effectively induced a conservative response criterion, whereby batters required greater certainty before committing to a swing. In contrast, the far-ahead condition showed a tendency of reduced non-swing rate (42.7%), consistent with a lower response criterion. Importantly, this shift reflects a context-dependent adjustment of decision policy rather than a change in perceptual sensitivity10,40. From a strategic evaluation perspective, decreasing situational stakes may reduce the anticipated cost of unfavorable outcomes (e.g., striking out), thereby increasing tolerance for uncertainty in swing decisions. In this sense, batters recalibrate their judgment utility based on contextual cues, extending findings from soccer in which players integrate knowledge of opponent action tendencies into decision-making9,10,41,42 to the domain of baseball. From an ecological perspective, score context may also reshape the landscape of swing affordances43,44,45 dynamically altering which actions are perceived as viable under baring competitive constraints.

Moreover, the non-swing rate for balls across conditions partially mirrored the pattern of perceived stress (even > no-score and/or far-ahead), indicating that lower stress associated with less conservative swinging decisions. The higher confidence reported in the far-ahead compared to even condition further supports this interpretation. The absence of group differences in swinging at strikes may relate to using only fastballs, allowing intermediates to perform comparably to skilled batters. Future studies should incorporate more pitch types to test this possibility.

Higher perceived stress during even condition in intermediates

The absence of group-by-game score interaction in accuracy aligns with Simonet et al.14, who reported potential ceiling effects when contextual or kinematic information alone was sufficient for anticipation. However, confidence and stress were not examined in that study. Here, although overall accuracy did not differ between groups, experiential responses did: both groups reported greater confidence and lower stress in the far-ahead condition, whereas IBs experienced significantly higher stress than SBs in even condition. Given that both groups had at least four years of baseball experience, the anticipation task might not have been sensitive enough to differentiate overall accuracy levels. Nevertheless, even in a laboratory setting without actual swinging, IBs exhibited stronger affective response, possibly reflecting less adaptive calibration to competitive pressure. Future work should enhance ecological validity by implementing dynamically updated score contexts (e.g., trial-by-trial adjustments) to better approximate real competitive environments.

Contextual processing of score-related auditory cues: Increased right STG engagement in intermediates

During score presentation, IBs showed higher right STG (area Te3) activity than SBs (Fig. 3A). While the STG more broadly has been implicated in speech and language processing46, one earlier work has also linked STG activity to outcome evaluation and response switching in decision-making contexts47. Moreover, the right Te3 area has been associated with passive listening (reviewed by48). Given that participants were exposed to authentic dugout recordings containing layered and ecologically valid auditory cues, the higher STG activity in IBs may reflect greater neural recruitment for extracting task-relevant score information from a complex auditory environment. Importantly, rather than attributing this activity to a single cognitive operation, the pattern may indicate less automized contextual processing in IBs, requiring additional integration of auditory and score information. In contrast, SBs may rely on more experience-dependent tuning to informational structure embedded in such competitive contexts, consistent with ecological accounts of expertise emphasizing efficient perception-action coupling45,49,50.

Context-sensitive semantic and symbolic processing of even-score information via left AG/pMTG activity in experts

A group-by-game score interaction further revealed that SBs demonstrated greater left AG/pMTG activity for even versus far-ahead scores (Fig. 3B). The AG has been implicated in semantic integration51–53, including number-related cognition. The left pMTG has likewise been associated with language-related processes54,55, as well as arithmetic and numerical operations (reviewed by56,57). Given the explicit numerical nature of score information, increased left AG/pMTG activity in SBs may reflect greater semantic-symbolic evaluation of score-related meaning, particularly under even-score situations that carry greater competitive relevance. This interpretation is consistent with prior findings that expertise is associated with more differentiated engagement of task-relevant information as shown in increased occipital alpha ERD14, although cross-modal comparisons should be interpreted cautiously.

Context-dependent attentional control under far-ahead scores via left dPCC/pMCC and bilateral MFG in intermediates

IBs showed higher activity for the far-ahead than even condition in the left dPCC/pMCC and bilateral MFG (Fig. 3B). The cingulate cortex is functionally heterogeneous58; specifically, the dPCC is strongly integrated with fronto-parietal and dorsal attention networks (DAN), both of which are central to cognitive control59. The co-activated bilateral MFG are key components of DAN, further supporting increased goal-directed attentional allocation60,61. Notably, the right MFG serves as a convergence site between dorsal and ventral attention systems, enabling flexible adaptation to contextual demands62,63. The concurrent engagement of the left dPCC/pMCC and bilateral MFG in IBs during the far-ahead condition may therefore reflect increased recruitment of control-related attentional processes in response to favorable contextual score cues. Rather than indicating superior control capacity, this pattern suggests that attentional engagement in IBs is context-dependent and increases under conditions of reduced competitive pressure. This interpretation is further corroborated by the lower subjective stress ratings reported by IBs in this condition.

Distinct neural patterns of action anticipation under game-score influence in experts and intermediates

Neural activations during action anticipation (pitching video presentation, Fig. 4A) replicated previous findings that bilateral AON and cerebellar regions support sport-specific anticipation21, including pitch outcome judgment and swing decisions in baseball4,5,30. Crucially, the influence of game score differed markedly between groups. In IBs, game-score effects were localized to the left caudate in SBs (Fig. 4B), whereas IBs recruited 8 distinct regions (Fig. 5A).

In SBs, the effect was centered in the body of the caudate, a key node of the associative loop (for reviews, see64,65). This region integrates inputs from the dorsolateral PFC, preSMA, and posterior parietal cortex66,67 to support strategic planning and execution and goal-directed action-outcome learning (reviewed by65). Reduced caudate activity (i.e., more negative BOLD activity) in the even relative to the far-ahead condition may reflect a shit in strategic processing under high-stakes pressure. Rather than indicating diminished engagement, this pattern may suggest increased neural efficiency effect or a transition from effortful strategic planning to more streamlined, expertise-dependent action selection.

Conversely, IBs exhibited an opposite pattern: the no-score condition elicited higher activation than the other two score-informed conditions. These clusters were located primarily within the DAN, including MFG, SPL, and visual cortices. This broader recruitment likely reflects compensatory, effortful attentional engagement under heightened uncertainty (i.e., when score information was unavailable). Additionally, game score interacted with pitch type in the left MFG and sensorimotor regions (Fig. 5B), and a mSFG cluster exhibited a 3-way interaction among game score, pitch type, and decision (Fig. 5C). Further investigation is needed to clarify the functional significance of these interactions in varying competitive contexts.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into how game-score cues influence action anticipation in baseball batters, underscoring the dynamic interplay between expertise and the processing of contextual information. Expert batters, shaped by extensive competitive experience, exhibited greater attunement to even-score cues with lower perceived stress compared to intermediates. At the neural level, they showed lower right STG activity during score presentation, suggesting more efficient integration of auditory and contextual cues or more stable action selection. Moreover, group-by-game score interaction effects with opposite activation patterns across distinct brain regions indicated that experts and intermediates engaged different processes to handle specific score scenarios: experts showed context-sensitive semantic and symbolic processing for even-score information via left AG/pMTG, whereas intermediates possibly engaged attentional control under far-ahead scores via left dPCC/pMCC and bilateral MFG. During anticipation, SBs showed reduced caudate activity in the even-score condition, reflecting decreased cognitive engagement, while IBs showed increased dorsal attention network activity in the no-score condition, suggesting compensatory recruitment under uncertainty. These results suggest dynamic neural reallocation between score processing and action anticipation stages depending on skill level, highlight how expertise shapes the integration of contextual and kinematic information to guide adaptive anticipatory decisions

While this study incorporated advanced tools including fMRI and subjective ratings, the used of a 2D video task and keypress response, as opposed to embodied and real-time action, represents a limitation. Future studies should enhance ecological validity by integrating more immersive or embodied measures, such as portable EEG or functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy systems, ideally in field-based environments where perception and action unfold dynamically and reciprocally.
