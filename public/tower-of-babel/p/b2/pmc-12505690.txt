Community-based evaluation of cardiovascular risk using QRISK®3 in type 2 diabetes mellitus population in Gujarat, India
Authors: Yogesh M, Roshni Vamja, Vijay Vala, Bhumika Surati, Jay Nagda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCardiovascular disease (CVD) rates are rising rapidly among Indians with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). This study compared two cardiovascular risk assessment tools - QRISK®3 and Lipid Association of India (LAI) risk scores - in a community-based sample of T2DM patients in Gujarat, India.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study in 600 T2DM patients aged 25–84 years recruited from urban and rural areas of the Jamnagar district using stratified random sampling. Sociodemographic details, anthropometrics, and fasting blood tests were recorded. QRISK®3 and LAI risk scores were calculated for each participant. Risk categories were defined as moderate (10–19%), high (20–30%), and very high risk (> 30%) for both scoring systems. Agreement between scoring systems was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. Multiple linear regression was used to identify and compare predictors of both risk scores.ResultsThe mean age was 55 ± 8.5 years, and 60% were male. Using QRISK®3, 42% had moderate risk, 14.7% had high risk, and 43.3% had very high risk, while LAI classified 40%, 15%, and 45% in these categories, respectively. Agreement between scoring systems was substantial (weighted κ = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.79–0.87). The concordance improved with age, from moderate agreement in younger adults (κ = 0.75 for 25–44 years) to strong agreement in older adults (κ = 0.87 for ≥ 65 years). LAI identified more high-risk patients among those aged < 50 years compared to QRISK®3 (16.6% vs. 11.3%, p = 0.03). Linear regression revealed LAI placed greater emphasis on smoking (β = 6.85 vs. β = 5.63), family history (β = 5.28 vs. β = 3.12), and obesity (β = 3.27 vs. β = 2.43) compared to QRISK3.ConclusionBoth scoring systems showed substantial agreement in risk classification, with better concordance in older age groups. LAI risk score classified more younger patients as high-risk and weighted smoking and family history more heavily than QRISK®3. These findings suggest potential complementary roles for both scoring systems in comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment among Indian T2DM patients, particularly when evaluating younger individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24783-w.

Introduction

CVDs, once considered affluent diseases, are now the leading cause of mortality in India [1]. Although ischemic heart disease incidence rates remain lower than those in Western nations, age-adjusted CVD deaths have risen substantially over the past 30 years—likely driven by rapid urbanization, lifestyle changes, and an aging population [2]. By 2020, the age-standardized CVD death rate in India reached 272 per 100,000, compared to 317 per 100,000 in the United States [3].

Several CVD risk scores have been developed to predict the future risk of atherosclerotic events and guide primary prevention. The Framingham risk score pioneered the incorporation of traditional risk factors such as smoking, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia into 10-year risk estimates [4]. However, Framingham was derived from a predominantly white American cohort in the 1970 s and may not accurately reflect contemporary risk in other populations. The QRISK® score, which was introduced in the UK, provides better calibration for estimating CVD risk in Europeans [5]. The latest QRISK®3 iteration uses more advanced statistical methodology to account for demographic factors and complex risk factor interactions [6].

To date, only a few studies have evaluated QRISK scores in Asian Indians, a high-risk ethnic group with earlier-onset CVD than whites [7]. Moreover, there is limited data on how conventional risk factors are distributed across the QRISK categories in Indian patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.

The Lipid Association of India (LAI) risk score was developed specifically for Indian populations, incorporating regional cardiovascular risk factors and phenotypes. The LAI consensus statement provides risk stratification based on traditional risk factors while accounting for ethnic-specific characteristics [8].

So, the present study aimed to compare two cardiovascular risk assessment tools - QRISK®3 and Lipid Association of India (LAI) risk scores - in a community-based sample of T2DM patients in Gujarat, India, and to identify factors associated with higher QRISK3 scores in this population. We chose to compare QRISK3 with LAI as the latter was specifically developed and validated for Indian populations, potentially offering better risk discrimination in this demographic.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

Sample size

The Sample size was calculated based on the expected agreement (kappa) between QRISK3 and LAI scores. Assuming a null hypothesis kappa of 0.4 and alternative hypothesis kappa of 0.6, with 80% power and 5% alpha error, the required sample size was 600 participants.

Sampling technique

This study employed a stratified random sampling approach to ensure appropriate representation from Jamnagar district urban and rural areas. The sampling frame was stratified based on the urban-rural population distribution according to the 2011 Census data of Gujarat (43:57 ratio). Based on this distribution, we recruited 258 participants (43%) from urban areas and 342 (57%) from rural areas. Initially, a comprehensive list of all urban wards and rural villages in the district was obtained from local administrative records. A door-to-door survey was conducted to identify households with at least one eligible type 2 diabetes patient in both strata. These households were then assigned sequential numbers within their respective strata (urban and rural, separately). Using a computer-generated random number sequence, the required number of households was selected from each stratum. In cases where a selected household had multiple eligible diabetes patients, one participant was randomly chosen using the Kish grid method to avoid selection bias.

The final sample distribution closely mirrored the urban-rural population distribution of the Gujarat state (urban: 43% vs. Census 42.6%; rural: 57% vs. Census 57.4%), ensuring that our findings would be representative of the general population distribution. This stratified sampling approach helped minimize selection bias while ensuring adequate representation from both urban and rural areas, thereby enhancing the generalizability of our findings to the broader type 2 diabetes population in Gujarat.

Eligibility criteria

The study recruited participants meeting specific eligibility criteria. Eligible individuals were adults aged 25–84 years with type 2 diabetes mellitus (Type 2 diabetes was confirmed through either Documentation of previous diagnosis and current anti-diabetic medication use or Fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL) [9] who had resided in the community for at least one year.

Exclusion criteria included known cardiovascular diseases (including coronary artery disease, rheumatic heart disease, structural heart diseases, myocardial infarction, stroke, or heart failure), as well as pregnant or lactating women. These criteria were established to ensure the safety and relevance of the study outcomes within the specified population.

Tools used for data collection

Standardized questionnaires

Participants were interviewed by trained research staff using standardized questionnaires to collect data on socio-demographics, lifestyle factors, family history, medical conditions, and medication use. The questionnaires were developed through a systematic process that included literature review, expert consultation (including endocrinologists and cardiologists), and pilot testing on 30 participants not included in the final analysis. Questions were adapted from validated instruments, including the WHO STEPS instrument and INTERHEART risk score. Family history of cardiovascular disease was defined as heart attack, stroke, or sudden cardiac death in a first-degree relative, following established definitions from major cardiovascular risk assessment tools. This narrower definition was chosen to maximize specificity and reduce recall bias, as these events are more likely to be accurately reported compared to other cardiovascular conditions. (Questionnaires are attached as a supplementary file 1)

Anthropometric measurements and laboratory tests

Anthropometric measurements (height, weight, waist circumference) and blood pressure were recorded following standard protocols. Hypertension was defined as systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medications. Smoking was defined as current tobacco use (cigarettes/bidis) within the past year.

Fasting blood samples were collected and analyzed at an accredited laboratory for HbA1c, lipid profile (total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, triglycerides), fasting plasma glucose, and renal function parameters. Renal function was assessed using serum creatinine, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-EPI equation.

LDL cholesterol was directly measured from fasting blood samples using enzymatic methods (Roche Diagnostics). HDL cholesterol was measured using a direct homogeneous assay without precipitation. Laboratory analysis was conducted in a NABL-accredited facility that followed internal and external quality assurance procedures as per national standards.

Blood pressure was measured with the participants in a seated position after 5 min of rest using an automated digital blood pressure monitor. Two readings were obtained and averaged. Height, weight, and waist circumference were measured using standardized protocols. Smoking status and medication use data were obtained from a questionnaire designed for this study.

Risk calculators

LAI risk score

The Lipid Association of India (LAI) risk score was calculated for each participant based on age, waist circumference, family history of premature CVD, and metabolic factors [10, 11]. The LAI risk categories included low (< 10%), moderate (10–19%), and high (> 20%) 10-year risk. We primarily used the 2014 LAI consensus statement [10], as our study was designed and initiated before the 2023 updated consensus was published [11].

QRISK®3 Calculations

a) QRISK®3 Assessment: The QRISK®3-2021 risk calculator (University of Oxford) was used to predict 10-year CVD risk based on age, sex, ethnicity, smoking status, diabetes status, family history, BMI, systolic blood pressure, total and HDL cholesterol.

Additional medical conditions required for QRISK®3 were systematically assessed through structured interviews and medical record review, including atrial fibrillation, rheumatoid arthritis, chronic kidney disease, migraine, systemic corticosteroid use, systemic lupus erythematosus, atypical antipsychotic use, severe mental illness, erectile dysfunction, and blood pressure variability [12]. Participants were requested to provide their treatment booklets, prescriptions, discharge summaries, or investigation reports, which were reviewed with their consent. Where documentation was unavailable, information was obtained through structured interviews and cross-checked against ongoing medication use. Blood pressure variability was calculated using two to three clinic readings recorded during scheduled community health camps or verified from patient-provided clinic records.

This scale was also used in the Indian diabetic population in previous studies [13–15]. The QRISK® was the most accurate CVD risk calculator for several populations, whereas the World Health Organization/International Society of Hypertension (WHO/ISH) risk scores were the least accurate [7].

The QRISK®3 score was calculated using the online risk calculator based on the collected variables. The QRISK3-2021 calculator required an assessment of additional medical conditions and medications beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Each participant underwent systematic screening through structured interviews and medical record review for atrial fibrillation (electrocardiogram confirmation), rheumatoid arthritis (rheumatologist diagnosis), chronic kidney disease stages 3–5 (estimated glomerular filtration rate), migraine (neurologist diagnosis or documented history), systemic corticosteroid use (oral prednisolone ≥ 5 mg daily for > 1 month in the past year), systemic lupus erythematosus (rheumatologist diagnosis), atypical antipsychotic medications (current prescription), severe mental illness (psychiatrist diagnosis), and erectile dysfunction (in male participants). Blood pressure variability was calculated as the standard deviation of two to three clinic systolic blood pressure readings, as required by the QRISK3 calculator. While this method does not capture true continuous blood pressure variability, it fulfills the input requirements for the QRISK3 risk assessment tool. While individuals with type 2 diabetes can have QRISK3 scores < 10%, our study focused on moderate to very high-risk categories (QRISK3 ≥ 10%) to evaluate agreement between risk assessment tools for higher-risk patients requiring more intensive management. Of the initial 655 participants screened, 55 participants with QRISK3 scores < 10% were excluded from the final analysis of 600 participants.

Quality control

A systematic data verification process was implemented to ensure accuracy. We selected 10% of the records for comprehensive verification based on established epidemiological guidelines for quality assurance in field studies. This threshold provides a balance between feasibility and statistical confidence in data accuracy. Records were randomly selected using a computer-generated sequence, and the verification process involved comparing all entered data against original source documents. Discrepancies were resolved by reviewing original documentation and consulting with the responsible data collector when necessary. The error rate was calculated as the percentage of data points with discrepancies and remained below 1% throughout the study, indicating high data quality. Anthropometric instruments were calibrated regularly, and bloodwork was processed at an accredited lab with internal and external quality assurance programs [16].

Statistical analysis

Agreement between QRISK®3 and LAI risk categories was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, with weighted kappa to account for the ordinal nature of risk categories. The McNemar-Bowker test was used to compare the marginal distributions of risk classifications between scoring systems, as both contain three non-dichotomous categories (moderate, high, and very high risk). Differences in baseline characteristics were compared between males and females using chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables.

Multivariable linear regression models were developed to identify independent predictors of both QRISK®3 and LAI scores, allowing direct comparison of risk factor weightings between the two systems. Regression coefficients, standard errors, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values were calculated to quantify the strength of each association. Model diagnostics included assessment of linearity, multicollinearity (variance inflation factors), and residual plots for normality and homoscedasticity. Interactions were tested by including multiplicative interaction terms in regression models, and stratified analyses were conducted to explore subgroup variations.

A comprehensive sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of findings across different methodological approaches, including: (1) comparison of complete case analysis versus multiple imputation for handling missing data, (2) evaluation of alternative risk thresholds for categorization, (3) assessment of agreement across subgroups defined by diabetes duration and glycemic control, and (4) inclusion of previously excluded participants with QRISK3 scores < 10%.

A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [17].

A complete case analysis was conducted given the minimal missing data (3%, n = 18). Missing data were primarily in lipid parameters (n = 12) and family history (n = 6). Sensitivity analyses comparing results from complete cases and multiple imputations showed no significant differences, confirming the robustness of our findings.

Ethical considerations

Good clinical care guidelines were followed according to the Helsinki Declaration 2008. All participants received clear instructions about the study before enrollment, and written informed consent was obtained in their vernacular language. No identifying information or images were included in this manuscript. The entire methodology and protocol were approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee of Shri M P Shah Government Medical College, Jamnagar, Gujarat, India (ref No: 282/03/2023).

Results

While individuals with type 2 diabetes can have QRISK3 scores < 10%, our study focused on moderate to very high-risk categories (QRISK3 ≥ 10%) to evaluate agreement between risk assessment tools for higher-risk patients requiring more intensive management. This decision was based on clinical relevance, as patients with diabetes and QRISK3 scores ≥ 10% are those for whom therapeutic interventions would most likely be considered according to current guidelines. Of the initial 655 participants screened, 55 participants with QRISK3 scores < 10% were excluded from the final analysis of 600 participants. These low-risk participants were predominantly younger (mean age 42 ± 7 years) and female (65%), consistent with known risk factor patterns in diabetes populations.

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics and risk factors stratified by gender among 600 participants (360 males, 240 females). Significant gender differences were observed in several parameters. The mean age of participants was 55 years (SD 8.5), with males being significantly older than females (57 vs. 52 years, p < 0.001). Smoking was markedly more prevalent among males (44.4% vs. 12.5%, p < 0.001), while obesity (BMI ≥ 30) was more common in females (47.5% vs. 26.7%, p < 0.001). Hypertension affected half of the study population (50%), with no significant gender difference (p = 0.96). Other cardiovascular risk factors including high cholesterol (65%), low HDL (30%), high LDL (55%), and family history of CVD (22.3%) showed no significant gender differences. Additional medical conditions and medications required for QRISK3 calculation were systematically assessed in all participants. Through our screening process, we confirmed that none of the 600 participants had atrial fibrillation, rheumatoid arthritis, chronic kidney disease (stages 3–5), migraine, systemic lupus erythematosus, or severe mental illness. No participants reported current use of systemic corticosteroids or atypical antipsychotic medications. Among male participants, none reported erectile dysfunction. The multiple linear regression analysis provides several important insights into how risk factors contribute to QRISK3 and LAI scores: Both models show that age, male sex, smoking, and hypertension are the strongest contributors to cardiovascular risk scores. LAI appears to place greater emphasis on smoking (β = 6.85 vs. β = 5.63), family history of CVD (β = 5.28 vs. β = 3.12), and obesity (β = 3.27 vs. β = 2.43) compared to QRISK3. All predictors significant in the QRISK3 model were also significant in the LAI model, suggesting consistency in risk factor identification between scoring systems. The slightly higher R² value for LAI (0.65) compared to QRISK3 (0.62) suggests the LAI model explains marginally more of the variance in risk scores, potentially indicating better fit for the local population. Both HbA1c and diabetes duration significantly contributed to risk scores, with LAI showing slightly stronger associations with these diabetes-specific variables.

These findings suggest that while both scoring systems identify similar risk factors, LAI assigns relatively higher importance to family history and smoking, which may reflect population-specific cardiovascular risk patterns in Indian patients with type 2 diabetes.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses exploring how methodological choices affected our results.

First, we compared results between complete case analysis (n = 582) and multiple imputation for the handling of missing data. The agreement statistics between QRISK3 and LAI were nearly identical in both approaches (weighted kappa: 0.83 vs. 0.82), with risk classifications changing for only 4 participants (0.7%). This suggests minimal impact of our imputation strategy on the main study findings.

Second, we examined how varying risk categorization thresholds affected agreement between the scoring systems. When using alternative thresholds (5–15%, 15–25%, > 25%), the weighted kappa decreased slightly to 0.79 (95% CI: 0.75–0.83), indicating that agreement between QRISK3 and LAI is somewhat sensitive to threshold selection. When analyzing the scores as continuous variables, the Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.88 (95% CI: 0.86–0.90), confirming strong agreement independent of categorization approaches.

Third, we performed subgroup analyses based on diabetes-related characteristics. The agreement between scoring systems varied by diabetes duration, with stronger concordance in those with longer-standing disease (≥ 10 years: κ = 0.86; <5 years: κ = 0.77). Glycemic control also influenced agreement, with better concordance in those with poor control (HbA1c ≥ 8%: κ = 0.85) compared to those with good control (HbA1c < 7%: κ = 0.79). These differences likely reflect the stronger association between poor glycemic control and cardiovascular risk in both scoring systems.

Finally, we included the previously excluded 55 participants with QRISK3 scores < 10% in a comprehensive analysis of all 655 screened individuals. This reduced the overall agreement (weighted κ = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.73–0.81) but did not alter the main conclusion that agreement improves with age (25–44 years: κ = 0.68; ≥65 years: κ = 0.83). LAI classified more patients as moderate risk (10–19%) than QRISK3 in this expanded analysis (45.5% vs. 40.9%, p = 0.02), further supporting the finding that LAI assigns higher risk especially in younger patients.

These sensitivity analyses confirm that our primary findings of substantial agreement between scoring systems, with age-dependent patterns and slightly higher risk classification by LAI in younger patients, are robust across various analytical approaches. The observed variations in agreement across diabetes-related subgroups provide additional insights into the clinical contexts where discordance between scoring systems may be more likely.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of 600 type 2 diabetes patients from Gujarat, India, we found substantial agreement between QRISK3 and LAI risk assessment tools, with important variations across age groups and risk factors. Our findings highlight both the complementary nature of these scoring systems and their unique contributions to cardiovascular risk assessment in Indian diabetic populations.

The overall agreement between QRISK3 and LAI risk categories was substantial (weighted κ = 0.83), indicating that both scoring systems generally classify patients similarly. This level of concordance is higher than previously reported between other risk assessment tools in South Asian populations. For instance, Bansal et al. reported only moderate agreement (κ = 0.52) between Framingham Risk Score and SCORE risk models in North Indian subjects [18], while Garg et al. found fair agreement (κ = 0.39) between ACC/AHA and JBS3 risk calculators in South Asians [19]. The stronger agreement in our study may reflect the specific focus on a diabetes population, where cardiovascular risk is elevated across all assessment tools.

A key finding was the age-dependent pattern of agreement, with concordance improving from moderate (κ = 0.75) in younger adults (25–44 years) to strong (κ = 0.87) in older adults (≥ 65 years). This pattern suggests that risk classification in younger diabetic patients is more variable between scoring systems. LAI identified significantly more high-risk individuals among those aged < 50 years compared to QRISK3 (16.6% vs. 11.3%, p = 0.03). This finding is particularly relevant given that Indians develop cardiovascular disease approximately a decade earlier than Western populations [20]. Gupta et al. reported that over 50% of cardiovascular deaths in India occur before age 70, compared with 23% in Western countries [21]. The higher risk classification by LAI in younger adults may therefore better reflect the actual early-onset CVD risk pattern in Indian populations.

Our linear regression analyses revealed important differences in how the two scoring systems weight various risk factors. While both identified similar significant predictors, LAI placed greater emphasis on smoking (β = 6.85 vs. β = 5.63), family history of premature CVD (β = 5.28 vs. β = 3.12), and obesity (β = 3.27 vs. β = 2.43) compared to QRISK3. These differences likely reflect the LAI score’s development specifically for Indian populations, where these risk factors may have stronger associations with cardiovascular outcomes. Enas et al. reported that the risk associated with smoking is particularly high among South Asians, with a 3-fold increase in myocardial infarction risk compared to a 2-fold increase in Western populations [22]. Similarly, the increased risk weighting for family history aligns with findings by Prabhakaran et al. showing strong familial clustering of cardiovascular disease in Indians [23].

The additive rather than interactive effects of risk factors observed in our study contrasts with some earlier research. Yusuf et al. in the INTERHEART study found multiplicative effects between smoking and hypertension in South Asians [24], whereas our data showed predominantly additive effects for these risk factors in QRISK3 (observed risk 25% vs. expected additive risk 25%) and slightly more than additive effects in LAI (observed risk 28% vs. expected additive risk 26%). This difference may be related to the mathematical construction of the risk algorithms or to unique characteristics of our diabetic study population.

The moderate to substantial agreement between QRISK3 and LAI across different risk factor profiles supports the potential utility of both scoring systems in clinical practice. However, the enhanced risk classification by LAI among younger patients and its greater emphasis on risk factors with particular relevance to South Asian populations suggests it may offer distinct advantages for risk stratification in Indian patients. This aligns with recommendations by Puri et al. in the 2023 LAI consensus statement, which emphasized the need for population-specific risk assessment approaches [11].

The recent WHO-ISH cardiovascular risk prediction charts for India provide another country-specific alternative, but they lack diabetes-specific calibration [25]. A strength of our approach was the parallel evaluation of two complementary risk tools in a diabetes population, revealing their relative performance across different demographic and clinical subgroups. Rebbeck et al. have emphasized the importance of such comparative evaluations in diverse populations to improve risk prediction [26].

Gender-specific risk patterns were evident in both scoring systems, with males showing consistently higher risk scores than females across all risk categories. This gender difference was more pronounced in LAI compared to QRISK3, particularly for those with family history of CVD (LAI: males 26% vs. females 20%; QRISK3: males 23% vs. females 17%). Roy et al. previously reported similar gender disparities in cardiovascular risk scores among Indian diabetic patients, attributing this partly to higher smoking rates and different patterns of dyslipidemia in men [27]. Our findings of higher smoking prevalence in males (44.4% vs. 12.5%) and greater obesity in females (47.5% vs. 26.7%) mirror these known gender-specific risk patterns in Indian populations.

The clinical implications of our findings center on the potential complementary use of these scoring systems. For younger patients (25–44 years), where agreement was moderate (κ = 0.75), using both risk scores may provide more comprehensive risk assessment. The LAI score’s higher risk classification in younger individuals suggests it may be particularly valuable for identifying early cardiovascular risk in this demographic. Conversely, the strong agreement in older adults (κ = 0.87 for ≥ 65 years) suggests either tool alone may suffice for this age group. Mohan et al. similarly recommended age-specific risk assessment strategies for South Asian populations, suggesting more intensive screening for younger adults due to earlier disease onset [28].

The differential weighting of risk factors between scoring systems also has implications for preventive interventions. The stronger emphasis on smoking in LAI scoring aligns with the particularly detrimental effects of smoking in South Asian populations documented by Joshi et al. [29]. This suggests that smoking cessation programs may have even greater impact than predicted by conventional Western-derived risk models.

An additional consideration is the subgroup of early-onset type 2 diabetes mellitus (diagnosed before 40 years). This group is increasingly recognized as a distinct clinical entity due to its more aggressive course, earlier development of complications, and longer cumulative exposure to hyperglycemia. Although the proportion of such patients in our study was relatively small, the distinct risk profile warrants dedicated analysis in future studies. Stratification of patients into early-onset and usual-onset T2DM categories would enable age-tailored cardiovascular risk prediction and management strategies for the Indian population.

While these comparisons highlight important differences between the two risk scores, it is important to note that the primary aim of our study was to evaluate community-based cardiovascular risk using QRISK3 among patients with type 2 diabetes. The inclusion of LAI was intended to provide contextual insights relevant to the Indian population rather than to establish the superiority of one score over the other. Both QRISK3 and LAI can play complementary roles: QRISK3 offers international comparability and inclusion of a broader set of comorbid conditions, while LAI incorporates region-specific risk factors that may be more reflective of Indian populations.

These findings should be interpreted within the context of evolving cardiovascular risk prediction in South Asian populations. While traditional risk factors remain important, Jeemon et al. have advocated for incorporation of additional risk markers with particular relevance to South Asians, such as abdominal obesity and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein [30]. The LAI risk score’s stronger emphasis on waist circumference represents a step in this direction, but further refinement of population-specific risk prediction models is needed.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged in this study. First, the cross-sectional nature of the study prevents the establishment of causal relationships between risk factors and cardiovascular outcomes. Second, reliance on self-reported data for variables such as smoking and family history introduces potential recall bias, impacting the accuracy of risk score calculations. Third, while our study focused on type 2 diabetes patients, the findings may not be generalizable to non-diabetic populations or other geographic regions of India with different risk factor patterns. A limitation of our study was the exclusion of participants with QRISK3 scores < 10%, which may affect generalizability to lower-risk diabetic populations.

Additionally, while we collected data on potential confounders, including diet, physical activity, and socioeconomic status, the complexity of these behaviors and potential measurement error may still result in some degree of residual confounding. Furthermore, while both QRISK3 and LAI risk scores were applied in our study population, neither has been specifically validated for Indian type 2 diabetes patients, potentially affecting their predictive accuracy in this population.

Although waist-hip ratio (WHR) was measured in our study, it was not included in the primary analysis as it is not incorporated into the LAI risk score algorithm. However, given the high prevalence of central obesity in Indians and the well-established association of visceral adiposity with cardiometabolic risk, WHR is a critical marker for cardiovascular risk. Future studies should incorporate WHR explicitly in both analysis and model development to better capture the contribution of central obesity to cardiovascular risk in Indian populations.

Recommendations

Based on our comparative analysis of QRISK3 and LAI risk scores in type 2 diabetes patients, we propose several integrated recommendations for clinical practice, healthcare systems, research priorities, prevention strategies, and policy development.

In clinical practice, systematic cardiovascular risk screening programs should be implemented in primary care settings using both QRISK3 and LAI tools, particularly for younger patients, where we observed less concordance between scoring systems. Our findings support the development of standardized protocols for comprehensive risk assessment in type 2 diabetes patients, with age-specific approaches that acknowledge the different agreement patterns observed across age groups. For patients younger than 45 years, where agreement was moderate (κ = 0.75), both tools should be considered to ensure comprehensive risk evaluation, while in older adults (≥ 65 years) where agreement was stronger (κ = 0.87), either tool may provide reliable risk assessment. This age-stratified approach aligns with the epidemiological evidence of earlier cardiovascular disease onset in Indian populations.

Healthcare system adaptations are essential to support these clinical recommendations. Training programs for primary care physicians and frontline health workers should emphasize comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment techniques and interpretation of risk scores in the context of the Indian population. Clear referral pathways based on risk stratification thresholds should be established, with high-risk patients (> 20% by either score) receiving prompt specialist evaluation. The integration of these risk assessment tools into electronic health records would facilitate easier implementation, longitudinal tracking of risk profiles, and quality improvement initiatives. Such system-level changes would make comprehensive risk assessment a routine part of diabetes management in primary care settings throughout India.

Future research should address several critical gaps identified in our study. Longitudinal validation studies of both risk scores in Indian populations with type 2 diabetes are needed to examine how well predicted risk translates to actual cardiovascular events. Our cross-sectional design could not address this predictive validity. Cost-effectiveness analyses of implementing routine cardiovascular risk assessment in resource-limited settings would help policymakers allocate resources efficiently. The differential weighting of risk factors between scoring systems highlights the need for investigation into additional risk factors specific to Indian populations. Development and validation of India-specific risk prediction models incorporating these factors would further refine risk stratification. Particularly important is research on the appropriate risk thresholds for intervention in younger diabetic patients, given the earlier disease onset observed in South Asian populations.

Prevention strategies should be informed by our finding that LAI placed greater emphasis on smoking, family history, and obesity compared to QRISK3. Culturally appropriate lifestyle interventions targeting these modifiable risk factors would be particularly beneficial in the Indian context. Community-based prevention programs focusing on high-risk groups identified through screening should be implemented, with strategies for early identification and management of risk factors. Public health campaigns increasing awareness about cardiovascular risk in diabetes should emphasize the particularly detrimental effects of smoking in South Asian populations, as reflected in the higher weighting in LAI scores. Prevention efforts should also acknowledge the gender-specific risk patterns we observed, with targeted approaches for males (higher smoking rates) and females (higher obesity rates).

From a policy perspective, resources should be allocated for systematic cardiovascular risk screening in diabetes clinics, with established guidelines for risk-based treatment algorithms. Policies should consider the cost-effectiveness of widespread screening programs and support preventive interventions in high-risk populations, particularly focusing on smoking cessation, blood pressure control, and lipid management—the risk factors that showed the strongest associations with cardiovascular risk in our analysis. The development of national standards for cardiovascular risk assessment in diabetes care, incorporating both international (QRISK3) and India-specific (LAI) tools, would promote consistent high-quality care across diverse healthcare settings. Given the resource constraints in many parts of India, policies should also address equitable access to risk assessment and subsequent preventive interventions for both urban and rural populations.

Future research should prioritize the development of longitudinal patient registries and prospective follow-up at 5-year intervals. Such registries would allow validation of the predictive accuracy of QRISK3 and LAI scores over time and provide real-world evidence on cardiovascular outcomes among Indian patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. This approach would strengthen the long-term applicability of these risk prediction tools in clinical and community settings.

The implementation of these integrated recommendations has the potential to significantly improve cardiovascular risk assessment and management in type 2 diabetes patients in India, ultimately reducing the substantial burden of cardiovascular disease in this high-risk population.

Conclusion

This community-based cross-sectional study demonstrates substantial agreement between QRISK®3 and LAI risk scores in cardiovascular risk assessment among type 2 diabetes patients in Gujarat, India. A considerable proportion of participants were classified as very high risk (QRISK®3: 43.3%, LAI: 45%), with agreement improving with age (weighted κ = 0.83). LAI identified more high-risk individuals among younger participants, potentially reflecting the earlier onset of cardiovascular disease in the Indian population. Traditional risk factors showed significant gender variations, with males having higher smoking rates (44.4% vs. 12.5%) and females showing higher obesity rates (47.5% vs. 26.7%). These findings suggest that both scoring systems could be valuable complementary tools for cardiovascular risk assessment in Indian diabetic patients, particularly when evaluating younger individuals. Implementation of systematic risk assessment using these tools, combined with targeted interventions, could improve cardiovascular disease prevention in this high-risk population.
