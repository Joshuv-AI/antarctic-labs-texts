Community-based intervention to address antibiotic resistance: protocol for an embedded mixed-methods intervention trial
Authors: Sweety Suman Jha, Arista Lahiri, Madhumita Dobe, Budhaditya Mukherjee, Atul Raj, Amit Banik, Chandrashekhar Taklikar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAntibiotic resistance is a global threat that occurs naturally; however, the misuse and overuse of antibiotics in humans and animals are accelerating the process, necessitating ‘one health’ approach to mitigate the situation. Considering the ‘one health’ approach in the proposed study, it is the novel concept for implementation in the Indian context. The proposed study would generate evidence on the burden of antibiotic resistance, awareness and practices of proper antibiotic consumption among community members in West Bengal. The intervention package proposed in this study will be a novel intervention, which can be integrated with the existing healthcare delivery system as well.Methods and analysisThe proposed study will be an embedded mixed-methods interventional community-based study to be conducted in greater Kolkata region and West Medinipur district among adults (≥18 years) who will give consent for participation, and preferably residing in a particular area for more than 10 years. Intervention in the form of a behavioural intervention package, targeting awareness, perceived severity and perceived susceptibility regarding the issue of antibiotic resistance will be developed and implemented through randomisation of clusters. Data collection in the form of survey, observation, in-depth interview, collection of human, animal and environment samples will be carried out.Ethics and disseminationThe ethical clearance has been obtained from the Institutional ethics committee, Indian Institute of Technology, Kharagpur. The findings of this project will be communicated to the State Government Health Officials.Trial registration numberCTRI/2023/02/050054.Keywords: Communicable Disease Control, Community Health, Preventive Medicine

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global, multisectoral issue that requires coherent, comprehensive action in human, animal, plant and environmental health in the framework of a ‘one health approach’.1 2 AMR threatens the effective prevention and treatment of an ever-increasing range of infections, while the misuse and overuse of antimicrobials is accelerating the process of AMR.3 4 The cost of healthcare for patients with resistant infections is higher than care for patients with non-resistant infections due to longer duration of illness, additional tests and use of more expensive drugs.3 5 Even the mortality burden attributable to AMR infections is challenging as well. AMR alone is reported to kill more people than cancer and road traffic accidents combined together, with 10 million more people projected to die from it by 2050.6 Economic projections suggest that AMR would decrease gross domestic product by 2%–3.5% at the end of 2050.4 Though India has framed its National Action Plan for AMR, another major issue setting the stage back is the lack of sufficient research and paucity of data that not only hampers the estimation of exact rise and extent of AMR in India but also prevents a nationwide comparison.4

Prior research in the context

Antibiotic resistance is becoming a global threat and a growing number of infections like pneumonia, tuberculosis, gonorrhoea and salmonellosis are becoming difficult to treat as the antibiotics used to treat them become less effective.7 Laxminarayan and Chaudhury stated in their essay report that in 2010, India was the world’s largest consumer of antibiotics for human health.8 Growing antibiotic use in the animal sector is leading to greater selection of pathogens and is driven by increased demand for meat and poultry. The extreme growth in consumption of chickens is due to the expansion of this sector in India alone, where areas of high consumption (30 kg per km2) are expected to grow 312% by 2030.8 Waste water treatment plants handling antibiotic manufacturing facilities have been implicated in the transfer of resistance genes into human microbiota and create a serious threat to antibiotic effectiveness.8

In a review conducted by Kumar et al, it was reported that meta-analyses of the drug susceptibility results of various laboratories in India showed an increasing trend of development of resistance to commonly used antimicrobials in pathogens like Salmonella, Shigella, Vibrio cholerae, Staphylococcus aureus, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Neisseria meningitidis, Klebsiella, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, HIV, plasmodium and others.9 Multiresistant Enterobacteriaceae due to the production of extended spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL) have become very common in India. Various studies in South India reflected the drug resistance pattern like multidrug-resistant extended spectrum β-lactamase producing Klebsiella pneumoniae, ciprofloxacin-resistant Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi, emergence of vancomycin-intermediate staphylococci, fluoroquinolone resistance among Salmonella enterica serovar Paratyphi A, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii resistant to ceftazidime, cefepime and ciprofloxacin.9

The ‘one health’ concept expresses the importance of interdependence of human, animal and environmental parameters for the containment of AMR. In India, rates of AMR in all these three sectors have been rising disproportionately in the past decades.10 As per the scoping report on AMR in India (2017), it was reported that among the Gram-negative bacteria, more than 70% isolates of Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii and nearly half of all Pseudomonas aeruginosa were resistant to fluoroquinolones and third-generation cephalosporins.11 In the poultry industry of India, 1916×103 tonnes of broiler meat is produced each year with maximum production by Haryana (18.4%), followed by West Bengal (17.1%) and Uttar Pradesh (14.1%).4 As reported by Gandra et al,11 in poultry, ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae have documented the rate of ESBL producers to vary from 9.4% in Odisha to 33.5% in Madhya Pradesh to 87% in Punjab. Other four studies reported the presence of Salmonella species in broilers to vary from 3.3% in Uttar Pradesh to 23.7% in Bihar, with 100% isolates being resistant to ciprofloxacin, gentamicin and tetracycline in Bihar and West Bengal. The groundwater and surface water that are used for drinking and other purposes have been reported with 17% rate of Escherichia coli, resistant to third generation cephalosporin, in central India, 7% in north India (Kashmir), 50% in east India (Sikkim) and 100% in south India (Hyderabad).4

Barker et al12 in their qualitative study explored that antibiotics and the consequences of their misuse were poorly understood by study subjects. They were unable to define the term antibiotics properly. Participants with limited access to an allopathic doctor, either for logistic or economic reasons, were more likely to purchase medications directly from a pharmacy without a prescription. Participants with poor income were more likely to stop antibiotics after symptoms subsided. Participants, irrespective of income, were more likely to seek an allopathic doctor for their children than for themselves.

Saha et al13 evaluated susceptibility of antibiotics and resistance pattern of bacteria responsible for UTI in West Bengal for the period of 2008–2013. E. coli was the most abundant uropathogen with a prevalence rate of 67.1%, followed by Klebsiella spp (22%), and Pseudomonas spp (6%).

Nair et al14 in a qualitative study conducted in West Bengal among four groups of healthcare providers, that is, allopathic doctors, informal health providers, nurses and pharmacy shopkeepers, explored that patients demand antibiotics from healthcare providers and seek the fastest cure possible, which influences the prescription choices of healthcare providers, particularly informal health providers. Many allopathic doctors provide antibiotics without any clinical indication due to inconsistent follow-up, lack of testing facilities, risk of secondary infections and unhygienic living conditions. Pharmaceutical company representatives actively network with informal health providers and formal healthcare providers alike, and regularly visit providers even in remote areas to market newer antibiotics. Registered medical practitioners and informal healthcare providers frequently blame the other related people for being responsible for antibiotic resistance, and yet both are interdependent in referring patients to one another.

Comprehensive intervention to tackle AMR at the community level

The problem of AMR is a significant example of the importance of ‘one health’ as antibiotic use in any one of them can potentially affect the health of the others. Antibiotic resistance occurs naturally, but misuse and overuse of antibiotics in humans and animals is accelerating the process.7 To mitigate the problem of AMR, various departments related to human health, animal health and environmental health should collaborate so that antimicrobials are used judiciously. Addressing the rising threat of AMR requires a holistic and multisectoral, that is, ‘one health’ approach as antimicrobials which are used to treat various infections in animals may be the same or be like those used in humans. Resistant bacteria arising either in humans, animals or the environment may spread from one to the other, and from one country to another. AMR does not recognise geographic or human/animal borders. With increasing resistance and downfall of the supply curve for new drug development, there is a growing need for strategies to enhance rational use of antibiotics. At the community level, irrational antibiotic usage including improper information on antibiotics, incomplete courses of medicine and self-medication are major factors contributing to AMR.15 Community sensitisation in the form of awareness and education may contribute towards controlling the rise of AMR.

Rationale of the research

Community sensitisation and behaviour change intervention through mass campaign, individual level health education and IEC materials targeting the knowledge and perception of antibiotic resistance is the need of the hour, since its prevention can be achieved only through awareness, willingness and determination. Embedded mixed-method design makes the research methodologically more robust. Combining both quantitative and qualitative findings to understand the current situation of antibiotic resistance and the related factors would also help in developing the intervention material more vigorously. There are very few works done on antibiotic resistance at community level in India. Considering the ‘one health’ approach in the study is the novel one in the Indian context. Data on the burden of antibiotic resistance, awareness level and practices of proper antibiotic consumption is lacking in West Bengal. Intervention developed for the study targeting the awareness of community members on proper antibiotic consumption and harmful effects of antibiotic resistance would not only benefit the people of West Bengal but also it can be replicated in other states of the country for the benefit of the entire nation. The intervention package proposed in this study will be a novel intervention, which can be integrated with the existing healthcare delivery system as well.

The proposed study aims to evaluate the changes in awareness and practices related to antibiotic consumption along with their predictors, due to the intervention. Further, in a subsample, the effect of intervention in terms of the change in the proportion of antibiotic resistance will be evaluated. The study will generate evidence on awareness and practices of proper antibiotic consumption along with relevant behavioural predictors among community members in West Bengal and the burden of antibiotic resistance in selected communities.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

The proposed study will be an embedded mixed-methods interventional community-based study. The quantitative component of the study will involve cluster randomised intervention design with 1:1 allocation ratio. The qualitative component will be exploratory in design, embedded before the intervention phase, and to enrich the intervention package to be implemented. The study sites will be a densely populated urban Municipal Corporation area (ie, Kolkata Municipal Corporation), and another rural and tribal-dominated district (ie, West Medinipur district) in West Bengal, India.

Based on the population density and geographical spread of the urban areas, clusters will be defined in terms of settlements of 500–1000 households (up to maximum population of 3000) around a major road/lane with a nearby major marketplace (ie, where mostly the community members from selected clusters come for procurement of groceries, foods and household items) within the bounds of a municipal ward. Therefore, at first, urban wards (ie, municipal units) will be selected randomly and in the selected wards, a maximum of four clusters based on the above criteria will be selected, which will be geographically distant and non-overlapping. Selecting multiple clusters from one municipal ward will help in adjusting for the local factors that may influence the outcome. In the rural areas, the Health and Wellness Centres (HWCs) are the grassroots level unit of primary healthcare delivery. Therefore, the villages served by an HWC in the selected district will be considered a cluster for the rural part of the study.

Study participants

Adults (≥18 years) who will give consent for participation and individuals suffering from any symptoms of systemic infection (eg, fever, pain in the abdomen, diarrhoea, urinary problems, cough and cold, etc) within the last 3 months of the field survey will be the respondents. Subsequently, critically ill, cancer patients or bed-ridden patients, individuals diagnosed to be immunocompromised, individuals on immunosuppressive therapy, respondents diagnosed with any psychiatric illness will be excluded. In this context, clinical history by the patient or patient’s caregiver and/or available prescription will be considered. Based on the recall pattern and availability of prescriptions, medicine strips, etc as observed in a prior pilot (refer to table 1), and as per the suggestion of the domain experts, the aforementioned limiting duration of 3 months was fixed for inclusion. Now, apart from the mentioned inclusion criteria, participants will be given preference based on residence in a particular area for more than 10 years, awareness about the term ‘antibiotic’ and individuals having a history of consumption of any antibiotic. Similarly, healthcare workers will preferably be excluded from the quantitative part of the study.

Sampling and participant allocation

For the quantitative part of the study, allocation ratio of intervention and control group will be 1:1. The following assumptions are taken into consideration to calculate the sample size: Power of 90%, and confidence level of 95%. Based on the pilot study by the researchers in Kolkata (refer to table 1), the proportion of appropriate practice of antibiotic use ≈20%, which is less than the observed resistance proportion (58%). A minimum detectable OR of 2 will be taken. Therefore, using the Fleiss formula for difference in proportions, the minimum effective sample size for each group is 121. Now, considering the average number of participants per cluster to be 50, and assuming an intraclass correlation of 0.3, the sample size in each arm of the cluster randomised trial becomes: 121×[1+49×0.3]=1900. The number of clusters required in each arm of the study will be 1900/50=38. The clusters will be selected from the study areas based on probability proportion to size technique. In each cluster, 50 individuals will be randomly selected depending on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Given the density and a lack of appropriate line listing (typically in the urban clusters), this recruitment of participants will be based on the divisions created by lanes in the area, and at the subcluster level, households will be approached randomly to meet the required number. In this approach, the target will be to reach the cluster level sample size ensuring representation from throughout the cluster. From each selected household, one participant will be selected. Sample size calculation is based on the antibiotic practices, not on antibiotic resistance proportion (which is a secondary objective in this case). Testing of antibiotic resistance (in appropriate sample) will be conducted in randomly selected 10% clusters in each arm. For the qualitative part, purposive sampling will be done and saturation of themes will be considered for reaching the required sample size.

Randomisation and blinding

Since a total of 76 clusters will be selected for this study (38 in intervention arm and 38 in control arm), based on population and administrative regions, 32 clusters will be selected from Kolkata area and the rest (ie, 44) from West Medinipur district. Again, based on population and number of administrative areas, out of the 44 clusters to be selected from West Medinipur district, 6 will be urban municipal clusters, and the other 38 will be rural clusters catered by a HWC. Randomisation will be done in blocks (with block size of 4) with random number generation software. The intervention will be an individual and group-targeted behavioural (health promotion) intervention; blinding will not be implemented.

Intervention

Intervention will be a behavioural intervention package, targeting awareness, perceived severity and perceived susceptibility regarding the issue of antibiotic resistance. Awareness generation regarding antibiotic resistance and appropriate practices for antibiotic use, through community group meetings and discussion sessions in the intervention clusters. Posters and IEC materials to be displayed in the local dispensaries and health centres, highlighting the perils of antibiotic resistance and promoting proper antibiotic practices among the beneficiaries. House-to-house awareness campaign with leaflets will be conducted by field staff recruited under this project. A schematic for the intervention package is provided in the online supplemental figure 1.

The intervention package will be further modified content-wise with the findings from the qualitative part of the study.

Data collection techniques

For collection of relevant information to ascertain the effectiveness of the intervention package, the following standard methodology will be employed during preintervention and postintervention phases.

Quantitative survey: Survey for assessment of awareness on antibiotic resistance and appropriate antibiotic practices will be conducted with the help of a predesigned pretested questionnaire. This questionnaire has already been piloted in an urban field in Kolkata by the researchers. This trilingual questionnaire (ie, in English, Bengali and Hindi) will be used during the baseline assessment and also in the postintervention survey. The field survey staff will be provided with a predesigned checklist for relevant observations at the time of house visits during the survey. The same checklist will be used during preintervention and postintervention phases.

Culture and sensitivity analysis of human samples: Based on the existing symptoms reported by the participants, one or more of the following samples will be collected: urine, stool, sputum and throat swab. All the samples will be collected by using the universal aseptic precautions and sterile technique. The participants will be mobilised to their nearest health centre for sample collection purposes. For urine culture and sensitivity, mid-stream urine will be collected by the participant in a sterile container. Sputum collection will be done by first rinsing the oral cavity; thereafter, sputum will be advised to be collected in the sterile container provided to the person, with a deep cough. For throat swab, trained personnel (laboratory technician) equipped with sterile gloves, face mask and face shield will collect samples after pressing the tongue down with a tongue depressor and then gently rubbing the posterior pharyngeal wall and the tonsils with a long neck sterile swab stick. A stool sample will be self-collected by the participant with the help of a disposable spatula supplied along with the stool container. The samples collected will be transported to the designated laboratory (ie, the Microbiology laboratory, All India Institute of Hygiene and Public Health, Kolkata) in a coldbox with temperature control at 4°C–8°C. Once the sample is received in the laboratory, the samples will be inoculated on the various culture media for overnight incubation at 37°C, depending on the type of the sample. The CLSI guidelines will be followed for culture and sensitivity testing on the samples received. For enumerating the sensitivity profile of the individual bacterial strains and detecting the AMR profile of each strain, antibiotic sensitivity testing will be performed using Mueller Hinton agar plates following Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method. For drug sensitivity testing, fluoroquinolones and quinolones, azithromycin, tetracyclines and cephalosporins will be focused on, according to the organisms identified. Since the objective of bacterial culture in this case will be to identify pathogenic organisms from the samples, and then test for drug sensitivity, no specific organism will be targeted beforehand. In urban areas, the main sewage channel is accessible through designated spots from the roads. Hence, in rural areas, sewage samples will be collected from multiple upstream and downstream spots from the main sewage channel as identified by municipality personnel. In rural areas, sewage samples will be collected from sewage disposal sites near the households. Trained personnel will collect the sewage samples maintaining appropriate protection (PPE and long handle mug will be used). Water samples from different water sources present in a community will be collected. In urban clusters with piped water supply, water samples from multiple points will be collected. In rural areas where piped water supply is not present, water samples from the different corners of a pond or similar waterbody (from where people collect water for use) will be collected. All the samples will be collected in sterile containers. Animal samples, eg, poultry, meat, fish and egg will be collected from the major shops (identified through qualitative exploration) in a community in separate containers. All the samples will be transported in 4°C–8°C temperature to the designated facility. For each of these different types of samples, cluster-wise pooling will be done and these samples will be subjected to testing for antibiotic residues (for antibiotics, eg, quinolones and fluoroquinolones, azithromycin and tetracyclines). All the samples will be prepared as a liquid-base sample for testing of antibiotic residues through ELISA. The preparation of liquid-base samples for ELISA testing will be done as per the standard guideline mentioned in the respective kits. Plan for data analysis

The data collected will be entered in a spreadsheet software and cleaned before statistical analysis. The major variables that will be considered for analyses are sociodemographic variables, eg, age, gender, type of family, marital status, education, occupation, residence; and clinical variables, like symptoms during last 3 months and/or current symptoms, comorbidities. As indicators of care-seeking behaviour, visits to health centre or private physicians or any other person for ailment, whether taken/taking antibiotics with names of the antibiotics will be noted. Certain behavioural constructs, for example, awareness and opinions regarding antibiotic use, normative influencers for antibiotic use and self-efficacy beliefs regarding appropriate antibiotic consumption practices will be determined. Antibiotic residue concentration (in ng/mL) in animal food samples, and environmental samples (eg, household waste water, sewage, local reservoir water) will be noted in terms of concentrations. Data collected from the surveys will be anonymised before further processing. Individual awareness scores measured through the surveys will be used in the calculation of cluster-wise mean awareness scores in preintervention and postintervention phases. The awareness score will be obtained by combining the scores for each awareness question in the questionnaire. Appropriate practice for antibiotic use will be measured through the variables stated in the above table. For each cluster, the proportion of each practice variable will be measured, and finally, these proportions will be combined, adjusting for cluster weights to get a pooled proportion. The change in proportion following intervention will be modelled through multivariate analysis. At first, comparative techniques will be used to understand intervention effect, and at a later stage, when considering all the behavioural constructs, a generalised structural equations model will be attempted. For the subgroup of clusters where antibiogram and antibiotic residue analysis will be done, the change in pattern of antibiotic resistance will be analysed with the help of non-parametric methods.
