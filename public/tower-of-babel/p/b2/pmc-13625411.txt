Lactobacillus acidophilus abolishes oxalate-mediated renal epithelial barrier disruption and calcium oxalate monohydrate crystal adhesion to renal epithelial cells
Authors: Anantaya Putpim, Chadanat Noonin, Paranyu Chawanpaiboon, Onnipa Pawano, Somsakul Phuangkham, Paleerath Peerapen, Visith Thongboonkerd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIt is generally known that kidney stone disease (KSD) is associated with alterations in urinary microbiome, but the roles of the urinary microbiome in KSD pathogenesis remain unclear.MethodsThis study addressed the impact of Lactobacillus acidophilus (a commensal bacterium found in normal urine) on renal epithelial integrity, calcium oxalate monohydrate (COM) crystal-cell adhesion, expression of membrane receptors of COM crystals, and oxalate degradation under oxalate-induced stress (a known inducer of KSD). Inner medullary collecting duct cells (mIMCD-3) were cultured for 24 h under control or oxalate-induced (by 0.6 mM sodium oxalate; NaOx) conditions without or with L. acidophilus (at 1 × 103 colony-forming unit (CFU)/ml) co-incubation.ResultsNaOx reduced transepithelial resistance (TER) of the mIMCD-3 monolayer and downregulated ZO-1, a tight junction (TJ) protein. Additionally, NaOx enhanced the COM crystal-binding capability of mIMCD-3 cells by upregulating a COM crystal receptor, annexin A2, on cell membranes. Such harmful effects of NaOx were abolished when mIMCD-3 cells were co-cultured with L. acidophilus. Moreover, culturing L. acidophilus in artificial urine (AU) supplemented with NaOx for 24 h revealed that the oxalate level in AU decreased, suggesting the oxalate-degrading activity of the bacterium in an AU environment.ConclusionsL. acidophilus prevented oxalate-mediated renal epithelial barrier disruption and COM crystal adhesion to renal epithelial cells by preserving ZO-1 and annexin A2 expression at their basal levels, at least in part, via its oxalate-degrading property.Clinical trial numberNot applicable (This is not a clinical trial).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11658-026-00985-x.Keywords: Annexin A2, COM crystal, Crystal receptor, Microbiome, Oxalate degradation, Tight junction, Transepithelial resistance

Introduction

Kidney stone disease (KSD) is a highly prevalent disease impacting approximately 10% of people worldwide [1]. The stones are an outcome of crystal aggregation and retention in the renal system, and various crystalline components give rise to different stone types [1, 2]. Most kidney stones have calcium oxalate, particularly in its monohydrate form (COM), as their major crystalline component [3–6]. Oxalate and calcium supersaturation in urine is an initial step of crystal formation. In the saturated urine, oxalate tends to combine with calcium, leading to COM crystal formation. Later, these crystals become larger, aggregate, and adhere to renal tubular epithelial cells (TECs), leading to intratubular crystal retention that helps develop kidney stones [1, 7]. Such adhesion of COM crystals to TECs has been well documented to be mediated by various COM crystal receptors on the cell surfaces, such as annexin A2 [8, 9], ezrin [8, 10], heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) [8, 11], heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) [8, 12, 13], etc. Consequently, the intrarenal crystal retention triggers oxidative insult, inflammatory cascade, and aberrant protein expression in TECs and renal tissues, all of which further aggravate KSD development [14, 15].

Both oxalate and COM crystals are harmful to TECs, resulting in disruption of tight junction (TJ) and other epithelial barriers [16–22]. TJ is important for maintaining tissue homeostasis by regulating charge- and size-selective paracellular transports [23]. It is an apical membrane complex composed of many proteins, i.e., claudins, occludin, and ZO-1 [16, 23]. Previous evidence has illustrated that COM crystals can disrupt TJ by reducing occludin and ZO-1 expression, causing an increase in the permeability of the TECs monolayer as indicated by lower transepithelial resistance (TER) [18–22]. In addition, oxalate and COM crystals can upregulate COM receptors on TECs, leading to an increase in crystal adhesion that promotes cell injury [10, 24, 25], which in turn triggers crystal adhesion [26–28]. This vicious cycle generates detrimental conditions that favor stone development.

Recently, the beneficial roles of the microbiome in human health have been widely recognized. Many studies have investigated the impact of the microbiome on homeostasis, and distortions of microbial balance have been observed in various human diseases [29, 30]. Compared with healthy individuals, subjects with KSD show significant differences in their urinary and intestinal microbiomes, with a greater difference revealed in the urinary microbiome [31–35]. Moreover, Lactobacillus spp. are predominant in the urine of healthy individuals, suggesting their protective roles against KSD [31, 34, 36]. In murine KSD models, distortions of the intestinal microbial community are suggested to have an impact on the expression of TJ proteins and intestinal barrier functions [37, 38]. Our recent study on L. acidophilus, a bacterial species identified in healthy urine [39, 40], has demonstrated direct inhibitory effects of the bacterium on COM crystals [41]. These studies have underscored the beneficial roles of the microbiome in preventing KSD.

While there are many studies of the effects of the gastrointestinal microbiome to prevent KSD, including L. acidophilus [42], there is a lack of experimental evidence showing the protective roles of the urinary microbiome against KSD via its association with renal epithelial barrier function in the kidney. To gain more knowledge about how the urinary L. acidophilus helps prevent KSD, this study aimed to investigate its effects on renal epithelial barrier integrity and COM-cell adhesion in medullary collecting duct cells under oxalate-induced stress (a known inducer of KSD). Moreover, potential mechanisms of its protective roles were examined (i.e., its effects on COM crystal receptor expression on renal TEC surfaces and oxalate degradation).

Materials and methods

L. acidophilus culture

L. acidophilus (ATCC 314™) (ATCC) (bacterial species validated by genome sequencing) was cultured in MRS broth (Himedia Laboratories) in a 37 °C/5% CO2 incubator. After its mid-log phase was reached, bacterial cells were harvested by 10-min centrifugation at 6000 × g for use in subsequent experiments.

mIMCD-3 cell culture

Medullary collecting duct cells were chosen for the study because their origin is close to the renal pelvis, where the presence of urinary microbiome and occurrence of kidney stones have been reported [43–46]. According to “Cellosaurus,” a knowledge resource on cell lines (https://www.cellosaurus.org/index.html), it was unfortunate that a human medullary collecting duct cell line was not available. Therefore, a mouse inner medullary collecting duct cell line (mIMCD-3) (ATCC), which has previously been used as an in vitro model to study KSD [47, 48], was employed in the present study. The cells were cultured in a growth medium comprising DMEM/F12 (Gibco), 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 60 µg/ml streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich), and 60 U/ml Penicillin G (Sigma-Aldrich) in a 37 °C/5% CO2 incubator.

Cell death assay

mIMCD-3 cells suspended in the growth medium were seeded in six-well plates (4 × 105 cells/well). After 24-h culture, L. acidophilus at a final density of 1 × 103 CFU/ml (a urinary bacterial concentration under the normal physiologic condition [49]) or NaOx at a final concentration of 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 or 1.0 mM was added and further incubated with cells for another 24 h. Both floating dead cells (if any) and attached live cells were collected and incubated for 15 min in annexin V-binding buffer containing annexin V-FITC (BD Biosciences), followed by a 2-min incubation with propidium iodide (BD Biosciences). The stained cells were analyzed by using a BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Four different groups of treatments

For all subsequent assays, the cells were cultured in 6-well, 24-well, or 12-Transwell plates with 0.4-µm-pore polyester membrane inserts (Corning). Thereafter, the cultured medium was replaced with fresh growth medium without other supplements (control group), growth medium containing 0.6 mM NaOx (NaOx group), growth medium containing 0.6 mM NaOx + L. acidophilus at a final density of 1 × 103 CFU/ml (NaOx + L. acidophilus group), or growth medium containing L. acidophilus at a final density of 1 × 103 CFU/ml (L. acidophilus group). After another 24-h incubation, the cells were subjected to experiments/measurements as follows.

Transepithelial resistance (TER) measurement

For TER measurement, 12-Transwell plates with 0.4-µm-pore polyester membrane inserts were used. Collagen IV (Sigma-Aldrich) was used to coat the membrane inserts before mIMCD-3 cells were seeded in the Transwell (7 × 104 cells/well). The cells were allowed to grow and polarize for 24 h. Thereafter, the cells were divided into four groups as described above. After 24-h incubation under different conditions, TER was measured using the Millicell-ERS Volt-Ohm meter (Millipore) from three different areas per well for each biological sample.

Immunofluorescence staining

The mIMCD-3 cells seeded and cultured for 24 h on coverslips placed in 24-well plates (7 × 104 cells/well) were divided into four groups as described above. After 24-h incubation under different conditions, the cultured medium was replaced with 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde/phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by a 15-min incubation at 25 °C to fix the cells. For staining of ZO-1 (a TJ-associated protein), cell permeabilization was achieved by a 10-min incubation in 0.1% Triton X-100/PBS, followed by a 30-min blocking in 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA)/PBS at 25 °C. For staining of annexin A2, ezrin, HSP70, and HSP90 (COM crystal receptors), the fixed cells were proceeded to 1% BSA/PBS blocking without permeabilization. The cells were then incubated at 4 °C overnight with mouse monoclonal anti-ZO-1 (Invitrogen), anti-annexin A2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-ezrin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-HSP70 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), or anti-HSP90 antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), followed by an incubation with Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (Invitrogen) together with Hoechst nuclear stain (Invitrogen) at 37 °C for 1 h. Cell images were captured under the Eclipse 80i microscope connected with DS-Ri2 digital camera (Nikon). NIS-Elements BR (Version 5.30.03) software (Nikon) was used to measure fluorescence intensity from ≥ 100 cells in ≥ 10 random fields per biological sample.

Crystal-cell adhesion assay

Firstly, COM crystals were prepared using the established standard protocol [50, 51]. The mIMCD-3 cells seeded and cultured for 24 h in six-well plates (4 × 105 cells/well) were divided into four groups as described above. After 24-h incubation under different conditions, the cells were washed with DMEM/F12 and then incubated for 1 h with 0.1 mg/ml COM crystals in the growth medium at 37 °C. Thereafter, non-adhered crystals were washed out using PBS, whereas the remaining adhered crystals were imaged under the Eclipse Ts2 microscope connected with DS-Fi3 digital camera (Nikon). The adhered crystals in each biological sample were counted from 15 random fields per well.

Western blotting

The cells seeded and cultured in six-well plates were divided into four groups as described above. After 24-h incubation under different conditions, cell lysis was done with Laemmli’s buffer, and protein concentrations were measured with the Bio-Rad Protein assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Subsequently, 30-µg proteins were resolved by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and transferred from the gel to a nitrocellulose membrane. Membrane blocking was performed by incubating with 5% skim-milk/PBS for 1 h at 25 °C. Thereafter, the membrane was incubated at 4 °C overnight with mouse monoclonal antibody against annexin A2, HSP70, HSP90, ezrin, or β-actin (all from Santa Cruz Biotechnology) in 1% skim-milk/PBS, followed by an incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated rabbit anti-mouse IgG (Sigma-Aldrich) in 1% skim-milk/PBS at 25 °C for 1 h. SuperSignal West Pico chemiluminescence substrate (Pierce Biotechnology), together with autoradiography, was used to visualize immunoreactive protein bands. ImageQuant TL software (GE Healthcare) was applied to quantify band intensity.

Oxalate-consumption assay and measurement of oxalate levels in artificial urine (AU) by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

To mimic an in vivo environment of the renal system, protein-free AU (AU Siriraj formula) was used in this experiment to examine the oxalate-degrading ability of L. acidophilus in the urine environment. The protein-free AU Siriraj formula was prepared according to a previously reported protocol [52]. The final components of the protein-free AU Siriraj formula included 3 mM CaCl2·2H2O, 4 mM creatinine, 30 mM KCl, 2 mM MgSO4·7H2O, 0.4 mM Na2HPO4, 9 mM Na2SO4, 5 mM Na3C6H5O7·2H2O, 0.1 mM NaC2O4, 54 mM NaCl, 3.6 mM NaH2PO4·H2O, 2 mM NaHCO3, 15 mM NH4Cl, 200 mM urea, and 1 mM uric acid (pH 6.2). Then, 4 mM NaOx was added to the AU and incubated in the 37 °C/5% CO2 incubator without (control) or with L. acidophilus at a final density of 1 × 103 CFU/ml for 24 h. Thereafter, the mixtures were centrifuged at 6000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatants were harvested, passed through 0.2-µm filter, and subjected to HPLC (Perkin Elmer) to measure the remaining oxalate level using ACE EXCEL 5 C18–Amide column (4.6 × 250 mm) (VWR). Methanol and 40 mM NH4H2PO4 (adjusted pH to 2.0 using orthophosphoric acid) were mixed together at a ratio of 15:85 and used as an isocratic mobile phase with a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min and a sample injection volume of 10 µl. Detection was conducted with a UV detector at λ214 nm. Chromera software (Perkin Elmer) was used for data acquisition and processing. NaOx standard solutions at 1, 2, 4, and 8 mM dissolved in AU were prepared and injected into HPLC to generate a calibration curve of area under the curve (AUC) against standard oxalate concentration. Finally, oxalate levels were calculated and presented as relative abundance to the control condition.

Statistical analysis

To obtain quantitative data, three biological replicates were used in each assay. Comparative analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons (for normally distributed data) or Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons (for data without normal distribution). Differences were considered significant when P values were < 0.05.

Results

Co-culture of mIMCD-3 cells with L. acidophilus and the optimal concentration of NaOx

This study addressed the effects of L. acidophilus on TJ integrity and alteration in COM crystal receptors on mIMCD-3 cells that were maintained under an oxalate-induced stress. Therefore, it is critical to clarify that both L. acidophilus and NaOx were not too toxic to the renal cells to prevent unwanted confounding factors that might occur owing to cell death, rather than the inducing effects of both L. acidophilus and NaOx. We first clarified that L. acidophilus at its physiologic urinary concentration of 1 × 103 CFU/ml did not affect mIMCD-3 cell death after 24-h co-incubation (the duration used for all other experiments to test the effects of L. acidophilus and NaOx). Flow cytometric analysis with annexin V and propidium iodide showed comparable total cell death (including early apoptosis, late apoptosis, and necrosis) between control and L. acidophilus-treated cells (Fig. 1A, B).

A Scatter plots of annexin V and propidium iodide signal intensities of control and L. acidophilus-co-cultured conditions. B Quantitative data of total cell death (percentage of cells stained with annexin V and/or propidium iodide). Individual data points represent all measurements obtained from three biological samples (each with three technical replicates from independent experiments) and are plotted with mean ± SEM. No statistically significant difference was found

To choose the most appropriate concentration of NaOx for mIMCD-3 induction, we also quantified cell death after exposure to NaOx at 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, or 1.0 mM. It should be noted that 0.2 mM is generally considered a normal oxalate concentration in the human urine, whereas other concentrations (0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mM, which are > 0.25 mM) are hyperoxaluric [53]. Flow cytometric data showed that NaOx at 0.2–0.6 mM did not increase total cell death, whereas the higher concentrations (0.8 and 1.0 mM) significantly increased total cell death (Fig. 2A, B). We therefore selected the highest concentration that did not induce cell death (0.6 mM) as the optimal concentration of NaOx for all experiments in the present study.

Effect of L. acidophilus on NaOx-mediated decrease in TER

To examine the effects of NaOx and L. acidophilus on epithelial barrier function, TER was measured 24 h after the polarized mIMCD-3 cells were exposed to 0.6 mM NaOx in the absence or presence of L. acidophilus at a final density of 1 × 103 CFU/ml. NaOx clearly reduced TER, whereas L. acidophilus dramatically increased TER compared with the control cells (Fig. 3). When NaOx induction was conducted in the presence of L. acidophilus (NaOx + L. acidophilus group), a significant increase of TER was observed compared with both NaOx-treated and control cells (Fig. 3).

Effect of L. acidophilus on NaOx-mediated downregulation of a TJ-associated protein

To examine whether the effects of NaOx and L. acidophilus on TER observed were due to alterations in TJ-associated protein expression, immunostaining of ZO-1 was performed. In agreement with the TER data, NaOx had a profound effect on ZO-1 expression, which was downregulated compared with control mIMCD-3 cells (Fig. 4A, B). L. acidophilus alone did not affect ZO-1 level as compared with the control, but was able to abolish the downregulated effect of NaOx on ZO-1 expression (Fig. 4A, B). The results indicated that L. acidophilus prevented epithelial barrier disruption in mIMCD-3 cells caused by NaOx via preserving TJ-associated protein expression.

Effect of L. acidophilus on NaOx-enhanced adhesion of COM crystals to mIMCD-3 cells

Exposure to oxalate has been reported to cause renal MDCK cell surfaces to be prone to COM crystal adhesion [54]. Therefore, an experiment to address the effect of L. acidophilus on NaOx-mediated COM adhesion to mIMCD-3 cell surfaces was conducted. As expected, when COM crystals were added to mIMCD-3 cells exposed to NaOx for 24 h, a greater number of crystals was found to adhere to the cell surface compared with the control cells (Fig. 5A, B). However, L. acidophilus significantly declined the number of adhered crystals as compared with the control cells. Additionally, L. acidophilus abolished the enhancing effect of NaOx on crystal adhesion to mIMCD-3 cells (Fig. 5A, B).

Effect of L. acidophilus on NaOx-mediated upregulation of a COM crystal receptor on mIMCD-3 cells

To address potential mechanisms of the effects of NaOx and L. acidophilus on COM-mIMCD-3 adhesion, we performed Western blotting to examine expression levels of COM receptors on mIMCD-3 cells. The findings demonstrated that only annexin A2 (Fig. 6A, B; Supplementary Fig. S1) was upregulated by NaOx, whereas ezrin (Fig. 6C, D; Supplementary Fig. S1), HSP70 (Fig. 6E, F; Supplementary Fig. S1), and HSP90 (Fig. 6G, H; Supplementary Fig. S1) levels were not affected by NaOx. Co-culture of mIMCD-3 with L. acidophilus effectively prevented the enhancing effect of NaOx on annexin A2 expression (Fig. 6A, B) and did not affect expression levels of other COM receptors (Fig. 6C–H). Next, the membrane localization of annexin A2 (Fig. 7A), ezrin (Supplementary Fig. S2A), HSP70 (Supplementary Fig. S3A), and HSP90 (Supplementary Fig. S4A) was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining without permeabilization. In agreement with Western blot results, the level of annexin A2 was significantly greater in the NaOx-exposed cells, whereas L. acidophilus effectively prevented the upregulation of annexin A2 mediated by NaOx (Fig. 7A, B). There were no significant differences among groups observed for ezrin, HSP70, and HSP90 (Supplementary Figs. S2B, S3B, and S4B).

Oxalate consumption by L. acidophilus in AU

To investigate whether the protective effect of L. acidophilus on NaOx-mediated epithelial barrier disruption and COM-mIMCD-3 adhesion was due to the oxalate-degrading ability of L. acidophilus, an oxalate-consumption assay was performed. Oxalate level in AU in the absence or presence of L. acidophilus was measured by HPLC. First, the standard curve of oxalate in AU was generated by varying NaOx concentrations in AU. Histograms of the standard NaOx concentrations in AU revealed that the retention time of oxalate was 5.438 min (Fig. 8A, red arrow). After 24-h incubation, oxalate levels in the AU supplemented with 4 mM in the absence or presence of L. acidophilus at a final density of 1 × 103 CFU/ml were then measured using the constructed standard curve. The results showed a significant decline in oxalate level in the AU with L. acidophilus compared with the control AU without L. acidophilus, indicating oxalate consumption by L. acidophilus in the AU environment (Fig. 8B).

Discussion

Recently, an increasing number of reports on the association between the urinary microbiome and KSD have been published [55]. A decline in urinary bacterial diversity in patients with KSD compared with individuals without KSD has been revealed [33, 34, 56]. Such a decline has also been observed in the urinary microbiome of felines with urethral obstruction [57]. Furthermore, differential abundance of urinary bacteria between stone-containing and non-stone kidneys has been exhibited [33, 35, 36, 44, 56, 58]. For instance, Lactobacillus spp. are highly abundant in healthy individuals in comparison with patients with KSD [34, 36, 56, 58]. Accordingly, Lactobacillus is the genus associated with healthy kidneys and has been suggested to play a protective role against KSD [34, 59].

Recently, L. acidophilus at its physiologic urinary density (1 × 103 CFU/ml) has been reported to directly inhibit COM crystal formation and growth in vitro [41]. Such a direct inhibitory effect of L. acidophilus is due to its S-layer surface protein [41]. In this present study, the protective roles of L. acidophilus in other aspects of KSD pathogenesis, i.e., epithelial barrier maintenance and modification of COM receptor expression, were investigated. mIMCD-3 cells, which originated from inner medullary collecting duct epithelium, were chosen for the present study. These cells are likely to directly contact the urinary microbiome owing to their proximity to the renal pelvis, the uppermost location in which the urinary microbiome has been identified [43–45]. Consistent with a recently published study [41], the L. acidophilus concentration used in this present study was at its physiologic urinary range (1 × 103 CFU/ml), which did not cause severe cytotoxicity or affect mIMCD-3 epithelial cell death.

High urinary oxalate level or hyperoxaluria (approximately > 0.25 mM) is a risk factor of KSD [53, 60]. High oxalate level leads to the formation of urinary COM crystals and crystal deposition in kidney tissue [61–63]. To find the optimal concentration of NaOx to mimic its effect in vivo, the high urinary oxalate level without causing significant cell death was investigated. From the 0.2–1.0 mM range tested, NaOx at 0.6 mM was found to be the optimal concentration for studying mIMCD-3 cells in vitro.

Although not causing cell death, high oxalate has deleterious effects on renal cells by reducing metabolic activity, increasing oxidative stress, and inducing transdifferentiation of tubular epithelial cells to mesenchymal cells [61, 64, 65]. The epithelial-to-mesenchymal transdifferentiation accompanies the downregulation of TJ-associated proteins, such as occludin and ZO-1. Such TJ protein downregulation indicates disruption of epithelial barrier function, which contributes to KSD pathogenesis [16, 17, 64, 66]. The epithelial barrier disruption induced by oxalate has been shown by a study in MDCK distal tubular cells, in which TER, claudin-1, occludin, and ZO-1 levels declined after exposure to oxalate [67]. In addition to oxalate ions, several studies have demonstrated that COM crystals also reduce TER and ZO-1 expression in MDCK cells [18–22]. In agreement with previous reports, this present study revealed that NaOx caused a reduction of TER in mIMCD-3 collecting duct cells owing to the downregulation of ZO-1 expression.

Expression of epithelial barrier-regulatory genes in the mouse colon has been reported to be influenced by colonic microbiome because these genes are downregulated in mice maintained under the germ-free condition [37]. In addition, an antibiotic treatment leads to decreased expression of claudin-1, occludin, and ZO-1 proteins in murine intestines [38]. Consequently, intestinal epithelial permeability increases, related to abundant changes in intestinal bacteria and a decrease in bacterial production of short-chain fatty acids [38, 68]. However, administration of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum strains, isolated from cheese and human feces, can prevent downregulation of claudin-2 and occludin in the colon of rats fed with urolithiatic agents [69, 70]. Additionally, several Bifidobacterium spp. and Lactobacillus spp. isolated from human feces can increase the TER of Caco-2 cell monolayer [71]. Treatment with L. fructosus C2, a chicken intestinal bacterium, in Caco-2 cells can prevent pathogenic bacteria-mediated increase in paracellular permeability and downregulation of occludin [72].

Although several studies have reported beneficial roles of microbiome in maintaining intestinal epithelial barrier, its roles in renal tubular epithelial barrier, especially in the aspect of KSD pathogenesis, have not previously been documented. Here, we demonstrated that L. acidophilus could strengthen the renal tubular epithelial barrier as indicated by elevated TER in mIMCD-3 cell monolayer co-cultured with L. acidophilus. When mIMCD-3 cells were exposed to NaOx under co-culture with L. acidophilus, the effect of NaOx-mediated TER reduction was completely abolished. Similar to the commensal bacterial effects on intestinal TJ proteins, L. acidophilus also abolished the NaOx-mediated downregulation of ZO-1.

As discussed above, high oxalate level leads to the formation of COM crystals. Adhesion of the formed crystals to the epithelium lining renal tubules is an important step in crystal retention in the tissue and KSD pathogenesis [1, 2, 60]. Several membrane proteins on MDCK cells, including annexin A1, annexin A2, α-enolase, ezrin, HSP70, and HSP90, have been reported to bind COM crystals and serve as COM membrane receptors [8, 12, 73–75]. Apical membrane localization of annexin A1 in MDCK cells is induced by high calcium exposure, thereby enhancing the COM binding capability of the cells [73, 76]. Neutralizing annexin A1 with annexin A1 antibody and inhibiting its membrane translocation using caffeine and estrogen mitigate high calcium-enhanced COM crystal binding to MDCK cells [11, 54, 73]. The membrane level of α-enolase also increases in parallel with the increased COM crystal adhesion in MDCK cells exposed to oxalate [24, 54]. Such oxalate-induced upregulation of α-enolase can be prevented by estrogen treatment, and as a consequence, COM crystal binding decreases [54]. In addition, high uric acid increases HSP70 and HSP90 levels on MDCK apical membranes, and neutralization of surface HSP70 and HSP90 prevents uric acid-enhanced COM crystal-cell adhesion [77].

In this present study, expression of all COM crystal receptors mentioned above was examined in mIMCD-3 cells treated with NaOx. Unfortunately, annexin A1 and α-enolase could not be detected (data not shown). This was possibly because of no expression or extremely low levels of annexin A1 and α-enolase on mIMCD-3 surfaces. Among annexin A2, ezrin, HSP70, and HSP90, our present study revealed that NaOx enhanced only annexin A2 expression. Moreover, NaOx enhanced the membrane localization of annexin A2 on mIMCD-3 cells, which correlated with the increased capability of the cells to bind COM crystals. However, annexin A2 expression and membrane localization could be maintained at basal levels when the NaOx-exposed cells were co-cultured with L. acidophilus. These findings were in agreement with the crystal-binding capability of mIMDC-3 cells cultured under the same conditions.

Several Lactobacillus spp., especially L. acidophilus, have been documented with oxalate-degrading ability when they are cultured in standard media [78–81]. Reduction of fecal bacteria with oxalate-degrading ability and a decrease in fecal oxalate-degrading activity have been reported in parallel with an increase in urinary oxalate level in rats treated with ceftriaxone for 1 week [82]. Administration of ceftriaxone together with a synbiotic containing oxalate-degrading bacteria, including L. acidophilus, restores fecal oxalate-degrading activity and reduces rat urinary oxalate level [82]. Moreover, plasma oxalate level in hyperoxaluric patients receiving oxalate-degrading Oxalobacter formigenes gradually decreases over time [83]. Oral administration of bacteria that reduce urinary oxalate level mitigates crystal deposition and inflammation in rat kidneys [70]. While the oxalate-degrading ability of L. acidophilus in the gastrointestinal tract has been strongly evident, its oxalate-degrading ability in the urine environment has not been reported.

The beneficial effects of L. acidophilus exhibited in this present study were hypothesized to be a result of its ability to degrade oxalate in the urine. To prove our hypothesis, an oxalate-consumption assay was conducted by maintaining L. acidophilus at its physiologic urinary concentration (1 × 103 CFU/ml) in AU supplemented with NaOx. As hypothesized, the oxalate concentration decreased after 24 h of culture compared with the control NaOx-supplemented AU without L. acidophilus. However, this result was not concordant with a previous report showing that 1 × 103 CFU/ml L. acidophilus was unable to degrade oxalate [41]. This difference is likely because the previous study performed an experiment in a very limited carbon source buffer, which contained only Tris/NaCl/NaOx, and the incubation time was only 1 h [41]. The carbon-starving condition is known to delay the growth of many bacteria [84, 85]. Hence, the 1-h incubation in a carbon-starving condition was insufficient for L. acidophilus to degrade oxalate. The AU-Siriraj formula used in our present study provided more carbon sources to L. acidophilus, and with the longer incubation time of 24 h, L. acidophilus obviously reduced the oxalate level in AU. Therefore, the beneficial effects of L. acidophilus were likely to be mediated by its oxalate-degrading activity.

However, the oxalate-degrading activity might not be the sole mechanistic factor responsible for the beneficial effects of L. acidophilus. Other factors, such as the surface-layer (S-layer), an outermost structure on L. acidophilus cells, may also play roles in such beneficial effects, as has been shown in our recent study illustrating that L. acidophilus without S-layer loses its inhibitory activity against COM crystal adhesion to renal TEC surfaces [41]. In another study, antibacterial peptides and metabolites secreted from L. crispatus, L. gasseri, and L. jensenii could also prevent crystallization induced by Proteus mirabilis in AU [86]. Moreover, L. crispatus may reduce calcium oxalate crystal growth and aggregation by secreting molecules that can shift the microenvironment toward growth and aggregation inhibition [87]. Therefore, further investigations are required to precisely identify all the factors responsible for the protective effects of L. acidophilus against KSD.

Although this present study has clearly demonstrated the preventive effects of L. acidophilus against KSD in the urinary system, its limitations should be noted. First, all of the experiments were performed in vitro, which cannot simulate all the in vivo conditions. In particular, many factors, such as other urinary bacteria, the complexity of urinary proteins, urine pH, etc., can act as either agonists or antagonists of L. acidophilus. Second, a mouse cell line (mIMCD-3) was used instead of a human cell line (because of the unavailability of the human medullary collecting duct cell line). Expression of some proteins in mouse cells may differ from those in human cells, as in the cases of annexin A1 and α-enolase discussed above. Accordingly, the degree of the preventive effects of L. acidophilus on human cells may be different from that observed on mouse cells, and effector or target proteins may also differ. Last but not least, this present study investigated the protective roles of L. acidophilus against the COM KSD model induced by high oxalate. In fact, kidney stones can be non-oxalate types, and even the COM stones can be induced by several factors other than high oxalate. Therefore, the data observed in the present study may not necessarily recapitulate what happens in other stone types and COM stones induced by other factors.

Conclusions

Our results clearly illustrate that oxalate reduced the TER of inner medullary collecting duct epithelial cells by downregulating ZO-1 expression, a TJ protein. Such reduction of TER and ZO-1 downregulation indicated oxalate-mediated epithelial barrier disruption. Moreover, oxalate promoted COM crystal adhesion to epithelial cell surfaces by upregulating the expression of annexin A2 (a membrane receptor of COM crystals). However, when the cells exposed to oxalate were co-cultured with L. acidophilus, the effects of oxalate on epithelial barrier disruption, enhanced COM crystal-cell adhesion, and annexin A2 upregulation were negated. Such beneficial effects of L. acidophilus were, at least in part, due to its oxalate-consumption ability, resulting in lowering of the oxalate level in the AU. A schematic summary of all these results is illustrated in Fig. 9. Taken together, this present study provides direct evidence of the protective effects of L. acidophilus, a bacterium found in the healthy urinary microbiome, on KSD development. The presence of L. acidophilus in the urinary system can decrease the risk of kidney stone formation and KSD progression.
