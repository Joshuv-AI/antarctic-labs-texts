Cannabinoid type 1 receptors in the mice prefrontal cortex regulate object location memory acquisition via GABAergic neurons
Authors: Tomohiro Tokutake, Jun Yokose, Yusuke Yano, Yuki Shigetsura, Shin-Ichi Muramatsu, Atsumi Nitta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdverse psychiatric symptoms caused by cannabis are a significant concern, and Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) has been identified as a key contributor to these symptoms. THC binds to cannabinoid type 1 receptors (CB1Rs), which are abundant in the brain and associated with cognition. The prefrontal cortex (PFC) is crucial for cognitive functions. However, the functions of CB1Rs in the PFC in cognition processes remain unclear. Here, we injected arachidonylcyclopropylamide (ACPA), a CB1Rs agonist, into the PFC of male C57BL/6J mice via the cannula and conducted cognitive tests, including the novel object recognition test and object location test (OLT).ResultsThese tests assessed memory in three stages: acquisition, consolidation, and retrieval. ACPA was administered immediately before each stage, and its intra-PFC administration specifically impaired memory acquisition in the OLT. In addition, in vivo microdialysis revealed that ACPA reduced extracellular GABA levels within the PFC. Next, we produced an adeno-associated virus with a glutamic acid decarboxylase promoter and an hM3Dq-encording chemogenic activator to activate GABAergic neurons in the PFC. Subsequently, deschloroclozapine (DCZ), an hM3Dq agonist, restored the memory acquisition impaired by ACPA.ConclusionOur findings suggest that CB1Rs in the PFC are involved in memory acquisition through the regulation of GABA release, offering new insights into how cannabis use lead to cognitive impairment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12993-025-00306-w.

Background

Cannabis is a regulated substance widely used worldwide; however, its use is a major social concern [1]. Cannabis has been reported to negatively affect cognitive functions, including memory, in animals and humans [2, 3]. Notably, memory impairment is a well-known acute effect of cannabis [3]. Cannabinoid type 1 receptors (CB1Rs) in the brain are thought to be involved in the mechanism underlying memory impairment [4, 5]. These receptors have been identified as specific binding sites for Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) [6]. CB1Rs are Gi/o protein-coupled receptors possessing seven transmembrane domains [7, 8]. They are highly expressed in the brain [9, 10], with GABAergic interneurons in the cortex expressing more CB1Rs than excitatory neurons in axon terminals [11]. A major function of CB1Rs is to inhibit the release of neurotransmitters, including glutamate and GABA, when stimulated by an agonist [12].

However, the functions of CB1Rs in each brain region are poorly known, and effective treatment for cannabis-related memory impairment has not been developed yet. Elucidating the mechanisms of action of CB1Rs can aid in the development of new treatments. Here, we focused on the prefrontal cortex (PFC), which is central to executive functions, including decision- making and memory [13, 14]. Several studies conducted in rodents have used recognition memory tests to measure cognitive functions, and the PFC has been identified to be tightly involved in recognition memory [15–17]. We examined the functions of CB1Rs in recognition memory in the PFC and the therapeutic targets for cannabis-caused memory impairment. The mice were administered with arachidonylcyclopropylamide (ACPA), a CB1Rs agonist, into their PFC and subjected to a recognition memory test. The stages of memory include acquisition, consolidation, and retrieval [18, 19]. We defined training as the acquisition stage, immediately after training as the consolidation stage, and test as the retrieval stage in the recognition memory tests. We administered ACPA into mice PFC right before each stage. Our study may shed light on the mechanisms of cannabis-induced memory impairment and offer a new therapeutic strategy.

Methods

Animals

Male adult C57BL/6J mice (7–8 weeks old) of 20–25 g were purchased from Nihon SLC (Hamamatsu, Japan) and housed under controlled temperature (23 °C ± 2 °C) and humidity (50% ± 5%), and a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle (7:00–19:00). The mice were provided standard rodent chow (CE-2, CLEA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and water ad libitum. All mice were allowed to acclimatize to the environment for 1–2 weeks. The animal experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Toyama (Approval Number: A2023PHA14), and experiments were conducted in accordance with the Institutional Animal Experiment Handling Rules of the University of Toyama. The number of animals used was carefully estimated and kept to the minimum necessary for meaningful data interpretation.

Drugs

ACPA was purchased from Funakoshi (Tokyo, Japan) and dissolved in saline. ACPA (10 µg/ml) or saline was bilaterally administered into the PFC of mice 5 min before behavioral tests at 1 µl/mouse (0.5 µl/min/each side = 10 ng/mouse) for 1 min using a micro syringe pump (ESP-64; Eicom, Kyoto, Japan). The ACPA infusion dose and rate/speed were based on a previous study [20].

Deschloroclozapine (DCZ) was purchased from Med Chem Express (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) and dissolved in vehicle (0.001% DMSO in saline). DCZ (1 µg/kg) or vehicle were intraperitoneally (i.p.) administered 30 min before administering ACPA or saline. The DCZ dose was selected as the lowest dose previously reported to activate hM3Dq via intraperitoneal injection in C57BL/6J mice [21].

Cannulation into the PFC for behavioral tests

Cannula implantation was conducted based on our previous study [22]. The mice were anesthetized with three mixed anesthetics (i.p., domitol 0.75 mg/kg, Orion, Espoo, Finland; midazoram 4 mg/kg, Sandoz, Tokyo, Japan; butorphanol 5 mg/kg, Meiji Seika Pharma, Tokyo, Japan.) and fixed in a brain stereotaxic apparatus (SR-5 M, Narishige, Tokyo, Japan). The cannula (Terumo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) was implanted into the mice PFC (anterior/posterior [AP]: +1.8 mm, medial/lateral [ML]: ±0.5 mm, dorsal/ventral [DV]: 2.25 mm from Bregma) [23]. The cannula and reinforcing screw were fixed together with dental general-purpose acrylic resin (GC Dental Products, Kasugai, Japan) and superfast-curing ordinary-temperature resin (GC Dental Products). The cannulated mice were kept in individual cages to prevent the breaking of the cannula. Behavioral tests were performed 1–2 weeks after cannula implantation.

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) production and microinjection into the PFC

An AAV was constructed and injected into the PFC based on previous studies [24, 25]. The pAAV-hSyn-hM3Dq (Gq)-mCherry plasmid was purchased from Addgene (Watertown, MA, USA). The hSyn promoter region including the above AAV plasmid was replaced with the glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) promoter [26], which generated the AAV1-mGAD-hM3Dq-mCherry plasmid. The titer of the AAV vector was 3.2 × 1011 vg/ml. The mice were anesthetized with three mixed anesthetics and fixed in a brain stereotaxic apparatus. The AAV vector (0.5 µl/side) was injected into their PFC (AP: + 1.8 mm, ML: ± 0.3 mm, DV: 2.25 mm) at 0.025 µl/min. AAV injection were performed at different medial/lateral sites from cannulation because treatments for the same sites are likely to severely damage brain tissue. Experiments were performed 3–4 weeks after microinjection. This study was approved by the Board of Safety Committee for Recombination DNA Experiments of the University of Toyama (G2020PHA-5).

Behavioral tests

Locomotor activity test

The locomotor activity test was performed based on a previous study [27]. The mice were placed individually in a transparent acrylic cage with a black frosted Plexiglas floor (45 × 25 × 40 cm) 5 min after intra-PFC ACPA (0.5 µl/each side = 10 ng/mouse) or saline administration and 30 min after i.p. DCZ (1 µg/kg) or vehicle administration. The locomotor activity was measured every 5 min for a total of 60 min using digital counters with infrared sensors (Scanet MV-40; Melquest, Toyama, Japan).

Novel object recognition test (NORT)

NORT was performed based on our previous study [28]. The mice were habituated to a Plexiglas box (30 × 30 × 35 cm) for 30 min for 3 days. Twenty-four hours after the last habituation, the mice were placed in the corner of the Plexiglas box, and two identical objects (objects A and A’) were placed in the box and explored by the mice for 10 min (training). Twenty-four hours after training, object A was placed at the same location as the previous day, whereas object A’ was replaced by novel object B. The mice were placed in the corner of the Plexiglas box and allowed to explore the objects for 10min (test). The training and test were filmed with a video camera, and the approach time was measured visually. The exploratory behavior was defined as the sniffing, biting and touching behaviors for each object. Specifically, the exploration time was counted when facing the object and located within 1 cm of it, whereas behaviors such as sitting or climbing on the object and touching body parts without facing the object were excluded from exploratory time. The exploratory preference percentage was defined as follows: training: (approach time to object A’) / (approach time to objects A + A’) × 100 and test: (approach time to novel object B) / (approach time to objects A + B) × 100.

Intra-PFC ACPA (0.5 µl/each side = 10 ng/mouse) or saline was administered at each memory stage as follows: acquisition, 5 min before training; consolidation, immediately after training; and retrieval, 5 min before the test.

Object location test (OLT)

OLT was performed based on a previous study [25]. The mice were habituated to a Plexiglas box (30 × 30 × 35 cm) for 30 min for 3 days. Twenty-four hours after the last habituation, the mice were placed in the corner of the Plexiglas box, and two identical objects (objects C and C’) were placed in the box and explored by the mice for 10 min (training). Twenty-four hours after training, object C was placed in the same position as the previous day, whereas object C’ was moved to a new location. The mice were placed in the corner of the Plexiglas box and allowed to explore objects for 10 min (test). Short-term OLT was performed by changing the time between training and test from 24 h (long-term) to 3 h (short-term). The mice’s exploratory behavior during training and test was recorded with a video camera, and their approach time was measured visually. The exploratory behavior was defined as the sniffing, biting and touching behaviors for each object. Specifically, the exploration time was counted when facing the object and located within 1 cm of it, whereas behaviors such as sitting or climbing on the object and touching body parts without facing the object were excluded from exploratory time. The exploratory preference percentage was defined as follows: training: (approach time to object C’) / (approach time to objects C + C’) × 100 and test: (approach time to object C’ [new location]) / (approach time to objects C + C’ [new location]) × 100.

Intra-PFC ACPA (0.5 µl/each side = 10 ng/mouse) or saline was administered at each memory stage as follows: acquisition, 5 min before training; consolidation, immediately after training; and retrieval, 5 min before test or all stages. DCZ (1 µg/mice) or vehicle was administered 30 min before the training.

Cannulation for in vivo Microdialysis

The mice were anesthetized with three mixed anesthetics and fixed in a brain stereotaxic apparatus. The cannula (AG-2, Eicom) was only unilaterally implanted into the mice PFC (AP: + 1.8 mm, ML: + 0.5 mm, DV: 1.25 mm) [23]. The cannula and reinforcing screw were fixed together with dental general-purpose acrylic and superfast- curing ordinary-temperature resins. GABA levels in the PFC were measured 1–2 days after the implantation.

Measurement of extracellular GABA levels in the Pfc using in vivo Microdialysis

In vivo microdialysis was performed based on a previous study [29]. Dialysate (Ringer’s solution) was collected into a sample vial (SV-08; Eicom) every 30 min at 1.0 µl/min using a fraction collector (EF-80; Eicom). Next, 7 µl of o-phthalaldehyde solution (OPA; 4 mmol/l) and 0.04% mercaptoethanol in carbonate buffer (pH 9.5) were added to 21 µl aliquots of the GABA samples. After allowing the OPA solution to react with the GABA samples for 1.5 min, 21 µl aliquots were injected into the HPLC system (HTEC-50; Eicom) using a sampling injector (M-500; Eicom). GABA was separated on an SA-50DS column (Eicom) at 30 °C using phosphate buffer (pH 3.5) containing EDTA (0.5 µg/l) and 50% methanol as the mobile stage at a flow rate of 500 µl/min. The obtained compounds were quantified using an electrochemical detector comprising a glassy carbon working electrode (WE-GC; Eicom, set at + 600 mV) against a silver–silver chloride reference electrode (RE-500; Eicom). ACPA (10 µg/ml) was dissolved in Ringer’s solution and used as the dialysate for 0–30 min. DCZ (1 µg/kg) or saline was i.p. administered at 0 min. The baseline GABA levels were calculated as the average of two successive fractions immediately before administration (− 30 min and 0 min). Chromatograms were generated and analyzed using an integrator (PowerChrom; AD Instruments, Bella Vista, NSW, Australia) connected to a personal computer.

Nissl staining

Nissl staining was performed based on a previous study [30]. The mice were anesthetized with three mixed anesthetics and perfused with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for fixation. The brains were collected and soaked overnight in 4% PFA, which was subsequently replaced with sucrose solutions (10%, 20%, and 30% in PBS). The brains were embedded in Tissue-Tek® O.C.T Compounds (Sakura Finetek Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and cut into 20 μm sections with CM3050S (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were fixed in methanol for 10 min and washed with PBS containing 0.1% Tween20 (PBS-T). Then, the sections were incubated with 0.1% cresyl violet acetate (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA) for 10 min at 37 °C and washed with 10% acetate in 95% ethanol for 5 min and 100% ethanol for 5 min. After washing, the sections were mounted using Mount-Quick (Daido Sangyo, Tokyo, Japan). Bright-field images were captured using a BZ-X800 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed based on a previous study [31]. The mice were anesthetized with three mixed anesthetics and perfused with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% PFA for fixation. The brains were collected and soaked overnight in 4% PFA, which was subsequently replaced with sucrose solutions (10%, 20%, and 30% in PBS). The brains were embedded in Tissue-Tek® O.C.T Compounds and cut into 0.20 μm sections with CM3050S. The sections were fixed in methanol for 10 min and washed with PBS-T. Then, the sections were incubated with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min and washed again with PBS-T. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with 10% goat serum (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) in PBS for 1 h and incubated with an anti-GAD67 antibody (monoclonal, 1:50, ab213508, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) with 10% goat serum in PBS at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS-T, the sections were incubated with DAPI at room temperature for 10 min and with CF™ 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:1,000; Biotium, Fremont, CA, USA) at room temperature for 2 h. After washing, the sections were mounted using Fluoromount (Diagnostic BioSystems, Pleasanton, CA, USA). mCherry was observed by autofluorescence without using antibodies. Fluorescent images were obtained using BZ-X800 and ZEISS LSM 780 microscopes (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Prism version 9 (Graph Pad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for statistical analysis. All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The results were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. Statistically significant differences between two groups were assessed using an unpaired t-test (parametric test). Outliers were identified by Smirnov-Grubbs test and were excluded prior to statistical evaluation. Statistical differences among four groups were determined using two-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test. F values were described by the main effect of ACPA (saline or ACPA), main effect of DCZ (vehicle or DCZ), and interaction effect. Over time changes in in vivo microdialysis were determined using repeated measures two-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test.

Results

Intra-PFC ACPA administration through three stages (Acquisition, Consolidation, and Retrieval) of recognition memory only impaired object location memory

C57BL/6J male mice were subjected to recognition and spatial tasks (NORT and OLT) after ACPA or saline administration into the PFC to investigate the contribution of CB1Rs to recognition memory. We implanted a cannula into the mice PFC bilaterally to administer ACPA or saline (Fig. 1a). The locomotor activity of mice administered with ACPA did not change compared with that of mice treated with saline (t19 = 0.07548, p = 0.9406; unpaired t-test) (Fig. 1b). Next, the NORT and OLT were performed sequentially after each habituation session (Fig. 1c). Mice which were different from the mice used locomotor activity test in the Fig. 1b were utilized in these tests. ACPA was repeatedly administered through three memory stages (acquisition, consolidation, and retrieval) in the NORT and OLT. Mice equally explored the two objects in the training phase of NORT and OLT (Fig.S1a, b). In the NORT, mice administered with ACPA (ACPA group) did not exhibit a significant different exploratory time compared with mice treated with saline (saline group) in the test phase (t19 = 0.1747, p = 0.8631; unpaired t-test) (Fig. 1d). In the OLT, the ACPA group displayed a significant reduction in the exploratory preference compared with the saline group (t19 = 4.279, p = 0.0004; unpaired t-test) (Fig. 1e). These results revealed that CB1R activation in the PFC may contribute to object location memory.

We examined the effect of ACPA administration timing on the exploratory preference in the NORT individually to identify which memory stages is crucial for memory impairment with ACPA in the PFC. ACPA or saline was administered in the pre-training, post-training, or pre-test of the NORT (Fig. S1c). The mice approached objects equally in the NORT training (Fig. S1d-f). In the pre-training, post-training and pre-test stage, the exploratory preference for the objects in the ACPA and saline groups was similar (Fig. S1g-i). These results suggested that CB1R in the PFC do not affect novel object recognition.

Intra-PFC ACPA administration impaired short- and long-term memory acquisition in the OLT

We next examined the effect of ACPA administration timing on the exploratory preference in the OLT individually since we observed an attenuation of exploratory preference with ACPA (Fig. 1e). ACPA or saline was administered with a single dose in each memory test stage during the OLT (Fig. 2a). The mice approached objects equally in the OLT training (Fig. S2a-c). In the pre-training stage, mice in the ACPA group displayed a significant reduction in their exploratory preference compared with those in the saline group in the test phase (t16 = 3.805, p = 0.0016; unpaired t-test) (Fig. 2b). In contrast, in the post-training and pre-test stages, the exploratory preference to the object was similar in the two groups (t14 = 0.3577, p = 0.7259; unpaired t-test, t14 = 0.7663, p = 0.4562; unpaired t-test) (Fig. 2c, d).

We administered ACPA or saline into the PFC of mice before training and performed the test 3 h after training to further assess whether ACPA influences short-term memory acquisition in the OLT (Fig. 2e). Mice in the ACPA group showed significant reduction in their exploratory preference compared with those in the saline group in the test phase (t10 = 3.287, p = 0.0082; unpaired t-test) (Fig. 2f) but not in the training phase (Fig. S2d). These results suggest that CB1R activation in the PFC is crucial for short- and long-term memory acquisition in the OLT.

Intra-PFC ACPAadministration reduced extracellular GABA levels in the PFC

CB1Rs are highly localized in the GABAergic neurons of the presynaptic terminals in the PFC [11] and inhibit GABA transmission [32, 33]. We performed in vivo microdialysis in the PFC of free-moving mice and APCA was administered for the first 30 min of 120 min to examine whether ACPA decreases extracellular GABA levels in the PFC (interaction effects: F5,50 = 2.629, p = 0.0346; main effect of time: F5,50 =0. 9049, p = 0.4854; main effect of ACPA: F1,10 = 8.046, p = 0.0177, repeated measures two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test) (Fig. 2g). The area under the curve (AUC) value of the GABA levels in the ACPA group was lower than that in the control (Ringer’s solution) (t10 = 2.872, p = 0.0166; unpaired t-test) (Fig. 2h). These results indicate that suppression of GABA release by intra-PFC ACPA administration may be a core factor in the impairment of memory acquisition (Figs. 1e and 2b and f).

ACPA-induced memory impairment was relieved by hM3Dq chemogenic activation of GABAergic neurons in the PFC

We examined whether the activation of GABAergic neurons in the PFC during the OLT acquisition phase reversed the ACPA-induced memory impairment. We locally injected the AAV1-mGAD-hM3Dq-mCherry plasmid, comprising AAV with a GAD promoter encoding Gq-coupled excitatory designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs conjugated into the PFC 1–2 weeks before cannula implantation (Fig. 3a). The specific expression of hM3Dq-mCherry in the GABAergic neurons in the PFC was confirmed (Fig. 3b). In addition, mice expressing hM3Dq-mCherry, which were i.p. injected with DCZ, did not display any significant change in their locomotor activity compared with control mice injected with the vehicle (t13 = 0.05658, p = 0.9557; unpaired t-test) (Fig. 3c). Next, we confirmed that whether the GABA levels in the PFC were significantly increased following the i.p. DCZ administration through in vivo microdialysis (interaction effects: F5,50 = 2.016, p = 0.0924; main effect of time: F5,50 = 1.302, p = 0.2784; main effect of DCZ: F1,10 = 4.496, p = 0.0600; repeated measures two-way ANOVA) (Fig. 3d). The AUC values of GABA levels in mice administered with DCZ were higher than those in mice treated with the vehicle (t10 = 2.277, p = 0.0460; unpaired t-test) (Fig. 3e).

Finally, we investigated whether the ACPA-induced memory impairment in the OLT was correlated to the reduction in extracellular GABA levels in the PFC. We expressed hM3Dq-mCherry in GABAergic neurons in the PFC through AAV injection and implanted the cannula (Fig. 3f). In the OLT, DCZ or vehicle was i.p. injected 30 min before the test phase, and ACPA or saline was administered into the PFC 5 min before the OLT training phase. During the training phase, no significant differences were observed in the exploratory preference for the two objects among all groups (Fig. S3a). The intra-PFC ACPA administration significantly reduced the exploratory preference compared with the saline group (vehicle-saline group vs. vehicle-ACPA group; p < 0.0001, interaction effects: F1,23 = 40.33, p < 0.0001; main effect of DCZ: F1,23 = 0.3609, p = 0.5539; main effect of ACPA: F1,23 = 2.405, p = 0.1346; two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test; with respect to Fig .3g, the same statistical procedures were applied to all subsequent intergroup comparisons). In contrast, mice treated with DCZ and ACPA (DCZ-ACPA group) exhibited an increased exploratory preference compared with those in the vehicle-ACPA group (vehicle-ACPA group vs. DCZ-ACPA group, p = 0.0003) (Fig. 3g). Moreover, i.p. DCZ administration (DCZ-saline group) reduced the exploratory preference of mice compared with the control vehicle administration (vehicle-saline group vs. DCZ-saline group, p = 0.035), whereas the DCZ-ACPA group was rescued the reduction of exploratory preference (DCZ-saline group vs. DCZ-ACPA group, p = 0.0175) (Fig. 3g). These findings indicated suppression of GABA transmission through the activation of CB1Rs in the PFC.

Discussion

Our findings revealed that CB1Rs in the PFC play a crucial role in the regulation of object location memory acquisition through GABAergic neurons. In addition, our results provide new insights into a more targeted understanding of how CB1Rs in the PFC impact cognitive function, potentially paving the way for further research into therapeutic strategies for mitigating cannabis-related cognitive impairments.

In the present study, the mice received three doses (pre-training, acquisition; post-training, consolidation; and pre-test, retrieval) of ACPA, an agonist of CB1Rs, via the cannula into their PFC and were subjected to NORT and OLT (Fig. 1). The object location memory was specifically disrupted by the intra-PFC ACPA administration (Fig. 1e). In addition, the object location memory was specifically impaired by the pre-training ACPA administration (Fig. 2b, f). The PFC is crucial for spatial navigation as it contributes to successful spatial location encoding with the synchronous firing activity by working in concert with the hippocampus (HPC) [34–36]. CB1Rs are expressed in different subtypes of GABAergic neurons in the rodent cortex [37, 38]. They are abundantly expressed in cholecystokinin (CCK)-positive interneurons in the brain [37, 39, 40]. CCK-positive interneurons in the HPC maintain theta oscillations during exploratory behavior and encoding of spatial information [41]. However, the role of CCK-positive interneurons in the PFC for spatial encoding remains unclear; therefore, we conjecture that CB1R activation in CCK-positive interneurons via ACPA injection in the PFC may disrupt the synchronization between the PFC and HPC, thereby impairing the object location memory. Previous studies have suggested that somatostatin (SST) -positive but not parvalbumin-positive GABAergic neurons in the PFC play significant roles in spatial encoding [42, 43]. CB1Rs are also expressed in SST-positive GABAergic neurons [38]. SST-positive GABAergic neurons in the PFC might also aid in object location memory acquisition through CB1R activation. Therefore, further studies examining CB1R functions in different subtypes of GABAergic neurons, such as CCK and SST-positive neurons, are needed to elucidate the mechanism through which CB1Rs control the acquisition of object location memory in the PFC.

Previous studies have shown that both intraperitoneal administration and intra-PFC administration of CB1R agonist impaired behavioral performance in the NORT [44]. CB1R agonists dose-dependently impaired both short- and long-memory NORT [45]. However, no significant changes were observed in the NORT through all memory stages in the present study (Figs. 1d and 2g-h). In contrast, the memory impairment was observed in the OLT (Figs. 1e and 2b, f). Several studies have reported that the PFC is involved in object location memory [46–50]. Others have suggested that the PFC influences the NORT and object in place test (OIPT) but not the OLT [51–54]. Based on reports that cannabis affects spatial working memory [2, 3, 5], we considered the possible involvement of CB1Rs in processing spatial information and chose a simple task like the OLT over a more complex one like the OIPT. Our results suggest that CB1Rs in the PFC are involved in the processing of spatial information. However, the discrepancy between our results and those of previous reports might be attributed to differences in the methods of PFC damage and protocols of recognition tests. In the future, various memory other than NORT and OLT, such as OIPT, should help elucidate the function of CB1Rs in the PFC. Our findings do not contradict previous results and highlight the relationship between the PFC and recognition memory.

ACPA reduced the extracellular GABA levels in the PFC (Fig. 2h), whereas the GABAergic neurons activation by hM3Dq significantly increased the extracellular GABA levels (Fig. 3e). Unfortunately, we could only measure the total amount of GABA every 30 min because the extracellular GABA level obtained in 10 min during the behavioral test was the detection limit by microdialysis. Nevertheless, the activation of GABAergic neurons using hM3Dq recovered the ACPA-induced memory impairment, confirming our hypothesis (Fig. 3g). This may indicate that CB1R controls memory through the regulation of GABA release. The CB1Rs on presynaptic terminals and axons of GABAergic neurons regulate the inhibition of GABA transmission [32, 33], indicating that the reduction of GABA release through the CB1Rs can lead to cognitive dysfunction [5]. Interestingly, memory impairment in the OLT was observed in the DCZ-vehicle group (Fig. 3g). Moreover, the excitation/inhibition (E/I) imbalance in the PFC is involved in cognitive dysfunction and neuropsychiatric disorder [11, 55, 56]. It is possible that CB1R activation by agonists may disrupt the E/I balance within the PFC, which may in turn impair cognitive functions. Further study will be required to comprehensively assess the E/I balance within the PFC.

Male rodents have better spatial learning abilities than females [57]. We considered that males are more susceptible to CB1R agonists in spatial processing than females; therefore, we selected only male mice for our experiments. Little is known about the sex differences in acute cognitive functions associated with CB1Rs. However, given the sex differences in the effects of cannabis and THC [58, 59], further studies should be conducted on female mice.

THC can act as a cannabinoid receptor, including CB1R and cannabinoid type 2 receptor (CB2R) [60]. The mRNA level of CB1R is 100–300 times higher than that of CB2R in the brain [61]. In human cannabis use, THC is likely to affect the processing memory via CB1Rs. Therefore, the present study could contribute to the development of novel approaches to address the cognitive and behavioral consequences of cannabis use disorder.

Conclusion

In summary, we examined the function of CB1Rs in the PFC in recognition memory by administering a CB1R agonist directly into the mice PFC and using behavioral pharmacological techniques. Furthermore, our chemogenic and biochemical methods revealed that CB1Rs regulated memory acquisition by controlling the release of GABA.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that acute cannabis uses and CB1 receptor agonists impair cognitive functions, particularly memory, in humans [62, 63]. Moreover, reduced levels of GABA in the brain have been reported among cannabis users, potentially contributing to altered cognitive and behavioral outcomes [64]. However, direct evidence linking GABAergic dysfunction to cannabis-induced cognitive impairment has remained an open question. The present preclinical study contributes to bridging this gap by suggesting a mechanistic relationship between reduced GABA signaling and cannabinoid-related cognitive deficits.

Our findings provide an understanding of how CB1Rs in the PFC influence cognitive functions, especially object location memory, through the GABAergic mechanism.
