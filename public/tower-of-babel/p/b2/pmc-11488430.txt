Introduction

Chronic appendicitis in pediatric patients is rare and diagnostically challenging, often leading to misdiagnosis and delayed treatment. This case report details a 13-year-old male with chronic appendicitis successfully treated with Endoscopic Retrograde Appendicitis Therapy (ERAT), a novel, minimally invasive technique. Unlike the conventional surgical approach of appendectomy, ERAT preserves the appendix, maintaining its immune and digestive functions. This unique case highlights the potential of ERAT as a superior alternative to surgery, underscoring the need for further research and clinical trials to validate its efficacy and safety in pediatric gastroenterology.

Case description

A 13-year-old male presented with lower abdominal pain that had persisted for a year and gradually worsened, eventually becoming wheelchair-bound due to inability to walk independently one month before hospitalization. His medical history included a diagnosis of gastritis by endoscopy and colitis by colonoscopy six months earlier, with no specific changes in the colonic mucosal biopsy. The family history was noteworthy: his maternal grandfather died of “rectal cancer” and his maternal grandmother died of diabetes, hypertension, and coronary artery disease.

The examination showed tenderness in his lower abdomen, but no abnormality on abdominal ultrasound. A CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis identified multiple lymph nodes of varying sizes in the right lower quadrant and a filamentous high-density shadow in the appendix. Blood tests showed normal white blood cell count, neutrophils, and C-reactive protein levels (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

The Endoscopic Retrograde Appendicitis Treatment (ERAT) was conducted by a team of experienced gastroenterologists with over five years of experience in minimally invasive procedures. The ERAT procedure was performed on February 2024, following a thorough preoperative assessment, which included imaging and a detailed clinical history. This information is essential for evaluating the quality of ERAT and its applicability in clinical practice. The patient underwent ERAT during which a colonoscopy accessed the cecum via the anus, revealing an edematous appendiceal orifice covered with purulent plaque. After irrigation of the appendiceal lumen with endoscopic instruments, a large amount of pus and fecaliths was expelled, including a 1 cm diameter fecalith. One-month post-ERAT, the patient showed significant symptom relief without recurrence, and follow-up MRI and blood tests were normal.

Discussion

Chronic appendicitis typically presents as persistent pain in the right lower quadrant, often accompanied by nausea and fever. When the appendix is enlarged, the elevated white blood cell counts and ultrasound or CT findings can help in the diagnosis. However, this case was unusual and untypical because it involved prolonged abdominal pain with normal blood tests and without enlargement of the appendix by ultrasound or CT, and even no appendicitis detected through colonoscopy.

In the case of chronic appendicitis in this pediatric patient, specific lymph nodes were identified as being involved in the inflammatory process. The involved lymph nodes include the mesenteric lymph nodes, particularly those located near the ileocecal junction. These nodes can become reactive due to adjacent inflammation, potentially contributing to symptoms that mimic appendicitis. Understanding their role is critical in differentiating chronic appendicitis from other abdominal pathologies, as their involvement can indicate a broader inflammatory response. The diagnosis of appendicitis in this patient was determined through a combination of clinical evaluation and imaging studies. The patient initially presented with lower abdominal pain and underwent multiple diagnostic tests including ultrasound, X-ray, and gastroscopy, but no signs of acute inflammation were found. Of note, an important finding was that the CT showed the presence of fecal stones in the appendix, which suggested that appendicitis may have been caused by fecal stone obstruction.

The endoscopic findings revealed a significant amount of pus accumulation within the appendix, along with the presence of fecaliths. These findings are characteristic of acute appendicitis, raising questions regarding their inconsistency with prior imaging studies that suggested no inflammation. This discrepancy underscores the importance of correlating clinical presentations with diagnostic imaging to improve diagnostic accuracy.

The laboratory tests prior to the intervention showed no significant increase in neutrophils, and inflammatory markers remained within normal ranges. The follow-up period extended for 2 months, during which the patient did not experience any recurrence of symptoms. Before and after the ERAT procedure, antibiotic therapy was administered, but specific values and cultures from the appendix were not obtained. This omission highlights an area for improvement in future case management.

It is believed that the appendix serves both immune and digestive functions. It acts as a lymphoid organ by producing B and T lymphocytes to participate in the humoral immune response, and as a digestive organ by secreting digestive enzymes and hormones related to intestinal motility and growth, aiding digestion and reducing gastric bloating [1]. Additionally, the appendix serves as a reservoir for beneficial bacteria, releasing them to restore intestinal health when the beneficial bacteria in the gut diminish or disappear [2]. ERAT can not only treat appendicitis but also facilitate a more accurate diagnosis by identifying changes in the appendiceal orifice and extracting intraluminal purulent fluid to confirm appendicitis [3]. Most importantly, as a novel and minimally invasive technique, ERAT sets a “visible and innovative” way to surmount the problem of how to treat appendicitis while preserving the appendix, especially in the children.

Given the immune and digestive functions of the appendix, the application of ERAT in children is particularly important. ERAT preserves the child's appendix, thereby reducing digestive dysfunctions and infection risks associated with the loss of the appendix in childhood.

The diagnosis of appendicitis in this patient was established through a combination of clinical assessment and imaging. Initially presenting with lower abdominal pain, the patient underwent several diagnostic tests, including ultrasound and X-rays, which revealed no signs of acute inflammation. Notably, a significant finding was the echogenic appearance of the mesenteric lymph nodes, suggesting possible mesenteric lymphadenitis rather than classic appendicitis. Given the child's previous digestive issues, including gastritis, a differential diagnosis was necessary. While this case report primarily presents short-term outcomes following ERAT, it is crucial to highlight the need for long-term follow-up studies to evaluate the potential impacts on the immune and digestive functions of the appendix. Future investigations should focus on assessing whether the appendix retains its physiological role and how it affects overall health in pediatric patients.

To effectively consider ERAT as a treatment option for appendicitis, it is essential to establish criteria for case selection. These may include age, clinical presentation, imaging findings, and prior episodes of abdominal pain. Patients presenting with mild, recurrent abdominal symptoms and no appendix perforation may be ideal candidates for ERAT, allowing for a more conservative approach while minimizing surgical intervention.

Conclusion

This case study highlights the successful application of ERAT in a pediatric patient with chronic appendicitis, demonstrating its potential as a minimally invasive and effective treatment option. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [4]. ERAT not only provided significant symptom relief but also preserved the appendix, thereby maintaining its immune and digestive functions, which are particularly important in children. The procedure's ability to accurately diagnose and treat appendicitis while avoiding the risks associated with surgical resection marks a promising advancement in pediatric gastroenterology. Further studies and clinical trials are warranted to establish ERAT as a standard treatment protocol for chronic appendicitis in children, ensuring both safety and efficacy.
