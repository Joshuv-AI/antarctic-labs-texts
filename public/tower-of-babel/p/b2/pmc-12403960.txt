The effectiveness of vitamin B6 in reducing mastalgia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Foruzan Sharifipour, Shahla Faal Siahkal, Marzieh Bagherinia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMastalgia represents the most frequent complaint among women at some point during their lives that can have a profound impact on both the physical and mental well-being of individuals affected by this condition. This study aims to systematically evaluate the effect of vitamin B6 on the severity of mastalgia.DesignThis study is a meta-analysis of interventional studies that compare the effects of vitamin B6 to a placebo in healthy women with Mastalgia. The literature search encompassed studies published from inception until November 2024 and was conducted across various electronic databases, including Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar, in both English and Persian languages. The severity of breast pain was considered as the outcome measure. Breast pain linked to the menstrual cycle is called cyclic breast pain or cyclic mastalgia. The data were analyzed using Stata software version 17 and a random effects model. The quality of the selected articles was assessed utilizing the Cochrane Risk of Bias assessment checklist.ResultsA total of 301 studies with N = 951 participants (intervention:536, control: 415) were reviewed, with three studies ultimately included in the meta-analysis. The combined analysis of these three studies indicated that there were no differences between the two groups of vitamins B6 and placebo in a reduction of pain intensity (SMD = -3.57, 95% CI: -9.15 to 2.01; very low certainty of evidence), with high heterogeneity among the studies (I² = 99.56%). The overall quality of the included studies was assessed to be low.ConclusionThis meta-analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant effect of vitamin B6 on mastalgia severity. The evidence is limited by the small number of available studies, low methodological quality, and substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity. Further well-designed, large-scale randomized controlled trials are needed to reliably determine the efficacy of vitamin B6 for mastalgia.PROSPERO CRD 42,024,613,796.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-03991-x.Keywords: Vitamin B6, Mastalgia, Systematic review

Background

Mastalgia, or breast pain is the most common breast-related complaint among women, affecting approximately two-thirds of them at some point during in their lives [1]. This discomfort may occur in one or both breasts and is generally classified as either cyclic or non-cyclic, with potential intra- or extra-mammary origins [2]. Cyclic mastalgia is typically bilateral, intermittent and recurrent, and typically localized to the upper outer quadrant of the breast, often occuring the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle [3].

The precise etiology of cyclic mastalgia remains unclear; however, hormonal fluctuations including are believed to play a central role. These include elevated estrogen levels, progesterone deficiency, and hyperprolactinemia [4]. Furthermore, benign and inflammatory breast conditions, such as duct ectasia, fibrocystic changes, and fibroadenoma, are recognized contributors to breast pain [5].

Beyond its physical manifestations, mastalgia can significantly affect a woman’s psychological well-being and quality of life. It has been associated with reduced physical performance, diminish energy levels, and increased level of depression and anxiety [6, 7]. While mild mastalgia often requires no intervention, severe pain that disturbs daily activities may necessitate short-term treatment [8]. A variety of interventions have been proposed, including non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs) [9], relaxation therapy [10], nutritional and vitamins supplements [11, 12], herbal remedies such as Vitex agnus-castus [13], evening primrose oil [14], and hormone therapies like danazol and bromocriptine [15, 16].

Among the various nutritional options, vitamins B6 (pyridoxine) has garnered attention for its potential role in managing mastalgia [11]. As a crucial coenzyme in homocysteine metabolism, Vitamin B6 regulate neurotransmitters such as gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), epinephrine, serotonin, and melatonin, which may contribute to pain management. Studies have explored the efficacy of vitamin B6 in managing pain associated with carpal tunnel syndrome, diabetic neuropathy, back pain, depression, and migraine headaches [17–21]. Furthermore, its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties have been demonstrated in animal models, where it inhibited pro-inflammatory cytokine production, such as TNF-α and IL- 6 levels [22]. Also, Zhou et al. (2021) showed that vitamin B6 significantly reduced prolactin levels in men with hyperprolactinemia over a 16-week treatment period [23]. Numerous studies have suggested the potential benefits of vitamin B6 in alleviating breast pain [24–26]. However, to date, its overall efficacy has not been thoroughly analyzed in any comprehensive meta-analysis. Consequently, this study aims to systematically evaluate the impact of vitamin B6 on the severity of breast pain. By providing a comprehensive and evidence-based assessment of vitamin B6 for mastalgia, this meta-analysis can inform both clinical practice and future research, ultimately contributing to improved management strategies and enhanced quality of life for women experiencing breast pain.

Methods

Study type and protocol registration

The present study is a systematic review and meta-analysis that used the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [27] (Supplementary Table S1). The study protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database under the registration number CRD42024613796.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

The study included interventional studies (clinical trials or quasi-experimental trials) published in English or Persian that evaluated the effect of vitamin B6 supplementation (without restrictions on the prescribed dose) on the severity of mastalgia in women of reproductive age. Mastalgia was defined as breast pain, regardless of its association with the menstrual cycle or underlying breast conditions. Specifically, studies were included if they involved women experiencing cyclic mastalgia (breast pain related to the menstrual cycle), including those with fibrocystic changes presenting with cyclic pain patterns. The severity of mastalgia was assessed in the original studies with valid and reliable scales.

Although mastalgia can be classified as either cyclic (pain related to the menstrual cycle) or non-cyclic (pain unrelated to the cycle), this review specifically included studies of women with cyclic mastalgia. All included studies enrolled participants experiencing breast pain associated with the menstrual cycle, and studies of non-cyclic mastalgia were excluded from the analysis. This focus was chosen because non-cyclic mastalgia may be associated with underlying pathological conditions, including malignancy, and its etiology and management differ significantly from cyclic mastalgia. Non-cyclic breast pain is less likely to respond to vitamin-based therapies, as it may reflect structural or neoplastic breast disease rather than hormonal fluctuations. Therefore, to ensure the safety and clinical relevance of our findings, and to avoid confounding by cases with potential underlying malignancy, only studies of cyclic mastalgia were included in this review.

Exclusion criteria

Study exclusion criteria included letters to the editor, qualitative, review, observational articles, articles presented at conferences, theses, and inability to access the full text of relevant articles. Additionally, studies involving women with premenstrual syndrome (PMS) without mastalgia were excluded, as the focus of this meta-analysis was specifically on the effect of vitamin B6 on breast pain. While PMS can be associated with various symptoms, including mastalgia, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of vitamin B6 in alleviating breast pain itself. Pregnant women and women with breast malignancy were also excluded due to the potential confounding effects of hormonal changes during pregnancy and the different underlying mechanisms of breast pain associated with malignancy. Furthermore, the safety and efficacy of vitamin B6 supplementation during pregnancy and in the context of breast malignancy require separate consideration.

Participants and interventions

Participants in this study were women of reproductive age who had cyclic mastalgia. The intervention group received vitamin B6 supplementation, while the control group received a placebo or routine treatment.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was to assess the effect of vitamin B6 on the severity of mastalgia or breast pain, as assessed by the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). The secondary outcome was any adverse events related to the intervention.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the following databases: Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar. The search covered all available literature up to November 2024, with no time restriction. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords such as breast pain, vitamin B6, mastalgia, and pyridoxine were used, combined with Boolean operators (AND/OR). To ensure thoroughness, reference lists of relevant reviews and included articles were manually screened. The search strategy was tailored to the advanced search features of each database. Study selection

Endnote (version 8) was used to organize the studies. The study selection process was as follows: First, after searching the databases, all identified articles were imported into Endnote software. Next, Endnote’s duplicate detection function was used to identify and remove exact duplicates based on matching titles, authors, and publication years. Following the removal of duplicates, two independent researchers screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining articles to assess their eligibility based on the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The full texts of potentially eligible articles were then retrieved and reviewed in detail by the same two researchers. Any disagreements regarding eligibility were resolved through discussion and, if necessary, with the involvement of a third researcher. Finally, studies that met all inclusion criteria were included in the meta-analysis.

All retrieved articles were imported into EndNote version 8, where duplicates were automatically identified and removed based on title, authorship, and publication year. Two independent reviewers then screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining records against the eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were assessed for inclusion. Discrepancies in study selection were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Studies that met all inclusion criteria were selected for meta-analysis.

Data extraction

Data extraction was carried out by Two reviewers (F.SH and M.B) independently using a predefined form. Extracted variables included: first author’s name, year of publication, research location, study design, sample size in the intervention and control groups, participants mean age, body mass index (BMI), age at menarche, outcome measure (severity of breast pain), tools used to measure outcomes, and dosage and duration of vitamin B6 administration.

Risk of bias assessment

For quality assessment, we used different tools based on study design. For Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs), we applied the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) tool (Version 1). This tool assesses the risk of bias across six domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessors, incomplete outcome data, and selective reporting. The risk of bias for each domain was categorized as “low risk,” “high risk,” or “unclear risk” [28]. For quasi-experimental (non-randomized) studies, we used the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool. This tool is specifically designed for non-randomized studies and evaluates bias across seven domains: bias due to confounding, bias in selection of participants into the study, bias in classification of interventions, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing data, bias in measurement of outcomes, and bias in selection of the reported result. The overall risk of bias for each non-randomized study was categorized as “low,” “moderate,” “serious,” “critical,” or “no information” [29]. The quality assessment of all included studies was performed independently by two researchers (F.SH and M.B). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Certainty of evidence

We evaluated the certainty of evidence using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) framework. This approach considers five domains that may reduce confidence in effect estimates: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Based on these, the overall certainty of evidence was classified into four levels: high, moderate, low, or very low. These levels are defined as follows: High: We are very confident that the true effect lies close to that of the estimate of the effect. Moderate: We are moderately confident in the effect estimate: The true effect is likely to be close to the estimate of the effect, but there is a possibility that it is substantially different. Low: Our confidence in the effect estimate is limited: The true effect may be substantially different from the estimate of the effect. Very Low: We have very little confidence in the effect estimate: The true effect is likely to be substantially different from the estimate of the effect [30]. The GRADE assessment was conducted independently by two researchers (F.SH, SH.F), and any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data analysis

We analyzed the data using Stata software version 17 and a random effects model. The study selected the standardized mean difference with a 95% confidence interval as the effect size due to the varying methods of assessing breast pain severity across studies. We interpreted the SMD effect size as small for values between 0.2 and 0.5, medium for values between 0.5 and 0.8, and large for values greater than 0.8 [31]. We used the random model and inverse variance type to account for the possibility of heterogeneity in the primary studies. We assessed the heterogeneity of the studies using I2 indices and the Cochran Q test. An I² of < 25% was considered low heterogeneity, 25–50% moderate, 50–75% high, and > 75% very high heterogeneity [32]. We used Egger & Begg tests to assess study publication bias, taking into account a significance level of 0.10 due to the small number of studies [33]. We performed a sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method to assess the robustness of the meta-analysis results. In this analysis, we systematically and individually exclude studies to evaluate their impact on the overall results [34].

Results

The initial search of electronic databases yielded a total of 602 records (PubMed = 46, Scopus = 231, Embase = 303, Web of Science = 22, Cochrane Library = 0). An additional 27 articles were identified through manual searching (Google Scholar and reference lists). After removal of duplicates using Endnote, 301 unique records remained for screening. During the title and abstract screening stage, 286 records were excluded for the following main reasons: non-interventional study design (e.g., reviews, observational studies): 44%, ineligible populations (e.g., non-cyclic mastalgia or malignancy): 31%, irrelevant interventions (e.g., non-B6 supplements): 19%, and non-quantifiable outcomes: 6%. Next, the full texts of 15 articles were reviewed in detail. Ten articles were excluded at this stage for the following reasons (see Fig. 1): study population with breast cancer (n = 3), different interventions (using vitamin D or vitamin E instead of vitamin B6 as the intervention; n = 3), did not satisfy all inclusion criteria (n = 4; e.g., non-comparative design). Ultimately, five studies were included in the systematic review. Of these, three were eligible for meta-analysis. Two studies were excluded from quantitative synthesis: Shobeiri et al. [35] compared vitamin B6 to vitamin E (not placebo), violating our pre-specified protocol requiring placebo-controlled trials. Soltany et al. [36] used a single-arm (before-after) design, precluding comparison against a control group. A detailed breakdown of the study selection process is depicted in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The included studies cover the period from 2014 to 2023. The mean age of women in the vitamin B6 intervention group was 35.4 ± 3.6 years, and in the control group, it was 35.1 ± 4.4 years. Our analysis included four clinical trials [24–26, 35] with a control group. Only one study [36] employed a before-and-after design, while three studies with a control group included a placebo control group, and one compared vitamin B6 intervention with vitamin E. The largest sample size was in the study by Ghazanfarpour et al. [26], with 81 subjects in the intervention group and 80 in the control group. Key methodological and clinical characteristics of the included studies—including blinding, group design, intervention details, pain assessment instruments, study setting, and outcome definitions—are summarized in Table 2. In terms of location, all included studies were in Iran. The full text of four studies was in English [25, 26, 35, 36], and only one was in Persian [24]. Four studies considered periodic breast pain as a symptom of premenstrual syndrome in women, and only one study [25] regarded periodic breast pain to be due to fibrocystic changes in the breast. Most studies employed the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) to assess pain intensity; details on pain scale types, dose and duration of vitamin B6 intervention, and other study features are provided in Table 2.

Quality assessment (risk of bias)

The risk of bias for the included studies was assessed using different tools based on study design. For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) tool was used, while the ROBINS-I (Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies - of Interventions) tool was applied for the quasi-experimental study.

Quasi-experimental Study:

The risk of bias for the quasi-experimental study by Soltany et al. [36] was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool. This study was judged to be at serious overall risk of bias, primarily due to lack of control for confounding variables, moderate risk in participant selection and missing data, and moderate risk in outcome measurement. Other domains, including classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, and selection of reported results, were rated as low risk) Supplementary Table S2).

Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs):

Among the four RCTs, the risk of bias due to random sequence generation was low in all studies [24–26, 35]. The risk of randomization concealment was unclear in three studies [24, 26, 35], and low in only one study [25]. The risk of bias due to lack of blinding of participants and outcome assessors was judged to be low in all four studies [24–26, 35]. Attrition bias was low in all studies, while reporting bias was low in two studies [25, 35] and high in two studies [24, 26] (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S3).

Risk of bias summary: review authors’ judgements about each risk of bias item for included randomized controlled trials using the Cochrane tool

Primary outcome meta-analysis

The combined meta-analysis of three studies [24–26] indicated no statistically significant difference in reduction of mastalgia intensity between vitamin B6 and placebo (SMD = -3.57, 95% CI: -9.15 to 2.01). Although the magnitude of the effect size appears large, the wide confidence interval and its inclusion of zero confirm a lack of statistical significance. This result indicates that vitamin B6 did not have a significant effect in reducing breast pain compared to placebo. Furthermore, the combination of these studies demonstrated very high heterogeneity (I² = 99.57%; Tau² = 24.13; Chi² = 139.88; p = 0.000) (Fig. 3). The Golbriath plot (Fig. 4) suggests that the most substantial contribution to heterogeneity originated from the study by Ghazanfarpour et al. Due to the small number of included studies, subgroup analyses to explore sources of heterogeneity could not be performed.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method revealed that excluding the Ghazanfarpour et al. study substantially altered the pooled estimate (post-exclusion SMD: -0.71, 95% CI: -1.45 to 0.03) (Fig. 5). This modification reduced heterogeneity from I² = 99.57–78.95% (Tau² = 0.23; Chi² = 4.75; p = 0.03) and narrowed the confidence interval. However, the key finding remained unchanged: the confidence interval still included zero, indicating that vitamin B6 did not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in mastalgia compared to placebo (p > 0.05). The SMD of -0.71 represents a medium effect size according to Cohen’s conventions, but this should be interpreted cautiously given the persistent statistical non-significance (Fig. 6).

Publication bias assessment

The evaluation of publication bias using the Egger & Begg tests revealed that it is negligible and does not affect the obtained results. The Begg test yielded results of P = 0.796, Z=-2.09, and the Egger test yielded results of P = 0.389, t=-7.07. Due to the small number of studies, drawing a graph was impossible.

GRADE score: quality of the evidence

The quality of evidence pertaining to the primary outcomes of this study is summarized in Table 3. Three interventional studies, involving a total of 353 participants, evaluated the efficacy of vitamin B6 in reducing the severity of mastalgia. However, the sample size was deemed insufficient, and the confidence intervals (CIs) were notably wide, leading to a determination of serious imprecision. The risk of bias across the included studies was scored as high, primarily due to reporting bias identified in two of the studies. To assess inconsistency, heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, which was calculated to be 99.87%, indicating very serious inconsistency. Publication bias was evaluated using the Egger and Begg tests, which revealed negligible evidence of bias, thus it was considered not serious. Additionally, the population, intervention, comparator, and outcomes were deemed to align appropriately with the review question, resulting in an assessment of indirectness as not serious.

Discussion

The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis do not demonstrate a statistically significant effect of vitamin B6 on mastalgia intensity with low certainty of evidence. While the point estimate suggests a possible direction of effect, the extreme heterogeneity and confidence interval spanning zero preclude definitive conclusions about efficacy. Consequently, despite the promising effect size, the clinical implications remain uncertain. A major limitation of this meta-analysis is the extremely high heterogeneity among the included studies (I² = 99.57%), which significantly restricts the interpretability and reliability of the pooled results. This heterogeneity likely arises from several sources, including variations in study design, participant characteristics (such as age and baseline severity of mastalgia), differences in vitamin B6 dosage and treatment duration, and the use of diverse pain assessment scales. Unfortunately, due to the small number of included studies, subgroup or meta-regression analyses to formally explore these potential sources of heterogeneity were not feasible. Nevertheless, sensitivity analysis identified the study by Ghazanfarpour et al. [26] as a major contributor to the observed heterogeneity, suggesting that specific methodological differences or population characteristics in this study may have substantially influenced the overall findings. Given the extremely high heterogeneity, the appropriateness of conducting a meta-analysis under these conditions is questionable. Although meta-analytic methods provide a summary estimate, the considerable variability among studies limits the confidence in the pooled effect size. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution. The sensitivity analysis excluding the Ghazanfarpour study yielded a more moderate pooled effect size (SMD = -0.71) with a narrower confidence interval; however, this interval still included zero, indicating persistent statistical uncertainty. It is important to emphasize that this post hoc pooled estimate should not be considered the “true” effect, as selecting results after excluding the largest trial risks introducing selection bias. Instead, this instability reflects a weak and inconsistent evidence base. These findings underscore the need for larger, well-designed randomized controlled trials with standardized methodologies to provide more robust and reliable estimates of the efficacy of vitamin B6 for mastalgia.

The included trials exhibited notable clinical heterogeneity, with vitamin B6 doses ranging from 40 to 200 mg/day, different pain measurement scales (VAS 0–10, VAS 0–3, Cardiff scale), and treatment durations spanning 2 to 3 menstrual cycles. While the use of standardized mean differences (SMDs) allows pooling across different scales, it cannot fully address these fundamental clinical differences. Ideally, subgroup or meta-regression analyses could have explored the impact of these variables on the outcomes. However, the small number of studies (only three in the quantitative synthesis) precluded such analyses. This limitation further challenges the interpretation of the pooled effect and highlights the need for more homogeneous future studies or larger datasets enabling more detailed analyses.

In this context, Masoumi et al. (2016) demonstrated that vitamin B6 has a minimal impact on the physical symptoms associated with PMS while proving to be more effective in addressing psychological symptoms [38]. Additionally, a systematic review conducted in 2011 concluded that vitamin B6 does not play a significant role in the treatment of Mastalgia [39]. Conversely, Shobeiri et al. (2015) found that vitamin B6 contributed to a reduction in the severity of breast pain following treatment [35]. The analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties of this vitamin suggest that it may be effective in reducing Mastalgia [20, 21]. However, we were unable to establish a definitive connection between the two. Due to the limited number of studies in this area and the significant variability among those studies, our findings may evolve with the emergence of well-designed clinical trials involving larger populations. Considering the key role of oxidation and inflammation processes in the appearance and expansion of Mastalgia, it is believed that the use of vitamin supplements, especially of our studied vitamin, can successfully decrease the pain severity related to this phenomenon. However, it seems that the dosages of vitamins, duration of administration, as well as using them concurrently with other standard therapeutic regimens can all potentially influence the clinical results of these regimens, and thus, examining different regimens containing the vitamin B6 in different doses and durations should also be considered.

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the number of eligible studies included in the meta-analysis was very limited, with only three randomized controlled trials meeting the inclusion criteria. This small sample size reduces the statistical power of the analysis and limits the generalizability of the results. Second, all included studies were conducted in Iran, which may restrict the applicability of the findings to other populations and healthcare settings. Cultural differences in attitudes toward breast health and pain reporting, along with varying dietary patterns (e.g., caffeine, fat intake, and micronutrient levels), can influence mastalgia prevalence and perception. Additionally, disparities in healthcare access and diagnostic practices across countries may affect how mastalgia is reported and managed, making cross-cultural comparisons challenging. Future research should address these factors to better understand mastalgia’s global epidemiology and improve management strategies. Third, there was considerable heterogeneity among the included studies in terms of study design, sample size, intervention dosage, duration of treatment, and outcome measurement tools. The extreme statistical heterogeneity observed (I² = 99.57%) indicates that nearly all variability arises from true differences among studies rather than chance. While pooling data under such heterogeneity is not strictly prohibited, the resulting summary standardized mean difference (SMD = -3.57) is difficult to interpret clinically given the wide divergence in effects. Under these circumstances, a narrative synthesis that qualitatively summarizes the findings may better reflect the uncertainty and prevent potentially misleading conclusions based on pooled effect estimates. In addition, the certainty of the evidence for the observed effect was assessed as very low, reflecting significant limitations in the included studies. These included a high risk of bias, substantial heterogeneity in the results, and small sample sizes, which collectively reduced the reliability of the findings. Another key limitation is the narrow focus of this meta-analysis on vitamin B6 as a single intervention. Although vitamin B6 is one of the proposed treatments for mastalgia, various other therapeutic options—including NSAIDs, hormonal therapies, herbal medicines (such as Vitex agnus-castus and evening primrose oil), and other vitamin supplements—have also been studied. By restricting the analysis to vitamin B6, this study does not provide a comprehensive comparison of all available treatments. Future research should consider conducting broader systematic reviews or network meta-analyses that include all proposed interventions for mastalgia to better inform clinical decision-making.

Although we conducted publication bias assessment using Egger’s and Begg’s tests, which did not indicate significant bias, the small number of included studies limits the reliability of these tests. Therefore, the results regarding publication bias must be interpreted with caution. This limitation is important to consider, as the possibility of undetected publication bias cannot be entirely ruled out.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths worth noting: (a) we conducted a comprehensive search, (b) eligibility, data extraction, and risk of bias assessments were performed independently by two researchers, and (c) Endnote X9 and Stata software were used for study screening and analysis. To maintain consistency in the study results, we excluded women who had PMS or breast malignancy to avoid confounding effects related to mental health disorders associated with these conditions. Unfortunately, there was insufficient data in the included studies to perform subgroup analyses. A randomized clinical trial with a large sample size aiming to produce comparable and reliable results regarding the effect of vitamin B6 on mastalgia severity is recommended.

Clinical practice

Given the beneficial effect of vitamin B6 in reducing mastalgia severity, clinicians may consider it as a complementary option in the management of mild to moderate mastalgia, particularly for women seeking non-hormonal or low-risk interventions. Its accessibility, affordability, and relatively favorable safety profile make it an attractive choice in primary care and gynecological settings. However, due to the limited number of high-quality studies and the observed heterogeneity in outcomes, routine prescription of vitamin B6 for mastalgia should be approached with caution. Clinicians are advised to individualize treatment decisions, monitor patient response closely, and remain updated as further robust evidence emerges. Until then, vitamin B6 might best be positioned as part of a broader, patient-centered management strategy for mastalgia.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while the effect size suggests a potentially beneficial impact of vitamin B6 on mastalgia intensity, the wide confidence interval and high heterogeneity between studies indicate that these results should be interpreted with caution. The findings underscore the need for further high-quality, large-scale randomized controlled trials to confirm the effectiveness of vitamin B6 for mastalgia and to better understand the sources of heterogeneity. Additionally, future studies should focus on standardizing treatment protocols and participant characteristics to improve the consistency and reliability of findings.
