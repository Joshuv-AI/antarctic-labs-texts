Aberrant anterior insula-sensorimotor interaction modulates pain empathy in women with primary dysmenorrhea
Authors: Meiling Shang, Tongtong Fan, Yanran Chen, Huiping Liu, Jing Yang, Ke Wang, Hui Li, Wanghuan Dun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Pain empathy engages multiple neural networks. Neuroimaging research has identified anterior insula (aINS) and the sensorimotor network in processing pain empathy among women with primary dysmenorrhea (PDM). However, it remains unclear how the interaction between the two networks modulates pain empathy during menstrual pain.Methods: This case–control study included 56 women with PDM and 55 healthy controls (HCs). Participants were instructed to take functional MRI (fMRI) scans during the menstrual phase and view images depicting painful and non-painful situations to elicit empathy, as well as the short form of McGill Pain Questionnaire to elicit pain intensity. Functional connectivity (FC) analyses of the aINS-related and sensorimotor networks, and their interactions, were conducted to investigate their relationship with pain and pain empathy. Mediation analysis was performed to investigate the role of FC mediating menstrual pain and empathy.Results: PDM patients exhibited higher scores of pain empathy, which was significantly correlated with pain intensity. With comparable bilateral aINS-precentral/postcentral FC, left aINS–postcentral connectivity showed positive correlations with both pain intensity and empathy in PDM patients, whereas.the positive interaction between the left aINS- and precentral-centered networks was inversely associated with pain empathy in HCs. Furthermore, correlation and mediation analyses revealed that left aINS-right postcentral FC mediated the relationship between menstrual pain and pain empathy in PDM women.Conclusion: aINS–sensorimotor network interactions play a critical role in pain empathy, which may nonetheless engage in divergent regulatory processes under menstrual pain, shaping psychological adaptation and empathic responses.Keywords: Pain empathy, Primary dysmenorrhea, Functional connectivity, Anterior insula, Sensorimotor network

Introduction

Pain empathy is fundamental for adaptive social functioning, facilitating appropriate emotional responses, moral judgment, and prosocial behaviors toward others in pain. It's a multidimensional process supported by an interacting neural network with affective, sensorimotor, and cognitive components integrated (Jauniaux et al., 2019). Affective sharing of others' pain is primarily associated with regions such as the anterior insula and the anterior/mid-cingulate cortex(Jackson et al., 2005), whereas embodied and sensorimotor resonance involves the primary and secondary somatosensory cortices, premotor cortex, and supplementary motor area(Zhang et al., 2025). In addition, higher-order appraisal and mentalizing processes may engage regions including the inferior frontal gyrus and other prefrontal-temporal networks, depending on task demands and contextual factors(Zhang et al., 2025). Within this broader framework, the anterior insula(aINS) and sensorimotor networks are particularly relevant as they respectively bridge affective-interoceptive processing and embodied representation of observed pain (Lamm et al., 2007; Lamm et al., 2011; Riečanský and Lamm, 2019). However, despite the well-established roles of these networks, the interaction between the aINS and sensorimotor network has rarely been investigated. Additionally, since individuals' prior experience, such as chronic pain, can influence the capacity of empathy (Ma et al., 2020), it remains unclear whether the interaction between these two networks mediates the effect of pain experience on pain empathy.

Affective (refers to observing others' emotions and inducing various reactions such as affective sharing and empathic concern) and sensorimotor responses (refers to feeling other people's sensorimotor states vicariously) are two fundamental processes that contribute to pain empathy (Riečanský and Lamm, 2019), (Betti and Aglioti, 2016; Bonfils et al., 2016). These two processes work in close coordination when individuals perceive others' pain (Betti and Aglioti, 2016). Correspondingly, neurophysiological studies have reported that the aINS would be recruited to evaluate the emotional states of pain observed in others (Wylie and Tregellas, 2010). In parallel, the sensorimotor network, mainly comprising somatosensory (postcentral gyrus) and motor (precentral gyrus) regions and extending to the supplementary motor areas, is activated during sensorimotor processing and mapping specific sensory aspects of others' pain onto the observer's motor system (Avenanti et al., 2005; Grinsvall et al., 2021; Mayer et al., 2015). Given that affective and sensorimotor components jointly shape empathic responding, elucidating how the aINS interacts with the sensorimotor network is essential for advancing our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying pain empathy.

Studies on experimental pain have shown that pain exposure can make an impact on brain regions involved in different mechanisms to infer the pain of others (Preis et al., 2013). Furthermore, reproducible findings of altered gray matter and spontaneous functional activity have been demonstrated in chronic pain research, in which aINS and sensorimotor networks were reported to be related to pain empathy (Dun et al., 2017; Preis et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2017). However, previous studies on empathy have primarily focused on healthy individuals or experimental pain, with little explicit attention given to clinical pain syndrome. This gap might limit our understanding of empathy under conditions of real-world pain, which induce physiological, affective, and experiential changes that may influence how individuals perceive and respond to others' pain(Bajalan et al., 2019; Mu et al., 2024): On the one hand, repeated pain experiences may facilitate empathic understanding through shared pain representations. On the other hand, they may alter the relative contribution of affective-evaluative and sensorimotor processes that support pain empathy. These possibilities suggest that recurrent pain conditions may serve as valuable models for investigating how first-hand pain experiences shape empathic processing. Among such conditions, primary dysmenorrhea (PDM), one of the most common types of cyclic menstrual pain with well-defined pain period, provides a unique opportunity to examine how naturally recurring pain experiences influence both empathic behavior and its underlying neural architecture{Guimarães, 2020 #112}. Consistent with this perspective, accumulating evidence indicates that PDM induces both structural and functional alterations in the brain(Mu et al., 2024; Su et al., 2025) Therefore, PDM can be considered a more ecologically valid pain model to investigate pain empathy during a naturally recurring painful state.

The aim of our study was to investigate the interaction between aINS and the sensorimotor network underlying pain empathy in PDM patients, as well as to examine the role of menstrual pain in modulating pain empathy. 56 patients with PDM and 55 healthy controls (HCs) were recruited to collect resting-state functional images (fMRI) and clinical variables, including pain intensity during the painful menstruation phase. Pain ratings of visual stimuli were used to assess participants' pain empathy displaying painful and non-painful pictures.

Methods

Participants

In this study, 56 patients with PDM and 55 age-matched and right-handed females were recruited. All participants were recruited from local colleges by word-of-mouth or advisement. The potential participants were diagnosed by an experienced gynecologist. Inclusion criteria for eligible patients with PDM were as follows: 1) in line with the diagnostic criteria of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, 2) average pain intensity measured by a visual analog score(VAS) of ≥4 in the past 6 months, 3) regular menstrual cycle of 27–32 days, 4) aged 18–30 years, and 5) right-handedness(The development of handedness occurs at an early age and has a significant impact on brain structure and function), 6) pain medication free in the last 48 h. The criteria for the HC group were similar, except that the HCs had menstrual cycles without menstrual pain (VAS = 0). Exclusion criteria included pelvic organic disease, psychiatric disorder, comorbid chronic pain, history of childbirth, drug and alcohol history, and any MRI contraindications. In addition, none of the participants reported hormonal contraceptive use within the previous 6 months.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of the Medical College in Xi'an Jiaotong University and registered on Clinicaltrial.gov (NCT04168489). All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

State pain characteristics

All PDM participants in the study underwent the short form of the McGill Pain Questionnaire including sensory pain rating (PRI—S), affective pain rating (PRI-A), a visual analog scale (VAS), and the present pain intensity (PPI) index to assess state pain in women with PDM.(Melzack, 1987) The PRI-S contains 11 items and the PRI-A contains 4 items. Both of them were rated on pain intensity with a four-point scale (0 [no pain] to 3 [severe pain]). A total score is calculated with higher scores indicating a higher level of pain. The PPI scale recorded pain using six descriptors ranging from 0 (no pain) to 5 (excruciating pain).

Picture stimuli

To elicit pain empathy, a classical pain-empathy paradigm was presented with pictures depicting limbs in painful and neutral situations (Jackson et al., 2005). A set of 48 digital color pictures showing limbs in painful and non-painful situations were represented from a first-person perspective randomly across participants (Fig. 1). All situations in the painful and non-painful pictures showed familiar events that occasionally occurred in everyday life. All participants in our study participated in two sequential sessions, each of which consisted of four blocks (six trials in each block). For each trial, painful or non-painful pictures were displayed at the center of the screen (4000 ms) following a fixation cross presentation (1000 ms). After viewing each picture, participants rated how much pain they thought the depicted person was experiencing, on a scale from 0 (“no pain”) to 10 (“the worst pain imaginable”). Pain empathy was assessed by subtracting scores of no-painful pictures from scores of painful pictures. This subtraction-based measure was used to isolate pain-specific empathic responses while reducing the influence of individual differences in overall rating tendencies and non-specific emotional reactivity. Participants underwent the picture stimuli outside the scanner.

Stimulation paradigm of eliciting pain empathy.

MRI data acquisition

All patients with PDM and HCs underwent MRI with a 3.0 T Signa GE scanner (GE Healthcare) using an 8-channel head coil at the 1st or 2nd day of the menstrual phase, corresponding to the period when pain symptoms are typically at their peak(Guimarães and Póvoa, 2020). Participants first examined a whole-brain T1 image with a rapid spoiled gradient-recalled sequence. The parameters were as follows: voxel size = 1 mm3, TR = 1900 ms, TE = 2.6 ms, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256, flip angle = 12°, and slices = 140. Given that individual differences in pain empathy have been shown to exhibit trait-like characteristics(Singer et al., 2004), resting-state functional connectivity was used to investigate intrinsic neural network properties associated with pain empathy performance. Whole-brain resting state functional MRI was performed using an echo-planar imaging sequence. The parameters used were TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, matrix = 64 × 64, flip angle = 90°, slices = 35, and voxel size = 3.75 mm × 3.75 mm × 4 mm. A total of 180 functional volumes were acquired. The participants were instructed to relax and keep their eyes closed. MRI scanning and questionnaire assessments were conducted during the same visit, with MRI acquisition performed before questionnaire completion.

Data pre-processing

Resting-state data were pre-processed using the DPABI software package based on SPM12 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/). The first ten time points were removed (170 volumes remained). The slice timing was corrected for different acquired times. Realignment was performed to correct head motion which obtained six head motion parameters. The participants with excessive head motion above 1.5 mm or 3°of angular motion were removed. Subsequently, all the realigned images were normalized to the MNI template. Spatial smoothing was performed using a Gaussian kernel (FWHM = 6 mm). The linear trend and signals of the white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and global brain tissue were regressed out. Finally, a bandpass filter between 0.01 and 0.08 Hz was applied to reduce the effect of low-frequency drift and physiological noise.

Fast eigenvector centrality mapping(fECM) analysis

FMRI image data were further processed using the fECM tool (Wink et al., 2012). Eigenvector centrality mapping (ECM) is a whole brain network measure that reflects the importance of a network node by computing Pearson correlations between time series and estimating eigenvectors during each iteration. The significant regions identified by fECM analysis were exported as a region of interest (ROIs) for further functional connectivity (FC) analysis. A node with a larger EC value indicates greater connectedness with the other nodes. The fECM algorithm is a faster and computationally more efficient method for calculating eigenvector centrality than traditional ECM. It computes the matrix-vector product directly with eigenvector estimation without storing the connectivity matrix during each iteration. fECM has been successfully applied to psychiatric disorders, such as Alzheimer's disease (Qiu et al., 2016).

FC analysis

FC analysis was performed using a seed region approach. Six seed regions were included in the analysis: the bilateral anterior insula (aINS), bilateral precentral and postcentral gyri, all of which have been consistently implicated in pain empathy. To define these ROIs, a hybrid strategy was adopted. Specifically, bilateral aINS, bilateral postcentral gyrus, and left precentral gyrus were defined a priori using the Human Brainnetome Atlas (Fan et al., 2016), whereas the right precentral gyrus was identified as a significant cluster in the fECM analysis using a small volume correction. The mean time courses of each seed region were extracted to calculate the temporal correlation with the time series for all other voxels across the entire brain. Fisher's r-to-z transformation was then used to convert the individual-level correlation coefficient map into a z-FC map. When multiple FC networks were identified in the voxel-wise regression analyses, additional network-level analyses were performed to investigate their relationships. First, the mean FC values of each identified network were extracted for each participant, and Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to assess associations between network strengths. Second, connectivity between the identified networks was quantified for each participant to characterize inter-network interactions. The resulting inter-network FC values were then correlated with pain empathy score to evaluate the behavioral relevance of the network interactions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 22.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). For demographic, pain intensity and pain empathy data, the Shapiro - Wilk W test was applied to check the normality distribution of all variables. Unpaired two-sample t-test and Mann - Whitney U test was performed to detect differences between the groups. All data were presented as mean (SD). Results were considered significant when p value was less than 0.05 (p < 0.05). Pearson correlation was performed to detect the relationship between pain intensity and pain empathy in the PDM group and results were corrected with Bonferroni correction.

For FC data, a two-sample t-test was conducted to assess the FC differences between groups. Regression analysis was adopted separately in the ROIs to examine the relationship between pain empathy and ROIs connectivity in each group. To assess the coupling of pain empathy and aINS, together with the sensorimotor network, the significant FC strength between the ROIs and related regions derived from regression analysis were extracted to determine the Pearson correlation coefficient. To examine whether the relationship between FC and pain empathy differed between groups, a linear regression model including FC, group, and their interaction term (FC × group) was performed. The significance level was set as p < 0.05. To assess the impact of pain, we also conducted regression analysis on pain indices (PRI—S, PRI-A, VAS and PPI) and ROIs connectivity in PDM. Finally, mediation analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between state pain and empathy with 5000 bootstraps to test statistical significance in the PDM group. Given that their association with the ROIs connectivity, the VAS was considered as an independent variable, and the significant connectivity was considered as mediating variable and pain empathy was considered as dependent variable.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The demographic and clinical data of the participants during the menstrual phase are provided in Supplementary table 1. There was no significant difference between patients with PDM and HCs in age (p = 0.598), average days per menstrual cycle (p = 0.951), and age of menarche (p = 0.100). All the patients with PDM had a history of chronic cyclic pain (mean ± SD = 8.53 ± 3.51 years). The pain indices and ratings were listed at Supplementary table 2. All participants with PDM reported VAS scores ≥4, thereby meeting the inclusion criteria. Pain ratings for pain pictures and neutral pictures in the PDM group were significantly higher than those in the HCs. Importantly, the pain empathy index (painful minus non-painful ratings) was also significantly higher in the PDM group (all p < 0.001). In addition, scores of PRI—S, PRI-A and PPI are positively correlated with pain empathy in PDM with Bonferroni correction (Supplementary table 3).

Association between brain activity and pain empathy in HCs

As shown in Supplementary table 4 and Fig. 3A, the HC group presented a significantly negative correlation between pain empathy and FC strength of the left aINS to the right middle frontal cortex (MTG, p < 0.05, FWE corrected) / left orbital frontal cortex (Orb, p < 0.001, uncorrected) in whole-brain FC regression analyses. Besides, the FC strength of the right precentral gyrus to right aINS/right hippocampus/right precuneus were negatively correlated with pain empathy (Supplementary table 5, Fig. 3B, p < 0.001, uncorrected). We observed that the aINS-centered network was positively correlated with precentral-centered network (r = 0.512, p < 0.001, Fig. 3C). Furthermore, to determine whether the interaction between the two networks contributed to pain empathy, inter-network FC was calculated and correlated with pain empathy scores. The interaction between these two networks was negatively correlated with pain empathy (r = −0.486, p < 0.001, Fig. 3D).

Association between brain activity and pain intensity and pain empathy in PDM

In contrast to the negative association in HCs, the correlation between left aINS-right MTG/right postcentral gyrus/left inferior parietal lobule (IPL) FC and pain empathy was observed to be positive in PDM group (Supplementary Table 6, p < 0.05, FWE corrected, Fig. 4A). No significant associations were observed between pain empathy and other ROI-based FC measures. Besides, further exploratory correlation analyses showed that VAS scores were positively associated with FC between left aINS-left Orb/right postcentral gyrus/right precuneus (all p < 0.001, uncorrected; Fig. 4B).

Discussion

The activation of the left aINS and sensorimotor networks has been emphasized in pain empathy in recent neuroimaging studies, and these pain experiences may play an important role in pain ratings calculated from observing others' pain. In this study, we investigated the contributions of the left aINS and sensorimotor function to pain empathy in HCs and their alterations in women with PDM. Our results can be summarized by the following major findings: 1) women with PDM had higher scores of pain empathy than HC during the menstrual phase and scores of pain was positively correlated with pain empathy; 2) scores of pain empathy were negatively associated with both the aINS- and the right precentral-centered networks in HCs; 3) there was a positive interaction between the two networks, and the interaction was negatively coupled with pain empathy in HCs; 4) The FC between the left aINS and postcentral gyrus was positively associated with pain empathy and the menstrual pain in PDM group; 5) the mediation analysis revealed that the FC between the left aINS and postcentral gyrus was a mediator between state pain intensity and pain empathy in women with PDM. Therefore, the interaction between left aINS and the sensorimotor network may reflect a neural pattern in pain empathy. Moreover, the positive association among menstrual pain intensity, left aINS–postcentral gyrus FC, and pain empathy in PDM suggests a potential link between first-hand pain experience and the neural processes involved in perceiving others' pain.

Coupling of pain empathy and aINS with sensorimotor network

Neuroimaging studies have revealed a large number of brain structures involved in pain empathy, including the aINS, prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and sensorimotor cortex (Mayer et al., 2015). In agreement with this, correlation analysis between brain activity and pain empathy in our study demonstrated that the left aINS-Orb/MTG and the right precentral-right aINS/hippocampus/precuneus connectivity patterns were associated with empathic processing. The aINS are key neural structures of pain empathy which reflect the recruitment of systems for evaluating the emotional or affective value of an individual's direct or indirect pain experience (Zaki et al., 2007). In addition, the hippocampus and Orb have been reported as part of the limbic system, which controls emotional responses to ambient environmental stimuli (Protopopescu et al., 2005; Sokolowski and Corbin, 2012). Converging studies have indicated that the aINS has strong connections with the Orb and hippocampus to process and evaluate the emotional aspect of pain observed in others (Ferrari et al., 2017). The precentral gyrus, also known as the primary motor cortex, is a key structure that is responsible for executing voluntary movements and is part of the SMN encoding the sensorimotor side of pain empathy (Wiech et al., 2014). Thus, our replication highlights the robust recruitment of these brain areas in empathic responses and further implicates the involvement of affective and sensorimotor processing in pain empathy.

Previous research on the electro- and magneto-encephalography approaches has provided critical support for the interaction of different specialized brain regions underpinning pain empathy (Betti et al., 2009). In our results, the empathy-related left aINS seed region network was negatively associated with the empathy-related right precentral seed region network, indicating an interaction between the aINS and sensorimotor network. The aINS is implicated in the detection and selection of relevant salient stimuli from the external environment (Uddin, 2015; Uddin et al., 2014). Additionally, numerous studies have revealed that the aINS links interoception with emotion to generate a subjective consciousness of internal bodily states (Wylie and Tregellas, 2010). Notably, the involvement of aINS in this process is supported by the integrating effects of visceral information (Phillips et al., 2003). According to the mirror mechanism, the SMN in pain empathy tends to be linked to mapping detailed sensory representations of others' behavior and preparing motor reaction (Mayer et al., 2015). A significant number of neurophysiological studies have shown altered activities of the sensorimotor network during the direct observation of others' pain experiences (Loggia et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2017). Through the detection of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, inhibition in the sensorimotor circuit interrupts emotion recognition abilities and reduces empathic responsiveness (Dun et al., 2017). In particular, the aINS is hub region of the salience network, which sends various projections to motor systems, including the precentral gyrus, to contribute to the implementation of an appropriate motor response (Devinsky et al., 1995). In the state of salient stimuli, such as first-hand pain, the aINS integrates interoception divergences toward the cingulate cortex; aINS then facilitates access to the motor system to implement appropriate avoidance reactions (Betti and Aglioti, 2016). In the current study, we further found that the interaction between the left aINS and sensorimotor network was negatively correlated with pain empathy. Given the high salience of pain, we speculated that when observing the pain of others, aINS may contribute to the selection and integration of pain-related cues from a continuous stream of sensory stimuli and may be involved in transmitting them to the motor system to generate appropriate behavioral responses such as prosocial or avoidance behavior.

Association between menstrual pain and pain empathy

As addressed in previous studies, patients with chronic pain exhibit a negative emotional state and more empathic feelings than HCs, which are associated with altered brain structure and function (Bushnell et al., 2013). In the current study, women with PDM had higher pain ratings after witnessing the pain of others compared with HCs, which was in line with previous studies. Interestingly, women with PDM also assigned higher ratings to non-painful pictures. As these images depicted body-related situations that closely resembled the painful stimuli, the elevated ratings may indicate a lower threshold for attributing pain to observed events. Recurrent menstrual pain may be associated with increased attention to bodily threat and heightened expectations of pain even in objectively non-painful situations(Shang et al., 2024). Consequently, PDM participants may perceive ambiguous bodily scenes as more pain-related than HCs. Furthermore, the pain empathy score (painful minus non-painful scores) was significantly higher in the PDM group than in the HC group, indicating an enhanced differential response to others' pain beyond the overall elevation in picture ratings.

In addition, the FC between the left aINS and postcentral gyrus was positively associated with pain empathy. Substantial evidence indicates that the somatosensory area is related to the empathic response to pain through an emotional reaction that perceives the pain of others (Lamm et al., 2011). Increased activity in the somatosensory area was observed when the intensity of the painful stimulus appeared (Li et al., 2022). In addition to its crucial role in pain empathy, the postcentral gyrus is also a very important structure involved in the processing of somatosensory information and encoding of sensory-discriminative components in chronic pain (Schnitzler and Ploner, 2000). Chronic pain studies have provided evidence of alterations in the function and structure of the postcentral gyrus (Nan et al., 2020). In a cross-sectional study, patients with complex regional pain syndrome exhibited an abnormal relationship between the aINS and the postcentral gyrus, which is related to the sensory perception of pain (Kim et al., 2017). As aINS is involved in conveying information of sense pain to the higher systems related to top-down cognitive modulation of pain, the authors suggested that aberrant communication may reflect the dysfunctional regulation of pain in patients(Kim et al., 2017). In addition, there was a significant association between FC and the sense pain scores in the exploratory analyses. The higher reciprocal communication between the aINS and somatosensory area may be associated with dysfunctional regulation of menstrual pain. An interpretation of higher pain rating scores in response to painful stimuli is that the sensory input of menstrual pain is associated with altered FC between aINS and postcentral gyrus, which may be related to sense the pain of others. The mediation analysis in our study demonstrated that the relationship between the menstrual pain and pain empathy was mediated by functional connections of the left aINS and postcentral gyrus, which could further support this inference.

Afferent input may be associated with brain plasticity, which is a process that involves adaptive structural and functional changes to the brain. For example, our previous study reported abnormal function and structure in pain-related brain regions during the menstrual period, which may be related with transient neural changes in the brain (Dun et al., 2017; Han et al., 2019). Additionally, we investigated the effects of menstrual pain on empathy-related networks and whether empathy varied at different pain states during periovulatory, luteal, and menstruation phases(Dun et al., 2020). The results showed altered neural connections associated with empathy in the PDM group. Besides, the abnormal networks observed in PDM did not vary across the menstrual cycle, suggesting a relatively stable pattern across menstrual cycle phases. In this study, higher empathy scores and altered connectivity between the aINS and sensorimotor regions were observed in patients with PDM during the menstruation phase. This phenomenon may be associated with neural changes related to menstrual pain experience and may not be fully explained by menstrual cycle phase. Longitudinal studies and a within-person subject analysis during and outside the menstrual period are necessary to further investigate this maladaptive effect.

This study has some limitations that should be addressed. Firstly, the current sample size is relatively small. More participants are required to conduct further investigation. In addition, functional connectivity analysis cannot assess the direction of information flow between the connected regions, and several findings were identified at a more liberal statistical threshold and should therefore be considered exploratory, such as the association between FC and pain in PDM patients and the further mediation analysis. Future studies should address the direction in these regions using other model-based approaches.
