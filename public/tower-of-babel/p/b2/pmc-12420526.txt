The Return of MenW: Religious Mass Gatherings as Global Catalysts for Meningococcal Spread
Authors: Jaffar A. Al-Tawfiq, Ziad A. Memish
Published: 2025

Between January 2024 and June 2025, England has reported a concerning rise in invasive meningococcal disease (IMD) caused by Neisseria meningitidis serogroup W, particularly the ‘strain A’ of the MenW: cc11 Hajj sublineage—closely associated with pilgrimage travel to Saudi Arabia [1]. Several of these cases were linked to Umrah pilgrims and their close contacts, underscoring the persistent public health risks posed by mass gatherings such as Hajj and Umrah [1]. Reinforcing MenACWY vaccination messaging year-round—especially for intending pilgrims—remains a critical strategy to mitigate this emerging threat. A recent systematic review of 21 studies involving 23,552 participants (nine included in meta-analysis) found that meningococcal carriage patterns shift during the Hajj period [2]. Carriage of serogroup A remained extremely low both before (0.003; 95% CI: −0.001 to 0.006) and after Hajj (0.001; 95% CI: 0.000 to 0.002). In contrast, serogroup W carriage increased slightly from 0.068 (95% CI: −0.014 to 0.15) pre-Hajj to 0.072 (95% CI: 0.037 to 0.108) post-Hajj. Overall N. meningitidis carriage rose from 1.6% before Hajj to 3.4% afterward [2].

Recent data from England show that 32 of 59 MenW IMD cases were associated with travel-related strains, nearly half of which were directly linked to returnees from Saudi Arabia [1]. The dominant strain, belonging to the highly virulent MenW: cc11 clonal complex, is well recognized for triggering outbreaks in settings characterized by large crowds and close contact, such as the annual Hajj pilgrimage [3–5]. This clear epidemiological connection underscores both the global mobility of the strain and the critical need for sustained surveillance and preventive measures targeting pilgrims and their households. Core genome Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST) analysis of Hajj sublineage genomes from 30 IMD cases across seven countries revealed five phylogenetic clusters comprising two distinct strains. Travel histories included Saudi Arabia, other Middle Eastern countries, India, Mauritius via Turkey, and some cases without documented travel. The dominant strain, encompassing four clusters, showed minimal representation from Africa and limited presence in the Middle East, with evidence suggesting Eastern Europe as a possible origin [6]. An Italian study further reported genomic analysis of N. meningitidis isolates from four invasive meningococcal disease cases linked to travel in Saudi Arabia in 2025. All belonged to serogroup W, sequence type 11 (ST-11), clonal complex 11 (CC11). Despite lacking epidemiological links, the isolates were uniformly susceptible to ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, penicillin G, and rifampicin, and phylogenetic analysis confirmed their placement within the Hajj strain sublineage [7].

A previously healthy two-year-old girl developed rapidly fatal meningococcal disease due to serogroup W135 shortly after her grandparents returned from Umrah, underscoring the importance of considering recent travel or contact history in early diagnosis and prompt antibiotic treatment. Despite mandatory vaccination policies for pilgrims, transmission of non-traditional serogroups such as W135 remains a significant risk, reinforcing the need for vigilant surveillance and preventive measures among families with pilgrimage exposures [8].

The surge in W: cc11 IMD cases linked to Umrah and travel in early 2024 was driven by multiple strains and sublineages [6]. Although some cases were associated with non-Saudi destinations, their peak during Ramadan—and subsequent sharp decline during Hajj, when vaccination coverage is highest—points to Umrah as a key contributor. These patterns highlight the critical need to reinforce mandatory vaccination policies and sustain vigilant global surveillance.

The current situation illustrates the ongoing challenges that global mass gatherings pose for infectious disease control, where importation of virulent strains can rapidly alter epidemiology in home countries [9]. It underscores the critical importance of pre-travel vaccination, particularly with the quadrivalent meningococcal conjugate vaccine (MenACWY), which protects against serogroups A, C, W, and Y. Vaccination should be strictly enforced and actively promoted among all prospective pilgrims to Saudi Arabia—not only during peak Hajj but throughout the year given the continuous flow of Umrah visitors [10].

To address this threat, public health authorities must intensify awareness campaigns directed at both pilgrims and healthcare providers. These efforts should emphasize the necessity of receiving the MenACWY vaccine, proven effective against MenW and required by Saudi regulations for entry to Hajj and Umrah. Despite this mandate, evidence shows that some travelers remain unvaccinated, increasing the risk of transmission and outbreaks upon return. For example, a recent survey of 5,352 pilgrims during the 2024 season found that the most commonly received vaccines were COVID-19 (79.9%) and MenACWY (79.0%) [11].

The Saudi Ministry of Health mandates that all pilgrims—regardless of their country’s disease incidence—receive the quadrivalent meningococcal (ACYW135) vaccine before entering Saudi Arabia for Hajj or Umrah. This requirement applies equally to international and domestic pilgrims, as well as to general visitors and business travelers. The vaccine must be administered at least 10 days prior to arrival, and all travelers aged two years and older must present an official vaccination certificate upon entry [12]. This universal policy reflects Saudi Arabia’s proactive approach to safeguarding public health during large-scale religious gatherings, aiming to prevent IMD outbreaks irrespective of regional epidemiology.

As COVID-19 restrictions have eased, the number of Hajj pilgrims has surged (Fig. 1), renewing concerns about the spread of communicable diseases such as meningococcal disease [13, 14]. The intense overcrowding and close contact inherent to Hajj create ideal conditions for respiratory transmission, and past outbreaks of invasive meningococcal disease—particularly serogroup W—have spread internationally through returning pilgrims [13, 15]. The recent rise in pilgrim numbers highlights the persistent challenge of ensuring full vaccination compliance, with incomplete coverage continuing to pose a risk for ongoing transmission [11].

Table 1 highlights the persistent challenges facing the mandatory quadrivalent ACWY meningococcal vaccination program and proposes practical solutions. Although the program has successfully prevented large outbreaks since 2002, gaps remain in coverage among domestic pilgrims and healthcare workers, risks of vaccine fraud and waning immunity, and loopholes created by e-visas issued without proof of vaccination [16]. Addressing these shortcomings requires stricter enforcement of existing policies, rigorous vaccine quality assurance, robust pre-travel verification mechanisms, and stronger global coordination through stockpiling and surveillance. In the longer term, expanding immunization to include MenB—and eventually pentavalent conjugate vaccines—would provide broader protection and ensure safer Hajj and Umrah pilgrimages. Currently, the Saudi Public Health Authority recommends MenB vaccination for high-risk groups (Table 2).

Enhancing surveillance and diagnostic capacity for IMD is equally critical. Rapid case detection and containment, supported by sequencing technologies to monitor outbreak strains, are essential to prevent wider community spread. In response to emerging ciprofloxacin resistance, UK health agencies have recently updated their recommendations to strengthen monitoring and to consider alternative antibiotic prophylaxis for close contacts of suspected cases.

This emerging cluster is a stark reminder that global health security and travel medicine must adapt dynamically to evolving infectious disease threats amplified by international travel. Sustained vigilance, vaccination, and cross-border cooperation remain paramount in preventing invasive meningococcal disease and protecting vulnerable populations worldwide.

Table 3 outlines key public health measures for preventing and controlling meningococcal disease linked to Hajj travel. These include heightened vigilance by healthcare providers when evaluating both outbound and returning pilgrims, reinforced vaccination promotion, and strict infection control in health facilities. Strengthening surveillance is vital for early detection of N. meningitidis cases, alongside continued research on pathogen emergence, transmission dynamics, and travel-related risks. The plan further emphasizes international collaboration between health authorities, active community engagement through awareness campaigns, and clear communication of Saudi Ministry of Health guidelines. Finally, providing pilgrims with accurate, up-to-date health risk information empowers them to take personal preventive measures, collectively reducing the risk of outbreaks.In conclusion, effective meningococcal disease prevention during Hajj and Umrah requires ongoing vigilance, robust vaccination, and education. Strengthening surveillance enables early detection and response, while international collaboration and community engagement improve compliance with health and vaccination guidelines. Providing pilgrims with timely, accurate health information empowers personal prevention, collectively reducing outbreak risks and safeguarding public health.
