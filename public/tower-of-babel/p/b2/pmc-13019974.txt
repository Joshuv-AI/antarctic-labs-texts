Hem-o-Lok clip migration causing ureteral stone formation after ureteroplasty for retrocaval ureter: A case report
Authors: Mingchi Wang, Zhenquan Lu, Kenan Xuan, Feihong Xu, Xishuo Zheng, Xiang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Hem-o-Lok clip, a permanent polymer ligation device, is rarely associated with long-term complications like migration and tissue perforation. A 57-year-old male presented with right flank pain and ureteral obstruction. Imaging revealed a stone and severe hydronephrosis. Ureteroscopy identified an embedded Hem-o-Lok clip as the stone nidus. After failed endoscopic retrieval, robotic-assisted laparoscopic segmental ureterectomy with end-to-end anastomosis was successfully performed to remove the clip and reconstruct the ureter. This case highlights that migrated Hem-o-Lok clips can serve as a lithogenic nidus, causing ureteral obstruction. Standardized clipping technique and long-term follow-up are essential to mitigate such risks.

Introduction

Urinary stone disease is a prevalent global condition influenced by metabolic abnormalities, anatomical factors, infections, and lifestyle.1 Iatrogenic foreign bodies are a non-negligible cause of secondary stone formation.2 The Hem-o-Lok clip has become a standard tool for vascular and tissue ligation in laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgeries. However, its non-absorbable nature carries the risk of long-term complications as a permanent foreign body.3 This report details a case involving a Hem-o-Lok clip that migrated into the ipsilateral ureter 12 years after retrocaval ureter repair, forming a lithogenic nidus and resulting in severe hydronephrosis. This case aims to serve as a reference for the early recognition and treatment of similar complications.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old male was admitted on December 8, 2025, presenting with right flank distension and pain for over three months, accompanied by gross hematuria, urinary frequency, and urgency. Prior magnetic resonance urography (MRU) at another hospital indicated right proximal ureteral stenosis with severe right hydronephrosis. He underwent a laparoscopic right ureteroplasty for the right retrocaval ureter 12 years ago. Physical examination was unremarkable. Complete blood count, liver and renal function, and electrolytes were within normal limits. Computed tomography urography (CTU) findings (Fig. 1): A right proximal ureteral stone, approximately 7 mm in diameter, with associated dilatation of the proximal ureter and pelvicalyceal system. Preoperative diagnoses were: Right proximal ureteral stone with right hydronephrosis; Status post right retrocaval ureteroplasty. After surgical contraindications were excluded, the patient underwent flexible ureteroscopic lithotripsy for right ureteral stone on December 9, 2025. Intraoperative ureteroscopy revealed a tortuous and stenotic ureter, with an immobile, ovoid stone visualized in the proximal segment. Holmium laser lithotripsy was initiated to fragment the stone, revealing a Hem-o-Lok clip as its core, with surrounding pale ureteral mucosa (Fig. 2). Multiple attempts to retrieve the clip with a stone basket were unsuccessful, suggesting deep embedding within the ureteral wall. To avoid residual Hem-o-Lok clip and ureteral stenosis caused by holmium laser thermal injury, and given the significant risk of ureteral avulsion or full-thickness perforation with forceful extraction, the procedure was prudently terminated after laser lithotripsy of the surrounding stone material and placement of a double-J stent to relieve obstruction. The patient recovered well postoperatively and expressed a desire for definitive management to remove the clip and reconstruct the ureter. Following a standard preoperative risk assessment, the surgical team performed a robot-assisted laparoscopic right ureteral stricture segment resection with end-to-end anastomosis on December 24, 2025. The procedure successfully excised the stenotic segment and completely removed one Hem-o-Lok clip (Fig. 3). The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery and was discharged accordingly. The patient achieved marked alleviation of postoperative lumbago. Postoperative urinary computed tomography (CT) demonstrated alleviated hydronephrosis and optimal positioning of the double-J stent, with serial serum creatinine levels showing a gradual downward trend, indicating successful surgical intervention and restoration of renal function. The patient remained asymptomatic at the 2-month follow-up. Discussion

The evolution of minimally invasive surgery has been paralleled by significant advancements in surgical instrumentation. Since the late 1990s, the Hem-o-Lok clip, a non-metallic, non-absorbable ligation device, has gained widespread adoption in laparoscopic surgery due to its broad ligation range, uniform closure force, ease of application for vascular and tissue closure, excellent tissue compatibility, and minimal artifact on subsequent imaging studies.3 Although studies suggest suture ligation may offer higher safety, it entails longer operative times and greater technical difficulty, cementing the Hem-o-Lok clip's role as an indispensable tool in minimally invasive surgery.4

Over time and with tissue healing, the clip's non-absorbable nature poses a risk of late migration, potentially leading to serious complications like tissue injury and perforation. Such complications are more frequently reported in hepatobiliary surgery, often manifesting as clip migration into the common bile duct post-cholecystectomy, forming a nidus for stones.5 Similar cases exist in urology, predominantly involving the lower urinary tract. An early report in 2008 by Banks et al. described Hem-o-Lok clip migration into the bladder with stone formation after laparoscopic radical prostatectomy.6 A larger study of 750 cases reported an incidence of intravesical clip migration of approximately 0.8% following robot-assisted radical prostatectomy.7 Migration within the upper urinary tract is rarer. A 2022 systematic review by Zhou H et al., encompassing 13 reported cases of upper tract Hem-o-Lok clip migration, noted that most occurred after robotic or laparoscopic partial nephrectomy, with fewer cases following pyeloplasty or pyelolithotomy.8 The detailed case report following retrocaval ureteroplasty described in this article has significant clinical value. The mechanism of Hem-o-Lok clip migration remains incompletely understood. Based on this case and existing literature, key contributing factors may include: (1) The Quantity Hypothesis. Some studies suggest a correlation between an excessive number of Hem-o-Lok clips used during laparoscopic cholecystectomy and clip migration, with the use of more than four clips in close proximity potentially increasing the migration rate.9,10 (2) The Inflammation Hypothesis. Research indicates that acute suppurative inflammation can significantly increase local tissue friability and cause severe edema.11 Under such pathological conditions, the persistent mechanical stress from the clip may initiate a pathological process akin to a "cheese-cutting effect," whereby the clip slowly cuts through the compromised tissue into adjacent lower-pressure anatomical spaces or lumens.12 (3) The Peristalsis Hypothesis. Both the biliary tract and ureter exhibit physiological peristalsis. If a clip is applied too close to the duct wall, long-term compression and friction can lead to wall injury, even pressure necrosis, creating a pathway for migration.13 (4) The Foreign Body Reaction Hypothesis. Kadekawa K et al. proposed that although made of an inert polymer, Hem-o-Lok clips may trigger a non-specific foreign body inflammatory reaction in some individuals. This chronic inflammatory response, involving tissue rejection and phagocytosis, can compromise local tissue integrity, providing a pathological basis for the clip to "erode" into adjacent low-pressure lumens.14 (5) The Local Pressure/Weak Tissue Hypothesis. In areas of tissue weakness or defect, such as an anastomosis, the clip may embed into the tissue and eventually migrate.8 This is exemplified by migration into the bladder via the vesicourethral anastomosis after laparoscopic radical prostatectomy. Incomplete urethral wall integrity at the anastomosis, combined with high local pressure and sometimes supplemental clip fixation after suturing, may facilitate embedding. Consequently, some researchers question the use of Hem-o-Lok clips at the vesicourethral anastomosis.15

While flexible ureteroscopic lithotripsy is a first-line, minimally invasive treatment for ureteral stones, the deeply embedded clip, combined with ureteral stricture in this case, posed a high risk of ureteral avulsion or perforation with forceful basket retrieval. Therefore, it was employed as the first-stage treatment, with the primary goals of rapidly relieving urinary obstruction, preserving renal function, and, crucially, confirming the embedded Hem-o-Lok clip as the stone nidus to inform the definitive second-stage surgical plan. The second-stage robot-assisted laparoscopic right ureteral stricture segment resection with end-to-end anastomosis not only definitively removed the deeply embedded clip but also excised the stenotic segment, addressing the etiology at its source. The advantages of robot-assisted surgery in complex upper urinary tract reconstruction are increasingly recognized. Xu MY et al., in their study on robot-assisted ureteral stricture repair, highlighted that the system's 3D high-definition visualization and flexible, precise instrumentation significantly enhance the accuracy of stricture resection and anastomosis, particularly in complex cases involving local fibrosis or anatomical variations.16 In our second-stage procedure, leveraging the technical advantages of the robotic system enabled precise resection and high-quality anastomosis, further validating the indispensable clinical value of robot-assisted technology in managing complex upper tract foreign body impaction and reconstructive surgery. Regarding clinical prevention, we have summarized some clinical experiences. First, during Hem-o-Lok clip application, avoid placing clips in immediate contact with the walls of hollow viscera like the ureter. Second, exercise caution and evaluate the necessity of clip use critically in areas of tissue weakness, such as anastomotic sites, prioritizing suture ligation when appropriate. Third, for patients with a history of Hem-o-Lok clip-assisted laparoscopic surgery who present with unexplained flank/abdominal pain or urinary obstruction, a high index of suspicion for clip migration is warranted. Prompt imaging studies like CTU should be performed for etiological diagnosis.

Conclusion

This case report confirms that non-absorbable polymer clips (Hem-o-Lok), despite their widespread use in laparoscopic surgery, carry a long-term risk of migration and acting as a foreign body nidus for ureteral calculi. This complication is characterized by a potentially prolonged latency period (exceeding a decade) and subtle clinical presentation. Therefore, clinicians must be fully aware of the long-term risks associated with non-absorbable biomaterials and exercise heightened vigilance when using such devices near hollow organs like the ureter. Instrument migration should be included in the differential diagnosis for patients with a relevant laparoscopic surgery history who develop delayed symptoms of urinary tract obstruction. For such complex cases, endoscopic monotherapy carries high risks and limited efficacy. The approach employed in this case, utilizing robot-assisted laparoscopic surgery, provided a safe and definitive treatment by enabling complete foreign body retrieval and precise ureteral reconstruction. In summary, this case offers practical guidance for the standardized use of non-absorbable ligation devices, optimization of long-term postoperative follow-up strategies, and provides valuable clinical reference for the early recognition and staged management of similar long-term complications.
