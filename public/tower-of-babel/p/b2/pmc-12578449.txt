Despite the introduction of many innovative approaches to the prevention and management of substance use disorder (SUD) by clinicians, peers, public health practitioners, policy-makers and scientists, opioid and substance-related deaths remain high across Canada. Governments in British Columbia (BC) and Alberta,1 the Canadian provinces with the highest rates of overdose deaths, are increasingly focusing on inpatient residential, or bed-based models of care.2 Whereas Ontario has a relatively well-established network of bed-based care facilities, 3 BC is contemplating expanding its capacity4 and Alberta has pledged to build 11 bed-based care facilities to address the overdose crisis.5

Interventions such as outpatient opioid agonist treatment (OAT) for opioid use disorders (OUD) have been extensively evaluated in terms of their reach,6,7 effectiveness,8,9 adoption among eligible prescribers, fidelity in implementation,10 and maintenance.11 Bed-based care models have not been studied nearly as extensively. In BC, despite widespread and longstanding availability of bed-based care, use and outcomes are sparsely reported because many facilities are private, operated by for-profit organizations with no mandate to share data or results. As bed-based care facilities are typically multifaceted and resource intensive, with inherent limits on capacity, ensuring they are a good return on investment is critical. That means proper evaluation of their impact on outcomes of importance is critical. We propose using a flexible framework, Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM), to evaluate bed-based care.12 We discuss how this framework incorporates the effectiveness of services delivered, as well as the extent to which they meet the needs of eligible clients, how they adapt, and whether they are sustained over the long term.

What is bed-based care for people with SUD?

Bed-based care facilities are residential centres for the treatment of SUD. They may be recovery oriented — that is, focused on clients achieving abstinence — or focus more on reducing harmful drug use.13–17 They may have a mono- or multidisciplinary focus, specialize in 1 form of SUD, admit patients voluntarily or involuntarily, and provide pharmacologic or psychological treatments or both. Regardless of the underlying philosophy and orientation, currently most bed-based care facilities for SUD feature many of the same core elements and have common challenges in their long-term sustainability, such as wait-lists owing to insufficient bed availability, high costs, a lack of specialized health workforce, and limited care coordination for transitions to the community.12 Because of differences in approach, measuring the effectiveness of attendance at a bed-based care facility is difficult.

How can the RE-AIM framework help inform and evaluate bed-based care?

The RE-AIM framework is one of the most commonly used approaches to planning and evaluation of public health interventions. 18 An overview is provided in Table 1. The framework was conceived more than 20 years ago to support the translation of scientific evidence into practice and policy19 and is designed to evaluate complex questions, such as “What intervention components are effective, with which implementation strategies, for whom, in what settings, how and why, and for how long?”20 Recent publications have highlighted a range of applied studies,21 discussed the framework’s evolution and misconceptions to date,22 and have extended it to better capture implementation context, via the Pragmatic, Robust Implementation Sustainability Model (PRISM).23 Bed-based care facilities typically offer multifaceted interventions that are resource intensive, with inherent limits on capacity. The RE-AIM framework is flexible enough to incorporate evaluation of the effectiveness of component services, as well as the extent to which they meet the needs of the eligible client base and how facilities expand, adapt, and are sustained over the long term.

Reach

Defining the target population is an important first step in the planning process of any program evaluation, which the RE-AIM framework defines as “reach.” If available resources are insufficient to reach those who need the intervention, inequities in health care access can be created or further exacerbated. To this end, a recent environmental scan from Ontario found that among 96 residential treatment facilities, admission wait times ranged from 28 to 121 days.3 British Columbia has reported 3778 available beds for an estimated population of 250 000 people with SUD (excluding alcohol and cannabis), of whom 5328 received inpatient treatment in 2023 and 2024.1 These beds, however, are not necessarily dedicated for people with SUD and include beds for alcohol detoxification. The needs of people with SUD are diverse and many clients require specialized, multidisciplinary care for comorbidities.24 For instance, although there were more than 2800 new births among people with SUD in BC from 2016 to 2021, the province has only 13 dedicated hospital beds for people with perinatal substance use, 12 of which are located in Vancouver, even though the incidence is 7 times higher in Northern BC.25 Furthermore, a recent study estimated that more than 33 000 people with OUD in BC have concurrent bipolar or schizophrenia spectrum disorder; 26 yet, the 105-bed Red Fish Healing Centre for Mental Health and Addiction facility is currently the only facility in BC designed for this population. If residential care facilities are to be a prominent component of provincial care for people with SUD, it is important to determine how many facilities are needed and their optimal situation to best meet actual need. This can be estimated using routinely collected health care data, and would allow planners to budget for the appropriate number of staff and facilities.

Effectiveness

To assess effectiveness, we drew primarily on the results of 2 literature reviews from Canada’s Drug Agency (CDA; formerly the Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health),27,28 and 2 recent large-scale studies on the long-term outcomes of people with OUD receiving outpatient versus residential care, both published in 2020. In the first review that evaluated the impact of bed-based care,27 only 1 of the 6 included studies, a systematic review29 that identified 3 individual studies, reported outcomes specific to people with OUD.30–32 All other studies in the CDA review grouped effects across SUDs or were specific to other substances (e.g., alcohol or stimulants). Although the researchers acknowledged heterogeneity in the outcomes assessed and time horizons in which the services were evaluated, outcomes (e.g., abstinence, return to inpatient care, drug use) were generally found to be positive.27 The second review28 included a 2019 systematic review comprising 23 studies,33 which had examined only 8 reported outcomes representative of the Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction’s definition of recovery (these include lifestyle and behavioural changes addressing biological, psychological, social, and spiritual aspects; abstinence; or stopping uncontrolled substance use to ultimately improve quality of life, health, and function).34

Existing studies have done little to disentangle the heterogeneity in the patient population and the form of care provided. For instance, the systematic reviews did not distinguish results by the type of substance used. Notably, none of the studies included people who used unregulated fentanyl, nor did studies elaborate on the “key ingredients” that made these programs successful for people with OUD. Information on the use of medications and psychosocial care within bed-based care facilities are particularly relevant, and this information was largely absent.

Some evidence is contradictory. A 2020 retrospective cohort study including more than 30 000 people who had undergone medically managed opioid withdrawal in Massachusetts found that receiving OAT within residential care resulted in a lower risk of death than outpatient OAT, although each modality was associated with substantially reduced mortality compared with no treatment.35 In contrast, in a 2020 nationally sampled study of 37 090 propensity score–matched individuals with OUD receiving outpatient OAT or inpatient care, all forms of inpatient care (short- or long-term) were associated with higher risk of subsequent overdose and hospital admission.36 These observational studies may have been limited by unmeasured confounding by indication; people choosing (or being offered or assigned) bed-based care may have been systematically different from those receiving outpatient care.

Given that experimental comparisons would likely be deemed unethical and infeasible, nonexperimental comparisons must be used to evaluate the potential effect of services among individuals eligible for but not accessing bed-based care facilities. Advances in comparative effectiveness research, notably target trial emulation,37,38 are valuable tools in refining understanding of effectiveness of bed-based models of care, and should be used moving forward. Routinely collected health care data in administrative databases can also provide important information about drug-related hospital admission, overdose, mortality, and reentry into treatment over a number of years, with little loss to follow-up if clients remain in the province. However, assessing people’s physical, mental, social, and spiritual well-being will require detailed prospective data collection on a subset of people using residential facilities. A combination of self-reported measures and routinely collected data will provide the best assessment of the effectiveness of bed-based care facilities.

Adoption and implementation

Monitoring adoption of best practices and fidelity of interventions’ implementation in bed-based care entails detailed evidence-based protocols for care delivery in these complex facilities. As bed-based care should include a range of pharmacologic and psychosocial treatments and other social supports, identifying the “key ingredients” that improve the physical, social, mental, and spiritual health outcomes of individuals is necessary. To this end, detailed process evaluations to facilitate continuous quality improvement,39 and in many cases establish the evidence base for best practices, will be vital.

Given what has been observed over the past 5 years, continued changes in the composition of the unregulated drug supply are likely and should be anticipated, which should prompt further refinements in clinical practices. For instance, nearly all evidence available for OUD treatment was generated before fentanyl was introduced into the unregulated drug supply. The introduction of unregulated benzodiazepines has further complicated treatment. For people who have developed iatrogenic dependence on benzodiazepines, a gradual long-term taper is generally recommended.40 However, as neither the prescriber nor the person presenting for treatment may know the extent to which benzodiazepines were present in drugs used, the extent of benzodiazepine dependence and the appropriate length of a taper are often unclear. Finally, anoxic and hypoxic brain injury, occurring as a result of drug overdose, is a seldom discussed issue in the treatment of people with SUD, yet the effects of brain injuries from drug overdose may be irreversible and thus require explicit consideration. 41 Rehabilitation within long-term bed-based care may be required, although best practices are not well established.42

As best practices evolve, monitoring both adoption of interventions and ongoing fidelity of their implementation within and across facilities will be necessary to ensure effective care. The framework for reporting adaptations and modifications to evidence-based interventions43 may be useful in systematically evaluating adaptations over time.

Maintenance

Institutional

Maintenance at the institutional level refers to the extent to which an intervention, or set of programs, becomes institutionalized as part of routine practice. Substantial upfront investments are required to build specialized facilities to treat people with SUD, but a greater challenge may be meeting and sustaining the human resources needed to maintain a standard, accessible, evidence-informed form of practice over the long term. For instance, BC’s newly established Red Fish Healing Centre for Mental Health and Addiction was recently estimated to employ 10 of the 20 addictions psychiatrists currently working in the province.44 Expansion of the model of care adopted by this facility therefore requires planning and foresight to ensure the required workforce is available, which may include optimizing the scope of practice for nonspecialist physicians and nurses.

Individual

Maintenance at the individual level refers to the long-term effects of a program on outcomes after discharge from residential care. This is particularly important because SUD is a chronic, recurring condition45 and the notion of “recovery” entails ongoing monitoring. Short-term withdrawal management (detoxification) for OUD, in the absence of ongoing pharmacologic treatment, is ineffective in achieving abstinence46 and should serve only as a means of stabilization and linkage to care in the community.47 Moreover, discharging clients of bed-based care facilities back into suboptimal living arrangements comes with a high risk of readmission, relapse, or death.48 In many cases, clients require discharge into housing facilities with varying levels of supports to prevent relapse. This often poses logistical challenges, given that regional health authorities are tasked with identifying housing placement after discharge,49 but their funding and administration are often outside their direct mandate. Provincial ministries of housing and child and family development must therefore be included to establish and maintain the continuum of care needed.

Conclusion

Bed-based models of care for people with SUD offer multifaceted, complex interventions that are resource intensive. Effectiveness is therefore difficult to evaluate. Using the RE-AIM framework helps to highlight suboptimal evidence of effectiveness of bed-based care and likely limited capacity relative to the size of the populations with SUD. Evidence within the domains of adoption, implementation, and maintenance are largely absent. We have shown how the RE-AIM model can allow providers and policy-makers to be explicit about what is known, and what information needs to be collected, to understand whether bed-based care for SUD can be sufficient for the needs of a target population, its efficacy in improving health outcomes at the time of discharge and over the long term, the implementation and adoption of clinical protocols that are both effective and adaptive to client needs, and mechanisms to ensure the sustainability of this complex and multidisciplinary form of care. Most provinces can at least partially evaluate such services through routinely collected and linkable health administrative data. Such data should be augmented by, and triangulated with, electronic health records and patient reported measures of experience and outcomes as well as facility-level data on clinical processes, with all evaluations publicly reported, even if facilities are privately run.
