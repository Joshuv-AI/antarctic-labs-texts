The prevalence and incidence of sleep disorder symptoms, correlates, and sex-stratified longitudinal associations with mental and menstrual health outcomes in UK Service Personnel: a prospective survey-based cohort study protocol
Authors: Alex J Rawcliffe, Charlotte Coombs, Andrea Docherty, Thomas O’Leary, Julie Greeves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesSleep disturbance is highly prevalent in military populations, associated with adverse mental health, physiological, and operational outcomes. However, the epidemiology and longitudinal course of sleep disturbances among UK Armed Forces personnel remain unknown. Emerging evidence suggests that sleep disturbance may act as an upstream vulnerability factor for mental health disorders, with additional links to broader outcomes such as menstrual dysfunction in women. Prospective data in UK military populations are scant. This study aims to quantify the prevalence, incidence, and longitudinal associations between sleep disorder symptoms, mental health, and menstrual health outcomes in UK service personnel. The study aims to further investigate demographic and service-related moderators and explore potential mediating pathways underlying these relationships.MethodsThis prospective, survey-based cohort study will aim to recruit ~n 1316 Regular and n 1387 Reserve UK Armed Forces personnel, stratified by military service branch (Army, Royal Navy, Royal Air Force) and sex (male and female). Participants will complete validated self-report measures of sleep, mental health (e.g. PTSD, depression, anxiety, nightmares, suicidal ideation) combat exposure, and menstrual health (women only) at baseline, 6, 12, and 24 months. Participants will also undertake a 14-day sleep monitoring protocol using wrist-worn actigraphy and consensus sleep diaries to derive objective and subjective sleep–wake indices. Analyses will aim to assess prevalence, incidence and chronicity of sleep disturbances, along with multivariable regression and longitudinal mixed-effects models to examine associations with mental health outcomes and potential moderating factors. Longitudinal mediation analyses (four models) will determine potential mechanisms linking sleep disturbance with adverse health outcomes.ResultsThis protocol describes the study design and planned analyses.ConclusionsThis study will provide the first comprehensive longitudinal assessment of sleep disturbance and its associations with mental and menstrual health in UK Armed Forces personnel. Findings will inform targeted interventions, including behavioural sleep strategies, and support evidence-based policy and practice within UK Defence. Statement of SignificanceThis study represents the first large-scale, prospective cohort investigation to characterize both the prevalence and incidence of sleep disturbances, and their associations with mental health outcomes, in UK service personnel. Primarily using a remote, survey-based methodology, this study will enable wide-reaching, scalable data collection across a geographically dispersed population, reducing barriers to participation and capturing individuals who may not engage with Defence clinical services. This approach is likely to yield more accurate and generalisable estimates of sleep health, including unmet need and early-stage symptomatology not reflected in healthcare data. In addition to examining longitudinal associations, the study will explore potential pathways linking sleep disturbances with adverse health outcomes, helping to identify possible mechanisms and targets for future intervention. The inclusion and planned stratified analysis of underrepresented groups, particularly female personnel and reservists, further enhances the validity and equity of the findings. Collectively, this work will hopefully provide a critical and inclusive evidence base to inform future research, guide Defence policy, and support the development of more accessible and effective sleep health strategies across the UK military.

Introduction

Failure of individuals to meet the required standards of military service can negatively impact operational capability and lead to a less effective workforce. A common, undesirable, and undetected feature that jeopardizes the overall success and health of service personnel during military service is sleep disturbance [1, 2]. Despite a growing awareness of the importance of sleep for optimal functioning, particularly mood and mental health outcomes [3], poor sleep quality and chronic sleep restriction continues to be reported [1, 4–7] and is considered a major health concern for many military organisations worldwide [8, 9]. Service personnel are frequently presented with unique operational challenges integral to military service, including operating within a highly regulated environment, sleeping in multi-occupancy rooms, routine travel across multiple time zones, and engaging in frequent high-risk tasks (e.g. simulated combat training and deployed operations). Additionally, insufficient sleep due to disrupted circadian sleep–wake cycles from chronic and irregular sleep–wake schedules, unfavourable sleeping environments and worries about family during prolonged training and deployments are some extant precipitating factors to poor sleep behaviours and the development of more severe sleep issues and associated comorbidities [10].

Military service personnel are at greater risk of chronic sleep disturbances relative to the general population [11, 12]. For instance, it is said that ~40% of the general population sleep on average less than the minimum recommendations of 7 hours per night [13], compared with ~80% of service personnel reporting chronic short-sleep duration of <6 hours per night, and ~60% achieving <5 hours a night [5]. Poor sleep quality and insomnia-like symptoms, which are frequently comorbid with other sleep, physiological, and psychological issues, are common in service personnel, including but not limited to, breathing-related sleep disorders (e.g. obstructive sleep apnea [OSA]), circadian rhythm disorders, obesity, menstrual dysfunction in women [14], metabolic and cardiovascular disorders, depression, anxiety, substance abuse, and suicidality [1]. Based on large observational studies, conducted mainly in US Army soldiers, most commonly reported sleep disorders include insomnia and breathing-related sleep disorders (similar to the general population), followed by parasomnias and circadian rhythm disorders, particularly those operating on rotating shift patterns [1, 15, 16]. Furthermore, clinically relevant and probable insomnia have been reported across multiple cohorts of British Army recruits [7, 17], UK veterans [18], and active-duty US personnel, with symptoms frequently persisting beyond military service and commonly occurring alongside other adverse health outcomes [1].

The prevalence of sleep disturbances in UK service personnel is currently unknown; however, there is evidence of a link between sleep issues and adverse mental health outcomes in military personnel from other nations. In a US Army cohort, Mysliwiec, et al. [16] reported a ~40% prevalence of comorbid insomnia with sleep apnoea (COMISA) and demonstrated a higher proportion of individuals with COMISA meeting clinical criteria for depression (71%) and PTSD (60%) compared to those with OSA alone (11%). Byrne, et al. [19] observed a 3-5 times greater prevalence of suicidal ideation in US service personnel with clinical and subthreshold insomnia compared with those without insomnia. Additionally, Gehrman, et al. [20] reported a 2-3 times greater risk of new-onset PTSD in US service personnel with a pre-deployment insomnia diagnosis compared with those without. Personnel with the most severe phenotypes (i.e. pre-existing insomnia and short-sleep duration) were at more than three times the risk of being diagnosed with new-onset PTSD.

Building on these findings, emerging longitudinal evidence suggests that sleep disturbance may function as an upstream vulnerability factor for the development and maintenance of common mental health disorders in military personnel [21]. Wright, et al. [22] demonstrated that insomnia symptoms predicted worsening depression and PTSD 12-months following deployment, whereas pre-deployment depression or PTSD did not predict subsequent sleep problems. Similar findings have been reported in large prospective military cohorts, where personnel reporting insomnia were more than twice as likely to develop depression or PTSD at follow-up compared with those without sleep disturbances [23, 24]. Evidence from deployment-cycle studies further indicate that sleep disturbances present prior to operational exposure are associated with greater psychological symptom burden following deployment [20, 25].

While much of the military sleep literature has focused on mental health outcomes, growing evidence suggests that the consequences of sleep disturbance may extend beyond psychological wellbeing. Importantly, in women, emerging evidence suggests that sleep disturbance may be linked to menstrual dysfunction through shared neuroendocrine and circadian mechanisms, including dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary-gonadal and -adrenal axes [26–28]. Sleep disruption, circadian misalignment, and stress-related increases in cortisol have further been associated with altered reproductive hormone signalling, irregular menstrual cycles, and dysmenorrhea in non-military populations [27, 29–31]. These relationships may be further compounded by the bidirectional associations between mental health symptoms (e.g. anxiety and depression) and menstrual dysfunction [32, 33], suggesting that sleep, mental health, and menstrual health may be closely interconnected. However, the temporal nature of these relationships and the mechanisms through which they influence one another remain poorly understood, particularly within military populations.

Existing evidence suggests that sleep disturbance may function not only as a consequence of military occupational stressors, but also as a pathway through which exposures such as combat, deployment, and other service-related demands influence subsequent psychological and physiological health outcomes [20, 34, 35]. Consequently, sleep may occupy a central position linking military stress exposure with mental health and menstrual health outcomes. Furthermore, the bidirectional relationships observed between sleep, mental health, and menstrual health raise the possibility that these factors may act as intermediary pathways through which adverse outcomes develop and persist over time [28, 36, 37]. Building on this evidence, military-specific factors, including combat exposure, deployment history, age, and rank, may further influence these relationships [8, 34, 35]. Examining both the potential mediating pathways and moderating factors linking sleep disturbance with mental and menstrual health outcomes may therefore provide important insight into the mechanisms underpinning risk and resilience within military cohorts, particularly for UK Defence, where the prevalence and longitudinal course of sleep disturbances and their health consequences remain poorly characterized.

Existing research has largely focused on clinical or veteran populations [18], potentially overestimating the burden of sleep disorders while providing limited insight into the emergence and progression of sleep disturbances earlier in military careers. Furthermore, many studies are also cross-sectional or have limited follow-up periods, and are unable to adequately examine longitudinal relationships between sleep disturbance with subsequent mental health and menstrual health outcomes. In the context of an increasingly demanding global security environment, improving understanding of sleep health within UK military personnel – particularly prior to operational deployment – may provide critical insight into modifiable risk factors that influence resilience, psychological health, and overall combat readiness. The resulting evidence base may support UK Defence by informing service policy, prevention strategies, and clinical pathways aimed at optimising personnel health, performance, and military effectiveness.

Study Objectives

This prospective, survey-based cohort study will assess the prevalence, incidence and correlates of self-reported sleep disorder symptoms in Regular and Reserve UK service personnel, with 6-, 12-, and 24-month follow-ups to examine the incidence of new-onset sleep disturbances and longitudinal associations with mental health outcomes. In addition, pseudo-objective sleep–wake indices will be derived from wrist-worn actigraphy, combined with consensus sleep diary data, collected over 14 days following completion of the online surveys. This study seeks to address gaps in knowledge by examining underreported military branches (e.g. RAF and Navy) and cohorts (e.g. women, Reserves), incorporating research-grade actigraphy, and including a broad range of clinically validated sleep and mental health self-report measures commonly used in standard diagnostic protocols [38]. This study will also examine both the potential mediating pathways and moderating factors linking sleep disturbance with mental and menstrual health outcomes in UK service personnel. Materials and methods

Pre-study design engagement

Based on the principles of the Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement (PPIE) framework [39], service personnel of all ranks, including those with lived experience of sleep disturbances, were consulted during the development of this study. Feedback was sought on the relevance and clarity of survey questions, participant burden, and the feasibility of wearing sleep monitoring devices. Their input informed refinements to the questionnaire design and study procedures to enhance acceptability and engagement while minimising participant burden. Representatives will continue to contribute through advisory input during the interpretation and dissemination of findings, including the development of accessible summaries and peer-reviewed publications. The PPIE activities ensured an integrated and collaborative approach to the design of this research, thereby enhancing the relevance, quality and Defence value of this research.

Study engagement and monitoring

As the target population is across UK Defence (i.e. Royal Navy, Army, Royal Air Force), a variety of engagement and dissemination methods (Table 1) will be used to optimize recruitment and completion of validated online surveys. Frequent distribution of study information (posters, leaflets, briefings) will support ongoing recruitment and survey completion throughout the study. All surveys will be piloted by a small number (~10) of military members across different digital formats (e.g. mobile phone, tablet, personal and Ministry of Defence [MoD] IT). This process will allow questions to be tested for clarity and understanding, confirm estimated completion time, and identify any potential technical issues prior to formal release across UK Defence personnel and establishments. To minimize this risk of self-selection bias, recruitment materials will describe the study as a broad health and wellbeing monitoring project rather than emphasising specific sleep, mental health, or female health outcomes. This approach is intended to encourage participation from personnel with a wide range of health experiences, including those without current symptoms, thereby improving the representativeness of the sample and reducing the likelihood of disproportionately recruiting individuals with pre-existing sleep or mental health concerns [40].

All personnel interested in volunteering for this research will be able to complete the online surveys on their personal mobile, tablet or laptop device, or their issued MoD computer from access to a QR code or internet URL. At the end of each two-week period, all survey data will be downloaded and processed in Microsoft Excel and R, during which data will be assured for completeness and quality (including monitoring of participation rates, including the proportion of key strata, i.e. service branch and sex) by research team members, and formatted for subsequent descriptive and statistical analysis using R programming language (v4.4.1) within RStudio (v2024.04.02).

Participants

All fully trained male and female members of the Reserves (i.e. part-time members of the Armed Forces who train alongside their civilian employment) and Regular (i.e. full-time members of the Armed Forces employed in continuous military service) UK Armed Forces will be eligible to take part in this study. No lower age limit will be applied but trainees (i.e. those who are undertaking their basic training or initial specialist training) will not be eligible. Service personnel who volunteer to take part and who retire from the service during follow-up will remain eligible, and this change in occupation will be controlled for within subsequent statistical analysis (i.e. additional strata).

Potential participants will be approached using standard procedures of the central MoD department responsible for survey distribution. This study’s questionnaire will be distributed from a central email and accessed on MoDs instance of LimeSurvey, inviting potential participants to take part using the methods detailed in Table 1. Study recruitment email will briefly describe the purpose of the study and will include an accessible Participant Information Sheet (PIS) and links to the questionnaire (both URL and QR code). Service personnel of lower ranks may not have routine access to emails; therefore, leaflets / posters with URL and QR codes will be emailed to large units, garrisons, including medical centres, for distribution on routine orders and to be physically posted on noticeboards around camps. Recruitment emails, leaflets, posters will include essential study information and links to the PIS and online questionnaire, whereby page-1 will include informed consent for baseline and follow-up assessments. Potential participants will be instructed to read the PIS and consider whether they wish to take part or to contact the researchers for any questions.

Experimental Procedures

This prospective, survey-based cohort study will use MoD’s instance of LimeSurvey (LimeSurvey, Community Edition Version 6.3.4) to create, host, and collect self-report survey data from all participants. Upon accessing the survey link, participants will be provided with a simple explanation of the aims of the study, defence benefit, and what to expect as a study volunteer. If willing, participants will provide their online consent and complete the survey at baseline, and again at 6-month, 12- and 24-month follow up. Participants will have the flexibility to pause their survey at any time and return to it later without losing their completed answers. If participants do not return to the survey within 48 hours of starting it, three automatic reminders (1-week apart) to complete their survey will be sent to their preferred contact method (e.g. email, personal or work phone). All survey entries will be date and time stamped to enable accurate follow-up dates to be established.

Once participants have completed the surveys at each timepoint, they will be subsequently invited, via LimeSurvey, to participate in an optional 14-day sleep monitoring protocol, aligned with American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) recommendations (Figure 1). This protocol will involve completing daily the Consensus Sleep Diary (either online or paper-based) and concurrently wearing a research-grade actigraphy wrist monitor to assess temporal sleep–wake patterns over the 14-days. Objective sleep will be measured using wrist-worn actigraphy, a validated and non-invasive method for estimating sleep–wake patterns in naturalistic environments, enabling the identification of potential sleep phenotypes (e.g. insomnia with short sleep duration) and their relationships with subjective sleep measures and mental health symptomatology in UK service personnel.

Ordinal study milestones from recruitment to completion, including pre-study activities.

Based on an estimated compliance rate of 70% [41], the 14-day monitoring period is expected to yield ~11 days of usable diary and wearable data, consistent with minimum recommended standards (i.e. 7-days) [42]. The diary and actigraph watch will be sent to participants by a research team member with instructions on how to complete the sleep diary, along with actigraph wear-time instructions.

Self-reported and objective sleep and mental health outcomes

All surveys will be integrated into a single questionnaire (Table 2), and which all participants will be asked to complete at baseline, 6-month, 12- and 24-month follow up. This comprehensive questionnaire will include a demographic and lifestyle survey adapted for UK service personnel, including ordinal, categorical and binary question formats, with elements derived from established public health surveys (HSE England). This adapted survey aims to capture participant demographics, occupational, health, and lifestyle factors relevant to sleep, mental health, and menstrual health outcomes in UK service personnel. Demographic variables include age, sex, nationality, ethnicity, marital status, pregnancy status, education level, and caregiving responsibilities. Military-specific information includes Service branch, Service type (Regular or Reserve), rank, occupational role, deployability status, years of service, basic training duration, deployment history, combat exposure, and operational commitments.

The questionnaire additionally captures factors that may influence sleep and health outcomes, including injury and illness-related absenteeism, smoking/vaping behaviours, alcohol consumption, and family or caregiving responsibilities. Participants will also be asked to report diagnosed and suspected sleep disorders, current sleep-related treatments and therapies (including prescribed medications, over-the-counter sleep aids, cognitive behavioural therapies, and sleep apnoea treatment). Participants reporting a diagnosis of obstructive sleep apnoea will additionally be asked about current treatment status, treatment modality (e.g. CPAP, mandibular advancement device), and typical treatment use to provide an indication of treatment adherence. Information on medications that may influence sleep, mental health, or menstrual health outcomes will also be collected, including prescription sleep medications, psychotropic medications, and hormonal contraceptive use among female participants. These data will be used to characterize the study cohort, identify potential confounding factors, and inform covariate, moderator, mediation, and stratified analyses.

Objective and subjective sleep assessment

Objective sleep data will be collected during monitoring periods (e.g. continuous 14-days at baseline, 6-month, 12-, and 24-month follow-up) using the CamNtech tri-axial wrist-based accelerometer (Motion Watch 8, a CE-marked Class 1 medical device, CamNtech, Cambridge, UK). Assessments will be scheduled during typical two-week periods (excluding leave, deployment, or convalescence). The MotionWatch 8 is a compact, waterproof wrist-worn device measuring 36 × 28.2 × 9.4 mm that features a triaxial accelerometer, an integrated light sensor (ranging from 0 to 64 000 lux), and 4 MB of non-volatile memory. Its actigraphy sensitivity is finely tuned to accurately distinguish sleep and wake states from wrist movements. This device has been validated and endorsed by AASM as a reliable outcome measure for evaluating sleep behaviours and treatment response in individuals with sleep issues (e.g. insomnia) [47]. Each device will be initialized and configured (with event markers enabled) prior to issuing to participants using CamNtech’s MotionWare software (Cole-Kripke algorithm) [48], with sampling epochs set at 30 seconds and synchronized to standard GMT time. Sleep–wake indices (and units of measure) collected for subsequent analysis will include total sleep time (TST, hours:minutes [hh:mm]), sleep efficiency (SE, %), time spent in bed (TIB, hh:mm), wake after sleep onset (WASO, hh:mm), frequency (count) and duration (hh:mm) of awakenings, and fragmentation index (%). In addition to the objective sleep assessment, participants will be asked to complete a daily sleep diary (Consensus sleep diary) [49], which will also capture recent sleep patterns and work schedules. Specifically, participants will report: i) whether they worked a shift or duty period in the preceding 24 hours (Yes/No); ii) the type of shift worked (day, evening, night, rotating, or other); iii) work start and finish times; and iv) whether the sleep period recorded occurred before, after, or during a work shift. These items are consistent with recommendations for documenting contextual factors that may influence actigraphy-derived sleep outcomes and will permit identification of participants exposed to recent shift work, irregular schedules, or operational duties during the monitoring period.

Estimated Sample Size

No published data exist on the prevalence of sleep disorders in UK service personnel, with most research dedicated to veterans [18] or non-UK service personnel with comorbidities. Insomnia and breathing-related sleep disorders emerge as the most prevalent sleep disorders in civilians and service personnel [50, 51]. Prevalence figures vary considerably between studies, particularly among military personnel. For example, Mysliwiec, et al. [16] reported 51% for OSA and 30% for insomnia in a relatively small cohort (n = 110) of military personnel returning from combat within 18-months of deployment. In a separate study, Mysliwiec, et al. [52], insomnia, and OSA prevalence rates during the calendar year 2010 for active-duty US service personnel were 64% and 63%, respectively. More recently, Markwald, et al. [53] reported an insomnia prevalence of ~17% based on a large epidemiological study in active-duty US service personnel. In a recent evaluation conducted by Molloy and Murphy [18], 70% of UK military veterans reported probable insomnia symptoms. Large variation in published estimates likely due to differences in assessment modality (self-report vs clinical diagnosis), sleep disturbance remittance thresholds, demographics, comorbidities, combat exposure, introduces uncertainty around true population proportions and, therefore, the maximum variance scenario (p = .5) was used as the estimated proportion (p) in the sample size calculation [54]. This common approach produces the largest possible sample size, thereby ensuring this study is adequately powered a priori despite differences in published estimates.

As of April 1st, 2025, UK Defence’s total Regular trained strength was 126 610 (Army: 56%, 10% women, Navy: 22%, 11% women, RAF: 22%, 16% women) [55]. During the same time period, the trained Reserve strength was ~30 000 personnel (Army Reserve: ~81%, ~15% women; Navy Reserves: ~10%, ~15% women; RAF Reserve ~9%, ~24% women) (UK Armed Forces Biannual Diversity Statistics, April 2025). UK MoD personnel statistics indicate that ~14 500 Regular personnel and ~4000 reservists leave the Armed Forces each year. Assuming similar annual leaving rates, this would equate to ~29 000 Regular personnel and ~8000 reservists leaving over a two-year period, corresponding to an estimated 23% and 26% loss, respectively, over 24-month follow-up (Ministry of Defence, 2025).

For prospective multivariable regression analyses examining whether baseline sleep disorder indicators (ISI, STOP-BANG, RBD single-question screen) and sleep duration (objective and subjective) predict subsequent mental health outcomes, an a priori power analysis in Gpower (Dusseldorf, V 3.1) [57] was conducted, with the sleep variables entered as a predictor block over and above prespecified covariates. As limited prospective military studies report standardized effect sizes directly comparable with multiple-regression f2 metrics, effect size assumptions were informed by conventional recommendations for regression analyses [58, 59] and the broader literature demonstrating moderate prospective associations between sleep disturbances and subsequent mental health outcomes [20, 22, 36]. Accordingly, α = 0.05 and 90% power, the required total sample was ~120 participants to detect a medium (f2 = 0.15) incremental effect, representing a clinically meaningful contribution of sleep-related variables beyond established demographic and military covariates. For longitudinal subgroup and interaction analyses, sample size adequacy was considered with reference to a repeated-measures mixed-model framework. Informed by conventional recommendations for repeated-measures designs due to limited longitudinal prospective data in military personnel [59], power was based on detecting a small-to-medium group × time interaction across four timepoints (baseline, 6-month, 12-, and 24 months), assuming α = 0.05, power = 0.90, and moderate within-person correlation (p = 0.5) across repeated measures. Under these assumptions, ~130 participants in total would be required to detect a medium interaction effect. Service type (Regular vs Reserve) was used as an illustrative grouping variable because it represents a primary sampling stratum; however, similar analytical approaches will be used to examine other moderators of scientific and operational relevance, including sex, baseline mental health status, combat exposure, deployment history, age, and rank.

Although formal power calculations were not performed specifically for longitudinal mediation analyses, the planned cohort substantially exceeds published recommendations for detecting small indirect effects using bootstrap procedures and for estimating complex longitudinal structural equation modelling (SEM) [60–62]. Assuming anticipated retention rates over 24 months, the expected sample size is projected to remain adequately powered for estimation of small-to-moderate indirect effects and stable parameter estimation within the overall cohort. Consequently, the study is expected to be sufficiently powered to support the planned four-wave longitudinal mediation analyses. Sex- and Service-specific mediation models will be considered exploratory and interpreted cautiously where subgroup sample sizes are smaller.

Data Analysis Plan

Statistical analysis approach

Sleep disorder symptom prevalence, mental health and menstrual health outcomes will be summarized descriptively overall and stratified by service branch (Army, Royal Navy, Royal Air Force), service type (Regular vs Reserve), and sex. Baseline differences between strata will be assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and general linear models for continuous outcomes, adjusting for age, combat exposure, length of service, and deployment history. Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons will be applied where factors include more than two levels.

Incidence of mental health and menstrual health outcomes at 6-month, 12-, and 24 months will be estimated among participants free from the outcome at baseline and expressed as cumulative incidence proportions; incidence rates will also be calculated where appropriate. Associations between baseline sleep characteristics and incident outcomes will be examined using multivariable logistic regression models, reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Chronicity of outcomes will be assessed by identifying persistent cases across follow-up timepoints (e.g. presence of symptoms at all follow-up time points), with additional analyses examining transitions between states (e.g. incident, remitted, persistent).

Cross-sectional associations at baseline will be examined using multivariable linear regression models, with sleep disorder indicators (i.e. ISI, STOP-BANG, and RBD screen; analysed separately) and sleep duration (objective and subjective) entered as predictors of mental health and menstrual health outcomes. All models will adjust for pre-specified covariates including age, sex, service branch, service type, rank, role, time in service, and combat exposure. Moderation analyses will include interaction terms (e.g. SleepDisorder × Sex; SleepDuration × Service), with significant effects probed using simple slopes and visualized using marginal effects plots.

Prospective associations will be examined using multivariable regression models (linear or logistic, as appropriate) to test whether baseline sleep characteristics (sleep disorder indicators and sleep duration) predict mental health and menstrual health outcomes at 6-month, 12-, and 24 months, adjusting for baseline outcome status and covariates. Objective sleep duration will be treated as the primary exposure, with subjective sleep duration included in complementary and sensitivity analyses.

Where repeated measures of mental health and menstrual health outcomes are available across timepoints (e.g. baseline, 6-month, 12-, and 24 months), linear mixed-effects models or generalized linear mixed models will be used to examine longitudinal associations between sleep characteristics and these outcomes over time. Mixed-effects models are selected to account for within-participant correlation, accommodate incomplete follow-up data under a missing-at-random assumption, and allow modelling of both time-invariant and time-varying exposures. Participant will be included as a random intercept, with time modelled as a categorical variable, and interaction terms (e.g. Sleep × Time) included where appropriate. Random slopes for time will be considered where supported by model fit and data structure. All data will be analysed using R programming language (v4.4.1) within RStudio (v2024.04.02), and statistical significance will be set at p < .05, with results reported as effect estimates and 95% confidence intervals.

To investigate potential mechanisms linking sleep disturbance with adverse health outcomes, longitudinal SEM will be used to examine pathways between sleep, mental health, menstrual health, and combat exposure across baseline, 6-month, 12-, and 24-month assessments. This approach enables estimation of direct and indirect effects while accounting for temporal relationships and repeated measures. Four a priori pathways will be explored: i) menstrual health indicators mediating associations between sleep disturbance and subsequent mental health outcomes in women; ii) sleep disturbance mediating associations between combat exposure and mental health outcomes; iii) mental health symptoms mediating associations between sleep disturbance and subsequent menstrual health outcomes; and iv) sleep disturbance mediating associations between combat exposure and menstrual health outcomes [20, 22, 28, 52, 64, 65]. Models will incorporate autoregressive pathways (e.g. baseline sleep predicting subsequent sleep) and cross-lagged associations between sleep, mental health, menstrual health, and combat exposure. This approach will enable assessment of whether changes in candidate mediators precede subsequent changes in outcomes while accounting for baseline status and repeated measures. Models will adjust for pre-specified covariates, including age, sex, service branch, service type, rank, role, length of service, deployment history, and shift work where appropriate.

Indirect effects will be estimated using bootstrapped confidence intervals. Evidence of mediation will be inferred where indirect effects differ significantly from zero [61, 66]. Where sample sizes permit, exploratory multi-group SEM analyses will examine whether identified pathways differ according to sex and service branch.

Data Quality, Participant Adherence, and Missing Data

All participants will be provided with written and verbal guidance (if required) on how to complete study procedures, including questionnaire completion, sleep diary recording, and actigraphy wear-time requirements for the 14-day sleep assessment period. Adherence and data completeness will be summarized descriptively across all outcome measures (e.g. number of completed sleep diary entries, number and type of questionnaires completed, number of nights with valid actigraphy data). Reasons for data incompleteness (e.g. loss to follow-up, non-response, competing operational demands, or technical issues) will be recorded where available. Given the fully online, self-paced nature of the study, participants are able to complete the survey element at a time and location of their choosing, which is intended to reduce barriers to participation. Nevertheless, to support data completeness, survey design will prioritize ease of use (e.g. mobile-compatible formats, clear instructions, and minimized response burden), alongside the use of automated reminders where appropriate.

Missing data will be characterized at the item (i.e. within a survey), measure (i.e. entire questionnaire missing at a timepoint), and wave-level (i.e. participant missing a whole follow-up), enabling differentiation between partial and complete non-response across timepoints. The extent and patterns of missing data will be reported for all primary and secondary outcomes at each assessment point. For each individual self-report measure (e.g. surveys and sleep diary), prorated total scores will be computed for participants if they provide responses >70% but <100% of the item in the measure, with missing responses replaced by the individual’s mean for the remaining items. This method of handling missing item-level data used within behavioural sciences is considered an appropriate approach so long as the surveys being used have demonstrated good levels of internal consistency and the item completion threshold is no less than 50% of the total number of items within the survey [67, 68]. Individuals who provide less than 70% of the data for a given measure will be considered invalid, and a total score will not be calculated for that specific measure [11].

Objective sleep data derived from actigraphy will be considered valid where participants provide a minimum of ≥10 nights of usable data across the 14-day monitoring period, consistent with established recommendations. Sleep diary data will be considered valid where ≥70% of daily entries are completed [69].

Resources

This internally funded prospective cohort study will be delivered by a multidisciplinary team with expertise in sleep medicine, mental health, military physiology, and epidemiology, supported by statistical and data management personnel. Data will be collected using secure, web-based survey infrastructure, specifically the MoD’s instance of LimeSurvey, enabling remote and flexible participation across geographically dispersed UK service personnel. Participants will have access to an Independent Medical Officer (IMO) and a welfare officer, who are independent of the research team, and can be contacted to discuss any study-related questions, concerns, or welfare issues. Recruitment will be facilitated through established military communication channels, enabling access to the target population. The fully online and self-paced design, combined with remote data collection, supports feasibility within the operational constraints of the military environment.

Participants will be required to provide informed consent electronically prior to participation in any study procedures, following confirmation of eligibility and capacity to consent. Participant data will be pseudonymized through the allocation of a unique study identification number at the point of consent and throughout the 24-month study period, enabling identification during each follow-up. Personally identifiable information will be stored separately and securely from research data, with access restricted to authorized members of the research team. No identifiable information will be included in any publications or shared datasets. Given the remote and online nature of the study, no travel reimbursement is anticipated. Barriers and Considerations

Several potential barriers and limitations are anticipated for this prospective cohort study involving UK service personnel. The study requires participants to complete multiple self-report measures – including multiple surveys assessing sleep, mental and menstrual health, and combat-related activity – alongside a 14-day objective sleep assessment using actigraphy and concurrent sleep diaries, at baseline, 6-month, 12-, and 24-month timepoints. The high participant burden associated with repeated and lengthy surveys may impact engagement, adherence, and data completeness over the study period. As a fully remote, web-based study, challenges may arise from variable internet access, differing levels of digital literacy, or technical issues with survey completion platforms. Distribution of actigraphy devices using military mail introduces potential delays, lost shipments, or device malfunction, which may result in missing objective sleep data. Participant adherence to the actigraphy wear protocol and accurate completion of sleep diaries may also be influenced by operational duties, deployments, or other service-related commitments. Attrition is an inherent limitation in longitudinal cohort studies, particularly over extended follow-up periods. Loss to follow-up may reduce statistical power and introduce bias if participants who withdraw differ systematically from those who remain engaged. The study team will monitor patterns of missing data, account for loss to follow-up in the pre-defined sample size estimates, and assess adherence to explore potential attrition bias, applying appropriate statistical methods to mitigate its impact. Finally, as all measures rely on self-report for mental health, sleep, and operational activity, there is potential for recall bias, reporting bias, or misclassification. While objective actigraphy data will complement subjective reports, the combination of self-reported and device-based data may still be influenced by participant compliance and the timing of assessments.

Whilst psychological processes such as emotion dysregulation, repetitive negative thinking, rumination, and sleep-related worry are plausible mechanisms linking sleep disturbance with adverse mental health outcomes, comprehensive assessment of these constructs falls beyond the primary aims of the present study. This study is principally designed as a large-scale epidemiological investigation to quantify the prevalence, incidence, correlates, and longitudinal consequences of sleep disturbances in UK service personnel, rather than to provide an exhaustive evaluation of all potential psychological mechanisms. Consequently, the proposed mediation analyses focus on variables of direct clinical and operational relevance that are measured across the cohort, including combat exposure, mental health symptoms, menstrual health indicators, and sleep disturbance. These factors align closely with Defence health priorities and are expected to provide important insight into potentially modifiable pathways influencing health, wellbeing, and operational effectiveness. Inclusion of additional psychological mediators would substantially increase participant burden and study complexity, potentially affecting recruitment, adherence, and retention within a large longitudinal cohort. Future mechanistic studies may build upon the findings of the present work to investigate more proximal psychological pathways in greater detail.

Discussion

Despite growing recognition of the importance of sleep for health and operational effectiveness, sleep disturbances remain a significant concern across military populations. Prospective epidemiological research examining both the prevalence and incidence of sleep disturbances in fully trained UK service personnel is lacking. Characterising both is essential to distinguish transient disruption from persistent or newly emerging disorders, and to clarify temporal relationships with common mental health conditions. However, understanding whether sleep disturbance simply co-occurs with adverse outcomes or is contributory through specific behavioural, psychological, and physiological pathways is equally important. Emerging longitudinal evidence suggests that sleep disturbance may function as an upstream vulnerability factor for the development and maintenance of depression, anxiety, PTSD, and suicidality [20, 22, 36, 37], while also interacting with occupational stressors and female-specific health factors through complex bidirectional mechanisms [14, 70]. Consequently, examining potential mediating pathways may provide important insight into how sleep disturbances contribute to adverse health outcomes and help identify modifiable targets for prevention and intervention beyond simple risk prediction.

Cultural norms within military settings, where sleep may be undervalued or deprioritized relative to operational demands, are likely to contribute to underreporting and delayed help-seeking. As such, reliance on clinician-derived data or formal referral pathways may underestimate the true burden and complexity of sleep-related problems, particularly among personnel who do not present for care or whose symptoms remain subclinical yet operationally impactful. This study addresses these limitations through a large-scale, survey-based prospective cohort design capturing self-reported sleep disturbances, mental and menstrual health outcomes, and relevant occupational exposures across a broad and representative UK Armed Forces population. By sampling beyond those engaged with Defence clinical services, the study is better positioned to provide more accurate and generalisable estimates of sleep health, including unmet need and early-stage symptomatology not reflected in healthcare utilisation data.

Ensuring adequate representation of underrepresented groups – particularly female personnel and reservists – is central to the scientific validity and translational relevance of the study. These groups differ from Regular male personnel in occupational roles, deployment patterns, stress exposures, and help-seeking behaviours, all of which may influence sleep and its relationship with mental health outcomes. Through proportionate sampling and stratified analyses, the study will generate more precise estimates within these subgroups to identify potentially distinct risk profiles and trajectories: this is especially pertinent for reservists, who’s civilian-to-military transitions may confer unique vulnerabilities, and for female personnel, who may experience differential exposures and barriers to care. Strengthening representation enhances the applicability of findings, supporting more equitable policy development, targeted intervention strategies, and informed resource allocation across the force.

Finally, the findings have the potential to directly influence Defence policy and clinical practice. The recent inclusion of sleep hygiene guidance within defence policy (Joint Services Publication 661) reflects increasing recognition of sleep as a health priority for UK Defence; however, education alone is unlikely to address moderate-to-severe sleep disturbances. By providing robust epidemiological data on prevalence, incidence, correlates, and comorbidity, this study has the potential to provide an evidence base to refine UK Defence sleep policy so that it more accurately reflects the scale and nature of sleep-related need within UK Defence. In parallel, the results will support optimisation of clinical pathways, including the Defence Sleep Treatment pathway, by improving understanding of symptom profiles, comorbidity patterns, and help-seeking behaviours. These findings may support earlier identification of at-risk personnel, more effective triaging, and improved targeting of evidence-based interventions such as cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia. Collectively, this work aims to bridge the gap between policy, provision, and population need, enhancing the scalability, accessibility, and impact of sleep health initiatives across UK Defence.
