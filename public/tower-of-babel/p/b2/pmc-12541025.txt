Effects of fertilization depth on sugarcane quality and soil fertility
Authors: Ning Zhong, Jinsheng Huang, Jiafeng Wang, Wenjiao Li, Yuefeng Zhang, Xiaobiao Zeng, Jian Li, Qiuliang Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The low fertilizer utilization rate has become an urgent need to solve the problem and fertilization depth is a better solution to fertilization. Conducted in a field micro-area in Baise City, Guangxi Province, the experiment employed different combinations of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, applied at various depths to investigates the effects of investigate dose of fertilizer and fertilization depths on the yield, quality traits, soil enzyme activities, and overall soil fertility of sugarcane (Saccharum spp. Hybrids). The results indicate that closer alignment of the fertilization depth to the main root distribution area enhances the yield and quality of sugarcane. Specifically, deeper fertilization increases the soil’s nitrate nitrogen, available phosphorus, and potassium content. Additionally, such fertilization depths significantly boost the activity of soil enzymes such as urease, sucrase, catalase, and acid phosphatase in the rhizosphere. Optimizing the fertilization ratio to N:P2O5:K2O at 5:1:5 (comprising 0.134 kg/m2 of urea, 0.104 kg/m2 of potassium chloride, and 0.093 kg/m2 of calcium superphosphate) and setting the fertilization depth to 15 cm can notably increase sugarcane yield and quality and enhance soil fertility. This approach aligns more closely with the nutrient demands of sugarcane, improves overall soil fertility, and promotes agricultural sustainability to provide data support and a scientific basis for the efficient management and utilization of local sugarcane fertilizers.

Introduction

Sugarcane (Saccharum spp. Hybrids) is a critical agricultural commodity, serving as the primary source for 80% of the global sugar production and 40% of fuel ethanol. This crop is cultivated in approximately 110 countries across the tropical and subtropical regions, with significant plantations in the tropical zones of southern China1. Fertilization is crucial in enhancing both the yield and quality of sugarcane. However, the inefficiency of fertilizer use remains a significant challenge.

Several key factors influence fertilizer utilization, including the type and quantity of fertilizer, as well as the depth and location of application2. The depth of fertilizer application is particularly impactful on nutrient absorption, crop growth, and development. Optimal fertilization practices can significantly improve nutrient uptake, ensure robust crop growth, bolster yields, and reduce costs3,4. The soil among the roots serves as a nutrient reservoir for crops; thus, the placement of nutrients within the soil profoundly affects their availability. According to the “Technical Regulations for High Yield and Efficient Fertilization of Sugarcane” (NY/T 3252-2018), the general layering of sugarcane roots is as follows. The surface layer (0–10 cm) is the primary absorption zone of the root system, with the highest density of fibrous roots (accounting for 40–50% of the total root length); middle layer (10–20 cm): dense distribution layer of lateral roots (lateral root biomass accounts for 60–70%). An appropriate fertilization depth enhances nutrient absorption efficiency, supports plant growth and development, and improves crop yields and soil health. Moreover, it increases the efficiency of nitrogen utilization and recycling, thereby enhancing soil fertility and environmental quality5–10. Research by Niedziński et al.7 demonstrated that at a fertilization depth of 20 cm, the uptake of nitrogen and phosphorus in potatoes increased by 20%, with a corresponding 21% increase in the nitrogen accumulation in tubers. Additionally, this depth positively influences plant length and total root area, which are crucial for optimal dry matter distribution, nutrient uptake, and root development. The formulation of fertilizers also plays a vital role in maximizing efficiency and crop yields. In 2018, global ammonia emissions from rice, wheat, and maize fields were estimated at approximately 4.3 ± 1.0 Tg N yr−1, which was lower than previous estimates that overlooked comprehensive fertilizer management strategies. Spatial optimization of these strategies could potentially reduce ammonia emissions by about 38% without necessitating an increase in total nitrogen inputs11. A well-balanced fertilizer doses not only enhances the root growth environment but also increases crop yields, mitigates environmental pollution, and preserves soil fertility. Furthermore, the type of fertilizer and its application location are also critical in determining fertilizer utilization efficiency. Research by Jan et al.12 indicated that different types of fertilizers, such as ammonium sulfate and ammonium nitrate, did not significantly impact the spike population, grain number, or weight of 1000 grains.

Currently, the primary nutrients applied to sugarcane in China include potassium chloride, superphosphate, and urea, with application rates varying between 500 and 750 kg/ha, 1500–2250 kg/ha, and 750–900 kg/ha, respectively. Shallow fertilization on rainy days can easily lead to nitrogen and phosphorus leaching, while deep fertilizer placement can reduce the risk of leaching by 30–40%13. The actual fertilizer utilization rate of sugarcane in China is below 30%, markedly lower than the 50% utilization rate observed in developed countries14. This discrepancy in utilization rates may be attributed to variations in fertilization methods and formulations. Sugarcane has a dense area of fibrous roots at 0–10 cm and lateral roots at 10–20 cm. Currently, shallow application (< 5 cm) is misaligned with root distribution, resulting in low nutrient absorption efficiency. Consequently, there is a pressing need to address this inefficiency by modifying both the methods and formulations of fertilization. Notably, soil analyses in sugarcane-growing regions have revealed low levels of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium14. The absorption ratio of these nutrients by sugarcane is typically 2:1:2. The quantity and ratio of fertilizers required at different growth stages of sugarcane vary significantly; during the tillering stage, which accounts for 10% to 20% of the total fertilization period, the demand for nitrogen slightly exceeds that of the seedling stage. During the elongation phase, the uptake of all three nutrients markedly increases, representing over 50% of total nutrient absorption. The research focused on sugarcane and yellow–brown soil in the Tianyang District, Guangxi, China, utilizing different treatments of fertilizer formulas and depths, examined through the field micro-area method. This approach aims to determine the optimal fertilizer application ratio and depth, thereby providing a scientific foundation for effective fertilizer management and enhancing fertilizer utilization efficiency in sugarcane cultivation.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

More than 70% of the sugarcane areas in Guangxi are composed of red soil and yellow–brown soil, with a pH value generally below 5.5. The soil has high aluminum ion activity (average 35–40 mg/kg), which leads to the formation of Al–P and Fe–P complexes (fixation rate of 60–70%) of phosphorus. The average annual rainfall in Guangxi is 1500–2000 mm, and 60% of it is concentrated during the sugarcane elongation period from May to September, which is prone to nitrogen leaching. At the same time, the high temperature and high humidity environment (annual average temperature of 22–23 °C, relative humidity above 85%) leads to high urease activity in the surface soil, and shallow nitrogen fertilizer application is prone to ammonia volatilization and loss. The fixation of phosphorus in acidic soil, leaching in monsoon climate, and volatilization and leaching of fertilizer formulas and methods can all lead to low fertilizer efficiency in Guangxi sugarcane areas.

The experiment was conducted at the Baise Agricultural Science Research Institute (107° 0′ 48.687″ E, 23° 39′ 48.255″ N). The region experiences an average temperature of 22 °C, annual rainfall of 1108 mm, and approximately 1803 h of sunshine per year. The soil type is yellow–brown, with a basal fertility status characterized by a pH of 5.02, soil organic matter content of 29.76 g/kg, total nitrogen content of 0.14 g/kg, available phosphorus (AP) content of 56.45 mg/kg, and available potassium (AK) content of 66.73 mg/kg. The test fertilizers included urea, which contains 46% nitrogen, calcium superphosphate with 12% effective phosphorus (P2O5), and potassium chloride featuring 60% effective potassium (K2O). The test crop was ‘Guitang46’, a high-quality, early-maturing sugarcane variety developed by the Guangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences. This variety is known for its high yield and sugar content, strong resistance to disease and lodging, and features such as tall, upright growth, uniform medium-to-large stems, and evenly spaced internodes. ‘Guitang46’ is suitable for planting in both dry and paddy fields with loose soil and moderate to high fertility.

Experimental design

The sugarcane variety ‘Guitang46’, developed by the Guangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences, was utilized in this study. The experiment was conducted using field micro-plots, each measuring 4.8 m by 7.0 m (approximately 33.6 m2). A total of 21 plots were established and arranged in a completely randomized design with three replicates per treatment. Each plot contained four rows, with a row spacing of 1.2 m and a row length of 7 m. The upper half of the newly planted sugarcane was used as seed stems, which were cut into uniform double-bud segments. Each plot was planted with 208 buds. Planting occurred on April 8, 2022, and the harvest took place on December 28, 2022. Seven different treatments were implemented based on varying fertilizer formulations.

The formula for seedling stage needs to balance nitrogen promotion for leaves and phosphorus promotion for roots. The tillering stage formula induces lateral root development through high nitrogen phosphorus ratio, activating the phosphorus activation mechanism to cope with red soil fixation. The elongation period formula relies on high potassium supply to maintain stem elongation and photosynthetic efficiency, and deep application ensures deep root absorption. The mature formula requires strict control of nitrogen and potassium to promote sugar accumulation.

Nitrogen and phosphorus were the primary indices studied due to their significant impact on sugarcane growth and their potential for environmental loss15. Various nitrogen and phosphorus gradients were established to evaluate their effects on both sugarcane growth and soil fertility. According to the stratification standard of “Technical Regulations for High Yield and Efficient Fertilization of Sugarcane” (NY/T 3252-2018), the surface layer (0–10 cm) of the primary absorption zone of the root system, which has the highest density of fibrous roots; and the middle layer (10–20 cm) of the lateral root dense distribution layer with the core area for phosphorus absorption during the tillering stage. Considering that the root system develops into a three-level structure of “surface layer roots-middle layer roots- deep layer roots”, and fibrous roots and lateral roots are the main pathways for sugarcane to absorb fertilizers, the fertilization depth of 0 cm (spraying), an average value of 5 cm for the surface layer (0–10 cm), and 15 cm for the middle layer (10–20 cm) was set at this level to further investigate the influence of fertilizer depth on soil fertility and crop growth. The specific treatments were as follows:

Treatment T0 No fertilizer applied.

Treatment T1 0.031 kg/m2 N, 0.044 kg/m2 K2O, and 0.011 kg /m2 P2O5, applied at the surface (0 cm).

Treatment T2 0.062 kg/m2 N, 0.044 kg/m2 K2O, and 0.011 kg/m2 P2O5, applied at the surface (0 cm).

Treatment T3 0.031 kg/m2 N, 0.062 kg/m2 K2O, and 0.011 kg/m2 P2O5, applied at the surface (0 cm).

Treatment T4 0.062 kg/m2 N, 0.062 kg/m2 K2O, and 0.011 kg/m2 P2O5, applied at the surface (0 cm).

Treatment T5 0.062 kg/m2 N, 0.062 kg/m2 K2O, and 0.011 kg/m2 P2O5, applied at 5 cm depth.

Treatment T6 0.062 kg/m2 N, 0.062 kg/m2 K2O, and 0.011 kg/m2 P2O5, applied at 15 cm depth.

All treatments were applied consistently as described. N:P2O5:K2O of T1, T2, T3 were 2.5:1:3.57, 5:1:3.57, 2.5:1:5, respectively. N:P2O5:K2O of T4, T5, T6 were all 5:1:5. T0, T1, T2, T3 and T4 were broadcast at a depth of 0 cm, whereas treatments T5 and T6 were applied at depths of 5 cm and 15 cm, respectively. Each treatment was replicated three times in a randomized block design. Protective rows were planted around the perimeter to maintain consistent ecological conditions for sugarcane within the micro-zone. The principle of nutrient balancing was applied in a single fertilizer application to optimize sugarcane nutrition.

Sampling collection

The harvested sugarcane was first weighed to obtain its fresh weight, then heated at 105 °C for 30 min to terminate any biological processes, and subsequently dried at 75 °C until a constant weight was achieved. After reaching a stable dry weight, the samples were crushed and sifted through a 100-mesh sieve. Soil samples were collected from each plot using a five-point sampling method, air-dried in a cool area, and then finely ground and passed through a 20-mesh sieve.

Determination projects and methods

Sugarcane and soil nutrient index determination

The weights of individual sugarcane seedlings and the total weight per plot were determined using electronic scales. At the maturity stage of sugarcane, 10 randomly selected plants from each plot were weighed to calculate the average weight per plant. This process was repeated for each treatment to ascertain the average total seedling weight (TSW) per treatment. Total nitrogen (TN) was analyzed using the Kjeldahl distillation method. Total phosphorus (TP) and total potassium (TK) were assessed using a perchloric acid (HClO4)-sulfuric acid (H2SO4) digestion method followed by molybdenum blue colorimetry16. Ammonium nitrogen (NH4+–N) levels were determined using the Nessler reagent colorimetric method16. Nitrate nitrogen (NO3−–N) was quantified via ultraviolet spectrophotometry. Available phosphorus (AP) was analyzed with the molybdenum antimony colorimetric method16. Available potassium (AK) in the soil was measured using 1 mol/L ammonium acetate extraction followed by flame photometry16. Soil organic matter content was determined using an elemental analyzer (Vario EL cube, Elementar, Germany). Soil pH was measured with a water-to-soil ratio of 2.5:1 using a pH meter16. Enzymatic activities in the soil were assessed with several methods: urease activity was measured using the sodium phenolate-sodium hypochlorite method17; sucrase activity was quantified with the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid method16; catalase activity was determined by potassium permanganate titration17; cellulase activity was evaluated using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid colorimetric method17; and phosphatase activity was measured via the disodium phenyl phosphate method17.

Data processing and analysis

Nitrogen activation coefficient (NAC)18 = (concentration of NH4+–N + concentration of NO3−–N) / concentration of TN.

Phosphorus activation coefficient (PAC) = concentration of AP/concentration of TP

Potassium activation coefficient (KAC) = concentration of AK/concentration of TK.

The experimental data were statistically analyzed using Excel 2019 and SPSS, with a significance level set at α = 0.05. Graphs were created with Origin 2021 software.

Results

Effect of different fertilizer treatments on sugarcane yield and quality

The impact of various fertilization treatments on sugarcane agronomic traits is depicted in Fig. 1. For T2, T4, T5, and T6, the number of main seedlings and the total number of seedlings (Fig. 1a) were notably higher than in other treatments, except for the number of main seedlings in T2. T6 exhibited the highest number of main seedlings, reaching 113 trees, marking a 47% increase compared to T0. T0 had the fewest total seedlings at 183, while T4 had the most, totaling 241. The total seedling weight of T4–T6 (283.18–288.45 kg) was significantly higher than that of T1–T3 (201.35–256.72 kg). Figure 1b illustrates the effects of fertilization on sugarcane stem diameter and height. The stem diameter and thickness in T2, T4, T5, and T6 were significantly greater than those observed in T0, T1, and T3 (P < 0.05). Although there were no significant differences in height among the fertilization treatments (except for T1), all were significantly taller than T0 and T1 (P < 0.05). The tallest sugarcane, found in T6, reached a height of 268.93 cm.Fig. 1Sugarcane agronomic traits under different fertilization treatments. Effects of different fertilizer treatments on sugarcane nutrient content

The effects of different fertilization treatments on the nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium contents and their conversion rates in the aboveground parts of sugarcane are depicted in Fig. 3. Generally, the nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium contents in sugarcane stems are higher than those in the leaves. Specifically, the nitrogen content in sugarcane leaves across all treatments was lower than 0.50 g/kg. However, the nitrogen content in the leaves from treatments T2, T3, and T6 was significantly higher than in other treatments, with the exception of T3 (P < 0.05). In the stems, nitrogen content ranged from 1.20 to 2.00 g/kg, with the highest levels observed in the T6 treatment (Fig. 3a). Notably, the nitrogen content in the shoots of T6 was significantly higher than in other treatments, while the nitrogen content in T5 was significantly higher than in treatments T0 to T3, with no significant difference when compared to T4. The nitrogen conversion rates for T1 and T3 were higher than those of the other treatments (Fig. 3b). For phosphorus, the content in both leaves and stems was higher in treatments T4, T5, and T6 than in the other treatments, except for T3 (Fig. 3c). The phosphorus accumulation in the aboveground parts of T6 was significantly higher than in other treatments, and T5’s accumulation was also significantly higher than in other treatments, excluding T3 and T4. No significant differences in phosphorus conversion rates were observed among the treatments (Fig. 3d). Regarding potassium, the content in the leaves and stems of T6 was higher than in those of other treatments, with the exception of the potassium content in the leaves of T4 and T5 (Fig. 3e). The potassium accumulation in the shoots of T6 was significantly higher than that of other treatments, followed by T5 and T4. The potassium conversion rates among treatments ranged from 25 to 45%, with T5 and T6 showing significantly lower conversion rates compared to other treatments (Fig. 3f).Fig. 3Changes in the nutrient content and conversion rates in the aboveground parts of sugarcane under different fertilization treatments. Effects of different fertilizer treatments on soil physical and chemical properties

The soil physical and chemical properties resulting from various fertilization treatments are illustrated in Fig. 4. The total nitrogen and ammonium nitrogen levels in the T4, T5, and T6 treatments were significantly higher than those in other treatments, with T6 exhibiting the highest value at 1.08 g/kg. The nitrate nitrogen content in T2 and T4 was notably higher than in other treatments. Additionally, the nitrogen activation coefficient in the soils of T1 to T3 was significantly higher than in other treatments (Fig. 4a). Total phosphorus content was highest in T3, exceeding that of all other treatments except T1. The AP and AK contents of T4–T6 (72.3–78.5 mg/kg, 86.4–92.5 mg/kg, respectively) were significantly higher than those of T1–T3 (58.6–65.2 mg/kg, 68.3–75.1 mg/kg), indicating that a 5:1:5 ratio is more conducive to alleviating phosphorus fixation in acidic soils and improving potassium supply. The phosphorus activation coefficient was significantly lower in T0 and T1 soils compared to others (Fig. 4b). The total potassium content in T3, T4, T5, and T6 was significantly higher than in other treatments, except T1, with T3 again having the highest levels. The AK content followed a similar trend, with T3 leading, then T4, T5, and T6. The potassium activation coefficient in the soil of T3 was notably lower than in other treatments, followed by T4, T5, and T6 (Fig. 4c). The soil pH was highest in T0, followed by T4, T5, and T6. The organic matter content was significantly higher in the soils of T1 and T2 compared to other treatments (Fig. 4d).Fig. 4Changes in soil physical and chemical properties under different fertilization treatments. Discussion

Effects of optimized fertilization and deep fertilizer placement treatment on sugarcane yield and quality

Sugarcane yield and quality, comprehensive expressions of agronomic traits, serve as benchmarks for evaluating the crop’s merit and are critical sources of income for farmers. Plant height and stem diameter are valuable indices of sugarcane growth status. Typically, taller plants with thicker stems yield higher and better-quality sugarcane, whereas shorter plants with thinner stems correlate with lower yields and quality. This study found that a fertilization depth of 15 cm (T6 treatment) significantly increased the total seedling weight (35.26% increase) and stem nitrogen content (1.393 g/kg) of sugarcane, which is consistent with previous research results in different crops. Chen et al.5 found in summer maize that a fertilization depth of 15 cm promoted dry matter distribution and grain yield by increasing root length density and nitrogen accumulation, similar to the mechanism in which T6 treatment promoted nitrogen absorption in sugarcane (stem nitrogen content increased by 52.24% compared to conventional fertilization) in this study. Niedzi et al.25 pointed out in a potato pot experiment that a fertilization depth of 20 cm increased nitrogen and phosphorus uptake by 20% to 21%, and nitrogen accumulation in tubers. This is consistent with the significant increase in potassium content in sugarcane stems under T6 treatment (6.711 g/kg) compared to conventional treatment, indicating that deep fertilizer placement can improve absorption efficiency by optimizing the root nutrient contact area.

However, there are differences in the optimal fertilization depth for different crops. When Hou et al.26 studied rice, they found that the yield was highest at a fertilization depth of 5 cm (an increase of 15.1% compared to foliar fertilization), which is different from the optimal effect of T6 treatment on sugarcane in this study. This may be related to the distribution characteristics of crop roots—rice roots are shallower, while sugarcane, as a deep rooted crop, relies more on nutrients in the middle depth soil. A depth of 15 cm (in the main root distribution area) combined with a 5:1:5 ratio reduced the risk of nitrogen leaching by 38.6% (compared to shallow application without this ratio), and increased potassium utilization by 25.3%, confirming the scientific nature of the synergistic optimization of “fertilizer dose and depth layer”.Comparing the effects of other ratios (such as 2:1:2) in the literature8, this study demonstrates the regional adaptability of the ratio—in karst areas with strong acidity and high phosphorus fixation, 5:1:5 is the better choice.

The findings from this experimental study indicate that optimal yield and quality of sugarcane are achieved when the fertilization depth is closely aligned with the main root distribution area. Increasing nitrogen fertilizer within the required range for target yields results in an increase in both the number and total biomass of the main seedlings. Similarly, higher potassium fertilizer levels lead to increases in stem diameter, plant height, hammer weight, sugar content, and overall sugar accumulation. Thus, optimizing fertilizer ratios and application depths significantly enhances sugarcane yields and quality. This research provides valuable data and theoretical insights for studies focused on soil-sugarcane-nutrient interactions in specific karst regions. Our results are consistent with those of Menossi et al.27, supporting previous findings that nutrient uptake is integral to dry matter accumulation, which is essential for yield formation28,29. Additionally, varying the rates and depths of fertilizer applications has been shown to increase the nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium content in sugarcane stems, as well as the potassium levels in leaves, surpassing those observed with conventional fertilization methods. Optimizing fertilizer application rates and depths proves beneficial for enhancing nutrient uptake of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, aligning more effectively with the nutritional demands of sugarcane. Moreover, it allows for a reduction in the amount of chemical fertilizer used, thereby decreasing costs. These findings align well with those reported by Huang et al.30.

Effects of optimized fertilization and deep fertilizer placement treatments on soil fertility and enzyme activity

Soil nutrients are a crucial index of soil health. Additionally, soil enzyme activity serves as a significant marker of soil fertility and function, and it is a primary driver of nutrient cycling and metabolism in the soil28.

This study showed through fuzzy mathematics and principal component analysis that optimizing the nitrogen phosphorus potassium ratio (such as T4 treatment) and deep application (T6 treatment) significantly improved soil comprehensive fertility (IFI = 0.403 and 0.410, respectively), while conventional fertilization (T0) led to a decrease in fertility (IFI = 0.257). This is consistent with the study by Huang et al.30 in sugarcane, which found that optimizing fertilization reduces fertilizer input while improving nutrient utilization efficiency and reducing soil acidification risk. Research findings indicated that deeper fertilization significantly decreased the soil’s nitrate nitrogen, AP, and AK levels. This outcome contrasts with the results reported by Chen31, which suggested that deeper fertilization could increase these soil nutrient contents. The discrepancy may be attributed to variations in weather and rainfall conditions. Heavy, continuous rainfall could have leached the soil, thus reducing the availability of nutrients. These findings contribute to the understanding of how fertilization depth affects the soil-sugarcane-nutrient system in the acidic red soils of karst areas. By optimizing fertilization ratios and depths, overall soil fertility can be enhanced. The study demonstrated that such optimization improves both the quality and the comprehensive fertility of the soil in sugarcane fields.

Furthermore, urease participates in nitrogen transformation, while sucrase is associated with carbon metabolism. The increased activity of both indicates that deep fertilizer placement accelerates soil nitrogen carbon cycling, forming a synergistic effect with the increase of nitrogen and potassium content in sugarcane stems. The research showed that deeper fertilization significantly increases the levels of enzymes such as urease, sucrase, catalase, and acid phosphatase in the rhizosphere soil. Urease plays a vital role in catalyzing the decomposition of urea, while sucrose is involved in the transformation of organic matter. These findings align with those of Wenqing et al., who studied the impact of a cane-duck symbiosis on enzyme activity in sugarcane fields32.

This study made new progress in the optimization of sugarcane fertilization depth and dose through field micro area experiments. However, the impact of sugarcane fertilization depth and dosage on crop growth has not been optimized through long-term positioning experiments..

Conclusion

The study explored the impact of different fertilization ratios and depths on sugarcane yield, quality, soil enzyme activity, and soil physical and chemical properties.The synergistic mechanism of the nutrient ratio of 5:1:5 and 15 cm fertilization depth emphasizes its “quantitative effectiveness”: the ratio coordinates the relationship between “nitrogen promoting growth phosphorus resistance fixation potassium improving quality”, reduces nutrient loss through deep application, and ultimately achieves synchronous improvement in yield, quality, and soil fertility, providing specific parameters for quantitative fertilization of sugarcane in the local area Compared with the control (T0), the total yield of sugar cane treated with T6 reached 288.45 kg/30 m2, an increase of 39.32% compared with the control; the sucrose content reached 16.56%, an increase of 67.8% compared with the control, and the total sugar accumulation increased significantly by 22.3%. Soil nutrient analysis showed that this treatment increased the nitrate nitrogen, effective phosphorus and fast-acting potassium content by 45%, 32%, and 28%, respectively, and the activity of rhizosphere soil urease, sucrose and acid phosphatase increased by 11.0%, 17.5%, and 56.5% respectively compared with the control, significantly improving the nutrient conversion ability of the rhizosphere microenvironment. This optimized approach satisfies the nutrient demands of sugarcane more effectively, boosts overall soil fertility, and supports agricultural sustainability. The findings aim to determine the optimal fertilizer application ratio and depth, providing a scientific basis for the optimization of soil health maintenance of farmland ecosystems in Karst areas.

This study only targets a single sugarcane variety (Guitang46) and yellow–brown soil type. Further verification is needed to investigate the differences in response of different sugar accumulation characteristic varieties (such as high sugar and early maturing varieties) and red soil textures at the optimal fertilization depth, in order to improve regional fertilization techniques.
