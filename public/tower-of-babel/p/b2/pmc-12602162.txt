Evolutionarily New Solanaceous DCL2 Family Members Acquire Functions in Tomato
Authors: Jie Hao, Junfei Ma, Bin Liu, Jernej Jakše, Ying Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRNA silencing is one of the major defence activities against viral pathogens in plants. Silencing signals are initiated by Dicer‐like proteins (DCLs) to generate viral‐derived small RNAs (sRNAs). Viral sRNAs are then loaded into Argonaute proteins to form an RNA‐induced silencing complex to guide cleavage of target RNAs based on sequence homology. While the model regarding RNA silencing‐mediated defence against viral pathogens is largely established based on extensive studies using the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana , there are diverse sets of silencing components in other plants, especially in domesticated crops. Here, we tracked the expansion of solanaceous‐specific DCL2 genes during the course of evolution. We found that the DCL2a gene in tomato chromosome 6 is likely an evolutionarily new gene copy. We also found that DCL2b is more prone to be induced by viral pathogens in tomato plants, which is dependent on the combinations of cultivar and viral pathogen. Both DCL2a and DCL2b are critical to suppress the accumulation titre of a subviral agent, potato spindle tuber viroid (PSTVd). We noticed an unusually high accumulation of viral sRNAs shorter than 20 nt (16‐ to 19‐nt in length) in viroid‐infected tomato cv. Heinz 1706. Using synthetic small interfering RNAs, we demonstrated that shorter size sRNAs may also play a role in suppressing target RNAs, which can be interfered with by a viral suppressor of silencing, P19. Altogether, we provided further insights into the expansion of functional DCL2 family members in the Solanaceae family and their roles in combating viral and subviral agents.

RNA silencing plays essential roles in plant growth, acclimation to the surrounding environment and response to pathogen infections (Lopez‐Gomollon and Baulcombe 2022; Qiao et al. 2021; Axtell 2013; Yu et al. 2019; Guo et al. 2019; Simon and Meyers 2011). As a core factor in RNA silencing, Dicer‐like proteins (DCLs) cut double‐stranded RNAs (dsRNAs) into small RNA duplexes (e.g., microRNAs [miRNAs] and small interfering RNAs [siRNAs]). In Arabidopsis thaliana , four DCLs have been well characterised (Axtell 2013). DCL1 primarily generates miRNAs that are mainly 21 and 22 nt in length. DCL2 produces 22‐nt siRNAs involved in antiviral defence and secondary siRNA amplification. DCL3 produces 24‐nt siRNAs that guide RNA‐directed DNA methylation for transcriptional gene silencing. DCL4 produces 21‐nt siRNAs that mediate post‐transcriptional silencing, including antiviral responses and trans‐acting siRNA pathways. One strand from the small RNA (sRNA) duplexes is selectively incorporated into the Argonaute proteins (AGOs) to form the RNA‐induced silencing complex (RISC). The RISC then uses the sRNA to search for complementary homology sequences in other RNAs (targets) to guide cleavage of target RNAs (Axtell 2013).

In plant defence against viral pathogens, RNA silencing is the major player limiting the replication of viruses (Lopez‐Gomollon and Baulcombe 2022; Ding 2023). During RNA virus replication, their genome strand and the complementary intermediate strand form dsRNAs, which are diced by DCL2 and DCL4 to form 22‐ and 21‐nt viral short interfering RNAs (vsRNAs), respectively. DCL3 also recognises some viral dsRNAs and dices them into 24 nt vsRNAs. Notably, DCLs can also directly recognise the viral dsRNA genome regions to generate vsRNAs (Itaya et al. 2007). vsRNAs can be used by plant RNA‐dependent RNA polymerase 1 (RDR1) and RDR6 to prime single‐stranded viral RNAs to synthesise more dsRNAs for amplifying the viral signals. vsRNAs are loaded into multiple AGOs (e.g., AGO1, AGO2 and AGO5) to guide cleavage of viral RNA targets. This core model is mainly based on studies using A. thaliana as a model plant (Lopez‐Gomollon and Baulcombe 2022; Ding 2023). In A. thaliana , there are 4 DCL genes (DCL1, DCL2, DCL3 and DCL4), 6 RDR genes (only RDR1, RDR2 and RDR6 are functional), and 10 AGO genes (named from 1 to 10) (Zheng et al. 2015).

However, there are non‐canonical silencing factors for specialised functions. For instance, DCL5, unique to monocots, is essential to generate 24‐nt phasiRNAs that are mainly expressed in the reproductive organs (Patel et al. 2021). In rice, a unique AGO18 gene confers broad‐spectrum virus resistance by sequestering an endogenous miRNA (Wu et al. 2015). Therefore, less conserved RNA silencing components likely contribute to specific traits, including antiviral resistance.

Tomato genome has four DCL2 orthologues (Wang, Hardcastle, et al. 2018; Zheng et al. 2015), three of which are clustered in chromosome 11 and aligned in tandem. Based on the previous reports, they are termed DCL2b (Solyc11g008540), DCL2c (Solyc11g008520) and DCL2d (Solyc11g008530) (Figure 1). The other one (DCL2a) resides in chromosome 6 (Solyc06g048960). DCL2a and DCL2b have been characterised for generating endogenous noncanonical miRNAs (e.g., miR6026) (Wang, Hardcastle, et al. 2018). DCL2b is involved in combating viral infection, including potato virus Y (PVY), tobacco mosaic virus (TMV), tomato mosaic virus (ToMV) and potato spindle tuber viroid (PSTVd) (Wang, Hardcastle, et al. 2018; Suzuki et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2024; Wang, Deng, et al. 2018).

We aimed to track the evolutionary history of the expansion of DCL2s in the Solanaceae family. Based on the BLAST results using available genomic data in the Solgenomics database (Figure 1), we could only find one copy of the DCL2 gene with strong homology covering more than two‐thirds of the DCL2a sequence in the ancient solanaceous species (Huang et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2011) like Petunia axillaris (Peaxi162Scf00296g00099.1), Nicotiana benthamiana (Niben101Scf08272g00021.1) and Nicotiana tabacum (Nitab4.5_0001003g0020.1). There are two DCL2 copies in Capsicum annuum (pepper) (CA12g04390 and CA12g04400) and Solanum melongena (eggplant) (SMEL4.1_11g002290.1.01 and SMEL4.1_11g002310.1.01). In Solanum tuberosum (potato), there are three copies (Soltu.DM.06G011550, Soltu.DM.11G004150 and Soltu.DM.11G004160), and the copy in chromosome 6 emerged. Compared with the abovementioned species, potato and tomato diverged more recently in evolution (Yu et al. 2022). In wild tomato species ( Solanum pennellii and Solanum pimpinellifolium ), we found one copy in chromosome 6 and three tandemly arranged copies in chromosome 11, resembling the domesticated tomato. Therefore, it might be most parsimonious to reason that the canonical DCL2 copy resides in the region corresponding to tomato and potato chromosome 11, which duplicated locally to form the tandemly arranged DCL2 copies. The one in chromosome 6 emerged later during evolution. Interestingly, we could observe a partial copy of the DCL2 gene between the two copies in the eggplant genome (SMEL4.1_11g002300.1.01), implying that the duplication events independently occurred in various solanaceous species during the course of evolution.

In an effort to understand the function of those DCL2 family members in tomato plants, analysis of our published RNA‐Seq dataset (all RNA‐Seq and sRNA‐Seq dataset accession numbers listed in Table S1) showed strong induction of the tomato DCL2b, DCL2c and DCL2d but not DCL2a upon PSTVd infection in the cultivar Heinz 1706 (Figure 2A). This is interesting because it is unclear in the literature regarding whether Dicer‐like genes could be induced by pathogens or not. We then infected multiple tomato cultivars (procedures previously described in Takeda et al. 2018), extracted total RNA using RNAzol (Molecular Research Center) and performed reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) analyses using a commercial kit (New England Biolabs) to detect the expression of DCL2a, DCL2b and DCL2d (primers listed in Table S2). DCL2c was not included in this analysis because we could not design specific primers to specifically detect DCL2c due to its high homology to other DCL2 family members. It is important to note that we used Histone H2A (Solyc11g073250) instead of Actin as the internal control because Actin transcripts showed significant differential expression upon PSTVd infection in our RNA‐Seq dataset (Zheng, Wang, et al. 2017). Interestingly, DCL2 family members showed quite distinct expression patterns in commercial cultivars (Figure 2B). The induction of DCL2b and DCL2d was only significant in cv. Heinz 1706 and microTom. DCL2a was not induced in most tomato cultivars and exhibited a significant reduction in the cultivar Rutgers. In line with this data, a previous study also showed that DCL2a did not exhibit significant expression changes in cultivar MoneyMaker upon PSTVd infection (Suzuki et al. 2019). The corresponding titres of PSTVd in these plants were more or less similar based on RNA gel blots (procedures described previously in Ma et al. 2022), except for M82 (Figure 2C). We found M82 very difficult to infect with PSTVd and had low titres after infection. But still, a small portion of M82 plants did have high titres of PSTVd. Because DCL2 family members consistently showed very low expression in M82 plants, they are unlikely the causal factor for the low titres.

We expanded our RNA‐Seq analyses for public datasets, including tomato mosaic virus (ToMV)‐, tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV)‐ and tobacco rattle virus (TRV)‐infected tomato samples as well as potato virus Y (PVY)‐ and PSTVd‐infected potato samples (Zheng, Ding, et al. 2017; Wang, Deng, et al. 2018; Ross et al. 2022; Yue et al. 2021). Differential expression analysis was performed by using RNA‐Seq analysis tools incorporated in CLC Genomics Workbench/Server (v. 25.0.1) as described previously in detail (Secnik et al. 2025). Interestingly, DCL2b was commonly induced by various viruses in all infected tomatoes (Table 1). PVY but not PSTVd triggered a mild induction (~1.8 fold) of DCL2b in potato samples (Table 1). We also noticed that DCL2 genes were not listed as differentially expressed genes in PSTVd‐infected pepper samples in a previous report (Hadjieva et al. 2021). Therefore, the viral induction of solanaceous DCL2 family members depends on the combination of species/cultivar and viral pathogens. Nevertheless, the induction feature is more common in domesticated tomato plants.

Using previously reported Sldcl2a and Sldcl2b Crispr knockout lines in the tomato cv. M82 background (Wang, Hardcastle, et al. 2018), we analysed the viroid‐derived sRNA (vdsRNA) profiles in PSTVd‐infected plants 5 weeks post‐inoculation. The sRNA libraries were constructed as previously reported (Chen et al. 2012). Small RNA datasets were first checked for the presence of the adapters using FindAdapt tool (Chen et al. 2024) and piped for trimming to Cutadapt (Martin 2011) tool. Cleaned reads were mapped to reference viroid or viral genomes using the srnaMapper tool (Zytnicki and Gaspin 2022). The length of the mapped reads was counted and transcripts per million values calculated by custom bash script. The data showed that DCL2b is mainly responsible for generating 22‐nt vdsRNAs (Figure 3A), which is in agreement with previous reports (Wang, Deng, et al. 2018; Wang, Hardcastle, et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2024). At the same time, there was a surge production of 21‐nt vdsRNAs, implying that either DCL4 or DCL2a was complementing the loss of DCL2b. Given that DCL4 was normally not induced by viral pathogens, it is possible that DCL2a was responsible for generating some 21‐nt vdsRNAs in the loss‐of‐DCL2b plants. Interestingly, loss of DCL2a did not change the profile of 21‐nt vdsRNA production but led to a surge production of 22‐nt vdsRNAs (Figure 3A), implying that DCL2b was complementing the loss of DCL2a in these plants. The patterns of the absolute values of these vdsRNAs resembled their ratios (Figure 3B). In contrast, total endogenous sRNAs showed more or less similar ratio profiles among all infected wild‐type (WT), dcl2a and dcl2b plants (Figure 3C), further supporting that DCL2a and DCL2b function more potently on viroid RNA substrates. Noticeably, the surge production of 22‐nt vdsRNAs in the dcl2a plants or 21‐nt vdsRNAs in the dcl2b plants did not inhibit PSTVd replication, which was reflected in the relatively higher PSTVd titres in the dcl2a and dcl2b plants (Figure 3D). PSTVd titres in the dcl2a plants were significantly higher than those in the M82 WT plants. While PSTVd titres in the dcl2a plants were not significantly higher than those in the M82 plants, it is likely caused by one outlier plant that showed a higher accumulation titre (Figure 3D). This observation demonstrated that both DCL2a and DCL2b are needed for suppressing PSTVd replication. On a different note, we noticed that loss of either DCL2a or DCL2b led to significant reduction of 24‐nt vdsRNAs (Figure 3A,B), hinting that DCL3 acts in coordination with DCL2a and DCL2b in tomato plants.

Notably, we also found unusually high levels of smaller sizes of vdsRNAs in PSTVd‐infected Heinz 1706 tomato plants, ranging from 16‐ to 19‐nt (Figure 4A). This population was not found in the tomato samples infected with TRV that were processed at the same time (Figure 4A). This was unlikely to be caused by sample preparation because (1) all libraries were constructed at the same time and (2) total sRNA size distribution was highly similar among mock, PSTVd‐ and TRV‐infected samples (Figure 4B). We expanded the search to other combinations of viral pathogens and hosts in the public datasets (Zheng, Ding, et al. 2017; Wang, Deng, et al. 2018; Ross et al. 2022; Yue et al. 2021; Hadjieva et al. 2021; Thibaut and Claude 2018; Feng et al. 2014; Marquez‐Molins et al. 2023; Stajner et al. 2019). Due to the different sample handling procedures, some libraries have a clear cut‐off of 18 nt. To evaluate whether viral sRNAs smaller than 20 nt are only present in the tomato–viroid combination, we used the 19‐nt/21‐nt ratio to normalise these datasets. As shown in Figure 4C, we only found a high ratio in PSTVd‐ and CEVd‐infected tomato Heinz 1706 samples. We could not observe this trend in any virus‐infected samples or in PSTVd‐infected hosts other than tomato.

The biogenesis pathways of these 16‐ to 19‐nt vdsRNAs are not immediately clear. In dcl2a and dcl2b knockout plants, we observed inconsistent accumulation patterns of these sRNAs (Figure 3). Nevertheless, we are interested in understanding whether the smaller size class of sRNAs is still functional in guiding target RNA cleavage. We used a specific 17‐nt synthetic siRNA duplex (Itaya et al. 2007) to target GFP mRNAs in the protoplasts isolated from Nicotiana benthamiana line 16c using our established protoplast transfection protocol (Jiang et al. 2019), in comparison with the 21‐nt siRNAs (see Table S2 for sequences). Two days after transfection, GFP transcripts were detected by RNA gel blots using a specific riboprobe reported previously (Jiang et al. 2019). We also included a viral suppressor of silencing protein, P19 cloned from tomato bushy stunt virus (Qi et al. 2004), to assess the silencing effects. As shown in Figure 4D, 17‐nt siRNAs exhibited significant efficiency in suppressing GFP mRNAs in comparison to mock treatment without adding P19, although less efficient than the 21‐nt siRNAs. P19 could inhibit silencing activities of both 17‐ and 21‐nt siRNAs.

By and large, our data demonstrated that tomato gained a new functional DCL2a, the absence of which led to a surge of 22‐nt vdsRNAs for complementing tomato defence against PSTVd. In the dcl2b knockout plants, DCL2a may contribute to the surge production of 21‐nt vdsRNAs, which was normally generated by DCL4 (Katsarou et al. 2016; Dadami et al. 2013). On the other hand, our data also showed that DCL2 family members are more prone to being induced by viral infections. While the exact biological significance behind this phenomenon is not immediately clear, one possibility is that the induced expression of DCL2s upon viral infection may help balance plant growth and defence against viral pathogens. This is in line with the observation that the expression of DCL2 family members is normally suppressed by a tomato specific miRNA, miR6026 (Wang, Hardcastle, et al. 2018). In addition, our data showed unusually high accumulation of 16–19‐nt vdsRNAs in infected tomato cv. Heinz 1706. 17‐nt siRNAs can contribute to target RNA cleavage at the less efficient level as well, and this function is suppressible by the viral suppressor of silencing protein P19.
