Regulating white blood cell activity through the novel universal receptive system
Authors: Victor Tetz, Kristina Kardava, Maria Vecherkovskaya, Alireza Khodadadi-Jamayran, Aristotelis Tsirigos, George Tetz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The understanding of the mechanisms that control key features of immune cells in various disease contexts remains limited, and few techniques are available for manipulating immune cells. Thus, discovering novel strategies for regulating immune cells is essential for gaining insight into their roles in health and disease. In this study, we investigated the potential of the recently described Universal Receptive System to regulate human immune cell functions. This was achieved for the first time by specifically targeting newly discovered surface-bound DNA- and RNA-based receptors on leukocytes using endonucleases and generating “Leukocyte-Tells.” Using this approach, 1,496 genes were upregulated, many of which are related to immune cell signaling, migration, endocytosis, and phagocytosis pathways. The antimicrobial and anticancer activities of Leukocyte-Tells exceeded those of control leukocytes in vitro. Under some conditions, such as in antibiofilm experiments against biofilms of Staphylococcus spp., Pseudomonas spp, Candida spp., and other microorganisms, Leukocyte-Tells showed up to 1,000,000-fold higher activities than did control leukocytes. Additionally, Leukocyte-Tells exhibited significantly higher levels of TNF, IL-1β, IFN-γ, IL-6, and IL-10 production. Our findings reveal, for the first time, that the Universal Receptive System can orchestrate fundamental properties of immune cells, including enhanced antimicrobial and anti-tumor activities. This novel approach offers a new avenue for understanding the biology and regulation of white blood cells. Regulation of leucocyte activity is crucial for a properly functioning immune response and developing novel cell-based therapies. Our previous findings demonstrated the existence of nucleic acid-based receptors (Teazeled receptors, TezRs) that govern the responses of various cell types to a diverse array of environmental factors. Here, we discovered that by modulating these TezRs, we could upregulate immune cell signaling, migration, endocytosis, and phagocytosis pathways in leukocytes. By leveraging TezRs, we generated Leukocyte-Tells, which exhibited significantly higher antimicrobial and anticancer activities than the original immune cells.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21866-x.

Introduction

Despite substantial progress in developing novel techniques aimed at understanding cellular regulation and activity, a considerable knowledge gap remains in this area of research1. Commonly used related techniques for modulating white blood cell (WBC) activities affecting cell activation, proliferation, differentiation, and genetic editing are rooted in what is traditionally defined as “classical biology”2–5. Although numerous associated cellular pathways have been identified and described, the exact mechanisms underlying their interplay, coordination, and adaptation to various stimuli remain elusive6. These knowledge gaps limit the targeted modulation of cellular characteristics.

Our recent work describes, for the first time, a Universal Receptive System that is present in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms. This system is composed of extracellular DNA- and RNA-based elements with receptive and regulatory functions, designated as Teaseled receptors (TezRs), along with reverse transcriptases and recombinases, which are implicated in downstream signaling processes7,8. The discovery of DNA- and RNA-based TezRs as receptors and regulators, made by our team, was later supported using artificially constructed DNA receptors9.

Previously, we demonstrated the effectiveness of targeting the Universal Receptive System to promote the emergence of novel cellular states and properties without directly affecting the genome7,8,10–12. Specifically, we observed the system’s primary roles in cellular responses in prokaryotes and eukaryotes to various chemical, physical, and biological factors, as well as in controlling cell memory and forgetting. Moreover, we documented significant changes in the gene-expression patterns of genes involved in essential cellular processes, including energy metabolism, proliferation, apoptosis, and protein expression10.

Notably, our previous findings expanded the known role of the Universal Receptive System in controlling some of the fundamental properties of immune cells13. Specifically, we showed that the Universal Receptive System can control the production of diverse bioactive molecules (including antimicrobial and anticancer compounds) by leukocytes. Thus, our findings highlight the broad potential of targeted modulation using the Universal Receptive System to orchestrate diverse cellular processes. This approach substantially affects cellular activities and holds promise as a tool in “new biology” for engineering novel cellular states. Our findings also highlight novel roles of DNA and RNA, distinct from their traditional gene-encoding and gene-translating functions. This discovery was further supported by additional data revealing direct interactions between extracellular DNA and proteins, a phenomenon termed the “Pliers function.” These interactions can induce altered protein folding and the generation of novel protein isoforms, resulting in post-translational changes to extracellular proteins14–16.

Leukocytes comprise a heterogeneous population of highly specialized nucleated immune cells. They actively engage in immune surveillance and execute immune responses, enabling eradication of infectious pathogens and cancers. Three major leukocyte subpopulations (lymphocytes, monocytes, and granulocytes; distinguished by their cellular architecture, granularity, and nuclear morphology) operate in distinct ways to contribute to immune protection17,18. Some leukocytes directly recognize and eliminate infected or cancer cells through cytolytic activity and cytokine release, whereas others function indirectly by releasing danger signals, producing antimicrobial and anticancer peptides, mediating immune-cell recruitment, and serving as scavengers and antigen-presenting cells19. These highly regulated functions ensure control over diverse immunological threats and minimize the risks for undesired immune responses associated with various types of immunopathological responses.

Importantly, relatively few methods exist for modulating the fundamental properties of leukocytes ex vivo to increase their antimicrobial or anticancer activities. Those methods are primarily based on stimulation with cytokines and using different culture conditions20,21. For instance, the activity of T cells can be enhanced using a combination of anti-CD3 antibodies and a costimulatory signal (such as an anti-CD28 agonist) or natural killer cells stimulated with IL-2 and IL-15, as well as dendritic cells activated through Toll-like receptors interacting with pathogen-associated molecular patterns22,23.

Gene editing is another strategy used to modify the anticancer effector function of the immune system, with chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells representing the most notable strategy24,25. This approach has demonstrated efficacy in treating several blood tumors, including B-cell leukemias, lymphomas, and multiple myelomas26–28. CAR-T cells are generated by modifying patient-derived T cells to express engineered protein receptors, enabling them to eliminate target cells expressing the corresponding antigen29. Another approach utilized in experimental therapies involves the genetic modification of peripheral blood T cells ex vivo to express tumor-specific T cell receptors that target specific tumor antigens30,31.

Although these methods have significantly advanced the field of immune cell programming and modulation, they have remained confined to a relatively restricted spectrum of options, emphasizing the need for alternative approaches to regulate the fundamental properties of leukocytes32,33.

In this study, we discovered a critical and previously unknown role of the Universal Receptive System in orchestrating the key characteristics of human immune cells. Our findings demonstrate that deactivating the recently discovered TezRs led to the emergence of leukocytes (named here as “Leukocyte-Tells”) with higher antimicrobial and anticancer activities than described for WBCs. This discovery marks a substantial step forward in using a Universal Receptive System to orchestrate numerous critical pathways within immune cells, thereby enhancing our understanding of their biology in the context of various diseases.

Results

Leukocyte-Tells exhibited enhanced antimicrobial activity

First, we confirmed the presence of cell surface-bound DNA and RNA molecules on the surface of WBCs by analyzing changes in leukocyte fluorescence following treatment with 1 µg/mL DNase I and RNase A for 15 min. Propidium iodine (PI)-stained leukocytes displayed clear red fluorescence, confirming the presence of cell surface-bound nucleic acids, which were removed following nuclease treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1). To distinguish fluorescence due to cell-surface nucleic acids from fluorescence due to intracellular PI staining, we studied permeabilized leukocytes treated with Triton-X 100 to increase their membrane permeability34 (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Initially, we evaluated whether Leukocyte-Tells exhibited increased antimicrobial activity, ensuring that the process used to generate these cells did not affect their composition or viability (Supplementary Tables S1, S2).

We determined minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimal fungicidal concentration (MFC) values, representing the lowest concentrations of leukocytes required to completely inhibit bacterial and fungal growth, respectively35,36. The MICs of Leukocyte-Tells, Leukocyte-Control cells (both from pooled donors), and leukocytes derived from five individual donors (non-pooled) against various bacterial and fungal strains are shown in Table 1. Notably, Leukocyte-Tells exhibited 10–10,000 higher activity against all tested bacterial and fungal strains than the controls (Table 1). Moreover, the similarity between results obtained with non-pooled individual leukocytes and pooled Leukocyte-Control strongly suggests that the observed enhancement in antimicrobial activity of Leukocyte-Tells was independent of leukocyte pooling (Table 1).

We further assessed the growth-inhibitory effect of increasing the bacterial load, using S aureus MRSA VT 4490 as an example, in the presence of 105 Leukocyte-Tells or Leukocyte-Controls (Fig. 1B). Notably, bacterial-growth inhibition with Leukocyte-Controls was observed only when the bacterial load was less than 105 colony-forming units (CFUs). Starting with 105 CFUs, bacterial growth reached approximately 50% of that in the control group. In contrast, Leukocyte-Tells substantially controlled bacterial growth, completely inhibiting bacterial growth at the initial S. aureus inoculum of 105 CFUs and even at a higher initial inoculum of 107 CFUs (reducing the bacterial load to approximately 30% of the control level). We also analyzed whether a shorter timeframe of bacterial exposure to Leukocyte-Tells (6 h) would affect the MIC, thereby eliminating potential confounding effects of cell death, even though no significant cell death occurred in Leukocyte-Tells even after 24 h of storage at 37 °C (Supplemental Tables S2). Our data indicated that Leukocyte-Tells also exerted a significant growth-inhibitory effect on bacterial growth, even within a 6 h timeframe.

Similarly, using the agar-overlay method, we demonstrated that Leukocyte-Tells had greater antimicrobial activity than Leukocyte-Controls, even when challenged with a high inoculum of gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus) (Fig. 1C) or gram-negative bacteria (B. cenocepacia) (Fig. 1D)37.

Furthermore, we assessed the effect of Leukocyte-Tells on preformed biofilms, which show higher resistance to immune cells38,39. Leukocyte-Tells reduced the viable cell counts in 24 h old S. aureus biofilms by 100,000–1,000,000-fold more than control treatment did and were 100–10,000-fold more active than the Leukocyte-Controls (Fig. 1E). Even more striking differences were observed in 24 h old Candida albicans biofilms, which were particularly insensitive to Leukocyte-Controls (Fig. 1F). Under the same conditions, Leukocyte-Tells reduced the viable counts of fungal biofilms to 100,000–1,000,000-fold lower levels than in untreated biofilms and were 10,000–1,000,000-fold more effective than Leukocyte-Controls.

Thus, Leukocyte-Tells exhibited greater antimicrobial activity than did Leukocyte-Controls with various bacterial and fungal species, suggesting their enhanced potential for controlling high microbial burdens.

Leukocyte-Tells exhibited enhanced in vitro anticancer activity

Based on the results of our antimicrobial-activity studies, we investigated whether Leukocyte-Tells might exhibit higher anticancer activity relative to that of Leukocyte-Controls. To this end, we analyzed the anticancer effects of Leukocyte-Tells and controls against a non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell line (NCI-H1299) and a glioblastoma cell line (T98G).

Assessing tumor cell viabilities showed that the viabilities of the NCI-H1299 and T98G cells were significantly lower after treatment with Leukocyte-Tells than after treatment with the control-group cells (Fig. 2A, B).

Furthermore, NCI-H1299 monolayers treated with Leukocyte-Controls were uninterrupted and confluent, whereas those treated with Leukocyte-Tells exhibited extensive apoptosis, with numerous disrupted areas (Fig. 2C). Thus, the destruction of TezRs in leukocytes enhanced their activation state, which improved their anticancer activity.

Leukocyte-Tells showed altered gene-expression levels

Our analysis revealed 1,496 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between Leukocyte-Tells and Leukocyte-Control cells. Most DEGs (1,281 of 1,496) were upregulated in Leukocyte-Tells, although some DEGs (215 of 1,496) were downregulated (Fig. 3A).

To further contextualize gene-expression changes occurring in Leukocyte-Tells, we performed gene-set enrichment analysis40,41. Our analysis revealed enrichment for genes associated with numerous functional pathways in Leukocyte-Tells (Fig. 3B–D). The identified enriched pathways were grouped into three distinct and broad categories: immune cell signaling (Fig. 3B), immune cell migration (Fig. 3C), and cell membrane uptake (Fig. 3D). Within the cell-signaling group, key pathways enriched in Leukocyte-Tells included chemokine signaling, cytokine signaling, and mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling, all of which are indicative of increased Leukocyte-Tell activity42–44. Similarly, enrichment for pathways such as leukocyte transendothelial migration and actin cytoskeleton remodeling was suggested by the observation that terms associated with immune cell mobility were enriched, which is characteristic of acute inflammation-associated responses (Fig. 3C)45–47. Finally, enrichment for terms associated with endocytic and phagocytic pathways indicated that enhanced cell membrane uptake occurred, representing another key feature of different activated immune cell subtypes (Fig. 3D)48,49.

Next, we analyzed just the DEGs related to all three functional pathway categories (Fig. 3E–G). The most significantly upregulated genes in leukocytes within the immune cell-signaling pathway included numerous cytokines and cytokine receptors, such as CXCL17, CCR3, CCL4, and XCL2 (Fig. 3E)50–52. Additionally, leukocytes were characterized by significant upregulation of GHR and PTPRF, which can help initiate immune cell activation and regulate various immune-signaling pathways through the phosphorylation of target proteins, respectively (Fig. 3E)53,54. Some of the most highly upregulated genes in leukocytes during immune cell migration were ESAM, JAM3, RAP1GDS1, and RAPGEF5 (Fig. 3F)55–58. Finally, in terms of immune cell uptake, the most upregulated genes were associated with endocytosis, vesicle trafficking, and cytoskeleton reorganization, such as SLCO2A1, ANXA3, DNM3, and EHD3 (Fig. 3G)59–62. We also observed substantial alterations in energy-associated pathways in Leukocyte-Tells, marked by the upregulation of genes implicated in metabolism, such as GPD2, FAH, PYGL, and PDK2 (Fig. 3H)63–66. These genes have been associated with alternative energy supply pathways, notably glycolysis66.

We identified a substantial proportion of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) among the DEGs, including long intergenic non-protein-coding RNAs and pseudogenes (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). These elements function as master regulators of gene expression and play crucial roles in orchestrating complex cellular processes, such as apoptosis, growth, differentiation, and proliferation67–69. Interestingly, these ncRNAs and pseudogenes were the most significantly upregulated and downregulated DEGs overall in Leukocyte-Tells, respectively.

Thus, the Leukocyte-Tells showed gene-expression patterns were suggestive of an enhanced activation state70,71.

Leukocyte-Tells exhibited enhanced cytokine production upon stimulation

Cytokines play crucial roles in orchestrating diverse cellular processes within the immune system, including immune cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation72–74. Leukocyte-Tells exhibited greater phagocytic activity and production of granule enzymes

Gene-expression analysis clearly indicated that the Leukocyte-Tells showed enhanced phagocytic activity(Fig. 5). We compared their ability of Leukocyte-Tells to phagocytose E. coli with that of Leukocyte-Controls. Our findings confirmed the initial gene-expression analysis, revealing that Leukocyte-Tells showed approximately 20% higher bacterial internalization than their Leukocyte-Control counterparts (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Previously, we demonstrated the importance of the Universal Receptive System in regulating diverse cellular functions, including the production of antimicrobial and anticancer bioactive compounds (AABCs) by leukocytes7,13. In this study, we evaluated its impact on shaping crucial features of human immune cells.

By leveraging the Universal Receptive System, we generated Leukocyte-Tells with significantly higher antimicrobial and anticancer activities than the original immune cells. This was accomplished by selectively inactivating extracellular DNA- and RNA-based TezRs receptors. Depleting the TezRs led to the concurrent modulation of multiple pathways implicated in leukocyte immune responses, with substantial changes in gene-expression patterns and upregulation of key pathways associated with immune cell function, including migration, immune cell signaling, the use of alternative energy sources, and cell membrane transport.

In this study, we utilized leukocytes from multiple donors, which revealed consistent activation and highly reproducible patterns across all tested samples. To emphasize the reproducibility of the cell responses controlled by the Universal Receptive System, we suggest using the term “cell genome memory managing” to indicate that the induced cellular states are pre-programmed and can be reactivated.

The results of our in vitro antimicrobial studies represent a considerable advancement, revealing a stark difference in potency between Leukocyte-Tells and their control counterparts (which were isolated from the same multiple-donor group) and between individual, non-pooled leukocytes from different donors. Specifically, the Leukocyte-Tells demonstrated a striking increase in potency (up to 100,000-fold) against bacteria and fungi. Moreover, Leukocyte-Tells exhibited stronger antibiofilm activity against microbial communities, which are known to be resilient against immune cells39. Furthermore, the greater antimicrobial activity of Leukocyte-Tells persisted irrespective of microbial resistance to antibiotics, highlighting the robustness of immune cell-based therapies in overcoming bacterial resistance.

In addition to their enhanced antimicrobial activity, Leukocyte-Tells showed higher anticancer activity in vitro, as evidenced by a > 10,000-fold increase in efficacy against a p53-null NSCLC tumor cell line and a 100-fold increase in efficacy against a p53-mutant glioblastoma cell line (relative to those of control cells)79,80.

The question arises as to whether the observed changes were directly associated with the activity of the Leukocyte-Tells themselves or indirectly caused by the increased secretion of AABCs in response to stimulation, as observed previously81. The antimicrobial and anticancer activities of the AABCs isolated from Leukocyte-Tells, when applied in our lab to the same microbial strains and cancer cell lines without Leukocyte-Tells, were lower than those shown by the Leukocyte-Tells, indicating that the observed effects were not solely attributable to AABCs81. These results indicate that other types of antimicrobial and anticancer effects generated by the Leukocyte-Tells also contributed to the observed effects.

The pronounced antimicrobial and anticancer activities of Leukocyte-Tells may also be partially attributable to increased phagocytosis. Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis confirmed that key genes associated with phagocytosis were induced, including members of the Rho family of GTPases and phosphatases82. Although the observed increase in phagocytosis was statistically significant, its magnitude was highly unlikely to solely account for the multi-fold increase in the antimicrobial and anticancer efficacies of Leukocyte-Tells versus those of the controls. Moreover, the Leukocyte-Tells exhibited increased production of cytokines, including TNF, IL-1β, IFN-γ, and IL-6. This elevation in cytokine release was associated with the activation, proliferation, and enhanced cytotoxicity of leukocytes and the production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species74. In the future, we plan to study the antimicrobial and anticancer activities of Leukocyte-Tells in depth in relevant animal models.

Interestingly, based on transcriptomic profiling, the loss of TezRs in leukocytes led to the same pattern of gene upregulation related to energy metabolism and migration as previously observed in prokaryotes and other eukaryotes8. For example, certain bacterial species showed increased dispersion over unexpectedly long distances in response to TezR inactivation10. Similarly, we observed that multiple genes associated with migration and vascular extravasation were upregulated in Leukocyte-Tells. Furthermore, our previous findings revealed that modifying TezRs facilitated the growth of certain obligate aerobic bacteria in an anaerobic environment by upregulating glycolytic pathways or by activating glycolytic pathways in bacteria cultivated in an aerobic environment following the loss of TezRs8. The latter observation agrees with our current findings demonstrating that multiple genes associated with accelerated metabolism were upregulated and that alternative energy sources were utilized, including key glycolytic enzymes (i.e., ADH and PKM)83.

Importantly, in this study, we used leukocytes from multiple donors, which revealed consistent activation and a highly reproducible pattern across all tested samples. These data imply that the observed increase in anticancer and antimicrobial activities, although unusually high, was inherent to immune cells, as we did not modify the cellular genome; all modulations were performed by targeting TezRs.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that the distinctive attributes of Leukocyte-Tells hold strong potential for advancing the understanding of the full spectrum of roles played by the Universal Receptive System within cells. These observations warrant further evaluation of Leukocyte-Tells as antimicrobial and anticancer agents.

Materials and methods

Bacterial and fungal strains and culture conditions

S. aureus ATCC 29,213, E. faecalis ATCC 29,212, E. coli ATCC 25,922, and M. smegmatis ATCC 607 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). Clinical isolates were obtained from a private collection (Human Microbiology Institute, NY, USA), including S. aureus VT 209R (patient with skin and soft tissue infections), S. aureus MRSA VT 4490 (patient with pneumonia), Bacillus pumilus VT 1278 (patient with a respiratory infection), K. pneumoniae VT 1698 (patient with a urinary tract infection), and A. niger VT 1591 (patient with an invasive fungal infection). P. aeruginosa MR45, isolated from a patient with cystic fibrosis, was obtained from the CF Foundation Therapeutics Development Network Resource Center for Microbiology at Seattle Children’s Hospital (Seattle, WA, USA). C. albicans 3153 A was provided by Dr. Paul Fidel (Louisiana State University Health Sciences Center).

All bacterial strains, except for M. smegmatis ATCC 607, were passaged weekly on Columbia agar (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and stored at 4 °C. All liquid subcultures were derived from colonies isolated from these plates and were grown in Mueller Hilton broth (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), unless stated otherwise. For experiments involving solid medium, the bacteria were cultured on Columbia agar (Sigma-Aldrich). All cultures were incubated aerobically at 37 °C. M. smegmatis ATCC 607 was cultivated on Middlebrook 7H11 medium (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C.

All fungal strains were subcultured from freezer stocks on Sabouraud dextrose agar (Oxoid, UK) plates and incubated at 30 °C overnight to obtain yeast and mold colonies for subsequent liquid subcultures on Sabouraud dextrose agar (Oxoid, UK).

Cell lines

The H1299 (ATCC CRL-5803) and T98G (ATCC CRL-1690) cell lines were authenticated via short tandem-repeat profiling at our institute (both from ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA). For those experiments, the cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich), with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Invitrogen Corporation, NY, USA), 1 mM L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich), penicillin G (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) (all final concentrations in the medium) and grown in a humidified chamber (95% air, 5% CO2) at 37 °C. The cells were detached from their flasks using trypsin/ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (0.05%, Invitrogen). All cultures were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma. Possible contamination by mycoplasma strains in the cultured cells was assessed monthly, as previously described84. Cell viability was quantified using the trypan blue exclusion assay with an automated cell counter (Celldrop Brightfield Cell Counter, DeNovix, Wilmington, DE, USA), consistently showing > 90% viability.

Generating Leukocyte-Tells

Leukocytes and platelets were isolated from the buffy coats (BioIVT, PA, USA) of healthy preselected donors according to a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Human Microbiology Institute (approval number 6450). All donors gave written informed consent. The buffy coat donors had an age range of 18–65 years and were gender-neutral. The donors self-reported having no infections in the last 30 days and not taking any antibiotics, corticosteroids, or anticancer drugs during the last 90 days. The buffy coats were collected in compliance with PART 606 (Current Good Manufacturing Practice for Blood and Blood Components). To generate Leukocyte-Tells after RBC removal, the remaining cellular components (including leukocytes and platelets isolated from several donors) were treated with DNase I and RNase A (1 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), as previously described7,81. As control leukocytes (Leukocyte-Control cells), we used untreated leukocytes and platelets. To generate Leukocyte-Tells and Leukocyte-Control cells, we used leukocytes and platelets isolated from the same five random donors with differences in terms of their sex (three males and two females), age (24–51 years), and race (three Caucasian individuals and two black individuals). For experiments involving single donor-derived cells, leukocytes were isolated as described above, without being pooled. All cells were processed without freezing within 24 h of generation. The number of leukocytes in the samples was determined using an automated SYSMEX XN-330 hematology analyzer (Sysmex, Japan). Cell viability was assessed using a Nucleocounter NC202 (Chemometech), where a viability of > 70% was required for use in this study in accordance with the FDA’s recommendation as a minimum cell viability for final cell-based therapy products85. The viability of some Leukocyte-Tells was monitored after incubation at 37 °C for 6 h and 24 h. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Fluorescence microscopy

Lymphocytes were treated with DNase I and RNase A (1 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), washed three times with 1× PBS (Sigma-Aldrich), and stained with the membrane-impermeable dye, PI (10 µg/mL) for 10 min. The cells were then washed thrice using 1× PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 10 min. Following incubation, lymphocytes were placed in 1× PBS for 5 min and thoroughly washed three times using 1× PBS.

Lymphocytes-Control cells underwent the same process but without nuclease treatment.

Images were captured using a 63× immersion oil objective (Plan-Apochromat 63x/1.4 oil DIC M27, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) on an LSM880 laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss AG) and then visualized with a Texas Red/Cy3 filter set (excitation: ~535 nm, emission: ~617 nm). The fluorescent microscopy images were processed using ImageJ software for visualization purposes. Linear adjustments to contrast and brightness (to eliminate background noise) were made uniformly across all images for visualization with all test groups. No other post-acquisition processing or image manipulation was performed. Leukocyte-Control cells were not subjected to enzymatic treatment but were otherwise processed identically as the Leukocyte-Tells. A reduction in red fluorescence (when compared with that in the untreated control group) enabled visualization of changes in cell-surface bound DNA and RNA following DNase I and RNase A treatment. Control cells (not treated with nucleases) were stained with PI following membrane permeabilization. Specifically, 1 × 106 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde as described above, resuspended in 500 µL PI solution with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich), vortexed for 30 s, and incubated at 37 °C for 30–40 min in the dark. The cells then were processed in the same way as those not treated with Triton X-100 and visualized via fluorescent microscopy, as described above. Only linear adjustments to contrast and brightness were applied uniformly across all images for visualization. No other post-acquisition processing or image manipulations were performed.

Total RNA extraction and purification, library preparation, and sequencing

The cells were harvested via centrifugation at 300 × g for 15 min and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). RNA was extracted from leukocytes using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA). RNA quality was assessed spectrophotometrically at 230, 260, and 280 nm with a Promega GloMax Discover Microplate Reader (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer, and samples with RIN values > 8.0 were deemed of sufficient quality for downstream gene-expression analysis. Ribodepletion was performed using the Ribo-Zero Magnetic Gold Kit (Epicenter, Madison, WI, USA). A total of 30 ng of total RNA/per sample was used as input. Transcriptome sequencing libraries were prepared from RNA using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using a 2 × 150 nucleotide paired-end strategy (130 MM reads, maximum).

RNA-seq data processing

For FASTQ quality-control analysis, we used FastQC and CutAdapt86 to trim and remove the adaptor sequences. Sequencing reads were mapped to the reference human genome (GRCh38) using STAR aligner software (v2.5.0c)87. Alignments were guided using a gene transfer format (GTF) file (Ensembl v. GRCh38.85). The mean read insert sizes and their SDs were calculated using Picard Tools (v1.126; http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard). Read-count tables were generated using HTSeq (v0.6.0) with the GTF file mentioned above. The read-count tables were normalized based on their library size factors using DEseq2, and differential expression analysis was performed88,89. Transcripts with an adjusted p value of < 0.05 and a log2 fold-change value of ± 1.0 (2-fold) were considered significant DEGs. Reads-per-million-normalized BigWig files were generated using BEDTools (v2.17.0) and the bedGraphToBigWig tool (v4)90. Gene-set enrichment analysis was performed using the GSEA tool91. To compare similarity levels among the samples and their replicates, we used principal-component analysis and Euclidean distance-based sample clustering. All downstream statistical analyses and plot generations were performed using R (v3.1.1) (https://www.r-project.org/).

Estimating mics and MFCs in vitro

MICs for bacteria and MFCs for leukocytes were determined via serial microdilution in accordance with a modified version of previous Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute guidelines92. The standard inocula for testing bacteria, yeast, and mold cells were 5 × 105, 2.5 × 103, and 5 × 104 CFUs/mL, respectively. A 20 µL aliquot of the bacterial inoculum was added to the wells of 96-well plates containing 180 µL of leukocytes (1 × 108 cells/mL in RPMI-1640), serially diluted (10-fold) in RPMI-1640 medium. Plates with bacterial cultures were incubated at 37 °C for 6 or 24 h, and plates with fungal cultures were incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. Untreated leukocytes from the same set of multiple donors were used as a negative control (Leukocyte-Controls). MICs and MFCs were defined as the lowest dilutions of leukocytes required to completely inhibit visible bacterial and fungal growth, respectively93. To evaluate microbial viability, bacterial and fungal suspensions were serially diluted, and 100 µL of diluted suspension was spread onto agar plates. Columbia agar plates were used for bacterial cultivation, and Sabouraud dextrose agar was used for fungal growth. Plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight, and CFUs were counted on the next day.

Effect of leukocytes on bacterial biomass at planktonic growth

We inoculated wells of 96-well plates containing 180 µL of leukocytes (1 × 105 cells/mL in RPMI-1640) with 20 µL of bacteria (from 1 × 105 to 1 × 108 CFU/mL). The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h and the OD of the microorganisms was evaluated using a Promega GloMax Discover Microplate Reader. The values were subtracted from the mean OD600 of wells containing 1 × 105 leukocytes/mL.

Agar-preincubation method

Petri dishes were filled with 20 mL 1.5% agar. A pre-autoclaved, soft 0.7% agar overlay was melted at 60 °C in a water bath and allowed to cool down to 45 °C without solidification. One hundred microliters of the tester strain culture (107 bacteria/mL) and an equal volume of tested leukocytes (107 cells/mL) were added to the cooled soft agar (0.7%), vortexed, and poured onto the surface of a solid agar plate (1.5%)94,95. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h before the bacterial growth was assessed.

Effect of leukocytes on bacterial and fungal biofilms

We added 200 µL S. aureus VT 4490 or C. albicans 3153 A (each with a concentration of 5 × 105 CFUs/mL) in Mueller Hilton (Sigma-Aldrich) or Sabouraud dextrose broth (Oxoid), respectively, to the wells of 96-well flat-bottom microtiter plates35. Following a 24 h incubation at 37 °C, the resulting biofilms were washed twice with PBS to remove non-adherent bacteria. Some of the control wells were used to identify the number of viable counts in biofilms. The numbers of S. aureus VT 4490 and C. albicans 3153 A cells in the respectful biofilms after 24 h of growth were determined as 4.1 × 108 cells/mL and 6.2 × 109 cells/mL, respectfully. After the 24 h growth period, 200 µL leukocytes (1 × 105 cells/mL) in RPMI-1640 was added. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After exposure, the contents of the wells were aspirated, and each well was washed three times with PBS. The biofilms were scraped thoroughly, with particular attention being paid to the edges of the wells96. The well contents were aspirated and the total CFU numbers were determined via serial dilution and plating bacteria and fungi on Columbia agar and Sabouraud dextrose agar, respectively.

Tumor cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined by performing 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assays. Briefly, H1299 or T98G cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) without antibiotics. After 24 h of incubation (80% confluent), the growth medium was replaced with fresh medium without FBS but with increasing concentrations of leukocytes (103–108 cells/mL). The control wells contained increasing concentrations of untreated leukocytes. The plates were incubated for 24 h. Each well was supplemented with 20 µL MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA), followed by an additional 3 h incubation at 37 °C. Subsequently, the medium containing MTT was removed, and 50 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals with gentle shaking. To assess the percentage of live cells in the samples, the absorbance was measured at 570 nm, while accounting for the background absorbance of leukocytes and cancer cells. The same method was used to evaluate the leukocyte viability after heating or protease treatment.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)-release assay

Cell viability was assessed using the CyQUANT LDH Cytotoxicity Assay (Thermo Fisher). Briefly, H1299 and T98G cells (5 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates in RPMI-1640 medium. After a 24 h incubation, the growth medium was replaced with fresh medium containing increasing concentrations of leukocytes (103–108 cells/mL). Control wells also contained increasing concentrations of leukocytes. The plates were incubated for an additional 24 h, and LDH release in each well was assayed by measuring the absorbance at 490 nm. Cytotoxicity was determined by comparing LDH release with that of the positive control (cells treated with 1% Triton X-100) after subtracting the background values of leukocytes alone (not cocultured with cancer cells).

Phagocytosis assays

The phagocytic activity of leukocytes was assessed using E. coli cultures. WBCs were collected as previously described and seeded in black-walled 96-well plates for 2 h at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. The E. coli cells were stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC; Thermo Fisher) and washed three times to remove the unbound dye. Phagocytosis was assessed by subtracting the average fluorescence intensity of the ‘no-cell’ negative controls from those of the experimental wells. We incubated 1 × 104 leukocytes with Dextran-FITC (1 mg/mL) at 37 °C for 60 min, counter-stained the cells with an F4/80 antibody, and analyzed the cells using a BD FACSCanto flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson).

Cytokine detection

To evaluate cytokine secretion in response to LPS, 105 leukocytes were incubated with LPS (5 ng/mL) for 1 h. The concentrations of TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10 were measured in the culture medium using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (FineTest, Wuhan Fine Biotech) and a standard curve. The detection ranges of the assays were 15.625–1000 pg/mL for TNF-α, 15.625–1000 U/mL for IFN-γ, 3.906–250 pg/mL for IL-1β, 4.688–300 pg/mL for IL-6, and 1.56–100 pg/mL for IL-10.

Statistical analysis

At least three biological replicates were studied under each experimental condition in three independent experiments using material from at least five donors, unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as the mean value ± SD. Microbial quantification data were log10-transformed before analysis. The specific statistical tests used are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) or Excel 10 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) were used for statistical analyses and illustrations.

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Human Microbiology Institute.
