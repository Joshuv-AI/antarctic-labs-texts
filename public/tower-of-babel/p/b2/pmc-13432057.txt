Effect of root canal moisture on intratubular penetration of bioceramic sealer using two obturation techniques: A confocal laser scanning microscopic study
Authors: Maryam Jasim Al-Qaralusi, Hikmet Abdul-Rahim Al-Gharrawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Effective root canal obturation requires deep sealer penetration into dentinal tubules to prevent reinfection, a process that may be influenced by filling techniques and root canal moisture levels.Aims:This study aims to evaluate the influence of root canal moisture on the intratubular penetration of TotalFill bioceramic sealer using single-cone (SC) and hot-modified (HM) obturation techniques.Settings and Design:This was an in vitro study.Materials and Methods:Forty extracted single-rooted lower premolars were prepared to a size 40/0.04. The root canals were irrigated with 10 mL of 3% NaOCl, followed by 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, then randomly allocated according to obturation technique into SC and HM groups (n = 20). Each group was further subdivided according to canal moisture condition before obturation into dry or moist subgroups (n = 10). TotalFill sealer (FKG, Switzerland) was mixed with Rhodamine B and used for obturation. Specimens were sectioned at 3, 6, and 9 mm from the apex and analyzed by confocal laser scanning microscopy to assess maximal sealer penetration depth.Statistical Analysis:Data were analyzed by one-way and two-way ANOVA, accompanied by the Bonferroni post hoc test.Results:Moist canals demonstrated significantly greater sealer penetration than dry canals at three levels. The obturation technique showed no significant effect at any level (P > 0.05), and no interaction between moisture and technique was observed.Conclusions:Root canal moisture enhances penetration of TotalFill bioceramic sealer into dentinal tubules, regardless of obturation technique. Both obturation techniques yield comparable sealer penetration into dentinal tubules.

INTRODUCTION

The primary objective of the root canal procedure is to remove intraradicular pathogens and prevent reinfection. This can be predominantly accomplished through chemo-mechanical preparation combined with three-dimensional filling of the root canal system.[1] Root canal sealers are critical to enhancing the sealing quality of the obturation by filling the canal abnormalities and spaces between gutta-percha cones. Sealer infiltration into dentinal tubules will improve disinfection and enhance the long-term success of endodontic therapy by entombing any remaining microorganisms within these microscopic structures.[2]

The depth and extent of sealer penetration are also directly associated with improved mechanical interlocking with dentinal walls, resulting in better retention of the obturating material and reduced risk of microleakage. The capacity of a sealer to penetrate dentinal tubules directly affects its sealing efficacy and the overall integrity of the obturation.[3] The tubular infiltration of endodontic sealers depends on several factors, including their physicochemical properties, smear layer removal, humidity, dentinal permeability, anatomical root canal zone, and filling technique. Among the various factors influencing sealer penetration, the moisture condition of the root canal and dentinal tubules is particularly significant because it can impede the ingress of filling materials. Furthermore, dentin moisture affects dentin wettability and the interaction between the sealer and dentinal walls, thereby influencing sealer penetration, adhesion, and bond strength.[4]

In recent decades, numerous endodontic sealers have been developed in dentistry, including those based on calcium hydroxide, zinc oxide eugenol, silicone, methacrylate, glass ionomer cement, resin, MTA, and, more recently, bio-ceramic calcium silicate. Bioceramic sealers have favorable physicochemical properties, including high flowability, hydrophilicity, negligible shrinkage, and the ability to chemically bond to dentin; all these properties contribute to enhanced sealing ability and reduced microleakage.[5]

Although numerous studies have examined various properties of bioceramic sealers.[678] There is still limited research about the impact of residual canal moisture on the infiltration of bioceramic sealer into the dentinal tubules. Singh et al. investigated the impact of residual canal moisture on the infiltration of premixed bioceramic sealer into the dentinal tubules with the single cone (SC) obturation technique. Their findings demonstrated that residual moisture significantly enhanced dentinal tubule penetration. However, the sole application of the SC approach obscures the potential influence of alternative obturation techniques on sealer penetration.[9] The present in vitro experimental study aimed to evaluate the effects of root canal moisture on the penetration depth of TotalFill bioceramic sealer into dentinal tubules with SC and hot-modified (HM) obturation techniques using confocal laser scanning electron microscope (CLSM).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Institutional ethical committee approval

This in vitro experimental research was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Project No. MUOPR54, Ref. No. REC130 on May 30, 2025).

Sample size calculation

The sample size was obtained using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität, Germany) software. A minimum ideal sample size of 20 per group had been required, yielding a total sample size of 40 teeth with an alpha error probability of 0.05.

Sample selection and preparation

Forty permanent, single-rooted lower premolars extracted for orthodontic purposes from patients between the ages of 18–25 years were chosen. The criteria for sample selection were mature apices, centrally located apical foramina, straight roots, single and patent canals, and teeth with root caries, resorption, cracks, or previously treated were excluded from the study caliper. The teeth were cleaned of debris and calculus, stored in a 0.1% thymol solution until use. The crowns were removed using a low-speed diamond disc at the cementoenamel junction to standardize root length at 13 mm.[10]

Root canal preparation

The pulpal tissue was eliminated using a barbed broach (Dentsply Maillefer, USA), and a size 10 K-file (MANI, Japan) was subsequently employed to confirm apical patency and define the position of the apical foramen. The EDGEENDO X7 (EDGEFILE®, U.S.A.) rotary system files were employed sequentially, starting with 20/04, followed by 25/04, 30/04, and 35/04, and finishing with 40/04. Each file employed during instrumentation was subsequently followed by irrigation of the canals with 3% sodium hypochlorite using a side vented needle with a gauge of 30.[11] Passive ultrasonic irrigation (E98, Woodpecker, Germany) was used to activate sodium hypochlorite in the root canal for 30 s. Subsequently, the specimens were irrigated with 3 mL of 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid to eliminate the smear layer. A final cleansing of the root canal was performed with 10 mL of normal saline.[9]

Grouping and drying protocols

Forty roots were randomly divided into two main groups (n = 20). According to the obturation technique, include the following groups: Group A: Single-cone (SC) obturation; and Group B: HM obturation. Each group was further subdivided, based on root canal moisture before obturation, into dry or moist subgroups (n = 10), resulting in four experimental groups: A1 (SC-dry), A2 (SC-moist), B1 (HM-dry), and B2 (HM-moist).

Moisture standardization

In the dry subgroup, the canal was dried using a size 40 taper 0.04 paper point (Dia Dent, Korea) until the last paper point was completely dry. In the moist subgroup, the irrigating solution in the root canal was removed using an endo aspirator (CERKAMED, Poland) for 5 s, followed by a single paper point for 1 s.[12]

Sealer preparation and obturation

The specimens were obturated by mixing TotalFill sealer (FKG, La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland) with 0.1% Rhodamine B (Himedia, India) to enable fluorescence under CLSM. The dye was weighed precisely on an analytical balance and incorporated into the sealer on a sterile glass slab with a spatula until a homogeneous mixture was achieved. The same mixture was used for all samples to ensure standardization.[13] All procedures were performed by a single-experienced endodontist to eliminate operator variability. In the SC group, a prefitted master cone (Dia Dent, Korea) has been introduced into the operating length and checked for tug-back. Subsequently, a lentulo size 30 (Mani, Japan) was employed to introduce sealer into the root canals. The apex of the pre-fitted master cone has been coated with TotalFill sealer and filled to the entire working length. The cone was subsequently cut and sealed at the canal orifice using a heated plugger.[13]

In the HM group, obturation was performed with System B (Eighteeth, China). To verify the tug-back, the pre-fitted master cone has been introduced into the operating length and checked for tug-back. Subsequently, a lentulo size 30 was used to insert sealer into the root canals. The apex of the pre-fitted master cone has been coated with TotalFill sealer and filled to the entire working length. The coronal portion of the cone was resected utilizing a heated fast-pack tip set at (180°C) positioned 6 mm short of the operating length, followed by vertical compaction with a plugger for 4 s. Backfilling was performed using the Fast-Fill system at 180°C until the canal was completely filled.[14] Following obturation, the access cavities of all samples were sealed with a temporary cement barrier (Medicem, Germany) and stored in an incubator at 37°C with 100% humidity for 1 week.[15]

Sample preparation for confocal laser scanning microscopy analysis

All the specimens were inserted into a mold made of clear, cold-cure acrylic. Then, specimens were sectioned perpendicular to their longitudinal axis at 3 mm, 6 mm, and 9 mm from the root tip using a microtome (Mecatome T210, Presi, France) to obtain three slices of 1 mm thickness. All of the slices were analyzed using CLSM (LEICA DMi8, JH technology, Germany), performed at ×10 magnification [Figure 1]. The excitation and emission wavelengths for Rhodamine B were set at 532 nm and 580 nm, respectively.[16] Digital images were captured and imported into ImageJ (V1.53K, National Institutes of Health, USA) for quantitative analysis of sealer penetration. Sealer penetration depth was evaluated at the buccal, lingual, mesial, and distal aspects of each section. In each region, ten standardized measurement points were selected, and the penetration depth was determined from the canal wall to the extent of red fluorescence using confocal microscopy. The mean value of the 10 measurements was calculated for each region. Subsequently, the average of the four regional means was computed to get a single mean penetration depth value for each root canal section (coronal, middle, and apical), expressed in micrometers (μm).[1517] The measurements were made by operators blinded to which samples corresponded to which group. The measurements were repeated twice to ensure reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed employing SPSS software version 27 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assured using the Shapiro–Wilk test (P > 0.05). A two-way (ANOVA) test was used to determine the presence of a significant difference in sealer penetration among four groups at three levels. A one-way (ANOVA) test was used to determine the presence of a significant difference in sealer penetration among three levels at each group. Post hoc pairwise comparisons (Bonferroni) were performed to test multiple comparisons among levels. The statistical significance level is set at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of the data in this study, confirming normal distribution (P > 0.05). The mean ± standard deviation of sealer penetration in (μm) for the four groups at three levels (Apical, Middle, Coronal) is presented in Table 1. One-way ANOVA revealed a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05) in sealer penetration among the three levels within each group. Post hoc pairwise comparisons (Bonferroni test) revealed a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05) in the amount of sealer penetration between the apical and middle levels and between the apical and coronal levels. While there was no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05) detected between the middle and coronal levels, as shown in Table 1. Two-way ANOVA revealed that moisture had a statistically significant effect (P < 0.05) across the three evaluated levels (apical, middle, and coronal). In contrast, the main effect of the technique was not statistically significant at any level (P > 0.05). Furthermore, no significant interaction between moisture and technique was observed (P > 0.05), as shown in Table 2.

DISCUSSION

The principal aim of root canal filling is to block a pathway between the root canal system and peri-radicular tissue, thereby establishing a barrier against reinfection. Permeation of root canal sealers into the dentinal tubules is essential to establish a secure bond between the root canal filling materials and dentin, facilitate sealing, and prevent the colonization of residual pathogens and reinfection of the root canal system.[18] The results demonstrated that root canal moisture significantly enhanced sealer penetration at all root levels, whereas no significant difference was observed between SC and HM obturation techniques.

The improved penetration under moist conditions can be clarified by the hydrophilic nature of calcium silicate-based sealers, which allows them to flow more readily in the presence of moisture.[19] These materials depend on moisture to initiate and sustain the hydration process. On exposure to water, calcium silicate particles undergo hydration, yielding calcium silicate hydrate gel and calcium hydroxide as by-products. The synthesis of calcium hydroxide results in minimal volumetric expansion, which may improve sealer adaptation by reducing interfacial gaps between gutta-percha and dentinal walls.[20] In the present study, the moist canal condition was established using endodontic aspiration followed by a single paper point, aiming to simulate a clinically relevant level of residual moisture rather than complete dryness.[21] However, it should be acknowledged that this approach represents an operational definition of moisture and may not fully reproduce the complex in vivo environment, where dentinal fluid, apical anatomy, and potential communication with periapical tissues can influence moisture levels.

Furthermore, the moisture conditions in this study were standardized procedurally rather than quantitatively, and residual moisture within the canal was not directly measured. This may affect the reproducibility of the experimental conditions and limit the clinical interpretation of the findings. Excessive canal drying, particularly with paper points, may diminish the moisture required for proper sealer hydration. Inadequate moisture can impede flow and hinder effective penetration into dentinal tubules.[22] Previous research indicates that residual dentin moisture significantly influences the interaction between radicular dentin and bioceramic sealers.[91223]

However, the present results contrast with those of Pooja et al., who reported that the premixed bioceramic sealer had higher bond strength in a dry canal than in a moist canal. Differences in sealer formulation, outcome measures (bond strength), and experimental protocols may explain this discrepancy.[24] Mirzaei Sadeghloo et al. investigated the relationship between bond strength and dentinal tubule infiltration using different sealers. Their research demonstrated a beneficial association between the strength of push-out bonds and dentinal tubular infiltration. This demonstrates the clinical importance of sealer penetration in enhancing adhesion.[25]

Regarding obturation techniques, no significant difference in dentinal tubule penetration was observed between SC and HM techniques at any root level. This finding may be explained by the inherent physicochemical properties of calcium silicate-based sealers. Their fine particle size allows penetration into dentinal tubules measuring approximately 2–3.2 μm in diameter, even without the application of compaction pressure. In addition, the alkaline pH of these sealers may contribute to collagen fiber denaturation, while their high flowability, low contact angle, and slight volumetric expansion.[26]

The use of heat with calcium silicate-based sealers remains controversial. While some studies suggest that these materials tolerate heat, elevated temperatures may alter physicochemical properties such as viscosity, flow, setting time, and film thickness, potentially influencing sealer penetration. Despite these considerations, the HM technique was included to reflect modern clinical practice, in which clinicians combine bioceramic sealers with warm obturation to enhance the three-dimensional infill of the root canal system.[14] The present findings agree with those of Reynolds et al., who also reported that the obturation technique (SC or warm vertical compaction) did not influence the depth of dentinal tubule penetration.[13] Conversely, previous studies have demonstrated that penetration of calcium silicate-based sealers into dentinal tubules affected by the obturation technique.[2728]

Sealer penetration was evaluated using CLSM, which enables nondestructive, high-resolution visualization of Rhodamine Blabeled sealer within dentinal tubules. Unlike SEM, CLSM minimizes specimen preparation artifacts and enables threedimensional reconstruction of penetration patterns, providing more accurate and comprehensive quantitative measurements.[29] In the current study, all experimental groups demonstrated increased sealer penetration depth at 6 mm and 9 mm levels compared with 3 mm level. The results align with prior research.[123] This phenomenon can be elucidated by the decrease in both the quantity and diameter of dentinal tubules in the apical area relative to the coronal section of the root. The presence of sclerotic dentin and cementum-like tissue in the apical third may occlude dentinal tubules, creating regions lacking tubules and restricting sealer penetration. A further contributing aspect may be the diminished effectiveness of irrigants in eliminating the smear layer in the apical third relative to the coronal region.[9]

This study has certain limitations, including the use of single-rooted premolars, a single bioceramic sealer formulation, and two obturation techniques, which inherently restrict the applicability of the results. Furthermore, as an in vitro study, the findings may not entirely reflect clinical variables such as temperature, humidity, or variations in anatomical complexity. Future research should incorporate multi-rooted and molar teeth, evaluate a broader range of bioceramic and conventional sealers, and explore additional warm obturation techniques.

CONCLUSIONS

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, the moist canals revealed better dentinal tubule infiltration with TotalFill bioceramic sealer compared to dry canals at all root levels. Both SC and HM obturation procedures generate similar sealer infiltration into dentinal tubules. Overall, higher sealer penetration was observed in the coronal and middle thirds compared to the apical third.
