Stage of manual skill acquisition with Simodont virtual reality simulator for pre-clinical dental training: an interventional matching study
Authors: Uziel Jeffet, Nir Uziel, Noga Retzkin Binyamin, Ido Vana, Noa Sadan, Yoav Shapinko, Shifra Levartovsky, Tamar Brosh, Diva Lugassy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of virtual reality (VR) simulators in dental education. However, these studies primarily focused on the early stages of psychomotor skill acquisition and often did not account for confounding variables when assigning participants to study groups. The present study aims to determine the stage of manual skill acquisition at which VR simulator training is most effective, using a matched interventional study design.MethodsA planned crossover study with a matched design was conducted among 70 undergraduate dental students from Tel Aviv University. Matching was performed based on age, gender, dominant hand, spatial perception, and fine motor skills. To evaluate manual performance under both direct vision (Class I cavity preparation) and indirect vision (Class 1 occlusal-proximal [OP] preparation), students were evenly assigned to two groups (Group 1 and Group 2), matched to control for potential confounding factors. Using a crossover approach, each group alternated roles as the experimental or control group for the two phantom-based tasks. The experimental group trained with both the Simodont virtual reality simulator and the conventional phantom simulator, while the control group practiced solely with the conventional simulator on plastic teeth. The experimental group was further subdivided into three subgroups based on the timing of Simodont exposure corresponding to different stages of skill acquisition: cognitive, associative, and autonomous.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed between the experimental and control groups in manual performance scores under direct vision (p = 0.461) or indirect vision (p = 0.916). Additionally, no significant differences were found among the three experimental subgroups representing different stages of skill acquisition (p > 0.05).ConclusionThe integration of the Simodont simulator during the preclinical year does not appear to enhance dental students’ manual performance under either direct or indirect vision conditions.

Introduction

Dentists must demonstrate a high level of manual dexterity and proprioceptive awareness to perform dental procedures effectively. Therefore, dental students are required to develop manual skills during the early stages of their dental education. Traditionally, the acquisition of these skills in most dental schools worldwide is based on exercises performed on plastic teeth mounted in a “phantom head simulator" [1]. During the pre-clinical year of the dentistry degree, students are introduced to a conventional simulator with a phantom head, which simulates dental procedures on plastic teeth using dental instruments such as turbines and handpieces. However, the conventional simulator presents limitations, including insufficient tactile feedback, prolonged learning curves, and the ability to simulate only simplified tooth structures within the phantom model.

Due to the complexity of manual skill acquisition, a considerable proportion of dental students fail pre-clinical courses and are not permitted to progress to the subsequent year, which involves treating patients [2]. Recent technological advances have led to the development of virtual reality simulators, which have been reported to accelerate and improve the acquisition of manual skills in phantom courses [3, 4].

The most common virtual reality simulator used in dental education is the DentSim computer-assisted simulator (Image Navigation Ltd., New York, USA), which consists of a simulated patient with a head and dentoform, and two infrared cameras that transmit beams to two computers to generate a three-dimensional (3D) virtual image of the tooth preparation, the preparation process, and performance grading [5]. The newly developed Simodont (NISSIN Dental Products, New York, USA) simulator is based on haptic (touch) technology and includes a force-feedback robotic arm connected to software that creates a realistic experience of oral treatment procedures [4]. The system enables repeated training with tactile feedback for drilling into various tissues, supporting the development of self-assessment skills and reducing dependence on clinical instructors [6]. Although the ultimate goal of virtual reality simulators is to replace conventional phantom training, most studies suggest that these innovations should not be used in isolation. Instead, they should supplement the traditional phantom laboratory due to limitations in task validity assessment and the high cost of initial setup and maintenance [7–9].

Wei et al. [10] demonstrated that dental students who received additional training with the Simodont simulator showed significantly improved cavity preparation performance compared to students trained exclusively with the conventional phantom simulator. De Boer et al. [11] examined the effect of Simodont training on dental students with no prior manual skill experience and found that only students who trained with Simodont were able to pass the manual skills test. Yuan et al. [12] investigated the effectiveness of Simodont training in preclinical students for teaching cavity preparation after the theoretical knowledge of the task had been standardized. They concluded that the Simodont simulator significantly improved cavity preparation performance compared with the conventional phantom simulator alone.

Notably, these studies primarily evaluated cavity preparation performance during the initial, cognitively stage of skill acquisition and did not assess whether the reported benefits have an impact on more advanced stages of motor learning that are essential for achieving full skill competence. Furthermore, in these studies, experimental and control groups were assigned randomly without matching specific characteristics (e.g., age, gender, fine motor skill competence, spatial perception, and dominant hand), which are recognized contributors to manual performance and may therefore act as confounding variables in evaluating the effects of simulator training and potentially affect the internal validity of the findings.

Recent studies have reported favorable outcomes associated with the use of haptic virtual reality simulators in preclinical endodontic training. Sánchez-Herrera et al. [13] evaluated the effect of a haptic virtual simulator on endodontic access cavity preparation and reported improved performance following simulator training. Similarly, Usta et al. [14] concluded that the integration of a haptic simulator into preclinical endodontic education has a positive effect on students’ manual dexterity. Likewise, these studies were conducted during the initial stage of skill acquisition and moreover, in the former study, groups allocation were based only on prior preclinical grades, whereas in the latter study, participants were assigned through randomization.

According to Ackerman’s theory, skill acquisition consists of three stages [15]: (1) Cognitive stage – the initial phase of learning, in which the learner must understand instructions to perform a given task; (2) Associative stage – the intermediate phase, in which perceptual speed and efficiency are developed to perform the task more quickly and accurately; and (3) Autonomous (automatic) stage – the final phase, in which task performance relies predominantly on psychomotor abilities and less on cognitive processing.

Since prior research has concentrated on the cognitive stage of skill acquisition, where performance is strongly influenced by individual differences, it is uncertain whether these benefits extend to later stages of skill acquisition characterized by greater automatization and reduced inter-individual variability. Consequently, a gap exists in the literature regarding the stage of manual skill acquisition in dental students at which additional training with the Simodont simulator is most effective.

Recently, the Simodont simulator has been introduced at our University School of Dentistry to enhance preclinical training in cavity preparation, serving as a supplementary tool to the conventional phantom simulator. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to determine whether additional training with the Simodont simulator improves the manual dexterity of preclinical dental students compared to training with the conventional phantom simulator alone. This study also aimed to identify the stage of manual skill acquisition at which Simodont training is most beneficial. Based on the limitations of previous studies and the control of baseline potential confounding factors in the present design, we hypothesized that additional Simodont training would not significantly improve cavity preparation performance compared to conventional phantom training alone.

Methods

Participants

A planned-crossover study with a matched design was conducted during the 2025 academic year among 70 third-year undergraduate dental students. All participants were in their first year of practicing on a phantom head and had not received any prior manual dexterity training for dental procedures using dental instruments.

Prior to commencing the study, all participants provided written informed consent, approved by the Ethics Committee (No. 0009807-1), after the principal investigator had explained the study’s aims and procedures.

Inclusion criteria consisted of third-year dental students who had signed informed consent and had no previous manual dexterity training. Exclusion criteria included a history of vision-related pathology (e.g., strabismus, amblyopia, anisometropia, glaucoma, or cataract) or dexterity-related disorders (e.g., carpal tunnel syndrome). The entire class volunteered to participate in the study, preventing selection bias.

Purdue Pegboard Test

All students underwent a validated assessment at the beginning of the study, prior to commencing the phantom head simulation course. This assessment included the Purdue Pegboard Test under direct vision to evaluate baseline fine motor skills and under indirect vision to assess baseline spatial perception.

The Purdue Pegboard Test [16] is a reliable and valid occupational therapy assessment tool (Fig. 1). It consists of a board with two vertical columns of holes, and four cups located distally: the right and left cups each contain 25 pins, while the remaining two cups hold washers and collars, respectively. The test comprises four distinct tasks. In the first three tasks, as many pins as possible must be inserted into the board’s holes within 30 s; in the final task, the time limit is 60 s. The score for each task is determined by the number of pins inserted. The four tasks are as follows:

The Simodont (NISSIN Dental Products, New York, USA) is a three-dimensional (3D) virtual reality haptic simulator designed for intensive preclinical training of dental students and for the assessment of their manual skills. The system comprises two computers: a graphics PC and a panel PC. It utilizes two separate projectors to generate 3D images, which are viewed through specialized 3D glasses. To replicate a realistic clinical experience, the Simodont incorporates a handpiece gimbal, a hand mirror gimbal, and a horseshoe-shaped finger rest area, with virtual exercises displayed on a high-resolution 3D screen. The software version used was 4.20.0.15. For the manual tasks, the scenarios CAR P7-36-01 (tooth No. 36) and CAR P7-16-01 (tooth No. 16) were employed. Prior to each session, the Simodont (v12.22.12) was calibrated using the integrated Calibration Wizard. This standardized procedure aligned the handpiece and mirror movements with the virtual environment and synchronized the haptic force feedback to ensure anatomically accurate tactile sensations.

Research methodology (Fig. 3)

Course of study

Before performing the first task on plastic teeth mounted in a mannequin head (Nissin Dental Products INC, Nakagoku, Japan), all dental students received theoretical instruction and a live demonstration of the manual task by a clinical instructor. For the assessment of the first phantom task under direct vision (Class I cavity preparation on tooth No. 36, with a depth of 1.5–2.0 mm and a width equal to 1/4 of the intercuspal distance) and the second task under indirect vision (OP preparation on tooth No. 16, with a depth of 1.5–2.0 mm and a minimal distance of 1.5 mm from the distal marginal ridge), the 70 students were matched (gender, age, dominant hand, baseline fine motor skills and spatial perception assessed by Purdue Pegboard Test under direct and indirect vision) into two equal groups (Group 1 and Group 2, n = 35 each) to minimize bias from potential confounding factors. Using a crossover design, Groups 1 and 2 alternated roles as the experimental and control groups for these two phantom tasks.

For the first phantom task, the Class I preparation, Group 1 (experimental group) trained with both the Simodont simulator and the conventional phantom head simulator for drilling on plastic teeth, while Group 2 (control group) practiced exclusively with the conventional phantom head simulator. Group 1 received an additional 120-minute training in a single session on the Simodont, which simulated a Class I cavity preparation using haptic technology. The virtual exercise replicated the same task performed in the phantom lab, requiring students to prepare a Class I cavity preparation on the same tooth and according to the same guidelines used for access cavity preparation (outlines of cavity preparation, proportion of cavity walls, cavity depth, line angles of the cavity, directionality of the gingival floor and axial wall and injury to adjacent tooth) but in a virtual reality environment.

In the second phase of the crossover design, Group 2 served as the experimental group and Group 1 as the control group for the OP preparation on tooth No. 16.

For each phantom task, all students completed three consecutive phantom laboratory sessions (240 min per session), corresponding to the cognitive, associative, and autonomous stages of skill acquisition. Accordingly, both the experimental and control groups received identical training duration in the conventional phantom head simulator (total of 720 min). Only students in the experimental group received an additional 120 min training session using the Simodont virtual simulator, which was scheduled to occur at one of the three stages (cognitive, associative, or autonomous). Therefore, the experimental group in each phase was further subdivided into three subgroups according to the timing of Simodont training relative to the stages of skill acquisition, based on Ackerman’s three-stage model [15]:

Cognitive stage group – The first laboratory session corresponded to the cognitive stage: immediately after receiving theoretical instruction and a demonstration of the task, before beginning practical execution.Associative stage group– The second laboratory session corresponded to the associative stage: after receiving theoretical instruction and demonstration and following initial practice of the procedure.Autonomous stage group– The third laboratory session corresponded to the autonomous stage: after receiving theoretical instruction and demonstration, and near completion of the practice phase.

The students’ manual scores were derived from the final exam performed at the end of each manual task training in the conventional phantom head laboratory and were compared between the control and experimental groups. Three independent instructors graded each task according to standardized criteria, including cavity outline and depth. All instructors were trained and calibrated on the scoring protocol prior to the study. Scores were given on a continuous scale from 0 to 100, with a passing threshold of 60. Student identification numbers were coded, and the instructors were blinded to both the students’ identities and their group assignments.

The Manual score at phantom laboratory was defined as the primary outcome, while the Purdue Pegboard test under direct and indirect vision served as a secondary measure of manual dexterity.

Statistics

Assumptions of normality for the study variables were evaluated using Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. For internal consistency of the feadback questionnaire, Cronbach’s Alpha level was calculated for the total 5 items of the questionnaire.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for both groups regarding the independent variables: age, gender, dominant hand, basic fine motor skills, and spatial perception.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (Version 29.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

For comparisons between the experimental and control groups, Mann–Whitney tests were applied for age and scores on the Purdue tests under both direct and indirect vision conditions. Chi-square tests were used to analyze the distribution of gender and hand dominance between groups. Kruskal–Wallis tests were employed to compare data among the three sub-groups (cognitive stage, associative stage, and autonomous stage groups). A linear mixed-model analysis was conducted to account for the crossover design of the study on the manual grades, in which each group alternated roles as the experimental or control condition for the two manual tasks.

Results

Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests indicated that all study variables (students’ scores on the Purdue Pegboard tests under direct and indirect vision, age, and manual grades in the phantom laboratory) were not normally distributed (p < 0.05). Cronbach’s Alpha value for the feedback questionnaire consisting of 5 items was 0.741 which indicated a high level of internal consistency.

Comparison of study group characteristics

This analysis was conducted to evaluate potential confounding effects of independent variables that might influence manual grades, thereby impacting the internal validity of the study.

Mann–Whitney tests revealed no statistically significant differences between Group 1 (n = 35) and Group 2 (n = 35) for age (p = 0.432), all 4 tasks of Purdue under direct vision (p > 0.194) and indirect vision (p > 0.173). Chi-square tests showed no significant differences in gender distribution (p = 1.000) or hand dominance (p = 1.000) between the groups.

Sub-group comparisons – Group 1

Kruskal–Wallis tests comparing the cognitive stage group, associative stage group, and autonomous stage group in Group 1 (Table 2) revealed no statistically significant differences in age (p = 0.322), all 4 tasks of Purdue under direct vision (p > 0.753) and indirect vision (p > 0.713). Chi-square tests indicated no significant differences in gender distribution (p = 0.813) or hand dominance (p = 0.997) among the sub-groups.

Mixed linear model

Manual tasks (cavity preparation on tooth No. 36 and No. 16) were included as a repeated measure to account for the sequence of tasks. Student ID was included as a random effect, while Condition (Experimental vs. Control), Gender, and Dominant hand were included as fixed factors. Age and baseline fine motor skills and spatial perception assessed by the Purdue Pegboard Test under direct and indirect vision were included as covariates. The model revealed no significant main effects for Manual task (F(1,119) = 0.424, p = 0.516), Condition(F (1,119) = 0.656, p = 0.420), gender (F(1,119) = 0.393, p = 0.532), or dominant hand (F(1,119) = 1.40, p = 0.238). No significant interaction effects were observed among the study variables, with the exception of a significant interaction between Condition and gender (F(1,119) = 4.84, p = 0.03). The interaction indicated a tendency for female students to achieve higher mean manual grade than male in experimental and control group (difference of 1.5 points).

Feedback questionnaire

At the conclusion of the Simodont training, 71% of the students completed the feedback questionnaire (Table 1). The mean response values across all seven questions ranged from 1.6 to 2.6 on the scale used. Notably, the majority of students agreed only with the statement regarding the realism of the images of teeth and dental instruments. For all other questions, most students partially disagreed or disagreed, indicating a perception that the use of Simodont did not substantially aid in acquiring the manual skills required for phantom laboratory practice.

Discussion

Implementation of virtual reality simulators incorporating force feedback (haptic) technology during the preclinical year of dental education has been well documented [9–14]. Although a recent systematic review [18] concluded that the application of virtual reality in dental education improves students’ clinical outcomes and self-assessment, the consensus in most studies is that virtual simulators should enhance rather than replace conventional phantom head simulators [19].

Virtual reality simulators have inherent disadvantages, including the absence of a physical finger rest and the inability to perform bimanual coordination required for indirect vision, soft tissue retraction, and light reflection using a dental mirror. Dentists must practice bimanually: one hand performs precise drilling, while the other hand manipulates the dental mirror at exact angles to provide optimal visualization of the small and inverted operative field. Learning this skill is challenging due to the brain’s natural difficulty in simultaneously controlling two new complex motor actions requiring coordination of both hands. Therefore, the Simodont simulator cannot fully reproduce the ergonomics and hand coordination required in dental procedures, highlighting a limitation in using it as a replacement for conventional phantom training. These limitations might have contributed to the lack of significant differences in manual performance observed between the experimental and control groups in our study, particularly for task performed under indirect vision.

Our study demonstrated that the benefits of Simodont training for improving manual skills for dental procedures in prior studies [11–14] may be overestimated if individual differences are not accounted for. To our knowledge, this is the first study employing matched design controlling for confounding factors (especially manual dexterity and spatial perception) that may affected prior studies reporting advantages of the Simodont simulator [11–14]. Particularly those conducted during the early cognitive stage of skill acquisition, when inherent individual variability has a stronger influence on performance. Lugassy et al. [20] concluded that manual skill in dentistry, a key competence, partially reflects innate ability, as assessed by the Purdue Pegboard Test under both direct and indirect vision, as was also performed in the present study. Hattori et al. [21] reported lower scores for dental students using the Simodont compared to conventional simulators, attributing the difference to variability in students’ depth perception abilities.

Previous studies have reported beneficial effects of Simodont training on manual performance in preclinical dental courses, however, these findings should be interpreted in light of important methodological differences compared to the present study. For example, De Boer et al. [11] reported that only students who trained with the Simodont simulator were able to pass a manual skills test, Yuan et al. [12] found that Simodont training significantly improved cavity preparation performance compared with conventional phantom training alone. Similarity, Murbay et al. [22] evaluated the impact of integrating the Simodont simulator into a preclinical operative dentistry course and showed significantly improved clinical performance in the Simodont group. In those studies, participants were allocated to experimental and control groups using simple randomization without matching for baseline characteristics. In contrast, the present study employed a matched design controlling for confounding factors known to influence manual performance. By minimizing the impact of individual variability and assessing performance across all stages of motor skill acquisition, the apparent advantages of additional Simodont training reported in previous studies may be diminished.

Dundar et al. [23] examined the effect of virtual reality simulators on tooth preparation skills of dental students. In their study, fourth-year dental students who received two sessions of 60 min training on the Simodont were compared to fifth-year students who did not receive simulator training. They found that the fourth-year students who trained with the Simodont performed better in terms of tooth preparation angles compared to those who did not use virtual reality simulators. However, this study compared students from different academic years, introducing a potential confounding factor. Wei et al. [10] also explored Simodont’s effectiveness in teaching access and coronal cavity preparation in preclinical training. In their study, twenty dental students were randomly divided into two groups. The experimental group received 120 min Simodont training session focused on access and coronal cavity preparation, while the control group practiced the same task using only the conventional phantom head simulator. They showed greater consistency in task performance among students trained with Simodont compared to traditional methods. In contrast, in our study students received the same duration of training time with Simodont, however, no significant differences were observed between groups. This discrepancy may be attributed to our matched-pairs crossover design, which controlled for individual variability and baseline characteristics (age, gender, dominant hand, fine motor skills, and spatial perception). This highlighting the importance of controlling for confounders such as age, gender, dominant hand, fine motor skills, and spatial perception. Additionally, although the experimental group received additional training, and based on the principle that increased practice is generally associated with improved dexterity (practice make perfect [24]), one might have expected higher manual scores in this group. However, the relative training duration should be considered. While the experimental group received an additional 120 min of Simodont training, both groups underwent extensive conventional phantom head training (720 min). Consequently, the incremental virtual simulator exposure may have been insufficient to yield a measurable performance advantage, potentially explaining the limited impact of Simodont training observed in this study.

While virtual reality simulators appear to enhance student performance and self-confidence, they cannot currently replace conventional phantom head simulators. Studies suggest that virtual reality is most effective during the early cognitive stage of preclinical dental training [25]. Bakr et al. [26] recommended a gradual introduction of virtual simulators starting from the first year, progressing through cariology exercises in the second year, and adjunct crown preparation training in the third year alongside phantom simulators. Our study found no significant differences in performance among the cognitive, associative, or autonomous stages of motor skill acquisition. It is possible that the division of the experimental group into three subgroups may have reduced the statistical power of the analysis, thereby limiting the ability to detect significant differences between subgroups, even if true effects were present.

The feedback questionnaire in this study revealed that students only agreed that the images of teeth and dental instruments appeared realistic; however, they did not feel that the virtual simulator enhanced their manual performance. This perception may be related to the simulator’s limited ability to accurately replicate tactile sensations of enamel, dentin, and bone, as well as practical drawbacks such as high initial cost, the need for instructor guidance prior to training, and requirements for precise calibration. Only 71% of students completed the feedback questionnaire. Although 29% of students did not respond, the likelihood of substantial non-response bias is considered low. This is because the students who completed the questionnaire tended to report more positive perceptions of the Simodont simulator than those who did not respond. Therefore, while the feedback results should be interpreted with caution, they likely provide a reasonable representation of students’ overall perceptions regarding the simulator’s usefulness and realism.

Limitations

Due to limited availability of the simulator, training time was restricted to 120 min per student. This limited the potential effect on students’ manual skill improvement. Longer or repeated training sessions could potentially enhance skill acquisition and better reflect the benefits of virtual reality simulator during skill acquisition of dental students. Future research should investigate the benefits of extended training durations to maximize the educational value of virtual simulation. Subdividing the experimental group into three subgroups reduced the sample size per subgroup, which may have limited the study’s statistical power to detect significant differences. It should be emphasized that the findings of this study related only to two manual tasks performed on teeth No. 36 and No. 16, and therefore may not be directly generalizable to other teeth or dental procedures. Furthermore, the study was conducted at a single center with a specific cohort of dental students, which limits extrapolation to other curricular contexts.

Conclusion

The use of the Simodont simulator during the preclinical year may not improve dental students’ manual performance under direct or indirect vision within the 120‑minute exposure used in this study. Although virtual reality simulators represent promising educational tools, they currently cannot replace conventional phantom head simulators in dental training.
