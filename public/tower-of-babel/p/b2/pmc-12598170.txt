“B”-Stitches: A Technique for Repair of Traumatic Horizontal or Oblique Meniscal Tear
Authors: Winyou Ratanachai, Charoen Chotigavanich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A horizontal tear of the meniscus is one of the most common meniscal tear patterns. This article presents a technique for horizontal meniscal repair called the B-stitch technique, including the logic behind its design. The B-stitch technique is a repair method that provides a reproducible and safe repair construct. It directs most of the compression from the suture loop to compress the tear. It also minimally strangulates the meniscal tissue while causing the least disturbance to the blood supply. Compression from the loop does not cause narrowing of the meniscus and preserves most of its native anatomy.

A horizontal tear splits the meniscus into superior and inferior laminae or leaves. It is one of the most common tear patterns, occurring in 30% to 60% of all meniscal tears. Tears can run horizontally or obliquely.1

The treatment of traumatic horizontal tears has traditionally involved partial meniscectomy, where removal of 1 meniscal leaf, often the inferior leaf, is performed.2 Although this technique may relieve short-term symptoms, recent research has shown that the long-term adverse effects of partial meniscectomy significantly increase the contact pressure in the knee.3 Moreover, the peak contact pressure is similar to that of dual leaflet resection.4,5 The major long-term consequence of meniscectomy is progressive degenerative change.3 Therefore, meniscectomy is not an ideal option because it alters the normal biomechanical structure and load distribution.

Meniscal preservation is preferred because of supported evidence of improved outcomes and slow progression of degenerative changes.6 Currently, meniscal tissue preservation in horizontal tears has been proven to preserve long-term outcomes, especially in young patients.7 Although horizontal cleavage repair can restore some contact pressure,4 recent repair designs are still unable to restore the previous anatomy during the course of healing.

In this study, we present a technique for horizontal meniscal repair, called the B-stitch technique. In addition, the logic behind the design is discussed.

Surgical Technique

Patient Positioning and Visualization

The patient is placed in the supine position, and the lower extremities are prepared and draped using sterile protocols (Video 1). Standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals are positioned. The anteromedial portal is created under needle localization to ensure easy access to the posterior aspect of the medial meniscus. An additional mid-medial or lateral portal is created as an accessory portal to serve as another working portal. The lateral portal is created anterior to the lateral collateral ligament. If it is necessary to increase the medial joint space, pie crusting of the medial collateral ligament is performed by placing the knee in full extension while applying valgus stress and puncturing an 18-gauge needle at a point 1.2 cm above the joint line on the line connecting the adductor tubercle and the posterior border of the tibial shaft.8 Release of the lateral structures is not necessary because there is sufficient working space on the lateral joint line.

Meniscus Preparation

A horizontal cleavage tear is identified and carefully rasped to determine the extent of the tear and assess meniscal tissue quality. The avascular, non-repairable, and frayed portions of the superior and inferior leaflets are debrided using a punch, rasp, and shaver.

B-Stitches

Meniscal stitching is performed from the periphery to the center, maintaining at least a square configuration in each loop. Knot tying is performed away from the articular surface. A PassPort Cannula (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is placed at the anteromedial portal.

Step 1: Outer Stitch

The 90° straight SutureLasso SD (model AR-4068-90; Arthrex) is passed through the mid-medial portal. The stitch begins at the superior leaflet of the meniscal tear at the meniscocapsular junction. Care is taken to not include the capsule in the loop. The lasso is then advanced to the undersurface of the inferior leaflet. With a suture retriever from the anteromedial portal, the loop of the lasso is retrieved to the outside of the joint to reroute a No. 2-0 FiberWire (Arthrex) from the anteromedial portal to the mid-medial portal (Fig 1, Video 1).Fig 1Outer stitch (left knee, medial meniscus). Step 2: Middle Stitch(es)

The lasso is advanced from the mid-medial portal to a more central portion of the tear to a distance less than the thickness of the meniscus at that point (A [distance of lasso advancement] shorter than B [thickness of the meniscus at the suture spot]). A suture retriever is used from the anteromedial portal to pull the lasso loop from the undersurface of the tear to reroute a FiberWire loop. Step 3: Inner Stitch

The lasso is advanced from the mid-medial portal to pass through the FiberWire loop and then through the superior and inferior leaves of the tear, again at a distance more centrally and less than the thickness of the meniscus at that point. Therefore, another square loop configuration is formed. The anteromedial portal is used to reroute the suture lasso loop, together with the anterior end of the FiberWire, through the mid-medial portal. In cases in which the tear width is greater than 2 loops of B-stitches, additional loops are made by repeating step 2 until the entire width of the tear is covered. Because of this, the continuing FiberWire loops are kept ready on the upper surface of the superior leaf for step 3.

The knot is then carefully tied to the periphery of the meniscus. Tension is adjusted to ensure proper tear compression and to avoid deformation or strangulation of the meniscus. The cross section of the loop forms a B shape, as shown in Figure 3 and Video 1.

When B-stitches are used for oblique tears, the inner end of the B-loop can be extended more central to the lower lip of the open mouth of the torn meniscus (Fig 4). Optimal compression of the superior and inferior leaflets without significant deformation is shown in Figure 5. Additional B-stitches can be applied to cover the entire tear surface. The stability of the final construct is confirmed using a probe. Discussion

Design Logic of B-Stitches

Figure 7 presents a graphic comparison of the loops of all-inside circumferential stitches and B-stitches. The blue line represents the sutures, whereas the gray line represents a horizontal tear of the meniscus. The yellow line represents an imaginary line perpendicular to the tear. At time A, sutures are applied. At time B, the loops are tightened until completion at time C. From time A to time B, any rectangle is adjusted to form a square, and eventually, a circle is created at time C. The direction of compression occurs along the longer side of the rectangle, which becomes shortened or compressed. In the B-stitch, the longer side of the rectangle is intentionally placed perpendicular to the tear plane. As the loop tightens, compression force compresses and stabilizes the tear surface. Comparison of B-Stitches and Other Stitches Regarding Their Abilities to Achieve 3 Essential Elements of Successful Meniscal Repair

Saliman9 concluded that the main objectives of horizontal cleavage repair are anatomic reduction, biological stimulation, and compression of the tear surface.10 Although we agree that these are essential elements for successful repair, the terminology was adjusted to include other factors and simplified to “ABC,” which stands for anatomic reduction, biologically friendly, and compression of the tear surface.

Advantages and Disadvantages of B-Stitches

The B-stitch technique is a repair method that provides a reproducible and safe repair construct. Most of the compression from the suture loop is directed to compress the tear. This technique also minimally strangulates the meniscal tissue while causing the least disturbance to the blood supply. Compression from the loop does not cause narrowing of the meniscus and preserves most of the native anatomy. There is no risk to soft tissue because this is an all-inside repair.
