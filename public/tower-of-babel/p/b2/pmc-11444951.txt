Simple and Safe Soft-Tissue Coverage Technique Using the Bipedicled Flap in the Lower Leg
Authors: Dong Uk Jin, Yohan Lee, Young Ho Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSkin and soft-tissue defects in the lower legs present significant challenges for surgeons, especially when tendons, bones, or implants are exposed. In such cases, simple dressings or skin grafts are insufficient, necessitating flap surgery. The study included 5 male and 5 female patients, with an average age of 54.5 years. The defects were due to various causes, including trauma, tumor surgery, postoperative complications such as dehiscence and skin necrosis, and chronic osteomyelitis. The size of the defects ranged from 4 × 3 cm to 16 × 13 cm. The outcomes assessed included flap viability, postoperative complications, and patient satisfaction. Patient satisfaction following the operation was excellent in 3 patients, good in 6 patients, and average in 1 patient, with an overall average score of 11.5 (good) out of 15 among the 10 patients evaluated.ConclusionsThe bipedicled flap is a simple and secure method for treating soft-tissue defects in the lower legs. Therefore, this technique can be considered as one of the viable options for treating such defects.Keywords: Lower leg, Soft tissue defect, Bipedicled flap

Skin and soft-tissue defects in the lower legs can pose significant challenges for reconstructive surgeons. Particularly problematic are cases where bone, tendon, fixation materials, or implants are exposed or where there are large and deep soft-tissue defects.1) In such scenarios, skin grafts prove inadequate, necessitating more substantial soft-tissue coverage. Surgical treatments available for soft-tissue coverage include local fasciocutaneous flaps, muscle flaps, and free flaps, with the choice depending on the location and size of the defect, as well as the condition of adjacent tissues.2)

The bipedicled flap, initially introduced by Crawford3) in 1957, is an effective option, providing a tissue source based on 2 vascular pedicles with dual perfusion, both distally and proximally. This technique involves designing the longer edge of the bipedicled flap parallel to the closer margin of the defect. An incision is made through the skin and subcutaneous tissue down to the fascia, enabling the flap to be mobilized effectively to cover defects.3,4) The bipedicled flap offers several advantages, including ease and safety in elevation, minimal donor-site morbidity due to the low risk of damaging perforator vessels, and a dual blood supply. It also preserves tissue quality and local sensation.4,5) Therefore, if utilized appropriately, this method can be of great assistance in soft-tissue coverage for lower legs.

This study presents the outcomes of using the bipedicled flap for soft-tissue reconstruction in the lower legs. Furthermore, the merits and limitations of this surgical approach, compared to other methods of soft-tissue coverage, are analyzed through a comprehensive literature review.

METHODS

Study Sample

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. 2310-016-1473). As this retrospective review of medical records did not involve further patient intervention, exemption was given for written consent.

From January 2013 to May 2020, a total of 10 bipedicled flaps were performed for skin and soft-tissue defects of the lower legs. The study included 5 men and 5 women with a mean age of 54.5 years (range, 17–80 years). In all patients, the skin and soft-tissue defects were located on the lower legs. Two patients presented with open fractures due to trauma, 3 experienced defects following tumor surgery, and 4 suffered from postoperative complications, including 2 cases of wound dehiscence, 1 case of surgical site skin necrosis, and 1 case of heel sore-induced skin necrosis. Additionally, 1 patient presented with chronic osteomyelitis. The size of skin defects ranged from 4 × 3 cm to 16 × 13 cm (Table 1). We evaluated the viability of the flap, postoperative complications, and patient’s satisfaction. Patient satisfaction was divided into 3 categories: appearance, sensation, and function, with each category receiving a score out of 5 for a total of 15 points. The final scores indicated satisfaction levels: 1–3 points, poor; 4–6, below average; 7–9, average; 10–12, good; and 13–15, excellent.

Surgical Technique

A single orthopedic surgeon (YHL) performed all flap operations. Preoperative evaluations did not include special procedures like computed tomography angiography or Doppler sonography. Patients were positioned supine and anesthetized using either general or spinal methods, with a pneumatic tourniquet applied. A longitudinal line was drawn parallel to the defect, with its length extending to twice that of the defect and its width approximately half of the defect’s length. This approach aimed to ensure sufficient blood supply, thereby safeguarding tissue vascularity and significantly reducing necrosis risk.5) An incision was made along the designed line down to the fascia level. Undermining up to the subfascial level was performed in all cases to form the flap. During this process, care was taken to avoid damaging the perforator vessels while dissecting, even though the perforator vessels were typically not directly visible. However, when they were observed, preservation without damage was considered crucial throughout the dissection. Once the flap was sufficiently mobilized, it was moved towards the defect, applying minimal tension, and sutured layer by layer. After the procedure, we checked the viability of the flap. Secondary defects were approximated without tension. After 1–2 weeks, they were either primarily closed or treated with a split-thickness skin graft.

RESULTS

All flaps were successfully performed, and there were no cases showing any special complications. The mean period of follow-up was 25 months (range, 12–47 months) after the operation. Patient satisfaction following the operation was excellent in 3 patients, good in 6 patients, and average in 1 patient, with an overall average score of 11.5 (good) out of 15 among the 10 patients evaluated (Table 2).

Case 1: Patient No. 1

A 61-year-old man presented with skin and soft-tissue defects on the anterior side below the knee resulting from the removal of a recurrent squamous cell carcinoma, and another defect on the lateral side of the lower leg due to fibula resection for osteofibrous dysplasia (Fig. 1A and B). The size of the defect was 12 × 9 cm on the anterior side below the knee and 21 × 4 cm on the lateral side of the lower leg. In this case, since there were already nearly parallel 2 defects, we decided to cover the defect on the anterior side below the knee using the lateral side defect without making an additional incision. The medial aspect of the lateral side defect was undermined at the subfascial level. Upon confirming sufficient mobilization, the bipedicled flap was advanced towards the defect on the anterior side below the knee and sutured without tension (Fig. 1C-E). After advancing and suturing, the flap’s viability remained in good condition. Then, the lateral side defect was approximated without any tension (Fig. 1F and G). Twelve days after the surgery, a primary closure of the lateral side defect was possible, and it was sutured layer by layer (Fig. 1H-J). No complications were observed. Two weeks after the second surgery, both wounds healed stably. Seven months after the surgery, patient-rated satisfaction was excellent (Fig. 1K and L).

Case 2: Patient No. 4

A 61-year-old woman presented with skin and soft-tissue defects on the anterior side of the mid-lower leg, measuring 16 × 13 cm, resulting from the removal of a recurrent spindle cell carcinoma. Postoperatively, there was exposure of the tibia bone (Fig. 2A and B). An appropriate design was made based on the size of the defect, marking areas for longitudinal incision on the medial side of the defect for bone exposure coverage (Fig. 2C). An incision was made along the longitudinal line, accessing the subfascial level (Fig. 2D). During subfascial undermining performed to sufficiently mobilize the flap, 2 perforator vessels were encountered and carefully dissected while ensuring their preservation (Fig. 2E and F). In this case, it was determined that coverage for the tibia bone exposure was more appropriate than primary closure of the defect. So, the sufficiently mobilized flap was positioned with minimal tension and sutured in layers for tibia bone exposure coverage (Fig. 2G and H). Two weeks after the surgery, once the soft-tissue had appropriately granulated, a split-thickness skin graft was performed on both sides of the defect (Fig. 2I and J). Six months after the surgery, patient-rated satisfaction was good (Fig. 2K and L). No complications were observed.

Case 3: Patient No. 2

A 17-year-old man presented with a 4 × 3 cm skin and soft-tissue defect on the posterior right heel, resulting from skin necrosis due to a heel sore following corrective surgery for Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (Fig. 3A). Marginal debridement was performed until fresh bleeding was observed from the original wound, after which primary closure was deemed impossible, necessitating additional soft-tissue coverage (Fig. 3B). Consequently, a bipedicled flap was chosen for this case. An appropriate design was made, followed by an incision and undermining (Fig. 3C). After ensuring sufficient mobilization of the flap, it was sutured layer by layer to cover the defect (Fig. 3D-F). The viability of the flap was then confirmed, and the donor site was also immediately amenable to primary repair, allowing for closure (Fig. 3G). Three months after surgery, patient-rated satisfaction was good (Fig. 3H). No complications were observed.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to explore the efficacy and safety of the bipedicled flap for treating skin and soft-tissue defects in the lower leg. It was demonstrated that by selecting the appropriate indications for the bipedicled flap, a high success rate can be achieved in situations where soft-tissue coverage of the lower leg is needed.

The bipedicled flap has been successfully utilized in various anatomical locations to treat skin and soft-tissue defects. Initially reported by Von Langenbeck and Bhttrdge6) in 1859 for cleft palate closure, its application to the lower extremity was later described by Crawford3) in 1957. Schwabegger et al.7) suggested that the flap’s incision width should be twice the length of the defect, with a width approximately half the defect’s length, thus maintaining a width-to-length ratio not exceeding 1:4. This ratio is crucial to ensure adequate blood supply and to minimize the risk of necrosis and tension.2) While this is an old and straightforward method, it remains regarded as valuable, and various subsequent studies have been introduced.

The bipedicled flap offers several advantages for tissue reconstruction. The procedure is relatively simple and safe, avoiding the need to sacrifice or anastomose perforating vessels, unlike rotational or free flaps.8) This is accompanied by the advantage of relatively short surgery times. In the present study, perforating vessels were directly observed in only 1 of the 10 cases. In the 1 case where perforating vessels were observed, we were able to carefully dissect and preserve the perforator vessels and move them along with the flap. In the other 9 cases, even though we could not see the perforator vessels directly, we carefully dissected under the fascia. When moving the flap, any damage or excessive tension on the perforator vessels was prevented. As a result, this method is advantageous for preserving blood supply, and by maintaining the dual blood supply from both proximal and distal ends, it significantly reduces donor-site morbidity in the flap.4) Additionally, by preserving the perforating vessels, the accompanying sensory nerve branches are also retained, allowing for the maintenance of local sensation. The skin tissue closely resembles the surrounding area and has a low occurrence of “dog ear” formation, making it advantageous from a cosmetic perspective.2)

Despite its benefits, some complications have been associated with the bipedicled flap (Table 3).1,4,7,8,9,10,11,12) Primarily, complications include delayed wound healing, infection, and wound dehiscence, with some cases also showing partial necrosis and venous congestion. The incidence of these complications was observed in 46 out of 234 cases across the 8 analyzed studies, representing a 20% proportion. However, it is noteworthy that, except for 4 cases, the conditions improved with conservative management, including the use of dressings and medications such as antibiotics and anticoagulants, without the need for additional surgical intervention. Among the reported 234 cases, flap failure was reported in 1 case (approximately 0.43%), and 3 cases involved partial necrosis or wound dehiscence, requiring additional surgical treatment in a total of 4 out of 234 cases (1.7%). Among these, 3 cases were resolved with additional flap surgery, and 1 case was resolved through an additional skin graft. This is considered safer than free flaps where flap failures have been reported.13,14) Due to its safety without necessitating vascular anastomosis or transfer required by free or complex local flaps, the efforts required by the reconstruction surgical team for monitoring are also reduced.12) Our case series reported no complications. The 2-stage procedure is aimed at sufficiently reducing swelling, occurring after the first surgery, and developing soft-tissue granulation to enhance the success rate of donor-site coverage or increase the possibility of primary closure, which we believe contributed to the absence of complications. Of course, there are disadvantages such as patient discomfort and an increased risk of infection associated with the secondary procedure.

To successfully employ a bipedicled flap, several conditions need to be fulfilled. Since this method utilizes the surrounding soft-tissue, it is crucial that the soft-tissue near the defect is uninjured and well-vascularized.1) Although the bipedicled flap has been utilized for transverse defects in the heel and around the ankle, its primary use is for longitudinal defects in the lower leg, taking into account the direction of blood flow of the skin flap.1,4,7,8,9,10,11,12) Regarding defect size, Baker and Oudit4) suggested that the use of this flap is not recommended when the defect covers more than 25% of the circumference of the affected body part and alternative reconstruction options should be considered. It could be a limitation of the bipedicled flap, as a local flap, whereas free flaps typically encounter fewer limitations in terms of location and size. However, the reported case of patient no. 4 in our cases, who presented with a soft-tissue defect exceeding 25% of the circumference of the affected body part and was effectively covered by appropriate shifting to address the bone defect, demonstrates that such guidelines are not absolute when the procedure is carefully executed. Moreover, instances utilizing a double-bipedicled flap, involving incisions on both the medial and lateral aspects for coverage, have demonstrated the capability for substantial coverage.1,4,7,8,9,12) Bhagat et al.15) reported that among 14 patients, 8 were treated with bipedicled flaps only, while 6 required an additional perforator flap for wider soft-tissue coverage, introducing a method using multiple local flaps. With appropriate preoperative planning and experience, the indications for the surgery can potentially be expanded more broadly.

This study has several limitations. It was a retrospective study with a relatively small sample size. Additionally, due to the low prevalence and varied nature of soft-tissue defects, as well as the nature of flap surgery, direct comparisons of outcomes and effectiveness between different flap operation techniques were not made, and statistical analysis was also not conducted. However, based on our case series and comprehensive reviews of existing literature, we conducted an effective analysis of the advantages, possible complications, disadvantages, and indications of the bipedicled flap. Future research comparing bipedicled flaps with other local and free flap methods is crucial to deepen our understanding of their efficacy.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the bipedicled flap is a simple and safe method for effectively treating soft-tissue defects in the lower legs. Therefore, this technique can be considered as one of the viable options for treating such defects.
