An acetyltransferase family required for biosynthesis of diverse marine carotenoids
Authors: Tianjun Cao, Xuechun Bai, Tingting You, Yanyou Jiang, Martin Lohr, Xiaobo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceApproximately half of global photosynthesis occurs in oceans. Marine eukaryotic algae synthesize specialized carotenoids to facilitate efficient light capture underwater. While the multistep biosynthesis of these pigments has been partially characterized, the shared acetylation step essential for formation of fucoxanthin and other major marine carotenoids remained unexplored. We identified the elusive enzyme responsible for this critical modification. Our findings demonstrate how distinct algal lineages evolved diverse pigment profiles by adapting the substrate specificity of this enzyme, thereby contributing to the remarkable diversity of photosynthetic pigments in marine ecosystems. These insights resolve a long-standing question in algal biology and open broad avenues for enhancing light-harvesting in crops and developing sustainable sources of high-value carotenoids.

Marine algae play a vital role in Earth’s carbon cycle and marine primary productivity (1). Among them, ochrophytes (photosynthetic stramenopiles, such as diatoms and brown algae), haptophytes, and dinoflagellates dominate oceanic phytoplankton communities and are major contributors to global carbon fixation (2, 3). Their ecological success stems from unique photosynthetic adaptations, including pigment profiles distinct from terrestrial plants. Notably, their light-harvesting complexes contain significantly higher carotenoid-to-chlorophyll ratios (4) and produce specialized carotenoids like fucoxanthin, peridinin, and vaucheriaxanthin esters (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Table S1) that are absent in land plants (5, 6). These unique pigments, particularly fucoxanthin and peridinin, exhibit exceptional green light absorption efficiency—a crucial adaptation to the blue-green light spectrum of marine environments (7). Beyond their essential photophysiological roles, these algal carotenoids have gained increasing attention for their significant nutritional and pharmaceutical potential for human health (8–11).

From a biochemical perspective, these carotenoids also exhibit remarkable diversity with distinct lineage-specific characteristics. Ochrophytes and haptophytes primarily produce fucoxanthin and its derivatives, while the majority of photosynthetic dinoflagellates synthesize peridinin (Fig. 1). However, despite their diversity, most of these carotenoids share a common structural feature: acetylation on one of the two terminal rings, as seen in fucoxanthin, peridinin, dinoxanthin, vaucheriaxanthin esters, and gyroxanthin diester (5, 12–14). While widespread across lineages like Ochrophyta, Dinophyta, and Haptophyta, this modification remains poorly characterized compared to other carotenoid alterations like allene formation or hydroxylation (15, 16).

Among marine carotenoids, fucoxanthin stands out both for its ecological significance and as the premier model system for studying carotenoid modification chemistry in marine algae. As the main light-harvesting carotenoid in diatoms—which contribute 20% of global carbon fixation and drive silica precipitation into the deep sea—fucoxanthin accounts for ~10% of Earth’s carotenoid pool (17). Recent work in the diatom Phaeodactylum tricornutum has partially elucidated its biosynthetic pathway from the common precursor violaxanthin (Fig. 1) (18). The pathway features evolutionary innovations in carotenoid metabolism, including the expansion and neofunctionalization of enzyme families: PtVDL1/2 within the violaxanthin de-epoxidase family (18, 19), PtZEP1 within the zeaxanthin epoxidase family (19), and PtCRTISO5 in the carotenoid isomerase family (20). Certain members in these families were initially discovered in land plants, catalyzing steps in the early pathway up to lycopene, or steps in the photoprotective xanthophyll cycle (16, 21). However, a critical gap persists in the biosynthesis of marine carotenoids: The absence of any known carotenoid acetyltransferases has prevented the use of homology-based approaches to identify the enzyme responsible for the conserved acetylation step.

In this study, we identify a xanthophyll acetyltransferase (XACT), resolving this long-standing gap in marine carotenoid biosynthesis. We first identified XACT in P. tricornutum, where it is essential for fucoxanthin production. Subsequent analysis of XACT orthologs from other chromalveolate algae revealed its broader role in synthesizing diverse acetylated pigments across chromalveolate algae. We further demonstrate how its substrate specificity shapes distinct profiles of acetylated carotenoids in different lineages. This finding represents a critical advance toward engineering both photosynthetic systems and high-value carotenoid production in algae, plants, and microbial hosts.

Results and Discussion

Reverse Genetics Identified a Candidate Acetyltransferase Essential for Fucoxanthin Biosynthesis.

To identify the missing carotenoid acetyltransferase in P. tricornutum, we employed a candidate gene knockout strategy. Our candidate selection began with the machine learning-based annotation tool CLEAN (22), which identified 789 genes classified under the EC 2.3.1 category (encompassing acetyltransferases and other acyltransferases). This initial set was subsequently refined by retaining those genes meeting at least one of the following criteria: i) significant down-regulation under high light—known to suppress light-harvesting pigment biosynthesis, as evidenced by the concomitant down-regulation of established pathway genes VDL2, ZEP1, and CRTISO5 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A); or ii) the presence of a predicted chloroplast localization signal. This filtering process yielded a final list of 38 candidate genes (Dataset S1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B).

We further expanded our selection criteria by incorporating functional insights from terrestrial plants. In wheat, XAT was found to catalyze lutein esterification (23), while in tomato, PYP1 was identified as essential for xanthophyll ester formation (24). Although these enzymes mediate the transfer of long-chain acyl groups to carotenoids, the protein families to which they belong also include members capable of acetylation. While no high-confidence homolog of wheat XAT was found, the protein encoded by Phatr3_J43541 showed moderate sequence similarity to tomato PYP1. Given its annotation as an EC 2.3.1 enzyme, it was also included in the knockout candidate list (Dataset S1).

To functionally characterize these candidate genes, we generated CRISPR/Cas9 knockout lines (Dataset S2). Given the frequent occurrence of mosaic transformants, we implemented a two-step characterization strategy (25). First, we isolated subclones to obtain homozygous (or biallelic) mutants, which was successfully achieved for several candidates. Second, we tested our hypothesis that gene disruption would lead to fucoxanthin depletion along with precursor accumulation. While initial visual inspection of transformant colonies revealed no obvious pigmentation changes—likely due to the nonclonal nature of the lines—we employed high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) coupled with a photodiode array detector (PDA) for enhanced detection sensitivity. This analysis demonstrated that the Phatr3_J36932 mutant accumulated trace amounts of a distinct pigment with an absorption spectrum strongly resembling that of fucoxanthin but with an increased polarity as indicated by its shorter retention time (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A, peak n6). No novel pigment peaks were detected in samples from mutants of the other candidate genes. However, purified biallelic mutants were unavailable for some genes (Dataset S1), and we cannot rule out that these genes have a functional role in pigment metabolism.

Phatr3_J36932 was prioritized as a compelling candidate based on three lines of evidence: i) its assignment to the “lightcyan1” module in PhaeoNet, a coexpression network enriched for photosynthesis-related genes (26, 27); ii) the presence of an N-terminal acetyltransferase domain and a C-terminal lipocalin-like domain within its encoded protein (Fig. 2A); and iii) targeting prediction results (Dataset S1) and chloroplast proteome data (28) suggesting its subcellular localization to the chloroplast. Based on this cumulative evidence, we selected Phatr3_J36932 for further investigation.

Phatr3_J36932 (XACT) Is Required for Fucoxanthin Accumulation and Normal Cell Growth.

Following initial identification of the Phatr3_J36932 mutant, selected mutant colonies were subjected to two rounds of subcloning. Notably, on one subclone plate, a subset of colonies exhibited a distinct green phenotype (Fig. 2B), suggesting a potential disruption in fucoxanthin biosynthesis. A representative colony, designated Subclone-32 (S32), was isolated. The green phenotype persisted when S32 was grown in liquid culture (Fig. 2C), and it was chosen for further characterization.

Pigment profiling revealed that the S32 mutant completely lacked fucoxanthin but showed a compensatory accumulation of the novel pigment (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). The shorter retention time of this pigment and the similarity of its absorption spectrum to that of fucoxanthin were consistent with the absence of the acetyl residue present in fucoxanthin. Mass spectroscopic analysis of the novel pigment corroborated this conclusion (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B, D, and E). High-resolution mass spectrometry detected positive ions at m/z 617.4204 [M + H]+ and 599.4100 [M − H2O + H]+, consistent with the molecular formula C40H56O5. The observed [M − 2H2O + H]+ ion, indicative of eliminated hydroxyl groups at both the 3 and 3’ positions of the new carotenoid, matched the expected fragmentation pattern for fucoxanthin upon loss of acetic acid and one water molecule. Collectively, these data identify the novel pigment as the deacetylated derivative of fucoxanthin, fucoxanthinol (29).

We then characterized the genotype of S32. PCR amplification of the gRNA target region followed by Sanger sequencing yielded mixed chromatogram signals (Fig. 2D), suggesting that S32 was either a purified heterozygous mutant or remained nonclonal. Subsequent subcloning of S32 yielded derived lines that all produced sequencing results identical to the original S32 isolate (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C), supporting the interpretation that it is a heterozygous mutant. Subsequently, deep sequencing of the target region confirmed a heterozygous state, revealing two distinct indel mutations: a 3-base pair deletion (−3 bp) and a 1-base pair insertion (+1 bp), with no residual wild-type sequence, thereby establishing successful editing of Phatr3_J36932 (Fig. 2 D and E).

A −3 bp variant is typically expected to preserve the reading frame and thus cause minimal disruption to protein translation. In the case of Phatr3_J36932, however, the mutation resulted in the loss of leucine 153 (L153) and a histidine-to-asparagine substitution at position 154 (H154N). Notably, a sequence alignment revealed that L153 is highly conserved among homologous proteins across diverse species and within the alcohol acetyltransferase (AATase) family (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Subsequent functional assays confirmed that this mutation leads to a complete loss of enzymatic activity (see details below). Based on these findings, we performed genetic complementation experiments to determine whether the phenotype alterations observed in the S32 mutant could be specifically attributed to this allelic variant.

We complemented the S32 strain with Phatr3_J36932 cDNA (SI Appendix, Materials and Methods). Multiple transformants exhibiting restored brown pigmentation were obtained, and the presence of the complementation construct in these lines was confirmed by PCR (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). Three representative complemented lines—S32-Comp1, S32-Comp2, and S32-Comp3—showed phenotypic rescue of pigmentation (Fig. 2 B and C), which was further corroborated by HPLC analysis confirming the recovery of fucoxanthin accumulation (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). This genetic complementation demonstrates that the phenotypic defect in S32 is specifically attributable to the loss of Phatr3_J36932 function. We therefore designate this gene as encoding a XACT, an activity subsequently confirmed by in vitro activity assays.

As described above, PtXACT comprises two distinct domains: an N-terminal acetyltransferase domain and a C-terminal lipocalin-like domain. To determine whether the lipocalin domain is required for its function, we separately expressed each domain in the S32 mutant background. The acetyltransferase domain (Domain 1) was sufficient to fully restore the wild-type phenotype, performing comparably to the full-length protein. In contrast, the lipocalin domain (Domain 2), even when targeted to the chloroplast via a transit peptide, failed to complement the pigment deficiency (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). These results indicate that the lipocalin domain is dispensable for the PtXACT function in fucoxanthin biosynthesis.

Consistent with the role of fucoxanthin in light harvesting, the xact mutant (S32) exhibited a reduced growth rate, which was restored to wild-type levels in complemented lines (Fig. 2G). Spectral data revealed that S32 shows reduced absorption capacity in the green light range between 550 and 580 nm (Fig. 2H), resembling the phenotype of the crtiso5 mutant, which also lacks fucoxanthin (20). Chlorophyll fluorescence measurements showed a decrease in maximal fluorescence (Fm) in the mutant, while the Fv/Fm ratio remained largely unchanged (Fig. 2I), suggesting a reduction in light-harvesting antenna size rather than a defect in photochemical efficiency.

XACT Acetylates Multiple Downstream Precursors in the Fucoxanthin Biosynthetic Pathway.

The absence of fucoxanthin and concomitant accumulation of fucoxanthinol in the xact mutant suggested that PtXACT likely catalyzes the acetylation of fucoxanthinol. To biochemically validate this function, the recombinant full-length PtXACT from P. tricornutum with an N-terminal maltose-binding protein (MBP) tag was expressed and purified from Escherichia coli (SI Appendix, Materials and Methods and Fig. S5). In vitro enzymatic assays demonstrated that PtXACT specifically converts fucoxanthinol (see SI Appendix, Table S2 for substrate preparation) into fucoxanthin in the presence of acetyl-CoA (Fig. 3A). No product formation was observed in control assays lacking either PtXACT or acetyl-CoA (Fig. 3A). Mass spectrometry analysis revealed a consistent 42 Da increase of both the [M + H]+ and the [M − H2O + H]+ ion peaks between the substrate and product, confirming the acetylation of a hydroxyl group (Fig. 3B).

XACT is a member of the alcohol acetyltransferase (AATase) family (PANTHER ID: PTHR28037; SI Appendix, Fig. S3). In plants, a distinct class of alcohol acyltransferases—sometimes also abbreviated as “AAT,” yet functionally and phylogenetically divergent from AATases—are classified under the PANTHER family PTHR31623. This family encompasses a broad range of enzymes, including BAHD acyltransferases, and while both PTHR28037 and PTHR31623 families harbor the conserved “HXXXD” catalytic motif (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), their substrate specificities differ substantially. The plant alcohol acyltransferases accept multiple CoA-activated acyl donors (31); in contrast, yeast members with the PTHR28037 domain strictly prefers acetyl-CoA (32). We investigated the acyl donor preference of PtXACT and found it to also exhibit strict specificity for acetyl-CoA, with only negligible activity toward propionyl-CoA among all tested acyl-CoA substrates (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). This narrow substrate preference directly accounts for the acetylation of the resultant carotenoid products.

We further expressed a truncated version of PtXACT containing only Domain 1 with an N-terminal MBP tag. In vitro enzymatic assays confirmed that this domain alone is sufficient to catalyze the conversion of fucoxanthinol into fucoxanthin, corroborating our in vivo complementation findings (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). As previously noted, the −3 bp mutation leads to deletion of the conserved residue L153 and a H154N substitution. To assess the functional importance of L153, we purified both the full-length protein and the isolated acetyltransferase domain, each lacking L153. In vitro activity assays revealed that neither variant was capable of acetylating fucoxanthinol (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), highlighting the essential role of L153 in PtXACT catalytic activity.

While a previous model of the fucoxanthin biosynthetic pathway proposed that the acetyltransferase acts primarily on allenoxanthin (19, 20), we observed an accumulation of fucoxanthinol, rather than allenoxanthin, in the xact mutant (S32). This result suggests a flexibility in the order of acetylation relative to other downstream modifications. We therefore hypothesized that i) XACT may exhibit broader substrate specificity toward multiple carotenoid intermediates; and ii) subsequent enzymatic steps can process nonacetylated precursors.

To test these hypotheses, we assessed the activity of PtXACT against a series of carotenoid substrates. Pigments ranging from violaxanthin to fucoxanthin were isolated from previously characterized mutants, saponified for removal of acetyl groups and purified by preparative HPLC (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). In vitro assays demonstrated that PtXACT acetylates 7’,8’-didehydro-neoxanthin (designated here as phaneroxanthinol, Phxol) to produce phaneroxanthin, as confirmed by mass spectrometry (Fig. 3C). The deacetylated form of haptoxanthin, namely allenoxanthin, and the non-native 9’-cis-neoxanthin were also effectively acetylated by PtXACT (Figs. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). Mass spectral analysis of the products revealed characteristic mass shifts indicative of acetylation, along with neutral losses consistent with deacetylation and dehydration due to hydroxyl removal at both terminal ends (Figs. 3 B and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S9B).

We further examined all-trans neoxanthin as a candidate substrate. Acetylation of this compound by XACT would be expected to produce dinoxanthin; this carotenoid and its potential derivatives are not naturally accumulated in P. tricornutum (Fig. 1). Consistent with this pigment profile, in vitro assays showed no activity of PtXACT toward all-trans neoxanthin. Similarly, neither diadinoxanthin nor the upstream precursor violaxanthin served as substrates for acetylation (Figs. 3G and SI Appendix, Fig. S9C). Together, these findings indicate that the substrate specificity of XACT is strictly confined to late-stage intermediates within the fucoxanthin biosynthetic pathway.

Fucoxanthin Synthesis Proceeds Through a Flexible, Nonlinear Pathway.

As discussed above, the accumulation of fucoxanthinol, rather than allenoxanthin, in the xact mutant suggests the presence of multiple branching points between allenoxanthin and fucoxanthin in the biosynthetic pathway. To investigate whether nonacetylated xanthophylls such as allenoxanthin or phaneroxanthinol can also be modified by ZEP1 or CRTISO5, respectively, to produce fucoxanthinol, we prepared deacetylated carotenoid substrates through saponification of purified pigments. In vitro assays demonstrated that PtZEP1 with cofactors added converted allenoxanthin into phaneroxanthinol (Fig. 4A), while PtCRTISO5 catalyzed the conversion of phaneroxanthinol to fucoxanthinol in the presence of reduced FAD (FADred) as a cofactor (Fig. 4A). Although the biosynthetic pathway of fucoxanthin was previously depicted as a linear sequence from allenoxanthin to fucoxanthin, our results show that both CRTISO5 and ZEP1 can process nonacetylated substrates. These findings support a revised model for fucoxanthin biosynthesis in P. tricornutum, comprising three potential branch pathways in which acetylation can occur at the level of allenoxanthin, phaneroxanthinol, or fucoxanthinol (Figs. 1 and 4B).

XACT Is Exclusively Conserved Within Chromalveolate Lineages That Accumulate Acetylated Carotenoids.

Given the widespread occurrence of acetylated carotenoids among chromalveolates, we sought to determine whether their biosynthesis is catalyzed by a conserved protein family. Using the acetyltransferase domain (Domain 1) of PtXACT as query, we performed BLAST searches against publicly available genomic and transcriptomic databases, including UniProt, with an E-value cutoff of 1e−5.

Putative orthologs of PtXACT (E-value < 1e−20) were identified in diatoms and other photosynthetic stramenopiles, haptophytes, and numerous dinoflagellates. Notably, XACT candidates were absent from lineages lacking acetylated carotenoids, such as land plants, green algae, red algae, and cryptophyte algae. In addition, XACT genes were not detected in nonphotosynthetic stramenopiles such as oomycetes, which are close relatives of photosynthetic brown algae and diatoms but do not synthesize carotenoids (33) (Dataset S3). Cyanobacteria were the only other clade in which we found homologs, with E-values ranging from 2e−11 to 3e−4. The cyanobacterial protein is presumably not involved in carotenoid biosynthesis as it appears to be not generally conserved and is virtually absent from Synechococcales, a major order of cyanobacteria.

Phylogenetic analysis using cyanobacterial sequences as the outgroup confirmed that the algal XACTs form a monophyletic clade (Fig. 5). Targeting prediction of XACT proteins from selected species supported their general localization within plastids (Dataset S4). In chromalveolate algae, nucleus-encoded plastid proteins are transported into the plastid stroma via an N-terminal bipartite presequence consisting of a signal peptide followed by a chloroplast transit peptide. By using a set of 9 different targeting prediction tools, the presence of a signal peptide could be corroborated for all 18 investigated sequences. The tools HECTAR and MULocDeep which allowed detecting the additional presence of a chloroplast transit peptide supported its presence in 14 out of the 18 XACT sequences. Analysis of the domain architecture of a larger set of XACT proteins further revealed lineage-specific differences: The lipocalin domain is present in XACT of dinoflagellates and ochrophytes from the Diatomista lineage (including diatoms and their close relatives), but absent from XACT in haptophytes and ochrophytes of the Chrysista lineage (such as brown algae and their relatives) (34).

Loss of XACT Disrupts Carotenoid Biosynthesis and Causes Neoxanthin Accumulation in Nannochloropsis Oceanica.

The broad conservation of XACT among chromalveolate algae suggests that (i) XACT is widely employed in the biosynthesis of acetylated carotenoids across diverse chromalveolate lineages, and (ii) variation in substrate specificity underlies the diversity of light-harvesting carotenoids found within these groups (4). To functionally characterize an XACT ortholog from an alga of the Chrysista lineage, we selected Nannochloropsis, an ochrophyte of the class Eustigmatophyceae, for further investigation (37). Notably, Nannochloropsis does not produce fucoxanthin but instead accumulates other acetylated carotenoids, primarily vaucheriaxanthin 3-acetate 19’ acylates (Vaux), together with violaxanthin (38). Previous studies suggested that Nannochloropsis converts neoxanthin to dinoxanthin via an acetylation step (18), implying the involvement of an uncharacterized acetyltransferase. The availability of a well-established genetic toolkit renders Nannochloropsis an ideal system for investigating the functional coevolution of XACT enzymes and their associated carotenoid pathways (39).

To directly assess the function of the XACT ortholog in Nannochloropsis (NoXACT, No09G02500), we generated several N. oceanica XACT mutants using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated double-strand breaks, followed by homology-directed targeted integration of a donor DNA cassette carrying an antibiotic resistance gene for selection (SI Appendix, Materials and Methods). Genotyping confirmed successful disruption of NoXACT, as evidenced by the insertion of the resistance cassette and the flanking homology arms (Fig. 6 A and B). Pigment profiling of the NoXACT knockout mutants revealed a complete loss of Vaux and the concomitant accumulation of a distinct compound (Fig. 6C), which UV-Vis and mass spectrometry analyses identified as all-trans-neoxanthin (Fig. 6C). These results establish NoXACT as essential for Vaux biosynthesis and indicate that all-trans-neoxanthin is a precursor in this pathway. We further propose that dinoxanthin, the acetylated derivative of neoxanthin, likely serves as a metabolic intermediate (Fig. 1). Nevertheless, we cannot rule out the possibility of nonlinear routing in Vaux biosynthesis, analogous to the branched pathway observed in diatom fucoxanthin biosynthesis.

To further verify the activity of NoXACT, we employed two independent strategies: (i) heterologous expression in Nicotiana benthamiana, which naturally accumulate neoxanthin isomers, and (ii) in vitro assays with recombinant enzyme. Transient expression of NoXACT in tobacco leaves resulted in the appearance of two new pigment peaks, identified as all-trans- and 9′-cis-dinoxanthin based on their spectral characteristics and retention times (Fig. 6D and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). This was accompanied by a decrease in endogenous neoxanthin levels and a lowered neoxanthin-to-violaxanthin ratio (Fig. 6D). Although the conversion in planta was limited, these findings confirm the functional activity of NoXACT in a heterologous system and highlight its potential for engineering algal carotenoid biosynthesis in terrestrial plants for biofortification (40).

Finally, to obtain direct biochemical evidence and enable comparison with PtXACT, we performed in vitro enzymatic assays using recombinant NoXACT protein, as detailed in the following section.

Divergent Substrate Specificity of Carotenoid Acetyltransferase Homologs Across Diverse Species.

To characterize the catalytic activity of NoXACT and other XACT family members from genetically intractable chromalveolate lineages, we performed a series of in vitro assays using recombinant enzymes. A panel of allenic substrates (SI Appendix, Table S2 and Dataset S5), including fucoxanthinol, phaneroxanthinol, allenoxanthin, all-trans-neoxanthin, and 9’-cis-neoxanthin, was employed to evaluate the substrate preferences of selected XACT orthologs. The first four of these pigments are known precursors of major marine carotenoids. The enzymes tested included PtXACT, NoXACT, IgXACT (from the haptophyte Isochrysis galbana), StXACT (from the dinoflagellate Symbiodinium tridacnidorum), and EsXACT (from the brown alga Ectocarpus siliculosus). All enzymes were heterologously expressed in and purified from E. coli (SI Appendix, Materials and Methods and Fig. S5).

In these assays (Fig. 7), PtXACT converted preferentially allenoxanthin (n4) and phaneroxanthinol (n5), and a limited conversion of fucoxanthinol (n6) to fucoxanthin (a6) was also detected. Products corresponding to acetylated 9’-cis-neoxanthin (=9’-cis dinoxanthin, a7) or all-trans-neoxanthin (=dinoxanthin, a2) were not observed, however, consistent with the previous assays using individual substrates (Fig. 3H). Haptophytes such as Isochrysis galbana predominantly accumulate fucoxanthin-derived carotenoids and, like diatoms, lack dinoxanthin (41). Consistently, IgXACT mirrored PtXACT in its activity profile, acting on fucoxanthinol, phaneroxanthinol, and allenoxanthin, but not all-trans-neoxanthin, reflecting the natural absence of dinoxanthin in haptophytes.

As previously noted, eustigmatophytes such as Nannochloropsis synthesize Vaux instead of fucoxanthin, likely utilizing all-trans-neoxanthin as a precursor. Similarly, many dinoflagellates, including Symbiodinium species, produce peridinin rather than fucoxanthin, with all-trans-neoxanthin proposed to serve as a biosynthetic precursor via the intermediate all-trans-dinoxanthin (42). In line with the different carotenoid biosynthetic pathways proposed for these algae, both NoXACT and StXACT exhibited strong activity with all-trans-neoxanthin, whereas PtXACT and IgXACT showed no detectable activity with this substrate (Fig. 7).

Beyond their distinct specificity for all-trans-neoxanthin, XACT enzymes also exhibit catalytic promiscuity. For instance, multiple members display activity toward 9’-cis-neoxanthin (n7), a compound that commonly accumulates in land plants, green algae, and algae with secondary plastids of green algal origin but is absent from red algae and chromalveolates (43). Furthermore, NoXACT and StXACT also act on allenoxanthin (n4), phaneroxanthinol (n5), and fucoxanthinol (n6) (Fig. 7). Since these substrates are not native to the source organisms of these enzymes, our results suggest a lack of selective pressure against such ancillary activities in eustigmatophytes and dinoflagellates. Consistent with our observations for PtXACT, however, the XACT enzymes from other species were not able to utilize either violaxanthin or diadinoxanthin as substrates (SI Appendix, Fig. S11) suggesting a strict specificity of XACT toward xanthophylls with a C6-C8 allenic group at the 3-hydroxy-β-ionone ring that is acetylated.

Similar to diatoms, brown algae utilize fucoxanthin as a key light-harvesting pigment but lack the diatom-specific enzymes required for its biosynthesis. Two potential pathways have been proposed for fucoxanthin production from neoxanthin: One involves addition of water and allylic oxidation prior to acetylation (44), and the other proceeds via acetylation followed by hydratation/allylic oxidation with dinoxanthin as an intermediate (19). Our finding that EsXACT efficiently acetylates fucoxanthinol (n6)—its preferred substrate among those tested—while showing no activity toward trans-neoxanthin (n2) (Fig. 7) strongly supports the former pathway as the primary route in E. siliculosus. While an auxiliary role for the alternative pathway in fucoxanthin biosynthesis in E. siliculosus or other brown algae cannot be completely ruled out, our work indicates the most urgent next research objective: identifying the missing oxidoreductase responsible for converting neoxanthin to fucoxanthinol (Fig. 1).

To corroborate our findings, we quantified the apparent Km and Vmax values of all five XACT enzymes by using individual substrates and analyzing the data by hyperbolic fits of saturation plots and linear fits of Lineweaver–Burk plots (Table 1 and SI Appendix, Table S3 and Fig. S12). A preferred substrate typically exhibits either higher binding affinity (reflected by a lower Km) or a greater catalytic rate (indicated by a higher Vmax). In the presence of multiple substrates competing for the enzyme’s active center their relative conversion rates can be estimated by comparing their specificity constants kcat/Km (45), that are proportional to Vmax/Km at a fixed enzyme concentration. By calculating Vmax/Km for each tested substrate and normalizing the results to the substrate with the highest value, we estimated their relative substrate specificities (Table 1 and SI Appendix, Table S3). Collectively, these results are consistent with those obtained from the mixed-substrate assays (Fig. 7 and SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Specifically, PtXACT displayed a higher specificity for allenoxanthin and phaneroxanthinol relative to fucoxanthinol; in contrast, EsXACT showed a strong specificity for fucoxanthinol. We note the caveat that for lipophilic compounds such as carotenoids, the solubilization method can substantially affect substrate availability (46), thereby altering the apparent Km and Vmax values determined in vitro.

To investigate the significance of the lipocalin domain present in a subset of XACT enzymes, we compared the catalytic behavior of the full-length PtXACT protein with that of its truncated variant, which retains only the AATase domain (Domain 1). Although the absence of the lipocalin domain shifted the Km values in favor of phaneroxanthinol and fucoxanthinol, the concomitant increase in Vmax for allenoxanthin left the relative substrate preference profile of PtXACT essentially unchanged (Table 1). Future studies should investigate how the sequence of the AATase domain itself modulates substrate selectivity. Given that lipocalins are known to bind small-molecule ligands (47), we emphasize that the lipocalin domain may still fulfill critical regulatory functions in vivo—roles that are likely masked in our in vitro assays, due to lack of the native ligands of this domain. Future work should therefore identify the endogenous ligands of PtXACT’s lipocalin domain and assess whether ligand binding modulates the AATase activity of PtXACT.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that evolutionary divergence in XACT enzyme specificity has shaped the diversification of carotenoid biosynthetic pathways across algal lineages. The resulting lineage-specific substrate preferences among XACT homologs have tailored the spectrum of light-harvesting pigments, contributing to the adaptation of these organisms to diverse marine environments.

Materials and Methods

Full experimental details are provided in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods. Briefly, algal strains were maintained under defined culture conditions, and targeted gene knockouts and complementation lines in P. tricornutum as well as knockout mutants in Nannochloropsis oceanica were generated using CRISPR-Cas9 approaches. Pigment composition was quantified by HPLC, and recombinant proteins were heterologously expressed in E. coli, purified, and subjected to in vitro enzymatic and kinetic assays with purified substrates. Functional validation included transient expression in Nicotiana benthamiana. All products were identified by using photodiode array (PDA) or mass spectrometry detectors. Physiological characterization of mutants was performed through growth and chlorophyll fluorescence measurements. Evolutionary relationships were examined using maximum-likelihood phylogenetic reconstruction, and statistical significance was evaluated using appropriate replicated datasets.
