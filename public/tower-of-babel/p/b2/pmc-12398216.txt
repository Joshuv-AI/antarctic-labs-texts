Thyroid hematoma after blunt neck trauma
Authors: Nogognan Ignace Lengane, Milckisédek Judicaël Marouruana Somé, Charles Ilboudo, Solo Traoré, Bertin Priva Ouédraogo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThyroid gland hematoma following blunt neck trauma is a rare but potentially life-threatening condition due to the risk of airway compromise. While it most commonly occurs in goitrous glands, unusual mechanisms of trauma can still make such cases exceptional. We present a case of thyroid haematoma following scarf-related neck strangulation in a patient with a longstanding goitre. Although uncommon, thyroid injury should be considered in cases of neck trauma or swelling. Stable hematomas without airway compromise can be managed conservatively. Surgical intervention is warranted primarily when airway obstruction is threatened.Keywords: Thyroid, Hematoma, Trauma, Neck

Introduction

Blunt trauma to the neck rarely causes rupture or haematoma of the thyroid gland, though the risk increases in the presence of pre-existing thyroid pathology such as a goitre due to vascularity. Most reported cases involve road traffic collisions or direct impact injuries [1,2].

We describe an unusual case of thyroid haemorrhage following scarf-related neck strangulation in a patient with a known goitre, and we discuss its clinical management in light of existing literature.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old female patient with no previous medical illness experienced a blunt neck trauma to motorcycle accident. She was known to have a goitre for several years, but she did not use any medication. The patient's scarf became entangled in the motorcycle wheels, resulting in strangulation and causing her to fall. Upon arrival, the patient was conscious and fully oriented. She reported neck swelling and dysphagia (Fig. 1). She did not experience any loss of consciousness during the incident. Her vital signs at presentation were respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, heart rate of 86 beats per minute, 100 % O2 saturation on room air, blood pressure 146/88, and temperature 37,3 °C. She was not in respiratory distress, her voice was hoarse immediately following the injury. Fibreoptic evaluation for upper aerodigestive tract showed a bulging of the left lateral pharyngeal wall with mass effect on the larynx. The computed tomography (CT) of the head and neck revealed thyroid haemorrhage in the left lobe and mild tracheal deviation to the right without intracranial haemorrhage or cervical spine deformity (Fig. 2). The trauma resulted in haemorrhage within a previously existing goitre. The patient was admitted to the surgical intensive care unit for close airway observation, and monitoring for progression of her neck hematoma and vital signs. Over the ensuing four days, the size of the patient's neck hematoma remained stable, no stridor or respiratory distress developed, and the dysphagia and voice weakness resolved. Follow-up laboratory test revealed normal thyroïd function at 1, 3, and 6 months after the event.

Discussion

Thyroid hematoma secondary to blunt neck trauma is extremely rare. Traumatic rupture of the thyroid gland can be subclassified as either a rupture of a normal thyroid or a rupture of a thyroid with a preexisting underlying pathology [3]. Thyroid rupture usually occurs when a preexisting thyroid pathology is present. Most of the cases reported in the literature describe this injury in association with a previously goiterous gland [[1], [2], [3]]. The traumatic rupture of a previously normal thyroid gland is very rare. The goiterous gland carries a high risk of bleeding after trauma due to its size and vascularity [1,4,5]. Several circumstances have been associated with haemorrhage of the thyroid gland: trauma, deceleration injury, cervical hyperflexion, and Valsalva manoeuvres that increase venous pressure, including straining during defecation or heavy lifting [3,6].

Symptoms of thyroid hematoma are dyspnoea, focal tenderness and swelling in the anterior part of the neck. Other symptoms like dysphagia, hoarseness of voice and overlying ecchymosis [2,7]. Transient hyperthyroidism and thyroid storm have been reported after a thyroid blunt trauma [8].

Emergency radiological exams, such as CT scan and/or neck ultrasonography, may establish preoperatively the diagnosis of thyroid injury [1]. CT may confirm the thyroid rupture and surrounding hematoma, achieving a correct evaluation of larynx and upper digestive tract. The outcome is favourable and no mortality is reported in all cases in the literature [9]. Fibreoptic evaluation of larynx is also helpful to rule out laryngotracheal injury [6].

Currently, there are no standardized guidelines for managing blunt thyroid injuries. The treatment of choices between conservative treatment and surgical intervention depends mostly on the clinical symptoms of the patients [10,11]. However, Heizmann proposed a classification system and treatment algorithm, recommending emergency neck exploration in cases involving airway compression, laryngeal trauma, or major vascular injury [12,13]. A recent literature review by Lemke identified 33 cases of thyroid rupture over the past 30 years, with over half of the patients having a history of thyroid disease and 48 % undergoing surgical intervention [14]. Similarly, Von Ahnen reviewed 36 cases, reporting that 59 % underwent surgery, with no fatalities attributed to the injury [15]. For our patient, a nonoperative approach was chosen, along with continuous monitoring of vital signs, as she remained fully conscious and showed no signs of worsening thyroid haemorrhage and dysphagia.

Conclusion

Despite their rarity, thyroid injuries should remain part of the differential diagnosis following blunt cervical trauma or in the presence of cervical swelling. In the acute phase, if the thyroid hematoma is stable and does not compromise the airway, conservative management with close observation is generally appropriate. Surgical exploration of the neck is primarily indicated when there is a risk of airway obstruction.
