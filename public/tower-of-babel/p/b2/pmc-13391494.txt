Synergistic pulsed electrocatalysis enables green and efficient synthesis of nylon monomer adipic acid
Authors: Weijin Cao, Changlong Wang, Minwei Song, Hongmei Li, Min Liu, Yufeng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdipic acid (AA), a pivotal monomer, is produced via high-temperature nitric acid oxidation of cyclohexanone–cyclohexanol mixture (KA oil); a NO2-intensive process with a large carbon footprint. Electricity-powered electro-oxidative upgrading of KA is appealing, but impractical due to poor O2 utilization, oxygen evolution, and rapid catalyst passivation under potentials, causing an activity–stability–selectivity trade-off. We present an integrated electrocatalytic platform combining an α-FeOOH/NiFe layered double hydroxide (NiFe-LDH) heterojunction catalyst with pulsed electrolysis (PE) for green, efficient AA production. The heterointerface directs electron bridging between α-FeOOH and NiFe-LDH, lowering energy barriers for C–C scission, and creating surface sites for selective AA. PE mode decouples electro-oxidation from catalytic-site regeneration, cyclically refreshing poisoned centers to prevent intermediate accumulation. This strategy achieves 6.73 mmol h−1 cm−2 AA yield with 90.06% Faradaic efficiency over 300 h. Moreover a flow electrolyzer coupling anodic AA synthesis with cathodic CO2-to-formate conversion at 300 mA cm−2 enables scalable, sustainable nylon-monomer production.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

Adipic acid (AA) is a key commodity chemical for the production of nylon-6,6, polyurethanes, plasticizers, and lubricants1–5. Its industrial synthesis predominantly relies on the nitric acid oxidation of KA oil (cyclohexanone-cyclohexanol mixture)1–3,6,7. In chip manufacturing, KA oil also serves as a high-purity solvent essential for photoresist formulation, coating uniformity, and edge bead removal. It also serves as a cleaner for pipelines and wafer residues, ensuring process stability in advanced nodes. However, this long-standing dominant thermocatalytic route requires large quantities of corrosive nitric acid, operates under harsh reaction conditions, entails high energy input and capital cost, and releases substantial NOx emissions (Fig. 1a). These challenges highlight the need for a strategy that green, mild, and sustainable produces nylon-plastics for enhanced circular economy.

Renewable electricity-powered electrochemical processes offer an attractive and environmentally benign alternative for chemical synthesis (Fig. 1b)8–10. Among the candidate electrocatalysts for KA oil oxidation, NiFe-based layered double hydroxides (LDHs) are particularly promising electrocatalysts due to their compositional tunability, layered architecture with abundant accessible sites11–14, and synergistic Ni–Fe interactions that promote C–C bond cleavage15–20. Electrocatalysis proceeds via the in-situ transformation of Ni2+ to Ni3+/γ-NiOOH as the primary active phase21–24. However, a fundamental challenge is that while high potentials generate sufficient NiOOH for rapid oxidation, they also accelerate catalyst dissolution and structural degradation as well as exacerbate the competitive oxygen evolution reaction (OER)25–29; the later not only consumes electrons for organic oxidation, but also produces non-selective hydroxyl radicals that over-oxidize or decompose the target products30–35. A long-standing challenge in LDH-based electrocatalysts in AA synthesis is the inherent “activity–stability–selectivity” trade-off36–40.

Rational design of heterojunctions provides a promising strategy to mitigate this trade-off41–43. We thus reason that an α-FeOOH/NiFe-LDH heterojunction would simultaneously enhance catalyst robustness and modulate its electronic structure. This is due to the interfacial Fe3+δ–O–Ni2+ coupling would stabilize the LDH layers against collapse and promote directional electron transfer from Ni2+ to Fe3+δ, thereby facilitating the generation of NiOOH at lower overpotentials. Moreover, for multi-step, multi-intermediate transformations, the static potential in constant-potential electrolysis (CE) often causes surface buildup of intermediates or products, blocking active sites (“poisoning”) and shifting the reaction toward undesired side pathways. This also requires further optimizations.

To address these issues, we introduce pulsed electrolysis (PE) as a co-optimization strategy with the aim to modulate the applied potential dynamically, providing temporal control over elementary steps within the catalytic cycle. As a result, we report here a synergistic platform that integrates an α-FeOOH/NiFe-LDH heterojunction catalyst (NiFe-1), synthesized via a Fenton-like process, with a tailored PE protocol. On the one hand, the catalytic heterojunction promotes the formation of active NiOOH at reduced overpotentials. On the other hand, the pulsed waveform decouples surface events due to the fact that high-potential pulses drive selective C–C cleavage, whereas low-potential pulses facilitate product desorption, restore active sites, and suppress OER. Altogether, our strategy effectively overcomes the activity–stability–selectivity trade-off and enables a green, efficient, and durable electrosynthetic route to AA.

Results

To elucidate the role of α-FeOOH incorporation in NiFe-LDH, α-FeOOH/NiFe-LDH (NiFe-1), and control samples pristine NiFe-LDH (NiFe), and nickel foam (NF-1) were all synthesized via a Fenton-like process. We then evaluated their performances toward the cyclohexanone oxidation reaction (KOR) in a three-electrode configuration. The linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves (Fig. 1c and S1) recorded in 1 M KOH with and without 0.4 M cyclohexanone highlight the intrinsic differences among the catalysts. In blank electrolyte (1 M KOH), NiFe-1 shows the highest oxygen evolution reaction (OER) activity, with an onset potential of ~1.48 V vs. RHE and a weak Ni2+/Ni3+ redox at ~1.38 V vs. RHE. Upon introducing cyclohexanone, the current densities of all electrodes increase dramatically, and the onset potentials shift to much lower values than for OER, confirming that KOR is thermodynamically and kinetically more favorable. Importantly, NiFe-1 reaches 1000 mA cm−2 at 1.41 V vs. RHE, whereas NiFe only attains 97.72 mA cm−2 and α-FeOOH exhibits a notably low current density under the same conditions (Fig. 1d, S2). This suggests the interfacial synergistic effect of the α-FeOOH/NiFe heterostructure in enhancing the electrochemical activity.

Corroborating these findings, the Tafel slopes (Fig. 1e) reveal NiFe-1 displays the smallest slope (29.4 mV dec−1) among all samples, indicating markedly improved reaction kinetics compared to NiFe and NF-1. To elucidate the potential-dependent interfacial processes, we next conduct electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). In the high-frequency regime, the impedance response reflects surface electrooxidation dynamics, whereas the low-frequency region captures non-uniform charge distribution and the behavior of adsorbed oxidized intermediates. The Bode plots shown in Fig. 1f and S3–S7 clearly distinguish the catalytic transitions. Under OER conditions, the low-frequency inflection point for NiFe-1 appears at 1.45 V vs. RHE, consistent with the onset of OER. A similar threshold is observed for NiFe, whereas NF-1 exhibits a slightly higher onset at 1.50 V vs. RHE (Figs. S5 and S7). Upon addition of cyclohexanone, KOR becomes dominant, and the phase-angle peak decreases and shifts to higher frequencies; this indicates the rapid oxidation of adsorbed cyclohexanone and accelerated charge-transfer kinetics. Notably, NiFe-1 exhibits a significantly smaller phase angle in the high-frequency region and a characteristic double-peak feature (Fig. 1f), indicative of a more dynamic and efficient Ni3+ generation–consumption cycle. Those observations confirm that α-FeOOH effectively facilitates the formation of the active NiOOH phase, which in turn accelerates KOR kinetics.

Moreover, the catalytic performance of NiFe-1 also stems from its larger electrochemical active surface area (ECSA) and enhanced charge-transfer behavior. Indeed, NiFe-1 shows the highest non-Faradaic double-layer capacitance (Cdl = 9.81 mF cm−2), corresponding to an expanded active surface area of 245.25 cm2 (Fig. 1g and S8–S11). In the ECSA-normalized LSV curves, NiFe-1 still outperforms NiFe and NF-1, confirming its improved catalytic activity. Furthermore, NiFe-1 demonstrates high activity toward KOR (Fig. 1h), further validating its robust catalytic versatility.

However, prolonged electrolysis led to progressive current decay, which we attribute to catalyst surface changes that hinder cyclohexanone adsorption. Constant-potential electrolysis (CE) showed a rapid increase followed by a sharp decline in current density (Fig. 2a). This behavior originates from the exothermic nature of KOR: the initial temperature rise boosts catalytic activity, but as cyclohexanone is depleted, heat generation diminishes. Sustained operation promotes the formation of a passivation layer, increasing charge-transfer resistance and impeding the recovery of current density.

To track reaction pathways, we periodically sampled the electrolyte for NMR analysis (Fig. S12–S14). The charge-dependent spectra (Fig. 2b) show progressive cyclohexanone consumption and AA accumulation, identifying that AA is the dominant product. Minor quantities of glutaric acid (GA) and succinic acid (SA) were also detected as secondary products. At 1.474 V vs. RHE, NiFe-1 achieved an AA productivity of 3.89 mmol h−1 cm−2 and a maximum Faradaic efficiency (FE) of 75.03%; these performances are higher than those of NiFe and NF-1 (Figs. S15–S16). These results highlight the pivotal role of the α-FeOOH/NiFe-LDH heterojunction. To understand CE-induced deactivation, the active-site accessibility was assessed using open-circuit potential (OCP) measurements at varying electrolysis durations (Fig. 2c). For NiFe-1, the addition of cyclohexanone led to an OCP shift of 0.448V indicative of strong adsorption. Following prolonged CE operation, this OCP shift was reduced to 0.373 V, indicating a decline in adsorption capacity due to surface blocking. Simultaneously, an increase in charge-transfer resistance was observed (Fig. 2d), verifying the gradual occupation of active sites by adsorbed intermediates and products.

Cyclic voltammetry (Fig. S17) revealed a Ni2+/Ni3+ redox transition at ~1.35 V vs. RHE. Complementary in-situ Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 2e) confirmed the formation of γ-NiOOH at ~1.30 V vs. RHE, which suggests a potential-driven interfacial reconstruction to generate the active NiOOH phase. This dynamic interfacial reconstruction governs the adsorption/desorption behavior of reaction species. Furthermore, in-situ FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 2f) captured the progressive accumulation of adsorbed AA and cyclohexanone, with characteristic bands detected in the ranges of 1550–1515, 1417–1400, 1230–1180, 1258–1129, and 1160–1135 cm−1. The gradual increase in band intensities provides direct evidence of the progressive accumulation of both reactants and products on the catalyst surface. Such accumulation physically blocks the catalyst's active sites, consistent with the electrochemical poisoning behavior observed.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further clarified the impact of catalyst reconstruction on adsorption energetics. Two surface states were examined: the original α-FeOOH/NiFe-LDH and the reconstructed α-FeOOH/Ni(Fe)OOH (Fig. 2g and h); the former binds cyclohexanone (–2.33 eV) and AA (–2.52 eV) strongly, which is consistent with the block of active sites under low potentials. In contrast, the reconstructed α-FeOOH/Ni(Fe)OOH surface shows a much weaker AA adsorption AA with an adsorption energy of 0.87 eV. While this favors the product desorption, t is accompanied by the compromised binding of cyclohexanone (adsorption energy is –0.86 eV). These contrasting adsorption behaviors reveal an intrinsic mismatch imposed by static-potential operation, which contributes critically to catalyst deactivation observed in CE mode.

These findings indicate that the catalyst surface undergoes dynamic yet competing processes, which cannot be effectively controlled under constant potential operations. Thus, we decide to employ pulsed electrolysis (PE) as a means to temporally decouple reactant activation and surface regeneration. By alternating a high potential (EH = 1.474 V vs. RHE) to drive oxidation with a low potential (EL = 1.324 V vs. RHE) to refresh the surface, PE mitigates product accumulation and restores active sites (Fig. 2i). The carbon-free reverse current observed during high-to-low transitions further facilitates removal of passivated surface species30–32. This PE strategy ultimately enables sustained catalytic activity by synchronizing surface phase transitions with reaction dynamics.

The advantage of this PE protocol is illustrated by the stark contrast between chronoamperometric behavior under CE and PE modes (Fig. 3a, b). Under CE operation, the current density initially increases, partly due to a temperature rise from the exothermic KOR reaction, but then rapidly decays irreversibly. This decay directly reflects the accumulation of passivating species and the blockage of active sites, a fact fully consistent with the OCP and FTIR analyses (vide supra). In sharp contrast, the PE mode maintains a stable and nearly constant current density. This suggests that PE strategy effectively suppresses surface poisoning by periodically refreshing the catalyst surface and accelerating product desorption. Post-electrolysis OCP measurements further corroborate this regeneration effect (Fig. 3c). Notably, electrodes subjected to PE maintain a markedly higher cyclohexanone adsorption capacity relative to those operated under CE conditions. This strongly suggests that periodic low-potential intervals in PE effectively mitigate poisoning-mediated active site loss. EIS measurements further reveal only negligible changes after PE operation, whereas CE operation led to significant increases in charge-transfer resistance (Fig. 3d). Taken together, these observations demonstrate that PE preserves the intrinsic activity of NiFe−1 by synchronizing surface reconstruction and desorption dynamics. As a result, the performances in PE model outperform that of CE at all applied potentials all applied potentials, achieving a maximum AA yield of 6.73 mmol h−1 cm−2 and a FE of 90.06% at 1.474 V vs. RHE; these values correspond to 1.74-fold and 1.20-fold improvements compared with CE mode, respectively (Fig. 3e, f).

Notably, an inadequate tEH results in incomplete oxidation of key intermediates, which undergo premature desorption during tEL and consequently compromise the overall product selectivity. In contrast, excessively prolonged tEL provides no kinetic advantage; instead, it reduces the time-averaged reaction rate. Through systematic optimization of these parameters (Fig. 3g and S18–S20), the optimal time window was identified as a high potential of 1.474 V vs. RHE (tEH = 20 s) and a low potential of 1.324 V vs. RHE (tEL = 2 s), achieving an AA yield of 93.69%. This condition ensures sufficient oxidative driving force at high potential for complete intermediate conversion while enabling rapid desorption and regeneration of active sites during the short low-potential interval. Through systematic optimization of the amplitude and duration of high and low potentials in PE, we already demonstrated that the high potential, which is the oxidation potential, provides the essential thermodynamic driving force for KOR. It is suggested that an excessively high oxidation potential exacerbates the OER, whereas a low potential fails to effectively initiate the oxidation step. The low potential functions as the reduction potential, preventing the passivation of active sites caused by over-oxidation and thereby maintaining the stability of the active phase. On the other hand, it also accelerates the desorption of products (e.g., adipic acid) from the electrode surface, alleviating concentration polarization and preserving electrode stability. Figure S21 further quantitatively analyzed the electrolysis process in PE mode, and we found that the formation of by-products was significantly inhibited. While the relative concentrations of glutaric acid and succinic acid all decreased, the relative concentration of adipic acid significantly increased to 93.69%. As a result, the NiFe-1 catalyst is competitive with reported electrocatalysts for KOR at comparable potentials (Table S4).

Moreover, substrate concentrations, electrolyte composition, pH are all found to be crucial. While the optimum highest current density is shown at the cyclohexanone concentration of 400 mM (volcano-type relationship), the electrolyte composition affects the overall charge transfer kinetics, which are confirmed by the EIS analysis. Moreover, the pH of the electrolyte is also sensitive. While the low concentration of OH− cannot effectively initiate the reaction due to insufficient OH−, performing the reaction at high pH increases the solution viscosity, which significantly intensifies the resistance to mass transfer. It is worth noting that the conductivity of 1 M KOH (0.43 S/m) is significantly higher than that of the same concentration of NaOH (0.28 S/m). It provides a more favorable condition for charge transfer kinetics and interfacial mass transfer (Figs. S22–25).

To directly visualize the dynamic surface processes under PE operation, we conducted time-resolved in-situ FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 4a). Under CE mode, the IR signals for adsorbed AA intensify continuously, confirming the progressive surface coverage. Upon switching to the PE mode, these adsorption signals markedly weaken during the low-potential phases, however. This observation provides direct, real-time evidence for the cyclic adsorption and desorption of products.

In order to further explore the dynamic evolution process of catalytic active sites in PE mode, in-situ Raman spectroscopy was carried out by extending the pulse period proportionally while maintaining the constant oxidation/regeneration time ratio of PE mode. Figure S26 shows that the high potential oxidizes Ni2+ into highly active γ-Ni3+OOH, and the low potential enables the reversible reduction of Ni3+ back to Ni2+. Those observations directly confirm that the PE mode achieves cyclic oxidation and in-situ regeneration of active sites. We thus propose a catalytic cycle for the PE-driven oxidation (Fig. 4b): (1) During the low-potential (EL) phase, the catalyst surface is refreshed, and cyclohexanone adsorbs on the regenerated active sites. (2) Upon switching to the high-potential (EH) phase, the surface reconstructs to form NiOOH, which drives the selective oxidation of cyclohexanone to AA. (3) The subsequent return to the low potential triggers the desorption of the AA product, completing the cycle and freeing the sites for the next cycle. Those processes ensure sustained high activity and selectivity. The performance of NiFe-1 was again supported by the DFT calculations, which provide thermodynamic and electronic evidence. The projected density of states (PDOS) reveals that the NiFe-1 heterojunction exhibits a d-band center closer to the Fermi level (d-band center is −1.71 eV, and −2.04 eV for NiFe-1 and NiFe, respectively; Fig. S27), and a more continuous electronic state distribution compared to NiFe (Fig. 4c and S28); this enhances its electron transfer capability and interaction with adsorbates.

We systematically investigated the interfacial charge transfer in NiFe-1 by combining Bader charge analysis and DFT calculations. Bader charge analysis shows that the total charge of NiFe-1 decreases by 2.0121 e, confirming electron transfer from NiFe-LDH to α-FeOOH (Table S1). Notably, the substantial charge loss at Ni sites Ni sites (−0.291 e/atom) compared to Fe sites (−0.102 e/atom) clearly shows that Ni serves as the primary contributor to interfacial electron transfer (Fig. S29). Moreover, differential charge density maps visually verify this charge transfer, with electron depletion regions around Ni sites in NiFe-LDH and electron accumulation regions around metal sites in α-FeOOH (Fig. S30). The fact that electron depletion is mainly concentrated at Ni sites agrees well with the Bader charge analysis, jointly validating the mechanism and key sites of interfacial charge transfer. Most importantly, the computed free energy landscape for the complete reaction pathway (Fig. 4d) identifies that the oxidation of 6-hydroxycaproic acid (C6H10O3) to adipic acid (C6H10O4) as the rate-determining step (RDS) for the NiFeOOH system, which limits the overall reaction kinetics. This step exhibits the highest free energy barrier (2.02 eV) among all elementary steps. In stark contrast, the energy barrier for this key step is dramatically lowered to 0.34 eV on the α-FeOOH/NiFeOOH (NiFe-1) heterojunction model due to the interfacial synergy. The significant decrease in the reaction energy barrier confirms the core role of α-FeOOH in stabilizing the key transition state and promoting the C-C bond breaking/oxidation process in the RDS. Figure 4e is the atomic structure model of the optimized NiFeOOH and α-FeOOH/NiFeOOH system after adsorbing different intermediates during the reaction process, and the adsorption configuration differences of the intermediates on the surface of the two catalysts are intuitively compared. As a result, high activity and selectivity were observed experimentally. Nevertheless, it should be noted that DFT simulations are based on simplified structural models and idealized computational parameters, which cannot fully capture the dynamic evolution of surface species and the complex multi-field coupling effects in real catalytic environments. In addition, the inherent approximations in exchange-correlation functionals may lead to certain deviations in the quantitative description of transient states and adsorption configurations during the reaction process.

We subsequently correlated the performance of NiFe-1 with its composition and microstructure using a comprehensive suite of characterization techniques. Electron microscopy reveals a well-defined architecture, where vertically aligned nanosheets are uniformly anchored on nickel foam (Fig. 5a). Figure S31 further illustrates the ultrathin nanosheet-liked structure of NiFe-1 with a layered and wrinkled texture. TEM (Fig. 5b) and AFM (Fig. 5c) further confirm the ultrathin nature of these nanosheets, with a thickness of ~1.5 nm. This ultrathin structure maximizes the exposure of coordinatively unsaturated sites that are essential for the dynamic catalytic turnover process41. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping demonstrates a homogeneous distribution of Ni and Fe throughout the nanosheet framework (Fig. 5d). XRD analysis (Fig. 5e) identifies characteristic reflections corresponding to both α-FeOOH (PDF#26-0792) and NiFe-LDH (PDF#18-0877) phases, validating the successful construction of the designed α-FeOOH/NiFe-LDH heterojunction structure. The ICP-AES test results in Table S2 show that the Fe content in NiFe increased from 0.05 wt% to 0.15 wt% in NiFe-1. All those results validate the successful construction of the designed α-FeOOH/NiFe-LDH heterojunction structure.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) provides further insight into the interfacial electronic interaction (Fig. S32). The high-resolution Ni 2p spectra (Fig. 5f) display a positive binding-energy shift of ~0.3 eV for NiFe-1 relative to NiFe, indicating reduced electron density at Ni sites. This binding energy shift is consistent with the occurrence of interfacial charge transfer from NiFe-LDH to the more electronegative Fe sites in α-FeOOH, reflecting strong electronic coupling and a modified local electronic structure. Such a charge redistribution is favorable for weakening Ni sites–adsorbate interactions, thereby facilitating intermediate desorption and accelerating reaction kinetics17,44. Notably, the XPS signatures of NiFe-1 remain essentially unchanged after electrocatalysis (Figs. S33 and S34), evidencing the robust catalyst structural and electronic stability imparted by the heterojunction under PE operation.

To probe the local coordination environments and oxidation states, Fe and Ni K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy measurements were performed. The Fe K-edge XANES spectrum of NiFe-1 exhibits an absorption edge position closer to that of Fe2O3 than Fe foil, confirming that Fe predominantly exists in the +3 oxidation state (Fig. 5g and 5g illustration). Quantitative curve-fitting analyses of the Fourier-transformed EXAFS (Fig. 5h, Table S3, Fig. S35–S39) reveals a substantial decrease in the Fe–O–Ni/Fe coordination number for NiFe-1 (3.6 ± 1.2) relative to the pristine NiFe sample (5.6 ± 1.6). This decrease in coordination number indicates interfacial structural distortion and the generation of uncoordinated metal sites, which are crucial in stabilizing reaction intermediates and facilitating C–C bond cleavage. Complementary Ni K-edge XAS analysis (Fig. 5i and 5i illustration) shows an enhanced white-line intensity in NiFe-1, a characteristic indicative of a higher Ni3+ proportion. This observation is consistent with the interfacial electron-transfer mechanism inferred from XPS results. Moreover, the Ni K-edge FT-EXAFS spectra (Fig. 5j and S40–S44) also show a reduced intensity for the Ni–O–M scattering path, corroborating a lower Ni coordination number; an observation analogous to that obtained from the Fe K-edge analysis. The consistent reduction in coordination numbers of both Ni and Fe confirms the formation of a highly defective metal interface with abundant un-coordinated active sites.

Wavelet transform EXAFS analysis further clarifies the catalytic relevance of these interfacial structural motifs (Fig. 5k, and 5l). At the Fe K-edge, strong Fe–O contributions centered at R ≈ 1.5 Å and Fe–O–M (M = Ni/Fe) scattering features in R ≈ 2.5–3.5 Å show that Fe species in α-FeOOH remains primarily oxygen-coordinated, while participating in indirect Fe–O–Ni interactions across the hetero-interface. For Ni K-edge, Ni–O signals at R ≈ 1.6 Å and Ni–O–M scattering features in R ≈ 2.8 Å are indicative of the layered LDH coordination environment and interfacial metal-oxygen-metal coupling. The coexistence of Fe-O-Ni/Fe and Ni-O-M pathways collectively optimizes intermediate adsorption–desorption energetics through electronic coupling while enhancing interfacial mechanical rigidity, thereby suppressing catalyst corrosion during prolonged operation.

Thus, the closed-loop recovery and recycling of nylon 6 can be established by integrating the electro-synthesis of AA, as developed in this work (Fig. 6a). To further demonstrate the practical utility and energy efficiency of the system, a coupled electrolyzer was constructed, in which a three-dimensionally ordered macroporous tin dioxide (3DOM-SnO2) catalyst was employed at the cathode. The electrochemical performances of 3DOM-SnO2 and SnO2 at different voltages were characterized at PE mode (Figs. S45–S48). It demonstrated that 3DOM-SnO2 showed higher current density and Faradaic efficiency to SnO2, and formic acid was the primary product of 3DOM-SnO2 during the CO2RR. The LSV reveals a substantial advantage of the CO2RR||KOR configuration over conventional water electrolysis (OER||HER) (Fig. S49). For instance, CO2RR||KOR system requires only 2.06 V to achieve a current density of 100 mA cm−2, which is markedly lower than that of OER||HER. This reduced operating voltage highlights the synergistic advantages of replacing both the anodic OER with value-added KOR and the cathodic HER (Hydrogen Evolution Reaction) with CO2RR, thereby substantially reducing the total energy consumption. Figure S50 shows that the FEs of the products formate and AA is maintained above 70 % in the cell voltage range of 1.9-2.9 V. The long-term operational durability of the integrated electrolyzer was also evaluated under both CE and PE modes (Fig. 6c and 6d). Under CE operation, severe current decay and fluctuating Faradaic efficiencies are observed, which can be attributed to progressive and irreversible poisoning of the catalyst active sites. In stark contrast, the PE mode enables both stable current output and a persistently high FE for AA over 300 h of continuous operation. This prolonged durability stems from the intrinsic periodic surface regeneration of the PE strategy. Moreover, post-reaction catalysts analysis also shows no structural changes (Figs. S51-54), suggesting the high stability for NiFe-1 after 300 h of reaction in PE mode.

After 300 h of electrolysis in PE mode, AA accumulates in the electrolyte as potassium adipate. Due to its low solubility at low temperatures and the weak acidity-induced precipitation, AA was isolated through the following steps: (i) adding dilute sulfuric acid under stirring until pH 1.5–2.0 was reached; (ii) cooling the acidified solution in an ice–water bath for 60 mins to promote crystallization; (iii) collecting the white crystalline product by vacuum filtration and washing the filter cake with ice-cold water. Finally, a total of 158.7 g of high-purity AA was obtained. XRD analysis confirmed that the isolated product exhibits diffraction patterns identical to commercial adipic acid, verifying its high crystalline purity (Fig. S55). Moreover, the operating time of NiFe-1||3DOM-SnO2 is close to 300 h, and the FE maintains at ~90 %; those performances are competitive with reported systems (Table S5).

Discussion

In conclusion, this work demonstrates a synergistic strategy that integrates an α-FeOOH/NiFe-LDH heterojunction catalyst with pulsed electrolysis to overcome the persistent activity-stability-selectivity trade-off in the electrocatalytic synthesis of adipic acid from KA oil. The α-FeOOH/NiFe-LDH heterojunction interface facilitates electron transfer and stabilizes the catalyst structure, and the reaction performed in the PE model dynamically refreshes active sites and prevents poisoning. This synthetic approach led to a benchmark AA production rate of 6.73 mmol h−1 cm−2 with 90.06% FE. Moreover, the coupling of anodic KOR with cathodic CO2 reduction to formate in a single electrolyzer further improves the process’s energy efficiency and showcases the potential for integrated chemical manufacturing. Collectively, this study provides fundamental insights into dynamic catalyst interfaces and reaction engineering, and paves a technically feasible and environmentally benign pathway for the sustainable production of adipic acid and other value-added chemicals through electrocatalytic refinery concepts.

Methods

Materials

All solvents and chemicals were used as purchased without further purifications. Chemicals: 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (Sigma-Aldrich, 99 %), Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, reagent, pellet; VWR Chemicals), Iron(III) chloride hexahydrate (reagent grade, ≥98%, Sigma-Aldrich), 37 wt % HCl (J. T. Baker), 65 wt % HNO3 (J. T. Baker), 30 wt % H2O2 (J. T. Baker), Potassium hydroxide (KOH, reagent, pellet; VWR Chemicals ≥85%), Ethanol (absolute, ≥99.8%; Sigma-Aldrich), Isopropanol (anhydrous, 99.5%; Sigma-Aldrich), cyclohexanone (reagent grade, ≥99.8%; Sigma-Aldrich), cyclohexanol (Chromatographic grade, ≥99.9%; Sigma-Aldrich), Ni foam (Recemat BV, Netherlands), Nickel plate (99.5%, Alfa Aesar), Perfluorinated membrane made from Nafion™ 117 (Sigma-Aldrich) and anion-exchange membrane (Fumasep FAB-PK-130, fuel cell store). Prior to the synthesis, all the flasks were washed with a solution of aqua regia (HCl/HNO3 = 3:1 v/v) to remove any traces of metal residue, followed by washing with water and acetone, and finally dried at 80 °C overnight. Milli-Q water (18.2 MΩ) was used for all the syntheses and catalysis experiments. The Nafion 117 membrane was pretreated by boiling sequentially in 3% H2O2, deionized water, 0.5 M H2SO4, and deionized water (each for 1 h). The FAB-PK-130 anion-exchange membrane was conditioned in 1 M KOH for 24 h to convert to OH− form and rinsed thoroughly. The geometric area of the Nafion 117 membrane in the H-cell was 1.0 cm2, and the active area of the FAB-PK-130 membrane in the MEA flow cell was 4.0 cm2 (2 × 2 cm).

Preparation of NiFe-1

The nickel foam (NF) was cut into slices (1 cm × 3 cm) and successively ultrasonicated in hydrochloric acid (3 M), acetone, ethanol and water for 15 min, respectively. Then, the NF was put into a 50 mL beaker containing a mixture of FeCl3·6H2O (2.5 mmol) and 5% H2O2 (25 mL) that had been reacted for 5 mins. After vertical suspension impregnation the nickel foam for t = 60 s, the solution was decanted and the NF rinsed three times with ultrapure water. The NiFe-1 electrode was then removed from the beaker, put into a plastic petri dish, and dried at 60 °C in an oven for 24 h.

Preparation of NiFe

NF was cut into slices (1 cm × 3 cm) and ultrasonicated in hydrochloric acid (3 M), acetone, ethanol and water for 15 min, respectively. Then, the NF was put into a 50 mL baker containing a mixture of FeCl3·6H2O (2.5 mmol) and 25 mL H2O that had been stirred for 5 mins. After vertical suspension impregnation the nickel foam for 60 s, the solution was decanted, and the NF rinsed three times with ultrapure water. The electrode was then removed from the beaker, put into a plastic petri dish, and dried at 60 °C in an oven for 24 h.

Preparation of NF-1

NF was synthesized in the same manner as described for the preparation of NiFe-1 without the addition of FeCl3·6H2O.

Preparation of 3DOM-SnO2

First, the styrene monomer was pretreated: 45 mL of styrene monomer was taken and ultrasonically cleaned three times (15 min each time) with a 10 wt% NaOH solution to remove the polymerization inhibitor. After being washed with distilled water until neutral, it was vacuum-dried for later use. Subsequently, 240 mL of distilled water and 1.2 g of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) were added to a three-necked flask, and Ar gas was introduced for 30 min to purge the reaction system of air. After heating to 75 °C, 31 mL of the pretreated styrene monomer was added, and the mixture was stirred for 30 min to achieve uniform dispersion. Then, 40 mL of a 0.01 g·mL−1 potassium persulfate aqueous solution was added dropwise, and the polymerization reaction was completed by continuous stirring for 24 h. After the reaction solution was cooled to room temperature, the precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 3500 r·min−1 for 16 h, washed three times with absolute ethanol to remove impurities, and finally vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 12 h to obtain monodisperse polystyrene (PS) microsphere templates with a particle size of approximately 400 nm.

A total of 0.05 mol of SnCl4·5H2O and 3.8 g of trisodium citrate were dissolved in 20 mL of absolute ethanol, followed by magnetic stirring for 60 minutes to form a homogeneous and transparent precursor solution. 1.5 g of the PS microsphere template was placed in a Buchner funnel, and the aforementioned precursor solution was slowly added dropwise under vacuum filtration to ensure full infiltration of the template. After being naturally air-dried at room temperature, the sample was placed in a vacuum drying oven at 60 °C for 8 h to obtain the precursor@PS composite. The precursor@PS composite was transferred into a tube furnace, with Ar gas introduced as the protective atmosphere. The temperature was raised to 300 °C at a heating rate of 1 °C·min−1 and held for 1 h, then further increased to 500 °C and maintained for 3 h. Subsequently, the temperature was elevated to 600 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C·min−1 and kept for 2 h, ultimately yielding the 3DOM-SnO2 material.

Preparation of SnO2

10 mmol SnCl4·5H2O was dissolved in 50 mL of absolute ethanol, followed by magnetic stirring at a rotation speed of 500 r·min−1 for 30 minutes to form a transparent solution; subsequently, 25% aqueous ammonia was added dropwise to adjust the pH to the range of 3.5–4.5, and continuous stirring was maintained for 1 h to yield a milky white sol. The sol was then sealed and allowed to stand at room temperature for 24 h for natural aging to form a gel, which was further vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 12 h to remove the solvent, resulting in xerogel powder. For calcination and crystallization, the xerogel was placed in a muffle furnace under an air atmosphere, heated to 500 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C·min−1, and held at this temperature for 2 h to eliminate residual organic substances and chloride ions; after cooling to room temperature, the product was ground to obtain tin SnO2 powder.

Materials characterization

The crystal structures of the samples were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8 Discover) at a scan rate of 1° min−1. Surface morphologies were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Quanta 450 FEG) at 5 kV and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM2100F, JEOL).

Atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker Multimode 8) was employed to characterize the surface roughness and structural details. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ESCALAB 250Xi) was used to determine the chemical states. In-situ Raman spectroscopy was conducted on an inVia Reflex spectrometer. The in-situ Raman was probed using a Raman spectroscopy (inVia Reflex). In-situ Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) measurements were performed using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 spectrometer equipped with a customized electrochemical cell. All FTIR spectra were corrected by subtracting the background spectrum recorded at open-circuit potential in the same electrolyte. The in-situ electrochemical cell employed in this work is of model EC-RAIR-H. Fe K-edge XAFS analysis was performed on the BL14W beamline of Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) using a Si (111) crystal monochromator. The spectrum was recorded with a 4-channel Bruker 5040 silicon drift detector at room temperature, and the Fe K-edge EXAFS spectrum was collected in a transmission mode. The spectra were processed and analyzed by the software code Athena.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculation

The density function calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) with the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) functional parametrized by Perde Burke-Ernzerh of (PBE) was employed to describe the exchange correlation potential. A plane-wave energy cutoff of 400 eV was used and the energies and forces on each atom were converged to 10−4 eV and 0.05 eV/Å, respectively. A gamma-only point was used for the Brillouin zone integrations in geometry optimization, while other calculations employed a Γ-centered k-point mesh of 3×3×3.

Electrochemical measurements

The oxygen evolution reaction (OER), and alkaline cyclohexanone/cyclohexanol electrooxidation were conducted using Netherland Ivium and CHI760E electrochemical workstation at room temperature with a three-electrode system in a H-type cell, which was separated by a Nafion-117 membrane. All measured potentials were corrected for the uncompensated resistance (Ru) using the positive feedback method implemented in the CHI760E electrochemical workstation. Prior to each measurement, Ru was determined by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) at open-circuit potential over a frequency range of 100 kHz-10 Hz with an AC amplitude of 5 mV. The Ru in the H-type cell was 2.3 Ω. An 85% compensation level was applied to avoid overcompensation and oscillation. All reported potentials in this work are presented as E-iR with this 85% correction unless otherwise stated. NiFe-1/NiFe/NF-1 was directly used as the working electrode (WE), a Pt wire was used as the counter electrode (CE), and Hg/HgO electrode was used as the reference electrode (RE). All electrochemical tests are performed at 25 °C. The Hg/HgO electrode was available from ALS, and 1 M potassium hydroxide was used as an electrolyte solution. E0 = 98 mV vs. RHE (25 °C). In order to ensure the accuracy of the potential, the Hg/HgO reference electrode was calibrated as follows: it was immersed in 1 M KOH solution with a commercial RHE electrode to measure the potential difference between the two. When the deviation between the measured and theoretical values (98 mV) does not exceed ±2 mV, the electrode is considered normal; otherwise, the electrode is refilled or replaced, and the deviation is used to correct subsequent potential conversions. Reactions were performed in 10 mL 1.0 M KOH electrolyte solution with and without 400 mM cyclohexanone/cyclohexanol. 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was recorded on a Bruker AVANCE III HD (600 MHz) spectrometer.

Electrolyte Preparation Method: The corresponding mass of potassium hydroxide solid was accurately weighed, and deionized water was added, stirring until completely dissolved to prepare a 1.0 M KOH (pH = 13.65) electrolyte. For the electrolyte containing cyclohexanone/cyclohexanol, an appropriate amount of cyclohexanone/cyclohexanol was added to the above 1.0 M KOH electrolyte, and the mixture was stirred evenly until completely dissolved, resulting in a prepared electrolyte concentration of 400 mM. All electrolytes were freshly prepared on the day of the test, and no special storage conditions were required.

For two-electrode electrolysis, NiFe-1 was employed as the anode, and 3DOM-SnO2 was used as the cathode. The potentials were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode potential (RHE) through the Nernst equation: (ERHE = EHg/HgO + 0.0591×pH + 0.098 V). The obtained iR-compensated potential (E-iR) is then used directly as the working potential in all electrochemical analyses. The scan rate for LSV was kept at 5 mV s−1. The electrochemical surface area (ECSA) was evaluated in terms of the double-layer capacitance (Cdl). The equation for ESCA measurements is: ECSA = Cdl/Cs, where Cs was the specific capacitance, which was assumed to be 0.040 mF/cm2 in 1 M KOH solution, based on values previously reported. Cdl was calculated as the slope of the plot of capacitive current from a non-faradaic double-layer region against scan rate. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed in 1.0 M KOH with 400 mM cyclohexanone/cyclohexanol at different scan rates in a potential window where no Faradaic process occurs. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed in the frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz with a 5 mV AC amplitude.
