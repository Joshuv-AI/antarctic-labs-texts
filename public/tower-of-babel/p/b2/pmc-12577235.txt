Global and national burden of falls among individuals aged 55 years and older (1990–2021): analysis of the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Weiwei Wu, Bole Zhang, Jinbo Li, Yeting Zhao, Liyuan Wang, Jing Zhao, Weixuan Hu, Yulin Li, Yongliang Feng, Suping Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo comprehensively evaluate the epidemiological burden, temporal trends, socio-demographic inequalities, and long-term forecasting of fall-related injuries in older adults (aged ≥ 55 years) through systematic analysis of the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 dataset.MethodsWe extracted data on fall-related incidence, prevalence, deaths, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for adults aged ≥ 55 from the GBD database (1990–2021). Temporal trends were assessed using estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs) and joinpoint regression. Age-period-cohort modeling was applied to disentangle the effects of age, time period, and birth cohort. Socioeconomic inequalities were evaluated using the slope index of inequality and concentration index. Future projections to 2046 were generated via Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort analysis.ResultsBetween 1990 and 2021, the absolute number of fall-related incidence, deaths, and DALYs among older adults doubled globally. While age-standardized rates remained relatively stable, distinct patterns emerged across Socio-demographic Index (SDI) regions: high SDI regions had the highest incidence and prevalence, whereas low SDI regions experienced higher mortality. Joinpoint regression identified sex-specific acceleration phases, notably 2005–2021 for incidence (APC = 3.747), with women demonstrating higher ASIR but men showing elevated ASPR/ASDR. Significant socioeconomic inequalities were observed and have widened over time. Projections indicate a continued increase in the absolute burden of falls through 2046, despite stable or declining age-standardized rates.ConclusionsFalls represent a major and growing public health challenge among older adults worldwide, with pronounced disparities across sex and socioeconomic levels. There is an urgent need for targeted prevention strategies and enhanced resource allocation—particularly in low SDI settings—to reduce the health impact of falls in aging populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25016-w.Keywords: Falls, Burden of disease, Trends, Health inequalities

Introduction

Falls represent a critical public health challenge worldwide, with risks significantly increasing in older age. According to the 2021 World Health Organization, unintentional injuries account for approximately 700,000 global deaths annually, with falls being the second leading cause [1]. Older adults bear a disproportionate share of fall-related mortality and morbidity. Epidemiological studies indicate that more than one-quarter of older individuals experience falls each year, making it the leading cause of injury-related death [2]. Beyond immediate health consequences, falls among older adults extend periods of dependency and increase demands for medical care, rehabilitation, and long-term support [3]. The resulting economic burden places considerable strain on healthcare systems worldwide, contributing to substantially elevated medical expenditures [4, 5]. Amid accelerating global population aging, fall-related injuries have emerged as an urgent public health priority. Effective fall prevention strategies are therefore essential not only for safeguarding individual health but also for ensuring healthcare system sustainability and mitigating broader socioeconomic impacts.

In recent years, the incidence of fall-related fractures has shown a marked increase starting from the age of 50, with women exhibiting a significantly higher incidence (80.5%) compared to men aged 50–59 (40.5%) [6, 7]. Concurrently, studies have recommended including trauma patients aged 55 and above in geriatric trauma protocols [8]. To better understand the global epidemiology of falls in older adults, this study utilized data from the Global Burden of Disease, Injury, and Risk Factor Study (GBD) database. Based on the latest research and the characteristics of the GBD database, we ultimately selected people aged 55 and above to assess the disease burden of falls. Our analysis aims to: (1) describe the epidemiology of falls at global and national levels; (2) examine temporal trends from multiple perspectives; (3) evaluate socioeconomic inequalities using WHO-recommended health equity approaches; and (4) project the burden of falls over the next 25 years.

Methods

Data sources

The GBD database is a global project led by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) at the University of Washington, which has been providing comprehensive and in-depth empirical assessments for the global health field for three decades [9]. With each update and iteration, the GBD database has been continuously refined, encompassing more diverse etiologies, risk factors, and geographical scopes, and significantly enhancing the precision of age group analysis [10]. The collected data was modelled by spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression to allow for smoothing over age, time, and location in locations lacking complete datasets. The Meta-Regression with Bayesian priors, Regularisation and Trimming (MR-BRT) program was utilized to adjust data bias resulting from varied case definitions and study methods across different countries. All disease estimates for GBD include 95% uncertainty interval (95% UIs) for each metric, which are based on the 2.5th and 97.5th ordered values of 1000 draws of the posterior distribution [11].

We relied on the research outcomes of the GBD 2021 to assemble detailed data on falls among individuals aged 55 and above worldwide from 1990 to 2021. These data encompass the number of cases and crude rates of incidence, prevalence, deaths, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), accompanied by 95% UIs, to comprehensively reflect differences in age, gender, region, and country. More importantly, we included the Socio-demographic Index (SDI), which is divided into five categories: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high [12]. The statistical analysis and graphing were performed using R software (4.2.3) and Joinpoint Regression Program (version 4.9.1.0).

For the secondary use of GBD research data, we have received formal approval from the University of Washington Institutional Review Board (STUDY00009060) and signed a waiver of informed consent statement to ensure that the research process complies with ethical guidelines. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Descriptive analysis

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the burden of falls among individuals aged 55 and above, descriptive analyses were carried out at the global and national levels. It visually presented the number of cases and age-standardized rates (ASRs) of incidence, prevalence, deaths, and DALYs related to falls among people aged 55 and older worldwide, in countries (204 countries and territories), and across the five quintiles of the SDI (low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high) from 1990 to 2021.

Trend analysis

To develop more precise prevention strategies, we will conduct a comprehensive review of the trends of falls among adults ≥ 55 years, covering both the overall, local, and multidimensional perspectives. Firstly, the overall trends of the fall burden in this population were quantified by utilizing the change rates of the estimated number, crude rates, and ASRs, as well as the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC).

To quantify temporal trends in the burden of fall-related injuries among older adults (aged ≥ 55 years), we performed joinpoint regression analysis using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 4.9.1.0) developed by the National Cancer Institute [13]. The minimum and maximum numbers of joinpoints are 0 and 5 respectively. Permutation test (4499 permutations, α level 0.05) was used to select the optimal number of joinpoints. For each segment, we computed the annual percentage change (APC) and its 95% confidence interval.

Moreover, the correlation trends between the fall burden and the effects of age, period, and birth cohort were explored in depth, which is of vital significance for an in-depth understanding of the multi-dimensional influence of the fall risk. For this purpose, an age-period-cohort model based on the intrinsic estimator (IE) method was introduced [14].

Cross-country inequality analysis

Health inequality monitoring can provide a solid foundation for evidence-based health planning and drive continuous improvement of related policies, programs, and practices aimed at reducing disparities in access to health services. This study adopted two key standard indicators - absolute gradient inequality (measured by the slope index of inequality) and relative gradient inequality (measured by the concentration index) to precisely assess the uneven distribution of falls burden among adults ≥ 55 years in various countries [15, 16].

Predictive analysis

To formulate public health policies more effectively and allocate health resources rationally, we carried out an in-depth prediction of the fall burden for adults ≥ 55 years within the next 25 years. We employed a Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model with Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) in an attempt to precisely predict the trend of the fall burden for adults ≥ 55 years globally until 2046 [17, 18].

To project future trends in fall-related burden within the next 25 years, we applied a BAPC model using the “BAPC” R package. The argument “retro = TRUE” was specified to generate retrospective predictions for the last 25 years within the observation period, enabling in-sample validation and model accuracy assessment. This approach retains all observed data while producing fitted values and projections for the same timeframe, ensuring a robust evaluation of the model’s predictive performance before extrapolating future trends. The other Settings are “secondDiff = FALSE, stdweight = wstand, verbose = TRUE”.

Results

The burden of falls among adults ≥ 55 years

At the global level, compared with 1990, the number of incidence, prevalence, deaths, and DALYs in 2021 all increased, while the ASRs did not change significantly (Table 1). Across the SDI quintile regions, the high SDI region had the highest number of incidence, prevalence, deaths, and DALYs. The low SDI region had the highest ASRs of deaths (ASDR), and the low-middle SDI region had the highest ASRs of DALYs. At the national level, in 2021, Andorra had the highest ASRs of incidence (ASIR) and ASRs of prevalence (ASPR) of falls among adults ≥ 55 years. India had the highest ASDR, and Greenland had the highest ASRs of DALYs (Fig. 1, Table S1).

Trends in burden of falls among adults ≥ 55 years

In the global and SDI quintile regions, the number of incidence, prevalence, deaths, and DALYs of falls among adults ≥ 55 years in 2021 more than doubled compared to 1990 (Table 2). In terms of ASIR, both the global average and the values in all five SDI quintile regions all increased in 2021 compared with 1990, with the middle SDI region showing the largest growth. For ASPR, only the global rate and that of high-middle SDI region decreased in 2021 compared with 1990 (Fig. 2). Notably, the ASDR and ASR of DALYs presented a differentiated trend: both increased in high SDI regions in 2021 compared to 1990, while the global averages and those in other SDI regions decreased.

Local trends in burden of falls among adults ≥ 55 years using joinpoint regression

The Joinpoint results grouped by gender are shown in Fig. 4. Overall, the changes in men and women are basically consistent with the overall gender. From 1990 to 2021, the number of incidence, prevalence, deaths, and DALYs increased year by year, and the number of cases in women was always higher than that in men. Joinpoint regression analysis revealed that the number of incidence cases of the overall gender increased most rapidly from 2005 to 2021 (APC = 3.747, Table S3), the number of prevalence cases increased most rapidly from 2016 to 2021 (APC = 3.702), the number of death cases increased most rapidly from 2011 to 2014 (APC = 4.836), and the number of DALYs increased most rapidly from 2013 to 2019 (APC = 3.277). Age-standardized rate analyses demonstrated heterogeneous trajectories. While ASIR displayed an overall upward trend despite interim fluctuations, ASPR, ASDR, and ASRs of DALYs showed declining patterns. Interestingly, only ASIR is higher in females than in males, while the rest of ASRs are higher in males than in females.

Age-period-cohort analysis on burden of falls among adults ≥ 55 years

Before performing the age-period-cohort analysis, we conducted a two-factor interaction analysis examining age, period, and cohort effects on the incidence, prevalence, and deaths of falls among adults aged 55 and above (Figure S1, Figure S2, and Figure S3). The results of the age-period-cohort analysis are shown in Fig. 5, and the results of the stratified analysis by gender are shown in Figure S4 and Figure S5. After adjusting for period and cohort effects, it can be found that the risk of incidence, prevalence, and death of falls increases with age (Table 3). Discussion

This study described the disease burden of falls among adults ≥ 55 years at the global, SDI regional, and national levels from 1990 to 2021. The ASRs of falls among people aged 55 and above vary significantly across different regions and countries. The DALYs of falls among people aged 55 and above are positively correlated with the relative SDI level, and this difference becomes more obvious with the increase in years. In the next 25 years, although the ASIR and ASPR are expected to grow at a relatively gentle pace, the incidence, prevalence, number of deaths, and DALYs of falls in this age group will continue to increase. With the intensification of the population aging trend, falls among older adults are a public health issue that deserves more of our attention.

In our study, the disease burden of falls among older adults varied substantially across countries. As reflected in our SDI stratified results, socioeconomic development shows a significant association with the burden of falls in older adults. Many research findings show that there are regional differences of varying degrees in the relevant factors influencing the occurrence of falls [19, 20]. Relevant research has found that regions such as East Asia and Oceania are undergoing rapid urbanization, leading to an increased risk of falls in built and industrial environments [21]. A cross national study found that the prevalence of fall related fractures in the aging population of India is higher than that in other countries [22]. This is due to India’s poor healthcare infrastructure, and the shortage of resources limits treatment and prognosis. High income countries spend about 1% of their healthcare budgets on falls [5]. In contrast, low SDI regions lag behind several other regions in achieving universal health coverage. Their public health systems face severe limitations, not only lacking health professionals but also having restricted allocation and utilization of resources. Meanwhile, the unfair distribution of resources between different classes and geographical regions leads to unequal access to necessary and effective healthcare, especially for the poor [23, 24]. Most of the guidelines related to falls come from developed countries [25]. Countries with low SDI should pay more attention to the problem of falls among older adults, formulate their own guidelines for preventing falls among older adults, and rationally allocate medical resources.

Age is one of the main factors influencing falls. The growth of the aging population, has led to an increasing number of falls among older adults, imposing huge socioeconomic pressure [22]. The age-period-cohort model in this study found that the older the age, the higher the risks of fall occurrence, prevalence, and death. With increasing age, the mobility of older adults declines, making them more prone to falls [26]. The increase in the number of elderly people suffering from chronic diseases, especially physical diseases, also increases the occurrence of falls among older adults [27]. The occurrence of falls is also related to gender. Our research is consistent with the results of other studies, showing that the incidence of falls among women is higher than that among men [3, 28]. This might be because the prevalence of osteoporosis among older women is higher than that among men [29, 30]. Interestingly, this study found that both the prevalence and deaths of falls among women are lower than those among men. This difference may stem from more high-risk occupational factors for men, including labor-intensive work and high-risk sports.

The social factors contributing to falls among older adults should not be overlooked. When the healthcare service system is imperfect and the healthcare level is low, older adults may lack essential health knowledge, rational medication guidance, follow up services after physical examinations, etc., thus increasing the risk of falls [31]. An analysis of the disease burden of falls among people aged 55 and above in different SDI regions revealed that between 1990 and 2021, the ASIR and ASPR of falls were the highest in high SDI regions, but the ASDR was the highest in low SDI regions. The global health inequality analysis also indicates that the health inequality regarding falls among older adults is worsening. This indicates that although progress has been made in medical and preventive strategies in high SDI regions, population changes, especially population aging, remain a major obstacle to reducing the burden of falls. In low SDI regions, the relatively high fall-related mortality rate may be attributed to underdeveloped medical care. Therefore, all sectors of society, especially in low SDI regions, should pay more attention to the issue of falls among older adults, improve the safety of community facilities, perfect the healthcare system for fall prevention and treatment, and at the same time, pay attention to the mental health of older adults, so as to jointly create a safer and more friendly living environment for them.

It is worth noting that the prediction results show that falls among older adults will remain a problem that cannot be ignored in the next 25 years. Population growth and the increasingly severe aging phenomenon are likely the reasons for the projected increase in the number of fall cases among people aged 55 and above by 2046. The United Nations has indicated that the global population is continuously growing, and it is estimated to reach 9.7 billion by 2050 [32]. Between 2015 and 2050, the proportion of the world’s population aged 60 and above will almost double, rising from 12% to 22% [33]. The accelerating aging of the population may influence the projected burden of falls among older adults. At the same time, economic development and related contextual factors may also significantly shape future trends. The BAPC model employed in this study comprehensively incorporates temporal effects by capturing multidimensional influencing factors [18]. It has been widely applied and validated in multiple studies based on GBD data [34–36]. To enhance the reliability of the projections, the model generated retrospective predictions for the past 25 years within the observation period, enabling in-sample validation and accuracy assessment. Therefore, all countries in the world should improve their healthcare systems, be well prepared to take advantage of this demographic transition, and pay particular attention to population aging and the allocation of medical resources. Due to the existence of health inequalities, the increase in the burden of falls mainly occurs in low SDI countries, indicating that greater investment in healthcare is needed in these regions to reduce inequalities. There are gender differences in the disease burden of falls, and policymakers should take this into account when adjusting prevention strategies.

This study has certain limitations. Due to the inconsistent quality of fall related data registration in various countries, less developed countries may underestimate the number of fall cases, resulting in possible spatial and temporal differences in falls. However, when cleaning and calibrating the data, the GBD database uses statistical modeling methods such as spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression, Bayesian prior meta regression, regularization, and pruning (MR BRT) procedures to reduce the negative impacts caused by these limitations. Secondly, since the GBD database is currently based on countries and regions, it cannot effectively analyze the disease burden by ethnicity at present. Despite these limitations, the study still has many strengths. Unlike other studies on the burden of fall diseases, our research focuses on people over 55 years old. Our analysis captured the earlier onset of fall risk and provided a more comprehensive lifespan perspective among older adults. Secondly, we provided the first BAPC model-based projections of fall burden through 2046, supporting forward-looking policy planning. In addition, we conducted comprehensive descriptive, trend, and health inequality analyses, which deepen the understanding of the epidemiology of falls among older adults and provide an evidence-based foundation for formulating global public health policies and optimizing the allocation of healthcare resources.

Overall, falls among older adults impose a huge disease burden and economic pressure every year. Although the ASIR and ASPR of falls do not show a significant growth trend in the next 25 years, the values of these two indicators have increased significantly. Therefore, falls among older adults remain a public health issue worthy of attention. All countries, especially underdeveloped regions, should increase their medical investment in fall prevention and rescue. When formulating policies, policymakers should take into account the differences in the disease burden of falls related to factors such as age and gender, and further consider targeted and flexible intervention measures. Each country should develop a personalized healthcare system to address the different medical needs of each country and reduce the negative impacts of falls.
