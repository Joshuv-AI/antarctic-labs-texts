Long non-coding RNAs in neurodegenerative diseases—Molecular mechanisms, liquid biopsy biomarkers, and therapeutic targets: A review
Authors: Yipeng Cheng, Mingyue Qiu, Zhijie Yu, Xu Tang, Jingjing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs), such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and Huntington’s disease (HD), are age-related disorders characterized by progressive neuronal loss, cognitive decline, and limited options for disease-modifying treatments. Increasing evidence suggests that long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play significant roles in neurodevelopment, neuronal homeostasis, and disease progression; however, their involvement in shared pathogenic pathways and clinical applications remains inadequately defined. This review consolidates recent experimental, transcriptomic, bioinformatic, and emerging clinical findings regarding the role of lncRNAs in NDDs. We examine how lncRNAs modulate common disease mechanisms, including protein misfolding and aggregation, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, ferroptosis, synaptic failure, and aging-related neurodegenerative processes. These regulatory functions occur through various mechanisms, including epigenetic modifications, transcriptional regulation, post-transcriptional processes, and RNA–protein interactions, as well as novel mechanisms such as liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), peptide coding, and exosome-mediated intercellular communication. Current evidence supports the potential of lncRNAs as minimally invasive liquid biopsy biomarkers, detectable in blood, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and extracellular vesicles. Additionally, lncRNAs may serve as therapeutic targets through antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), gene editing, and engineered delivery platforms. Overall, lncRNAs have emerged as central molecular regulators and promising candidates for translation in NDDs. Nonetheless, challenges related to specificity, validation, delivery across the blood–brain barrier, and clinical standardization must be addressed before their routine application in precision neurology.

Introduction

With the accelerated aging of the global population, the incidence rates of neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs) such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and Huntington’s disease (HD) continue to rise, presenting a significant public health challenge that threatens the health and quality of life of the elderly [1, 2]. Although the clinical phenotypes of various NDDs differ, they share common pathological features, including progressive neuronal death and irreversible functional decline. Currently, available clinical drugs provide only temporary symptomatic relief and do not reverse the progression of these diseases [3]. The core bottlenecks in addressing NDDs include: first, the lack of early diagnostic biomarkers, as many patients experience severe neuronal loss and brain structural damage by the time of diagnosis; second, the incomplete elucidation of pathogenic mechanisms, making single-target approaches inadequate to address the complexity and heterogeneity of these diseases; and third, the extremely low success rate of drug development. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore novel intervention strategies. Increasing evidence suggests that NDDs are not solely caused by mutations in coding genes or protein abnormalities, but are complex disorders driven by dysregulation of the transcriptome, abnormal RNA metabolism, and disrupted cellular homeostasis.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a class of transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides that do not encode proteins or only encode short peptides [4]. With advances in high-throughput sequencing technology, researchers have discovered that lncRNAs exhibit spatiotemporal specificity and cell subtype specificity. They are particularly abundant in the nervous system and are involved in critical biological processes, including neurogenesis [5], neuronal migration [6], synapse formation [7] and plasticity, and glial cell activation [8]. The classical regulatory mechanisms of lncRNAs have become relatively well-established. Recent studies have revealed advanced modes of lncRNA regulation, including mediating liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), encoding peptides [9], localizing to mitochondria to regulate energy metabolism, and facilitating intercellular communication via exosomes. These findings have greatly expanded our understanding of lncRNA functions and offered new perspectives for addressing NDDs.

Overview of lncRNAs

The first abundantly expressed lncRNA in humans was discovered in 1990; it possesses a structure similar to that of messenger RNA (mRNA) but lacks coding potential [10]. However, progress in the field of lncRNAs remained relatively slow during the early stages due to the lack of precise detection technologies. It was not until 2002, when the sequencing of mouse complementary DNA (cDNA) libraries revealed tens of thousands of novel non-coding transcripts, that the identification and functional characterization of lncRNAs significantly accelerated [11]. To date, the GENCODE database has annotated more than 20,000 human lncRNAs, while other databases estimate that the number could exceed 100,000 [12, 13]. Novel regulatory mechanisms of lncRNAs in NDDs

Classical regulatory mechanisms

The classical regulatory mechanisms of lncRNAs are relatively well-established, providing a foundation for their function in the nervous system. At the epigenetic level, lncRNAs recruit histone modification enzymes [22], DNA methyltransferases [23], or chromatin remodeling complexes [24] to silence or activate specific genes. At the transcriptional level, lncRNAs can bind directly to transcription factors [25] or promoter [26] regions to regulate mRNA. At the post-transcriptional level, the primary function of lncRNAs involves the competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) mechanism [27], which alleviates the inhibitory effects of microRNAs on target mRNAs by sequestering them. Furthermore, lncRNAs regulate mRNA splicing [28], stability [29], nuclear retention [30] and translation [31]. Although classical mechanisms can explain some functions of lncRNAs, they are insufficient to elucidate their spatial-specific and cell-specific roles in complex neuropathologies. Recent studies have revealed intricate regulatory mechanisms of lncRNAs, providing important theoretical insights into the occurrence and progression of NDDs (Figure 1).

lncRNAs and LLPS

LLPS is an emerging biophysical phenomenon that can sequester molecules to implement physiological and pathological functions. LLPS facilitates the assembly of numerous membraneless compartments, including stress granules and P-bodies, which contain RNA and protein [32]. LncRNAs can act as backbone molecules that promote the phase separation of RNA-protein complexes due to their long chain structure, repeated sequences, and secondary structure. LLPS plays a fundamental role in various biological functions, including chromatin aggregation and cellular signal transduction [33]. Studies have indicated that lncRNAs capable of binding phospholipids [34] and participating in LLPS are involved in AD pathogenesis. However, few studies have directly investigated the regulation of phase separation by lncRNAs in NDDs, although some mechanistic insights have provided foundational support. For example, the lncRNA maternally expressed gene 3 (MEG3) modulates metabolism and muscle homeostasis by facilitating the LLPS of SUZ12 polycomb repressive complex 2 subunit (SUZ12), a core component of the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) [35]. These findings suggest that lncRNAs may function as scaffolds for LLPS, modulating the spatial localization and stability of protein complexes. This mechanism may also influence the epigenetics of the nervous system, thereby affecting neuronal epigenetic and stress responses through the regulation of LLPS.

lncRNAs interact with RNA-binding proteins

The interaction between lncRNAs and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) represents a fundamental regulatory mechanism in neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs). Abnormalities in nucleocytoplasmic transport, misfolding, and aggregation of RBPs—such as transactive response DNA-binding protein 43 kDa (TDP-43), tau, α-synuclein, and fused in sarcoma (FUS)—are critical pathological hallmarks of NDDs. LncRNAs can interact with RBPs through sequence complementarity or structural binding, thereby maintaining normal localization and function or exacerbating abnormal aggregation under pathological conditions [36]. Studies have found that the lncRNA family with sequence similarity 151 member B divergent transcript (FAM151B-DT) binds to tau protein, α-synuclein, and heat shock cognate 70 kDa protein (HSC70) to form a complex that regulates the autophagic degradation of pathogenic proteins [37]. LncRNAs maintain neuronal homeostasis by regulating RBPs and participating in various biological processes, such as RNA splicing, localization, and degradation [38], which can lead to widespread imbalances in post-transcriptional regulation [39]. Additionally, regulatory relationships between lncRNAs and RBPs also influence aging-related NDDs [40]. Furthermore, lncRNAs can modulate signal transduction within the nervous system by establishing competitive regulatory networks with RBPs [41]. The specificity of interactions between lncRNAs and RBPs provides an important basis for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying the early stages of NDDs.

lncRNAs encoding peptides

Over the past decade, traditional views have held that lncRNAs lack protein-coding ability. However, a significant body of research has demonstrated the existence of functional peptides translated from lncRNAs [42]. Currently, the peptide-coding function of various lncRNAs has been confirmed in pathological processes such as cancer, metabolic disorders, and inflammatory responses [43, 44], providing critical insights for investigating similar mechanisms in NDDs. Studies indicate that peptides play a role in the onset and progression of diseases by regulating key pathways, including protein homeostasis, mitochondrial function, neuroinflammation, and synaptic function [45].

For instance, lncRNA 1810058I24Rik encodes a mitochondrion-localized peptide, Stmp1, which is highly expressed in retinal ischemia-reperfusion (IR) mouse models. This peptide exacerbates microglial activation and neuroinflammation by triggering the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome [46]. Additionally, lncRNA MN3 encodes a peptide, SERTM2, specifically enriched in spinal cord motor neurons. Both wild-type and SERTM2 knockout motor neurons, differentiated in vitro from mouse embryonic stem cells, exhibit differences in resting membrane potential and an overall decrease in excitability upon SERTM2 depletion. In vivo studies indicate that the absence of this peptide impairs performance in treadmill tests and modulates motor neuron excitability through interaction with the TASK1 potassium channel [47]. Furthermore, lncRNA potassium voltage-gated channel subfamily Q member 1 opposite strand/antisense transcript 1 (KCNQ1OT1) exacerbates inflammatory responses and promotes dopaminergic neuron death in PD by regulating the miR-186/NLRP3 axis and the NLRP3 inflammasome [48]. LncRNA PR/SET domain 16 divergent transcript (PRDM16-DT) has been identified as an astrocyte-specific lncRNA in AD, participating in the disease’s pathological processes by affecting synapse formation [49, 50].

Although direct evidence of lncRNAs being translated into peptides is currently lacking, these transcripts may possess undiscovered peptide-coding potential. Moreover, studies suggest that different splice isoforms may confer distinct subcellular localization and translational potential to lncRNAs [51]. For example, lncRNA 3222401L13Rik is upregulated in aging astrocytes and affects neuronal protective functions by regulating Neuronal PAS domain protein 3 (Npas3) [52]. This dynamic expression pattern provides a crucial foundation for further investigation into the peptide-encoding mechanisms of lncRNAs.

lncRNAs in mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a prevalent early pathological characteristic in NDDs [53]. Recent studies have identified a class of lncRNAs localized in mitochondria that can directly regulate mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) stability [54], respiratory chain efficiency [55], mitophagy [56], and mitochondrial fission and fusion [57]. For instance, lncRNA lncMtDloop, encoded by the D-loop region of the mitochondrial genome, maintains mitochondrial RNA levels and function during aging. Research has demonstrated that lncMtDloop enhances the transcription of mitochondrial genes by binding to mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM), facilitating its recruitment to the promoter regions of mitochondrial DNA. Additionally, the expression of lncMtDloop is significantly downregulated in the brain tissues of AD patients and triple-transgenic Alzheimer’s disease (3xTg-AD) mouse models. Ectopic expression of lncMtDloop not only significantly improves mitochondrial function and morphology but also effectively alleviates behavioral deficits in AD model mice [58]. The lncRNA LOC339803 regulates glycolysis and mitochondrial energy metabolism to preserve mitochondrial integrity by maintaining hexokinase 2 (HK2) expression levels in neuronal cells. Abnormal expression of LOC339803 has been observed in the brain tissues of multiple sclerosis (MS) patients, leading to HK2 downregulation and mitochondrial dysfunction [59]. The lncRNAROSALIND, encoded by the nuclear genome and localized to the mitochondrial matrix, is significantly upregulated in recurrent tumors. Studies indicate that ROSALIND protects the mitochondrial translation process from oxidative stress-induced damage, while its downregulation can cause severe defects in mitochondrial respiratory function [60].

Furthermore, mitochondrial lncRNAs are implicated in regulating ferroptosis, a type of programmed cell death characterized by intracellular iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, downregulation of the antioxidant enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and mitochondrial membrane ruffling [61]. In ALS lesions, significant iron accumulation occurs in the spinal cord [62]. For example, lncRNA LINC00472 is upregulated in both AD patients and shows a positive correlation with forkhead box O1 (FOXO1). Knockdown of LINC00472 reduces abnormal iron ion accumulation in neurons and restores GPX4 expression by regulating FOXO1. Research indicates that LINC00472 mediates cellular ferroptosis by targeting FOXO1, contributing to AD regulation [63]. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have linked mitochondrial lncRNA-mediated RNA regulation, energy metabolism, and oxidative stress processes with ferroptosis, indicating promising research directions for the future.

lncRNAs and protein homeostasis

Protein misfolding and abnormal aggregation represent core pathological mechanisms across all NDDs. Amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau proteins are the principal abnormally aggregated proteins, with their pathological alterations serving as primary drivers of AD. Aβ is generated from amyloid precursor protein through sequential cleavage by β-secretase and γ-secretase, with aggregates triggering neuroinflammation [64]. Tau, a microtubule-associated protein localized within neurons, stabilizes microtubule structures and facilitates axonal transport. Abnormal aggregation of tau exhibits neurotoxicity and disrupts organelle functions [64]. Studies have indicated that lncRNAs regulate key pathogenic proteins at multiple levels (Table 1).

LncRNA beta-site APP-cleaving enzyme 1 antisense transcript (BACE1-AS) is significantly upregulated in the brains of AD patients. It has been found to enhance β-secretase activity and promote Aβ production by binding to the sense strand of beta-site APP-cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1) mRNA, stabilizing it and enhancing protein translation [65]. Similarly, BACE1-AS was upregulated in human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells treated with Aβ1−42, establishing an in vitro model of AD amyloid plaques. Silencing BACE1-AS attenuated the cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP) by BACE1, reducing the generation of Aβ1−42 and Aβ1−40, consequently delaying amyloid plaque formation. This study indicates that lncRNA BACE1-AS is a critical factor in Aβ1−42 formation [66].

LncRNA MEG3 was significantly downregulated in an Aβ25−35-induced AD rat model. Upregulation of MEG3 enhanced spatial learning and memory in rats, decreased Aβ deposition in the hippocampus, and mitigated oxidative stress and inflammatory damage by inhibiting the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) signaling pathway. This study suggests that lncRNA MEG3 is a key regulatory factor in Aβ formation [67].

Another study found that lncRNA 17A was significantly upregulated in the brains of AD patients and SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells, with overexpression of lncRNA 17A promoting Aβ secretion and increasing the Aβx−42/Aβx−40 ratio. This study confirms that lncRNA 17A is involved in the pathological progression of AD by regulating Aβ secretion [68].

LncRNA EBF3-AS was significantly upregulated in the hippocampus of amyloid precursor protein / presenilin 1 (APP/PS1) AD mice. Knockdown of lncRNA early B-cell factor 3 antisense RNA (EBF3-AS) inhibited Aβ25−35-induced apoptosis, indicating that lncRNA EBF3-AS promotes neuronal apoptosis in AD [69].

LncRNA brain cytoplasmic RNA 1 (BC1) was aberrantly upregulated in the brains of AD mice. It binds to Fragile X Mental Retardation Protein 1 (FMRP), relieving the translational repression of APP mRNA by FMRP, thereby promoting the generation and aggregation of Aβ. InhibitingBC1 expression improved cognitive function by disrupting Aβ deposition, indicating that lncRNA BC1 influences Aβ production by regulating FMRP-mediated APP translation and participates in the pathological progression of AD [70].

LncRNA nuclear paraspeckle assembly transcript 1 (NEAT1) was significantly upregulated in an AD cell model established by Aβ induction in SH-SY5Y cells. Knockdown of NEAT1 alleviated Aβ-induced inhibition of cell viability, reduced apoptosis, and decreased phosphorylated Tau (p-Tau) protein levels. Furthermore, miR-107 was significantly decreased in Aβ-treated SH-SY5Y cells, while overexpression of miR-107 could reverse Aβ-induced cellular damage. This study indicates that NEAT1 exacerbates Aβ-induced neuronal damage by competitively binding tomiR-107 [71].

LncRNA FAM151B-DT was significantly downregulated in the brains of AD and PD patients. Knockdown of FAM151B-DT inhibited autophagy and promoted the aggregation of tau protein and α-synuclein, whereas overexpression of FAM151B-DT enhanced autophagic clearance by interacting with HSC70, tau protein, and α-synuclein, significantly reducing the aggregation of abnormal proteins [37].

α-Synuclein (α-syn) is a core pathogenic protein, and its abnormal aggregation is a hallmark of PD. Additionally, dysfunction of associated pathogenic proteins, such as leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2), Parkin, PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1), and glucocerebrosidase (GBA), contributes to impaired clearance of α-syn and exacerbates its aggregation, ultimately leading to selective neuronal loss.

A study has demonstrated that an antisense lncRNA upstream of PINK1 inhibits its transcription. The PINK1 protein plays a crucial role in the clearance of damaged mitochondria and is associated with α-syn aggregation. This research suggests that lncRNAs may indirectly influence the aggregation and clearance of α-syn by regulating mitophagy-related proteins [72].

The lncRNA NEAT1 is upregulated in a PD mouse model. It positively regulates PINK1 protein levels by inhibiting its degradation. Conversely, the knockdown of NEAT1 effectively inhibits 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced autophagy in vivo, thereby alleviating damage to dopaminergic neurons. This finding indicates that NEAT1 promotes MPTP-induced autophagy in the PD model by stabilizing the PINK1 protein [73]. Furthermore, NEAT1 is upregulated in 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+) SH-SY5Y cells, and its downregulation alleviates α-syn-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation by regulating miR-1301-3p [74].

The lncRNA urothelial carcinoma associated 1 (UCA1) has been implicated in the progression of PD by regulating the expression of α-syn. Both UCA1 and α-syn are highly expressed in the brain tissues of PD mouse models and in MPP+ SH-SY5Y cells. Knockdown of UCA1 effectively inhibits MPP+-induced neuronal apoptosis by preventing α-syn aggregation [75].

The lncRNA BACE1-AS is upregulated in the substantia nigra of PD rat models. Downregulation of BACE1-AS reduces levels of α-syn, inducible nitric oxide synthase, and glutamine, indicating that BACE1-AS mediates oxidative stress injury in dopaminergic neurons by regulating α-syn [76].

In ALS and HD, pathogenic proteins, including superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), FUS, chromosome 9 open reading frame 72 (C9orf72), and TDP-43, are primarily RNA-binding proteins. HD arises from a monogenic dominant mutation in huntingtin (HTT), with its pathogenic mechanism linked to the intrinsic toxicity of the mutant protein [77]. Studies have revealed that lncRNAs influence the toxicity of mutant TDP-43, FUS, HTT, SOD1, and C9ORF72. The lncRNA NEAT1-2 is significantly upregulated in motor neurons during the early pathological stages of ALS, induced by the nuclear clearance of TDP-43, suggesting that lncRNAs directly respond to TDP-43 pathological changes [78]. Additionally, huntingtin antisense transcript variant 1 (HTTAS-v1) is significantly downregulated compared to controls in HD patients. It negatively regulates HTT gene expression in Dicer-knockout mouse embryonic stem cells, indicating that HTTAS-v1 is a key regulator of HTT [79].

lncRNAs and neuroinflammation

Neuroinflammation is a central factor in the progression of NDDs. Aberrant activation of classical inflammatory signaling pathways, such as nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), is crucial for neuronal damage. The overactivation of microglia and astrocytes amplifies inflammation by releasing large amounts of pro-inflammatory factors. Studies indicate that lncRNAs can regulate glial cell activation and polarization, as well as the release of inflammatory factors such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-6, by targeting inflammation-related signaling pathways, including NF-κB, MAPK, and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), or through ceRNA networks and epigenetic modulation. A subcluster of neurons and glial cells co-expressing lncRNA small nucleolar RNA host gene 14 (SNHG14), myocardin-related transcription factors A (MRTFA) and B (MRTFB) was found to be more abundant in the AD group based on single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets. This subcluster is enriched in MAPK, immune, and apoptosis-related pathways, suggesting that lncRNA SNHG14 may interact with MRTFA and MRTFB, facilitating communication between neurons and glial cells [81].

The lncRNA metastasis associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1) was significantly downregulated in AD models and nerve growth factor (NGF)-stimulated PC12 cells, while miR-125b was upregulated. Overexpression of lncRNA MALAT1 inhibited neuronal apoptosis, promoted neurite outgrowth, reduced IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) levels, and increased IL-10 levels. Conversely, knockdown of MALAT1 promoted neuronal apoptosis, repressed neurite outgrowth, elevated IL-6 and TNF-α levels, and reduced IL-10 levels. Additionally, MALAT1 inversely regulated the expression of miR-125b. These findings suggest that lncRNA MALAT1 may interact with miR-125b to inhibit neuronal apoptosis and inflammation while promoting neurite outgrowth in AD [82].

The lncRNA SNHG14 is significantly upregulated in the APP/PS1 mouse model of AD, correlating with increased levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, along with a significant downregulation of miR-223-3p. Moreover, AVE0991 (a non-peptide analogue of Ang-(1–7)) downregulates SNHG14 expression, mitigating its sponging effect on miR-223-3p and blocking the neuroinflammatory response. This finding confirms that lncRNA SNHG14 influences the neuroinflammatory response by modulating the miR-223-3p/NLRP3 signaling pathway [83].

The lncRNA MEG3 is significantly downregulated in AD mouse models. Upregulation of MEG3 alleviates inflammatory damage by modulating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, exerting neuroprotective effects [67].

The lncRNA PRR34 antisense RNA 1 (PRR34-AS1) is significantly upregulated in the serum of AD patients and in Aβ25−35-treated HMC3 and BV2 microglial AD models, while the expression of miR-29c-3p is significantly downregulated. PRR34-AS1 exhibits a negative correlation with the neuroinflammatory processes in AD. Knockdown of PRR34-AS1 alleviates the aforementioned pathological damage by upregulating miR-29c-3p expression. The results suggest that the PRR34-AS1/miR-29c-3p signaling axis participates in the pathological progression of AD by modulating microglia-mediated inflammatory responses [84].

The lncRNA NEAT1 is closely associated with the pathological progression of PD. In the MPP+-induced PD model, levels of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β are significantly upregulated, indicating a relationship between NEAT1 and the neuroinflammatory response [73].

The lncRNA MALAT1 is upregulated in MPTP-induced PD mice and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced BV2 cells. Silencing of MALAT1 increases cell viability while inhibiting apoptosis and inflammation in LPS-induced BV2 cells. Additionally, MALAT1 enhances the methylation of the suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3) promoter, decreasing its expression by recruiting DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1), DNA methyltransferase 3 alpha (DNMT3A), and DNA methyltransferase 3 beta (DNMT3B). Furthermore, knockdown of SOCS3 eliminates the inhibition of LPS-induced BV2 cell injury mediated by sh-MALAT1. In vivo, silencing of MALAT1 improves neurological impairment and neuroinflammation in MPTP-induced PD mice. These findings suggest that MALAT1 exacerbates PD processes by inhibiting SOCS3 expression through increased promoter methylation [85]. Additionally, the lncRNAs SOX21 antisense RNA 1 (SOX21-AS1) and SOCS3 are upregulated in PD cell and mouse models. Inhibition of SOX21-AS1 represses LPS/adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-induced activation in BV2 cells and mitigates nerve damage caused by activated BV2 cells, alleviating the pathological features of PD. SOX21-AS1 epigenetically inhibits SOCS3 by recruiting enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) to its promoter. Overexpression of SOX21-AS1 partially offsets the repressive impact of SOCS3 enhancement on BV2 cell activation and the protective effect on nerve cells. This study suggests that SOX21-AS1 enhances LPS/ATP-induced BV2 cell activation and nerve damage caused by activated BV2 cells through its recruitment of EZH2 to the SOCS3 promoter [86].

The lncRNA small nucleolar RNA host gene 1 (SNHG1) and mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (MAPK1) are upregulated in the MPTP-induced PD mouse model. SNHG1 activates the MAPK signaling pathway, exacerbating neuronal apoptosis and oxidative stress by sponging miR-125b-5p, which alleviates inhibition of MAPK1. Conversely, knockdown of SNHG1 or overexpression of miR-125b-5p inhibits MAPK1 expression and mitigates pathological damage. These results indicate that the SNHG1/miR-125b-5p/MAPK1 signaling axis may participate in PD by modulating neuroinflammation [87].

lncRNAs and ferroptosis

Neuronal loss is a common endpoint in NDDs, but the mode of neuronal death is not exclusively apoptosis. Recent evidence confirms that ferroptosis drives early synaptic impairment and neuronal death in AD and PD. Ferroptosis is a novel form of cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. Accumulating evidence indicates that various NDDs are prominently associated with localized iron accumulation in specific regions of the central or peripheral nervous system [88]. Moreover, studies have shown that ferroptosis is broadly implicated in multiple pathological events in PD, including abnormal α-syn aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation, all of which exhibit crosstalk with ferroptosis signaling pathways [89]. Further research demonstrates that lncRNAs play a crucial upstream regulatory role in ferroptosis pathways (Table 3). Studies have established a ferroptosis-related signature model, including mRNAs, microRNAs (miRNAs), and lncRNAs, and characterized its association with immune infiltration in AD [90, 91]. These findings offer new insights into the role of ferroptosis-related molecular patterns and immune mechanisms in AD, providing a theoretical basis for the identification of diagnostic markers for the disease.

The lncRNA methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14) is downregulated, while lncRNA taurine upregulated gene 1 (TUG1) is upregulated in clinical patients and experimental models of AD. Overexpression of METTL14 or silencing of TUG1 effectively attenuates Aβ1–42-induced ferroptosis and neurotoxicity in SH-SY5Y cells [92].

The lncRNA LINC00472 is significantly upregulated in AD samples derived from GEO datasets and is positively correlated with FOXO1 expression. Knockdown of either LINC00472 or FOXO1 inhibits ferroptosis by reducing iron accumulation and upregulating GPX4 expression in Aβ25−35-induced AD mouse models and HT22 cells. This study reveals that LINC00472 plays a significant role in the regulation of ferroptosis in AD [63].

The lncRNA NEAT1 is significantly upregulated in the MPP+-induced PD model of SK-N-SH cells. Knockdown of NEAT1 enhances cell viability and glutathione (GSH) levels in the PD cell model while reducing levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), malondialdehyde (MDA), and Fe2+. NEAT1 functions as a sponge to suppress miR-150-5p expression, and overexpression of miR-150-5p suppresses ferroptosis in the PD cell model. This study suggests that knockdown of NEAT1 mitigates MPP+-induced ferroptosis through sponging miR-150-5p [93].

Additionally, a study based on bioinformatics analysis of 97 human substantia nigra tissue samples (54 PD patients and 43 healthy controls) from the GEO database revealed that NEAT1 was significantly upregulated across multiple cell types in the substantia nigra of PD patients. Moreover, PD-associated ferroptosis genes were significantly enriched in signaling pathways such as MAPK and calcium signaling, suggesting a close association with neuroinflammatory processes [94]. Similarly, a study investigating α-syn-driven ferroptosis in PD included 16 human substantia nigra tissue samples (8 PD patients and 8 healthy controls) from the GEO database. Bioinformatics analysis identified hub genes such as phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PIK3CA), ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM), and bromodomain containing protein 4 (BRD4), as well as the key lncRNA NEAT1, confirming that the NEAT1-PIK3CA/ATM ceRNA network can mediate α-syn-driven ferroptosis in PD [95].

lncRNAs in synaptic failure

Synaptic loss is a precursor to neuronal death, leading to cognitive and motor impairments. LncRNAs exhibit significant alterations during the early stages of synaptic impairment and the decline of neuroplasticity in NDDs [96], thus presenting considerable potential as early biomarkers. A study demonstrated that neuronal BC1 RNA plays a critical role in the protein-synthesis-dependent establishment of neuronal excitation-repression equilibria [97]. Similarly, brain cytoplasmic 200 (BC200), a cytoplasm-localized lncRNA predominantly expressed in neurons, can be transported to dendrites and is essential for maintaining long-term synaptic plasticity by targeting translational regulators within the neuronal somatodendritic compartment [98]. The astrocyte-specific lncRNAPRDM16-DT was significantly downregulated in postmortem brain tissues from AD patients. Knockdown of lncRNA PRDM16-DT impairs astrocytic glutamate uptake and lactate secretion, leading to a reduction in neuronal dendritic spine density in primary mouse astrocytes, while upregulation reverses this dysfunction. These findings indicate that PRDM16-DT is involved in regulating synaptic function by modulating astrocytic support for neurons [49]. Additionally, a study investigated the effects of lncRNA metastasis associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1) on synapse-related physiological processes, revealing that MALAT1 was upregulated in various cell types; however, knockout of MALAT1 in human tumor cells did not significantly affect cell proliferation, the cell cycle, or nuclear architecture. Moreover, genetic loss of MALAT1 in a knockout mouse model did not result in any apparent phenotype or histological abnormalities, suggesting that the functions of MALAT1 may be cell-type- or tissue-specific [99].

lncRNAs linking aging and neurodegeneration

Aging is a significant risk factor for NDDs. Aging-associated NDDs are characterized by progressive neuronal atrophy, neural tissue degeneration, and loss of neurological function, ultimately leading to cognitive and motor impairments. The aging process is further complicated by epigenetic reprogramming, telomere damage, and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). The senescence of neural cells exacerbates the incidence of NDDs. Research indicates that regulating the levels of specific lncRNAs in nervous system cells may counteract senescence, representing a promising approach for delaying neurodegeneration (Table 4). Through multifaceted regulatory mechanisms at the transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and epigenetic levels, lncRNAs have emerged as crucial mediators in the progression from organismal aging to neurodegenerative pathogenesis.

Another study indicated that combined cognitive and motor interventions could effectively delay cognitive decline in naturally aging rats by downregulating the expression levels of NEAT1 in the hippocampus [84].

The lncRNA growth arrest specific 5 (GAS5) was found to be downregulated in the brain tissues of AD and aged mice. Overexpression of SNCA-AS1 upregulates both SNCA and α-synuclein, with alterations in numerous PD-specific genes and pathways associated with aging-related processes [103].

The lncRNA LINC-PINT was upregulated in patients with PD and exhibited conspicuous increases in maturing primary culture neurons. Downregulation of LINC-PINT exacerbated the death of cultured N2A and SH-SY5Y cells exposed to oxidative stress. The lncRNA BC089918 was significantly downregulated following sciatic nerve injury and promotes neurite outgrowth in dorsal root ganglion neurons, suggesting its relevance to nerve injury and regenerative repair in the elderly [105].

The lncRNA TUG1 was upregulated in primary human astrocytes induced to undergo cellular senescence by exposure to the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) trans-activator of transcription. Downregulation of TUG1 expression reduced the levels of senescence-associated markers [106].

Clinical applications

LncRNAs are implicated in multiple biological processes and are closely associated with the onset and progression of NDDs. They can be released into various body fluids, including peripheral blood (serum and plasma), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and saliva, through active secretion or apoptotic pathways. Studies have demonstrated that detecting lncRNAs in the body fluids of patients with NDDs is an effective diagnostic approach.

lncRNAs as liquid biopsy biomarkers

In a study involving 88 patients with AD and 72 healthy controls, plasma expression levels of lncRNA 17A,51A, BACE1, and BC200 were found to be upregulated. Plasma BACE1 levels were significantly elevated in AD patients compared to healthy controls. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis revealed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.667 for BACE1-AS [107]. Furthermore, a separate study reported that plasma BACE1 levels were significantly regulated in AD patients, distinguishing them from healthy controls with 88% specificity for AD [108]. These observations suggest that BACE1 may serve as a potential biomarker for AD.

The lncRNA NEAT1 was significantly upregulated in the CSF and peripheral blood of PD patients. Downregulation of NEAT1 reduced cell apoptosis in a PD model using PC12 cells treated with MPP+. Additionally, knockdown of NEAT1 decreased inflammatory infiltration in brain tissue and cell apoptosis in the PD mouse model, indicating that NEAT1 is a potential biomarker for PD and a target for therapy [109].

A bioinformatics analysis of AD-related databases identified lncRNA RN7SK as a key gene involved in AD pathogenesis and associated with inflammatory responses. Notably, RN7SK was significantly upregulated in peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples from AD patients (P ═ 0.006) [110].

A comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of datasets from the GEO, which included 589 samples from patients with AD and healthy controls, revealed that a combined biomarker panel consisting of five lncRNAs—SNHG14, prostate androgen-regulated transcript 1 (PART1), nicotinamide nucleotide transhydrogenase antisense RNA 1 (NNT-AS1), AC093010.3, and ARMCX5-GPRASP2—demonstrated strong diagnostic efficacy for AD. Notably, PART1 was significantly downregulated, whereas SNHG14 was significantly upregulated in the serum samples of AD patients, revealing the potential roles of lncRNAs in the pathogenesis of AD [111].

A meta-analysis involving datasets from 553 patients with AD and 513 healthy controls identified significant upregulation of BACE1-AS, NEAT1, and GAS5 in plasma samples from AD patients. The pooled sensitivity, specificity, and AUC of lncRNAs for AD identification were reported as 0.74 (95% CI [0.63, 0.82], I2 ═ 79.2%), 0.88 (95% CI [0.75, 0.94], I2 ═ 88.9%), and 0.86 (95% CI [0.82, 0.88]), respectively. This study illustrates the high diagnostic accuracy of lncRNAs for AD, highlighting their potential as liquid biopsy biomarkers for the disease [112].

Fifteen epigenetically regulated lncRNAs were selected to develop a blood-based diagnostic model and nomogram for AD. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, along with decision and calibration curves, indicated strong predictive performance of the model. Furthermore, the methylation levels of lncRNAs in blood samples may serve as indicators for diagnosing AD, providing new insights for the development of non-invasive liquid biopsy markers for the disease [113].

Similar investigations have been conducted in patients with PD. A study utilizing Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of peripheral blood plasma from six PD patients and six healthy controls revealed that lncMTG2-1:1, lnc-CTSD-5:1, lnc-PCCA-3:1, lnc-VTCN1-3:1, lnc-ZNF25-7:1, andlnc-DAZ3-1:1 may represent key lncRNAs in PD. Notably, lnc-CTSD-5:1 exhibited the most significant alterations, and silencing this lncRNA restored tyrosine expression levels in MPP+-treated SH-SY5Y cells [114].

Various cell types in the central nervous system (CNS), including neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia, and embryonic neural stem cells, are capable of secreting exosomes. Research has demonstrated that lncRNAs can be selectively packaged into these exosomes, which can traverse the blood-brain barrier and transport brain-derived RNAs, proteins, and lipids into peripheral blood, CSF, saliva, and other bodily fluids. Consequently, exosomal lncRNAs can accurately reflect pathological changes in the CNS and significantly enhance diagnostic specificity, enabling non-invasive or minimally invasive disease detection. They represent a promising direction for future liquid biopsy applications.

The lncRNA BACE1-AS is notably upregulated in exosomes isolated from the plasma of AD patients [107]. Additionally, lncRNAs prostate cancer antigen 3 (PCA3) and RP11-462G22.1 are significantly upregulated in extracellular vesicles (EVs) isolated from the CSF of AD patients [115].

A study involving PD patients and age- and sex-matched healthy controls evaluated lncRNA levels extracted from exosomes in plasma samples, identifying 15 upregulated and 24 downregulated exosomal lncRNAs in the PD cohort. Bioinformatics analyses revealed that lnc-MKRN2-42:1 was positively correlated with Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) III scores, highlighting significant differences in exosomal lncRNA profiles between PD patients and controls [116].

To date, few studies have examined EV-derived lncRNAs directly associated with AD. Furthermore, lncRNAs carried by exosomes from diverse cellular origins and varying particle sizes demonstrate notable differences in types, expression levels, and regulatory roles in disease, indicating that current research in this area remains insufficient. Continued investigation is warranted to explore these avenues further.

lncRNAs as therapeutic targets

Therapeutic strategies targeting lncRNAs are classified into two main categories: the first involves inhibiting the expression or function of pathogenic lncRNAs, while the second focuses on restoring or enhancing the expression levels of protective lncRNAs. Knockdown of SNHG1 reversed cell apoptosis, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) alterations, caspase-3 activity, and the expression levels of apoptosis-related proteins in AD cell models induced by Aβ25-35[117]. Additionally, studies indicated that Wilms tumor 1 antisense RNA (WT1-AS) is downregulated in Aβ25-35-treated SH-SY5Y cells; however, overexpression of WT1-AS effectively inhibited cell apoptosis and improved learning and memory abilities in AD mouse models [118].

Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) present a promising therapeutic approach. ASOs can modulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level by targeting specific RNAs, particularly those that are challenging to regulate using small molecules or antibody drugs. Research has shown that multiple ASO-targeting lncRNAs significantly improved pathological conditions and behavior in mouse models, with some progressing to early clinical trials demonstrating safety and tolerability [119]. Recent decades have seen the Journal of Clinical Investigation (JCI) publish several significant preclinical advances, underscoring the clinical translational potential of ASOs. Presently, two ASOs have gained approval for treating degenerative motor neuron diseases, such as spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) and ALS [120]. BIIB080 was the first ASO to enter clinical trials targeting tau expression for mild AD [121].

BIIB067, an ASO targeting SOD1, effectively downregulated SOD1 mRNA expression levels by binding to specific regions of the SOD1 pre-mRNA. Administration of this ASO into the CSF of mice, rats, and monkeys expressing human SOD1G93A significantly reduced SOD1 mRNA levels in the brain and spinal cord, thereby delaying disease onset and prolonging survival in ALS animal models [122].

Treatment with Jacifusen or ION363, targeting FUS with an ASO, could delay motor neuron degeneration in ALS mouse models by reducing FUS protein levels in the brain and spinal cord [123].

TUG1-targeting ASO (TUG1-ASO) represents a specific nucleic acid-based therapeutic that incorporates a drug delivery system, demonstrating antitumor efficacy against glioblastoma (GB) in mouse models. The study enrolled patients aged 18–75 years with recurrent glioblastoma (rGB) following postoperative temozolomide combined with radiotherapy to evaluate the safety, tolerability, and maximum tolerated dose (MTD) of TUG1-ASO [124]. While this research primarily addresses malignant glioma rather than traditional NDDs, it provides critical evidence for the therapeutic potential of lncRNAs in neurodegenerative disorders. Given their stability and feasibility for brain delivery, ASOs are emerging as a highly promising therapeutic strategy, with numerous ASOs currently in preclinical and clinical development. There is hope for the widespread application of these therapeutic technologies in treating NDDs.

The clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats / CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9) system has introduced novel therapeutic approaches for lncRNAs in recent years. Knocking out pathogenic lncRNAs via the CRISPR/Cas9 system or specifically upregulating protective lncRNAs through CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) offers promising new strategies for intervening in the pathological progression of NDDs [125]. In HD and various nucleotide repeat expansion disorders, CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing has successfully been employed to eliminate pathogenic repeat sequences or gene fragments [126]. While much research has focused on protein-coding sequences, this paradigm can be extended to lncRNAs by disrupting lncRNA promoters, splice sites, or functional domains, thereby regulating their expression levels in neuronal cells. We believe the CRISPR/Cas9 system can also effectively regulate lncRNA expression in neurons through such mechanisms.

Exosome membrane-camouflaged nanocarriers have emerged as a novel strategy for targeted drug delivery in NDDs [127]. A study isolating exosomes from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs) (WT-Exo) and employing small interfering RNA (siRNA) to establish a knockdown of lncRNA9969 (KD-Exo) revealed that WT-Exo significantly reduced cellular damage and improved Aβ clearance in Aβ-injured SH-SY5Y cells. In contrast, the protective effect of KD-Exo was notably diminished. In APP/PS1 mice, treatment with WT-Exo significantly improved spatial memory deficits and upregulated hippocampal expression of synaptic proteins such as synaptophysin (Syn) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). This study indicates that hUC-MSC-derived exosomes exert neuroprotective effects by delivering functional lncRNA9969, elucidating the central role of exosomal lncRNAs in AD.

Challenges and prospects

LncRNAs have emerged as critical factors in NDDs due to their essential roles in gene expression regulation, neuroinflammation, and neuronal survival [128]. However, the complex pathological mechanisms underlying NDDs, the intricate pathological networks involving multisystem interactions, and the challenges posed by the blood-brain barrier significantly hinder therapeutic strategies targeting lncRNAs [129].

From a molecular mechanism perspective, despite the known involvement of lncRNAs in oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and protein homeostasis within core pathological processes, several obstacles remain. The human genome harbors a vast array of annotated lncRNAs, yet most of their functions remain poorly understood, limiting comprehensive assessments of their biological effects in NDDs. Furthermore, lncRNAs exhibit tissue and cell type specificity, making it challenging to obtain high-quality human brain tissue samples for dynamic functional studies.

In biomarker development, lncRNAs are recognized as promising invasive and high-sensitivity biomarkers due to their stability and tissue specificity in CSF and peripheral blood. However, the field encounters difficulties in validating specificity and translating these findings into clinical practice. To enhance diagnostic accuracy, lncRNA detection can be integrated with existing technologies. For instance, protein amplification techniques can currently detect ultra-low concentrations of abnormal proteins in biological fluids, facilitating the creation of multi-dimensional early diagnostic models for more precise disease assessment [130]. The application of artificial intelligence and multimodal sensing technologies will enable highly sensitive detection and real-time monitoring of lncRNA biomarkers, laying the groundwork for early disease diagnosis in the future. Additionally, cutting-edge technologies such as single-cell transcriptome sequencing and spatial transcriptomics may pave the way for early diagnosis of NDDs.

Regarding therapeutic targets, dysregulated lncRNA expression during NDDs presents an opportunity to correct imbalanced gene expression networks by targeting key lncRNA molecules, constituting a vital direction for exploring novel therapeutic strategies in NDDs. Nevertheless, the application of ASOs in NDDs faces significant challenges, primarily due to the blood-brain barrier. As negatively charged biomacromolecules, ASOs struggle to penetrate the blood-brain barrier effectively and are prone to clearance from the central nervous system [131]. Future efforts must focus on enhancing the efficiency and safety of drug delivery across the blood-brain barrier, optimizing the targeted delivery of lncRNA-based therapeutics through nanoplatforms and biomaterials. Moreover, while the CRISPR/Cas9 system enables the modification of pathogenic lncRNAs, off-target effects and safety concerns pose challenges to clinical application.

LncRNAs hold substantial potential for application in NDDs, yet clinical translation faces numerous challenges. Despite these hurdles, the ongoing development of high-throughput screening technologies, bioinformatics algorithms, and novel drug delivery systems, combined with the integration of molecular neurobiology, nanotechnology, artificial intelligence, and precision medicine, is anticipated to provide new directions for NDD treatment and clinical drug development.

Conclusion

Given their high expression specificity, multilayered regulatory modes, and broad pathological relevance, lncRNAs have emerged as a focal point in neurodegenerative disease research. They serve as key regulators mediating pathological events such as abnormal protein aggregation, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, synaptic damage, and aberrant cell death, while also representing ideal molecular targets for early diagnosis, monitoring, and targeted therapies for these disorders. With ongoing advancements in innovative fields such as biomacromolecular phase separation, ferroptosis, exosomes, and spatial transcriptomics, research targeting lncRNAs is rapidly transitioning from elucidating basic mechanisms to achieving clinical translation. In the future, the establishment of a precision diagnosis and treatment system centered on lncRNAs may yield novel intervention strategies for the prevention and treatment of NDDs.
