Early Childhood Trauma in Humanitarian Crisis Settings: Factors and Effects (A Review)
Authors: David Timilehin Isarinade, Felix Oluwafemi Ajayi, Glory Olalekan Adebajo, Abiodun David Akintayo, Susan Diseiye Elliot-Wokoro, Gbenga Joseph Alabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Childhood trauma is strongly connected to both mental and physical health problems that can occur and persist for a lifetime. Humanitarian crises often cause trauma, especially to vulnerable populations such as children and women. This review seeks to evaluate early childhood trauma in the context of humanitarian crises, highlighting the predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating, and protective factors as well as identifying the short- and long-term consequences of childhood trauma while also examining some strategies that would help mitigate the effect of childhood trauma in humanitarian settings. We searched four databases, namely PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and grey literature sources for relevant literature. We reviewed 50 eligible studies. We found that factors that can either predispose, precipitate, or perpetuate physical, mental, and social trauma to children are more pronounced during humanitarian crises, and this could have serious consequences on the neurodevelopmental process of a child. Factors such as age, gender, location, and family predispose children to trauma, while factors like family separation, migration, food insecurity, and poor healthcare access are found to precipitate childhood trauma. These have harmful effects on children’s mental health, especially when they occur in early childhood. Family response to traumatic experiences in humanitarian crises, duration of exposure, and exposure to several types of traumas worsen the impact of humanitarian crises on the child’s mental health. Implementation of strategies and policies such as the Mental Health Gap Action Program can significantly reduce the incidence of early childhood trauma in humanitarian crises.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-025-00206-5.

Introduction

A humanitarian emergency or crisis is “an event or a series of events that represents a critical threat to the health, safety, security and wellbeing of a community or a large group of people, usually over a wide geographical area” [1].

It usually involves circumstances where humanitarian needs are sufficiently large and complex enough to demand significant external assistance and resources, with a need for multisectoral response and engagement of a wide range of international humanitarian actors (IASC) [2]. Humanitarian crisis events typically threaten a community’s safety, wellbeing, or health to the point that existing support structures and systems are overwhelmed, necessitating external aid [3].

Humanitarian crises are broadly classified into (1) natural disasters, like floods, hurricanes, tornadoes, earthquakes, and droughts; (2) man-made disasters, including war, armed conflict, forced displacement, industrial accidents, and refugee crises; and (3) major infectious disease outbreaks like the 2014–2016 Ebola Outbreak in West Africa and the COVID-19 pandemic. Sometimes, different kinds of crises may happen simultaneously, such as famine and disease outbreaks occurring in the middle of a war situation or following natural disasters [3–5].

According to the United Nations in 2023, 1 in every 23 people all over the world requires humanitarian assistance. This constitutes 339 million people across 68 countries [6]. This is significantly greater than the 274 and 235 million people needing humanitarian assistance in 2022 and 2021 respectively [7]. This shows an upward trend in the prevalence of humanitarian crises worldwide.

Usually, humanitarian crises often disproportionately affect vulnerable populations including low and middle-income countries, women and children [3]. A significant fraction of maternal, newborn and Under-5 mortality occurs in humanitarian settings, and even children who manage to survive are often unable to thrive or face different forms of abuse [8].

The essence of this review is to address childhood trauma in the context of humanitarian crises, highlighting the predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating and protective factors as well as identify the short and long-term consequences of childhood trauma in humanitarian crises.

Methods

Literature Source

This review was conducted using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses guidelines (PRISMA) [9]. We conducted a comprehensive literature search from various databases and sources. Published between 2000–2024. Databases searched included PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library and Web of Science. Grey literature was sourced from UNICEF, WHO and government databases including reports. Peer reviewed sources used included original studies, systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Search Strategy

Keywords used in the search included early childhood trauma,” “humanitarian crises,” “conflict settings,” “natural disasters,” “children,” “psychological effects,” and “mental health.” Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied to refine search results and ensure coverage of both broad and specific topics.

Eligibility Criteria and Study Selection

The PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes) framework was used to define the eligibility of studies used in this review. Publications included studies of (1) Population: Studies published in English conducted among children who have experienced trauma due to humanitarian crises and studies that provided context on understanding childhood trauma. Studies focused on adolescents and adults were excluded.

(2) Intervention: Studies that assessed traumatic events in children exposed to humanitarian crises, discussed risk factors for childhood trauma , protective factors from traumatic events following humanitarian crises or those that provided a context for understanding childhood trauma were included.

(3) Comparison: Studies that compared the effects of factors such as age, gender and family background on childhood trauma in the setting of humanitarian crisis.

(4) Outcome: Studies that evaluated early childhood outcomes such as developmental, psychological and social outcomes in the context of childhood trauma. Studies that examined the effects of preventive measures and interventions were also included.

Results

The initial search yielded 1010 articles; 122 duplicates were removed. The titles and abstracts of the remaining 890 articles were screened by two independent reviewers (G.O.A and G.J.A), 705 articles were excluded due to irrelevant population or being conducted in non-humanitarian crisis settings based on the consensus of the independent reviewers. The full text of the remaining 183 articles was reviewed for relevance. 50 sources including original studies, meta-analyses, reports and systematic reviews that were found relevant and subsequently included in the study.

Discussion

Health in Humanitarian Settings

The World Health Organization defines health as a “state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not just the absence of disease or infirmity” [10]. This definition stresses the importance of not just the physical aspects of health but also the mental and social sides of health. For an individual to be said to be in good health, well-being must be demonstrated in at least these three areas. In settings with humanitarian crises, there are varying degrees of distortion in these health domains.

The physical health of persons in the midst or center of humanitarian crises is usually challenged by increased morbidity and mortality from infectious diseases, malnutrition, physical assault or trauma, noncommunicable diseases, inadequate reproductive health, maternal and child health; infectious disease; injury and other physical trauma; sexual and reproductive health; nutrition; and non-communicable diseases such as diabetes, heart disease and mental health [5, 11, 12].

Social determinants of health also suffer greatly during humanitarian crises, giving rise to dangerously elevated levels of health inequity with increased poverty, unequal access to health care, lack of education, presence of stigma, and discrimination contributing to these health inequities. These social factors give rise to poor short and long-term effects on the health of people affected [13, 14]. According to the WHO, almost all those affected by humanitarian emergencies experience psychological distress [14]. 20% of people in humanitarian situations are likely to have a mental disorder such as depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, bipolar disorder, or schizophrenia [15].

For instance, refugees who had fled from conflict in Tigray in Ethiopia to Sudan in 2020 reported how children were depicting violence and dead bodies in their drawings while some were said to have started behaving violently and erratically [15].

Child and Childhood Trauma

The Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC) article 1 defines a child as “every human being below the age of eighteen years unless under the law applicable to the child, majority is attained earlier” [16].

Trauma is a perceived experience that often threatens injury, or death, and causes feelings of terror, fear, and helplessness [13].

Childhood trauma is described as “the mental result of a sudden external blow or a series of blows, that renders a child temporarily helpless and breaks through ordinary coping and defensive mechanisms” [17, 18].

Humanitarian emergencies usually come with several forms of trauma, especially towards the vulnerable population consisting of women and children.

Predisposing Factors for Childhood Trauma in the Setting of Humanitarian Crisis

A. Age

Childhood is the most vulnerable period in the life of any individual and as such children are very susceptible to the various effects of humanitarian crises. Studies show that the most vulnerable age in the event of a crisis is between 8 and 12 years [19]. The effects of crisis in this age group have long-lasting impacts on the child’s neurological and physical development, which could ultimately lead to the death of children due to the inability to cope with the stress of this crisis [20].

B. Gender

The female gender is overtly predisposed to being harmed during crisis periods from events such as rape, prostitution, trafficking and child marriage. Children’s safety and security are strongly influenced by their gender and developmental stage. Adolescent girls are more vulnerable to harm because they are maturing sexually and this may be more pronounced during humanitarian crises [21].

C. Location

A child’s location plays a vital role in their exposure to humanitarian crises and consequently childhood trauma because some regions of the world are more prone to disasters, unlike some other parts [22]. Examples are the earthquakes in Japan and Turkey, hurricane-prone regions like Puerto Rico, and the southwestern regions of the U.S. The location also influences the severity and degree of trauma a child is exposed to as proximity to the area of crisis increases the chances of getting exposed [23].

D. Family background

The ability of a child to withstand stress is highly dependent on the quality of relationships with family and friends [24]. The responses of children to crises are influenced by the kind of care received from their caregivers [24]. Some reviews found that a supportive parent–child relationship can reduce outcomes such as PTSD and depression whereas conflict may elevate negative consequences [25–29].

E. Health and disabilities

Children living with disabilities which include but are not limited to visual, and hearing impairments have an increased risk of being deprived of information, access to goods and services and other benefits to children in terms of unequal access to goods, services, spaces, and information. Disability also includes children who have long-term physical, psychosocial, intellectual or sensory (visual and hearing) impairments [30].

Aside from factors mentioned previously, socioeconomic status and cultural background have also been found in the literature to predispose children to childhood trauma during humanitarian crises [31].

Precipitating Factors for Childhood Trauma in the Setting of Humanitarian crisis

A. Separation

This occurs commonly among children who are separated from their families either by the death of one or more members of the family or by dislocation which has higher rates in humanitarian crises and exposes them to trafficking, child labor, and opportunistic crimes such as assault/robbery, sexual and gender violence [32].

B. Living status

Children who have been displaced due to crises and have become refugees are usually at an increased risk of neglect, violence, abuse, and discrimination which may result in decreased or no access to essential services such as formal education, immunization [21]. They may also be faced with problems such as inappropriate detention, movement restrictions or xenophobia, increasing their exposure to further trauma [21].

C. Food insecurity

Crises and disasters make food unavailable either through the destruction of farms or farm products or by preventing the transportation of food products, which further impairs the development of children as the required nutrients for growth are not readily available.

D. Poor health facilities

During humanitarian crises, health facilities are destroyed or overwhelmed with rising patient numbers and limited facilities and resources.

Aside factors mentioned previously, lack of immunization measures, kind of humanitarian crises and duration of exposure have also been found to precipitate childhood trauma [33–36].

Perpetuating Factors for Childhood Trauma in the Setting of Humanitarian crisis

Childhood trauma arises when a child is subjected to distressing life events or conditions that overwhelm their emotional capacity to cope [37].

Childhood trauma occurrence is more than what we think. About two-thirds of children will have experienced at least one traumatic event before clocking 16 years [38]. These events include various forms of abuse, neglect, accidents, disasters, exposure to violence, armed conflict, or life-threatening health conditions [38].

Children subjected to these traumatic experiences are at an increased risk of developing difficulties in several key areas of their growth and development [39–41].

Experiencing trauma in the early years of life has been associated with long-term deleterious physical and mental effects such as abnormal brain development, poor emotional and social skills, learning difficulties, poor relational skills, and chronic health conditions [41–44]. Studies show that trauma that occurs in early childhood results in severe lifelong deleterious effects when compared to those experienced much later in life [14, 37, 45]. Complex trauma occurs in the setting of repeated traumas perpetuated by parents or other caregivers [37, 45].

For example, post-traumatic stress disorders (PTSD) occur more in the setting of childhood trauma than in adults [37]. Facing multiple types of traumatic experiences can increase the risk of developing PTSD compared to experiencing only one form of trauma over time [37]. PTSD in itself causes nightmares, destabilizing memories and flashbacks which are traumatic adding to the burden of unpleasant experiences these children go through [46]. They may also exhibit self-destructive and aggressive behaviors, sleep difficulties, or remain persistently hyperactive with heightened awareness and reactivity to their surroundings [46]. There is no specific response to trauma especially in children however, children generally experience distress following a traumatic event, with many of them returning to their preexposure functioning level shortly after [37].

Children respond to trauma in different ways depending on the nature of the trauma, their age and developmental level, family background, and the environment in which they live. Personal strengths and vulnerabilities also shape these reactions [37, 47]. Young children who experience trauma may find it hard to form secure attachments, display heightened separation fears, struggle with routines like eating and sleeping, or revert to earlier developmental behaviors. [48, 49]. School-age children who experience trauma may become more anxious about safety, act out or isolate themselves, struggle to concentrate in class, or be haunted by memories of the event through dreams or intrusive thoughts [48, 49].

Additionally, adolescents who experienced trauma may exhibit anxiety, depression, high-risk behaviors (e.g. drug and alcohol misuse, dangerous driving, sexual promiscuity, self-harm, illegal activities), anger, feelings of guilt, a negative view of societal norms, and suicidal thoughts.

Protective Factors

Certain factors and events promote healthy development and reduce the risk of adverse outcomes despite experiencing unpleasant life events [37]. A study found that the most significant protective factor against negative outcomes following trauma is the reliable presence of a nurturing and emotionally supportive adult figure in the child’s life. [50]. These adults help children recover from trauma by enabling them to process distressing events positively, prevent further trauma, promote self-regulation, and healing [37].

The National Child Traumatic Stress Network identified several protective factors with strong social support by family members, friends, school personnel, and individuals within the broader community playing a key role [37]. Additionally, social support can strengthen spiritual or cultural beliefs, personal goals or aspirations, talents, and skills (such as excelling academically or in sports), and coping strategies that are adaptable to different situations that contribute to life’s meaning. [37].

Preventing re-traumatization involves intentionally modifying children’s environments to remove potential triggers such as particular sights, sounds, smells, objects, people, or places that could remind them of their original trauma. It is also vital to protect them from new traumatic experiences, as further exposure can worsen the harmful effects of trauma and disrupt their healing process [37].

Impact of Childhood Trauma on Mental Health

WHO defines mental health as a state of well-being where a person can attain their full potential, is able to manage everyday stress, work efficiently and productively, and make meaningful contributions to his or her community [51].The mental health of children begins from achieving normal developmental milestones, and the ability to learn adequate healthy social skills to cope with normal day-to-day challenges either at home, in schools or in their various communities [52].

A. Biology of children’s brain development and possible entry point of trauma-related events

The brain is a complex structure in human anatomy and physiology, whose development involves various processes that influence behavior and personality. These processes begin around 2 weeks after conception and start with development from embryonic cells (neurulation) and continue until about 5 years post-delivery [53]. However, several studies have shown that brain development is a life-long process capable of undergoing changes throughout the lifespan but early brain development in childhood takes preeminence [54–56]. In a study by Zeanah et al. [57], three groups of children were studied (i.e. institutionalized, foster care and children living with their biological parents) concerning their brain development. With different exposure and environmental influences, it was discovered that these children have different patterns of brain development across groups and the same pattern within groups [57]. This is a vivid illustration of the impact of trauma-related events on children’s brain development (such as under specification and miswiring of brain circuits), as children living with their parents will have minimal home-based trauma than their counterparts in foster care or institutional homes.

B. Evidence-based effects (direct and indirect) of childhood trauma on the developing brain

Several studies have shown the deleterious effects of various childhood traumas on the developing brain [31, 58, 59]. Several gene mutations arise as consequences including the Met allele mutation associated with oppositional defiant behavior and antisocial behavior [60]. Other trauma spectrum disorders include post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depressive disorders, personality disorders, dissociative identity disorders, acute stress disorders, adjustment disorders, anxiety disorders including panic disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, and obsessive–compulsive disorder [61, 62]. A retrospective study of the longest war (Israeli Palestinian crisis) conducted in Israel, found that there was a positive correlation between experiencing war in childhood and a reduction in sleep quality in later life further strengthening evidence on the many adverse psychological effects of childhood war trauma documented in literature [63, 64].

Interventions for Prevention and Control of Mental Health Effects of Humanitarian Crises

A. Cognitive behavioral therapy

Eliminating adverse childhood experiences is the best strategy to prevent childhood trauma. According to a report from the US Centers for Disease Control and Intervention, five evidence-based strategies were reported: enhancing economic support to families, and communities, fostering support to parents and positive parenting, providing quality education for both children and their caregivers, improving parenting skills and, promoting healthy child development [65].

B. Prospective management of childhood trauma-induced mental health challenges

Enhancement of primary care through adequate financing and favorable government policies will help in improving, identifying and addressing several risk factors for child-related trauma including abuse and neglect. Likewise, behavioral parent training programs may reduce occurrence, and treatment of children and families affected [66].

Mental health professionals are also vital in the management of these mental health challenges by ensuring the application and proper implementation of the Mental Health Gap Action Program (mhGAP). This program was launched by the WHO to scale up the accessibility of treatment for several mental health conditions [67]. The program targets low and middle-income countries where treatment gaps exceed 75% [68]. The mhGAP aims to reduce this gap by improving care accessibility through key interventions and partnerships, supported by the commitment of governments, health professionals, and communities [69]. Specific action programs for early childhood trauma include measures within the health sector such as provision of skilled care at birth, effective community-based services for maternal and child health care, prenatal screening for Down syndrome, and prevention of alcohol abuse by mothers [69, 70]. Multisectoral measures relating to public health include fortification of food with iodine and folic acid, interventions to reduce child abuse, identification and initial care in primary healthcare settings, referral and supervisory support by specialists [70]. Mental health specialists should prioritize routine enquiry (also known as trauma-informed care) about childhood experiences, especially in every clinical assessment [71]. The government should also make basic needs available, accessible, and sustainable to children and their parents or caregivers.

A Cochrane review of studies evaluated the effects of psychological intervention in preventing mental disorders in children and adolescents in low and middle-income countries and found that participants were more likely to leave interventions for reasons of ineffectiveness of interventions. There was no significant difference in the 3 month follow up period in intervention groups and control groups in terms of the occurrence of PTSD, anxiety, and depressive symptoms [72]. However, this study noted a paucity of evidence to suggest whether effectiveness of the interventions was linked to acceptability as there were limited studies in this area which points to the complexity of health research in humanitarian crisis settings compared to areas where there are no humanitarian crises [73].

Feasibility and Implementation Challenges of Strategies in Humanitarian Settings

Many strategies and evidence-based interventions have been designed to prevent and ameliorate adverse events following humanitarian crises. These interventions are holistically designed and cut across several domains including physical, social and mental well-being of individuals and families in the community [73, 74]. However, interventions in humanitarian crises are complex in nature, with many barriers to implementation and cost constraints. Some of these challenges are discussed below.

A. Resource limitations

Many conflict zones are areas grapple with low economic capacity with major dependence on foreign aid and donations of non-governmental organizations. Though funding for humanitarian actions has increased in recent years, the sharp rise in humanitarian need outweighs available resources [75, 76]. These meagre resources are often allocated to meet immediate relief at the expense of strategic long-term interventions.

B. Sociocultural and political barriers

Persistent gaps exist in public health information when designing intervention strategies in crisis areas. Interventions need to be developed based on the sociocultural and political context of the community to achieve maximal results [74]. Diplomacy with political actors is key as many humanitarian organizations seek to alleviate adverse effects of crises [77, 78]. For example, the efforts in interventional strategies in the unending Ethiopian crisis have been marred by political instability and inadequate diplomacy. Social acceptance of strategies also plays a vital role as the social acceptability of interventions may limit the acceptability and efforts designed to measure the effectiveness of interventions.

C. Growing insecurity

Insecurity in areas of active conflicts poses barriers to humanitarian workers and the implementation of interventions [77]. The target population in many active conflicts often remains inaccessible, increasing the costs of security and failure to meet strategic goals in conflict zones especially those targeting vulnerable populations such as children. An example is the Russian-Ukraine war where roads and bridges linking communities are being destroyed leaving many inner cities unreachable [79].

D. Stakeholders’ cooperation

In crisis settings, many players are involved in carrying out interventions [74]. While this is advantageous, there is need for collaborative efforts as lack of alignment of goals often leads to conflicts of interest which negatively impacts the effectiveness of operations [80]. Allocation of resources is often focused on different interventions that suites the goals and bilateral agreement without proper communication with other players.

Limitation of the Study

While we strived to an present evidence-based understanding of childhood trauma, its effects, preventive and propagating factors, and interventions to combat adverse outcomes, our study was purely narrative; hence we did not carry out quantitative analysis.

Secondly, while we prioritized using reliable and credible sources, including studies with diverse methodologies, populations, and settings may have introduced heterogeneity. Inherent biases, such as selection bias, reporting bias, or varying quality in the referenced studies, may have influenced our interpretations and conclusions.

Our study was broad in its context and did not consider individual analysis of specific humanitarian crises. This may limit the applicability of our findings to local contexts; however, our study will strengthen global understanding of this subject area and bridge the global foundational knowledge gap in preventing and managing childhood trauma occurring in many emerging crises locally.

Conclusion

Humanitarian crises are associated with a lot of factors that can either predispose, precipitate, or perpetuate physical, mental, and social trauma to children, and this has far-reaching consequences on the neurodevelopmental process of a child. Children who experience trauma in the context of a humanitarian crisis are at greater risk of negative health outcomes compared to children raised in a non-crisis context. Predisposing factors like age, gender, location, family, and precipitating factors like family separation, migration, food insecurity, and poor healthcare access have deleterious effects on children’s mental health, which are common occurrences in humanitarian crises. Family reaction, duration of exposure, and exposure to several types of traumas worsen the impact of humanitarian crises on the childrens' mental health. Strategies like improving family economic standing, encouraging positive parenting, provision of quality education, and promotion of healthy child development will go a long way in supporting the child in humanitarian crises. Implementation of policies like the Mental Health Gap Action Program (mhGAP) in humanitarian settings as well as provision of mental health support to vulnerable populations in humanitarian settings will help reduce the incidence of early childhood trauma in humanitarian crises settings.

There must be intentional community evidence- based holistic prevention and intervention strategies to mitigate adverse effects of resulting trauma on children especially in areas of continuous conflict like the Russian-Ukraine war, Israel-Palestine war and the Ethiopian crisis in Africa.
