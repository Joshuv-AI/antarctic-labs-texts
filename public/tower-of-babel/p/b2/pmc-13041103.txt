Single-cell hdWGCNA and experimental validation identify an innovative T-cell-associated prognostic model and immune microenvironment in lung adenocarcinoma with lymph node metastasis
Authors: Sixuan Wu, Lijun Zeng, Yuehua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLymph nodes (LNs) are the most common and typically the earliest sites of metastasis in tumors. This study aims to establish a T-cell-based prognostic model, providing a critical foundation for evaluating the prognosis of patients with lymph node-metastatic lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD).MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database were utilized, combined with high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA), differential expression analysis, and Cox/LASSO regression, to construct a T-cell-related prognostic model incorporating key genes CD69, GOLGA8A, and IL16. Potential drug-gene interactions were identified through protein-protein interaction analysis, RNA-binding protein regulatory network analysis, drug regulation studies, and molecular docking. The expression of model genes was validated in lung adenocarcinoma cell lines using gene expression profiling, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), immunohistochemistry (IHC), and multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) assays.ResultsThe established T-cell-associated prognostic model (CD69, GOLGA8A, and IL16) was significantly correlated with immune microenvironment characteristics, tumor mutational burden, and potential therapeutic responsiveness. The low-risk group exhibited a more favorable immune profile. The predictive map, constructed by integrating clinical variables, significantly improved the model’s interpretability and utility for personalized survival prediction. Further molecular analysis identified multiple drug-gene interactions, providing novel insights into therapeutic strategies. Experimental validation confirmed the differential expression of model genes in tumor tissues.ConclusionsThe T-cell marker gene-based prognostic risk model provides a foundation for evaluating the prognosis of lymph node-metastatic LUAD and shows potential for optimizing targeted therapy and immunotherapy strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15649-4.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, Lymph node metastasis, Single-cell RNA sequencing, T-cell prognostic model, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the predominant subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), is characterized by high invasiveness and mortality, imposing a substantial burden on patients and healthcare systems worldwide [1–3]. Despite advances in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, including targeted therapy and immunotherapy, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, and the tumor displays marked biological heterogeneity, leading to poor prognosis [4–6]. This study was designed to characterize the cellular landscape of LUAD through single-cell analysis, thereby providing a foundation for the development of more precise prognostic models and personalized therapeutic strategies.

In recent years, immunotherapy has become a research focus due to its ability to activate the patient’s immune system to combat tumors [7, 8]. Its primary mechanism involves blocking immune checkpoints on the surface of tumor cells, thereby enhancing the antitumor activity of T cells [9, 10]. In lung cancer treatment, immunotherapy has achieved notable progress, with the application of programmed death receptor-1 (PD-1) and programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) inhibitors extending from advanced lung cancer to perioperative treatment for resectable NSCLC [11–13]. Clinical trials such as KEYNOTE-189 [14] and KEYNOTE-407 [15] have demonstrated that pembrolizumab combined with chemotherapy significantly improves the prognosis of patients with advanced NSCLC. However, despite substantial benefits for some patients, many develop resistance, and the complexity of the immune microenvironment complicates the prediction of therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, gaining a deeper understanding of the immune microenvironment in LUAD is of considerable clinical importance.

Lymph nodes (LNs) are the most common and often the earliest sites of cancer metastasis [16, 17]. However, the mechanisms by which metastatic cancer reshapes immune cells within LNs remain poorly understood. Although LN biopsy is routinely performed for cancer diagnosis and staging, immunological studies utilizing such samples are still limited. In this study, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was employed to compare the immune microenvironment between intrathoracic metastatic and non-metastatic LNs in patients with LUAD. T cells are pivotal in tumor immune responses, participating in immune surveillance and contributing critically to antitumor activity [18–20]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the degree of T cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment is closely associated with patient prognosis [21, 22]. However, tumor cells can suppress T cell function through multiple mechanisms, resulting in immune escape and reduced immunotherapy efficacy [23, 24]. Therefore, elucidating the role of T cells in LUAD and identifying strategies to modulate their function to enhance immunotherapy outcomes hold substantial clinical significance.

This study employed scRNA-seq to systematically characterize the cellular heterogeneity and differentiation trajectories of intrathoracic lymph node metastases in LUAD, identifying multiple cell subpopulations, including epithelial cells, immune cells, and T cells, and revealing their intercellular communication networks and dynamic changes. Based on T cell–related differentially expressed genes (DEGs), a prognostic model was constructed using high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA), with clinical parameters incorporated into a nomogram to enhance clinical applicability. Survival outcomes, immune landscapes, and drug sensitivity were evaluated across different risk subgroups. Furthermore, protein–protein interaction (PPI), drug regulation, and RNA-binding protein (RBP) networks of the model genes were analyzed, followed by molecular docking to assess their potential therapeutic relevance. Overall, this study elucidates the clinical significance of T cell–related prognostic models and their key genes in LUAD.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

The scRNA-seq dataset GSE277742 analyzed in this study was retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [25]. To assess the robustness and applicability of the constructed prognostic model, an additional independent validation cohort, the GSE68465 transcriptomic expression dataset, was utilized. The mRNA expression profiles and corresponding clinical information of LUAD patients were retrieved from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database [26]. The overall study design and analytical workflow are presented in Fig. 1. LUAD tumor samples and corresponding adjacent non-tumor tissues were collected from 30 pathologically confirmed LUAD patients at the First Affiliated Hospital of University of South China. The acquisition of human specimens followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of First Affiliated Hospital of University of South China (NO.2025LL0701001). Consent was obtained from all participants, and the informed consent forms were signed.

Flowchart of the study

The scRNA-seq data analysis

The “Seurat” package was first employed to import the expression matrix in 10X Genomics format and construct Seurat objects [27]. The mitochondrial gene ratio was calculated, and quality control was performed, retaining only cells with more than 50 detected genes and a mitochondrial gene proportion below 15%. The expression matrix was then normalized using NormalizeData, and 1,500 highly variable genes were identified with FindVariableFeatures, with the top 10 highly variable genes annotated. Data were subsequently standardized using ScaleData. For principal component analysis (PCA), dimensionality reduction was conducted with RunPCA, and batch effects across samples were corrected using the “harmony” package, generating feature gene loading plots, PCA scatter plots, heatmaps, and JackStraw significance analyses. In clustering analysis, an adjacency matrix was constructed with FindNeighbors based on the top 20 principal components, and clustering was performed at multiple resolutions using FindClusters. The optimal resolution was determined with the “clustree” package [28], and clustering results were visualized using RunUMAP. Marker genes for each cell subpopulation were identified with FindAllMarkers under the criteria of logFC > 1 and adjusted p < 0.05, and corresponding heatmaps were generated. For cell annotation, marker gene bubble plots were created using “SCpubr”, cell types were manually assigned based on external marker gene sets, and validation was performed with “SingleR” and “celldex”. Cells were further grouped according to their origin to compare specific cell types between treatment and control groups. Finally, Seurat objects were converted into “monocle” format, and a cell trajectory model was constructed using significantly DEGs as the ordering basis, with cell state maps, pseudotime plots, cell type distribution plots, and clustering plots generated to elucidate potential developmental pathways and transitional relationships. Throughout data processing, “dplyr” and “magrittr” were used for data manipulation, and “limma” was applied to assist with differential expression analysis.

Cell–cell communication analysis

This analysis was conducted using the “CellChat” package to infer cell–cell communication from single-cell transcriptomic data [29]. Expression matrices and cell type information were first extracted from annotated “Seurat” objects to construct CellChat objects, and the “CellChatDB.human” ligand–receptor database was imported to identify secreted signaling pathways. The expression data were then preprocessed by identifying highly expressed genes and interaction pairs, performing PPI projection, calculating intercellular communication probabilities, and removing cell types containing fewer than 10 cells. Based on these data, communication networks at the signaling pathway level were inferred, and the results were integrated to generate a network ring diagram illustrating the number and strength of global interactions, as well as the communication patterns between specific cell types and others. Independent communication network diagrams for each cell type were further generated, and cell communication bubble plots were produced to visually depict the predominant signaling pathways and interaction strengths among different cell types.

The HdWGCNA analysis

WGCNA was performed on T cell subpopulations in scRNA-seq data using the “hdWGCNA” package. In the “Seurat” object, genes were first filtered according to the “≥5% cell expression” criterion, with cell_type as the grouping variable, and metacells were constructed in Harmony space based on cell_type × Type to enhance signal stability. The metacell expression matrix was then normalized, and an analysis matrix was generated for the target cell population. An appropriate soft power was determined through soft thresholding, followed by construction of the topological overlap matrix (TOM) and generation of a gene hierarchical clustering tree to identify non-gray modules and complete module partitioning. Using the full dataset, module eigengenes (MEs) and eigengene connectivity (kME) were calculated to identify core genes within each module, and module names were reassigned. For phenotype association and visualization, module scores were combined with “Seurat"’s FeaturePlot to depict the spatial enrichment of each module, while module correlation heatmaps, point plots, and violin plots stratified by cell_type were generated to quantitatively assess module–cell type relationships. Module network diagrams and core gene network diagrams were also constructed to analyze the internal connectivity structure of each module. Finally, Module uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was applied to project the core and representative genes of each module into a shared two-dimensional space, providing an intuitive representation of potential functional co-expression modules, key regulatory genes, and their relationships with different T cell subpopulations. This analysis systematically characterizes the modular transcriptional regulatory architecture and potential functional specialization within T cell subpopulations.

Enrichment analysis

This study employed R packages, including “clusterProfiler” [30], to perform multi-level functional annotation and signaling pathway enrichment analyses of genes within each module, encompassing three categories: GO (Gene Ontology), KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes), and DO (Disease Ontology). Module genes were first uniformly converted to Entrez IDs, followed by separate GO annotation (covering the BP, CC, and MF subcategories), KEGG pathway analysis, and DO disease association analysis. Enrichment results with a significance threshold of p < 0.05 were retained. For visualization, bar plots and bubble plots were used to display enrichment results, and pie charts were generated for GO analysis to comprehensively illustrate the number of genes, enrichment levels, and significance of each term, thereby elucidating the potential roles of module genes in biological functions, signaling pathways, and disease associations.

In addition, based on “clusterProfiler” and incorporating GO and KEGG gene set files, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was applied to DEGs between distinct risk groups [31]. Before analysis, low-expression genes and normal samples were removed, and samples were stratified into high- and low-risk groups according to risk scores. The log₂ fold change (logFC) for each gene was then calculated, and ranked GSEA was conducted separately for GO and KEGG gene sets to identify significantly enriched pathways (p < 0.05). Finally, GSEA enrichment curves for the top five pathways in each risk group were plotted to highlight the key regulatory pathways associated with different risk states and their potential biological implications.

Development of a T cell–associated prognostic model

This study employed the R packages “limma” and “pheatmap” to perform a systematic differential analysis of transcriptomic expression data between normal and tumor tissues. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to calculate the logFC and P values for each gene, and multiple hypothesis testing was corrected using the FDR method. Genes meeting the criteria of |logFC| > 0.585 and FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed and were visualized using heat maps and volcano plots to illustrate their expression patterns and significance levels. Univariate Cox regression analysis was employed to identify candidate genes significantly associated with patient prognosis, with the results presented in a forest plot depicting hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Subsequently, the “glmnet” package was utilized to construct a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-Cox regression model, and the most predictive key genes were selected through cross-validation. A risk scoring formula was established, with the model trained on the TCGA cohort and externally validated using the GEO cohort. Finally, samples were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score, and the prognostic predictive performance and generalizability of the model across datasets were systematically evaluated.

Evaluation of model performance and development of a nomogram

This study comprehensively employed multiple R packages, including “survival” and “survminer”, to systematically evaluate the prognostic predictive performance and clinical applicability of the risk scoring model from multiple perspectives. In the TCGA and GEO cohorts, Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed to plot survival curves, and log-rank tests were used to compare overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) between high- and low-risk groups, thereby assessing the model’s stability and generalizability across datasets. Risk score curves, survival status distribution plots, and core gene expression heatmaps were then generated to visually illustrate the survival patterns and molecular expression differences between the risk groups. For clinical feature integration, the risk score and multiple clinical variables were included in univariate and multivariate Cox regression models to calculate HR and p-values for each variable. Forest plots were used to evaluate the independent prognostic significance of the risk score after adjusting for clinical covariates. Subgroup survival analyses were further conducted to explore the model’s predictive utility in different population strata (e.g., age, sex), thus validating its robustness in the context of population heterogeneity.

Additionally, using R packages such as “survival”, “regplot”, and “rms”, a nomogram integrating the risk score with clinical variables was constructed. The nomogram was derived from a Cox proportional hazards model and visualized using the “regplot” function, clearly presenting the contribution of each variable to survival prediction. To assess predictive accuracy, the “cph” function combined with bootstrap resampling (B = 1000) was used to generate multi–time point calibration curves, evaluating the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. The “timeROC” package was also applied to construct time-dependent receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves using the Aalen weighting method, enabling comparison of the predictive performance of the risk score, nomogram, and individual clinical variables at different time points. Finally, the independent prognostic value of the nomogram was validated through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, and HRs with 95% CIs were visualized in forest plots, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the model’s predictive robustness and clinical utility.

Immune landscape profiling and tumor mutation burden (TMB) analysis

This study was conducted based on immune infiltration analysis results obtained from CIBERSORT. Tumor samples with p < 0.05 were first selected, and differences in the abundance of various immune cell types between high- and low-risk groups were compared, with violin plots used to visualize the distribution patterns. The ssGSEA method was then applied to score immune-related functional pathways, and box plots stratified by risk groups were generated to illustrate trends in immune functional characteristics under different risk states. In addition, immune subtype classification results were integrated with risk levels, and chi-square tests were performed to assess the distribution differences of each subtype between the distinct risk groups, with proportional composition and statistical significance displayed in graphical form. Spearman correlation analysis was further performed to calculate correlation coefficients between the expression levels of key genes and the degree of immune cell infiltration, and heat maps were generated to depict the potential associations.

In the prognostic analysis, the combined effect of tumor mutational burden (TMB) and risk score on survival prediction was evaluated. TMB and risk score data were integrated, TMB values were log₂-transformed, and violin plots were generated by risk group to display their distributions. Samples were subsequently classified into high-mutation (H-TMB) and low-mutation (L-TMB) groups, and joint variables were constructed based on risk stratification. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were then used to compare survival differences among the groups. Furthermore, RNA stemness scores (RNAss) were analyzed in conjunction with risk scores. Correlations were calculated using the Spearman method, and results were visualized with scatter plots and marginal density plots to highlight the potential relevance of tumor stemness in risk stratification and prognostic assessment.

Drug sensitivity analysis

This study utilized the “oncoPredict” package [32] to analyze the relationship between gene expression and drug sensitivity. Gene expression data were processed by removing normal samples, standardizing the data, and screening for low-variability genes. Expression and drug response data from the GDSC2 database were then loaded, and the calcPhenotype function was used to calculate drug sensitivity, followed by batch effect correction and removal of low-expression genes. Subsequently, the “ggplot2”, and “ggpubr” packages were employed to process risk grouping and drug sensitivity data. The Wilcoxon test was applied to assess differences in drug sensitivity between distinct risk groups, and box plots were generated for visual analysis.

Protein interaction network, drug modulation network, and RBP network of model genes

The GeneMANIA website (http://genemania.org) [33] was used to construct PPI networks based on multiple types of biological information. In this study, this platform was applied to construct and analyze the protein interaction network of the model genes [34]. Drug–gene relationship files were then downloaded from the DSigDB website (https://dsigdb.tanlab.org/), and drug enrichment analysis was performed. The required R packages, including “clusterProfiler”, “org.Hs.eg.db”, “enrichplot”, “tidytable”, and “ggplot2”, were loaded, and thresholds for p-values and adjusted p-values were set. The drug–gene relationship file and the intersection feature gene file were subsequently read, and a gene list was extracted for enrichment analysis. Data analysis was conducted using the enricher function, and significantly enriched results were identified. The enrichment results were then visualized, and the drug–gene network relationship data were saved as a text file, along with a network node attribute file containing drug and gene classification information. Finally, the drug regulation network between the drugs and the three model genes was generated using Cytoscape software (https://cytoscape.org/) [35]. In addition, RBP files for the three model genes were downloaded from the EnCORI website (https://rnasysu.com/encori/), and Cytoscape software was employed to construct a regulatory network diagram illustrating the interactions between the model genes and RBPs.

Molecular Docking analysis

To evaluate the drug sensitivity of potentially targetable model genes, molecular docking analyses were performed between the model genes and candidate drugs. Association data were first retrieved from the DSigDB database, and drug enrichment analyses were executed to assess enrichment levels and statistical significance. Subsequently, drug structure files were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and corresponding protein structure files were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). Molecular docking was then carried out using the CB-DOCK2 platform (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/index.php) to examine binding patterns and affinities between the candidate drugs and model genes. Binding affinity, expressed in kcal/mol, was used to indicate binding strength, with lower values representing more stable interactions.

The interactions among CD69, IL16, CD74, CXCR4, and CD44 were predicted using the Gramm-X protein docking network server (http://vakser.bioinformatics.ku.edu/resources/gramm/grammx). PyMOL was employed to detect and visualize the hydrogen bonds formed. Additionally, the correlations between CD69, IL16, CD74, CXCR4, and CD44 were analyzed through the TIMER2 database (http://timer.cistrome.org/).

Cell culture and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

The cell lines used in this study-H1299, A549, PC9, HCC827, and Beas-2B-were obtained from Wuhan Proser Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Cells were rapidly thawed in a 37 °C water bath and seeded into RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ to preserve the logarithmic growth phase. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized by reverse transcription with the PrimeScript RT kit. qRT-PCR was performed using the SYBR Green method, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. Primer sequence information is presented in Table S1.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in 1× EDTA solution at 98 °C for 10 min, followed by blocking endogenous peroxidase activity with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide solution. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies. After washing with PBS, biotin-labeled IgG polymer was applied and incubated at room temperature for 10 min, followed by incubation with streptavidin-HRP for an additional 10 min. Color development was carried out using DAB reagent, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Staining intensity was graded as follows: Grade 0 (no staining), Grade 1+ (weak), Grade 2+ (moderate), and Grade 3+ (strong). The staining range was determined based on the percentage of positive cells: 0 (0%), 1 (1%-25%), 2 (26%-50%), 3 (51%-75%), and 4 (76%-100%). The H score was calculated as the product of the intensity score and the range score. Antibodies used and their respective dilutions: CD69 (1:400; ImmunoWay, YM0131), IL16 (1:200; ImmunoWay, YT5175).

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF)

Tissue samples were baked at 63 °C for 1 h to remove paraffin and release antigens, followed by paraffin removal using an automated staining machine (LEICA ST5020, LEICA). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by treating samples with hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. After blocking, sections were incubated for 1 h with primary antibodies: CD69 (1:100; ImmunoWay, YM0131) and IL16 (1:100; ImmunoWay, YT5175). After washing with TBST buffer, sections were incubated with secondary antibody (SM802, DAKO) for 10 min, followed by incubation with diluted opalescent dye (Opal 7 Color Manual IHC Kit, NEL801001KT, PerkinElmer) at room temperature for 10 min. Microwave treatment was applied to remove antibody complexes, and the staining steps were repeated until all markers were visualized. Finally, sections were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min and mounted. Multispectral scanning was performed using the Tissue-FAXS system, and the data were imported into Strata-Quest analysis software (Version 7.1.129, TissueGnostics GmbH, Vienna, Austria). Single-channel fluorescence signals were extracted through spectral segmentation, with the DAPI channel used for nuclear identification. Protein signals were positioned based on radius settings according to staining patterns, and the number of positive cells was quantified.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis, processing, and visualization were performed using R software (version 4.5.1) and GraphPad Prism 9. Clinical and molecular characteristics between subtypes were compared using chi-square tests. Multiple group comparisons were performed using ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni and Dunnett multiple comparison tests. T-tests were used for two-group comparisons, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of cell subtypes and their differentiation trajectories in LUAD single-cell data

The LUAD single-cell sequencing dataset GSE277742 was obtained from the GEO database. This dataset comprised two patient samples with pathologically confirmed lymph node metastasis (GSM8528678, GSM8528679) and two control thoracic lymph node samples without evidence of metastasis (GSM8528672, GSM8528673) (the dataset does not provide more detailed N-staging information). In the initial stage of data processing, rigorous quality control was performed to evaluate the distribution of gene detection counts, total transcript counts, and mitochondrial gene proportions across all samples (Figure S1A). Correlation analyses were subsequently conducted to examine the relationships among these metrics, including the association between gene counts and transcript counts, as well as the relationship between mitochondrial gene proportions and transcript counts (Figure S1B). PCA revealed that the samples displayed a distinct distribution pattern along the principal component axes, indicating differences in gene expression profiles and clustering structures among cell populations (Figure S1C). Variable gene analysis identified 1,500 highly variable genes (Figure S1D), with the top 10 ranked by variability listed in the right panel. Feature genes corresponding to PC1 through PC4 are shown in Figure S1E, and their expression patterns across samples are presented in the heatmap in Figure S1F. Finally, the statistical significance of the top 20 principal components was evaluated using p-value analysis (Figure S1G).

Through UMAP dimensionality reduction analysis, 21 distinct cell subpopulations were identified (Fig. 2A). Cell type annotation revealed that these subpopulations comprised multiple types, including neutrophils, T cells, epithelial cells, B cells, and NK cells. A bubble plot presents the marker genes used for cell annotation (Fig. 2B). UMAP visualization further illustrated the distribution patterns of various cell types between the tumor and control groups (Fig. 2C). Cluster heatmaps displayed the characteristic gene expression profiles of each subpopulation, with yellow regions indicating high expression levels (Fig. 2D). Based on these results, pseudo-time analysis was performed to characterize the differentiation trajectories of different cells and subpopulations (Fig. 2E, F). These trajectories were classified into three distinct states, reflecting the differentiation processes at various stages (Fig. 2G). The differentiation timeline used color intensity to indicate the progression of differentiation, with dark blue representing earlier stages, suggesting a gradual transition of cells from dark blue regions to lighter regions (Fig. 2H).

Cell-cell communication analysis

Intercellular communication analysis was subsequently performed. In the interaction network diagram, each node represents a cell type, with node size indicating the relative abundance of that cell type, and lines connecting nodes denoting the presence of interactions, where increased line thickness reflects a greater number of interactions (Fig. 3A). In the interaction strength network diagram, line thickness corresponds to the strength of interactions between cell types (Fig. 3A). Independent communication networks for each cell type revealed significant interactions between T cells and both B cells and NK cells, while epithelial cells also exhibited prominent connections with T cells, B cells, and NK cells; in contrast, no interactions were detected between neutrophils and other cell types (Fig. 3B). Heatmaps of ligand–receptor interaction analysis further illustrated the interaction patterns of various ligand–receptor pairs among different cell types, with the MIF–(CD74 + CXCR4) and MIF–(CD74 + CD44) pathways showing high communication probabilities across multiple cell types (Fig. 3C).

The HdWGCNA analysis of T cell–associated modules

The hdWGCNA analysis was subsequently performed to identify T cell–associated modules and to screen core genes for further investigation. Based on the results of goodness-of-fit and gene connectivity analyses, the optimal network construction was achieved at a power value of 9 (Fig. 4A). Cluster analysis of the genes clearly delineated their respective modules (Fig. 4B). T cells were categorized into six modules, with the top 50 core genes for each module presented (Fig. 4C). Module correlation analysis indicated regulatory relationships among the modules, with purple indicating positive correlations and green indicating negative correlations (Fig. 4D). Integration with the UMAP cell clustering plot showed that genes in modules 1, 3, and 5 were predominantly highly expressed in T cells (Fig. 4E). Module–cell type correlation analysis further confirmed this finding, with these three modules exhibiting the highest expression levels in T cells. Consequently, the genes from these modules were selected for subsequent construction of a T cell–related prognostic model (Fig. 4F, Table S2). In addition, a network diagram of the core genes within these three modules was generated (Figure S2A), while Figure S2B displays the gene network diagrams of all T cell–related modules, with three core genes listed for each. Figure S2C depicts the spatial distribution of these genes in the UMAP plot, with points of the same color indicating membership within the same module.

Univariate Cox regression analysis identified seven genes significantly associated with prognosis (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5E). Association between risk score and prognosis in LUAD patients

The distribution of risk scores among cancer patients and their association with survival status were first presented (Fig. 6A). As risk scores increased, patients were classified into high-risk (orange) and low-risk (blue) groups; those in the high-risk group generally exhibited shorter survival times, with blue points indicating survival and orange points indicating death, demonstrating a strong association between risk score and prognosis. Heatmaps further illustrated the differential expression of model-related genes between the two risk groups. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that survival rates in the high-risk group were significantly lower than those in the low-risk group in both the TCGA (p = 0.001) and GEO (p = 0.012) datasets (Fig. 6B). Analysis across different age groups revealed consistently poorer survival outcomes for high-risk patients in all age categories (Fig. 6C). Further examination of the effects of gender and clinical stage indicated that, regardless of these variables, high-risk patients maintained significantly lower survival rates compared with low-risk patients (Fig. 6D, E). The area under the ROC curve (AUC) values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival predictions were 0.674, 0.619, and 0.626, respectively (Fig. 6F). Finally, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses demonstrated that the risk score serves as an independent prognostic factor influencing patient survival (Fig. 6G).

Establishment and validation of the nomogram

A nomogram incorporating multiple clinical variables and the risk score was generated to predict patients’ survival probability. The nomogram depicts the relative contributions of gender, age, clinical stage, and risk score to the total score (Fig. 7A). Calibration curves were employed to evaluate the agreement between predicted OS and observed OS, with different colors representing 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates (Fig. 7B). ROC curve analysis was applied to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the model, with the nomogram achieving the highest AUC value (AUC = 0.668), indicating superior performance compared with other individual variables (Fig. 7C). Furthermore, univariate Cox regression analysis confirmed that the nomogram provides independent prognostic value (Fig. 7D).

Enrichment analysis, immune correlation analysis, and drug sensitivity analysis of the prognostic model

Enrichment and immune cell infiltration analyses were subsequently performed. GSEA demonstrated that genes in the high-risk group were predominantly enriched in functions such as cytosolic ribosome, ribosomal subunit, cytosolic large ribosomal subunit, large ribosomal subunit, and structural constituent of ribosome, whereas genes in the low-risk group were chiefly enriched in functions including immunoglobulin complex, immunoglobulin production, T cell receptor complex, plasma membrane signaling receptor complex, and antigen binding (Fig. 8A). At the pathway level, the high-risk group showed significant enrichment in cell cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, ribosome, spliceosome, and steroid hormone biosynthesis pathways, while the low-risk group was primarily enriched in allograft rejection, cell adhesion molecules (CAMs), intestinal immune network for IgA production, hematopoietic cell lineage, and primary immunodeficiency pathways (Fig. 8B). Immune cell infiltration analysis indicated higher levels of activated dendritic cells, and neutrophils in the high-risk group, whereas the low-risk group was dominated by M1 macrophages, CD8 T cells, memory B cells, resting memory CD4 T cells, resting dendritic cells, resting mast cells, and activated mast cells (Fig. 8C). Immune function activity analysis demonstrated that overall immune function levels were higher in the low-risk group (Fig. 8D). Correlation analysis between risk scores and immune cell types showed positive correlations with activated dendritic cells, neutrophils, and naive B cells, and negative correlations with CD8 T cells, resting memory CD4 T cells, M1 macrophages, resting mast cells, resting dendritic cells, and memory B cells (Fig. 8E). Immune subtype analysis classified LUAD patients into five subtypes: C1, C2, C3, C4, and C6 (Fig. 8F). The TMB was significantly higher in the high-risk group compared with the low-risk group (Fig. 8G), and patients with high TMB exhibited better OS (p = 0.024). Combined analysis of TMB and risk scores revealed that patients with high TMB and low risk had the most favorable survival outcomes (Fig. 8H). Finally, risk scores were significantly positively correlated with tumor stemness score (R = 0.5, p < 2.2e-16) (Fig. 8I).

Subsequently, a drug sensitivity analysis was performed. The IC50 values for axitinib, ribociclib, tozasertib, and mitoxantrone were significantly lower in the low-risk group than in the high-risk group, suggesting greater sensitivity to these agents. In contrast, the high-risk group demonstrated lower IC50 values for gefitinib, 5-fluorouracil, erlotinib, crizotinib, acetalax, ulixertinib, savolitinib, and lapatinib, indicating increased sensitivity to these drugs (Figure S3).

Functional and regulatory network analysis of prognostic model hub genes

Further analysis was performed on the three genes constituting the prognostic model. The GeneMANIA protein interaction network revealed that CD69, GOLGA8A, and IL16 interact with multiple function-related genes, with different colored lines denoting distinct functional categories, including voltage-gated channel activity, ion channel complexes, and transmembrane transport protein complexes (Fig. 9A). A drug regulation network was subsequently constructed based on these three model genes, in which orange nodes represent model genes, green nodes represent associated drugs, and connecting lines indicate potential gene–drug associations (Fig. 9B). In addition, an RBP regulation network was established, with blue nodes denoting RBPs and orange nodes representing model genes, illustrating potential regulatory interactions between these genes mediated by RBPs (Fig. 9C).

Molecular Docking analysis between model genes and related drugs

Figure S4 presents the results of enrichment analysis for multiple drugs associated with the model genes. Building on these findings, molecular docking experiments were conducted to assess the binding potential of the two model genes (IL16 and CD69) with the drugs most significantly associated in the enrichment analysis. The results showed that IL16 formed stable complexes with dimaprit (Fig. 10A) and clobenpropit (Fig. 10C), whereas CD69 bound to alfuzosin (Fig. 10B), dicloxacillin (Fig. 10D), cinchonine (Fig. 10E), and dacarbazine (Fig. 10F). The figures depict both the binding conformations and the key interaction sites of each complex, along with Vina scores used to quantify binding affinity, where lower scores indicate stronger interactions between the compound and the protein. Specifically, the docking scores for IL16 with dimaprit and clobenpropit were − 57.3 and − 88.1, respectively, while those for CD69 with alfuzosin, dicloxacillin, cinchonine, and dacarbazine were − 7.4, − 7.8, − 8.0, and − 5.9, respectively (Fig. 10A-F). These findings indicate that the identified key genes possess strong binding affinities with their corresponding drugs, suggesting potential therapeutic relevance.

The results showed significant downregulation of CD69 and IL16 in LUAD cell lines, while GOLGA8A was consistently upregulated (Fig. 11C).

Prognosis and mechanistic exploration of model genes

The prognostic and molecular mechanisms of the model genes were further explored. Kaplan-Meier curve analysis revealed that high expression levels of CD69 (p = 0.001), GOLGA8A (p = 0.002), and IL16 (p = 0.001) were significantly associated with improved prognosis in LUAD patients (Figure S5A). Previous cell communication analysis indicated that the MIF-(CD74 + CXCR4) and MIF-(CD74 + CD44) pathways exhibited high communication probabilities across different cell types. Based on this, the interrelationships between the model genes and these pathways were further investigated. Specifically, the Timer2 database showed significant positive correlations between CD69 and IL16 with CD74, CXCR4, and CD44 (Figure S5B, C), while GOLGA8A showed no significant correlations with these genes. Therefore, we employed GRAMM for protein-protein docking to investigate the interactions between CD69 and IL16 with CD74, CXCR4, and CD44. Molecular docking results indicated that both CD69 and IL16 bind to CD74, CXCR4, and CD44, forming key hydrogen bonds (Figure S5D). Typically, lower binding free energies suggest more stable systems and stronger interactions. The results demonstrated that the binding energies of CD69 and IL16 with CD74, CXCR4, and CD44 were relatively low, indicating stable and strong interactions.

Discussion

LUAD, the predominant subtype of NSCLC, carries a poor prognosis, and its metastasis is strongly linked to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [36–38]. Immunotherapy has emerged as a key treatment strategy, with T cells playing a central role in therapeutic efficacy [39–41]. Draining LNs are the most common and often earliest sites of metastasis and are important prognostic factors [42, 43]. Accordingly, this study aims to systematically characterize the immune microenvironment of metastatic LNs in LUAD, identify new T cell–related immunotherapy targets, and provide evidence to guide the optimization of systemic therapy for LUAD.

This study integrated single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data to systematically characterize cellular heterogeneity and functional dynamics in LUAD and their associations with prognosis. Based on scRNA-seq of metastatic intrathoracic lymph node samples from GSE277742, multiple cell subpopulations-including neutrophils, T cells, epithelial cells, B cells, and NK cells-were identified, and multiple differentiation trajectories and state transitions were reconstructed by pseudotime analysis. Cell–cell communication analysis showed significant interactions between T cells and B/NK cells, and close connections between epithelial cells and multiple immune cell types. Among these, the MIF–(CD74 + CXCR4) and MIF–(CD74 + CD44) axes exhibited higher communication probabilities across cell types, suggesting potential hubs of immune regulation in LUAD. Using hdWGCNA, T-cell–related modules were identified, and differential expression together with Cox/LASSO regression was utilized to establish a T-cell–related prognostic model consisting of CD69, GOLGA8A, and IL16. This model showed a significant association with OS in the TCGA and GEO cohorts, demonstrated stable discrimination and calibration, and exhibited systematic associations with immune infiltration patterns and TMB. GSEA/KEGG analyses revealed enrichment of ribosomal, bioenergetic metabolism, and splicing pathways in the high-risk group, whereas the low-risk group was enriched for immune effector and antigen-recognition pathways; the two groups also exhibited differential potential sensitivity to multiple targeted and chemotherapeutic agents. PPI, drug-regulatory, and RBP-regulatory networks constructed around the model genes enhanced biological interpretability, and molecular docking further supported strong binding affinities between IL16 and CD69 and multiple candidate compounds. Subsequently, clinical variables and risk scores were integrated to establish a nomogram for quantitative survival prediction, and the expression of the model genes in LUAD was experimentally validated. These results provide actionable evidence for precision stratification and immunotherapy strategies based on T-cell characteristics.

As a key gene in the prognostic model, CD69 underscores its potential role in immune responses within the tumor microenvironment. CD69 is an early activation marker of T cells [44–46] and is involved in T-cell proliferation and differentiation [47, 48]. Its expression is commonly associated with antigen-driven activation, suggesting a key role in regulating immune responses against tumor cells [49, 50]. In LUAD, elevated CD69 levels may indicate enhanced immune activity and may be associated with better prognosis. GOLGA8A, another key gene identified in this study, has been comparatively less studied in cancer. The gene helps maintain Golgi structure and is associated with protein sorting and transmembrane transport [51, 52]. In LUAD, it may regulate tumor-cell metabolism and proliferation by influencing Golgi function and glycosylation patterns. Its identification as a prognostic marker suggests potential therapeutic value and warrants further mechanistic investigation. IL16 is an immunoregulatory cytokine that recruits CD4⁺ T cells to inflammatory sites and is associated with various immune-mediated diseases [53, 54]. In LUAD, IL16 may promote the recruitment and activation of T cells within the tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity. Its expression correlation with patient prognosis observed here also suggests potential value as a clinical outcome predictor. In summary, CD69, GOLGA8A, and IL16, as core components of the prognostic model, delineate a T-cell-centered immune axis in LUAD, deepening understanding of tumor–immune interactions and providing evidence and direction for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies and patient stratification.

Immune cell infiltration analysis demonstrated significant differences in immune composition between the different risk groups, differences that may profoundly influence the tumor microenvironment and patient prognosis. The low-risk group was enriched for M1 macrophages, memory B cells, and CD8 T cells—cell types typically associated with effective antitumor immunity. M1 macrophages exhibit pronounced pro-inflammatory and antigen-presenting functions that enhance T-cell activation [55–57], and the concurrent enrichment of memory B cells and CD8 T cells indicates a more active adaptive immune response [58, 59], consistent with the superior survival observed in this cohort. Conversely, in the high-risk group, the risk score correlated negatively with CD8 T cells and resting memory CD4 T cells. Because CD8 T cells are essential for direct tumor-cell killing, reduced infiltration may reflect an inhibitory microenvironment and attenuated antitumor activity [60–62]. These findings underscore the need to further delineate the LUAD immune landscape to inform strategies for high-risk patients aimed at augmenting T-cell activity and restoring immune function, while also providing a foundation for studies of immune-escape mechanisms and therapeutic target identification.

Drug sensitivity analysis revealed significant differences in IC50 values between the low- and high-risk groups, providing a basis for personalized therapy in LUAD. In the low-risk group, axitinib—a selective VEGFR inhibitor—showed lower IC50 values, indicating enhanced sensitivity; given its ability to inhibit tumor angiogenesis, this finding may further improve outcomes in patients with favorable prognoses [63–65]. By contrast, the high-risk group exhibited lower IC50 values for the EGFR inhibitors gefitinib and erlotinib, suggesting increased sensitivity to targeted therapy, which may reflect subgroup-specific molecular characteristics and supports the clinical use of EGFR-targeted regimens [66–68]. Molecular docking further indicated strong binding affinities between the model genes and candidate drugs: IL16 with dimaprit and clobenpropit, and CD69 with alfuzosin and dicloxacillin, all yielding favorable Vina scores, suggesting potential value as intervention targets. Overall, these results delineate risk group–specific drug sensitivity profiles and underscore the importance of integrating molecular mechanisms with risk stratification to guide more effective, individualized treatment strategies for LUAD.

This study integrates scRNA-seq technology with advanced analytical methods to enhance the robustness and reliability of its conclusions. However, several limitations remain: (1) Reliance on a single GEO dataset may introduce cohort bias, limiting the reproducibility of the results; (2) The prognostic model based on T-cell-related genes showed promising performance, but its clinical utility requires validation in larger, independent cohorts. Predictive accuracy should be improved by incorporating additional molecular biomarkers, expanding sample sizes, and conducting multicenter external validation; (3) Further experimental studies are needed to clarify the roles and mechanisms of key genes in prognosis and the tumor microenvironment. These mechanisms should be validated through co-immunoprecipitation, luciferase reporter assays, and pathway inhibitor experiments to support the model’s functional and clinical applications.

Conclusion

In summary, LUAD single-cell data were systematically analyzed, revealing substantial cellular heterogeneity and differentiation trajectories within the tumor microenvironment. Multiple cell subtypes, including T cells, were identified, and their intercellular communication networks were delineated, underscoring the complexity of immune interactions. Using hdWGCNA, T-cell–associated modules and core genes were identified, and a corresponding risk score was constructed. This score was significantly associated with patient survival and retained prognostic value across multiple clinical strata. A nomogram was subsequently developed to enhance the model’s clinical applicability. Drug-sensitivity analysis and molecular docking further supported the model’s clinical relevance and provided a basis for targeted treatment strategies in LUAD. Overall, these findings deepen the understanding of LUAD biology and emphasize the importance of precision medicine in oncology.
