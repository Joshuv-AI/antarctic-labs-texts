Introduction

Complex coronary interventions require additions to the standard tool kit on the shelf that includes angulated and dual lumen microcatheters. We encountered a patient presenting with non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) with angiographic evidence of distal left main coronary artery (LMCA) bifurcation lesion (Medina 1,1,1) of 95% severity. It was complicated by protruding left anterior descending artery (LAD) stent struts into LMCA with severe in-stent restenosis and highly angulated and diseased left circumflex artery (LCX). We used the pierced-balloon technique when conventional attempts at wiring failed, to successfully navigate the side branch in the absence of a dual lumen microcatheter.

Case report

A 57-year-old gentleman with diabetes presented with chest discomfort and was diagnosed with NSTEMI. In 2020, he had NSTEMI and underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) with stenting to LAD for significant ostial LAD disease at a different centre. He was on aspirin 75 mg once daily and rosuvastatin 20 mg daily. The coronary angiogram done in the present admission showed critical LMCA distal bifurcation disease (Medina classification 1,1,1). He had distal LMCA 95% stenosis, ostial LAD in-stent restenosis of 90% severity with projecting stent struts of LAD into LMCA, and ostial LCX 90% stenosis (Supplementary material online, Videos S1–S4). The patient was having ongoing angina despite intravenous nitroglycerine. His SYNTAX score was calculated to be 22. The opinion of the cardiovascular surgeon was sought, but due to the operating theatre being occupied with ongoing surgeries, this patient could only be scheduled for surgery the next day. Therefore, PCI was advised due to his persistent angina.

Transfemoral 7F arterial access was used. Extra backup (EBU) 7F 3.5 catheter was used to intubate the left coronary artery. The LAD was wired with 0.014″ Cougar wire. The projecting struts of the previously deployed stent into the LMCA posed a challenge to wire the LCX. Despite multiple attempts and changes of wires, including Cougar, Choice Floppy, Whisper, and Choice PT wires with various manually shaped tips and primary and secondary curves, the LCX could not be successfully wired (Supplementary material online, Video S5). Additional efforts using the Finecross microcatheter in conjunction with the Choice PT wire was also unsuccessful. Reverse loop wiring also was attempted unsuccessfully. Side branch occlusion technique and low-pressure dilatation for plaque modification was not considered in this case as it would risk abrupt vessel closure without a backup wiring strategy. The patient continued to have angina. We did not have a double lumen microcatheter on the shelf. Therefore, we resorted to creating an indigenous balloon catheter system that would function like a dual lumen microcatheter. A 2.75 × 15 mm coronary balloon was selected and inflated to 6 atm. A Vygon radial needle was used to puncture the balloon through and through at an angle, ensuring that the hypotube of the balloon catheter was not compromised. The 0.014″ Whisper wire was threaded from its rear end, having been given a manually shaped primary curve. Air was manually squeezed out from the punctured balloon. The balloon was then threaded onto the main branch LAD wire. The Whisper wire was pushed alongside en bloc until it reached the bifurcation. We were able to negotiate the Whisper wire with relative ease into the LCX (Figure 1, Supplementary material online, Video S6). The LAD and LCX were predilated with 2 × 10 mm balloon and thereafter with 2.75 × 10 mm semi-compliant balloon. Pre-stent kissing balloon (KB) inflation was done with 2.75 × 10 mm non-compliant (NC) balloons at 12 atm. We decided for an upfront two-stent strategy and considered mini-culotte as our choice of bifurcation technique as it would leave no crushed stent segments in the LMCA. This technique was chosen as there were already protruding stent struts of LAD into LMCA. A 3.5 × 15 mm drug-eluting stent (DES) was deployed in the LCX; the LAD was rewired; strut dilatation was done sequentially with 2.5 × 10 and 3 × 10 mm NC balloons. A 3.5 × 18 mm everolimus DES was deployed from the LMCA to LAD; the LCX was recrossed through the distal strut; the stent strut was dilated sequentially with 2.5 × 10 mm NC balloons and 3.5 × 10 mm NC balloons. Stents were optimized with 1:1 balloon/artery ratio at 20–22 atm. The final KB inflation was done at 12 atm. Proximal optimization (POT) was done with 4.5 × 8 mm NC balloon at 18 atm (Figure 2, Supplementary material online, Videos S7–S13). with angiogram showing TIMI III flow. Puff test was done to confirm apposition of the LMCA stent. Intracoronary imaging was not conducted due to the patient’s financial constraints. This decision was made to ensure that care remained aligned with the patient’s available resources. The patient was discharged in a stable state 3 days after the procedure. One-month post-procedure, the patient experienced acute abdominal pain, and recurrent vomiting, collapsed, and subsequently died. Hedid not survive to reach the hospital. The cause of death could not be ascertained.

Pierced-balloon technique. Radial Vygon needle is used to puncture through and through an inflated long balloon without damaging the hypotube. The distal end of the side branch wire is threaded through the Vygon needle. The pierced balloon now is threaded on the main branch wire along with the side branch wire. Gentle movements are used to push the system en bloc through the guide catheter into the coronary. The pierced-balloon catheter now serves as a makeshift dual lumen catheter with the wire directed towards the side branch. SB, side branch wire.

Discussion

Pierced-balloon technique can be used to cross the highly angulated side branches or distal struts in bifurcation lesions. This technique can be used as an alternative to a double lumen microcatheter when the latter is unavailable. It is inexpensive, and a regular balloon catheter, albeit of longer length, needs to be used for practical reasons. The distal puncture can be based on the angulation that is necessary to orient the wire to the side branch. The system has to be taken in slowly en bloc, under fluoroscopy, and in sync through the guide catheter as rapid advancement can dislodge the wire from the balloon and the whole procedure may have to be repeated. We would suggest to keep at least 2 cm of the side branch (SB) wire ahead of the main branch balloon so that it does not slip away in the guide catheter during advancement. It has to be noted that the balloon on the main branch serves only to anchor and facilitate the SB wiring. It should never be inflated as it is a punctured balloon. Removal of the balloon is similar to removing any balloon from a coronary artery; however it is essential to maintain control of boththe main branch and the side branch wires, and to allow the system to back- bleed once the balloon is removed.

This technique has been previously reported1 to successfully cross the distal strut of a side branch in a case of bifurcation stenting. This is the only reported case in which this technique has been utilized in a de novo lesion. ‘Side car’ technique2 has been used in chronic total occlusions.

The SYNTAX score, which guides in determining the appropriate treatment approach between PCI and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), does not consider the presence of protruding stent struts from vessels into the main branch. These protruding struts may significantly impact the complexity of the bifurcation disease. The mere numerical assessment of SYNTAX in this case will understate the anatomical complexity of the case. Intracoronary imaging would have helped us optimize our results. The patient died a month later at home. The cause of death could not be ascertained as he died before reaching the hospital. Probable stent thrombosis should also be considered, although the symptoms preceding his death did not point towards the same.

Conclusion

Pierced-balloon technique is an inexpensive bail-out procedural strategy when dual lumen microcatheters are not available to deal with difficult, diseased, and highly angulated side branch. It is important to perform intracoronary imaging in left main coronary interventions.
