Preclinical and benchtop evaluation of a dedicated tool for insertable cardiac monitor explantation
Authors: Bryan Gonzalez, Vanessa Ogueri, Devon Guerrelli, Justin D. Opfermann, Mischa Cho, Charles I. Berul, Ryan P. O’Hara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsertable cardiac monitors (ICMs) are widely used for long-term rhythm surveillance, yet explantation can be challenging because of fibrotic encapsulation, tissue variability, and device migration. Current removal typically relies on general surgical instruments, and no dedicated tool exists for difficult ICM explants.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the performance of a purpose-built cardiac monitor extraction tool (CM-eX) compared with standard removal technique using benchtop and chronic animal models.MethodsThe CM-eX was designed and fabricated. Benchtop testing used a 2-layer synthetic gel model simulating fibrotic encapsulation and subcutaneous tissue. 80 ICM removals were performed using CM-eX, standard instruments, or direct wire pullout. Metrics included removal time and peak insertion and removal forces. Electromyography was recorded as a measure of operator demand. Chronic animal testing was performed in 9 rats implanted bilaterally with ICMs and explanted after a 3-month survival period, with each animal serving as its own control.ResultsIn benchtop models, removal time was similar between the CM-eX and standard instruments (P = .40), whereas the CM-eX required significantly lower removal force than direct pullout (P < .0001). Overall EMG activity was similar between methods. In the chronic animal model, the CM-eX was associated with faster explant in 8 of 9 paired comparisons, with a mean reduction of 28% vs standard instruments. No device slippage or procedural complications were observed.ConclusionIn preclinical testing, the CM-eX demonstrated feasible ICM removal with reduced extraction force in benchtop models and directional improvements in explant time in an animal model. These findings support its potential to facilitate more controlled and consistent ICM explantation.

Key Findings ▪A purpose-built insertable cardiac monitor (ICM) extraction tool (CM-eX) was successfully developed and evaluated in complementary benchtop and chronic animal models.▪In synthetic fibrotic encapsulation models, CM-eX required substantially lower extraction force than direct device pullout without dissection while achieving explant times comparable to standard surgical instruments.▪In a 12-week chronic rat model, CM-eX demonstrated shorter explant times in 8 of 9 paired comparisons, corresponding to a mean numerical reduction of 28% vs standard dissection.▪No device slippage, tissue tearing, or procedural complications were observed during CM-eX-assisted explantation, supporting the feasibility of controlled axial device engagement.▪These preclinical findings support further evaluation of dedicated ICM explant tools for selected challenging scenarios, including chronically implanted, fibrotically encapsulated, or migrated devices.

Introduction

Insertable cardiac monitors (ICMs) are safe and effective medical devices for continuous cardiac rhythm monitoring, with over 2 million devices implanted worldwide and approximately 75,000 new implants per year in the United States alone.1 Their small size, minimally invasive implantation technique, and extended battery life make them valuable diagnostic tools. Their use has expanded across adult and pediatric populations, supported by large registry analyses demonstrating high diagnostic yield and broad clinical utility.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Additional pediatric series have further highlighted the value of ICMs in younger patients and those with congenital heart disease.7, 8, 9 Current guidelines recommend explantation once a diagnosis has been achieved, because of patient preference, or once battery life is depleted,10 which may occur 3–5 years after implant.

However, device explantation can sometimes be challenging. Over time, fibrotic encapsulation, variable tissue thickness, and device migration can obscure the implant and complicate removal.11 These issues may lead to greater tissue manipulation, larger incisions, and prolonged dissection, which may increase procedural complexity and potentially affect patient comfort. Traditional removal is commonly performed using standard scalpels and forceps, which are generally effective but may be less optimized for selected chronic or migrated implants. Device migration can further complicate removal,12 even with preliminary ultrasound guidance, which has only been suggested as an adjunct in particularly difficult cases.13

Given the increasing number of implants and growing proportion of chronically implanted devices, there is a clear unmet need for adjunctive approaches that may facilitate selected challenging ICM explants, including chronically implanted, fibrotically encapsulated, migrated, or repeatedly replaced devices. A technical case report has also described the challenges of ICM removal in fibrotic tissue, further highlighting the need for improved extraction approaches.14 In response, we developed a novel ICM-specific extraction tool intended to engage the implant device securely and reduce the force and tissue manipulation required during explant. This study summarizes comparative testing and preclinical evaluation of an ICM-specific removal tool to standard surgical instruments in both controlled benchtop gel models and in a chronic animal study.

Methods

Study overview

This study evaluated a novel, procedure-specific extraction tool intended to reduce blind dissection and decrease procedural duration during ICM explantation. Tool performance was assessed using 2 complementary models: (1) a benchtop gel analog simulating fibrotic encapsulation and (2) a chronic animal model. An experienced operator performed benchtop and chronic animal explant procedures to minimize inter-operator variability. Quantitative comparisons between the novel extraction tool and standard removal techniques included removal time, applied force, and operator muscle activation.

ICM removal tool design

The ICM-specific extraction tool (CM-eX) was iteratively developed with computer-aided design software (Materialise, NV), guided by benchtop testing and surgeon feedback. Prototypes were fabricated using a Stratasys J5 3-dimensional printer (Stratasys Ltd.) in a rigid plastic, with a design consistent with potential single-use clinical application. The current prototype distal geometry was dimensioned around the Medtronic Reveal LINQ/LINQ II platform as an initial development target. The underlying design concept could be adapted to other ICM platforms through size-specific modifications.

The CM-eX, shown in Figure 1, consists of an elongated, tapered body with a rounded cross-section and smooth geometric transitions to minimize tissue trauma during insertion and manipulation. The distal end contains a chamber with implant-conforming ridges that terminates in a bifurcated tip with opposing gripping surfaces and serrations intended to assist blunt separation of fibrotic adhesions and improve monitor engagement during axial extraction. The proximal end consists of a grip for operator control. These features were intended to enable axial engagement of the device while minimizing lateral shear forces. The CM-eX has a cross-sectional dimension of 5.2 × 9.3 mm at the distal end and 8.3 × 12.0 mm at the proximal end. Benchtop evaluation

Benchtop fibrotic encapsulation model

Synthetic 2-layer gel analogs were developed to simulate tissue adherence and fibrotic encapsulation surrounding chronic ICM implants. Multiple candidate materials (eg, wax) were evaluated, but insufficiently replicated clinical explant conditions. Silicone-based Dragon Skin FX Pro (Smooth-On Inc.) was selected as a suitable material for its balance of resistance and malleability, and its low Shore A hardness (2A).15 This material exhibits a reported 100% tensile modulus of approximately 260 kPa, which lies within the same order of magnitude as published values for human dermal stiffness,11,16 acknowledging the variability associated with anatomical location. To create an underlying more flexible layer, Dragon Skin FX Pro was mixed with Slacker to produce a very soft mixture (Shore A 000–35)17 intended to approximate the deformability of soft tissue beneath the skin. The final configuration, achieved through iterative testing to reproduce clinically realistic resistance during explantation, consisted of a 2-layer construct: (1) a 5 mm superficial layer of Dragon Skin FX Pro (A:B, 1:1) to mimic skin and (2) a 15 mm sublayer of FX Pro (A:B, 1:1) mixed with Slacker at a 2:1 ratio resulting in a Shore A value of 35. The synthetic 2-layer gel simulated a fibrotic encapsulation layer overlying compliant subcutaneous tissue, producing realistic tactile feedback during explantation.

Multiple synthetic 2-layer gel analogs were prepared. A custom 3-dimensional-printed mold (7.0 cm × 10.0 cm × 2.4 cm) was used to ensure consistency across gels. Each gel analog was prepared in reverse order. First, a 5 mm synthetic skin layer was poured into the mold and allowed to partially cure for 15 minutes. 4 ICMs (Medtronic Reveal LINQ or LINQ II) were then manually suspended using brackets with uniform, parallel spacing onto the surface of the skin layer and cured for an additional 5 minutes. The synthetic subcutaneous layer was subsequently poured over the synthetic skin and suspended ICMs and fully cured. Each gel analog was then removed from the mold and inverted for benchtop testing (Figure 2A). Tensile pullout force measurement

As a control, we measured the peak tensile removal force by pulling the ICMs from the synthetic gel analogs without any dissection. This condition represents the most conservative tensile loading scenario in which no dissection or device engagement is achieved. Before suspending the ICMs in the subcutaneous layer, a looped wire was attached to 1 end of each ICM. After the gels cured, they were fixed to a flat surface, and a standard calibrated force gauge was connected to the other end of the wire (Figure 2C). Axial tensile force was gradually applied until the ICM became fully dislodged from the synthetic analog.

CM-eX vs standard dissection

To compare and assess the usability of the CM-eX vs standard dissection, paired ICM removals were performed in the prepared gel analogs. For each synthetic gel, 2 ICMs were removed with the CM-eX and 2 ICMs were removed with standard dissection in randomized order. For each removal, a scalpel was used to create an initial incision (10 mm) at the tip of each ICM. For standard dissection, the scalpel and forceps or hemostat were used to dislodge and remove the ICM. For experimental tests, only the CM-eX was used to remove the ICM following the scalpel incision. To measure the peak insertion and removal force of the CM-eX, the proximal end was designed with a screw modification to securely attach to the force gauge (Figure 2D).

Electromyography data

Surface electromyography (EMG) was used to quantify operator ergonomic demand during ICM removal between standard ICM dissection and CM-eX ICM removal in the synthetic gel analogs. EMG signals from a single, trained operator were measured with a Noraxon Ultium wireless EMG system (Noraxon USA Inc.).

Bipolar surface electrodes were placed over the dominant flexor carpi ulnaris and abductor pollicis longus (wrist) following standard placement guidelines. EMG data were continuously collected during ICM removal and segmented into 2 procedural phases: instrument insertion and device extraction. For each phase, quantified muscle signal values were integrated over the duration of the event and normalized to task duration as comparisons were within-subject and within-task. The resultant values were used to compare muscle activation demands between removal methods.

Chronic animal study

Study setup

Nine 6-month-old Sprague–Dawley rats, weighing an average of 226.8 ± 18.0 g, were implanted with ICMs subcutaneously at symmetric bilateral dorsal thoracic sites and allowed to recover for 12 weeks to permit fibrotic encapsulation. Standard implantation techniques, including the use of the manufacturer’s ICM insertion tool and postoperative care, were used to establish chronic implants. The rat model was selected as a practical chronic fibrosis screening model rather than a direct anatomical surrogate for human subcutaneous tissue. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Children’s National Hospital and followed the National Institutes of Health’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

ICM explant testing

12 weeks after implantation, animals were euthanized immediately before performing bilateral ICM explantation. The order of explant method was randomized using a simple randomization scheme (coin flip), with 1 side explanted using standard instruments (scalpel, hemostat, and forceps) and the contralateral side explanted using the CM-eX (following an initial incision with a scalpel). Statistical analysis

Paired comparisons between surgical instruments and the CM-eX were analyzed using paired t-tests, as each animal served as its own control. Independent comparisons (tool vs tensile pullout, animal vs benchtop model) used Welch’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate based on data distribution. Effect estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to indicate the precision of the estimates.

Paired comparisons were analyzed using 2-tailed paired t tests as the primary analysis. Because the a priori engineering hypothesis was that the CM-eX would shorten explant time rather than prolong it, a prespecified 1-tailed paired t test was also performed for the animal-model removal-time endpoint as a supportive directional analysis. Ratio-based metrics (percent time reduction) were log-transformed and analyzed using 1-sample t tests against a null ratio of 1. Statistical significance was defined as a P value of <.05.

Results

Synthetic encapsulation model

A total of 20 synthetic 2-layer gel constructs were prepared, yielding 80 individual ICM trials. Of these, 30 removals were performed using standard dissection with forceps, 34 removals using the CM-eX, and 16 removals using direct wire pullout without dissection. The removal of a given ICM did not affect the integrity or subsequent explant testing of adjacent devices within the same mold.

Removal time

Mean removal time in the synthetic encapsulation model was similar between techniques, with no statistically significant difference observed (CM-eX: 32.7 ± 9.2 seconds vs forceps: 29.5 ± 8.5 seconds; P = .40). These results indicate similar removal times between the CM-eX and standard instruments under controlled benchtop conditions.

Removal and insertion forces

Peak force measurements demonstrated distinct mechanical differences between explant approaches. Mean peak removal force using the CM-eX was significantly lower than using direct wire pullout without dissection (10.9 N vs 32.4 N; P < .0001, Figure 4), indicating reduced traction force when the ICM was engaged and extracted using the CM-eX. Post hoc power analysis for this comparison demonstrated statistical power exceeding 80%. Chronic rat model

All 9 rats developed visible and tactile fibrotic encapsulation around the implanted ICMs after 12 weeks, yielding 9 paired model comparisons. At the time of euthanasia, animals weighed an average of 351 ± 64 g, representing an average weight increase of 54.4% ± 21.9% over the 12-week survival period. Bilateral explantation was successfully completed in all animals without procedural complications.

Removal time

ICM removal using the CM-eX resulted in faster explant times than standard dissection in 8 of 9 animals. Mean ICM removal time was 96.2 ± 56.3 seconds with the CM-eX compared with 126.7 ± 56.9 seconds using forceps (Figure 5), corresponding to a mean absolute reduction of 30.4 seconds (28.2%). This difference did not reach statistical significance in the primary 2-tailed paired analysis (P = .076). CM-eX insertion force

Mean CM-eX insertion force in the animal model was 26.6 N (range, 10.9–67.2 N). No device slippage, tissue tearing, or procedural failures were observed during CM-eX-assisted explantation. CM-eX removal force, following retrieval of the ICM, was negligible (<2 N) and could not be reliably reproduced due to limitations of the experimental setup.

EMG analysis

Overall operator muscle activation, defined as time-normalized integrated EMG summed across the flexor carpi ulnaris and abductor pollicis longus, was similar between techniques (CM-eX:forceps geometric mean ratio, 1.13; 95% CI, 0.87–1.34). This analysis was performed on a set of 16 trials (8 forceps, 8 CM-eX), drawn from the larger benchtop dataset mentioned above. Muscle-specific analysis demonstrated modestly higher activation of the flexor carpi ulnaris during CM-eX use (1.21; 95% CI, 1.08–1.37), whereas activation of the abductor pollicis was variable and showed no consistent directional difference relative to forceps (1.14; 95% CI, 0.86–1.39).

Discussion

ICMs are widely used for long-term rhythm surveillance,1 yet device explantation remains dependent on general surgical instruments rather than procedure-specific technology. Reports of non-catheter laboratory implantable loop recorder services suggest that components of ICM implantation and management may be performed safely outside traditional electrophysiology laboratory settings, highlighting the potential value of streamlined, procedure-specific explant tools.18 As implantation duration increases, fibrotic encapsulation and tissue overgrowth can obscure the device and complicate removal.10, 11, 12 Previous reports have described adjunctive techniques for difficult explantation, including imaging guidance or alternative incision strategies, but potentially add burden to smaller patients or patients who require multiple ICM implantations.13,14 The present study evaluated a purpose-built extraction tool designed to engage the ICM along its longitudinal axis and facilitate controlled axial removal.

In the synthetic encapsulation model, explant times were similar between methods; however, the CM-eX required substantially less peak traction force than direct pullout without dissection. This mechanical advantage likely reflects improved axial capture of the device and reduced reliance on shear-based disruption of surrounding material. Insertion forces were higher in gels than in animal tissue, supporting the gel construct as a conservative testing platform. Importantly, EMG analysis demonstrated no increase in operator muscle activation with the CM-eX. Similar EMG activity suggests that any procedural advantages of the CM-eX were not associated with substantially increased operator muscular demand.

In a chronic rat model, the CM-eX reduced explant time in 8 of 9 paired comparisons, with a mean reduction of 28% relative to standard dissection. Although the primary 2-tailed analysis did not meet conventional significance thresholds, the prespecified 1-tailed directional analysis supported the hypothesized reduction in explant time. These findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of procedural efficiency requiring confirmation in larger studies. No device slippage, tissue tearing, or procedural failures were observed. These findings suggest that device-specific engagement may improve efficiency in encapsulated implants without observed slippage or procedural failure in this study.12

As ICM battery longevity continues to increase, devices may remain implanted for substantially longer periods, potentially resulting in more extensive fibrotic encapsulation and greater explant complexity. Although the present study evaluated devices after 12 weeks in a chronic animal model, the mechanical advantages of secure axial engagement may become increasingly relevant with longer implant durations. Future studies should evaluate CM-eX performance in extended-duration implant models that better reflect contemporary device lifespans.

The rat model provided a chronic encapsulation model with greater biological realism than benchtop testing, while remaining anatomically limited relative to humans. Devices were implanted for 12 weeks—a duration sufficient to produce consistent fibrotic encapsulation and tactile resistance to removal. Although shorter than typical human implant durations, the model reproduced key mechanical features of clinical explantation, including device obscuration and fibrotic adherence.11 Importantly, the CM-eX demonstrated benefit without artificially exaggerating tissue rigidity, suggesting that advantages may persist, or potentially increase, with longer dwell times and more advanced fibrosis.

The CM-eX was intentionally designed with a simple geometry compatible with low-cost manufacturing approaches and is envisioned as an adjunct for selected challenging explants rather than a replacement for standard instruments in all cases. Potential value may derive from reducing procedural complexity, limiting tissue manipulation, or avoiding adjunctive imaging and prolonged dissection in difficult cases.

Several limitations in this study warrant consideration. The animal study was conducted with 2 experienced operators, which reduces procedural variability but may limit generalizability. Sample size was modest, particularly in the chronic animal model. The rat implantation duration likely underestimates the extent of fibrosis encountered after several years of clinical implantation, and anatomical differences limit direct clinical extrapolation. The synthetic gel model provided a reproducible testing environment but did not fully capture the heterogeneity of human tissue characteristics, including variable implant depth, device migration, fibrosis severity, and calcification. Additionally, EMG analysis was limited to 2 forearm muscle groups and a subset of benchtop trials. Larger-scale studies, longer implant durations, histologic characterization of fibrosis, and testing in larger animal models more representative of human subcutaneous anatomy will be important next steps. The current prototype was rigid and designed for the device profiles of a single platform; future versions may include longer shafts, flexible segments, or alternate tip geometries. The underlying design concept targeting secure axial engagement through a device-conforming chamber and bifurcated tip is adaptable to any ICM geometry through modifications to distal dimensions and engagement features.

Overall, these findings support the feasibility and mechanical rationale of a dedicated ICM extraction tool. By enabling controlled axial engagement and reducing reliance on blind dissection, the CM-eX demonstrated directional procedural advantages and a plausible mechanical rationale that warrant further refinement, extended-duration implantation studies, and clinical evaluation to determine its role in routine clinical practice. Potential clinical use cases may include chronically implanted, fibrotically encapsulated, migrated, or repeatedly replaced monitors in ambulatory procedural settings.

Conclusion

The purpose-built ICM removal tool, the CM-eX, demonstrated feasibility with comparable benchtop performance and directional improvements in the chronic animal model. The CM-eX also required substantially less force than direct pullout in benchtop models and achieved a 28% reduction in removal time in a chronic animal model. The synthetic gel platform served as a conservative validation model, further supporting tool robustness. Operator muscle activation was similar between techniques when assessed using EMG.

These findings justify continued refinement and larger-scale testing, with the potential to support more efficient or technically straightforward ICM removals in clinical practice for challenging ICM explants. Future work will involve larger-animal testing, deeper implant models, broader device compatibility, and early human feasibility evaluation.
