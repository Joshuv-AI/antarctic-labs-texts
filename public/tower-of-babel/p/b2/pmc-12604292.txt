Inhibition of antiapoptotic BCL-2 proteins with Lipo-NPs induces fibroblast apoptosis, alleviating pulmonary fibrosis in VILI mice
Authors: Qian Yu, Rui Tang, Wen Tang, Shulei Fan, Daoxin Wang, Junnan Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMechanical ventilation in critically ill patients can potentially result in ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI). Additionally, mechanical stretching might activate the pro-fibrotic signaling pathways, which could contribute to programmed cell death and the peripheral distribution of fibrosis observed in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). Based on our previous findings, we further explored whether lipoaspirate nanoparticles (Lipo-NPs) could offer protective effects against pulmonary fibrosis associated with VILI.MethodsThe 8-week-old male SPF C57BL/6 mice were selected for endotracheal intubation after abdominal anesthesia, mechanical ventilation of the mice for 4 h, and tidal volume was set at 30 ml/kg. Lipo-NPs were extracted and identified using a tangential flow filtration (TFF) system. All the mice were divided into three groups: control, VILI, and VILI + Lipo-NPs. The Lipo-NPs were administered into the mice through the tail vein for 7 consecutive days, and an equivalent volume of PBS was administered into the VILI groups. Mice were sacrificed 7 days after mechanical ventilation.ResultsOur results suggested that Lipo-NPs could reduce lung tissue damage, pulmonary inflammation, and fibrosis of the VILI mice, accompanied by a reduction in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), as well as a decrease in transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) levels. RNA-sequencing followed by Ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) was performed to explore downstream of Lipo-NPs and identified idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) as the potential pathway. By the combined analysis of microRNAs (miRNAs) enriched by Lipo-NPs, we hypothesized that B cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) is a target that mediates the inhibitory effect of mmu-miR-143-3p on VILI-induced IPF. Analysis of lung tissue from mice experiencing VILI revealed elevated levels of antiapoptotic BCL-2 family proteins within α-smooth muscle actin-expressing (α-SMA+) fibroblasts. Treatment with Lipo-NPs induced fibroblast apoptosis, decreased fibroblast numbers, and reduced histologically evident fibrosis. Blocking Lipo-NPs mmu-miR-143-3p in vivo blunts the beneficial effects of Lipo-NPs.ConclusionLipo-NPs intervention can partially ameliorate pulmonary fibrosis induced by VILI. This effect is likely due to the enrichment of miR-143 in Lipo-NPs, which promotes fibroblast apoptosis by targeting the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2.Graphical AbstractAdipose tissue from obese mice was extracted and subjected to TFF to obtain Lipo-NPs. VILI mice were then interfered with Lipo-NPs through tail vein injection. The study revealed that mmu-miR-143 enriched in Lipo-NPs promoted apoptosis of lung fibroblasts by targeting the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2, ultimately alleviating IPF in VILI. Introduction

The concept of ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) highlights the potential for mechanical ventilation to cause iatrogenic damage to the lungs [1]. Currently, the primary objective in treating acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is to minimize secondary lung injury induced by ventilators, aiming at reducing both morbidity and mortality [2]. Promoting the repair of lung injury is a crucial research direction in the field of respiratory critical illness [3, 4]. Extensive basic and clinical studies spanning nearly two decades have demonstrated that alterations in tidal inflation patterns and ventilation frequency among critically ill patients undergoing mechanical ventilation may contribute to the susceptibility of VILI [5]. The primary mechanisms of injury associated with VILI include: barotrauma/volumetric injury resulting from excessive stretching of lung tissue; atelectrauma caused by repetitive opening and closing of alveoli, leading to shear stress; and biotrauma, which is a biological response to tissue damage that can result in lung and multi-organ failure. The occurrence of VILI may be observed in certain patients despite the implementation of a conventional lung-protective ventilation strategy [6].

The use of ventilators and the occurrence of VILI are significant factors contributing to pulmonary fibrosis following ARDS. Animal models have demonstrated alveolar stripping and collapse hardening, indicating that mechanical deformation of the alveolar space plays a role in the development of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). Mechanical stretching of the alveolar space can activate pro-fibrotic signaling pathways in type II alveolar epithelial cells (AECs), leading to programmed cell death and peripheral distribution of fibrosis in IPF [7]. Hypoxia resulting from mechanical deformation and collapse further exacerbates this damage and intensifies the pro-fibrotic response. Mechanical transduction can induce fibrosis progression through various mechanisms, such as direct disruption of the cell membrane, alteration of cytoskeletal architecture, and activation of ion channels [8]. Each of these actions triggers distinct signaling pathways that ultimately result in the generation and release of cytokines and chemokines, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition [9]. Therefore, mechanical damage is inherent in both ARDS and pulmonary fibrosis, potentially linking these two processes that promote fibrotic remodeling [10].

Research has demonstrated that obesity is associated with improved prognoses for certain pulmonary conditions compared to individuals of normal weight. These conditions include pneumonia, acute lung injury (ALI)/ARDS, VILI, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), IPF [11], and lung cancer. Obese patients exhibit lower mortality rates and more favorable outcomes, a phenomenon referred to as the obesity paradox [12]. The db/db (characterized by leptin resistance) mice with ALI/ARDS exhibited diminished lung injury and enhanced survival rates compared to WT mice with ALI/ARDS [13]. This phenomenon may be attributed to elevated levels of the transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) inhibitor, peroxisome proliferation-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) [14]. Consequently, the partial explanation for the obesity paradox observed in ALI/ARDS could involve the inhibition of TGF-β1-mediated pulmonary fibrosis associated with obesity.

Our extensive prior research has demonstrated that extracellular vesicles (EVs) from adipose tissue have inhibitory effects on the pulmonary microvascular endothelial barrier and the inflammatory response in mice with VILI, providing some protective benefits [15]. Specifically, circulating exosomal miR-122-5p derived from adipose tissue enhances the integrity of the pulmonary endothelial barrier by downregulating the TGF-β pathway, which helps to mitigate endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) and oxidative stress, thereby preventing ARDS [16]. Notably, unlike EVs obtained from adipose tissue, we have isolated a substance known as lipoaspirate nanoparticles (Lipo-NPs) using tangential flow filtration. This method is more accessible, cost-effective, efficient, and time-saving, and allows for easier storage. Lipo-NPs derived from lipoaspirate offer faster processing (hours vs. months) and lower production costs than ADSC-EVs, as they eliminate the need for clinical-grade cell culture facilities. One of the advantages of using TFF to isolate Lipo-NPs is its capability to process large sample volumes (>1 L), enabling large-scale production-unlike techniques like size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), which are constrained by smaller input capacities [17–19]. Additionally, TFF demonstrates superior batch-to-batch consistency compared to traditional methods such as ultracentrifugation [20]. Lipo-NPs exhibit protective and anti-inflammatory properties, particularly by inhibiting toll-like receptor 4-mediated inflammatory cytokine release in macrophages. When loaded with guanabenz, Lipo-NPs demonstrate enhanced suppression of inflammatory pathways, indicating their potential as a combined therapeutic approach for treating inflammatory disorders [21, 22]. Two viable storage options exist for Lipo-NPs: (1) lyophilization, which preserves EV functionality [23, 24], or (2) −80℃ storage (≤ 2 years), shown to maintain EV/lipoprotein stability despite being less clinically convenient [25, 26].

Our subsequent experiments conducted both in vivo and in vitro confirmed that Lipo-NPs can protect the pulmonary microvascular endothelial barrier within a mechanical ventilation model by inhibiting the TRPV4/ROCK1/pMLC2 signaling pathway while increasing VE-cadherin expression [27]. Building on our previous findings, we further explored whether Lipo-NPs could offer protective effects against pulmonary fibrosis associated with VILI.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All studies utilizing animals underwent approval from the Animal Care and Use Committee at Chongqing Medical University (No. IACUC-CQMU-2024–04062).

Murine VILI model and intervention

Male SPF C57BL/6 mice, 8 weeks old, intended for research on VILI, were anesthetized through an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital at a dosage of 50 mg/kg. Following anesthesia, their tracheae were intubated, and they were placed on a small animal ventilator (ALCV8S model; Shanghai Alcott Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for a duration of 4 h, with tidal volume set at 30 ml/kg, a respiratory rate of 75 breaths per minute, and PEEP at 1 cm H2O. Referring to our previous study experience, we opted for an intervention concentration of 5 mg/kg for Lipo-NPs [27]. Then, the mice were administered Lipo-NPs through the tail vein for 7 consecutive days. The VILI group was administered an equivalent volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Mice were sacrificed 7 days after mechanical ventilation.

Tangential flow filtration (TFF)

Lipo-NPs were purified with a KrosFlo Research 2i Tangential Flow Filtration System (Spectrum Labs, Los Angeles, CA, USA). Mouse subcutaneous adipose tissue fluid (500 mL) was processed through sterile hollow fiber polyethersulfone membranes having 0.65 μm (D02-E65U-07-S; Spectrum Labs) and 500 kD (D02-S500-05-S; Spectrum Labs) pore sizes to remove cell debris and unbound biomolecules, respectively. Initially, the membranes were flushed with triple the volume of sterile PBS (pH 7.4), prior to handling the biological material. The Lipo-NPs were then condensed to roughly 25 mL and underwent 6 diafiltration cycles in a sucrose buffer (5% sucrose, 50 mM Tris, 2 mM MgCl). Afterward, the Lipo-NPs in the sucrose buffer were additional concentrated to a volume of 5 mL, ready for further analytical and experimental procedures [20]. The samples were preserved at −80℃ prior to subsequent analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The Lipo-NPs were suspended in 200 µL of pre-chilled PBS. The shape of the Lipo-NPs particles was analyzed using TEM with an HT7700 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM)

The 3 µL sample was placed in a Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 gold net with a mesh size of 300, and prepared using Vitrobot. Here, the excess solution was absorbed by a metal strip covered with filter paper, and the droplets were spread into a thin film. Subsequently, the grid was rapidly immersed in liquid ethane at its freezing point (−183 °C), transferred to a controlled environment freezer, and imaged using FEI Talos 200 C high-resolution TEM at −175 °C with an accelerated voltage of 200 kV. Low-dose imaging techniques were employed to minimize electron beam radiation damage. The images were digitally recorded using the highly sensitive direct imaging camera FEI Falcon III.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

The particle size distribution and concentration of Lipo-NPs were determined using NTA with a Flow NanoAnalyzer (NanoFCM INC., Xiamen, China).

Western blot

The protein markers for Lipo-NPs, such as CD63 (diluted 1:1000, from Proteintech Group Inc., Chicago, USA), Annexin V (diluted 1:1000, Proteintech Group Inc., Chicago, USA), CD81 (diluted 1:1500, Abcam, Cambridge, USA), and Calnexin (diluted 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, USA), were evaluated through western blotting.

Dual luciferase assay

The wild-type (WT) and mutant (Mut) 3’UTRs of BCL-2 were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and subsequently inserted into the psiCHECK-2 luciferase reporter vector (Promega, Madison, USA). The constructed plasmids were co-transfected into 293 T cells (Procell, Wuhan, China) along with either miR-143-3p mimics or negative control (NC) mimics, using X-tremeGENE™ HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). Luciferase activity was then quantified using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, USA).

Transfection of mmu-miR-143-3p antagomir (Si-143) or antagomir-scramble (Si-scr) in vivo

Eight-week-old VILI mice were subjected to receive daily 50 nmol/40 µL 5-Cy5-labeled Si-143 (Ribo Biotechnology Co., ltd. Guangzhou, China) or Si-scr(Ribo Biotechnology Co., ltd. Guangzhou, China) on top of Lipo-NPs treatment for eight days.

Lipo-NPs labeling and uptake in vivo

The PKH26 red fluorescent dye (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) was utilized to label the Lipo-NPs according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with a 30-minute incubation period at 37℃. Post incubation, the labeled Lipo-NPs underwent ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g and 4℃ for an hour, succeeded by three PBS washes. Prior to the VILI challenge, PKH26-labeled Lipo-NPs were injected into the tail vein 1 h in advance. Following this, lung tissues were harvested and prepared for immunofluorescent staining. Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI blue fluorescent dye (1:1000, Proteintech Group Inc., Chicago, USA), and the CD31 endothelial cell marker was detected using green fluorescence (Alexa Fluor 488 conjugated IgG; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) at a dilution of 1:1000 (Proteintech Group Inc., Chicago). Fluorescence microscopy (TE2000-U; Nikon, Tokyo) was employed for image capture. Indocyanine green (ICG, MaoKang Biotechnology, Shanghai, China)-labeled Lipo-NPs were injected into mice via the tail vein, and their distribution in vivo was monitored using the AniView100 Pro (Biolight Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) in vivo imaging system [28].

Lung histopathology

Following the experimental procedure, the lungs were carefully excised and swiftly preserved in a 4% paraformaldehyde fixative. Next, they underwent embedding in paraffin wax, followed by slicing into 4 µm thick sections. These sections were stained using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) as well as Masson’s trichrome method. Each mouse contributed three slides for examination, with a total of three mice assessed in each experimental group. The severity of lung damage was evaluated using a validated scoring method [29] in an unbiased fashion, and fibrosis was quantified by applying the Ashcroft scoring criteria [30].

Cytokine assay

After the experiment concluded, a bronchoalveolar lavage was performed through tracheal intubation, where 1 mL of sterile saline was instilled into the lungs. Around 0.8 mL of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was then retrieved and subjected to centrifugation at 500×g for 20 min while maintaining a temperature of 4 °C. The BALF supernatant’s levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and TGF-β were measured using ELISA kits as per the protocol provided by the manufacturer (Cloud-Clone Corp, Wuhan, China).

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was carried out on 10% formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded, or OCT-embedded (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) tissue sections. The sections were immunostained with anti-α-SMA (1:500, Abcam, Cambridge, USA) and anti-BCL-2 family member proteins: BCL-2 (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Danvers, USA), BCL-XL (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Danvers, USA), BCL-W (1:500, Signalway Antibody, Maryland, USA), MCL-1 (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, incubation with fluorescently labeled goat anti-mouse and donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (1:100 dilution, Invitrogen, Waltham, USA) took place. TUNEL staining, following the manufacturer’s guidelines (Promega Biotech Co., Ltd, Beijing, China), was conducted to identify apoptotic cells prior to antibody staining. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (TE2000-U; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Semiquantitative evaluation of BCL-2 family members and α-SMA+ cell co-staining was conducted blindly on 10 images/sample with scores of 0 (0%), 1 (1%–33%), 2 (34%–66%), or 3 (67%–100%), with higher scores signifying greater staining intensity. Fibrotic fibroblasts were deemed α-SMA+ cells not associated with vessels or airways. Prior to immunostaining, precision-cut lung slices (PCLS) were washed in PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with PBS-Triton X-100 (1%), and blocked with normal horse serum (10%) in PBS-Triton. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies against cleaved caspase-3 (1:500; Cell Signaling Technology) and α-SMA overnight at 4 °C in normal horse serum (10%) in PBS-Triton. Post three washes with PBS-Triton, fluorescently tagged secondary antibodies (1:200, Invitrogen, Waltham, USA) were applied. Samples were mounted in glycerol-fructose solution and imaged under a fluorescence microscope (TE2000-U; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Colocalization analysis involved counting five images per PCLS sample to quantify the number of cleaved caspase-3 and α-SMA+ fibroblasts. Quantitative statistics and analysis of images selected were performed using Image J software.

Extraction and purification of RNA

For lung tissue specimens, following the addition of 1mL TRIzol reagent per 20–50 mg of tissue, the specimens were pulverized in a low-temperature setting utilizing high-frequency oscillation, impact, and shearing forces from the grinding beads (tissue grinder model: Tissuelyser-24). Subsequently, total RNA was extracted and purified via the phenol-chloroform approach. This involved mixing 0.2 mL chloroform with each 1 mL of the lysed TRIzol sample, vigorous shaking for 15 s, allowing the mixture to stand at room temperature for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 4℃ and 12,000 ×g for 15 min. The supernatant was then transferred to a fresh 1.5 mL centrifuge tube where RNA precipitation with isopropyl alcohol took place.

MiRNA enrichment analysis of Lipo-NPs

First, the raw data undergoes de-jointing and mass filtering, after which the filtered sequences are processed to merge identical sequences and record their corresponding abundances. Subsequently, the deduplicated sequences are compared against the Rfam and miRBase databases to acquire annotation information for various small RNAs (sRNAs). Novel miRNA prediction analyses are conducted by aligning the sequences with reference genomes. Particular emphasis is placed on the analysis of miRNAs, encompassing their characteristics and the statistical evaluation of miRNA expression levels. Furthermore, cluster analysis and target gene prediction for differentially expressed miRNAs are performed, followed by target gene enrichment analysis.

Transcriptomic analysis

The V2 version of the VAHTS Stranded mRNA-seq Library Prep Kit from Vazyme Biotech (product code NR612-02) was employed for the library construction, adhering to the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. The alignment of reads to either the human Ensembl genome GRCh38 or the mouse Ensembl genome GRCm38 was achieved using Hisat2 aligner (v2.1.0), conFig.d with the “--rna-strandness RF” parameter. Subsequent quantification of the mapped reads was done with featureCounts (v1.6.3). For differential gene expression analysis, the DESeq2 R package was utilized. Additionally, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway assessments were carried out using the clusterProfiler R package (v3.6.0). IPA analysis was also conducted, with the QIAGEN IPA software being applied for this purpose.

Statistical analysis

All results are represented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical evaluations were carried out using GraphPad Prism 10 Software. A P-value below 0.05 was deemed statistically considerable. Assessments of discrepancies in the recorded variables were made via a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. For analyses involving more than two groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, succeeded by Tukey’s post hoc test for correcting multiple comparisons.

The ICG-labeled Lipo-NPs, after tail vein injection, were primarily distributed in the lung tissue of mice, as observed by the in vivo imaging system (Fig. 3B). Mice received Lipo-NPs, which had been tagged with PKH26 dye, through tail vein injection. The effective internalization of Lipo-NPs is evidenced by the red fluorescence of the PKH26 dye observed in the lung tissue capillaries, marked by the endothelial CD31 marker (Fig. 3C). Blocking mmu-miR-143-3p blunts the beneficial effects of Lipo-NPs

We further examined whether antagomir-143 can offset the therapeutic effects of Lipo-NPs in vivo. Using the same study protocol (Fig. 5A), antagomir-143 was injected into VILI mice before each Lipo-NPs therapy. According to TargetScan analysis, miR-143-3p binds to BCL-2 with a 6-base complementary sequence in mouse (Fig. 5B). This interaction was further validated through dual luciferase reporter assays (Fig. 5C). The Cy5 red fluorescently labeled mmu-miR-143 antagomir was detected in mouse lung tissue following intravenous injection via the tail vein (Fig. 5D). Follow-up assessment of pulmonary fibrosis after 7 days of treatment revealed that, in comparison to Lipo-NPs, VILI mice subjected to the combination therapy (antagomir-143 and Lipo-NPs) exhibited a significant increase in pulmonary fibrosis (Fig. 5E), an elevated Ashcroft score (Fig. 5F), and enhanced TGF-β secretion (Fig. 5H). Both basic and clinical studies have demonstrated that TGF-β plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of IPF [33]. Antiapoptotic BCL-2 family members are expressed by α-smooth muscle actin-positive (α-SMA+) fibrotic fibroblasts in VILI, Lipo-NPs induces their apoptosis, and antagomir-143 reverses this effect

Discussion

Patients with severe hypoxemia due to ARDS/COVID-19 will receive mechanical ventilation as a supportive treatment, but subsequent pathological studies have shown that this can lead to pulmonary fibrosis. Mechanical stress plays a crucial role in the development of pulmonary fibrosis [35]. Pulmonary fibrosis is a progressive and ultimately fatal disease with no cure, which is one of the major complications following COVID-19. During mechanical ventilation, the lungs undergo non-physiological stretching, especially when tissue damage disrupts normal tissue structure and function’s mechanical homeostasis, triggering abnormal repair mechanisms. This results in the excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) and progressive proliferation of myofibroblasts and fibroblasts leading to failure of physiological and mechanical homeostasis. IPF is an unknown etiology chronic disease characterized by progressive loss of lung function eventually leading to respiratory failure and death. Increased stiffness in the outer lung membrane along with scar formation significantly alters respiratory mechanics during spontaneous respiration or mechanical ventilation making it more vulnerable to non-physiological stress promoting lung injury dysregulation [36].

The exact cause of IPF remains unknown, and current treatments only offer limited relief for declining lung function. Anatomically, IPF presents as fibrosis consistent with usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), featuring patchy areas of infection within normal lung tissue surrounded by dense, non-elastic connective tissue, thickened interstitium, cystic abnormalities, and traction bronchiectasis. The heterogeneous deformation of alveoli results in uneven distribution of mechanical forces throughout the lungs that are transmitted to more expandable regions. Mechanical deficiencies in fibrotic lungs become apparent during mechanical ventilation (MV), where even minimal strain can lead to harmful levels of transpulmonary pressure at end-inspiration. During maximal lung expansion induced either mechanically or through spontaneous respiration, highly compliant areas may undergo detrimental overexpansion due to dangerous stretching forces. This elastic response to regional stress resembles the behavior observed in “squishy-balls” stress toys and may serve as a mechanical basis for further damage to the lungs. Considering that fibrosis progression follows a mechanical gradient from high-stress (peripheral/basal) regions to low-stress ones, it is plausible that mechanical stimulation also plays a role in promoting fibrosis during spontaneous breathing patterns. Mechanical transduction refers to the biological phenomenon wherein applied mechanical stresses on tissues trigger molecular pathways that elicit adaptive responses; this intricate process is closely associated with pulmonary fibrosis and likely contributes significantly to lung scarring [37].

Increased mechanical tension activates the TGF-β signaling pathway in alveolar type II (AT2) cells, leading to a direct upregulation of TGF-β1 expression in AT2 cells and driving the spatially regulated progression of pulmonary fibrosis from the periphery to the center. The pathological process of IPF shares similarities with this progression, and inhibiting the TGF-β signal specifically in AT2 cells is sufficient to impede fibrotic advancement [38]. TGF-β1 plays a significant role as a growth factor in myofibroblast differentiation and the formation of a pro-fibrotic microenvironment. It becomes activated under high-intensity mechanical stress exerted on the ECM. The mechanical transduction pathway represents a crucial step in the pathogenesis of fibrosis and may serve as an important target for fibrosis therapy [39].

Early VILI alveolar microinjury increases local vascular permeability, causes edema, is accompanied by inflammatory cytokines, and promotes cytoskeletal remodeling. If these minor injuries cannot be properly repaired, then fibroblasts will be activated, followed by excessive extracellular matrix deposition [35]. Mechanical transduction, which links mechanical stress and strain to molecular pro-fibrotic pathways, may contribute to the progression of fibrosis [37]. The critical importance of mechanical strain in the multifactor pathophysiology regulating IPF has been demonstrated by related studies, wherein the initiation primarily occurs due to repeated injury and inadequate repair of epithelial cells, leading to overactivation of fibroblasts and subsequent fibrosis [35]. Early intervention aimed at disrupting the mechanotransduction pathway in the fibrotic response may potentially minimize IPF changes in tissues and fibroblasts [40].

Our findings also demonstrated that mechanical ventilation can induce VILI, resulting in pulmonary tissue damage and the development of pulmonary fibrosis in mice. However, following the administration of Lipo-NPs, the extent of injury was attenuated, accompanied by a reduction in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6, as well as a decrease in TGF-β levels, which serves as a marker for pulmonary interstitial fibrosis. Previous studies have also reported on the anti-inflammatory effects of Lipo-NPs, which possess advantages such as high cellular absorption capacity, prolonged circulation time within the body, and targeted delivery capabilities. And most importantly, Lipo-NPs can affect cell function by transferring biologically active RNA. Lipo-NPs contain more miRNAs, which may be related to lipoprotein binding and have unique functional properties [21]. We further investigated the enrichment of miRNAs in Lipo-NPs, with the most enriched ones being miR-1a-3p, miR-21a-5p, miR-143-3p, miR-126a-3p, and miR-30a-5p. Additionally, we conducted a transcriptomic analysis to compare the VILI group with the Lipo-NPs intervention in the VILI group. Through IPA tools, we identified that Lipo-NPs intervention primarily suppressed signaling pathways related to CTLA4 signaling in cytotoxic T lymphocytes, pulmonary fibrosis idiopathic signaling pathway, and mitochondrial dysfunction pathway. Furthermore, our findings revealed that the transfer of Lipo-NPs-enriched mmu-miR-143-3p to lung tissue inhibited fibroblast apoptosis induced by anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 and alleviated pulmonary fibrosis in VILI mice.

The process of apoptosis can be initiated through two distinct pathways, namely intrinsic and extrinsic. Fibroblasts exhibit resistance to apoptosis by inhibiting the intrinsic pathway through upregulation of BCL-2 expression. Previous findings have demonstrated that in individuals with IPF and fibrotic mice, lung macrophages evade apoptosis by eliminating dysfunctional mitochondria via mitochondrial autophagy. Enhanced mitochondrial autophagy in pulmonary macrophages contributes to increased production of TGF-β1 derived from these cells during the development of fibrosis. The BCL-2 inhibitor ABT-199 induces apoptosis in lung macrophages as well as potential fibroblasts and effectively reverses established pulmonary fibrosis. Following the establishment of fibrosis, mice harboring single-cell derived macrophages with conditionally deficient BCL-2 are capable of reversing dysregulated remodeling associated with lung fibrosis [41].

Investigation into lung tissue from individuals with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and mice exposed to repeated bleomycin doses indicated an increase in the levels of BCL-2, BCL-XL, and BCL-W, which are part of the anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family, in α-SMA+ fibroblasts. The intrinsic apoptotic pathway is governed by intricate interactions among the BCL-2 family proteins, encompassing both pro-survival and pro-death members, which act as activators, sensitizers, and pore formers. Despite the recognized role of BCL-2 family dysregulation in tumorigenesis, the significance of these mechanisms in fibroblast survival during human fibrotic lung disorders or in animal models of advancing pulmonary fibrosis is not clearly understood. In IPF, heightened amounts of the anti-apoptotic BCL-2 protein have been linked with apoptosis resistance in fibrotic lung tissue. Cells with upregulated anti-apoptotic BCL-2 proteins can reach a “primed” state, a precarious survival state near the apoptotic brink. Should pro-apoptotic proteins be unleashed by the suppression of BCL-2 family anti-apoptotic proteins, they may nudge cells past this brink, triggering cell death. The study employed two murine models of PF-ILD to assess the potential for reversing established pulmonary fibrosis. Upon induction of fibroblast apoptosis, there was a reduction in the number of fibroblasts, a decrease in lung collagen levels, and an alleviation of both imaging manifestations and histologically evident fibrosis. Targeting fibroblasts presents a promising therapeutic approach for patients with progressive interstitial lung diseases [42]. Furthermore, liposomes have been employed by some researchers to specifically target VCAM-1 for delivering BCL-2 inhibitors, offering a potential selective treatment option for IPF [43]. In this study, we demonstrate that anti-apoptotic members of the BCL-2 family are expressed in highly activated fibroblasts from mice with VILI and that Lipo-NPs induce fibroblast apoptosis while reducing IPF in VILI mice.

Cell proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation are regulated by miRNA, which binds to regulatory elements in the 3’ untranslated region (UTR) of target mRNA. Analysis using bioinformatics tools has identified a highly conserved miR-143-3p binding site in the 3’ UTR of BCL2. BCL2 is a member of the anti-apoptotic subgroup within the BCL2 protein family and is vital for controlling mitochondrial outer membrane permeability and apoptosis. Interaction with BH3-only “activator” proteins inactivates BCL2-like proteins, but disrupting this interaction allows activator proteins to enter the cytoplasm, where they trigger conformational changes in pro-apoptotic Bax-like proteins. This process results in the formation of pores in the mitochondria’s outer membrane and the release of pro-apoptotic factors into the cytoplasm. Due to its pro-survival properties, miR-143-3p reduces the stability of BCL2 mRNA resulting in an observable pro-apoptotic phenotype upon inhibition of BCL2 translation [44].

Current studies in the field of cancer have also revealed that miR-143 promotes apoptosis of tumor cells by specifically targeting BCL-2. For instance, it has been observed to exert this effect in various types of cancer including prostate cancer [45], glioblastoma multiforme [46], and thyroid papillary carcinoma [47]. Furthermore, miR-143 has demonstrated its pro-apoptotic role in other diseases as well by targeting BCL-2, such as mitigating hypoxia/reoxygenation injury [48] and myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury [49]. Additionally, exosome-mediated miR-143-3p induces β-cell apoptosis by promoting the degradation of BCL2 mRNA and inhibiting translation of BCL2 mRNA, thereby playing a novel role in β-cells [44].

This study has several limitations. In the subsequent phase, we conducted relevant verification of in vitro cell experiments. This paper primarily focuses on the validation of gene transcription signals and expressions, which can be further integrated with multiple omics approaches such as proteomics and metabolomics for comprehensive analysis in future studies.

Conclusions

Patients receiving mechanical ventilation, even with strict adherence to protective ventilation strategies, may still experience progression of pulmonary fibrosis. Our study further revealed that biogenic Lipo-NPs intervention can partially reverse VILI and subsequent IPF, even when pulmonary fibrosis has already developed as a result of mechanical ventilation. This reversal may be attributed to the protective role played by Lipo-NPs-enriched miRNA. Specifically, miR-143 induces apoptosis in fibroblasts by targeting the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2, ultimately alleviating pulmonary fibrosis in VILI mice. These findings provide novel therapeutic insights for future research and clinical practice.
