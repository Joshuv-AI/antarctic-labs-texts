Ligand-metal charge transfer and photothermal carbon dots synergistically enhance cerium dioxide nanozyme activity for diabetic infected wound healing
Authors: Weian Wu, Yunxin Ye, Qiuhui Jiang, Kaichen Zeng, Wenjing Wu, Haijiao Xie, Tao Luo, Lvhua Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO2 NPs), with intrinsic antioxidant, pro-oxidative activity under acidic conditions, and antibacterial properties, have been explored for diabetic wound therapy. However, their poor stability and limited catalytic efficiency remain major barriers. Here, we developed a facile strategy to anchor gallic acid (GA) onto CeO2 NPs through metal–polyphenol coordination. This polyphenol shell markedly enhances nanoparticle stability and modulates the electronic structure via ligand–metal charge transfer, optimizing Ce valence states and oxygen vacancy ratios to boost nanozyme activity. To further improve therapeutic efficacy, photothermal carbon dots (CDs) with high light-to-heat conversion efficiency were incorporated. Beyond serving as photothermal agents, these CDs also exhibited intrinsic enzyme-like activities. In vivo, the resulting hybrid nanozyme accelerated diabetic wound healing by integrating antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects, while promoting epithelial regeneration and collagen deposition. This work highlights polyphenol–metal coordination as a versatile approach for stabilizing and optimizing metal-based nanozymes.Keywords: Nanozyme, Cerium dioxide, ligand−metal charge transfer, Carbon dots, Wound healing

Introduction

Diabetes is a highly prevalent disease with a steadily rising incidence and an immense global health burden, representing a major public health challenge. Among its complications, chronic diabetic wounds significantly impair patient prognosis and quality of life [1]. Growing evidence suggests that excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), together with secondary bacterial infections, aggravates oxidative stress in diabetic wounds and suppresses macrophage activity, leading to immune dysregulation and prolonged inflammation [2,3]. Consequently, the design of biomaterials with integrated immune-modulatory, ROS-scavenging, and antibacterial functions holds great promise for the effective treatment of refractory diabetic wounds [4].

In recent years, cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO2 NPs) with strong enzymatic activity have been widely investigated for wound management, including diabetic ulcers. A growing body of studies shows that CeO2‐based systems accelerate re-epithelialization and collagen deposition, promote angiogenesis, and dampen excessive inflammation by scavenging ROS and modulating macrophage polarization toward an M2 phenotype. [5,6]. Their enzyme-mimetic behavior arises from oxygen vacancies that enable reversible Ce (III)/Ce (IV) cycling. Normally a higher Ce(III) fraction generally reflects greater vacancy density, facilitating oxygen exchange and strengthening redox reactions critical for scavenging excess ROS and combating infection in the acidic, biofilm-prone wound milieu [7,8]. Despite these advances, clinical translation is limited by poor aqueous dispersibility, aggregation, and suboptimal biocompatibility at the wound interface. Moreover, single-metal ceria often exhibits sluggish electron transfer and an insufficient density of oxygen vacancies, leading to low catalytic efficiency in complex physiological media. To address these issues, morphology regulation and metal doping are commonly employed [[9], [10], [11]]. However, morphology regulation usually requires complex synthesis conditions and metal doping can introduce biosafety concerns and broadly effective strategies that simultaneously enhance physicochemical stability and catalytic efficiency remain lacking.

Metal–phenolic ligand modification strategies have attracted considerable attention in the biomedical field due to their mild reaction conditions and favorable biocompatibility, offering an ideal approach to regulate the structure and function of cerium oxide [12]. Gallic acid (GA), a naturally occurring polyphenolic compound, has shown good results in treating diabetes mellitus and its related complications [13]. It possesses a planar molecular structure containing a galloyl group with one carboxyl and three phenolic hydroxyl groups. This structure not only endows GA with the ability to act as an organic linker—enabling to chelate metal ions and form stable metal–phenolic complexes—but also provides it with excellent adhesive properties [14,15]. Previous studies have demonstrated that metal–phenolic interactions can improve the dispersibility of nanomaterials, facilitate their adhesion to biological surfaces via hydroxyl groups and partially oxidized quinone structures, and modulate their catalytic activity through charge transfer [[16], [17], [18]]. However, systematic studies on the charge-transfer mechanisms at the polyphenol–CeO2 interface and their specific effects on CeO2 enzyme activity are still lacking and require further investigation.

Beyond interfacial charge-transfer regulation, photothermal effects provide an additional route to enhance catalytic performance and confer antibacterial properties. Photothermal therapy (PTT) not only eradicates bacteria through localized heating under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation [[19], [20]][], but also accelerates catalytic kinetics by promoting hot-electron transfer in metal–phenolic systems, thereby enhancing enzyme-like activity [21]. Here, carbon dots with high photothermal conversion efficiency were integrated into the metal–phenolic framework to achieve synergistic antibacterial and catalytic effects. Besides excellent photothermal properties, additional active sites and structural defects are introduced by N and S doping, which also endows them with Peroxidase (POD) activity, Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) free radical scavenging activities [22,23].

This study reports a novel metal–phenolic nanoparticle synthesized using GA as a ligand. Electron transfer modulates the Ce (III)/Ce (IV) valence ratio, increasing oxygen vacancies, thereby enhancing the POD- and SOD-like activities of CeO2. With the involvement of GA, macrophage polarization and the immune microenvironment can be effectively regulated. In combination with photothermal carbon dots, the antimicrobial capacity is further strengthened, while the POD, SOD and Catalase (CAT) -like nanozyme activity is synergistically amplified. These cooperative effects confer potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties, ultimately facilitating infected diabetic wound healing (Scheme 1).

The charge transfer between GA and CeO2, together with photothermal carbon dots acceleration of electron transfer, synergistically enhanced the nanozyme activity of CeO2. Leveraging its excellent antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties, it promoted the healing of diabetic bacteria-infected wounds.

Experimental section

Synthesis of CeO2 nanoparticles

The procedure started with dissolving cerium nitrate in distilled water, yielding a 0.5 mol/L solution. The solution was stirred continuously in a 65 °C water bath while citric acid was added dropwise until its concentration reached 1.5 mol/L. After 24 h of reaction, a uniform sol was obtained. The sol was dried in a vacuum oven for 12 h and then ground into a fine powder. Finally, the powder was calcined at 500 °C in a muffle furnace for 2 h, producing pale yellow cerium oxide powder.

Synthesis of carbon dots

Following a previously reported synthesis [21,24], 1 g of citric acid and 2 g of urea were dissolved in 20 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and subjected to a solvothermal reaction in an autoclave at 160 °C for 6 h. After the reaction, the system was cooled naturally to room temperature and then mixed with ethanol solution. Residual solvent and by-products were removed using density gradient centrifugation. The resulting precipitate was freeze-dried and ground into powder.

Synthesis of Ce@G and Ce@G@C nanoparticles

Under stirring, 1 mM CeO2 and 17 mg GA were dissolved in 20 mL deionized water, and the pH was adjusted to 9.0 using 0.1 M NaOH solution. The resulting mixture was stirred at room temperature for 3 h. After the reaction, the product was collected by centrifugation and washed with deionized water. The product was then freeze-dried under vacuum and stored at room temperature in a sealed container. Using the same procedure, CDs were mixed with the Ce@G solution, separated by centrifugation, washed, and finally freeze-dried under vacuum for storage.

The structural characterizations

The morphology and elemental distribution of Ce@G@C were examined using transmission electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (TEM-EDX, Talos F200X, FEI, USA). The dynamic light scattering and zeta potential was measured in water at 25 °C using a Zetasizer Nanoseries instrument (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90, UK). Chemical structures were confirmed using FT-IR spectroscopy (Nicolet iN10, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The crystal phase was identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a SmartLab SE diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan). UV–Vis absorption spectra were recorded using a UV spectrophotometer (UV-3600i, Shimadzu, Japan). Electrochemical performance was evaluated using an electrochemical workstation (CHI76E, Chenhua, China). Oxygen vacancies were examined by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy at room temperature (EMX Plus, Bruker, Germany). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed using a K-Alpha spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). ·OH generation

The peroxidase-like activity of the material was assessed using the TMB colorimetric method [25]. Material solutions of identical concentration were introduced into PBS buffer (pH 4.0) containing TMB (10 mg/mL), yielding a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. Subsequently, H2O2 was added at a final concentration of 1 mM, and the mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. After the reaction, the absorbance at 650 nm was recorded using UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy. The same assays were repeated with PBS buffers of different concentrations and pH values, or under 808 nm laser irradiation at 1 W cm−2. Hydroxyl radicals (•OH) produced in the reaction were captured by DMPO, and the DMPO–OH adducts were detected and quantified with an X-band electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrometer (EMX Plus, Bruker, Germany).

DPPH free radical scavenging

The DPPH radical scavenging capacity was evaluated using UV–Vis spectroscopy. Ethanol solutions containing 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl radicals (•DPPH) were mixed with different material solutions, each adjusted to a final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL. The mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 30 min, and the absorbance at 517 nm was measured. The same procedure was then applied to assess Ce@G@C and CDs at final concentrations of 0.025, 0.050, 0.1, and 0.2 mg/mL.

•O2 – scavenging

•O2 - Scavenging ability was determined using a commercial SOD assay kit, following the manufacturer's instructions exactly. The reaction solution was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and the absorbance at 560 nm was measured using UV–Vis spectroscopy. According to the kit instructions, one unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to achieve a 50 % clearance rate. To examine the effect of NIR irradiation, the same procedure was repeated under 808 nm laser irradiation at 1 W cm−2. Using the same protocol, the SOD activity of CDs at concentrations of 0.025, 0.050, 0.1, and 0.2 mg/mL was also tested.

Hydrogen peroxide scavenging

The hydrogen peroxide scavenging efficiency of the material was evaluated using a dissolved oxygen meter (JPBJ-608, REX, China). PBS (pH = 7.4) was supplemented with the material and H2O2 to final concentrations of 0.2 mg/mL and 10 mM, respectively. Dissolved oxygen levels were measured every 15 s for 600 s, and the corresponding data were recorded. To assess the effect of NIR irradiation, the same conditions were applied with 808 nm laser exposure at 1 W cm−2, and changes in dissolved oxygen were simultaneously monitored.

DFT calculation

All density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) within the framework of the projector augmented wave (PAW) method. For the treatment of electron exchange–correlation interactions, the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) generalized gradient approximation functional was employed, supplemented by Grimme's DFT-D3 dispersion correction and a Hubbard U term of 5.0 eV to account for the localized nature of Ce 4f states [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. A plane-wave basis set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV was used, and the structural optimizations were converged until the total energy difference and residual atomic forces were below 1 × 10−5 eV and 0.02 eV/Å, respectively. Brillouin zone integrations were performed using Monkhorst–Pack k-point grids with a reciprocal space resolution finer than 0.04 Å−1 [30].

The computational investigation commenced with the clean CeO2 (111) surface (denoted as CeO2). Full relaxations were carried out for the pristine surface, isolated gallic acid (GA), and GA coordinated to the surface through phenolic –OH groups (termed GA–CeO2). The corresponding adsorption energies were evaluated to quantify the binding strength. Oxygen vacancy formation energies were further calculated following full structural relaxation of the defect systems. To elucidate electronic properties, Bader charge analysis, charge density difference (CDD) mapping, and density of states (DOS) calculations were performed.

Catalytic activity was explored by constructing reaction free energy diagrams for both peroxidase (POD) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) pathways. For this purpose, all intermediates along the four-step POD cycle (∗ + H2O2 → H2O2∗ → 2OH∗ → ∗ + H2O) and the SOD cycle (2HO2 + ∗ → 2HO2∗ → H2O2 + O2∗ → ∗ + O2) were fully optimized, and vibrational frequency analyses were conducted. These results enabled the evaluation of Gibbs free energy profiles, providing thermodynamic insight into the reaction mechanisms.

Antioxidant and anti-Inflammatory assays

RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) and L929 cells with H2O2 (300 μM), followed by replacement with media containing different materials for continued culture. Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the ROS probe DCFH-DA, and fluorescence was recorded in the culture plates with a fluorescence microscope (BX53, Olympus, Japan). The gene expression levels of pro-inflammatory factors tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) were quantified using qRT-PCR to evaluate the immunomodulatory role of Ce@G@C. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the internal reference gene, with primer sequences provided in Supplementary Table S4. RAW264.7 cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 10 min, blocked with 10 % goat serum, and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. They were then incubated overnight with iNOS and CD206 antibodies. Afterwards, the cells were treated with a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody and counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Finally, images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (BX53, Olympus, Japan).

Cell migration assays

L929 cells were seeded into 6-well plates, and when confluence reached ∼85 %, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 1 % FBS. The cells were then subjected to different treatments: control (NC), H2O2 (300 μM), H2O2 (300 μM) + 200 μg/mL CeO2, Ce@G, and Ce@G@C. A scratch assay was subsequently performed, and cell migration was observed and recorded after 24 h of incubation.

In vivo evaluation of infected wound healing

Animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Huawei Testing Co., Ltd. (NO: 202504003). All procedures strictly followed the ARRIVE guidelines for reporting in vivo experiments. Male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old, 19 ± 2 g) were obtained from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou, China). Diabetes was induced by intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (60 mg/kg body weight). One week later, blood glucose was measured with a glucometer (MM1300, Omron, Japan). Mice with blood glucose levels consistently above 16.7 mmol/L were considered diabetic. Under sterile conditions, full-thickness circular skin wounds (8 mm in diameter) were created on the dorsum, followed by inoculation with a mixed suspension of E. coli and S. aureus (1 × 108 CFU/mL, 20 μL). The animals were randomly divided into five groups and received local treatments of PBS, CeO2, Ce@G, Ce@G@C, or Ce@G@C combined with near-infrared irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 10 min). Wound healing was documented by photography, and local nanozyme treatment with photothermal therapy was performed on days 1, 3, 6, and 9 post-surgery. Wound area was measured using ImageJ. On day 3, bacteria from wound tissues were plated on solid media and incubated at 37 °C for 16 h, and viability was determined by colony-forming unit (CFU) counts. Wound healing rate (%) was calculated as: [(original wound area − actual wound area on day n)/original wound area] × 100 %, where n = 3, 6, 9, or 12 days post-surgery. On day 12, mice were euthanized, and skin tissues and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were collected, fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin for pathological examination. Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and Masson staining were performed for histological analysis. Wound tissues were subjected to immunofluorescence staining (F4/80, CD206 and iNOS). Immunohistochemistry was further used to detect IL-6 and TNF-α expression in wound tissues, with quantification performed using ImageJ.

Statistics

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent experiments. Comparisons of data between three or more groups were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's HSD post hoc test. Results were considered statistically significant when P value < 0.05.

Result

Preparation and Characterization

CeO2 NPs was first dispersed in deionized water, followed by the addition of GA to allow its preliminary adsorption onto the CeO2 NPs surface. The pH of the suspension was then adjusted to 9 to promote stable GA–Ce coordination, yielding the Ce@G NPs [17]. As shown in Fig. S1, CeO2 NPs appeared light yellow, whereas Ce@G NPs displayed a dark gray color. Carbon dots (CDs) were synthesized from citric acid and urea via a solvothermal method in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and then simply mixed with Ce@G NPs in aqueous solution to form Ce@G@C NPs.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. S2 and Fig. 1A–C) showed uniformly distributed carbon dots with an average size of ∼2 nm with a lattice spacing of 0.22 nm. Both Ce@G NPs and CeO2 NPs exhibited polyhedral morphologies, with a GA-derived protective layer visible on the Ce@G surface and less agglomeration. On the surface of Ce@G@C NPs, lattice fringes corresponding to the characteristic (111), (311), (222), and (200) planes of CeO2 NPs were observed. However, the high crystallinity of CeO2 produced intense, well-defined lattice fringes that masked the low-contrast features of the CDs, complicating assignment of their characteristic lattice planes in Fig. 1C. To verify the successful loading of carbon dots, energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping showed uniform distribution of Ce, C, N, and S elements in Ce@G@C. Among them, C signals originated from both GA and CDs, while the additional N and S signals were attributed to adsorbed carbon dots (Fig. S3).

The zeta potential measurement (Fig. 1D) revealed that the surface potential of Ce@G@C was −35 mV. Generally, in aqueous systems, dispersions with a zeta potential absolute value greater than 30 mV exhibit high stability [31]. Dynamic light scattering (Fig. S4) measurements indicate that CeO2 readily aggregate in aqueous media, exhibiting an average hydrodynamic diameter of 763.6 nm with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.472. Upon subsequent surface coating with GA and CDs, the hydrodynamic diameter stabilizes at 299.2 nm and the PDI decreases to 0.089. These results demonstrate that the combined surface modification with GA and CDs effectively suppresses aggregation of CeO2 NPs and substantially enhances their dispersibility in water. Fig. S5 illustrates the dispersion stability of CeO2, Ce@G, and Ce@G@C in PBS, DMEM and FBS. The CeO2 NPs sedimented the fastest, with complete sedimentation occurring within 6 h. Due to charge neutralization by cations in PBS, the negatively charged Ce@G and Ce@G@C NPs also settled almost completely within 12 h. In contrast, their dispersions in DMEM and FBS remained stable and well-dispersed even after 24 h.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Fig. 1E) indicated that the phenolic ligand exchange of GA preserved most of the original crystal structure of CeO2 NPs, while in the Ce@G@C NPs sample, a characteristic graphene (002) peak of carbon dots appeared at 2θ ≈ 26°, further confirming the incorporation of carbon dots.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy results (Fig. 1F) showed that for CDs, the characteristic peaks at 3431 cm−1, 3191 cm−1, 1617 cm−1, and 1359 cm−1 were attributed to the stretching vibrations of –OH, N–H, C=O, and C=C, respectively, indicating the presence of hydroxyl, amine, carboxyl, and carbonyl functional groups in the carbon dots. The characteristic peaks of CeO2 NPs appeared at 1647 cm−1 and 580 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of Ce–O, while the peak at 3434 cm−1 was attributed to the stretching vibration of –OH. After GA modification, the O–H stretching band red-shifted, broadened, and decreased in intensity, suggesting that, in addition to GA–Ce metal chelation, hydrogen-bonding interactions also occur between them. For Ce@G@C NPs, the characteristic peaks of hydroxyl, amine, and carbonyl groups derived from carbon dots simultaneously appeared at 3431 cm−1, 3191 cm−1, and 1617 cm−1, with the –OH peak further red-shifted to 3108 cm−1, indicating hydrogen bond formation between carbon dots and Ce@G, thereby confirming the successful incorporation of CDs.

The ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) diffuse reflectance spectrum (Fig. 1G) of Ce@G shows a broad absorption band in the 400–500 nm range, which can be ascribed to the ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) process [32]. In this process, electrons are partially transferred from GA to Ce ions, resulting in the formation of a stable complex. The corresponding Tauc plots were calculated further (Fig. S6). Compared with pristine CeO2, Ce@G exhibits a reduced band gap, which can be attributed to the generation of additional oxygen vacancies upon GA coordination. The narrowed band gap not only confirms the structural modification but also contributes to enhanced redox activity [33].

The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) curves (Fig. 1H) revealed distinct mass variation patterns for GA, CeO2 NPs, and Ce@G NPs within the temperature range of 0–800 °C. Based on the mass loss of Ce@G NPs, the proportion of GA within the composite was estimated to be approximately 11 %. Furthermore, by employing a standard calibration curve derived from the characteristic absorbance peaks of carbon dots at varying concentrations, and analyzing the absorbance changes of the supernatant after composite loading, the loading efficiency of carbon dots in Ce@G@C NPs was estimated to be about 15 % (Fig. S7).

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) analysis (Fig. 1I) demonstrated that, compared with CeO2 NPs, Ce@G NPs exhibited higher peak currents for both oxidation and reduction processes, indicating enhanced electron transfer capability and the potential to facilitate more efficient redox catalytic reactions at the material surface.

In CeO2 NPs, cerium typically exists in two oxidation states, Ce (IV) and Ce (III), and remains in equilibrium with oxygen anions (O2−). When a portion of Ce (IV) is reduced to Ce (III), accompanied by the generation of oxygen vacancies to maintain charge neutrality. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis confirmed the changes in Ce valence states and oxygen vacancy concentrations following GA modification (Fig. 1J).

As shown in Fig. 1K, the high-resolution Ce 3d spectrum can be deconvoluted into five spin–orbit doublets, with the peaks at V0, U0, V′ and U′ assigned to Ce (III) species and the remaining V, U, V″, U″, V‴ and U‴ peaks to Ce (IV) species. Compared with pristine CeO2 NPs, the proportion of Ce (III) in the Ce 3d spectrum of Ce@G NPs increased from 21.6 % to 37.3 %, indicating that GA modification significantly promoted the conversion of Ce (IV) to Ce (III) (Table S1).

Fig. 1L shows that the O 1s spectrum can be resolved into three characteristic peaks: lattice oxygen (OLatt) at ∼529.5 eV, oxygen vacancies (OSur) at ∼530.0 eV, and surface-adsorbed oxygen (OAds) at ∼531.0 eV [34]. After GA modification, the overall binding energy of lattice oxygen shifted to higher values, likely due to the formation of Ce–O–GA coordination bonds at the Ce@G NPs interface. This interfacial bonding induces ligand-to-metal charge transfer, thereby decreasing the electron density of lattice oxygen, generating additional oxygen vacancies, and enhancing lattice oxygen mobility [35]. Notably, the variation in the OSur/OTotal ratio followed a similar trend to the Ce (III)/Ce (IV) ratio. The OSur content in Ce@G NPs (30.73 %) was significantly higher than that in CeO2 NPs (17.64 %), indicating a substantial increase in the concentration of defect sites associated with catalytic activity (Table S2) [36]. Electron spin resonance (ESR) analysis (Fig. S8) further confirmed the presence of oxygen vacancies. The results revealed that the signal intensity of Ce@G NPs at g = 2.0, characteristic of oxygen vacancies, was markedly higher than that of CeO2 NPs, consistent with the XPS findings. Taken together, the simultaneous increase in Ce (III) content and oxygen vacancy concentration is expected to markedly enhance the nanozyme catalytic performance of Ce@G NPs.

For the high-resolution O 1s spectrum of CDs, characteristic peaks were observed at 531.2 eV and 533.6 eV, corresponding to C=O and C–OH/C–O–C bonds, respectively. The S 2p spectrum exhibited four peaks attributed to C–S and S=O covalent bonds (arising from S 2p1/2 and S 2p3/2 splitting). The N 1s spectrum was deconvoluted into three peaks assigned to pyridinic N, amino N, and pyrrolic N. These results indicate that CDs are rich in nitrogen- and sulfur-containing functional groups, which facilitate defect formation, thereby endowing them with potential nanozyme activity (Fig. S9A–E) [37].

NIR triggered photothermal performance

The photothermal effects of CeO2 NPs, Ce@G NPs, and Ce@G@C NPs were first evaluated under 808 nm laser irradiation (Fig. 2A). At the same concentration and a laser power density of 1 W/cm2, there was no significant change in the temperature of ddH2O and CeO2 NPs, the temperature of Ce@G NPs only increased by 5.8 °C, and its slight temperature rise may originate from the charge transfer and intramolecular motion between GA and Ce [38]. In contrast, Ce@G@C NPs showed the highest photothermal temperature change (ΔT = 32.8 °C). The reason for this is that carbon dots have a strong absorption capacity in the infrared region. Previous studies have speculated that this type of CDs contains a large number of C=O groups (electron-withdrawing surface structures), and S doping can introduce lower energy levels, thereby reducing the optical band gap. The synergistic effect of various factors makes them generate a strong absorption band in the red to near-infrared region and gives them excellent photothermal properties [22,23].

The results of UV–Vis spectrophotometric analysis (Fig. S10) showed that as the concentration increased, the optical absorption band of Ce@G@C NPs became wider and the intensity increased, and its temperature change was significantly and positively correlated with both concentration and laser power (Fig. 2B and C). At a concentration of 200 μg/mL, Ce@G@C NPs had the highest absorbance at 808 nm, which was significantly higher than other concentrations. Under a laser power density of 1 W/cm2, the temperature gradually rose from 25.6 °C to 59.5 °C within 10 min. This relatively slow heating process is conducive to achieving a long-term thermal sterilization effect while reducing damage to wound tissue (Fig. 2F). To study the photostability and photothermal conversion efficiency, Ce@G@C at a concentration of 200 μg/mL was continuously irradiated with an 808 nm laser, the irradiation was stopped after 600 s and allowed to cooled naturally, and this was repeated for five cycles (Fig. 2D), and the photothermal conversion efficiency was calculated to be 56.92 % (Fig. 2E). The results showed that the peak temperature of Ce@G@C NPs remained stable in each cycle, indicating its good photothermal repeatability, as well as excellent thermal and photostability.

Multiple nanozyme activities

To investigate the regulatory effect of GA modification and photo-thermal on the enzyme-like activities of cerium oxide, a series of experimental studies was conducted (Fig. 3A). First, the POD-like activity was evaluated using the 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) colorimetric assay (Fig. 3B). The results demonstrated that (Fig. 3C), at pH 4.0, CeO2 NPs, Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs catalyzed the oxidation of colorless TMB into the blue product oxTMB in the presence of H2O2. Among them, Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs exhibited significantly higher absorbance of characteristic absorption peak than CeO2 NPs, indicating enhanced efficiency in catalyzing H2O2 decomposition to generate reactive oxygen species under acidic conditions. Furthermore, with increasing concentrations of Ce@G@C NPs, both the color intensity of TMB and its characteristic absorption peak gradually increased, confirming that its POD-like activity is concentration-dependent (Fig. 3D). Subsequently, the catalytic activity was evaluated under different pH conditions (pH 7.4, 4, 3 and 2). The results indicated that, under neutral conditions mimicking the physiological microenvironment (pH 7.4), none of the samples exhibited detectable oxidase activity. Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs displayed superior catalytic performance across pH 4, 3, 2 values compared with CeO2 NPs (Fig. S11). Given the excellent photothermal conversion capability of Ce@G@C NPs, its catalytic performance under NIR irradiation was further investigated. Under 808 nm NIR irradiation, the TMB solution in the Ce@G@C group exhibited markedly enhanced colorimetric change and characteristic absorption peaks compared with those under ambient temperature conditions (Fig. S12. To assess its capability for ·OH radical generation, ESR spectroscopy was employed. As shown in Fig. 3E, the ESR signal intensities of Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs were significantly higher than those of CeO2 NPs, with Ce@G@C NPs exhibiting further enhancement under NIR irradiation, indicating substantial ·OH radical generation. These results further confirmed that the photothermal effect enhances the nanozyme activity of Ce@G@C NPs. Therefore, Ce@G@C NPs can fully exploit the synergistic effect of its photothermal properties and POD-like activity, thereby achieving efficient antibacterial activity.

Effective scavenging of free radicals is crucial for interrupting the inflammatory cascade in diabetic wounds. Therefore, the free radical scavenging capability of Ce@G@C NPs was further investigated in this study. First, the free radical scavenging performance was evaluated using 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (·DPPH) radicals as a model reactive nitrogen species (Fig. 3F). ·DPPH possesses a stable nitrogen-centered free radical with an unpaired electron. Upon addition of Ce@G NPs or Ce@G@C NPs, its cha racteristic absorption peak at 515 nm decreased significantly, accompanied by a color change of the solution from purple to light yellow, indicating effective free radical scavenging (Fig. 3G).

In addition, the SOD-like activity was assessed using a commercial SOD assay kit (Fig. 3I). The results demonstrated that Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs effectively scavenged superoxide anions (O2−·), thereby inhibiting their reduction of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) to blue formazan (Fig. 3J). Moreover, their SOD-like activity was further enhanced under NIR irradiation (Fig. S13A). Notably, both the ·DPPH scavenging activity and SOD-like activity of Ce@G@C NPs exhibited concentration dependence, whereas CeO2 NPs showed only moderate ·DPPH and O2−· scavenging capabilities (Fig. 3H and Fig. S13B).

CAT-like activity catalyzes the decomposition of H2O2 into O2. Therefore, the CAT-like activity of the materials was evaluated by quantifying the amount of O2 generated. The results showed that the ability of Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs to decompose H2O2 was significantly weaker than that of CeO2 NPs. Under NIR irradiation, elevated temperatures accelerated H2O2 decomposition, while the localized photothermal effect facilitated hot electron transfer. As a result, the Ce@G@C NPs + NIR group yielded the highest O2 generation, significantly surpassing all other groups (Fig. 3K).

These results indicate that GA modification decreased the CAT-like activity of CeO2, while both its POD- and SOD-like activities increased. Decreased of the CAT-like activity is likely attributable to the GA coating, which improves the dispersion and specific surface area of CeO2, but simultaneously reduces the surface proportion of Ce (IV) associated with CAT activity and hinders H2O2 adsorption and decomposition, thereby diminishing CAT-like activity.

Under acidic conditions, the GA coating can partially dissociate, exposing more active sites. Meanwhile, the strong ligand-to-metal charge transfer interaction in the Ce–GA system increases the surface proportion of Ce (III) and the number of oxygen vacancies, which are critical catalytic centers for SOD- and POD-like activities. The increase in active sites directly enhances POD-like activity. For SOD-like activity, the small size of superoxide radicals (O2−·) allows their diffusion to remain unaffected by the coating, while GA facilitates their capture and promotes their adsorption and redox reactions at oxygen defect sites, thereby improving overall SOD-like activity [39]. Meanwhile, under the photothermal effect induced by NIR irradiation, accelerated ligand-to-metal charge transfer and enhanced enzymatic reaction kinetics increase the POD-like, SOD-like and CAT-like activities.

Interestingly, during the experiments, the CDs were found to exhibit not only SOD-like activity and ·DPPH radical scavenging capability, but also POD-like activity under acidic conditions, with all of these activities displaying concentration dependence (Fig. S14). Although metal-free doped carbon dots with antioxidant properties have been reported, metal-free carbon dots that combine antioxidant capacity with pro-oxidant behavior under acidic conditions without external excitation remain relatively rare.

After oxidation with H2O2 solution, the relative contents of C, O, N, and S in CDs changed, with the most pronounced variation observed in S. This suggests that S-containing sites may serve as key catalytic centers for their POD-like activity (Fig. S15). Previous studies have demonstrated that S in CDs can promote H+ adsorption, reduce the Gibbs free energy barrier for the conversion of H2O2 to ·OH, and facilitate H2O dissociation, thereby providing sufficient protons for the transformation of H2O2 into ·OH and ultimately enhancing POD-like nanozyme activity [40].

The POD-like and antioxidant properties of CDs may arise from multiple synergistic factors, including their structural characteristics, the number and distribution of active sites, the nature of surface functional groups, and the cooperative effects between functional groups and doped elements [41,42]. For its surface functional groups and structure, hydroxyl and carboxyl groups can act as substrate-binding sites, whereas carbonyl groups conjugated with the π-system serve as important catalytic active sites. These features have been confirmed to significantly enhance the SOD-like activity of CDs [43,44]. Heteroatom doping of the carbon lattice (e.g., with N or S) can further modulate catalytic performance. Nitrogen atoms, with strong electron-withdrawing ability, can induce charge redistribution and promote electron transfer, both of which are closely related to the catalytic activity of CDs [45]. In contrast, sulfur has an electronegativity similar to that of carbon; thus, S doping primarily redistributes spin density while exerting little effect on atomic charge distribution. Notably, the experimental results revealed that the sulfur content in CDs was relatively low, which may be attributed to the larger atomic radius of S compared with C, making its incorporation into the carbon framework less favorable. However, the larger atomic size can introduce additional structural defects into the sp2 carbon framework, thereby creating extra active sites [[41], [46]]. Overall, heteroatom doping can not only introduce new catalytic centers but also improve charge carrier separation efficiency, thereby enhancing both antioxidant and pro-oxidant properties at multiple levels.

DFT calculations

In order to further explore the interaction between GA and CeO2 NPs and its effect on catalytic performance, we conducted a density functional theory (DFT) analysis. The DFT calculation results showed that the adsorption energy of GA on the CeO2 NPs surface was −4.32 eV, indicating that GA can be stably adsorbed on the CeO2 NPs surface (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, we analyzed the charge transfer between the GA and Ce interface. The Bader charge analysis results showed that after GA modification, the number of electrons gained or lost by the Ce atoms changed (Table S3). This indicates that electron coupling occurred between GA and the CeO2 surface, and the electrons released by oxygen vacancies were redistributed between Ce and GA. Furthermore, the electron density difference isosurface map visually (Fig. 4B) shows the charge transfer during the adsorption process: the blue area represents electron accumulation and the yellow area represents electron depletion, which shows that electrons are transferred from GA to the CeO2 NPs surface and redistributed. Fig. 4C shows that the oxygen vacancy formation energy of Ce@G NPs (3.87 eV) is lower than CeO2 NPs (4.77 eV), which indicates that after GA doping CeO2 NPs, oxygen vacancies are more easily formed.

C) Calculation of oxygen vacancy formation energy for CeO2 NPs and Ce@G NPs. D) Total density of states (TDOS) of CeO2 NPs and Ce@G NPs. E) Catalytic mechanism and G)free energy diagram of POD-like activity in CeO2 NPs and Ce@G NPs. F) Catalytic mechanism and H) free energy diagram of SOD-like activity in CeO2 NPs and Ce@G NPs.

Through the analysis of the total density of states (TDOS) map (Fig. 4D), it is revealed that that Ce@G NPs exhibits additional density of states peaks at the Fermi level. This activation of the electronic structure increases the availability of states near the Fermi level and strengthens electron transfer across the system. As the enzyme-like activity of Ce@G NPs depend on efficient electron migration and redox processes, this optimized electronic structure provides a stronger foundation for catalysis, thereby markedly enhancing its enzyme-like activity.

Fig. 4E and G illustrate the POD-like catalytic pathway, in which H2O2 decomposes into two hydroxyl radicals (·OH). The Gibbs free energy profile shows that each reaction step on Ce@G NPs possesses a lower energy barrier than on pristine CeO2 NPs. Notably, Ce@G NPs stabilizes H2O2 in an intermediate state with a remarkably low energy (−3.7 eV), indicating enhanced substrate activation and barrier reduction, thereby conferring stronger POD-like catalytic activity. Similarly, for the SOD-like reaction, the Gibbs free energy of the Ce@G NPs intermediate is consistently lower than that of CeO2 NPs (Fig. 4F and H). Collectively, these theoretical results demonstrate that Ce@G NPs exhibits superior catalytic performance in both SOD- and POD-like reactions. Mechanistically, GA modification increases oxygen vacancies, elevate the density of states near the Fermi level and facilitate electron transport. This promotes substrate adsorption/activation and stabilizes key intermediates, thereby lowering apparent activation barriers and markedly enhancing enzyme-mimicking activity.

In vitro antibacterial activity and biofilm inhibition ability

Given the prominent photothermal effect and enzyme-mimicking activity of Ce@G@C NPs, its antibacterial performance was further investigated. Agar plate colony counting (Fig. 5A and B) revealed that, under both NIR-irradiated and non-irradiated conditions, CeO2 NPs inhibited Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) by 13.7 % and 28.7 %, respectively. In comparison, Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs (without NIR irradiation) showed slightly higher inhibition rates of S. aureus (22.7 % and 24.7 %, respectively) and E. coli (33.3 % and 43.9 %), respectively (Fig. S16). The antibacterial activity of these materials may originate from the oxidase-like activity of cerium oxide, as well as from its ability to deplete intracellular ATP in bacteria [47]. Although in vitro experiments demonstrated stronger POD-like activity for Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs, the limited production of H2O2 in bacterial culture media and the intrinsic antioxidant defense systems of bacteria resulted in negligible differences in antibacterial efficacy among the groups. However, upon NIR laser irradiation of Ce@G@C NPs, the inhibition rates against both S. aureus and E. coli approached 99 %. Live/dead bacterial staining further confirmed the same trend (Fig. 4C and D). Recent studies have shown that phenolic–metal systems can adsorb to bacterial surfaces, thereby exerting antibacterial effects or protecting probiotics [38,48]. Therefore, we propose that Ce@G@C NPs first adheres to bacterial surfaces through phenolic interactions. NIR-induced photothermal heating then elevates the local temperature, compromising membrane integrity, reducing physiological activity, and promoting uptake of the nanomaterial and H2O2. Coupled with its oxidase-like activity, these processes act synergistically to kill bacteria [49].

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 4E and F) revealed that all three materials induced varying degrees of morphological damage in S. aureus and E. coli. Notably, Ce@G@C NPs under NIR irradiation caused extensive bacterial shrinkage, rupture, and deformation, accompanied by the most pronounced membrane structural disruption.

To evaluate ROS generation with and without NIR irradiation, the 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCFH) probe assay was performed under physiological conditions. The results showed that CeO2 NPs and Ce@G NPs exhibited similar green fluorescence intensities under both irradiated and non-irradiated conditions, which were significantly higher than those of the control group. In contrast, Ce@G@C NPs under NIR irradiation exhibited the highest green fluorescence intensity, indicating that only Ce@G@C NPs could effectively enhance intracellular ROS levels in bacteria upon NIR exposure (Fig. S17).

The intrinsic properties of bacterial biofilms can cause wound healing to stagnate in the inflammatory phase, leading to persistent infections; therefore, early prevention or eradication of biofilms is particularly. Based on this, the preventive effects of CeO2 NPs, Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs on bacterial biofilm formation were evaluated. S. aureus and E. coli were pretreated with different materials and then cultured for 48 h under either near-infrared (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2) irradiation or dark conditions. The resulting biofilms were stained with crystal violet for quantitative analysis and with SYTO9 for fluorescence observation. Compared with the control group, treatment with CeO2 NPs, Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs partially inhibited biofilm formation. Among them, Ce@G@C NPs under NIR irradiation achieved the highest inhibition, with the rate significantly increased to 80 % (Fig. 5G, H and Fig. S18). Three-dimensional confocal laser scanning microscopy images (Fig. 5I, Fig. S19) revealed that, under irradiation, the biofilms pretreated with Ce@G@C NPs exhibited only weak green fluorescence and the thinnest structure. These results indicate that, under 808 nm NIR irradiation, Ce@G@C NPs can effectively inhibit bacterial biofilm formation.

Biocompatibility, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cell migration-promoting abilities

The biocompatibility of Ce@G@C NPs was systematically evaluated in L929 and RAW264.7 cells using the CCK-8 assay, supplemented by live/dead cell staining (Fig. S20 and Fig. S21). The results demonstrated that at a concentration of 200 μg/mL, the viability of all cells remained above 80 %. Fluorescence microscopy was employed to monitor the uptake of FITC-labeled Ce@G@C NPs by RAW264.7 cells. After co-incubation for 30min, 1 h, and 2 h, the fluorescence intensity in the FITC channel progressively increased, indicating that Ce@G@C NPs was internalized by RAW264.7 cells in a time-dependent manner (Fig. S22). Furthermore, hemolysis assays demonstrated that at this concentration, Ce@G@C NPs nanoparticles exhibited good hemocompatibility; therefore, this concentration was selected for subsequent studies (Fig. S23).

Diabetes leads to extensive protein glycation and the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), which elevate intracellular ROS levels and contribute to a sustained chronic inflammatory state in wounds. In addition, this process is associated with the inhibition of macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype.

To investigate the cellular antioxidant properties of Ce@G@C, the intracellular ROS levels in RAW264.7 and L929 cells under different treatment conditions were assessed using the DCFH-DA staining method. In L929 cells (Fig. 6A, Fig. S25), intracellular ROS levels were significantly elevated in the H2O2-treated group compared with the control group. All three materials (CeO2 NPs, Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs) reduced ROS levels, with the most pronounced decreases observed in the Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs groups. Flow cytometry also confirmed this phenomenon (Fig. S25). A similar trend was observed in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. S26).

To further investigate the effects of Ce@G@C NPs on macrophage function, macrophages were co-cultured with nanoparticles in the presence or absence of lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry was performed to assess the expression of iNOS (a marker of M1 macrophage activation) and CD206 (a marker of M2 macrophage polarization) (Fig. 6B–D, Fig. S27). The results showed that the iNOS level in the Ce@G@C NPs + LPS group was significantly lower than that in the CeO2 NPs + LPS and LPS groups; in contrast, the number of CD206-positive cells was markedly increased in the Ce@G@C NPs + LPS group. Moreover, LPS stimulation induced the upregulation of inflammation-related genes, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, in RAW264.7 cells, whereas the Ce@G@C NPs + LPS group significantly suppressed the expression of these genes, demonstrating its potent anti-inflammatory activity (Fig. 6E–G).

Given that impaired fibroblast, keratinocyte, and endothelial cell migration is a hallmark of delayed diabetic wound healing, we further evaluated the effect of Ce@G@C NPs on L929 cell migration under oxidative stress. The experimental results demonstrated that, compared with the H2O2 group, pretreatment with CeO2 NPs, Ce@G NPs or Ce@G@C NPs significantly enhanced the migratory capacity of L929 cells, with Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs showing more pronounced effects (Fig. 6H and I). Collectively, these results indicate that Ce@G@C NPs simultaneously modulate inflammation and restore cell migration, thereby creating a favorable microenvironment for accelerated diabetic wound healing.

In vivo evaluation of bacterial-infected wound healing in diabetic mice

Based on the excellent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, pro-healing, and antibacterial properties of Ce@G@C NPs, we further evaluated their therapeutic efficacy in a chronic infected wound model in diabetic mice (Fig. 7A). PBS, CeO2 NPs, Ce@G NPs, Ce@G@C NPs and Ce@G@C NPs combined with NIR irradiation were applied to 8 mm full-thickness splinted skin wounds infected with S. aureus or E. coli. Remarkably, the Ce@G@C NPs + NIR group achieved a wound closure rate of ∼56.5 % (±2.76) on day 3, with nearly complete healing by day 12 (Fig. 7B–D). To verify the anti-infective effect, bacteria collected from wounds were cultured on day 3, showing abundant colonies in the Control, CeO2, Ce@G, and Ce@G@C groups, whereas the Ce@G@C NPs + NIR group effectively suppressed bacterial growth through synergistic photothermal antibacterial activity (Fig. 7C–E).

Histological analysis and immunohistochemical staining were further employed to evaluate the tissue regeneration effects of Ce@G@C NPs + NIR. H&E staining (Fig. S28A) revealed that by day 12, the wound gap in the Ce@G@C NPs + NIR group was the shortest (0.79 ± 0.1 μm), with markedly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and the highest degree of wound closure, whereas abundant inflammatory cells were still observed in the CeO2 NPs, Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs groups. The degree of collagen deposition in granulation tissue is an important indicator of wound healing quality. Masson's trichrome staining (Fig. 7G) demonstrated that collagen deposition was markedly increased in the Ce@G@C NPs + NIR group, with deeper blue staining. Quantitative analysis (Fig. S28B) further confirmed that the collagen deposition rate in the Ce@G@C NPs + NIR group (68.3 ± 1.7 %) was significantly higher than in the other groups, while no significant differences were observed among the CeO2 NPs, Ce@G NPs and Ce@G@C NPs groups. These findings indicate that Ce@G@C NPs + NIR effectively promotes collagen deposition and accelerates wound tissue repair.

To investigate the mechanism by which Ce@G@C NPs regulate inflammatory responses and promote wound healing, immunofluorescence staining was performed on mouse wound tissues at day 12 using antibodies against F4/80, CD206, and iNOS. Specifically, F4/80 was used to label all macrophages, CD206 to label M2-type macrophages, and iNOS to label M1-type macrophages. M1 macrophages primarily exert pro-inflammatory functions, while M2 macrophages play key roles in suppressing inflammation and promoting tissue repair. Fig. 8A and Fig. S29 showed that, compared with the untreated group, the proportion of iNOS-positive cells was significantly reduced in the CeO2 NPs group. Moreover, owing to the intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties of GA and its electron transfer interaction with CeO2, the Ce@G and Ce@G@C groups showed a further decrease in iNOS-positive cells and a significant increase in CD206-positive cells. Notably, in the Ce@G@C + NIR treatment group, the number of iNOS-positive cells was significantly reduced, while CD206 expression reached the highest level. Collectively, these findings suggest that Ce@G@C combined with NIR effectively modulates inflammation, potentially by promoting the transition of M1 macrophages to M2 macrophages.

Further immunohistochemical staining of pro-inflammatory factors IL-6 and TNF-α was performed, followed by quantitative analysis of positive tissue areas. As shown in Fig. 8B–E, compared with the control group, CeO2, Ce@G, and Ce@G@C significantly reduced IL-6 and TNF-α levels in the tissues. Moreover, IL-6 levels were further reduced in the Ce@G and Ce@G@C groups compared with the CeO2 group, suggesting that the synergistic effect between GA and CeO2 can further suppress tissue inflammation. In addition, the proportion of IL-6- and TNF-α-positive areas was significantly lower in the Ce@G@C + NIR treatment group compared with other groups. These findings further confirm that Ce@G@C combined with NIR can effectively reduce inflammatory cytokine levels in diabetic wound tissues. Furthermore, H&E staining of major organs confirmed no histological abnormalities or signs of toxicity (Fig. S30).

Taken together, these findings suggest that the superior wound healing efficacy of the Ce@G@C NPs + NIR group can be primarily attributed to three factors: (1) enhanced antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities that effectively mitigate oxidative stress and inflammation. (2) promoting the transition of M1 macrophages to M2 macrophages. (3) pronounced photothermal antibacterial performance that efficiently eliminates bacteria and acts synergistically with antioxidant effects; and (4) promotion of cell migration and tissue regeneration, thereby accelerating wound closure.

Conclusion

This study proposes a straightforward approach that assembles GA with cerium oxide through metal–polyphenol coordination and incorporates photothermal carbon dots, aiming to accelerate the healing of diabetes-associated infected wounds. GA coating prevents CeO2 NPs aggregation and enhances solubility. Its coordination with CeO2 NPs promotes electron transfer, accelerates Ce (IV) reduction to Ce (III), and increases oxygen vacancies, as further validated by DFT calculations. In synergy with the photothermal effect, the nanozyme activity of CeO2 NPs is greatly enhanced. We also discovered that these photothermal carbon dots act as both antioxidants and light-independent pro-oxidants, a rare combination of properties for metal-free carbon dots. In summary, Ce@G@C NPs combines strong photothermal effects with acid-enhanced pro-oxidation, achieving potent antibacterial activity and effective biofilm inhibition. Furthermore, GA and CeO2 NPs act synergistically to enhance ROS clearance, drive macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, and promote cell migration under oxidative stress, collectively accelerating the regeneration of chronic inflammatory wounds. This green, economical, and simple phenol–metal coordination interface offers a new strategy for designing and optimizing other metal-based nanozyme materials.
