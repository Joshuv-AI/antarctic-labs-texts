Background

Clinical decision support (CDS) is a critical part of electronic health records (EHR), which provides patient-specific information combined with up-to-date clinical knowledge to the right healthcare provider at the right time in the workflow [1]. CDS tools have the potential to improve healthcare quality and efficiency when implemented correctly [2]. However, acceptance rates of CDS alerts often fall below 10% [3–5]. A large number of less relevant alerts can cause alert fatigue, and in that situation, healthcare providers start desensitizing to all alerts, including alerts of higher importance, which might endanger patient safety [6, 7].

Optimizing CDS alerts requires a comprehensive understanding of potential features that affect user response to alerts, particularly focusing on trigger criteria and clinical context [8, 9]. An alert with an overall low acceptance rate may be frequently accepted in one scenario and never accepted in other scenarios. Identifying features that predict whether an alert is likely to be accepted (e.g., patient age, location) can provide opportunities for improving that alert [10]. For example, an alert which recommends weight-loss counseling may normally have a reasonable acceptance rate but, if it fires in an operating room during surgery, its acceptance rate may be near-zero. Thus, knowledge of the feature “patient location” can be used to identify scenarios where the alert is unlikely to be accepted. A well-structured taxonomy of these features which conforms to EHR standards could enable better CDS design and help ensure interoperability and widespread adoption. Furthermore, such a taxonomy could lay the foundation for users’ developing predictive models that aim to enhance the logic behind alerts, tailoring them based on millions of users’ historical behavior and thereby mitigating the issues of alert fatigue.

In previous studies, attempts have been made to categorize CDS features, but several limitations existed. Most prior taxonomies were constrained to features highlighted in publications up to 2020 and do not consider features used in current alert logic [11–13]. In addition, previous taxonomies lacked interoperability standards, which cause barriers in using them in CDS design and implementation [14–16]. Thus, both an updated literature review and an analysis of public alert logic repositories are critical to constructing a comprehensive taxonomy.

The objective of this work is to develop a standard-based taxonomy of features that might affect user response to alerts using evidence from literature and public alert logic repositories. This taxonomy could facilitate the CDS implementation process but also assist researchers in gathering relevant data for analyzing user responses. The ultimate goal is to enhance the design and efficacy of CDS alerts, contributing to safer and more efficient patient care.

Methods

Taxonomy development

We developed a taxonomy of features using multiple sources: (1) the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) CDS Connect Repository, (2) alert logic from commercial EHR customers, and (3) published literature. Three categories (patient, provider, environment/context) were used a priori to develop the taxonomy. The final taxonomy was mapped to the Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) standard (v5.0.0: R5) for development of standardized CDS services [17]. In specific, we used FHIR resources, a set of modular components that define the most relevant objects in the healthcare context [18]. The study overview is in Fig. 1.

Study overview

The AHRQ CDS Connect Repository is a freely accessible CDS knowledge database developed from guidelines, peer-reviewed articles, best practices, and quality measures. It includes 30 alerts with detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria, which were analyzed in this study. We also extracted alert logic from alerts developed by Commercial EHR Customers. We randomly selected 500 unique alerts to review, but ended the review process early upon reaching saturation. Evidence from relevant literature was identified from two databases: MEDLINE (PubMed) and Embase (Ovid) to identify studies that describe CDS alerts (and, thus, potential features used in the alert logic). The search strategy was comprised of three parts: (1) using alert-related keywords in the title or abstract and MeSH terms (e.g., Decision Support Systems, Clinical[MeSH], decision support[Title/Abstract]); (2) using MeSH terms and keywords related to EHR (e.g. electronic health records[MeSH]); and (3) containing any of the following keywords in the title or abstract: alert*, reminder*, factor*, feature*, response*, acceptance*, adherence*, override*, compliant*, and adoption*. Inclusion criteria for the study were: (1) English language, peer-reviewed papers published from January 2000 to January 2022 and (2) alerts provided to healthcare providers, delivered on screen, and integrated into the EHR. The detailed queries are listed in Appendix (1) The queries in PubMed and Embase databases returned 3,621 and 332 results, respectively. After removing duplications, 3,846 records remained. After reviewing the title and abstract, 135 full papers were reviewed and 38 of them met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The flow diagram of included studies is shown in Appendix (2) In addition, we used a previously identified literature set, which included 243 studies related to features that impact user responses to CDS that were screened from 2,176 studies published before May 2020 [10]. Overall, knowledge sources covered insights from real-world implementations in clinic and in research. Therefore, these sources were excellent in developing a taxonomy of features that might affect user response to alerts.

Three categories: provider, patient, and environment/context were used a priori. Some example features are listed in Table 1. The exact categories were subject to change during the derivation process. For repositories, we extracted features used in the alert criteria. For previous literature, we extracted features for studies that assessed relationships between features and user response to alerts or offered suggestions about how to optimize alerts from patient, provider, and environment/context. These categories were informed by the CDS five rights and specify features that can be used in alert criteria [19]. Individual elements in the taxonomy were determined empirically. The final taxonomy was reviewed by CDS experts in our research team.

Results

Our extensive search across various databases and repositories yielded significant insights regarding potential features that could influence the user response to CDS alerts. Aggregating potential features extracted from three data sources, we identified 95 unique features, which we then mapped to the FHIR standard, encompassing 24 FHIR resources. An overview of these features, including their corresponding FHIR resources, the number of times they were used in alerts, and their frequency in the literature, can be found in Table 2. The detailed information about each feature and their relevant literature and alerts are listed in Appendix 4. To illustrate the taxonomy’s effectiveness in improving alert acceptance, we analyzed an influenza vaccine alert displayed in the operating room, which had a very low acceptance rate. Using this taxonomy, the contributing factors were mapped to the patient’s encounter location, procedure status, provider type, and alert type.

A taxonomy of features that might affect user responses to alerts

The common features differed depending on the knowledge source. For instance, in the AHRQ public alert repository, frequently occurring features were observations in flowsheets, procedures, diagnoses, medications, and patient age. On the other hand, the commercial EHR customer data primarily presented features such as diagnosis type, patient age, diagnosis grouper, diagnosis, medication value set. Literature-based insights revealed that provider type, medication, patient age, alert severity, and medication dose were the most common features. The item proportions across three knowledge sources within various feature categories are shown in Fig. 2. While most of the identified features seamlessly mapped onto the FHIR standard, certain features presented challenges. Main issues were calculated features, things that were unlikely to be in the EHR and site-specific features. Specifically, several features extracted from the literature, including patient tolerance and reasonable alternative medications, patient adherence with medication, features categorized under organization and alert metadata, and features like working experience, computer proficiency, task complexity, workload, and time pressure, did not align with the FHIR standard.

Discussion

We developed a standard-based taxonomy, including the diverse features affecting user responses to CDS alerts. Our study provided a comprehensive understanding of the multitude of features influencing user responses to CDS alerts across various sources, highlighting both the diversity and commonality of these features identified from both academic literature and industry repositories.

A finding from our research are the variations between real-world implementations and academic literature. While practical repositories like AHRQ and the Commercial Customer data lean towards direct patient-related factors such as diagnosis, medication, and age, the academic literature also considers external features. This discrepancy underscores the possibility that real-world implementations prioritize direct patient-specific data, while academic research delves further into systemic and external features that influence alert response.

The challenge of mapping certain features extracted from the literature onto the FHIR standard indicates the potential gaps in the standard’s comprehensiveness. For example, some features reflected the complexities that healthcare professionals face in real-world, such as workload, time pressure, and job complexity. However, these features were not explicitly included in a standard. The lack of a comprehensive standard not only hinders the problem of low acceptance of CDS from being effectively addressed, but also prevents potential solutions from being scaled up.

Furthermore, the dynamic nature of the healthcare landscape, reflected in the identification of new features post-May 2020, calls for periodic revisions and updates to such taxonomies. As the updates of EHRs and CDS systems, it’s imperative for CDS taxonomies to mirror these shifts, ensuring their sustained relevance.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, our study examined features from previous literature and public alert logic repositories to develop a taxonomy of features that impact user responses to CDS. Publication bias might exist in the literature review. In addition, there might be other sources of CDS alert logic that we did not review. Second, this taxonomy aims to identify potential features, but does not attempt to calculate the importance of each feature. Third, the adoption of FHIR remains a topic of ongoing discussion, particularly regarding the broad scope of the Observation resources, which include vital signs, laboratory tests, and clinical findings. While it covers multiple dimensions of taxonomy, further validation with healthcare providers is needed to ensure effective implementation and acceptance.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated a standard-based taxonomy of features that could impact user responses to CDS alerts, bridging insights from academic studies and industry practices. The taxonomy stands as a foundational tool, guiding the CDS development, implementation, and evaluation, with the overarching goal of improving user acceptance and healthcare quality.
