A chromosome-scale genome assembly of the peanut beetle, Ulomoides dermestoides (Coleptera: Tenebrionidae)
Authors: Huamei Xiao, Chunhui Ma, Shuangyuan Li, Huangjie Gao, Shaomin Tao, Chuanlin Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUlomoides dermestoides, a member of the Tenebrionidae family, is known by various common names such as Chinese beetle, Chinese weevil, peanut beetle, cancer beetle, or asthma beetle. Despite its origin in Asia, it has achieved global distribution owing to its perceived medicinal properties. In this study, we employed a combination of Illumina short-read, PacBio long-read, and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) sequencing technologies to produce a high-quality, chromosome-scale genome assembly of U. dermestoides. The assembled genome spans 253.38 Mb across 11 chromosomes, including the two sex chromosomes, X and Y. Key metrics include contig and scaffold N50 values of 9.83 Mb and 28.62 Mb, respectively, with an estimated genome completeness of 99.62%. Annotation efforts identified 15,553 protein-coding genes and revealed that 48.11% of the genome comprises repetitive sequences. This meticulously assembled genome serves as a pivotal resource for elucidating the genetic determinants underlying the species’ ecological habits and potential medicinal properties.Subject terms: Entomology, Genome evolution

Background & Summary

The historical and taxonomical background of Ulomoides dermestoides (Yangchong) as documented in ancient Chinese medical works, particularly in the “Supplements to the Compendium of Materia Medica”, provides intriguing insights. Originating from distant oceans and introduced to China around the end of the Ming Dynasty, this beetle is referred to as “洋虫 (Yangchong)” in Chinese. However, there is still ongoing debate and lack of consensus regarding its precise Latin name. In Chinese references, the beetle is commonly referred to as Martiarus demestoides Chevrolat, although alternative names such as Palembus dermestoides (Fairmaire, 1893) and U. dermestoides (Fairmaire, 1893) are also cited. This variability reflects the complexity in taxonomy and historical nomenclature associated with the species1.

U. dermestoides, widely distributed across Southeast Asia (including the Philippines, Vietnam, Indonesia, and China), the South Pacific (Fiji Islands), and other regions2, plays significant roles both as a pest and in traditional medicine. This beetle is known to infest stored grains and affect various crops such as corn, sorghum, oats, peanuts, rice, and soybeans3, in addition to consuming herbs like saffron, dates, and betel nut, as well as a range of fruits and vegetables4.

In traditional medicine practices, particularly in Japanese and Chinese cultures, U. dermestoides has been extensively utilized for treating ailments such as low back pain, coughs, and respiratory disorders including asthma5,6. Historical texts such as the “Supplements to the Compendium of Materia Medica” document its use either independently or in combination with other remedies for treating over twenty different diseases. Recent scientific studies have further explored its therapeutic potential, indicating promising applications in managing conditions like asthma, Parkinson’s disease, diabetes, arthritis, and even cancer7–11. The medicinal value of U. dermestoides stems from its rich nutritional profile, which includes proteins, fatty acids, diverse amino acids, minerals, and trace elements. In Chinese tradition, it is revered as the longevity beetle due to its extract’s significant content of antioxidants and anti-stress proteins. Both laboratory experiments and animal studies have highlighted its strong antioxidant properties, ability to combat aging, and potential to enhance immune function12–14.

The genetic information available for U. dermestoides has been limited, with only the mitochondrial genome accessible thus far15, hindering comprehensive study and exploration of its potential applications, particularly in medicine. To address this gap, we have successfully generated a high-quality, chromosome-scale assembly of the U. dermestoides genome. This achievement was made possible through a hybrid sequencing approach that combined Illumina short reads, PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) reads, and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) data. This integrated genomic resource is poised to significantly advance research into U. dermestoides by providing a detailed blueprint of its entire genome structure. Such comprehensive genomic data not only enhances our understanding of the genetic basis of this species but also opens up avenues for exploring its medicinal potential and developing targeted control strategies. By leveraging this genome assembly, researchers can now delve into previously inaccessible areas of research, paving the way for new applications and discoveries in both basic and applied sciences related to U. dermestoides.

Methods

Sample preparation

The original population of U. dermestoides was acquired from Wenzhou in Zhejiang Province, China. Following three months of rearing in the laboratory at the Key Laboratory of Crop Growth and Development Regulation in Jiangxi Province, individuals from this cultured population were selected for sequencing. Accurate identification of the beetles was conducted by Professor Guodong Ren at Hebei University. For their diet, the insects were provided with a variety of food items, including red jujube, lotus seed, reishi mushroom, safflower, oatmeal, and orange peel. The beetles were maintained in a controlled growth chamber, where the temperature was kept at 28 ± 1 °C, humidity at 75% ± 5%, and a light/dark cycle of 14/10 hours was implemented.

Flow Cytometry

The heads of adult male U. dermestoides were severed using a dissecting needle, and two heads were subsequently pooled into a single 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Each pooled sample was then homogenized with an abrasive rod in 200 μL of Galbraith’s nuclear dissociation solution. The obtained homogenized tissue was filtered through a 38 μm nylon mesh to isolate a nuclear suspension. Following filtration, the cells were centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 minutes at 4 °C, after which the precipitated nuclei were gently resuspended in 400 μL of 1 × PBS buffer by scraping the bottom of the tube. To stain the nuclei, propidium iodide (PI) solution was added to achieve a final concentration of 50 μg/mL, along with RNase A solution at a final concentration of 20 μg/mL. The stained nuclei were incubated in darkness at 4 °C for a duration of 5 to 20 minutes. For comparative analysis of genome size, 100 heads of adult Drosophila melanogaster Canton-S strain, which have a known genome size of 176.4 Mb, were processed using the same methodology. Flow cytometry analysis indicated that the DNA content in the nuclei of male U. dermestoides was 1.26 times greater than that of D. melanogaster, suggesting an estimated genome size of approximately 221.39 ± 3.74 Mb (Figure S1).

Genomic DNA and RNA sequencing

For Illumina sequencing, the genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated from approximately seven male U. dermestoides pupae by employing the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA). The purity of the gDNA was evaluated using a NanoPhotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA), while the quantity was determined utilizing a Qubit® 4.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA). Subsequently, the gDNA underwent fragmentation to achieve an approximate size of 350 base pairs with the assistance of the Annoroad® Universal DNA Fragmentase Kit V2.0. A paired-end gDNA library was generated using the Annoroad® Universal DNA Library Prep Kit (V2.0) following the prescribed protocol. Sequencing was conducted by Annoroad Gene Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd. on an Illumina HiSeq X-Ten platform (San Diego, CA, USA), resulting in the production of paired-end 150-bp reads. We obtained 27.69 Gb Illumina short reads (Table 1).

For long-read sequencing, the genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated from approximately seven male U. dermestoides pupae using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN). Following the evaluation of purity and quantity, the integrity of the genomic DNA (gDNA) was assessed using pulsed field gel electrophoresis with the Bio-Rad CHEF Mapper XA (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Each gDNA sample (15 µg) was fragmented into ~15 Kb pieces using g-Tubes (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA) at 2,000 rcf. The resulting fragments were purified using 0.45 × AMPure PB beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) and size-selected (15–18 Kb) using the Sage ELF system (Sage Science, Beverly, MA, USA). A SMRTbell library was prepared using specific primers, Sequel II DNA polymerase, and SMRTbell templates, utilizing the Pacific Biosciences SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0. Sequencing was performed by Annoroad Gene Technology on the Sequel II System (PacBio, Menlo Park, CA, USA), generating 8 M SMRT cells. Self-correction with Canu (v2.2)16 resulted in 17.33 Gb of clean reads (Table 1).

For Hi-C sequencing, the genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from the cephalothoracic tissue of two male U. dermestoides pupae and utilized to construct a Hi-C library following a standard protocol17. Briefly, the nuclear DNA was fixed with formaldehyde and then digested with DpnII (New England Biolabs, UK). Biotinylated nucleotides were added to the ends of the fragmented DNA, and DNA fragments of approximately 500 bp were collected through enrichment and size selection. Sequencing was conducted by Annoroad Gene Technology on the Illumina HiSeq X-Ten platform, generating paired-end 150-bp reads that yielded 35.41 Gb of raw Hi-C data (Table 1).

For transcriptome sequencing, total RNA was extracted from various developmental stages, including eggs, larvae (1st to 13th instar), male pupae, female adults, and male adults, respectively. To create a unified RNA sample for the larvae, equal amounts of RNA from all larval stages were pooled. RNA quality was assessed using a Bioanalyzer, while quantity was measured via spectrophotometry. Subsequently, RNA sequencing libraries for each developmental stage were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Library Preparation Kit. The resulting cDNA libraries underwent validation for quality and quantity through quantitative PCR (qPCR) and Bioanalyzer analysis. Sequencing of the libraries was performed by Annoroad Gene Technology on an Illumina HiSeq X-Ten platform, generating paired-end 150-bp reads. A total of 84.35 Gb transcriptome reads was obtained (Table 1).

Estimation of genomic characteristics

The Illumina raw reads were subjected to quality filtering via fastp (v0.23.2)18 to eliminate: (1) reads displaying adapter contamination (>5 bp of adapter sequence); (2) reads exhibiting low sequencing quality (≥15% of bases with Q-values < 19); (3) reads enriched in unknown bases (N bases > 5%); and (4) reads for which the paired counterpart was discarded during the previous steps. Subsequent genome survey and K-mer analysis, utilizing Jellyfish (v1.0.0)19 and GCE (v1.0.2)20 estimated the genome size of U. dermestoides to be 238.13 Mb, with a repeat sequence content of 49.06% and a heterozygosity rate of 0.61% at K = 17 (Table S1 and Figure S2).

Genome assembly

For genome assembly, three long-read genome assembly programs, Canu (v2.2)16, FALCON (v0.3.0)21, and SmartDenovo (v1.0)22, were employed to assemble draft genomes from PacBio and Illumina reads. The assemblies were compared based on contig N50 length, assembly size, and completeness, which was evaluated using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) (v5.4.5)23 and the insect database (insect_odb10). The SmartDenovo assembly exhibited the longest contig N50 length and the highest BUSCO score, and was therefore chosen for subsequent analyses. The clean Illumina short reads were utilized to polish the assembly twice with Pilon (v1.23)24. The final draft genome was 276.49 Mb in size, comprising 178 contigs with an N50 length of 9.83 Mb. Quality assessment revealed that 99.16% of the Illumina short reads uniquely mapped to the assembly, and the genome coverage rate was 99.97%, indicating a high-quality assembled genome (Figure S2).

To further anchor the genome assembly into chromosome-scale linkage groups, Hi-C analysis was performed. After applying quality filtering to the reads, as previously described for Illumina short-read sequencing data, the Hi-C clean reads were mapped to the draft genome using BWA (v0.6.2)25. Clean paired-end reads that uniquely mapped to the draft genome were selected based on their proximity to restriction sites identified from the Hi-C data. Scaffolding was performed by clustering the reads using a combination of ALLHIC (v1.0)26, 3D-DNA (branch 201008)27, Juicer (v1.6)28, and Juicebox (v2.20)29. The validity of the scaffold arrangement was assessed by examining the interaction strengths between pairs of reads. The order of the scaffolds was meticulously reviewed and manually adjusted, with orientations assigned to each cluster group.

In total, 95 contigs, representing 95.16% of the draft genome assembly, were oriented and anchored onto nine chromosomes, achieving a scaffold N50 value of 28.62 Mb (Fig. 1a and Table 2). The completeness of the genome was evaluated using BUSCO analysis, which identified 99.62% of universally conserved genes, with 97.3% being single-copy genes and 2.31% represented as duplicates (Table S2). These results strongly indicate that the anchored genome assembly is highly complete and of sufficiently high quality for subsequent detailed analyses.

Analysis of sexual chromosome

The analysis conducted with Satsuma (v2.0)30 assessed genome-wide synteny between U. dermestoides and the genomes of Tribolium castaneum (accession number GCF_000002335.3, karyotype = 9 + XY) and Aethina tumida (accession number GCF_024364675.1, karyotype = 6 + XY). The collinearity plot produced using Circos (v0.69)31 revealed strong collinearity between the ChrX chromosome of U. dermestoides and the X chromosomes of both T. castaneum and A. tumida (Fig. 2). Additionally, significant collinearity was noted between the ChrY chromosome of U. dermestoides and the Y chromosome of A. tumida.

Genome-wide collinearity analysis of different species by the Satusam software. (a) Collinearity between Ulomoides dermestoides and Tribolium castaneum. (b) Collinearity between Ulomoides dermestoides and Aethina tumida. (c) Collinearity between Tribolium castaneum and Aethina tumida.

These findings strongly indicate the presence of two sex chromosomes, X and Y, in the genome assembly of U. dermestoides. This inference is further supported by the conserved synteny observed between the respective sex chromosomes across these beetle species, highlighting the evolutionary relationships and structural similarities among them.

Repeats prediction

Known tandem repeats in the draft genome assemblies were identified using Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF) (trf409)32. Novel repeat sequences were detected with RepeatModeler (v2.0.4)33, which includes both RECON (v1.08)34 and RepeatScout (v1.0.6)35. Transposable elements (TEs) were predicted using rmblast (v2.14.0)36, and homology searches against the RepBase (v26.03 library)37 were conducted using RepeatMasker (v4.1.5)36. This combined approach of ab initio prediction and homology comparisons allowed for the annotation of approximately 48.11% of the peanut beetle genome as repeat elements (Fig. 1b). Among these, short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs) represented 0.06%, long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs) 3.87%, long terminal repeats (LTRs) 0.56%, and DNA transposons 2.07%. Notably, 40.40% of the repeat sequences were classified as unclassified (Table 3).

Genome annotation

Protein-coding genes were annotated using a combination of transcriptomic data, homology searching, and ab initio prediction methods. For homology-based annotation, reference protein sequences from 21 Coleoptera species were downloaded from the NCBI database (Table S3) and aligned against the U. dermestoides genome using TBLASTN (v2.2.29+) with an E-value cutoff of 1e-5. All BLAST hits were concatenated using Solar software (v0.9.6). Genomic regions corresponding to 1 Kb upstream and downstream of each candidate gene were extracted for predicting exact gene structures using GeneWise (v2.4.1)38, leading to the creation of a “Homology set”.

In the transcriptome-based annotation, raw transcriptomic reads were aligned to the genome utilizing HISAT2 (v2.2.1)39, and transcripts were assembled with StringTie (v2.2.1)40, forming the “RNAseq set”. For ab initio gene prediction, Augustus (v3.2.3)41, GlimmerHMM (v3.0.4)42, and SNAP (v2013-11-29)43 were employed independently on the repeat-masked genome. Parameters for these tools were trained using gene models with intact open reading frames (ORFs) from the RNAseq set, as identified by Transdecoder (v5.7.1)44. All gene models were integrated into a consensus gene set using EVidenceModeler (v2.1.0)45, with weights assigned to each evidence type as follows: RNAseq set > Homology set > Augustus > SNAP = GlimmerHMM. Genes encoding proteins shorter than 50 amino acids, supported only by ab initio predictions, and with low expression levels (<1.0), were systematically filtered out.

Non-coding RNA annotation

Three classes of ncRNAs—transfer RNA (tRNA), ribosomal RNA (rRNA), and small nuclear RNA (snRNA)—were thoroughly annotated in the draft genome analysis, following the exclusion of protein-coding genes, other ncRNA types, and repeat sequences. tRNA genes were identified using tRNAscan-SE version 2.046 with parameters optimized for eukaryotes, whereas rRNA genes were classified via BLASTN searches against draft genome fragments, utilizing rRNA sequences from various invertebrate species as queries with an E-value threshold of <1E-5. For snRNA gene identification, INFERNAL47 was employed alongside the Rfam database (release 14.9)48. Collectively, these analyses revealed a total of 15,553 protein-coding genes, 45 small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) genes, 286 tRNA genes, 373 rRNA genes, and 67 microRNA (miRNA) genes (Table 4).

Data Records

The Illumina, PacBio, and Hi-C sequencing data used for the genome assembly have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession numbers SRR2981605449, SRR2981605350, and SRR2981605251, with the associated BioProject accession number PRJNA113533552. The transcriptome sequencing data used for genome annotation are also available in the SRA database under accession numbers SRR29816047 through SRR2981605153–57. The chromosomal assembly has been deposited in GenBank under accession number GCA_040954815.158. Additionally, the genome assembly and annotated genes have been made available in the Figshare repository59. The data are also available at http://bioinformatics.cjlu.edu.cn/db/uders.

Technical Validation

The genome assembly measures 253.38 Mb with a scaffold N50 value of 28.62 Mb. This size is consistent with the genome size estimated by flow cytometry (Figure S1) and the K-mer analysis (Figure S2). The Hi-C heatmap displayed a well-organized interaction pattern along the diagonal and in the regions surrounding chromosome inversions (Fig. 1a), which supports the accuracy of the chromosome assembly. BUSCO assessment identified 99.62% of universally conserved genes based on the insecta_odb10 database (Table 2, Table S2), indicating that the U. dermestoides genome assembly is remarkably complete.
