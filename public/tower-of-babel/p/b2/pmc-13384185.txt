Spatial architecture of tertiary lymphoid structures represents an independent prognostic dimension in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Wei Liu, Chunbin Zhu, Zihang Cui, Jieyi Shi, Boan Zhang, Anan Xiong, Liming Wang, Qiang Gao, Guangyu Ding, Xiyang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsTertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) are associated with heterogeneous outcomes in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), but whether their anatomical context determines clinical impact remains unknown. We hypothesized that spatial compartmentalization of TLS influences their prognostic significance.MethodsWe developed SpatialDecoder, a deep learning pipeline that simultaneously segments TLS, classifies three maturation subtypes (Agg, Fol I, Fol II), and assigns spatial compartments (intratumoral, peritumoral, capsular), achieving accurate tissue segmentation (mean Dice similarity coefficient (DSC)=0.86), TLS segmentation (DSC=0.8609), and subtyping (macro Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC)=0.8810). Applied to 1188 resected HCC patients, it enabled large-scale spatial TLS mapping.ResultsSpatial mapping revealed a prognostic dichotomy: higher intratumoral TLS density independently predicted prolonged overall survival, whereas higher extratumoral (peritumoral and capsular) TLS density correlated with increased recurrence risk. Based on TLS distribution, we defined four spatial immune phenotypes: TLS-Enriched (high intra/low extra), TLS-Balanced (high/high), TLS-Excluded (low intra/high extra), and TLS-Deficient (low/low). These phenotypes stratified patients into a survival gradient (log-rank p<0.0001): TLS-Enriched conferred the best outcome (HR = 0.48), TLS-Excluded the worst (HR=1.80), with intermediate prognosis for the other two phenotypes. Multivariable analysis indicated independent prognostic value. Transcriptomic profiling uncovered distinct immune mechanisms across the four phenotypes.ConclusionsThis study establishes TLS spatial context as a determinant of their dualistic role in HCC immunity, providing a large-scale demonstration of opposite prognostic roles based on TLS location. The SpatialDecoder pipeline and four-phenotype framework transform TLS assessment from a binary metric into a spatially informed approach for postoperative risk stratification.Keywords: Hepatocellular Carcinoma, Tumor microenvironment - TME, Biomarker, Tumor infiltrating lymphocyte - TIL, Immunotherapy

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) poses a global health challenge, with rising incidence and persistently poor outcomes for advanced disease.1,3 This underscores the need to move beyond tumor-centric paradigms and decipher the biology of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), a key regulator of disease progression and therapeutic response.4 5

Within the TIME, tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) have emerged as ectopic hubs for the coordination of adaptive anti-tumor immunity.4 6 These organized lymphocytic aggregates exist along a histopathological maturation spectrum: from early lymphocyte clusters to fully formed germinal centers. TLS can be categorized into aggregates (TLS Agg), primary follicles (TLS-Fol-I), and secondary follicles (TLS-Fol-II) according to their maturation stages. This morphological continuum was first linked to improved clinical outcomes in studies7 and is now recognized as a hallmark of their capacity to generate potent, antigen-specific immune responses.4 The prognostic and predictive value of mature TLS is supported by evidence across diverse cancers, including melanoma,6 HCC,8 and sarcoma.9 Together, this convergent evidence4 10 positions mature TLS as a biomarker of favorable prognosis and enhanced response to immune checkpoint blockade, with predictive value that may be comparable to conventional markers like PD-L1.11

However, this paradigm is now being re-evaluated. A question persists: if TLS are universally beneficial, why do many patients with TLS-positive tumors still experience disease progression? Growing evidence reveals that TLS function is not intrinsic but is influenced by spatial context: their precise anatomical location and organizational architecture within the tumor-stroma continuum. This location-dependent functionality creates a prognostic dichotomy: while intratumoral TLS correlate with reduced recurrence in HCC8 and longer survival in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma,12 peritumoral TLS can signal adverse outcomes, as seen in breast cancer.13 Mechanistically, TLS can act as local immune amplifiers, sustaining T-cell function through structured interactions and chemokine-mediated recruitment,14 15 yet under specific conditions, they may nurture tumor progenitor cells.16 Thus, TLS embody a context-dependent functional duality.4 17 This evidence renders the mere presence or density of TLS an insufficient clinical biomarker, shifting the challenge to understanding its spatial architecture.12

The field of computational pathology offers a toolkit to decode this spatial complexity. The discipline has evolved from early quantitative image analysis18,21 into an era defined by deep learning and foundation models trained on millions of histopathology images.22 While these advances have enhanced whole-slide diagnosis and molecular prediction,23 24 a mesoscale gap persists. Most efforts are polarized between macroscopic (slide-level) and microscopic (cellular) analysis, lacking the granularity to precisely segment, subtype, and perform spatial analytics on defined tissue entities like TLS.25 26 Existing computational approaches for TLS analysis often rely on multi-stage manual workflows or fail to capture the subtype-specific spatial biology that likely defines their function.27 This creates a challenge: we recognize the importance of TLS spatial architecture yet lack the tools to map it systematically from histopathology images.

Several deep learning models have been developed for TLS detection in gastric,28 lung,29 colorectal,30 and pan-cancer settings.31 32 Although these tools have advanced automated TLS analysis, their application to HCC is hindered by two limitations. First, they do not assign TLS to specific spatial compartments (intratumoral, peritumoral, or capsular), despite the established prognostic significance of TLS location in HCC. Second, although a few models (eg, Wu et al28) can classify TLS maturity, they do not assign TLS to specific spatial compartments, and most lack the full three-tier output (Agg, Fol-I, Fol-II) in a unified, HCC-optimized pipeline. Furthermore, while numerous histopathological studies have linked TLS presence to HCC prognosis, an automated pipeline that systematically integrates both spatial compartment and maturation subtype to produce a potentially informative classification has not been previously reported. The present study provides such a pipeline.

To address these gaps, we developed SpatialDecoder, a deep-learning pipeline that enables compartment-aware, subtype-specific, and HCC-optimized TLS quantification. Applying this model to 1188 resected HCC patients, we constructed a high-throughput spatial TLS atlas and demonstrated the independent prognostic value of capsular TLS density.

We present a comprehensive deep-learning framework designed to: (1) establish an automated pipeline for TLS segmentation, compartment delineation (intratumoral/peritumoral/capsular), and TLS subtype classification; (2) leverage this pipeline to map the anatomical distribution and organizational patterns of TLS subtypes within the tumor-stroma-normal tissue triad; (3) define and validate spatially derived immune phenotypes that quantitatively link TLS architecture to clinical outcome. By integrating advanced computer vision with spatial biology analytics, this work aims to establish tissue context as an independent layer of prognostic information, paving the way for next-generation, microenvironment-informed patient stratification. Therefore, we hypothesize that the spatial localization of TLS, rather than their mere presence, is important in determining their immune role and prognosis.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed to test the hypothesis that the spatial localization of TLS, rather than their mere presence, influences clinical prognosis in HCC. This hypothesis is particularly relevant given the identification of an immune-related subclass of HCC characterized by lymphoid infiltration.11 We implemented a three-stage analytical workflow: (1) development of deep learning models for tissue compartment (figure 1a) and TLS segmentation; (2) classification of TLS into maturation subtypes (Agg, Fol-I, Fol-II) (figure 1b); and (3) derivation and validation of patient-level spatial immune phenotypes based on compartment-specific TLS metrics.

Patient cohorts

Discovery cohort (ZS cohort: Zhongshan Hospital cohort)

The primary cohort initially consisted of 1200 consecutive patients with HCC who underwent curative resection at Zhongshan Hospital between 2009–2013, with 2500 corresponding H&E-stained whole-slide images (WSIs). After a predefined quality control process, excluding cases with insufficient tissue, staining artifacts, or missing clinical annotations, 1188 patients with 2328 WSIs (figure 1c) were retained for TLS segmentation and classification (see online supplemental figure 1a).

Among these, 240 WSIs from 200 patients were exhaustively annotated by pathologists to serve as the ground truth for deep learning model development and validation. These 240 WSIs were used for both tissue compartment segmentation (annotations of intratumoral parenchyma, peritumoral stroma, and fibrous capsule) and TLS instance segmentation (annotations of TLS boundaries and subtypes). The 240 annotated WSIs were randomly partitioned at the patient level into three sets: a training set (70% of patients, 140 patients, 162 WSIs), a validation set (15% of patients, 30 patients, 45 WSIs) for hyperparameter tuning and model selection, and a test set (15% of patients, 30 patients, 33 WSIs) for final evaluation. All metrics reported in the results are derived from this independent test set unless otherwise specified. The remaining 2088 WSIs from 988 patients were used for locked-model inference to derive patient-level spatial phenotypes.

External validation cohort (The Cancer Genome Atlas-Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma)

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (LIHC) cohort33 34 was used for independent validation. From 360 patients with 374 H&E-stained WSIs, quality control yielded 320 patients with 330 evaluable slides (see online supplemental figure 1b). The same quality control, patch extraction, model inference, and phenotype definition procedures as applied to the discovery cohort were performed. Due to the nature of TCGA tissue sections (which often focus on the tumor core and may lack complete peritumoral or capsular areas), extratumoral compartment analysis was feasible only in a subset of patients with sufficient surrounding tissue.

Image preprocessing and quality control

WSIs were originally scanned at 40× magnification (1 µm=5 pixels; 0.2 µm/pixel). Manual annotations were performed on these original 40× images. For model development and analysis, the WSIs were linearly down-sampled by a factor of 4 to an equivalent 10×magnification, yielding a resolution of 0.8 µm/pixel. All subsequent preprocessing, patch extraction, and segmentation were carried out on these down-sampled images.

The down-sampled WSIs were tiled into non-overlapping patches of 512×512 pixels, each covering a physical area of 0.4096 mm×0.4096 mm (≈0.168 mm²). Patches with <10% tissue area (<26 214 pixels) were discarded as background or scant tissue. At the whole-slide level, slides with fewer than eight valid patches after filtering were excluded during initial quality control. This threshold ensures that only slides with tissue for TLS and tumor parenchyma assessment are retained. No additional minimum tumor size was imposed at the slide level, as the model processes the entire slide and integrates patch-level predictions. WSIs with artifacts (eg, bubbles, uneven cutting, fading) were also excluded based on pathology review.

SpatialDecoder framework overview

We developed SpatialDecoder (figure 2), an integrated computational pipeline comprising three modules: (1) a segmentation module named SpatialDecoder-Seg, built on nnU-Net with two targeted modifications (detailed in the next section), (2) a TLS phenotyping module that integrates handcrafted morphological features with UNI-derived deep representations using a dual-branch ensemble classifier (eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and fine-tuned UNI) and a Dynamic Selection Voting (DSV) algorithm; and (3) a spatial immune phenotype analysis module (figure 3). This pipeline enables quantitative mapping of TLS distribution and maturation, forming the basis for defining patient-level spatial immune phenotypes. The associated code is available at https://github.com/welliam126/SpatialDecoder.

Segmentation model development

A segmentation framework was built on the adaptive nnU-Net architecture35 with two targeted modifications to improve delineation of tissues and TLS, especially where boundaries are indistinct. Both modifications were integrated via a custom trainer.

Architecture modifications

First, a convolutional block attention module (CBAM)36 was inserted after the encoder to sequentially recalibrate channel-wise and spatial features. Second, the default loss function was replaced with a boundary-weighted Dice loss, which assigns higher penalties to misclassifications near edges, thereby forcing the model to focus on boundary regions.

Tissue compartment segmentation model

A model was trained to delineate three anatomical compartments: intratumoral parenchyma, peritumoral stroma (defined as the 2 mm band outward from the tumor boundary, following Ding et al12), and fibrous capsule (if present). Ground truth annotations for these compartments were provided by pathologists on the same 240 WSIs used for TLS annotation. The model used a combined Dice and Focal loss37 to address class imbalance.

TLS instance segmentation model

A separate model was optimized for TLS identification. To account for histological variability, we employed Macenko stain normalization,38 an adaptive class-balancing loss, and extensive data augmentation (including elastic deformation, random rotation ±30°, horizontal/vertical flipping, and brightness/contrast adjustment). The same CBAM and boundary-weighted Dice loss were applied. For training the TLS segmentation model, only patches containing at least one annotated TLS were used (to mitigate extreme class imbalance); for inference, all patches were processed, and the results were aggregated per patient.

Inference and region of interest-based TLS quantification

ROI definition

After model training, the tissue compartment segmentation model was first applied to each WSI to generate masks for intratumoral, peritumoral, and capsular compartments. These masks served as the region of interest (ROI) for TLS detection and abundance calculation; no fixed size or manually drawn ROI was used.

TLS density calculation

The TLS segmentation model was subsequently applied exclusively within these ROIs to identify TLS instances. To ensure density estimation stability, compartments with an area <0.05 mm2 (approximately 78 000 pixels at 0.8 µm/pixel resolution) were excluded from compartment-specific TLS analyses. This threshold was chosen based on the patch size used for segmentation (≈0.168 mm² per patch) and on stability analyses showing that density estimates became more variable below this area.

TLS density within each valid compartment was calculated as the total cross-sectional area of identified TLS normalized to the effective compartment area (ie, after excluding compartments <0.05 mm2), reported as TLS area per mm2. All area measurements are based on the down-sampled resolution (1 mm2=1 562 500 pixels2).

TLS phenotyping and spatial immune phenotype derivation

TLS definition and annotation criteria

For model training and ground truth generation, TLS were defined according to established histopathological criteria for H&E-stained sections: dense, organized lymphocyte aggregates with a clear contour, containing at least 50 lymphocytes. Diffuse, unorganized lymphocytic infiltrates were excluded. Based on maturation, TLS were categorized into three subtypes:

Agg (aggregates): loose clusters of lymphocytes without evidence of follicular organization; Fol-I (primary follicle-like): defined B-cell areas with early follicular formation but no distinct germinal center; Fol-II (secondary follicle-like): well-developed germinal center comprising proliferating blasts (see online supplemental figure 2).

Because our pipeline operates on routine H&E-stained WSIs without immunohistochemistry (IHC), TLS identification relies solely on morphological criteria. This approach follows prior validation studies showing that H&E-based TLS criteria (lymphocyte count ≥50, contour sharpness, germinal center morphology) correlate with IHC-confirmed TLS (eg, CD20+ B-cell follicles, CD3+ T-cell zones, CD21+ follicular dendritic cell networks).8 11

All annotations were performed on H&E-stained WSIs by two experienced pathologists independently. Any disagreements between the two annotators were adjudicated by a third senior pathologist to reach a final consensus. Interobserver agreement between the two primary annotators was assessed on a random subset of 80 WSIs using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), yielding a mean DSC of 0.843 (SD=0.04), indicating consistency. The same definitions were applied when labeling training patches and validating the model’s outputs.

TLS maturation state classification

To classify TLS into three maturation subtypes (Agg, Fol-I, Fol-II), we developed a dual-branch ensemble classifier. One branch uses an XGBoost model trained on eight handcrafted morphometric and textural features; the other branch uses a fine-tuned UNI foundation model (ViT-L) with domain-adaptive pretraining. Predictions are fused via a DSV algorithm. DSV retrieves the K=50 most morphologically similar patches from a validation set (by cosine similarity of UNI embeddings), evaluates each branch’s instantaneous F1-score for each subtype on those similar patches, and computes dynamic branch weights using a softmax function with selectivity parameter β=5.0. The final subtype is the class with the highest weighted probability. Full details are provided in online supplemental methods S3.2.

Derivation of patient-level spatial immune phenotypes

Based on the two spatial prognostic axes identified in the Results (intratumoral TLS density and extratumoral TLS density), we dichotomized each axis into “high” and “low” using the cohort median as a cut-off. The combination of the two binary axes yields four spatial immune phenotypes:

TLS-Enriched (high intratumoral/low extratumoral).

TLS-Enriched (high intratumoral/low extratumoral).

TLS-Balanced (high intratumoral/high extratumoral).

TLS-Balanced (high intratumoral/high extratumoral).

TLS-Excluded (low intratumoral/high extratumoral).

TLS-Excluded (low intratumoral/high extratumoral).

TLS-Deficient (low intratumoral/low extratumoral).

TLS-Deficient (low intratumoral/low extratumoral).

This rule-based framework is not a product of unsupervised clustering but a direct synthesis of two independently validated prognostic features. Its interpretability and reproducibility make it suitable for clinical workflows.

Validation framework

A discovery-validation strategy was employed to ensure generalizability.

Model locking: All models (for segmentation and classification) were definitively fixed (parameters, thresholds) using only discovery cohort data before application to the independent validation cohort.

Procedural consistency: All analytical procedures, statistical methods, and phenotype definitions established during the discovery phase were pre-specified and applied identically to the independent TCGA-LIHC validation cohort (n=320).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R (V.4.1.2). Survival endpoints were overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS). Group comparisons used Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis, or Fisher’s exact tests. Survival associations were assessed using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models. Kaplan-Meier curves were compared with the log-rank test. Correlation analyses used Spearman’s method. A two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Patients lost to follow-up were censored at the date of last contact.

Results

To systematically investigate the role of TLS spatial architecture in HCC, we first developed and validated a computational pipeline for TLS and tissue segmentation, along with TLS maturation subtyping. Leveraging this pipeline, we then quantified the anatomical distribution of TLS, defined spatial immune phenotypes, and established their divergent prognostic value. Finally, we indicated the generalizability of the analytical framework and the biological findings in an independent external cohort.

Development and validation of an integrated computational pipeline

A robust TLS segmentation model

To determine the optimal microscope magnification for TLS segmentation, we trained and evaluated models at multiple levels (4×, 10×, 20×, 40×). Based on performance on a validation set, the 10×magnification model achieved the highest DSC (0.8613), compared with models at other magnifications (see online supplemental table 1). The final model, trained at 10×magnification, was evaluated on an independent test set, achieving a DSC of 0.8609 (see online supplemental table 2). Consequently, this 10×model was employed for all subsequent large-scale TLS identification.

Comparison with a public pan-cancer TLS segmentation model

Comparison with a public pan-cancer TLS segmentation model. We compared our SpatialDecoder with the pan-cancer binary segmentation model of Chen et al32 using their provided inference pipeline and pre-trained weights on our internal HCC test set (n=100 patients). On this test set, the Chen model achieved a mean Dice coefficient of 0.7902 (SD=0.0186), while our SpatialDecoder achieved 0.8595 (SD=0.0140). A paired t-test showed a significant difference in favor of our model (mean difference=0.0693, 95% CI 0.0686 to 0.0699, p<0.0001). We acknowledge that Chen’s model was trained on pan-cancer data and not specifically optimized for HCC; therefore, this comparison primarily illustrates the performance gain achievable with HCC-specific training. Distributions (online supplemental figure 3a), the difference with its 95% CI (online supplemental figure 3b), and representative examples (online supplemental figure 3c) are shown in online supplemental material.

Accurate segmentation of spatial compartments

To enable compartment-specific TLS analysis, we developed a modified nnU-Net for delineating intratumoral, peritumoral, and capsular regions on H&E-stained WSIs. Compared with the standard nnU-Net, our model achieved higher average Dice (validation: 0.8783 vs 0.8322; test: 0.8600 vs 0.8088) (see online supplemental tables 3,4).

An ensemble classifier for robust TLS maturation subtyping

The ensemble model performed on both validation (see online supplemental tables 5–7) and test datasets (table 1). On the test set, it yielded higher AUROC values than both Uni-ViT (see online supplemental table 8) and XGBoost (see online supplemental table 9) across all three TLS subtypes. For example, for the Fol-II subtype, the ensemble achieved an AUROC of 0.8892, higher than 0.8754 (Uni-ViT) and 0.8413 (XGBoost). These results indicate that the ensemble outperformed either single-model approach. Analysis of the DSV weights demonstrated the mechanism’s adaptive weighting: XGBoost dominated for loose TLS and Uni-ViT for compact follicular TLS (see online supplemental table 10).

The spatial architecture of TLS reveals a prognostic dichotomy

Using this pipeline, we uncovered the biological and clinical significance of TLS spatial distribution.

Anatomical distribution and clinicopathological correlates of TLS

Leveraging our pipeline, we first mapped the anatomical landscape of TLS. Spatial analysis revealed a compartmentalization: TLS density was lower in intratumoral regions compared with peritumoral and capsular regions (figure 4a). Intratumoral TLS were often scattered and isolated, whereas capsular TLS frequently formed linear aggregates along the tumor border (figure 4b). While TLS counts across compartments were positively correlated (figure 4c), the correlation for mature TLS-Fol-II subtypes was weaker, and their relative abundance varied by location (figure 4d). This spatial patterning was associated with clinical factors: TLS distribution showed associations with hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, liver cirrhosis, and Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage (figure 4e), suggesting that the local microenvironment shapes TLS architecture. These spatial patterns formed the basis for a subsequent classification system (schematized in figure 4f).

A spatial prognostic dichotomy: intratumoral versus extratumoral TLS

We next asked whether this spatial compartmentalization had functional consequences for patient outcome. Univariate survival analysis uncovered an opposing prognostic relationship. Higher density of TLS within the tumor parenchyma was significantly associated with prolonged OS and RFS (figure 5). In contrast, a higher density of TLS in the combined peritumoral and capsular (extratumoral) compartments was associated with an increased risk of recurrence and shorter OS (figure 5). Multivariable Cox regression analysis showed that TLS in each compartment had independent prognostic significance (see online supplemental tables 11,12). This spatial prognostic dichotomy indicates that the pro-tumor or anti-tumor role of TLS is intrinsically linked to its anatomical niche. These findings prompted us to develop a spatial classification system based on intratumoral and extratumoral TLS densities (see figure 4f for a schematic overview).

A unified spatial framework for patient stratification

Derivation of four spatial immune phenotypes

We synthesized these two orthogonal prognostic axes into a unified spatial framework (figure 4f). We applied a rule-based definition (as described in the Methods), dichotomizing patients as having high or low TLS density in the intratumoral and extratumoral compartments based on cohort medians. The combination yields four spatial immune phenotypes: TLS-Enriched (high intra/low extra), TLS-Balanced (high/high), TLS-Excluded (low intra/high extra), and TLS-Deficient (low/low).

The spatial immune phenotypes stratify patient survival

We first evaluated the prognostic significance of this spatial classification framework within the discovery cohort. The four TLS immunophenotypes stratified patients into prognostic groups, revealing a survival gradient dictated by TLS spatial organization (log-rank test, p<0.0001 for both OS and RFS, figure 6a). Patients with the TLS-Enriched phenotype experienced the best outcomes (OS: HR=0.48, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.69, p<0.001; RFS: HR=0.56, 95% CI 0.43 to 0.73, p<0.001). In contrast, those with the TLS-Excluded phenotype had worse survival (OS: HR=1.80, 95% CI 1.42 to 2.29, p<0.001; RFS: HR=1.43, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.75, p=0.001). The TLS-Balanced and TLS-Deficient groups exhibited intermediate prognosis. Multivariable Cox regression indicates that this spatial immune classification was an independent prognostic factor, even after adjusting for all clinicopathological risk factors (see online supplemental tables 13,14).

Transcriptomic features of the four spatial immune phenotypes

Using transcriptomic data from TCGA, we investigated the potential biological mechanisms underlying the spatial immune phenotypes (figure 6b). The TLS-enriched subtype was characterized by enrichment of adaptive immune cells, including CD4+ effector memory T cells and resting dendritic cells, alongside upregulated expression of memory/naive T cell markers. In parallel, pro-survival and metabolic pathways, including Phosphoinositide 3-kinase/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K-AKT-mTOR) signaling, sphingolipid metabolism, lysosomal function, and autophagy regulation, were downregulated. Collectively, these molecular features suggested that the TLS-enriched subtype exhibited both active immune infiltration and concomitant suppression of tumor-supportive metabolic pathways.

The TLS-balanced subtype demonstrated a finely poised equilibrium between immune activation and immune suppression. On one hand, immune activation-associated genes (CD8A, GZMA, GZMB, PRF1, IFNG) were universally upregulated, and pathways such as T cell receptor (TCR) signaling, B cell receptor (BCR) signaling, and interferon (IFN)-α/γ responses were activated. On the other hand, multiple immune checkpoint molecules (CTLA4, PDCD1, CD274, LAG3, PDCD1LG2) were coordinately upregulated, accompanied by enrichment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and co-inhibitory molecules.

The TLS-deficient subtype exhibited immune silencing. Resting dendritic cells and memory/naive T cells were absent, expression of the immune checkpoints CTLA4 and PDCD1 was diminished, and both TCR signaling and IFN-α/γ response pathways were suppressed. As a result, the overall immune microenvironment adopted a deficient phenotype, lacking effective immune priming and effector functions.

The TLS-excluded subtype was characterized by a rejection phenotype in which aberrant antigen presentation coexists with immune evasion. This subtype showed upregulated expression of antigen presentation-related genes (PSMD1, PSMD12, PSMD14, PSMD2); however, the cytotoxic molecules GZMA, GZMB, and PRF1 were consistently downregulated. Furthermore, expression of the immune checkpoint CD47 (a phagocytosis escape signal) was elevated, while pathways including PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling, sphingolipid metabolism, lysosomal function, and autophagy regulation were upregulated.

External validation of the spatial prognostic dichotomy

Having internally validated the prognostic power of the four-phenotype framework within the discovery cohort (figure 5b), we next aimed to externally validate the core biological principle (the spatial prognostic dichotomy) in an independent patient population. Analysis of the TCGA-LIHC cohort (320 patients) indicated that higher intratumoral TLS density was significantly associated with improved OS (p<0.05; see online supplemental figure 4; pipeline performance, online supplemental tables 15–17).

Because many TCGA WSIs do not contain complete peritumoral or capsular regions, tissue segmentation identified evaluable extratumoral compartments in only 126 of the 320 patients. Consequently, analyses of extratumoral TLS and the four spatial immune phenotypes were performed on this subset. Using the same rule-based classification, we observed consistent prognostic stratification (figure 6c), further supporting the generalizability of the spatial immune framework.

Discussion

This study establishes a spatial paradigm for deciphering anti-tumor immunity in HCC. We demonstrate that the clinical impact of TLS is dictated not by their mere presence, but by their precise anatomical coordinates within the tumor microenvironment. Through the development of a dedicated computational framework (SpatialDecoder), we decoded this spatial architecture and uncovered a prognostic dichotomy: intratumoral TLS density correlates with improved survival, whereas peri- and capsular TLS associate with worse outcomes. This discovery resolves a longstanding paradox in TLS biology, where their role has appeared context-dependent,39 and suggests that global TLS counts may be insufficient as a standalone biomarker. By synthesizing spatial and maturation metrics, we derive an integrative spatial immune phenotype system, which provides independent stratification of patient prognosis.

Comparison with existing deep learning-based TLS analysis tools

Several deep learning tools have been developed for TLS detection in gastric, lung, colorectal, and pan-cancer settings.28,32 However, to our knowledge, prior tools do not provide spatial compartment assignment (intratumoral, peritumoral, capsular) or full three-tier TLS subtype classification (Agg, Fol-I, Fol-II) in HCC: capabilities important for understanding the location- and maturation-dependent prognostic impact of TLS. Our SpatialDecoder fills this gap by delivering a unified pipeline that simultaneously segments TLS, classifies its maturity, and maps its spatial location. When directly benchmarked against the task-comparable pan-cancer model of Chen et al32 on our HCC test set, SpatialDecoder achieved significantly higher segmentation accuracy (Dice 0.8595 vs 0.7902, p<0.001). For models with different tasks (eg, patch-level classification, object detection), we provide a functional comparison table (see online supplemental table 18) to transparently illustrate their limitations. Together, these analyses demonstrate that our work provides spatial insights prior to TLS studies.

Comparison with prior prognostic TLS studies in HCC

Prior studies have established a link between TLS presence and HCC prognosis. Calderaro et al8 showed that intratumoral TLS reduce early recurrence risk. Zhang et al40 suggested peritumoral TLS might correlate with worse survival, but their analysis relied on semi-quantitative scoring in a modest-sized cohort (n=170) and lacked automated segmentation or subtype classification. In contrast, our study provides a demonstration of the full spatial dichotomy (intratumoral favorable vs extratumoral adverse) within the same large cohort with external validation, using a fully automated pipeline (SpatialDecoder) that achieves pixel-wise tissue compartment segmentation (DSC>0.85), TLS detection, three-tier subtype classification, and a rule-based four-phenotype system. (see online supplemental table 19)

The spatial prognostic dichotomy resolves a biological paradox and provides a unifying principle

Our central finding that TLS location confers opposing prognostic values provides a coherent explanatory framework for a biological paradox. This spatial logic aligns with and extends established concepts of TIME heterogeneity.41 42 The protective role of intratumoral TLS suggests they may constitute a functionally ‘hot’ and engaged immune niche within the tumor core. Conversely, the accumulation of TLS at the invasive margin, particularly in an immature state, may reflect an ‘immune-excluded’phenotype,42 where effector cells are physically or functionally restrained by a dense stromal barrier,43 or even a tumor-co-opted niche that inadvertently supports progression.16 This spatial dichotomy finds a parallel in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, where intratumoral and peritumoral TLS similarly harbor opposing prognostic values,12 suggesting a similar pattern in liver cancers.

Compartmentalized TLS architecture reflects functional niche specialization

Our high-resolution mapping reveals that TLS are not randomly distributed but occupy specialized anatomical niches, implying compartment-specific functions. The relative scarcity of intratumoral TLS may indicate their existence within an immunosuppressive core, while their accumulation along the tumor capsule, an invasion frontier, likely signifies a peripherally-focused, yet distinct, immune response, potentially modulated by stromal signals.5 15 44 The associations between specific TLS spatial patterns and key clinicopathological factors, such as HBV infection45 and liver cirrhosis,46 provide insight into their drivers, linking TLS formation to persistent antigenic stimulus and chronic inflammatory milieus within the broader cancer immunoediting framework.39 47

Molecular and cellular landscape underlying the four spatial immune phenotypes

This study provides a mechanistic explanation for the prognostic differences among the four immune phenotypes at the levels of gene expression, immune cell composition, and signaling pathway activity. The TLS-Enriched phenotype, characterized by concurrent enrichment of adaptive immune components and suppression of pro-tumor metabolic pathways, constitutes the biological basis for the most favorable prognosis. The TLS-Balanced phenotype exhibits a “steady state” in which immune activation coexists with upregulation of multiple immune checkpoints and MDSCs48; in this context, cytotoxic efficacy is constrained by negative feedback mechanisms, resulting in an intermediate prognosis. The TLS-Deficient phenotype demonstrates global immune quiescence, which may permit relatively indolent tumor progression, and is also associated with an intermediate prognosis. The TLS-Excluded phenotype, which presents a paradoxical phenotype: upregulation of antigen presentation occurs alongside a complete lack of cytotoxic function. This, together with CD47-mediated phagocytosis escape signaling49 and comprehensive activation of pathways such as PI3K-AKT-mTOR50 and autophagy,51 generates an immune-excluded microenvironment. This microenvironment is shaped by the coordinated actions of antigen exposure, phagocytic escape, and metabolic repulsion, thereby accounting for the poorest prognosis. The above landscape generates testable hypotheses for future immunotherapy studies: the TLS-Balanced phenotype may represent a population that could benefit from combination therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors, whereas the TLS-Excluded phenotype might require strategies such as CD47 blockade, inhibition of aberrant metabolic pathways,52 or restoration of cytotoxic function to overcome the immune exclusion barrier. These hypotheses warrant prospective validation in clinical trial cohorts.

Methodological robustness underscores biological validity and translational resilience

The SpatialDecoder pipeline, leveraging pathology foundation models,22 enabled this precise spatial decoding and represents a methodological advance beyond semi-quantitative pathology.23 While inter-institutional stain heterogeneity—a universal challenge in computational pathology—was quantified and mitigated, the core prognostic principle of intratumoral TLS benefit was validated in the independent TCGA cohort. This resilience underscores that the biological signal we uncovered transcends technical variability and highlights the pipeline’s potential for standardized, multicenter application.

Clinical applicability in the resection setting

SpatialDecoder is designed for use on routine H&E sections from resected HCC specimens, the standard of care for patients with BCLC 0/A/B disease who undergo curative-intent surgery. In this population, the derived spatial immune phenotypes provide an automated, objective risk stratification tool. Specifically, a TLS-Excluded classification indicates a >twofold fold increased risk of death and 48% higher recurrence risk, independent of traditional clinicopathological factors. This information may inform postoperative risk stratification and hypothesis generation for future studies. For instance, TLS-Excluded patients could be considered a high-risk subgroup for enrollment in adjuvant therapy trials, whereas TLS-Enriched patients might represent a lower-risk subgroup for de-escalation investigations. Prospective validation is required before clinical implementation. Although the current pipeline is not intended for biopsy samples (often obtained from advanced, unresectable HCC), future work will explore its adaptation to smaller tissue volumes.

Clinical implications and future immunotherapy studies

Our spatial immune phenotypes provide a hypothesis-generating framework for future immunotherapeutic studies. Based on the established association between TLS (particularly mature TLS) and response to immune checkpoint blockade,10 11 we hypothesize that patients with the TLS-Enriched phenotype may derive greater benefit from such therapies, whereas those with the TLS-Excluded phenotype might be more resistant and potentially require combination strategies. Prospective validation in dedicated clinical trial cohorts is required to test these hypotheses.

Limitations

First, the retrospective design and the use of H&E morphology alone precluded immunophenotypic characterization of TLS-infiltrating cell subsets beyond germinal center identification. Future studies employing multiplex IHC or spatial transcriptomics are needed to dissect the specific immune cell populations (eg, CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells, macrophages) and signaling pathways that underlie the opposite prognostic roles of intratumoral versus extratumoral TLS.

Second, detailed adjustment for antiviral treatments, including interferon-based regimens, was limited by the retrospective nature of the cohort; although sensitivity analyses excluding the few patients who received such therapy gave consistent results and forced inclusion of cirrhosis did not alter the HRs, we cannot completely exclude residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as exact fibrosis stage or modern direct-acting antivirals. Future prospective studies with detailed treatment histories are warranted to validate our findings.

Third, the external evaluation of extratumoral TLS was performed on a subset of 126 TCGA patients due to limited availability of complete peritumoral/capsular tissue in public datasets; although the results showed a consistent direction of effect, a larger external cohort is needed to fully validate the adverse prognostic impact of extratumoral TLS. Despite these limitations, our work provides a spatial framework that advances our understanding of the HCC immune landscape and delivers a practical tool for patient stratification, contributing to the evolving quantification of immune contexture in cancer.41 By distinguishing immune-competent from immune-altered microenvironments, our study charts a course toward more precise and effective immunomodulatory strategies in liver cancer.

Conclusions

This study establishes that the spatial organization of TLS, rather than their mere abundance, is an independent determinant of clinical outcomes in HCC. By developing and validating a computational pathology framework (SpatialDecoder), we have decoded this spatial architecture, revealing an anatomical logic with direct prognostic consequence: intratumoral TLSs correlate with improved survival, whereas peritumoral/capsular TLSs associate with adverse outcomes.

Integrating spatial location with TLS maturation state enabled the derivation of a robust, four-category spatial immune phenotype system. This system transcends conventional TLS quantification, identifying a distinct patient subgroup with the ‘TLS-Enriched’ phenotype, characterized by mature, follicular TLS within the tumor core, which experiences the most favorable prognosis.

Beyond providing a validated, quantitative pipeline for spatial tumor immunology, our work moves from a “bulk” to a “topographic” understanding of tumor immunity. It establishes a spatial atlas of the HCC immune microenvironment, directly linking quantitative tissue architecture to patient fate.

This atlas offers a prognostic tool and provides a rationale for future studies to test whether these spatial phenotypes could serve as candidate biomarkers requiring prospective validation. In doing so, our study supports the development of spatially resolved, microenvironment-directed clinical management in liver cancer.
