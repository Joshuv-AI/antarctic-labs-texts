Effect of the number and localization of implants placed in the atrophic mandible on the fragility of the symphysis region
Authors: Banu Ozveri Koyuncu, Fatma Merve Gokce, Meltem Ozden Yuce, Ayse Gozde Turk, Ugur Tekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImplant-supported overdenture prostheses are the preferred treatment option for rehabilitating patients with atrophic edentulous conditions. However, limited research has examined the impact of these implant applications on the structural endurance of the mandible, which is already compromised due to atrophy, particularly when subjected to frontal traumatic forces. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of traditional full dentures, two-implant, and three-implant-supported overdenture prosthesis systems in atrophic, fully edentulous mandibles on their resistance to frontal traumatic forces.MethodsA computer-generated model of an atrophic mandible was developed. Five different treatment models were simulated using computer software: traditional full dentures, two-implant-supported locator attachment overdenture prostheses, two-implant-supported bar attachment overdenture prostheses, three-implant-supported locator attachment overdenture prostheses, and three-implant-supported bar attachment overdenture prostheses. Static linear analyses were performed by applying a 2000-N force over a circular area (1 cm in diameter) perpendicular to the frontal plane from the symphysis region in all models. Principal stress and von Mises stress values on the mandible were subsequently analyzed.ResultsThe highest tensile stress was observed in the three-implant locator attachment-supported overdenture prosthesis model, whereas the lowest tensile stress was recorded in the conventional full denture model. The highest compressive stress was found in the three-implant bar attachment overdenture prosthesis model. The findings indicate that the use of locator attachments reduces the mandible’s resistance to both tensile and compressive stresses, whereas bar attachments provide a safer alternative by enhancing structural stability.ConclusionThe findings of the study underscore the importance of selecting an appropriate prosthesis type to optimize stress management and mitigate fracture risk in atrophic mandibular models. Further research is needed to evaluate the biomechanical strength of the mandible under various force conditions.

Introduction

Tooth loss can lead to several complications, including difficulties with speech, mastication, and ingestion. These issues can, in turn, negatively impact an individual’s social interactions, emotional well-being, and overall quality of life [1]. Given the significant consequences of edentulism, it is crucial to explore effective rehabilitation options. Conventional full dentures and bridges have long been used to restore both function and aesthetics [2]. However, an alternative treatment—implant-supported dentures—has gained prominence. Since Brånemark introduced the concept of osseointegration, implant dentistry has advanced rapidly, particularly in recent years, and now plays a key role in rehabilitating both partially and fully edentulous patients [3]. Currently, dental implants are the preferred treatment for edentulous arches due to their superior functional and aesthetic outcomes [4].

The global increase in life expectancy has resulted in a rapidly expanding elderly population. This demographic shift is attributed to greater access to information, technological advancements, and heightened awareness of self-care practices [5, 6]. Although older adults maintain levels of physical activity comparable to those of younger individuals, age-related physiological changes—including sensory impairments, reduced motor reflexes, and the use of assistive devices—heighten their risk of falls. Consequently, maxillofacial trauma has become increasingly prevalent in this population [7, 8].

Complete edentulism is a common condition among the geriatric population, often leading to jaw resorption. Aging-related arteriosclerotic changes cause the jawbones to become harder and more brittle, increasing the risk of fractures due to falls. The mandible is particularly vulnerable, exhibiting a two-to-threefold higher fracture risk than other facial bones. This heightened susceptibility is attributed to reduced bone support, anatomical structure, and the mandible’s prominent position [9].

The first documented mandibular fracture resulting from implant surgery was reported by Albreksson, with an incidence rate of only 0.2%. Implant placement can weaken bone integrity by disrupting its structure [10]. However, a review of the literature indicates a lack of studies investigating the effects of implants on jaw fractures in geriatric patients [11, 12].

Finite element analysis (FEA) is a computational technique that simulates biomechanical problems by breaking them down into smaller components and analyzing stress and strain in computer-aided design models. This technique provides valuable insights into oral rehabilitation and plays a crucial role in implant dentistry, particularly for cases unsuitable for clinical studies or in vitro experiments [13].

The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of implant number (two versus three) and attachment design (locator versus bar) on biomechanical stress distribution in Class II atrophic edentulous mandibles under frontal traumatic loading. To achieve this, five computer-generated finite element models were developed, representing various implant configurations within the interforaminal region. Each model was subjected to a standardized 2000 N frontal force to simulate trauma. By comparing stress and strain distributions, the study aimed to identify the most biomechanically favorable design in terms of minimizing mandibular fracture risk in geriatric patients, thereby supporting evidence-based clinical planning.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

This study utilized Nobel Active (Nobel Biocare, Sweden) implants measuring 4.3 mm in diameter and 8.5 mm in length. These implants and their prosthetic components were scanned in three dimensions (3D) using a 3D scanner (3Shape D2000 extraoral laboratory scanner), and the resulting .stl models were transferred to Rhinoceros 4.0 software (Seattle, USA). Although manufacturer-provided CAD files for Nobel Biocare implants are available, the physical components were scanned to ensure full compatibility with the parameters of our finite element simulation workflow and to enable the incorporation of individualized prosthetic designs that are not represented within standard digital libraries.

Due to ethical considerations regarding the use of patient-specific data, no CBCT scans from actual individuals were utilized in this study. Instead, a computer-generated model of an atrophic edentulous mandible was employed. This approach enabled the simulation of a standardized anatomical structure without exposing patients to additional radiation or requiring individual consent. The virtual model was constructed based on established morphological data from the literature, representing a typical Class II atrophic mandible, in line with the study’s objective to evaluate biomechanical performance in a generalized atrophic scenario.

Meshing and material characteristics

The acquired data were transferred to 3D-Doctor software, where bone tissue was segmented using the “Interactive Segmentation” method based on Hounsfield values. The models were processed in VR Mesh and then imported into Algor software as.stl surface images. Subsequently, meshing and material property assignments were conducted to create solid models for analysis. All materials were modeled as homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic, as commonly applied in finite element studies of implant-supported prostheses.

The first stage of the project involved constructing a digital mandibular model classified as Class II atrophy according to the Luhr classification system [14]. Implant and prosthetic components were then applied, generating five study models. A fully bonded (perfect osseointegration) implant–bone interface was assumed in all models, which is consistent with previous FEA studies and allows stable evaluation of stress distribution [15].

Discussion

Contrary to popular belief, tooth loss is not an inevitable consequence of aging. Rather, functional decline associated with aging, along with the high prevalence of dental and systemic diseases, increases susceptibility to edentulism among geriatric patients. The advent of dental implant treatments has expanded the range of prosthetic solutions for edentulous individuals [2, 18].

However, comparative studies evaluating different treatment modalities for the edentulous mandible remain scarce. A review of existing literature reveals a lack of consensus regarding the effects of two versus four implants on implant site stability. For instance, Ketabi et al. reported that the number of implants supporting an implant-retained prosthesis directly influences bone resorption rates, with four-implant-supported prostheses exhibiting lower resorption levels [19]. The decision between two and four implants should consider the patient’s overall health, aesthetic preferences, and functional requirements. The prevailing recommendation favors four implants for improved intraoral comfort, reduced bone resorption, and enhanced load distribution against occlusal forces. However, in cases where bone availability precludes the placement of four implants, alternative treatment strategies must be considered [19, 20]. In this study, a third implant was placed in the midline, and its effects were investigated.

Severe mandibular atrophy is characterized by an interforaminal region height of 12 mm or less. Batenburg et al. suggested that four implants are optimal for implant-supported removable prostheses in cases of excessive mandibular resorption [21]. However, Chrcanocic et al. demonstrated that exceeding this number may compromise mandibular integrity and increase the risk of fracture [22]. Studies suggest that implant placement and mandibular fractures are closely related in patients with bone heights < 10 mm in the symphysis region, including the interforaminal area. Therefore, a thorough patient evaluation is necessary before implant placement [12].

Luhr et al. [14] proposed a classification system for atrophic edentulous mandibular fractures. According to this classification, mandibles with a fracture site height of ≤ 10 mm are classified as Class 3 atrophy, those between 10 and 15 mm as Class 2 atrophy, and those between 16 and 20 mm as Class 1 atrophy. The present study used a mandibular model exhibiting Class 2 atrophy in the anterior region and Class 3 atrophy in the posterior region.

Implants placed in the anterior region of the atrophic mandible, where bone height ranges from 8 to 10 mm, have been observed to exceed the inferior limit. Studies indicate that forming 4.4-mm implant slots in the region between the mental foramina increases stress on the bone. Additionally, the proximity of implant slots has been identified as a factor that increases the risk of fracture. The literature suggests that the number of implants, the prosthetic approach and the connection design influence the distribution of stress on both bone and implants [23].However, this study primarily aimed to assess the impact of different implant configurations on stress distribution under frontal traumatic force.

Due to the limitations of experimental studies on human mandibles, digital simulations and virtual models obtained through FEA were used [15, 24–26]. FEA is considered a superior stress analysis method due to its simplicity and effectiveness. This method allows modifications to model geometry and test conditions, yielding results in a relatively short time. Studies indicate that stress distributions obtained through FEA closely resemble real-world conditions, particularly when lateral and oblique loads are applied [27].

Sadig et al. evaluated the retention of ball-head, locator, and magnet retainers on two implants under axial, oblique, and posterior rotational forces. Their findings revealed that locator attachments provided the highest retention force and stability in all directions, while magnet attachments exhibited the lowest retention force [28]. In line with these findings, the present study employed locator attachment-supported overdenture prostheses.

Kan et al. conducted an FEA study in which a frontal traumatic force of 10 MPa was applied to the symphysis region of an edentulous mandible model. The model incorporated two implant-supported overdenture prostheses, each equipped with a locator attachment. Implants of varying sizes and placements were tested. The study found that the lowest Von Mises stress was observed in models where implants were placed monocortically in the lateral incisor region. The highest levels of stress were observed during the insertion of long bicortical implants in the canine region [25]. Based on these findings, this study placed implants monocortically in the lateral incisor region in the locator attachment group.

Ayali et al. examined stress distribution in a completely edentulous atrophic mandible model using two implant-supported overdenture prostheses with locator attachments. A force of 2000 N was applied to a 1-cm diameter area in the symphysis region. The models included a control model without implants, a model with 3.5 × 11.5-mm implants, and a model with 4.3 × 11.5-mm implants. Results indicated that the 4.3-mm implant model experienced the highest stress [24]. Similarly, Klein et al. recommended maintaining at least 1 mm of bone on the buccal and lingual surfaces of implants [29]. This study adhered to this guideline by ensuring a 1-mm bone margin in all models.

Pigozzo et al. investigated the impact of parallel versus angled implant placement on stress distribution in two-implant overdenture prostheses using the photoelastic stress analysis method. Despite inherent differences between photoelastic resin and real bone tissue, the study found that in parallel placement models, stress concentrated at the implant apex. In contrast, angled implants (10°) exhibited high stress at the mesial and distal implant surfaces.Parallel implant placement facilitated appropriate force transmission along the implant’s long axis [30]. In accordance with these findings, implants were placed in parallel in both the locator and bar attachment overdenture prosthesis models.

Bilhan et al. conducted a three-dimensional FEA study to assess stress distribution in ball and bar retainer systems applied to mandibular models with two, three, or four implants under masticatory forces. Vertical forces of 100 N, simulating masticatory forces, were applied to the prepared models in the anterior region, both unilaterally and bilaterally. The results indicated that increasing the number of implants and utilizing attachment types that splint the implants can effectively reduce stress around the implant [26]. During the study’s planning stages, researchers positioned the third implant in the midline in models with three implants. In our study, we followed this methodology and positioned the third implant in the midline accordingly.

Rismanchian et al. investigated the effect of the distance between the bar holder and the mucosa on stress distribution in mandibular bar-retained overdenture prostheses using three-dimensional FEA. In their study, implants were placed in the canine region. Four lower jaw models were then created with varying distances between the overdenture bar holders and the mucosa (0, 1, 2, or 3 mm).A vertical force ranging from 0.1 to 50 N was applied from the left first molar region, and the resulting stress distributions were evaluated. The study found that models with 1- and 2-mm spacing exhibited lower implant stress values, with the lowest stress observed in models with 3-mm spacing. It has been reported that a 1- or 2-mm distance between the holder and the mucosa is ideal for appropriate stress distribution in mandibular overdenture prostheses using Dolder bar holders [31]. Based on this recommendation, the bar in our study was positioned 2 mm from the mucosa in models with bar attachment-supported overdenture prostheses.

A recent study by Ferreira et al. reported that acrylic teeth increased stress values in substructures by 50% compared with porcelain teeth. They recommended using porcelain teeth instead of acrylic teeth to improve stress distribution and increase the stiffness of implant-supported overdenture prostheses, particularly in prostheses with long cantilever lengths [32]. Additionally, Zhao et al. found that monolithic lithium disilicate porcelain crowns exhibited superior fracture resistance compared with layered crowns, as they consist of a single material and lack a veneer layer [33]. Based on these findings, our study designed implant substructures from titanium in the form of cut teeth to create hybrid prostheses. The superstructures featured monolithic lithium disilicate porcelain crowns, while the gingival forms were designed using acrylic resin.

Despite the paucity of research in this area, Franciosi et al. reported that atrophic mandibular fractures most commonly occur in the body of the mandible. Furthermore, the condyle and symphysis-parasymphysis regions were observed to be frequently involved. However, patients in their study were exposed to varying traumatic forces [34]. In our study, a frontal traumatic force was applied to all models from the symphysis region, and the majority of stresses were observed in the symphysis and condyle regions.

In an FEA study, Krennmair et al. applied different forces to models with four implants and found that lower stresses occurred in models where implants were connected using bars [35]. In our study, we observed higher stresses in bar-attached overdenture prosthesis models with three or two implants connected by bars in both locator and bar attachment variations. This increase in stress is likely due to differences in implant diameter, length, and positioning. Some previous studies have suggested that increasing the number of implants or using splinted bar attachments may improve load distribution in mandibular overdentures. For example, Cakarer et al. reported that the locator system showed superior clinical results than the ball and the bar attachment designs [36]. Furthermore, Bilhan et al. reported that an increase in the number of implants and the use of a splinted attachment could be preferable in order to reduce forces emerging around the implants [37]. In contrast, the present study demonstrated higher principal stress values in the three-implant locator configuration. These discrepancies are likely related to differences in load direction (masticatory vs. frontal impact), implant spacing, and the severe degree of mandibular atrophy simulated in the present model, rather than true contradictions in biomechanical behavior.

Tensile stress refers to the internal force that attempts to elongate the material, whereas compressive stress denotes the force that attempts to compact or shorten it. In the context of mandibular biomechanics, excessive tensile or compressive stresses may compromise bone integrity, especially in atrophic conditions [17]. Tensile stresses were evaluated based on point data obtained from the symphysis, angulus, corpus, and condyle regions. The tensile and compressive stresses, ranked from lowest to highest, were as follows: the total prosthesis, the bar attachment overdenture prosthesis model with two implants, the locator attachment overdenture prosthesis model with two implants, the bar attachment overdenture model with three implants, and the locator attachment overdenture prosthesis model with three implants. The data indicated that the use of a locator attachment reduced the mandible’s resistance to tensile and compressive stresses in the symphysis, corpus, angulus, and condyle regions, whereas the bar attachment was found to be a safer option.Although bar attachments offer splinting advantages, the present results clearly indicate that the three-implant locator model generated the highest tensile stress (P1), which represents the most critical indicator of symphyseal fracture risk. In contrast, both two-implant configurations (locator and bar) showed considerably lower principal stress values, suggesting that increasing the number of implants does not always improve biomechanical behavior in atrophic mandibles. Therefore, in severely resorbed mandibles, a two-implant overdenture—particularly when splinted with a bar—may provide a safer biomechanical profile than three-implant designs under frontal impact loading.

Making a broader comparison with previous studies provides a more contextualised perspective on our findings. Although bar attachments have been shown to improve load distribution by splinting multiple implants and reducing peri-implant stresses, this benefit depends on the number of implants, the length of the bar, and the quality of the bone [23]. In contrast, locator attachments have been observed to result in increased stress transfer to peri-implant bone, especially in atrophic mandibles with reduced cortical thickness [36]. Research has indicated that the addition of a third implant or the reduction of the inter-implant distance does not necessarily lead to an improvement in biomechanical performance. In fact, it has been observed that this can result in an increase in stress concentration in the interforaminal region [38]. Finite element studies have also indicated that placing implants too close to the midline increases stress levels in the symphyseal area [39]. These results are consistent with those of the present study, which highlights the importance of carefully considering the number, distribution and attachment type of implants, as well as the distance between them, when planning treatment for atrophic mandibular overdentures.

With increasing life expectancy, the proportion of elderly patients is rising, leading to a higher incidence of falls. This increased risk is attributed to factors such as impaired vision and sensation, reliance on canes and assistive devices, reduced motor reflexes, balance disorders, and syncope. Consequently, the prevalence of trauma in this population is also escalating. As the use of dental implants becomes more widespread among elderly patients, the incidence of implant-induced fractures in atrophic mandibles has also increased. Therefore, further research is needed to evaluate the biomechanical strength of the mandible under various force conditions.

Limitations

Despite efforts to replicate clinical conditions through anatomically accurate modeling, defined boundary conditions, and appropriate material assignments, this study has several inherent limitations. First, the overdenture was modeled under mucostatic assumptions, meaning that the applied force was transmitted entirely through the implants without accounting for mucosal support. However, in clinical practice, mucocompressive impression techniques may allow for posterior ridge load sharing, potentially reducing implant-related stress. Second, the bone structures were assumed to be homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic, which does not reflect the true anisotropic and heterogeneous properties of cortical and cancellous bone tissues. These modeling simplifications may limit the direct applicability of the numerical results to in vivo conditions. Future finite element studies should consider incorporating mucosal loading effects and more realistic bone behavior to improve the accuracy and clinical relevance of stress distribution predictions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated significant variations in stress distribution among different implant-supported overdenture models. The highest tensile stress was observed in the three-implant locator attachment-supported overdenture prosthesis model, whereas the conventional complete denture model exhibited the lowest tensile stress. Conversely, the highest compressive stress values were recorded in the three-implant bar attachment-supported overdenture prosthesis model. Within the limits of finite element analysis, this study demonstrates that bar attachment systems with three implants enhance structural resistance of the atrophic mandible under frontal load better than locator systems. Prosthetic design should therefore consider attachment type and implant number to minimize fracture risk. Two-implant overdenture systems exhibited lower tensile stress values than three-implant configurations and therefore appear to offer a more favorable biomechanical safety margin in atrophic mandibles subjected to frontal impact loads.
