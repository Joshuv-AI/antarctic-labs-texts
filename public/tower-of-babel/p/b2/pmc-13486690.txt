Dorsal horn DCC amplification loop induced by endplate osteoclasts generates chronic nociplastic low back pain in male mice
Authors: Dayu Pan, Mengxi Shen, Elizabeth Abatan, Jinjian Zheng, Philip Nasser, Damien M Laudier, Chao Kong, Lei Yan, Zhaozhu Qiu, James C Iatridis, Mei Wan, Junying Zheng, Bradley K Taylor, Xu Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpine degeneration is associated with low back pain (LBP), a major cause of disability and functional decline. However, effective therapies for non-specific LBP remain limited because its underlying mechanisms are poorly understood. Here, we show that spine degeneration induces netrin-1 secretion in porous endplates to activate DCC signaling in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons in both LBP and naturally aged male mice. Conditional loss of netrin-1 in Trap+ osteoclasts, or Dcc in Avil+ DRG neurons reduced spinal hypersensitivity. Importantly, DCC activation induces Src phosphorylation and SNARE complex docking, leading to presynaptic glutamate release in the dorsal horn (DH). Consequently, presynaptic glutamate release induces expression of both netrin-1 and DCC in DH neurons as a form of postsynaptic plasticity that initiates a positive feedback loop to amplify spinal hypersensitivity. Moreover, netrin-1/DCC expression is also amplified in the parabrachial nucleus (PBN) in the brain in both LBP and aged male mice. Thus, osteoclast-derived netrin-1 in porous endplates activates DCC-mediated hypersensitivity in DRG neurons to be further amplified in the DH neurons. These results promote the concept of non-specific LBP as a form of nociplastic pain.Subject terms: Chronic pain, Neural circuits, Molecular neuroscience

Introduction

Chronic low back pain (LBP), which entails progressive breakdown of intervertebral discs (IVDs), vertebrae, facet joints, and spinal muscles, is one of the most prevalent diseases that leads to a decline in mobility1–3. Unfortunately, there are no effective disease-modifying treatments that protect the spine from accelerated degeneration and that provide sustained pain relief. The spine is composed of a series of fibrocartilaginous IVD interconnected by endplates. This unique structural arrangement enables the distribution of hydrostatic pressure across the endplates from mechanical loading of the spine4,5. Due to the spine’s complex architecture, spinal pain can originate from multiple sources within the spinal column. LBP constitutes different types of pain, including nociceptive pain, nociplastic pain with amplification of nociceptive hypersensitivity in the central nervous system (CNS), and neuropathic pain6. Nociplastic pain is defined as pain arising from increased nociception without clear evidence of ongoing tissue damage or somatosensory system disease7. Clinically, it is linked to augmented central pain processing and may coexist with nociceptive non-specific pain such as chronic LBP8. Consistent with this framework, recent reviews of discogenic LBP emphasize that symptomatic IVD degeneration is characterized not only by local inflammation and neurovascular ingrowth, but also by central sensitization at the spinal and supraspinal levels, thereby overlapping with the clinical construct of nociplastic pain6,9. In addition, epidemiological and psychophysical studies identify a clear chronic LBP subgroup with generalized hypersensitivity and impaired descending pain modulation, supporting a central component in structurally driven conditions such as discogenic LBP8,10. Here, we propose an endplate-driven central mechanism that may contribute to nociplastic11 features of LBP.

The presentation of Pain includes multiple sensory modalities with different underlying mechanisms, complicating diagnosis and treatment8. Although IVD degeneration has been associated with LBP, clinical studies find high variability and IVD degeneration in non-painful subjects12,13. Our previous study demonstrated that vertebral endplates undergo ossification, and that uncoupled osteoclast-mediated bone remodeling generates porous endplates in both lumbar spine instability (LSI) and aging mice14–16. Assessing Tartrate-Resistant Acid Phosphatase (TRAP) allowed us to determine whether manipulating osteoclast activity, and thus netrin-1 release, can impact initiation of chronic LBP. In this context, “porous endplate” denotes thinned endplates that are filled with microscopic cavities due to osteoclast activity; they are often accompanied by ingrowth of CGRP+ sensory fibers into the endplate/subchondral region14,17,18. Moreover, clinical studies indicate that the endplate can be a source in a subset of chronic LBP patients19, and are commonly present in human endplates exhibiting fibrovascular marrow, fatty marrow, or structural defects. Notably, nerve density is significantly higher in fibrovascular endplate marrow than in morphologically normal human endplates20. Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) is a well-established marker of nociceptive C and Aδ afferents, which are critically involved in pain transmission and neurogenic inflammation21–23. We reported that CGRP-positive sensory fibers innervate the vertebral endplate and contribute to the development of LBP14,16. CGRP+ nociceptors undergo pronounced plasticity during pathological pain states and play a central role in chronic pain1,24,25. Indeed, CGRP and its receptor have been extensively studied in pain-related disorders (e.g., migraine, LBP), and anti-CGRP biologics are in clinical use26–28. Therefore, we have focused on CGRP-positive sensory neurons for our investigations of endplate innervation and pain transmission. Our present findings build upon and extend our previous work by clarifying the downstream molecular and circuit mechanisms through which netrin-1–induced CGRP⁺ innervation contributes to chronic LBP. Specifically, we show how these CGRP⁺ inputs transmit pain signals via DRG neurons, engage a dorsal horn feedback loop, and ultimately relay information to PBN.

Netrins are a conserved family of secreted guidance cues that play essential roles in axon guidance and neural circuit formation during nervous system development29. Among the netrin family members, netrin-1 functions primarily through the transmembrane receptor Deleted in Colorectal Cancer (DCC) to mediate chemoattraction of commissural axons and promote axonal growth30. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified Dcc, the receptor of netrin-1, as a prime risk gene for chronic pain31–34. When it interacts with netrin-1, DCC serves as a chemoattractant that guides axonal growth during neurodevelopment35,36, which is essential for the development of nociceptive topognosis in both mice and humans37. DCC is also a regulator of maladaptive responses to chronic morphine administration including tolerance, dependence, and opioid-induced hyperalgesia38. Other studies have found that Dcc is also the most extensively pleiotropic psychiatric risk gene39. Netrin-1 expression in the CNS can regulate synaptic plasticity by promoting the trafficking of glutamatergic AMPARs36,40,41. However, in chronic LBP, the molecular mechanism of Netrin-1/DCC signaling at DRG level and its role in regulating synaptic plasticity at dorsal horn and brain levels remain unclear.

In this study, we sought to investigate nociceptive and nociplastic mechanisms that mediate LBP. Specifically, we examined the consequences of netrin-1 secretion by osteoclasts in the porous endplates on sensory neurons that innervate the spine, as well as the dorsal horn neurons that they project to. We show that netrin-1 expression in the porous endplates induces DCC expression in DRG neurons, which is further amplified in dorsal horn neurons, triggering spinal hypersensitivity and dorsal horn plasticity to mediate LBP. These findings reveal important molecular insights into the mechanisms that mediate LBP and provide previously unrecognized therapeutic targets that may interfere with the chronification of LBP. In addition, this study provides additional evidence for the theory that pathological changes in vertebral endplates, rather than IVD degeneration, serve as the root cause of chronic LBP.

Results

Netrin-1 is secreted by osteoclasts in the porous endplate to induce CGRP⁺ sensory innervation and DCC expression in both LBP mouse models and aging human

To study the mechanisms of spinal degeneration, we examined the caudal endplates of lumbar vertebrae at the L4/5 position in a surgical (LSI) and natural (age-induced) model of LBP. The surgical LSI model consists of resecting L3-5 spinous processes along with the supraspinous and interspinous ligaments to induce instability of the lumbar spine, which mimics human spinal degeneration and LBP42–44. Both LSI and aging increased spinal endplate porosity relative to sham and young controls, respectively, based on μCT analysis (Fig. S1A) and Safranin O and fast green staining of tissue sections (Fig. S1B, C). To examine whether spine degeneration in the LSI LBP mouse model mimics age-related spine degeneration, we performed biomechanical tests. These revealed reduced compressive stiffness of the L4/5 motion segment in LSI and aged mice (Fig. S1D), suggesting that instability of the spine is an important contributing factor to spine degeneration. Histological staining of endplate sections revealed that both LSI and aging significantly increased the number of netrin-1+ and TRAP+ osteoclasts residing in porous endplates, and that netrin-1 and TRAP staining often co-localized in endplate osteoclasts (Fig. 1A–D). LSI and aging also increased DCC expression in porous endplates, as well as CGRP (Fig. 1E, F), a marker of sensory nerve innervation, in porous endplates and expression of both molecules co-localized (Fig. 1G, H). Importantly, the expression of DCC was significantly increased in the DRGs of LSI and aged mice, while its expression was almost undetectable in sham or young control mice (Fig. 1I, J). To further examine the segment of endplate-innervating DRG neurons, we performed retrograde labeling from L3–L5 endplates using a retrograde AAV expressing GFP (AAV-retro-Syn-GFP). Robust GFP labeling was detected in DRG neurons across L2–L5, with a clear segmental peak at L4 compared with sham group (Fig. S2A, B). Co-immunostaining with markers for peptidergic (CGRP), myelinated (NF200), non-peptidergic nociceptors (IB4, isolection B4), and small-diameter non-peptidergic (TH) DRG neurons revealed that the majority of GFP⁺ neurons were CGRP⁺, while only a small fraction was NF200⁺ /TH⁺ /IB4⁺ neurons (Fig. S2C, D). These data establish that strongly enriched endplate-innervating CGRP+ sensory neurons arise predominantly from L4, with contributions from L2–L3 and L5, thereby providing the required anatomical specificity. Interestingly, DCC expressions in CGRP⁺ and NF200⁺ neurons were increased in both LSI and aged mice compared with sham controls. DCC expression in IB4⁺ and TH⁺ neurons did not change across groups (Fig. S3A–E). We next asked whether a similar phenomenon occurs during human aging. Human participants were categorized into young (<33 years) and aged (>44 years) groups based on age. We found that CGRP+ sensory innervation was increased in aged porous endplates, but not in young, intact human samples, and that CGRP co-localization with DCC was greater in aged specimens (Fig. 1K–O). The proportion of CGRP+ sensory nerve fibers in the endplate was three times higher in sections from aged (50%) as compared to those from young (16.7%) human specimens (Fig. 1N), with no significant differences between sexes or among lumbar levels (Fig. S3F–I). These results suggest that spine degeneration is associated with a netrin-1-induced sensory innervation and DCC expression in the porous endplate of both mice and humans, and led us to test the hypothesis that DCC is necessary for the establishment of LBP (Fig. S1E).

Loss of Dcc in sensory neurons reduces porous endplate innervation, neuronal activity, and mechanical hypersensitivity

To examine the contribution of DCC to neuronal activity and pain in the context of spine degeneration, we crossed Dccflox/flox mice (Dccfl/fl) with a peripheral neuronal driver line Advillin-Cre (AvilCre) to generate conditional Dcc knockout mice (Dccfl/flAvilCre). Selective deletion in sensory neurons was confirmed by the absence of DCC protein in DRGs of Dccfl/flAvilCre mice (Fig. S4A, B). CGRP+ sensory innervation of porous endplates was reduced in LSI Dccfl/flAvilCre mice compared to LSI Dccfl/fl mice (Fig. 2A, B). As Pirt is widely expressed in primary sensory neurons, Pirt-Cre;GCaMP6s(PirtGCaMP) mice allowed for the selective expression of GCaMP6s in >95% of DRG neurons45,46, thereby providing an effective tool for studying neuronal activity within the DRG. To examine the contribution of DCC to neuronal activity, we crossed Dccfl/fl mice with PirtGCaMP mice to generate Dccfl/flPirtGCaMP mice to selective delete Dcc in sensory neurons while simultaneously monitoring DRG activity by in vivo calcium imaging45. A striking spontaneous (i.e., no external stimuli applied) pattern of Ca2+ signals, labeled by GCaMP6, were increased in LSI PirtGCaMP mice relative to sham controls without LSI surgery. Importantly, the increased neuronal activity in LSI PirtGCaMP mice was normalized in the absence of Dcc, since the number of events in DRG neurons of LSI Dccfl/flPirtGCaMP mice were indistinguishable from those in sham Dccfl/flPirtGCaMP mice (Fig. 2C, D).

To investigate whether DCC contributes to spinal degeneration-induced pain, we conducted a series of behavioral tests. We first measured distance traveled and maximum speed by providing mice with running wheels. In control Dccfl/fl mice, LSI decreased locomotor activity by approximately half compared to sham surgery mice, which was restored to normal in the absence of Dcc (LSI Dccfl/flAvilCre) (Fig. 2E, F). We next measured vocalization threshold in response to force applied to the L4/L5 disc region as an indication of mechanical pain in the context of LBP. LSI decreased vocalization thresholds in control Dccfl/fl mice compared to sham mice, but thresholds were comparable between LSI Dccfl/flAvilCre mice and sham Dccfl/flAvilCre mice (Fig. 2G). We also assessed thermal heat sensation and found no difference between LSI Dccfl/flAvilCre mice and LSI Dccfl/fl mice (Fig. 2H), suggesting that DCC is not involved in thermal hyperalgesia in the context of LBP. Finally, LSI increased mechanical (von Frey) paw withdrawal frequency in LSI Dccfl/fl mice compared to sham Dccfl/fl mice, and these behavioral signs of mechanical allodynia were absent in LSI Dccfl/flAvilCre mice relative to LSI Dccfl/fl mice (Fig. 2I, J). Together, these results indicate that loss of DCC in sensory neurons reduces the excessive sensory nerve innervation of porous endplates, neuronal activity, and mechanical hyperalgesia observed in a surgical mouse model of LBP.

Netrin-1-induced DCC expression activates Src phosphorylation and SNARE complex assembly to stimulate glutamate release in DRG neurons

To determine if netrin-1-induced DCC expression stimulates glutamate release from DRG neurons47,48, we infected primary cultured neurons of L1-6 DRGs from Dccfl/fl mice with AAV9-Syn-Cre-GFP or AAV9-Syn-GFP, yielding a 75% transfection efficiency to delete Dcc (Fig. 3A, S4C, D). Netrin-1 exposure increased calcium influx (Fig. 3B) and glutamate release (Fig. 3C) in control primary cultured Dccfl/fl DRG neurons (AAV9-Syn-GFP), responses that were blunted in the absence of Dcc (AAV9-Syn-Cre-GFP). To determine whether netrin-1 directly regulates Dcc transcription, we performed a ChIP assay focusing on four predicted pCREB-binding sites within the Dcc promoter region (Fig. 3D). The results showed that Netrin-1 treatment markedly enhanced pCREB binding to promoter fragments 1 and 2 (Fig. 3E, F). Consistently, Netrin-1 increased Dcc mRNA expression in DRG neurons (Fig. 3G).

Given that the activation of Src through phosphorylation is a known step downstream of netrin-1 signaling48,49, we examined if DCC induces phosphorylation of Src. As expected, netrin-1 increased Src phosphorylation in primary Dccfl/fl DRG neurons compared to the vehicle group, but this phosphorylation was abolished upon AAV-Syn-Cre-induced loss of Dcc Fig. 2H, I. To further investigate whether phosphorylated Src (pSrc) binds to phosphorylated Syt-1 (pSyt-1) to induce neurotransmitter (glutamate) release, we performed immuno-precipitation (IP) experiments by using full DRGs of sham or LSI mice (Fig. 3J). In DRGs of LSI mice, Netrin-1 exposure induced the binding of pSyt-1 with pSrc, as detected by an antibody against pSyt-1 followed by blotting for pSrc (Fig. 3K). Reverse IPs, using an antibody against pSrc to detect binding of pSyt1, further confirmed this interaction (Fig. 3K). Moreover, given that Focal adhesion kinase (FAK) can recruit Src to promote Src activation50,51, Defactinib (FAK inhibitor) and PP2 (Src inhibitor) abolished netrin-1-induced FAK phosphorylation (Fig. S5A–C), Src phosphorylation (Fig. S5A–C), and the binding of pSrc with pSyt-1 (Fig. S6A–D).Thus, our results indicate that DCC mediates netrin-1-induced pSrc-pSyt-1 binding.

The soluble N-ethylmaleimide sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) complex consists of proteins on pre- and postsynaptic terminals that assemble to induce membrane fusion, playing a crucial role in presynaptic vesicle fusion during neurotransmitter release52. In the postsynaptic compartment, synaptic transmission and plasticity are regulated by rapid neurotransmitter receptor turnover, which is mediated by active exocytosis and endocytosis. To test the hypothesis that DCC-induced pSrc-pSyt-1 binding enhances SNARE complex formation in L1-6 DRG tissues from LSI mice, we performed Syt-1 IP and blotted for SNARE complex proteins, including Syntaxin-1, SNAP25, and VAMP2 (Fig. 3L). Importantly, pSyt1 formed a complex with SNARE proteins in LSI mice, but this interaction was not detected in sham mice (Fig. 3L). This was also confirmed in reversed IP with VAMP2 antibody and blotted with pSyt-1 (Fig. 3L). Protein phosphatase 2 (PP2) treatment of protein lysates prior to IP further verified the inhibition of Src could abolish Syt-1-SNARE complex formation (Fig. S6E). Taken together, our results indicate that DCC is required to phosphorylate Src, which subsequently interacts with pSyt-1 to induce SNARE protein complex assembly to stimulate presynaptic vesicle fusion and glutamate release (Fig. 3M).

Presynaptic remodeling of DRG neurons increases netrin-1 expression in the dorsal horn neurons that they project to

To investigate whether the above increase in sensory synaptic activity is associated with Netrin-1 expression in dorsal horn neurons, we performed electron microscopy (EM) on the spinal cords of control, LSI, and aged animals. EM analysis revealed a significant increase of presynaptic vesicles in morphologically defined excitatory (asymmetric, type I) synapses of DRG terminals in LSI mice as compared with their sham controls (Fig. 4A, B). Moreover, immunogold labeling for netrin-1+ revealed an increase in particles located both pre- (DRG), and postsynapticaptically (morphologically defined excitatory synapses) in LSI and aged mice as compared to sham mice (Fig. 4A,C, D). Primary controls for netrin-1 immunogold staining indicate antibody specificity (Fig. S7A, B). We next used FISH as an orthogonal, antibody-independent method to quantify cell-type– and lamina-specific transcriptional upregulation of Ntn1 in dorsal horn neurons and this confirmed greater netrin-1 expression in the dorsal horn neurons of laminae I, II, III and V of LSI mice and aged mice relative to young sham mice (Fig. 4E, G, H, Fig. S7C–F). Interestingly, DCC expression in laminae I, II and V neurons was greater in LSI mice and aged mice as compared to younger sham mice (Fig. 4F, I, J, Fig. S7G–J). Importantly, we found that netrin-1 and DCC are primarily expressed in dorsal horn neurons but mostly absent from microglia, astrocytes, or oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) (Fig. 5D–F, Fig. S8A, B).

Netrin-1 is secreted in response to neuronal depolarization and N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) activation in hippocampus40,53. To determine if presynaptic glutamate release activates the postsynatic NMDAR or α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptor (AMPAR) to induce netrin-1 secrection (Fig. 5A), L4–L5 dorsal horn tissue slices were isolated from sham and LSI mice, and subsequently cultured in the presence of an NMDA agonist, AMPA agonist or vehicle. NMDA agonist, not AMPA agonist increased dorsal horn netrin-1 release compared to vehicle treatment (Fig. 5B, C). Furthermore, we performed an in vivo NMDA-receptor blockade experiment with intrathecal injection of D-AP5 in LBP mice. One month after LSI, injections were followed by behavioral assessments at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. D-AP5 reduced hyperalgesia in LSI but not sham mice; this emerged at ~30 min and persisted for ~90 min (Fig. S9A–E). We harvested L4 dorsal horn slices 30 min after intrathecal D-AP5 injection and quantified netrin-1 by ELISA. D-AP5 reduced the LSI-induced increase in dorsal horn netrin-1 (Fig. S9F). Taken together, our results indicate that LBP-induced increases presynaptic glutamate stimulate NMDAR signaling in dorsal horn neurons and increases netrin-1 at the synapse, likely reflective of secretion into the synaptic cleft.

Postsynaptic netrin-1 release activates DCC signaling in postsynaptic dorsal horn neurons as a positive feedback loop

To investigate if netrin-mediated DCC signaling is activated in dorsal horn neurons, we examined pSrc and pSyt-1 levels in L4-5 dorsal horns of sham, LSI or aged mice (Fig. 6A). Both pSrc and pSyt proteins were elevated in LSI and aged mice relative to sham young mice (Fig. 6B–D). We next asked whether deletion of netrin-1 in dorsal horn neurons could reduce LSI-induced hypersensitivity (Fig. 6E). To this end, AAV-Syn-Cre-GFP virus was bilaterally injected into the dorsal horns of Netrin-1fl/fl mice (Fig. 6E, S9G–L) followed two weeks later by LSI or sham surgery (Fig. 6F), and then one month later with behavioral assessment. Maximum speed (Fig. 6G), distance traveled (Fig. 6H), and pressure tolerance (Fig. 6I) was smaller, while paw withdrawal frequency was greater (Fig. 6K, L) in Netrin-1fl/fl mice as compared to sham Netrin-1fl/fl control mice. However, these LSI-induced pain-like behavioral changes were absent in LSI Netrin-1fl/fl mice with DH AAV-Syn-Cre virus injection (yellow) but not LSI Netrin-1fl/fl mice with DH-AAV-Syn-ctrl virus injection (blue), Fig. 6G–L). Responses to noxious heat were not different between LSI Netrin-1fl/fl mice with DH AAV-Syn-Cre virus and DH-AAV-Syn-ctrl virus injection (Fig. 6J).

We next asked whether the postsynatic netrin-1 release from dorsal horn neurons can increase netrin-1/DCC expression in their presynaptic DRG neurons through a feedback mechanism (Fig. 6M). To determine whether netrin-1 from porous endplates osteoclasts activates PBN neurons, we injected AAV-Syn-GCaMP6 virus and implanted an optical fiber into the PBN for fiber photometry (Fig. 8E, F). Discussion

Chronic LBP is a leading cause of medical consultations, with persistent pain at rest profoundly impairing quality of life and daily physical activity54,55. We have shown previously that endplates undergo calcification and osteoclast-mediated bone remodeling, which results in endplate volume expansion and narrowed IVD space causing its degeneration in both LSI and aging mice43. Moreover, spinal degeneration induces osteoclast secretion of netrin-1 in the porous endplates to induce CGRP+ nerve innervation as the source of LBP56,57,

LBP is a typical example of non-specific pain. Here, we further investigate DCC-mediated non-specific pain mechanism. We demonstrated that netrin-1/DCC signaling is amplified in a dorsal horn as a feed-back-loop to transmit the nociplastic signal from endplate→ DRG→dorsal horn→brain. The term “nociplastic pain”56,57 was recently introduced by some in the international pain research community8 to categorize pain driven by central nervous system mechanisms that are distinct from peripheral nociceptive pain driven by ongoing inflammation or tissue injury and peripheral neuropathic pain driven by nerve damage. Our findings provide a mechanism of nociplastic LBP. Specifically, 80–90% of LBP patients in the clinic are classified as non-specific LBP with no single pathoanatomical cause identified6,58. Clinical studies suggest that pain arises from endplates19,59 although vertebral endplate porosity is not technically detected in clinical imaging and grading systems. Our results show that osteoclast netrin-1 in porous endplates induces sensory innervation for nociceptive signals amplified as nociplastic LBP in the spinal cord and PBN. This persistent, centrally driven amplification is consistent with the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) definition of nociplastic pain60.

Towards a better understanding of molecular mechanism of LBP, we show that netrin-1 induces the Src phosphorylation in sensory neurons of the DRG, facilitating its binding to pSyt-1, which in turn initiates the docking of the SNARE complex. This process promotes glutamate release from DRG neurons, thereby transmitting pain signals to the dorsal horn projection neurons they synapse with. Our in vivo calcium imaging and electrophysiological results further support that Ca²⁺-dependent vesicle fusion following SNARE complex docking enhances neuronal excitability. While the Avil-Cre line is widely used to target peripheral sensory neurons, Avil can also be expressed in a few non-sensory cell types such as Merkel cells, intestinal tuft cells, and autonomic neurons61,62. Therefore, potential off-target recombination may contribute partially to the observed phenotypes. For the in vivo calcium imaging, low-amplitude spontaneous DRG transients have been described in naïve preparations and can reflect normal background sensory drive; however, their subtype composition in vivo remains incompletely defined. In vivo calcium imaging in the conditional netrin-1 knockout in Trap+ osteoclasts remains to be performed. Importantly, we show that presynaptic glutamate release from DRG neurons induces the expression of both netrin-1 and its receptor, DCC, in dorsal horn neurons. Interestingly, our findings suggest that postsynaptic netrin-1, released by neurons in the dorsal horn, bind to DCC on both presynaptic DRG neurons and postsynaptic dorsal horn neurons to activate DCC signaling as a positive feedback loop to amplify spinal hypersensitive signaling. Although our data indicate that netrin-1 acts mainly through DCC signaling, we cannot completely exclude potential modulatory roles of other netrin receptors such as UNC5. Further studies will be needed to dissect receptor-specific contributions in more detail. If porous endplate degeneration persists and osteoclast-derived netrin-1 resolves, our results suggest that postsynaptic netrin-1 expression in dorsal horn neurons could independently sustain DCC hypersensitivity, irrespective of endplate-derived netrin-1 stimulation. Although our immunostaining confirmed that DH and PBN netrin-1 is expressed in neurons rather than glial or microglial cells, the specific neuronal subtypes expressing netrin-1 have not yet been determined. Future work using subtype-specific transgenic reporter lines will be required to clarify this.

To distinguish co-expression within the same neuron from signals arising from adjacent neurons, triple immunofluorescence for Netrin-1, DCC and NeuN was performed in superficial dorsal horn, and quantified at the single-neuron level. Significantly increased triple-positive neurons in Young+ LSI and Aged mice were observed relative to Young+ sham group (Fig. S8A, B). These data indicate that the elevated Netrin-1 and DCC signals arise predominantly within the same neurons, rather than from adjacent neurons. Our identification of excitatory synapses was based on morphological criteria (asymmetric type I synapses) rather than transmitter-specific vesicle labeling63. While this approach is well established for distinguishing synapse types in EM studies, it may not fully exclude potential heterogeneity in vesicle content or mixed synaptic phenotypes, which should be addressed by future transmitter-specific analyses. The functional contribution of the presynaptic netrin-1 signals in the dorsal horn also remain unclear. Further investigation is needed to definitively determine their origin and role. In addition, as we used male mice, future studies should consider sex as a biological variable with studies in female mice.

The PBN receives and integrates spinal nociceptive input and then conveys that information to forebrain targets, so we recorded from PBN to assess ascending pain transmission64,65. We designed our photometry studies to determine whether nociceptive or nociplastic signals reach the PBN at the population level. Because we did not differentiate contributions from elPBN, slPBN, or mPBN, future work using subregion-specific targeting or projection-defined photometry are warranted. While we identified netrin-1/DCC signaling as the primary pathway involved in chronic low pain signal transmission, inflammatory and neurotrophic factors, and ion channels are also involved in sensory neuron sensitization and pain transmission. For instance, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) signaling through EP4 creating feed-forward amplification of nociceptive responses66–68; Neurotrophic factors such as NGF/TrkA signaling in the periphery, which via retrograde transport to DRG, upregulates BDNF expression that is subsequently anterogradely transported to the spinal cord, where BDNF-TrkB signaling phosphorylates NMDA receptors to induce LTP-like central sensitization69–72; Nociceptor-associated ion channels, including TRPV1 and TRPA1 ion channels, which are expressed not only peripherally but also at presynaptic terminals in the spinal dorsal horn, where they potentiate glutamatergic transmission independent of NMDA receptors73. In elderly human subjects, we observed a threefold increase in CGRP-positive nerve fiber ingrowth of endplate, which was accompanied by elevated DCC expression, compared to younger individuals. Although we added separate single-channel images to better visualize DCC immunostaining in aged human samples, the DCC signal in human tissues is generally much weaker than that in mice. This difference, together with the thicker sectioning required for human specimens, makes it technically difficult to achieve staining quality comparable to mouse tissues. Interestingly, we observed that Dcc expression is upregulated in the PBNs in the LSI model of LBP. Netrin-1 knockout in Trap⁺ osteoclasts or Dcc knockout in DRG neurons significantly reduced LSI-induced hypersensitivity. Although Lu et al.74 reported that CGRP+ neurons can promote tissue healing through immunomodulatory interactions with neutrophils and macrophages under certain contexts, our findings suggest that netrin-1 from the vertebral endplate activates netrin-1-DCC signaling along the DRG-dorsal horn axis to induce the neuroplasticity that underlies chronic LBP. Moreover, our finding of active, self-sustained netrin-1/DCC signaling in PBN neurons suggests that skeletal pain-induced Dcc expression may underlie the psychological comorbidities that LBP patients can experience.

Methods

Human subjects

Human intervertebral disc (IVD) specimens were obtained from an institutional biobank administered by the Department of Pathology at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (New York, USA). The collection and use of these postmortem tissues were approved by the Mount Sinai Institutional Review Board (STUDY-19-01394: Orthopedics Cell and Tissue Repository). Written informed consent for tissue donation and research use was obtained from the donor before death or from the donor’s legally authorized next of kin. Human tissue procurement and research procedures were performed in accordance with applicable institutional policies and ethical standards. Human vertebral slides were collected from 20 donors (10 males and 10 females) aged 8–93 years. Donors were categorized into young (<33 years) and old (>44 years) groups based on age. Donors were included if demographic and clinical information (age, sex, cause of death) was available, if lumbar motion segments at L2/3–L4/5 were intact without gross deformity, vertebral fracture, or prior spinal instrumentation on macroscopic inspection, and if there was no documented history of primary bone disease. Donors were excluded when medical records or death certificates indicated disorders known to markedly affect bone metabolism or structure, including osteoporosis or osteopenia requiring pharmacologic treatment, metabolic bone disease (e.g., osteomalacia, hyperparathyroidism, Paget’s disease, osteogenesis imperfecta), chronic kidney disease–mineral and bone disorder, primary bone tumors or malignancies with confirmed bone metastasis, or long-term systemic glucocorticoid therapy. Detailed information about enrolled human subjects is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

To establish the LSI model, anesthesia was induced in three-month-old mice using ketamine (Ketalar, 0.13 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) and xylazine (Cat# PHR3264, Millipore sigma, 12 mg/kg, intraperitoneally). Behavioral tests

All behavioral tests were performed by the same investigators, who were blinded to the study or experimental groups. Treatment groups were randomly assigned by another investigator.

Hind paw mechanical sensitivity was determined using von Frey filaments of 0.07 g and 0.4 g (Aesthesio® Precision Tactile Sensory Evaluator)79. Mice were placed on a wire metal mesh grid covered with a clear plastic cage. Animals were acclimated in the enclosure for 30 min prior to testing. Von Frey filaments were applied to the mid-plantar surface of the hind paw through the mesh floor with enough pressure to buckle it. Ten applications were repeated at 1-s interval for each trial. Mechanical withdrawal frequency was calculated as the number of withdrawals over the ten applications.

Hind paw heat sensitivity (Hargreaves test) was determined with the Model Heated 400 Base (IITC Life Science)80. Following 30 min acclimation, the heat source was applied to the plantar surface of the hindpaw. Latency to paw withdrawal (jumping, licking, or rapid withdrawal) was recorded three times with at least 10 min interval.

Spontaneous wheel-running activity was recorded with BIO-ACTIVW-M (Bioseb, Pinellas Park, FL)81. The software enabled recording of forward and reverse activity in a cage that is similar to the home cage. We evaluated the distance traveled, maximum speed, and total active time over the course of 2 days.

Mechanical hyperalgesia was conducted with a force gauge (SMALGO algometer; Bioseb, Pinellas Park, FL)82. Mice were gentle restrained by one investigator to avoid moving, a sensor tip was applied directly to the dorsal skin over L4–L5 (0.5 cm above the line connecting the posterior iliac crest). Pressure force was progressively increased until a reaction was elicited from the mice (scream or shudder), upon which stimulation was stopped. Maximum force values were measured three times at a 15-min interval and subsequently averaged.

Mechanical test

Mechanical testing was done on each motion segment (L4–L4/5 disc-L5)83. In brief, specimens were loaded nondestructively in axial tension/compression to force peaks of ±0.5 N for 20 cycles. Data was taken from the last cycle. Compressive stiffness (N/mm) was calculated by linear regression of the upper 40% of the force range of the loading portion of the force-deformation curve.

Primary DRG neuron isolation and purification

Bilateral L1-6 DRGs were obtained from four-month-old male Dccfl/fl mice, one month after LSI injury or sham surgery. DRGs were carefully dissected out and then subjected to enzymatic digestion using 1 mg/mL type I collagenase (Cat# 17100-017, Thermo Fisher Scientific) 5 mg/mL of dispase II (Cat# 17105-041, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C for 70 min, with 10 DRGs placed in each tube. Subsequently, DRGs were dissociated into single cells in Neurobasal medium containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA). The cell suspension was then filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 500 μL of Triple Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and digested at 37 °C for 2 min. The reaction was halted by adding 1 mL of fetal bovine serum.

Next, the cell suspension was carefully layered onto a density gradient solution consisting of 1.057, 1.043, 1.036, and 1.029 g/ml in a 15 ml centrifuge tube. The tube was then centrifuged at 1900 × g for 15 min at room temperature. After centrifugation, the upper layer was removed, and the precipitates were discarded. The second layer, containing the desired cells, was collected and seeded into culture dishes. Before seeding, cells were washed with 2 mL of Neurobasal medium containing 1% BSA and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. After seeding neurons in culture dishes, the medium was refreshed every other day to maintain neuron viability and growth. Neurons were starved for 4 h prior to treatment with Defactinib (5 µM, Cat# HY-12289, MedChemExpress) for four days84,85. Subsequently, neurons received further treatment with Netrin-1 (1 µg/ml, Cat# 1109-N1, R&D systems) for 1 h before being collected for western blotting. To perform the DCC knockout in isolated DRG neurons, The AAV-Syn-Cre-GFP (Cat# SL100892, SignaGen, 1.1 × 1013 vg/ml), AAV-Syn-GFP (Cat# 50465, Addgene, 7 × 10¹² vg/mL) were added to cultured DRG neurons of Dccfl/fl mice and allowed to be infected for three days prior to the treatment with Netrin-1.

DRG tissue culture

Bilateral L3-5 DRGs were harvested from three-month-old male Dccfl/fl mice. DRGs were initially placed in Earl’s balanced salt solution (EBSS) on ice until they were transferred to a six-well plate. Each well was filled with 1.5 ml of medium, which consisted of 50% MEM with Earl’s salts and glutamine, 25% Hanks balanced salt solution, 25% horse serum, 20 mM Hepes acid-salt, and 6 mg/ml D-(+)-glucose. Millicell-CM inserts (Cat# PICM03050, Millipore) were then added to each well. For cell seeding, two DRGs were placed in each well, and the medium was refreshed twice a week to maintain tissue viability and growth. Three days after isolation, cultured DRGs were starved in serum-free medium for 4 h before being treated with the PP2 inhibitor (10 µM, Cat# S7008, Selleckchem) for 24 h. Subsequently, the cultured DRGs received further treatment with netrin-1 (1 µg/ml) for 1 h before being lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors prior to western blotting or IP assays.

Spinal cord tissue culture

L4-5 spinal cords were harvested from three-month-old male wild-type mice. The spinal cord was extruded caudally through the vertebral canal using a 10-ml syringe fitted with a blunt 18-gauge needle filled with ice-cold PBS. Subsequently, 6–8 transverse slices with a thickness of 400 µm were prepared using a vibratome and immediately transferred to ice-cold EBSS. The slices were then carefully placed into six-well plates containing culture medium. Each well was filled with 1.5 ml of the same medium described above for DRG tissue culture. Millicell-CM inserts were added to each well, and the spinal cord slices (2–3 slices per well) were transferred onto the Millicell-CM inserts using a pipette. Slices were then incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator, and the medium was changed twice a week. The typical change in color and transparency from whitish to transparent gray, indicating cell survival in the slices, typically occurred around the seventh to eighth day of culture. NMDA (50 µM, Cat# 454575, Millipore Sigma)40,41 and AMPA (10 µM, Cat# 0169, R&D systems)86 was applied for 30 min to induce netrin-1 release before tissue collection.

Mouse Netrin 1 ELISA

Netrin-1 protein levels were qualified using a commercial ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cat# LS-F5882, LSBio). Briefly, 100 µL of standard, blank, or appropriately diluted sample were added to each well of a pre-coated 96-well microplate and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Wells were then aspirated and washed three times with 1× wash buffer to remove unbound material. Next, 100 µL of Detection Reagent A working solution was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by three washes. Subsequently, 100 µL of Detection Reagent B working solution was added and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. After washing five times, 90 µL of TMB substrate solution was added to each well and incubated in the dark for 10–20 min at 37 °C until the reaction developed a visible blue color. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 µL of stop solution, resulting in a yellow color. The optical density (OD) was measured immediately at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Cat# A51119600, ThermoFisher).

Glutamate assay

Glutamate levels were quantified using a colorimetric glutamate assay kit (Cat# ab83389, Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, samples and standards were prepared in a 96-well plate using glutamate assay buffer. A reaction mix was freshly prepared for each well, containing 90 µL of glutamate assay buffer, 8 µL of glutamate developer, and 2 µL of glutamate enzyme mix, for a total volume of 100 µL per reaction. For background control wells, a background mix containing 92 µL of glutamate assay buffer and 8 µL of glutamate developer (without the enzyme mix) was used. Then, 100 µL of the corresponding reaction mix was added to each standard or sample well, and 100 µL of the background mix was added to the background control well. The plate was gently mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, protected from light. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Cat# A51119600, ThermoFisher)..

Calcium activity assay

Calcium concentrations were determined using a colorimetric calcium assay kit (Cat# ab102505, Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 50 µL of standards, samples, or blank controls were added to a 96-well plate. Then, 90 µL of chromogenic reagent was added to each well, followed by 60 µL of calcium assay buffer, for a total reaction volume of 200 µL per well. The plate was gently mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 5–10 minutes to allow color development. The absorbance was measured at 575 nm using a microplate reader (Cat# A51119600, ThermoFisher).

Western blot and Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Cell lysates were prepared using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails and incubated on ice with gentle agitation for 20 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for analysis. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 23225). Equal amounts of protein (10 µg per sample) were separated by 4–15% Tris–glycine SDS–PAGE (Bio-Rad, Cat# 4561086) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore Sigma, Cat# IPVH00010) at 90 V for 1.5 h on ice. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST (20 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.4) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies targeting Src (Cat# 2109S, Cell Signaling Technology), p-Src (Cat# D49G4, Cell Signaling Technology), FAK (Cat# 3285S, Cell Signaling Technology), pFAK (Cat# 3284S, Cell Signaling Technology), GAPDH (Cat# 14C10, Cell Signaling Technology), pSyt-1 (Cat# PPS085, R&D Systems), Syt-1 (Cat# 14558, Cell Signaling Technology), β-actin (Cat# 3700, Cell Signaling Technology) diluted in 5% BSA/TBST at a dilution of 1:1000 except GAPDH which is diluted 1:5000, and the membrane was incubated at 4 °C overnight. After washing three times with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 32106) and quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software. Band intensities were normalized to total protein or β-actin from the same sample. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays were performed using the Pierce™ Co-Immunoprecipitation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 26149) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 500 µg of total protein from each sample was incubated with 5 µg of antibody conjugated to the resin overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. After washing, bound proteins were eluted using elution buffer and analyzed by western blotting as described above. Antibodies used in this study are as follows: Syt-1 (Cat# 14558, Cell Signaling Technology), Syntaxin-1 (Cat# 110011, Synaptic Systems), GAPDH (Cat# 14C10, Cell Signaling Technology), SNAP25 (Cat# 836304, Biolegend; Cat# 5308, Cell Signaling Technology), VAMP2 (Cat# 13508, Cell Signaling Technology; Cat# 104211, Synaptic Systems), p-Src (Cat# D49G4, Cell Signaling Technology), pSyt-1 (Cat# PPS085, R&D Systems), β-actin (Cat# 3700, Cell Signaling Technology). Uncropped and unprocessed blot scans are provided in the Source Data file.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP assay was performed using the Pierce™ Agarose ChIP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. 26156) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications. Briefly, L1–L6 DRG tissues were rapidly dissected from vehicle- or netrin-1–treated mice, minced in ice-cold PBS and cross-linked in 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Cross-linking was quenched by adding 1.25 M glycine to a final concentration of 125 mM for 5 min, followed by two washes in ice-cold PBS. Tissues were lysed in SDS lysis buffer supplied with the kit, and chromatin was sheared by sonication on ice to an average size of ~200–1000 bp, which was verified on an agarose gel. After centrifugation, supernatants were collected, and 1–5% of each sample was saved as input DNA. Equal amounts of chromatin were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation with anti-phospho-CREB antibody (pCREB) or normal rabbit IgG (negative control). Immune complexes were captured with protein A agarose beads, washed sequentially with the kit’s low-salt, high-salt, LiCl and TE buffers, and eluted in ChIP elution buffer. Cross-links were reversed by incubation at 65 °C for 4 h in the presence of NaCl, followed by RNase A and proteinase K digestion. DNA was purified using the spin columns provided in the kit. Purified ChIP and input DNA were analyzed by quantitative PCR using SYBR Green chemistry on a real-time PCR system. Putative pCREB-binding motifs within the Dcc promoter were initially identified using the JASPAR transcription factor motif database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) and mapped onto the promoter region obtained from the Eukaryotic Promoter Database (EPD) (https://epd.expasy.org/epd/). The region spanning −2000 to +100 bp relative to the transcription start site (TSS) was analyzed using the CREB motif with a significance threshold of p < 0.001. This analysis identified two putative CREB-binding sites, which are located at approximately −1407 bp and −871 bp upstream of the TSS. We further designed four pairs of ChIP-qPCR primers to cover both the predicted binding regions and adjacent promoter regions. Specifically, four primer sets spanning −1 to −1500 bp relative to the TSS were used ( −1 to −340 bp, −341 to −700 bp, −701 to −1000 bp, and −1122 to −1500 bp), with the distal region ( − 1122 to −1500 bp). Four primer sets were designed as below:

Fragment 1: forward 5′-GCAGTGCTCTTTTCCGAGTC-3′,

Fragment 1: reverse 5′-AAACTGTTCCAAGCCCAGGTAT-3′;

Fragment 2: forward 5′-TCCTTCTCTGTTAACACACTCTCA-3′,

Fragment 2: reverse 5′-GGTTGAGGTTTTCATCAGTTGGG-3′;

Fragment 3: forward 5′-TTGGTTCGTGTTTCCTGGGAG-3′,

Fragment 3: reverse 5′-CGGAAAACAAACGCGAGACT-3′;

Fragment 4: forward 5′-TGCTCATGATGTTCTCTGGCT-3′,

Fragment 4: reverse 5′-TCAGGGAAAGACTCCACGCA-3′.

Enrichment for each fragment was calculated using the ΔCt method and expressed as fold enrichment over IgG controls after normalization to input DNA.

Immunofluroscent staining

Mouse lumbar spine samples were decalcified in 0.5 M EDTA (pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 21 days, with the decalcifying solution refreshed every 48 h. After decalcification, samples were rinsed thoroughly in PBS and dehydrated in 20% sucrose solution containing 2% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) at 4 °C overnight for cryoprotection. Following dehydration, tissues were washed in PBS to remove excess sucrose and then embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA, USA) for cryosectioning. For DRG, dorsal horn, and brain samples, tissues were post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h at 4 °C, dehydrated in 20% sucrose in PBS for 48 h, and subsequently embedded in OCT.

Cryosections were cut using a cryostat at 4 µm thickness for coronal sections of the L4–L5 lumbar spine, and at 20 µm thickness for L4 DRG, dorsal horn, and brain sections, following established protocols87. Human IVD samples were sectioned at 5 μm thickness, in a sagittal orientation. After incubation in a 37 °C oven for 1 h, the samples were washed with PBS for 5 min. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% normal donkey serum containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature (RT), and then these sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting netrin-1 (Cat# sc-518135, Santa Cruz) diluted at 1:100, Trap (Cat# PA5-116970, Invitrogen; Cat# ab191406, Abcam) diluted at 1:100, CGRP (Cat# ab36001, Abcam) diluted at 1:200, DCC (Cat# AF844, R&D system; Cat# A00560, Boster) diluted at 1:100, NeuN (Cat# ABN91, Millipore) diluted at 1:200, Iba-1 (Cat# ab5076, Abcam) diluted at 1:1000, GFAP (Cat# ab53554, Abcam) diluted at 1:500, NG2 (Cat# AB5320, Millipore) diluted at 1:100. Afterward, corresponding Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted at 1:200 and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Cat# H-1200, Vector) diluted at 1:500 were applied to the sections and left for 1 h at room temperature while avoiding exposure to light. Finally, sample images were imaged using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 780), and quantitative analysis was performed using Zeiss or Image J (NIH) software.

RNAscope in situ hybridization

RNAscope fluorescence in situ hybridization experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, using the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 (ACD, Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA) for fresh frozen tissue. Briefly, lumbar DRG (L4), dorsal horn (L4-5), and brain tissues were dissected at 1 month after sham surgery or LSI, frozen, and sectioned into 10 μm sections using a cryostat. Human IVD samples were sectioned into 5 μm sections in the sagittal orientation. Before hybridization, sections were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at 4 °C, rinsed twice with PBS, and dehydrated in graded ethanol (50%, 70%, 100%) for 5 min each. Slides were then pretreated with Protease III for 30 min at room temperature. In situ probes against the following mouse genes were ordered from ACD and multiplexed in the same permutations across quantified sections: Ntn1 (Cat# 407621, ACD, Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA), DCC (Cat# 427491, ACD, Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA). After the hybridization steps, signal amplification was performed using the AMP 1–3 reagents provided in the kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescent labeling was achieved with TSA fluorophores, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Sections were mounted with Vectashield Antifade Mounting Medium (Vector Laboratories) and imaged using a Zeiss LSM 780 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) with identical laser intensity, gain, and exposure settings across groups. For each sample, 10 Z-stack images were acquired using a 20× objective. A cell was considered probe-positive if at least three discrete fluorescent puncta were detected within the DAPI-defined nuclear boundary, consistent with the manufacturer’s guidelines88. Quantification was performed on three randomly selected, non-overlapping fields per section from at least three biological replicates, using ImageJ (NIH) and Zeiss ZEN software by observers blinded to experimental groups.

Electron microscope

Animals were transcardially perfused at 4 °C with freshly prepared EM-grade fixative containing 2–4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and 1% glutaraldehyde in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (Sorensen’s) with 5 mM MgCl₂, pH 7.4. Brains were perfused at 2 mL/min for 30 min. After perfusion, spinal cord tissue was dissected into pieces <2 mm³, transferred to fresh fixative, and post-fixed overnight at 4 °C. Unless otherwise stated, all subsequent steps were performed at 4 °C up to 70% ethanol in the dehydration series. Tissues were rinsed (3 × 10 min) in phosphate buffer containing 3% sucrose, then osmicated for 2 h on ice in reduced osmium (1% osmium tetroxide + 1.5% potassium ferrocyanide in buffer without sucrose, supplemented with 5 mM MgCl₂). Samples were rinsed in 100 mM maleate buffer + 3% sucrose and stained en bloc with 2% filtered aqueous uranyl acetate in the same buffer for 1 h protected from light. Specimens were dehydrated at 4 °C through graded ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 10 min each). At 70% ethanol they were brought to room temperature and further dehydrated (90% and 100% ethanol, 2 × 10 min each). Tissues were transitioned 10 min in propylene oxide, infiltrated with Eponate-12 resin (Ted Pella; 1:1 PO:resin 30 min, then 100% resin 2 × 1 h), embedded in fresh resin, and cured at 60 °C for 48 h.

Ultrathin sections (70–80 nm) were collected on formvar-coated 200-mesh nickel grids. All subsequent incubations were carried out with grids floating section-side down. All solutions were filtered (0.22 µm) immediately before use; antibody solutions were cleared by centrifugation (13,000 × g, 5 min). Grids were treated with 3% sodium metaperiodate for 30 min, rinsed in dH₂O (3 × 5 min), quenched in 50 mM NH₄Cl (10 min), rinsed in TBS (3 × 1 min), and blocked for 30 min in TBST (TBS + 0.05% Tween-20) containing 1% normal goat serum (NGS) and 1% BSA, plus 100 mM glycine. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-netrin-1 primary antibody (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-518135) at 1:25 or 1:50 in blocking buffer; no-primary grids served as negative controls. After equilibrating 1 h at RT, grids were rinsed in blocking solution (1 min) then TBS (10 min), and incubated for 3 h at RT in a humid chamber with 6-nm colloidal gold-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:10 in TBS) matching the host species of the primary antibody. Grids were rinsed in TBS (10 min) followed by dH₂O (quick rinse) and post-fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde in 100 mM sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7.4 for 5 min. After a brief dH₂O rinse, grids were contrasted with 2% aqueous uranyl acetate for 20 min, rinsed, blotted dry, and stored overnight in grid boxes before imaging89,90. Grids were examined on a Hitachi H-7600 TEM operating at 80 kV equipped with an AMT XR80 8-MP CCD. Imaging parameters (accelerating voltage, camera gain/exposure) were kept constant across samples. Synapses and membrane domains were identified by conventional ultrastructural criteria. Gold particles were counted within predefined ROIs by two independent, blinded observers. A profile was considered immunopositive when it contained ≥2 gold particles within the ROI and the linear density exceeded background by ≥2-fold (or ≥3 SD above background) in the same micrograph/section. At least 3 non-overlapping fields per section and 3 sections per animal were analyzed.

Samples were included only if they met predefined EM quality criteria, including optimal perfusion and ultrathin-section integrity without grid damage. Animals that reached humane endpoints (e.g., anesthesia intolerance or weight loss) before tissue collection were excluded prior to analysis. All image analyses were performed on a fixed number of non-overlapping fields per animal by investigators blinded to experimental groups. Synapses were classified by standard ultrastructural criteria63. Excitatory (Gray type I/asymmetric) synapses were identified by a prominent, thick postsynaptic density (PSD ≥ 30 nm) with a widened cleft and typically round vesicles91. Inhibitory (Gray type II/symmetric) synapses were defined by a thin PSD (~5–15 nm) with symmetric specializations and pleomorphic or flattened vesicles92.

In-vivo calcium imaging

Mice (Pirt-Cre; GCaMP6 and Pirt-Cre; DCC; GCaMP6 mice) were anesthetized with 1.5–2.0% isoflurane in O₂ (induction 3–4%) and maintained on a 37 °C heated stage. Ophthalmic ointment was applied and lactated Ringer’s was given s.c. as needed. A dorsal midline incision was made and a dorsal laminectomy at L3–L5 was performed to expose the left L4 DRG. As described previously, only the surface portion of the L4 transverse process was removed with a fine rongeur (16221-14, Fine Science Tools) to avoid damage to the spinal cord and DRG. The vertebral column was stabilized by clamping T10 and L4 with custom forceps mounted on micromanipulators to minimize motion. Imaging was performed on a Zeiss LSM 710 two-photon microscope, a Plan-Apochromat 10×/0.45 M27 air objective, and non-descanned detectors. Laser power at the sample was kept <40 mW. For overview imaging, the visible DRG was acquired at 512 × 512 pixels. For imaging data analysis, raw image stacks were imported into FIJI (NIH) for preprocessing and quantification. GCaMP fluorescence signals were processed using a custom post-processing pipeline implemented in MATLAB. Briefly, motion artifacts in all images were corrected using the NoRMCorre algorithm93, followed by denoising with the CANDLE filter94. Neuronal segmentation and extraction of temporal calcium dynamics (ΔF/F traces) were then performed using the CaImAn toolbox95. Calcium dynamics were quantified as normalized fluorescence changes (ΔF/F), where ΔF/F = (Fᵗ − F₀)/F₀. For each recording, the baseline fluorescence F₀ was defined as the mean pixel intensity during the initial 2–6 frames. For each subsequent frame, Fᵗ was calculated as the mean background-subtracted pixel intensity within a manually defined region of interest (ROI) over the entire recording period. Neuronal activation was operationally defined as an increase in fluorescence intensity of at least 20% above baseline (ΔF/F ≥ 20%). Analyses were performed blinded to group using FIJI (NIH) and MATLAB. Post-operative analgesia (e.g., buprenorphine 0.05–0.1 mg/kg s.c.) was provided per IACUC protocol. All procedures complied with institutional animal care guidelines.

Administration of AAV

We performed AAV-mediated viral of DRGs45. All surgical procedures were conducted under deep anesthesia using ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), and body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a feedback-controlled heating pad.

DRG injection for in-vivo calcium imaging

A dorsal laminectomy was performed to expose the L3–L5 vertebrae. The transverse processes overlying the L4 DRG were carefully removed using fine rongeurs. AAV-Syn-GCaMP6f (Addgene, Cat# 100837, 1.1 × 10¹³ vg/ml) was loaded into a pulled borosilicate glass micropipette (tip diameter ≈ 20–30 µm) and injected directly into the L4 DRG (30 nl over 5 min) using a Nanoliter 2010 microinjector (World Precision Instruments). The pipette was left in place for an additional 5 min to minimize backflow before slow withdrawal. Dorsal horn injection for conditional knockout and anterograde tracing

For AAV injections into the dorsal horn of conditional netrin-1 knockout mice or for anterograde tracing, a midline skin incision was made over the L4–L5 lumbar region96. The paraspinal muscles and fascia were gently separated to expose the intervertebral space (Fig. S9G–I). A pulled glass pipette connected to a microsyringe pump (UMP3, WPI) was inserted ~250 µm below the dorsal surface and ~500 µm lateral to the midline. A total of 500 nl per site of AAV-Syn-Cre (SignaGen, Cat# SL101461, 1.1 × 10¹³ vg/ml), AAV-Syn-Cre-GFP, or AAV-Syn-GFP was injected at a rate of 50 nl/min. The pipette was left in place for 10 min before removal.

Vertebral endplate injection

For viral labeling of vertebral endplates14, the paravertebral muscles were detached from the posterior elements, and the spinous processes together with supraspinous and interspinous ligaments were removed. Small holes (0.5 mm diameter) were drilled bilaterally into the caudal L4–L5 endplates using a micro drill (OmniDrill 35, World Precision Instruments). RetroAAV-Syn-GFP (SignaGen, Cat# SL116056, 1.1 × 10¹³ vg/ml) was injected (500 nl per site, 50 nl/min), and the drill holes were immediately sealed with bone wax to prevent leakage.

PBN injection for fiber photometry

For stereotaxic injections into the PBN, mice were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine as above. After the skull was exposed, a small burr hole was drilled above the PBN (AP: −5.2 mm; ML: 1.3 mm from bregma). Borosilicate capillaries were lowered to DV: −3.4 mm using a stereotaxic apparatus (Cat# 30-2031, RWD Life Science). AAV-Syn-GCaMP6f (Addgene, Cat# 100837) was injected (500 nl per site, 50 nl/min). The capillary was left in place for 10 min post-injection to prevent reflux. After all injections, surgical wounds were sutured and disinfected, and mice were allowed to recover on a heating pad. Postoperative analgesia (Buprenorphine, 0.1 mg/kg, s.c.) was administered as needed.

Cell-attached recording

Small- to medium-diameter DRG neurons were recorded in the cell-attached configuration at room temperature (22–24 °C). Cells were perfused with an external solution (in mM): 140 NaCl, 2 CsCl, 1 MgCl₂, 2 KCl, 5 HEPES, 5 glucose, 2 sodium pyruvate, and 1 BaCl₂; pH adjusted to 7.4 with NaOH; osmolarity 295–305 mOsm. Patch pipettes (4–6 MΩ; borosilicate glass) were pulled with tip resistances matched across groups. High-resistance seals (≥1 GΩ) were routinely obtained. Signals were amplified with a MultiClamp™ 700B amplifier and digitized with an Axon™ Digidata® 1550B (Molecular Devices). Data was acquired with Clampex 11.4 and analyzed in Clampfit 11.4 (Molecular Devices). Currents were filtered at 2–5 kHz (−3 dB, Bessel) and sampled at 10–20 kHz.

Fiber photometry recording

Fiber photometry was performed using a multichannel system (R821, RWD Life Science). We used a 200-µm core, NA 0.39 optic fiber positioned ~100–150 µm above the PBN to minimize tissue damage while sampling bulk, nucleus-level GCaMP6 signals across the PBN volume. This “above-target” placement is standard for fiber photometry and has been explicitly applied for PBN recordings with 200-µm/0.39-NA fibers; moreover, quantitative optical modeling shows that 0.39-NA, 200-µm fibers collect fluorescence from tissue extending hundreds of micrometers around/below the tip, enabling effective coverage of the target nucleus97. Excitation light at 470 nm (GCaMP) and 410 nm (isosbestic control) was routed through a fluorescence cube and coupled into the implanted optical fibers. Laser power at the fiber tip was maintained at 20–50 µW (measured with a photodiode power meter) and kept constant across groups. Emitted fluorescence was collected through the same fibers and detected by the system photodetector; signals were digitized with the vendor software and exported for analysis in MATLAB. For motion/photobleaching correction, the 470-nm trace was linearly regressed against the simultaneously recorded 410-nm trace, and the residual was used for quantification. We calculated ΔF/F = (F–F0)/F0, where F0 is the median of the photometry signal. Only calcium signals that exceeded 3 standard deviations were treated as events. The average spiking activity of 10 peak ΔF/F and the number of events per minute for each mouse were analyzed.

Safranin O and Fast Green staining

L3–L5 lumbar spines were harvested and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin overnight at 4 °C. After fixation, samples were decalcified in 0.5 M EDTA at 4 °C for 30 days with frequent solution changes, then paraffin-embedded. Coronal sections (4 µm) were prepared and stained with Safranin O (Cat# 146641000, ThermoFisher) and Fast Green (Cat# A16520.22, ThermoFisher) using a standard histological protocol: 0.02% Fast Green for 1 min, brief differentiation in 1% acetic acid for 10 s, then 0.1% Safranin O for 10 min. Sections were dehydrated, cleared, and resin-mounted. In these preparations, proteoglycan-rich cartilage appears red and bone matrix appears green. Stained sections were imaged under light microscopy, and endplate degeneration was graded using the ORS Spine criteria by scoring cellularity, lesions/fissures, and ECM structure (0–3 each) and summing to a total of 0–998.

μCT

L3–L5 spinal segments were collected as described above, fixed in 10% buffered formalin, and scanned by high-resolution μCT (Skyscan 1172, Bruker, Belgium) at 65 kVp, 153 μA, 9.0 μm/pixel. Images were reconstructed in NRecon v1.6 and analyzed in CTAn v1.9; coronal images of the L4–L5 unit were used for three-dimensional histomorphometry of the caudal endplate.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10. Differences between two experimental groups were evaluated using two-sided unpaired Student’s t-tests. When three or more groups were compared, significance was assessed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc correction for multiple comparisons. Results are expressed as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.
