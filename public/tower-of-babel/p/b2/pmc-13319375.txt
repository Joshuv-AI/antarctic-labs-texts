Functional Outcomes of Miniplate/Screw Versus Suzuki Frame Fixation in Eaton Type III Middle Phalanx Base Fractures: A Case Series
Authors: Enilton de Santana Ribeiro de Mattos, Alex Guedes, Maria da Conceição Cavalcanti de Figueirêdo Vilaboim
Published: 2026

We read with interest the study by Özdemir et al1 comparing the Suzuki frame and open reduction and internal fixation for Eaton type III middle phalanx base fractures. While the clinical question is relevant, several methodological nuances warrant closer discussion to optimize the interpretation of the results.

First, surgical allocation was not randomized. The Suzuki frame was systematically applied to the most comminuted, irreducible fractures, whereas volar miniplate open reduction and internal fixation was reserved for cases with relatively preserved articular surfaces. Although the authors acknowledge this selection bias within their methodology, they fail to account for its profound impact on their final conclusions. This structured severity imbalance renders baseline characteristics fundamentally incomparable between groups. As established in orthopedic methodology, differences in postoperative outcomes under these conditions cannot be reliably attributed to the therapeutic performance of the techniques themselves, but rather to the initial fracture morphology.2

Second, the application of a post hoc power analysis also introduces interpretive limitations. Statistical power calculations are traditionally designed as prospective tools to determine sample size requirements before data collection. Estimating power retrospectively based on the observed effect size, a concept often described as the retrospective power fallacy, mathematically mirrors the observed P-value without offering new insights into sample adequacy. This calculation cannot easily overcome the limited precision inherent to a small cohort of 15 patients per group.3

Third, the assertion of clinical equivalence based on the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) analysis reveals a discrepancy. The text indicates that the 95% confidence interval for the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand score difference remained entirely below the conservative MCID threshold of 6.8 points. However, Table 3 reports a 95% CI of 0.2−8.8 points. Since the upper bound of 8.8 points exceeds the 6.8-point threshold, the interval extends into the zone of clinical relevance. While sensitivity analyses explored higher thresholds, a confidence interval encompassing the primary MCID margin limits the ability to declare absolute noninferiority.4 True equivalence typically requires a prospectively registered trial with prespecified margins.5

Fourth, and perhaps most importantly, the study’s conclusion contains a structural logical contradiction. Having found “clinical equivalence,” the authors recommend that technique selection be guided by surgeon expertise, fracture characteristics, and resource availability. These, however, are precisely the criteria used to allocate patients to each surgical group in the first place. The study thus arrives at a conclusion that retroactively legitimizes the selection bias that compromises its internal validity, completing a circular argument that the methodology does not support.

We recognize the authors’ contribution to a clinically important topic. Nevertheless, the limitations outlined above, particularly the unrandomized allocation, the retrospective power fallacy, the conflicting confidence intervals, and the circular reasoning preclude a definitive claim of clinical equivalence. These findings are best framed as hypothesis generating, highlighting the need for a rigorously designed, prospective randomized trial.
