High-intensity hydrophilic versus lipophilic statins in patients with primary biliary cholangitis: a multi-institutional analysis
Authors: Hussam Kawas, Mohammad Alabbas, Mohamad-Noor Abu-Hammour, Barish Eren, Walid Hazem, Omar T. Sims, Dian-Jung Chiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsThis large-scale, real-world study aimed to determine if high-intensity hydrophilic or high-intensity lipophilic statins more optimally reduced the incidence of all-cause mortality, all-cause hospitalization, cardiovascular, liver, and symptom-related outcomes over a 5-year follow-up period in patients with Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, propensity score matched cohort study using the TriNetX Research Network, comparing PBC patients receiving high-intensity hydrophilic (rosuvastatin ≥ 20 mg) versus high-intensity lipophilic (atorvastatin ≥ 40 mg) statins (778 patients per group).ResultsHigh-intensity hydrophilic and high-intensity lipophilic statins did not differ in all-cause mortality (HR, 0.960 [95% CI, 0.710–1.296]; p = 0.788), major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (HR, 0.870 [95% CI, 0.740–1.023]; p = 0.091), liver, symptom-related, or the majority of cardiovascular outcomes (all p-values > 0.05). However, high-intensity hydrophilic statins showed significant advantages over high-intensity lipophilic statins in all-cause hospitalizations (HR, 0.595 [95% CI, 0.497–0.713]; p < 0.001) and cerebral infarction (HR, 0.400 [95% CI, 0.224–0.712]; p = 0.001).ConclusionsIn PBC patients requiring high-intensity statin therapy, hydrophilic statins provide equivalent survival with significant advantages in all-cause hospitalization reduction (40% decrease) and stroke prevention (60% decrease). These results support the preferential use of hydrophilic statins for lipid-lowering therapy in PBC while still acknowledging that both classes represent suitable therapeutic options.

Introduction

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a chronic, progressive autoimmune liver disease characterized by immune-mediated destruction of small intrahepatic bile ducts, leading to cholestasis, fibrosis, and eventual cirrhosis [1]. PBC predominantly middle-aged women and it presents unique clinical challenges that extend beyond hepatic manifestations, particularly regarding cardiovascular risk management [1].

One of PBC’s defining metabolic abnormalities is a distinctive dyslipidemia characterized by elevated total cholesterol and lipoprotein X accumulation, resulting from impaired biliary excretion [2]. This atypical lipid profile was historically considered “non-atherogenic”; however, recent evidence challenges this assumption [3]. Traditional cardiovascular risk calculators underestimate risk in PBC patients, and emerging data show rising cardiovascular comorbidities, including a 23% increase in coronary artery disease [4].

Statins remain underutilized in chronic liver disease despite established safety across diverse hepatic conditions, including PBC [5]. The pharmacokinetic differences between hydrophilic and lipophilic statins may be particularly consequential in cholestatic conditions. Hydrophilic statins (e.g., rosuvastatin) undergo minimal hepatic metabolism and rely on renal elimination, while lipophilic statins (e.g., atorvastatin) depend on hepatic cytochrome P450 metabolism and biliary excretion. In PBC, where bile acid transport and hepatic transporter function are impaired, hydrophilic statins may offer greater pharmacokinetic advantages than lipophilic statins; however, this hypothesis has not been tested in large-scale studies [6]. This large-scale, real-world study aimed to determine if high-intensity hydrophilic or high-intensity lipophilic statins more optimally reduced the incidence of all-cause mortality, all-cause hospitalization, cardiovascular, liver, and symptom-related outcomes over a 5-year follow-up period in PBC patients.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX Research Network, a global real-world database that aggregates de-identified electronic health records across 104 academic and community health systems worldwide (Fig. 1). Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with a PBC diagnosis (ICD-10: K74.3) were included if they received high-intensity statin therapy—defined as atorvastatin ≥ 40 mg (high-intensity lipophilic group) or rosuvastatin ≥ 20 mg (high-intensity hydrophilic group). Patients were excluded if they had cirrhosis at baseline. The index event was defined as the first occurrence of PBC diagnosis concurrent with the initiation of high-intensity statin therapy.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes included all-cause mortality and all-cause hospitalizations up to 5 years of follow-up. Secondarily, cardiovascular outcomes were comprehensively assessed, including major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE)—acute myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, acute heart failure, and ischemic heart disease (ICD-10: I20–I25)—as well as liver-related complications (cirrhosis progression, ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, variceal bleeding, hepatocellular carcinoma) and symptom-related outcomes (pruritus, fatigue, depression).The analysis window extended from 1 day to 1825 days (5 years) after the index event. Patients were not uniformly followed for the full 5-year period. The median follow-up duration was 655.5 days in the high-intensity hydrophilic statin group and 696 days in the high-intensity lipophilic statin group, consistent with variable real-world EHR capture across participating institutions. Outcome ascertainment was based on ICD-10, CPT, and laboratory codes predefined within the TriNetX platform. With regards to outcome analysis, TriNetX excludes patients from a specific outcome’s survival analysis if they had that outcome before the start of the analysis window.

Statistical analysis

To minimize selection bias and confounding while optimizing covariate balance between treatment groups 1:1 propensity score matching was performed based on demographics (age, sex, race/ethnicity), comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, cerebral infarction, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), PBC-related medications (obeticholic acid, cholestyramine), and key laboratory parameters including liver function tests, lipid panels, and markers of disease severity. This was performed using the TriNetX analytics platform, which utilizes a standardized automated matching algorithm based on available structured data elements in the electronic health record. The 1:1 matching strategy was selected over variable ratio matching (e.g., 2:1 or 3:1) to prioritize matching quality and minimize residual confounding. Matching success was evaluated using standardized differences < 0.1 and p-values > 0.05 for balanced covariates.

Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) was not included as a matching variable because statin class selection represents an independent clinical decision from PBC-specific therapy, and the TriNetX platform does not systematically capture biochemical response to UDCA—the clinically meaningful prognostic variable.

Baseline characteristics were compared using appropriate statistical tests, with successful matching defined as standardized differences < 0.1. Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method of survival analysis, with log-rank tests for group comparisons. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using Cox proportional hazards modeling. Risk ratios were computed for binary outcomes. All analyses were performed using the TriNetX analytics platform with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our findings to potential unmeasured confounding, E-values were used for the calculated hazard. The E-value represents the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the treatment and the outcome to fully explain away an observed effect. E-values greater than 2.0 indicate that our findings are robust to unmeasured confounding, as only a strong confounder could negate the observed association. Conversely, E-values closer to 1.0 suggest the observed effect could be explained by relatively weak unmeasured confounding.

Results

Sample characteristics

The study sample included 4,222 patients before propensity score matching; of these, 782 patients used high-intensity hydrophilic statins and 3,440 used high-intensity lipophilic statins. After propensity score matching, 1,556 patients (mean [SD] age, 69.2 [11.4] years; 74.9% female and 22.4% male) were included in the study: 778 with high-intensity hydrophilic statins and 778 with high-intensity lipophilic statins (Fig. 1). Using standardized mean differences, covariate means between the high-intensity hydrophilic and high-intensity lipophilic statin groups were well-balanced except for total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and albumin (standardized mean differences > 0.1 for all), reflecting expected pharmacologic differences between statin classes. In terms of race and ethnicity, 29 patients (3.7%) were Asian, 68 (8.7%) were Black or African American, 584 (75.0%) were White, and 68 (8.7%) were Hispanic or Latino in the hydrophilic group (Table 1).

Baseline and clinical characteristics of the study cohort

Primary outcomes

Compared with high-intensity lipophilic statins, high-intensity hydrophilic statins were associated with statistically significant reductions in all-cause hospitalizations (HR, 0.595 [95% CI, 0.497–0.713]; E-value, 2.22) (Fig. 1). There was no significant difference in all-cause mortality (HR, 0.960 [95% CI, 0.710–1.296]; E-value, 1.51).

Secondary outcomes

The analysis of cardiovascular outcomes revealed comparable efficacy between the two statin classes with one exception (Fig. 2). High-intensity hydrophilic statins were associated with statistically significant reductions in the incidence of cerebral infarction (HR, 0.400 [95% CI, 0.224–0.712]; E-value, 4.44). However, there were no significant differences between the two high-intensity statin groups in all-cause mortality (HR, 0.960 [95% CI, 0.710–1.296]; E-value, 1.51), major adverse cardiovascular events (HR, 0.870 [95% CI, 0.740–1.023]; E-value, 1.61), acute myocardial infarction (HR, 1.043 [95% CI, 0.620–1.754]; E-value, 1.25), or acute heart failure (HR, 0.767 [95% CI, 0.496–1.187]; E-value, 1.93).

Liver-related outcomes showed no significant differences with similar rates of cirrhosis (HR, 1.326 [95% CI, 0.941–1.868]; E-value, 1.61), ascites (HR, 1.023 [95% CI, 0.648–1.614]; E-value, 1.42), and hepatic encephalopathy (HR, 1.484 [95% CI, 0.774–2.844]; E-value, 2.25). Similarly, symptom-related outcomes were comparable between groups, with numerically lower but non-significant rates of pruritus (HR, 0.751 [95% CI, 0.465–1.215]; E-value, 1.79), fatigue (HR, 0.845 [95% CI, 0.584–1.223]; E-value, 1.15), and depression (HR, 0.676 [95% CI, 0.444–1.029]; E-value, 1.22) in the high-intensity hydrophilic group.

Event-free survival

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that the cumulative probability of being event-free was significantly lower with high-intensity hydrophilic statins compared to high-intensity lipophilic statins for all-cause hospitalizations and cerebral infarction (Fig. 3). For all-cause hospitalizations, the survival curves began to diverge within the first 6 months of follow-up, with high-intensity hydrophilic statins consistently maintaining higher event-free survival than high-intensity lipophilic statins throughout the 5-year observation period (63% vs. 48%, log-rank test p < 0.001). Similarly, for cerebral infarction, survival curves showed early divergence favoring high-intensity hydrophilic statins over high-intensity lipophilic statins, with the protective effect becoming evident within the first year and persisting throughout follow-up (96% vs. 88%, log-rank p = 0.001).

Discussion

This study conducted a large-scale, real-world comparative analysis of high-intensity hydrophilic versus high-intensity lipophilic statins in patients with PBC evaluating all-cause mortality, all-cause hospitalization, cardiovascular, liver, and symptom-related outcomes over a 5-year follow-up period. Several main findings emerged from our analysis. First, Our findings demonstrate equivalent overall safety and cardiovascular protection between the two statin classes; however, high-intensity hydrophilic statins provided significant advantages than high-intensity lipophilic statins in terms of all-cause hospitalization and stroke prevention. Specifically, high-intensity hydrophilic statins reduced the risk of all-cause hospitalizations by 40% across the 5-year follow-up period. Unlike lipophilic statins, which undergo extensive hepatic metabolism via CYP3A4 and rely on biliary excretion, hydrophilic statins like rosuvastatin are minimally metabolized by the liver (< 10%) and are predominantly eliminated through renal excretion [5, 6]. In PBC patients with impaired bile acid transport and potentially altered hepatic transporter function, this pharmacokinetic profile may result in more predictable drug exposure and reduced metabolic burden on an already compromised liver [6]. Hydrophilic statins demonstrate minimal interaction with CYP450 enzymes, potentially reducing complications in PBC patients who often require complex polypharmacy, including ursodeoxycholic acid, immunosuppressants, and medications for symptom management [7]. This reduced interaction potential may contribute to fewer medication-related adverse events requiring hospitalization [8]. Additionally, patients may receive combination therapy with fibrates (particularly bezafibrate or fenofibrate), which have now demonstrated anticholestatic efficacy [9]. The concurrent use of fibrates and statins carries increased risk of muscle toxicity (myopathy and rhabdomyolysis) [9]. Hydrophilic statins like rosuvastatin, with minimal hepatic metabolism and reduced CYP450 interactions, may offer a more favorable safety profile when combined with fibrates compared to lipophilic statins, potentially contributing to the observed reduction in hospitalizations.

Second, the 60% risk reduction in cerebral infarction that was observed challenges historical assumptions about cardiovascular risk in PBC patients and provides compelling evidence for aggressive lipid management in this population [10]. The clinical significance of this finding extends beyond statistical measures. PBC patients face a complex disease trajectory involving progressive liver dysfunction, debilitating symptoms including fatigue and pruritus, and increasingly recognized cardiovascular complications [11]. Stroke represents a particularly devastating outcome in this population, as it can severely compromise quality of life in patients already burdened by chronic liver disease symptoms. The substantial stroke reduction seen with high-intensity hydrophilic statins suggests that these patients may derive greater cerebrovascular protection than previously appreciated, potentially due to the chronic inflammatory state, endothelial dysfunction, and unique metabolic milieu characteristics of cholestatic liver disease.

Third, equivalent 5-year survival rates (91% for high-intensity hydrophilic statins and 91% for high-intensity lipophilic statins) demonstrate that both statin classes can be safely administered to PBC patients without compromising long-term outcomes, despite longstanding concerns about statin hepatotoxicity in patients with liver disease [12]. Historical reluctance to prescribe statins in patients with chronic liver disease stems from early case reports and concerns about hepatic adverse effects, despite accumulating evidence of statin safety across diverse hepatic conditions [13]. The absence of excess mortality with either statin class, combined with the observed reduction in hospitalizations and stroke prevention with high-intensity hydrophilic statins strengthens empirical evidence in support of proactive use of statins for cardiovascular risk management in PBC patients.

Fourth, the risk of cirrhosis, ascites, and hepatic encephalopathy, pruritus, fatigue, and depression did not differ between the two high-intensity statin groups. Unfortunately, propensity matching did not overcome the imbalance on albumin (i.e., a proxy for liver disease severity) which could have limited the potential for observational differences of statistical significance on cirrhosis and other outcomes that are directly and indirectly consequential to cirrhosis. Future studies with randomization, longer follow-up periods, and more sophisticated measures of PBC disease activity, including Mayo Risk Score and histologic staging, are needed to reassess these respective outcomes.

The study had notable limitations. An important limitation of our all-cause hospitalization finding is the inability to distinguish between PBC-related hospitalizations and those attributable to other causes within the TriNetX platform. This represents a significant limitation of administrative database research. Future prospective studies with detailed adjudication of hospitalization causes are needed to determine whether the observed reduction is primarily driven by cardiovascular benefits, improved tolerability, or other factors. The residual imbalances in total cholesterol, LDL-C, and albumin levels after propensity score matching (standardized mean differences > 0.1) may reflect both inherent pharmacologic differences between statin classes and potentially unmeasured disease severity factors. The albumin imbalance is particularly concerning as it serves as a proxy for hepatic synthetic function. This residual confounding may have obscured potential differences in liver-related outcomes between the two statin groups. Future studies should incorporate more advanced measures of PBC disease activity, including the Mayo Risk Score, alkaline phosphatase response to therapy, and histologic staging. Reliance on ICD-10 coding for outcome ascertainment may potentially lead to misclassification bias. It is probable that the frequency of symptom-related outcomes (i.e., pruritus, fatigue) may have been underreported and hence underestimated, as these often go unrecorded unless they prompt specific medical encounters. Though we performed competing risk analyses, the complex interplay between cardiovascular events, liver disease progression, and mortality in PBC patients may necessitate more sophisticated modeling approaches in future studies that account for time-varying covariates and changes in disease trajectory. Of equal importance, the study had noteworthy strengths. Unlike previous studies focusing solely on lipid parameters or short-term safety, this analysis evaluated a broad spectrum of clinically relevant outcomes including cardiovascular events, liver-related complications, symptom burden, and healthcare utilization over a 5-year follow-up period. To date, this study represents the largest comparative analysis of high-intensity hydrophilic versus high-intensity lipophilic statins. The study included data from multiple institutions (104 institutions), which in turn enhanced generalizability. Additionally, the inclusion of E-values quantitatively assessed the robustness of unmeasured confounding.

Conclusion

High-intensity hydrophilic and high-intensity lipophilic statins appear to provide cardiovascular and mortality protections overtime. This should reassure clinicians of the safety of both therapeutic options. Equally important, high-intensity hydrophilic statins appear to offer significant advantages in reducing hospitalizations and preventing strokes. Prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to validate these findings and to establish definitive treatment guidelines. Until such evidence becomes available, our results support the preferential use of high-intensity hydrophilic statins in PBC patients requiring lipid-lowering therapy, while acknowledging that both statin classes represent suitable therapeutic options with equivalent long-term safety profiles.
