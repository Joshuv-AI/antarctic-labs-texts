Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing Reveals Granzyme K+ CD8 + T Cells as a Target for Mitigating Plaque Instability Following Radiation
Authors: Manru Shi, Quan Lin, Shaohong Fang, Cheng Ma, Weiwei Xu, Di Sun, Naixin Wang, Jingxuan Cui, Zhonghua Tong, Yuwu Chen, Xing Luo, Sining Hu, Tuo Huang, Yunlong He, Yang Cao, Jiayin Liu, Jiaqi Li, Anxin Gu, Xiuwen Lan, Meng Chi, Chao Wang, Jiaqi Gu, Siqi Tian, Jiaao Liu, Zixuan Liu, Maomao Zhang, Mingyan E
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac issues following radiotherapy are increasingly prevalent among patients with thoracic cancer and coronary disease. However, the mechanisms underlying radiotherapy‐induced plaque instability and changes in plaque characteristics on imaging remain unclear. This study used single‐cell RNA sequencing to identify key features of vulnerable plaques following radiotherapy.MethodsWe applied dual‐mode synchronized optical coherence tomography‐intravascular ultrasound imaging in a rabbit model to reveal the characteristics of vulnerable plaques at various time points postradiotherapy. We then conducted single‐cell RNA sequencing on atherosclerotic lesions from ApoE −/− (apolipoprotein E−/−) mice across 3 stages: nonirradiated, early irradiation, and advanced irradiation. Bioinformatics was used to analyze cell populations, trajectories, and interaction differences among the groups. This was followed by pathological staining, flow cytometry, gene knockout, and antibody depletion.ResultsIn vivo optical coherence tomography‐intravascular ultrasound imaging at the initial stage accurately evaluated radiation exposure's effect on the stability of rabbit carotid plaques. High‐throughput single‐cell transcriptomics mapped the radiotherapy‐induced immune system remodeling in atherosclerotic lesions, and identified a granzyme K+ CD8+ (cluster of differentiation 8+) T‐cell subpopulation. The infiltrating cells highly expressed the cytotoxic molecule GZMB (granzyme B) in the plaques. The absence of CD8+ T cells or GZMB ameliorated radiotherapy‐exacerbated atherosclerotic lesions. This demonstrates the pathogenic function of GZMK+ (granzyme K+) CD8+ cytotoxic T cells in promoting radiotherapy‐induced atherosclerotic lesion progression.ConclusionsWe identified characteristic arterial imaging changes resulting from radiation injury, accurately and dynamically assessing the effects of radiation exposure on plaque stability. Radiation exposure affected plaque stability by modulating GZMK+ CD8+ T‐cell activation and GZMB function, suggesting GZMB as a mediator of radio‐cardiovascular injury and a potential therapeutic target.Keywords: atherosclerosis, CD8 T cell, optical coherence tomography, radiotherapy, single‐cell RNA sequencingSubject Categories: Coronary Artery Disease, Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), Animal Models of Human Disease

Malignant neoplasms and cardiovascular conditions are the leading causes of mortality across the globe, with the number of affected individuals increasing steadily. 1 The incidence of cardiovascular diseases is higher among patients with cancer, and vice versa, than in the general population. 2 , 3 , 4 Among individuals who have survived cancer, heart‐related illnesses are the second most frequent causes of illness and death, following the recurrence of cancer. 5

Radiotherapy is a primary treatment for malignant tumors, with more than half of all cases requiring radiotherapy. 6 However, in recent years, studies have shown a significant increase in adverse cardiac events following radiotherapy in patients with thoracic malignancies combined with coronary heart disease. 7 , 8 , 9 Radiation injury is a serious threat to the long‐term survival of these patients. 10 , 11 , 12 Nevertheless, the characteristic alterations in arterial plaque stability induced by radiotherapy, and the specific mechanisms responsible, have not yet been elucidated. Atherosclerosis is a progressive inflammatory condition characterized by the disruption of endothelial function, accumulation of lipids, proliferation of smooth muscle cells, apoptosis, and necrosis, as well as localized and systemic inflammatory responses. 13 , 14 Disease progression is significantly influenced by both the innate and adaptive immune responses. 15 Within the atherosclerotic microenvironment, a delicate balance is maintained between proatherosclerotic and antiatherosclerotic anti‐inflammatory reactions. 16 , 17 , 18 This equilibrium is orchestrated by a complex interplay of various vascular elements and immune cells including T cells, monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, B cells, and foam cells. 19 , 20 Single‐cell transcriptome sequencing has revealed differences in the immune microenvironments of stable and vulnerable plaques, providing new insights into atherosclerosis immunotherapy. 21 , 22

In the present study, we used dual‐modality optical coherence tomography‐intravascular ultrasound (OCT‐IVUS) imaging 23 , 24 , 25 to identify significant features of vulnerable plaques following radiotherapy. Subsequently, we used single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) technology to (1) map the cell atlas of existing irradiated atherosclerotic lesions in early and advanced stages, (2) identify potential disease‐causing cellular subsets in early‐stage irradiated lesions, and (3) determine shifts in cell‐to‐cell communication under different conditions, outlining a control system tailored to each cell type.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Rabbit Model of Atherosclerosis and Irradiation Procedure

Forty‐four male New Zealand White rabbits (aged 6 months, weighing 2.3–3.2 kg) underwent carotid artery balloon injury 26 and were fed a 0.25% cholesterol diet for 12 weeks. Animals were included if the carotid artery balloon injury was successful and excluded if vessel wall perforation occurred or if they died prematurely, preventing the collection of behavioral and histological data. The rabbits were randomly divided into 4 groups: HFD (high‐fat diet), HFD + radiotherapy (1W), HFD + RT (2W), and HFD + RT (4W). Randomization was performed using the =RAND() function in Microsoft Excel. The right carotid artery was dissected under isoflurane anesthesia, and a 2F Fogarty catheter was used to de‐endothelialize the common carotid artery 3 times. One rabbit in the HFD + RT(2W) group died due to infection. Rabbits were euthanized and the arteries harvested at 1, 2, or 4 weeks after irradiation; control rabbits underwent the same procedure without irradiation. The entire aorta, including its root, and blood samples were collected for subsequent experiments. During irradiation, the neck region, including the carotid arteries, was exposed to a single dose of 14 Gy (tested for dose‐dependent effects; Figure S1 through S1) using 12 mA, 250‐kV x‐rays filtered through 0.6 mm of copper. 27 The testing order was randomized daily, with each animal tested at a different time. Rabbits were secured in acrylic fixtures, and areas not intended for irradiation were protected with lead shielding. The individual rabbit was considered the experimental unit.

The protocol has been approved by the ethics committee of Harbin Medical University (HMUIRB2022010).

Dual‐Modality OCT‐IVUS Imaging

During in vivo imaging, the IVUS‐OCT catheter was navigated through the carotid artery. The probe was rotated within the catheter sheath, with saline acting as an acoustic coupling medium. The carotid artery was imaged by slowly retracting the catheter at 0.5 mm/s, with both IVUS and OCT images shown in real‐time. If a cross‐section of interest was found, the pull‐back was halted for precise targeting. After the imaging procedure, the aorta was preserved in formaldehyde, and particular segments were prepared for staining to facilitate histological examination. 28

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay

Blood samples were collected from rabbits and humans with or without radiation exposure. Subsequently, the samples were spun at a speed of 3000 revolutions per minute for 10 minutes to collect the cellular sediment. Serum concentrations of IL (interleukin)‐6, TNF (tumor necrosis factor)‐α, TGF (transforming growth factor)‐β1, IL‐1β, and GZMB (granzyme B) were analyzed in the pellets using an ELISA. 29

Mice and Irradiation Procedure

Ninety‐six male ApoE −/− (apolipoprotein E−/−), 20 GZMB −/− ApoE −/−, and 16 recombination‐activating gene 2 (Rag2)−/−apoE−/− mice were purchased from the Cyagen Laboratory, all on a C57BL/6 background, aged 8 to 10 weeks, and weighing an average of 33.2±1.1 g. They were randomly divided into groups for radiation or sham treatment (nonradiation control and early stage) 4 days after radiation and advanced stage (2 weeks after radiation) and housed in ventilated cages with free access to a diet containing 3.7% fat and water. Random numbers were generated using the standard=RAND() function in Microsoft Excel. One mouse in the HFD + radiotherapy (advanced stage) group died due to infection. The irradiated mice group was exposed to a 14 Gy x‐ray dose, tested by dose‐dependent effects (Figure S1 through S1), directed at the neck region, which included the carotid arteries, the aortic arch, and the cardiac base. During the experiments, the mice were secured in lead‐shielded jigs to prevent movement. Testing order was randomized daily, with each animal tested at a different time each test day. The individual mouse was considered the experimental unit. Sample sizes were determined based on rules of thumb.

The protocol has been approved by the ethics committee of Harbin Medical University (HMUIRB2022010).

Human Sample Collection

Carotid atherosclerotic plaque specimens (n=3) were collected from symptomatic individuals who had transient ischemic attacks or minor strokes due to carotid stenosis and subsequently underwent carotid endarterectomy within 2 to 14 days of symptom onset. Comprehensive clinical details of these patients are provided in Table S1. The samples were obtained from the Department of Cardiology, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Following collection, the plaques were divided into 5‐mm‐thick transverse sections. Every alternate section was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C, whereas the remaining sections were fixed in formalin for 24 hours, decalcified for 4 hours, and then embedded in paraffin for histological analysis.

The protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital (KY2025‐09). All participants provided written informed consent. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Blinding

In this study, the experimental procedures for each animal were conducted by 4 distinct investigators, with specific roles assigned as follows. The initial investigator (Q.L.) administered radiation treatment according to the randomization schedule and was the only individual aware of treatment group assignments. Meanwhile, a second investigator (N.W.) managed the anesthesia process, and a third investigator (M.S.) performed the surgical operations. Last, a fourth investigator (J.C.), who was also blinded to the treatment details, undertook the data evaluation.

Pathological Staining

The hearts and carotid arteries were flushed with phosphate buffered saline, embedded in a freezing mold with a tissue‐freezing agent, and sectioned from the aortic sinus extending to the upper part of the aorta. The sections were subsequently treated with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for fixation and subjected to Oil Red O, hematoxylin and eosin, and Masson trichrome staining as per the described protocol. 30

Immunofluorescence

Slides that had been thawed were immersed in chilled acetone at a temperature of −20 °C for fixation for 10 minutes, then washed and incubated with 0.3% Triton‐X 100 at 37 °C for 30 minutes. Next, they were incubated with the primary antibody (Granzyme K Catalog number PA5‐100743, Granzyme B ab255598, CD8 (cluster of differentiation 8) ab308264, CD68 (cluster of differentiation 68) ab53444, CD31 ((cluster of differentiation 31) ab281583, and SMA‐α (smooth muscle actin‐α) number 48938) at 4 °C overnight. The following day, slides were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) (0.5 g/L) was used to stain the nuclei for 10 minutes, followed by imaging with a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 700). 30

scRNA‐Seq Data Preprocessing

FASTQ files were aligned to the GRCm39 mouse reference genome using Cell Ranger version 8.0.1 (×10 Genomics), summarizing unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts per barcode. Seurat 31 version 4.0.0 was used to analyze the UMI count matrix. Low‐quality cells and potential multiplets were filtered out based on criteria including gene numbers (<200), UMI counts (<1000), log10GenesPerUMI (<0.7), mitochondrial RNA UMIs (>10%), and hemoglobin RNA UMIs (>5%). DoubletFinder 32 version 2.0.3 was applied to detect possible doublets. NormalizeData function was used for library size normalization, using the LogNormalize method to scale and log‐transform gene expression data. Identification of the 2000 most variable genes was achieved through the application of the Seurat FindVariableGenes function, with the settings of FastExpMean and FastLogVMR being applied. Principal component analysis was conducted using the RunPCA function to simplify the data. Clustering of cells was conducted based on gene expression signatures using the FindClusters function. Subsequently, the 2‐dimensional representation of the cells was generated using the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) method, as implemented by the RunUMAP function. The FindAllMarkers function, using the presto test, was used to detect signature genes within each cluster. To find differentially expressed genes, the FindMarkers function with the presto test was applied, setting a threshold of P<0.05 and |log2foldchange| >0.58 for significant expression differences. The enrichment analyses for differentially expressed genes in terms of Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were performed using R version 4.0.3, using the hypergeometric distribution as the statistical framework.

OE Biotech, based in Shanghai, China, undertook both the sequencing and the associated bioinformatics analysis.

RNA Velocity Analysis

RNA velocity analysis using the Python script velocyto.py 33 was used to reorganize the spliced and unspliced reads, which were sourced from the Cell Ranger output directory. The determination of RNA velocity for specific genes across various cells and the placement of RNA velocity vectors into a lower‐dimensional framework were accomplished with the R package velocyto.R (version 0.633). Following this, the velocity fields were integrated onto the UMAP visualization produced by Seurat.

Single‐Cell rEgulatory Network Inference and Clustering Analysis

Single‐Cell rEgulatory Network Inference and Clustering analysis was conducted using the RcisTarget and GRNboost databases with Single‐Cell rEgulatory Network Inference and Clustering 34 software (version 1.2.4), which corresponds to RcisTarget (version 1.10.0) and AUCell (version 1.12.0), all configured with their default parameters. The RcisTarget tool was initially applied to identify transcription factor‐binding motifs that were overrepresented among the genes. Subsequently, the AUCell tool (version 1.12.0) was used to assess the activity levels of each regulon group within each cell. To determine the cell type specificity for each predicted regulon, a score was generated based on the Jensen‐Shannon divergence, which measures the dissimilarity between 2 probability distributions. This score was derived from the divergence analysis between the binary vectors of regulon activity overlaps and the specific cell type assignments. 35 The scFunctions tool was used to determine the connection specificity index across all regulons (https://github.com/FloWuenne/scFunctions/).

CellChat Analysis

Investigation of cell–cell communication was conducted using the CellChat 36 R package (version 1.1.3). Initially, the normalized gene expression data were inputted to establish a CellChat object through the createCellChat function. Subsequently, the data underwent preprocessing with the identifyOverExpressedGenes, identifyOverExpressedInteractions, and projectData functions, all operating with default settings. Next, we used the computeCommunProb, filterCommunication (with the parameter of at least 10 cells), and computeCommunProbPathway functions to detect possible ligand‐receptor pairs. After that, the aggregateNet function was applied to integrate the cell communication network.

CD8 + T‐Cell Depletion

Selective depletion of cellular subsets was achieved by injecting 400 μg of depleting antibody into the peritoneal cavity twice weekly, starting 1 day before therapy. Specifically, for CD8+ T cells, the antibody used was anti‐CD8α (clone 2.43; BioXCell). Depletion was confirmed by flow cytometry of peripheral blood mononuclear cells. 37

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as mean±SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov and Shapiro‐Wilk tests, q‐q plots, and histograms. For data comparison, the Student t test was used for 2‐group analysis of normally distributed data, whereas ANOVA with the Bonferroni post hoc test was applied for multiple‐group analysis. A P value <0.05 was considered significant. Sample sizes were determined based on power analysis, existing literature, and previous experience. Randomization was used to distribute mice among experimental groups, with researchers blinded to group assignments whenever possible. Representative images were selected from repeated experiments that closely represented the mean values.

RESULTS

Plaque Characteristics of Irradiated Existing Atherosclerotic Lesions Via OCT‐IVUS Dual‐Mode Synchronized Imaging

OCT‐IVUS dual‐mode synchronized imaging was used to evaluate the effect of radiation exposure on arterial plaques in vivo. Rabbits with right carotid artery balloon injuries were fed an HFD and randomly assigned to radiation or sham treatment groups, followed by atherosclerosis induction for 12 weeks. Subsequently, the rabbits received targeted radiation treatment to the neck region (Figure 1A). Relevant indices used to assess plaque stability were significantly different between irradiated and nonirradiated groups (Figure 1B); specifically, the plaque lipid content (P=0.0351), maximum lipid angle (P=0.01), fiber cap thickness (P=0.03), and number of macrophages (P=0.01) increased (Figure 1C). Immunohistochemical analysis of plaque features revealed increased macrophage infiltration and decreased expression of the fibrous cap markerSMA‐α, confirming the OCT‐IVUS imaging results (Figure 1B). Subsequently, OCT imaging of the arterial plaques was performed at different time points (1, 2, and 4 weeks after radiotherapy) (Figure 1D). Plaque area (Figure S1), lipid plaque percentage (Figure 1E), and mean lipid arc (Figure S1) significantly increased in the early stages after radiotherapy, whereas the minimum lumen area (Figure S1) and fiber cap thickness (Figure 1F) decreased. The necrotic core peaked 2 weeks after radiotherapy (Figure S1). Additionally, levels of IL‐6 (P<0.001), TNF‐α (P<0.01), and IL‐1β (P<0.001) in peripheral blood increased during the early stages postradiotherapy (Figure S1). These results suggest that radiotherapy promotes plaque progression and increases plaque instability.

Radiation Exacerbates Atherosclerotic Plaque Progression and Instability in Atherosclerotic Mice

To evaluate the impact of radiation on atherosclerotic lesion progression, ApoE −/− mice fed an HFD were randomly assigned to early treatment (4 days postirradiation), advanced treatment (2 weeks postirradiation), or a control group. After 12 weeks of HFD‐induced atherosclerosis, the carotid artery, aortic arch, and the cardiac base were subjected to a localized single dose of 14 Gy radiotherapy. Frontal analysis of lesions in the aortic root, quantitative display of atherosclerotic lesions (Figure 2A), and Oil Red O staining showed increased lipid area in the late postradiotherapy period (Figure 2B). The necrotic core and plaque area were quantified using hematoxylin and eosin staining. Significant difference in plaque area were observed between the radiotherapy and sham groups in the aortic root (Figure 2C), although no significant differences were noted in necrotic core proportions (Figure S1). Next, we investigated whether plaque stability was affected by irradiation, an adverse event associated with atherosclerosis. Notably, fiber cap thickness measurements revealed that early atherosclerotic lesions in irradiated ApoE −/− mice had thinner fiber caps (Figure 2D) and reduced collagen deposition (Figure 2E) compared with sham‐treated mice. This indicated that plaque instability was evident at an early stage after radiotherapy. We then examined the relevant indicators of early arterial plaque instability after radiotherapy using immunofluorescence. Immunofluorescence analysis showed increased CD68+ macrophage area and disrupted continuity of endothelial cells in the CD31‐positive region in the radiotherapy group. The levels of the fibrous cap component, smooth muscle actin, were significantly reduced (Figure 2F). In conclusion, in atherosclerotic mice, radiotherapy first causes atherosclerotic plaque instability and then promotes plaque progression.

Cell Atlas of Irradiated Existing Atherosclerotic Plaque

This study aimed to comprehensively investigate the cellular composition of irradiated atherosclerotic lesions. Three samples of irradiated existing atherosclerotic lesions were collected, 1 each from the nonradiation control, 4 days postradiotherapy (early stage), and 2‐weeks postradiotherapy (advanced stage) groups (Figure 3A). Freshly collected aortas of mice from the carotid artery to the base of the heart were enzymatically digested to create a suspension of single cells, followed by scRNA‐seq using the ×10 Genomics Chromium platform. The sequences obtained were then demultiplexed, mapped to the reference genome, and underwent quality control assessments (Figure S2A and S2B), resulting in 6838 to 10 691 high‐quality cells being selected for subsequent analysis (Table S2). Unsupervised clustering and UMAP visualization were used to organize and interpret the scRNA‐seq data. Although automatic tools for cell‐type identification exist, manual curation is still required to minimize the potential for mistakes. Nineteen subclusters were identified, representing 10 predominant cell types (Figure S3A and S3B, Table S3). The 10 clusters were delineated based on recognized markers, encompassing populations of B cells, T cells, plasma cells, natural killer (NK) cells, myeloid cells, neutrophils, innate lymphoid cells, fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, and endothelial cells (Figure 3B and 3C). These 10 clusters exhibited distinct gene expression profiles and transcriptomes (Figure 3C). The T‐cell cluster and fibroblasts were the largest immune and nonimmune populations, respectively (Figure 3D). Immune‐related pathways, including antigen presentation, NK cell cytotoxicity, T‐cell activation, and IL‐17 signaling, were upregulated after radiotherapy in both the early stage and advanced stage (Figure 3E and 3F).

Specific Infiltration Patterns of Immune Cells Contribute to Irradiated Atherosclerotic Plaque Instability

UMAPs were generated using only immune cells to investigate the immune context of irradiated atherosclerotic lesions. Significant differences in distribution were observed between the sham and irradiated groups at various time points, as confirmed by cell proportion histograms (Figure S4A and S4B). T cells of the adaptive immune system were predominant in irradiated atherosclerotic lesions (Figure S4B). Density plots revealed notable diversity between the control and irradiated groups at different time points, suggesting that the precise subtype distribution within each immune cell class plays a key role in atherogenesis and warrants further examination (Figure S4C).

The diversity within T‐cell populations was further explored to identify the essential functional subtypes associated with irradiation‐induced atherosclerosis. Compared with the control group, the ratios of CD4+ to CD8+ T cells were inverted, with distinct cell groups emerging within the plaques (Figure S4D). Diversity was also observed within the CD4+ and CD8+ T cells that had infiltrated the plaques, indicating that functional T‐cell subsets were intricately interwoven (Figure S4E). In total, 8046 T cells were clustered into 4 types: CD8+ T cells (CD8a), CD4+ T cells (CD4), proliferative T cells (Mki67, Tpx2, Hmgb2, and Stmn1), and γδ T cells (Trdc) (Figure 4A and 4B).

CD8+ T cells were divided into 4 subclusters (c01, c02, c07, and c09) (Figure 4A). CD8‐c09 cells specifically overexpressed Ccr7, Lef1, Sell, and other initial signature genes and were annotated as naive T cells. Additionally, genes associated with cytotoxic functions, including Prf1, Gzmm, Gzmb, and Gzmk, exhibited high expression levels. CD8‐c01 and CD8‐c02 were defined as cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), with CD8‐c02 showing characteristic expression of Pdcd1 compared with CD8‐c01 subsets. The Eomes gene indicated that these subsets were in the terminal differentiation stage. CD8‐c07 cells exhibited high expression of NK cell function‐related genes (Klra1, Klra7, Klrb1c, Klrc2, and Klre1) and were defined as NK‐like T cells 38 (Figure 4C and 4D).

Similarly, CD4+ T cells were divided into 4 subclusters (c03, c05, c06, and c10) (Figure 4A), and CD4‐c10 overexpressed Ccr7, Lef1, Sell, and other initial signature genes and were annotated as naive T cells. CD4‐c06 cells demonstrated high expression of Foxp3 and Tnfrsf9 and were defined as regulatory T cells. CD4‐c05 cells exhibited overexpression of Lag3, Il21, and Tox2, and were defined as follicular helper T cells. Finally, CD4‐c03 cells showed high expression of Cd40Lg and were designated as helper T cells (Figure 4C and 4D).

The c‐08 cells overexpressed Mki67, Tpx2, Hmgb2, Stmn1, and other proliferative genes and were annotated as proliferative T cells. The c‐04 cells displayed high expression of Trdc and were defined as γδ T cells 39 , 40 (Figure 4C and 4D).

Notably, the proportion of cytotoxic CD8+ T‐cell subclusters increased in irradiated atherosclerotic lesions at an early stage (Figure 4E), which was primarily characterized by plaque instability (Figure 2B and 2D). As expected, atherosclerotic lesion‐related pathways, including positive regulation of CTL differentiation, were enriched in the early stage but not in the advanced stage (Figure S4F and S4G). The expression of the cytotoxic marker Gzmk in CD8+ T cells from apoE −/− mice with ES radiation was validated through immunofluorescent detection of the GZMK (granzyme K) protein, which colocalize with CD8 (Figure 4F). Overall, these results confirm that cytotoxic CD8+ T cells in irradiated atherosclerotic lesions at an early stage contribute to plaque instability.

Radiotherapy Drives the Enrichment of Distinct CD8 + T‐Cell States

CD8+ T cells play a central role in the formation of vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques. 41 Despite similar numbers of CD8+ T cells in irradiated and unirradiated lesions, significant differences were observed in subpopulation numbers. These expression variations prompted our hypothesis that the qualitative state of CD8+ T cells could be a significant factor in disease outcomes. To explore this, CD8+ T cells were subjected to velocity analysis 33 , 42 to reconstruct maturation dynamics (Figure 5A; Figure S5A). Velocity length was overlaid on velocity vector fields (Figure S5B and S5C), revealing 2 main CD8+ T‐cell states. Typically, CD8+ T‐cell differentiation is complex, involving multiple stages and subgroups. Nevertheless, increased velocity length indicated the capacity to modify phenotypic states along defined trajectories or lineages within the circulation. Irradiated atherosclerotic lesions followed unique maturation paths, culminating in 2 distinct terminal states: CTLactive and NKactive (Figure 5A through 5C). The apex of this trajectory, characterized by substantial velocity lengths, indicated cells undergoing differentiation (Figure S5A through S5C). Irradiated CD8+ T cells predominantly transitioned from a naive state (marked by Lef1 expression; Figure S5D) to a CTL‐active state characterized by Gzmk expression (clusters 1–2) (Figure 5D) and activation of the GZMK‐mediated programmed cell death signaling pathway and inflammatory response (Figure S5E). The contracted NK‐active state was defined by Klra7 expression (Figure 5D), which is predicted to stimulate the C‐type lectin receptor signaling pathway and regulate NK cell activation (Figure S5E). The continuum of CD8+ T‐cell states ultimately stabilized into CTLactive and NKactive phenotypes. Expanded CTL‐active CD8+ T cells exhibited high cytotoxicity following radiotherapy. Subsequently, we targeted GZMK + cytotoxic T cells to detect the expression of common cytotoxic genes, including GZMK, GZMB, GZMA, GZMM, and PRF. We found that GZMB expression was significantly upregulated in GZMK + CTL subclusters following radiation, although GZMK expression was not significantly altered (Figure S5F). These findings were verified by flow cytometry (Figure 5E) and immunofluorescence (Figure 5F). We also tested GZMB levels in human plaques and serum from patients undergoing radiotherapy and those who were not. Immunofluorescence revealed higher GZMB protein expression in plaques from patients receiving radiotherapy (Figure S5G), as well as in peripheral serum by ELISA (Figure S5H). The identification of distinct irradiated CD8+ T cell states prompted further investigation into the drivers of CD8+ T‐cell polarization using Single‐Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC) to reconstruct gene regulatory networks (Figure S5I). Specifically activated transcription factors and eomesodermin (51 g) drove the differentiation and development of CD8+ CTLs in the cytotoxic active state (Figure S5J), whereas CD8+ T cells in the NKactive state were regulated by Nuclear Factor‐κb‐associated Transcription Factors, such as Nfkb1 and Rel (Figure S5I). These data indicate that CD8+ T cells possess dynamic programming capabilities, leading to polarization into CTLactive and NKactive phenotypes within irradiated atherosclerotic lesions. Radiation enhances local lesion necrosis and instability by increasing the cytotoxic function of CD8+ CTLs, specifically through granulysin‐toxic GZMB secretion. Furthermore, eomesodermin is a key regulatory factor in the progression of unstable plaques promoted by radiotherapy.

Radiation Promotes Interaction Between GZMK + CD8 + T Cells and C‐C Motif Chemokine Receptor 2 (CCR2 +) Macrophages

To explore the interactions between the various cell types described above, we used the CellChat platform to examine cell–cell communication. Following early stage radiation, GZMK+CD8+ T cells emerged as a central hub of cellular communication, displaying the highest number and strength of interactions with different cell types. Nevertheless, these interactions diminished in the advanced stage (Figure S6A through S6C). At the early stage, irradiated atherosclerotic lesions exhibited a unique pathway related to CCR2+ macrophages, such as IFN (interferon)‐γ signaling 43 (Figure S6D and S6E). Given the pivotal role of macrophages in the onset, progression, and potential rupture of arterial plaques, we performed a receptor‐ligand pair analysis between GZMK+ CD8+ T cells and CCR2+ macrophage subclusters, identifying several enhanced interactions, such as IFN‐γ‐ (Ifngr1 + Ifngr1) and Macrophage Migration Inhibitory Factor (MIF)‐ (Cd74 + Cd44) (Figure S6F). Chemotactic and apoptosis‐related ligand‐receptor pairs also differed following irradiation (Figure S6F). Because CCR2 expression is associated with atherosclerosis progression, CCR2+ macrophages may promote inflammatory responses, whereas CCR2+ macrophages may be involved in tissue repair and stabilization. 43 Compared with Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid Cell 2 (TREM2+) macrophages, CCR2+ macrophages exhibited higher expression of CCR2 and IFN‐associated genes (Figure S7A, S7B and S7D). Simultaneously, the ratio of CCR2+ macrophages significantly increased following irradiation (Figure S7C). Gene Ontology analysis also revealed positive regulation of T‐cell proliferation by CCR2+ macrophages (Figure S7E).

Collectively, these results suggest that radiation promotes the interaction between GZMK+ CD8+ T cells and macrophages at the early stage, upregulating CCR2 expression, which significantly enhances macrophage activation and migration. IFN‐γ signaling may be critical for the interaction between cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and macrophages in irradiated atherosclerotic lesions.

Lack of CD8 + T Cells or GZMB Ameliorates Radiation‐Induced Atherosclerosis

To investigate the role of CD8+ T cells in atherosclerosis, we used anti‐CD8 antibodies to deplete CD8+ T cells in ApoE −/− mice. We established the advanced stage model as previously described, treated the mice with anti‐CD8 antibodies, and confirmed the absence of CD8+ T cells using flow cytometry (Figure S8A and S8B). All mice received the same radiation regimen and dose. Pathologically, atherosclerotic lesions in apoE −/− mice treated with anti‐CD8 antibodies exhibited a 20% reduction in total lesion area (Figure S8C and S8D) and a 60% increase in collagen deposition, as assessed by Masson trichrome staining (Figure S8C and S8E). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed similar changes in CD68+ and SMA+ cells (Figure S8F). These results suggest that the absence of CD8+ T cells ameliorated the progression and instability of atherosclerosis exacerbated by radiotherapy.

Having established the presence of CD8+ CTLs expressing GZMB in irradiated atherosclerotic plaques, we further examined the role of this cytotoxic factor in GZMB −/− ApoE −/−mice (Figure 6A and 6B). CD8 T Cells Deficient in GZMB Fail to Increase Atherosclerotic Lesions After Their Transfer Into Lymphocyte‐Deficient apoE −/− Mice

To determine whether GZMB was expressed and played a role in cells other than CD8+ T cells, we examined the expression of GZMB across various cell types using UMAP (Figure S9A). We found that GZMB was highly expressed only in T cells (Figure S9B). Therefore, we specifically mapped the expression of GZMB within T cells (Figure S9C) and discovered that it was predominantly expressed in CD8+ T cells (Figure S9D). This finding further confirmed our hypothesis.

To investigate the role of GZMB in CD8+ T‐cell‐mediated atherosclerosis, we conducted adoptive transfer experiments using 6×106 CD8+ T cells from various donors into lymphocyte‐deficient ApoE −/− mice 41 (Figure S9E). After 12 weeks on an HFD, the repopulation of CD8+ T cells in the spleen of recipient mice reached approximately 15% of that in apoE−/− mice (Figure S9F), and these cells were detected within atherosclerotic lesions (Figure S9G).

When conventional CD8+ T cells were transferred, radiotherapy significantly exacerbated plaque instability and progression, characterized by increased lipid content (Figure S9H and S9N), reduced collagen content (Figure S9I and S9O), and enlarged plaque area (Figure S9J and S9P).

In contrast, when GZMB −/− CD8+ T cells were transferred, radiotherapy did not significantly affect plaque instability, with no notable changes in lipid content (Figure S9K and S9N), collagen content (Figure S9L and S9O), or plaque area (Figure S9M and S9P). These findings suggest that GZMB expression in CD8+ T cells is crucial for mediating plaque instability in response to radiotherapy.

DISCUSSION

Radiotherapy is a crucial component of current cancer treatment but has raised significant concerns due to its association with chronic complications. 45 With increasing age, patients treated with radiotherapy exhibit a higher incidence of vascular diseases in the neck and peripheral heart, primarily due to radiation‐induced cardiovascular events. 46 Gabriels et al 27 demonstrated that radiotherapy can alter the metabolic characteristics and polarization of macrophages in atherosclerotic plaques, thereby promoting inflammation and plaque progression. Zhinan et al 47 found that high‐dose ionizing radiation accelerates atherosclerotic plaque progression by modulating P38/NCOA4‐driven ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in endothelial cells. Yamamoto 48 investigated the relationship between radiation‐induced atherosclerosis and the senescence‐associated secretory phenotype in murine carotid arteries. High levels of ionizing radiation accelerated atherosclerotic lesion formation in ApoE −/− mice, resulting in inflammatory plaque prone to bleeding. Stewart et al 49 also discussed the effects of radiation on atherosclerosis progression and its influence on plaque composition and stability.

scRNA‐seq has provided significant insights into the disparities in the immune microenvironments of stable and vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques. 21 This technology allows for detailed analysis of the cellular and molecular composition of plaques, revealing the complex interplay between immune cells and atherosclerosis development. 50 Fernandez et al 51 identified specific immune cell subsets within plaques associated with plaque stability and vulnerability to complications, such as hemorrhage. These studies are essential for understanding the potential of immunotherapy in atherosclerosis, because they help identify targets for therapeutic interventions aimed at modulating immune responses within the plaque microenvironment.

To gain deeper insights, we used scRNA‐seq to profile the transcriptomes of atherosclerotic plaques in apoE−/− mice exposed to radiation, capturing the disease at various stages (necrotic core, early stage, and advanced stage) with single‐cell precision. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first application of scRNA‐seq to irradiated preexisting atherosclerotic plaques, providing a valuable data set for future hypothesis‐driven studies aimed at unraveling the mechanisms behind arterial plaque instability following radiotherapy.

Our analysis of immune cells within irradiated atherosclerotic plaques, which constituted 59.5% of all cells, highlighted the intricate composition of these plaques. We identified 7 immune cell types, including NK cells, T cells, myeloid cells, plasma cells, B cells, and neutrophils. T cells play an essential role in both initiating and shaping atherosclerosis development. 52 CD8+ T cells have a complex role in atherosclerosis, where they can either protect against or contribute to lesion progression. 53 , 54 For example, major histocompatibility complex class I‐restricted CD8+ T‐cell responses prevent atherosclerosis, along with protective QA‐1‐restricted CD8+ T cells. 55 , 56 , 57 In addition, regulatory CD8+ CD25+ T‐cell subsets with immunosuppressive functions inhibit atherosclerosis. 58

However, CD8+ T cells can also promote atherosclerotic lesion progression by fostering mononuclear cell infiltration in hyperlipidemia and exerting cytotoxic functions, thereby enhancing vascular inflammation and macrophage accumulation. 41 , 54 Within atherosclerotic plaques, CD8+ T cells can promote inflammation and tissue damage by releasing cytotoxic molecules, such as GZMB. 41 Recent studies using flow cytometry and scRNA‐seq have demonstrated that CD8+ T cells accumulate in the aorta of atherosclerotic mice 22 and are significantly enriched in human plaques. 51 However, the exact roles of CD8+ T cells in irradiated atherosclerotic lesions remain unexplored.

In this study, we present several important findings (Figures 4 and 5). First, we found that specific infiltration patterns of CD8+ T cells contributed to irradiated atherosclerotic plaque instability. Second, we identified a subset of CD8+ T cells expressing GZMK in the early stage, implicated in unstable atherosclerotic plaques. These findings suggest potential pathways for further research into the functions of T cells within irradiated atherosclerotic plaques. These findings necessitate the use of advanced mRNA sequencing techniques and interventional clinical studies for further exploration.

Additionally, we explored the contribution of CD8+ T lymphocytes to atherosclerosis progression in irradiated plaques and examined the underlying molecular mechanisms. Using monoclonal antibodies 37 (specifically, CD8α‐depleting antibodies) to selectively eliminate CD8+ T cells in the peripheral blood and lymphoid tissues, we observed a significant reduction in CD8+ T cells within aortic atherosclerotic lesions, which subsequently attenuated atherosclerosis progression in hypercholesterolemic ApoE −/− mice postradiation. These results suggest that CD8+ T cells infiltrate irradiated atherosclerotic lesions, contributing to atherosclerosis development.

GZMB, a serine protease synthesized by various immune and nonimmune cells as well as tumor cells, 59 has recently been recognized for its apoptotic role in cells and multiple extracellular functions. 60 , 61 Its ability to cleave extracellular matrix components, cytokines, cell receptors, and clotting proteins indicates that GZMB may act as a multifaceted proinflammatory agent. 59 This suggests that GZMB could contribute to various inflammatory conditions, including age‐related inflammation (inflammaging), acute and chronic inflammatory diseases, and cancer. In this study, we investigated the contribution of GZMB to atherosclerosis immunopathogenesis in irradiated plaques and its underlying molecular mechanisms. Our mRNA sequencing data revealed that expanded CTL‐active CD8+ T cells show high cytotoxicity after radiotherapy, particularly associated with Gzmb expression. GZMB −/− ApoE −/− mice developed less severe lesions after radiation, demonstrating the pathogenic role of GZMB in this model. We also found reduced macrophage numbers in GZMB −/− ApoE −/− mice after radiation, indicating that macrophage recruitment is a mechanism by which radiation promotes plaque instability in atherosclerosis. These findings were further validated through cell communication analysis.

In conclusion, this study revealed the unique cytotoxic CD8+ T‐cell subsets within irradiated atherosclerotic plaques, which may contribute to disease pathogenesis through GZMB‐driven apoptosis and IFN‐γ‐mediated macrophage recruitment. The activation and proliferation of cytotoxic GZMK+ CD8+ T cells in these plaques could potentially worsen atherosclerosis and systemic inflammation (Figure S10). Our findings highlight the potential of GZMB as an emerging therapeutic target in radio‐cardiovascular disease, warranting further exploration in future studies.
