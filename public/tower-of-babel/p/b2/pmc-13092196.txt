Preparation of fermented plant-based beverages using grape seed: Comparison of lactic acid bacteria strains, physicochemical characteristics and flavor profiles
Authors: Ruiguo Cui, Yuting Xiao, Lixiang Huai, Jialin Xue, Zheshuo Cui, Caihong Cheng, Xiaoyu Liu, Lijun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Grape seed is typically regarded as by-products of wine and grape juice processing. In this study, the fermented plant-based beverages (FPB) were prepared by different lactic acid bacteria (LAB) strains fermentation using grape seeds. The changes in physicochemical properties and flavor of different FPB were comparatively investigated. The results demonstrated that different LAB strains showed significant differences on the qualities of FPB. Co-fermentation using two selected LAB strains, including Lactobacillus casei LWCC3002 (L.C) and Lactobacillus rhamnosus LWCC3004 (L.R), induced better biochemical transformations. The antioxidant activity of FPB increased by 10% after fermentation. Notably, the asparagine content increased significantly by about 20 times (1.91–38.33 μg/mL). Additionally, Gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) identified 46 volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in FPB. The content of 2-Ethyl-5-methylpyrazine and 2-Ethyl-3-methylpyrazine increased approximately 12-fold and 17-fold, respectively after fermentation. This study provides a technical support for FPB based on grape seed.

Introduction

Grape seeds, a major by-product of winemaking and beverage production, are rich in diverse phytochemicals with significant bioactive potential (Ponticelli et al., 2025). They contain a high concentration of polyphenols, accounting for 46–69% of the seed composition (Ramchandani et al., 2010), which are largely responsible for their potent antioxidant capacity (Tang et al., 2018). Grape seed polyphenols and flavonoids act as powerful antioxidants by neutralizing free radicals and mitigating oxidative stress (Shi et al., 2023). In vitro studies have shown that their radical-scavenging activity is approximately four to five times greater than that of conventional antioxidants, such as ascorbic acid (vitamin C) and α-tocopherol (vitamin E) (Mandic et al., 2008). Furthermore, grape seeds contain 11- 13% protein, making them an excellent source of plant-derived protein (Gupta et al., 2020). Therefore, the valorization of grape seed by-products into functional products not only promotes resource sustainability but also enhances the overall commercial value of grapes.

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) play a dominant role in the fermentation of foods and beverages (Anumudu et al., 2024). Fermentation by LAB has been shown to improve both the nutritional and sensory properties of plant-based beverages (Szutowska, 2020) while increasing the utilization rate and added value of raw materials (Mora-Villalobos et al., 2020). LAB are capable of metabolizing a variety of substrates, leading to biochemical transformations that modify the nutritional composition and bioactivity of the fermented products (Garcia et al., 2020). LAB fermentation can effectively enhance bioaccessibility and antioxidant activity of plant matrices by producing beneficial metabolites, especially organic acids (de Assis et al., 2021). Different LAB strains exhibit distinct metabolic and functional profiles (Liu et al., 2023). Species such as Lactobacillus plantarum, L. rhamnosus, and L. acidophilus are commonly employed in the fermentation of fruit and vegetable juices (Swain et al., 2014). These microorganisms significantly influence the metabolic profile of the substrate, thereby improving the bioactivity and health-promoting effects of the resulting beverages, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (Lu et al., 2025; Mantzourani et al., 2018). In addition, LAB fermentation can effectively reduce or eliminate undesirable flavors often associated with plant-based substrates (Yang et al., 2024). During fermentation, LAB metabolize sugars, lipids, and proteins to generate lactic acid, diacetyl, acetaldehyde and other volatile compounds that contribute to a mild sour aroma and complex flavor profile (Anumudu et al., 2024). Additional aroma compounds, such as aldehydes, ketones and esters, are also formed, enriching the overall flavor layers and imparting a unique, harmonious sensory character to the final product (Hu, Zhang, et al., 2022).

In this study, eight LAB strains were selected to ferment grape seeds for the preparation of fermented plant-based beverages (FPB). The influence of various LAB strains on the physicochemical properties, antioxidant activity, and flavor characteristics of FPB was evaluated comparatively. This study will provide a theoretical basis for the high value utilization of grape seeds and the development of novel plant-based functional beverages.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The raw material used in this study was Cabernet Sauvignon grapes harvested in September 2023 from Changli County, Qinhuangdao City, Hebei Province. The grapes were vinified at Huaxia Winery, COFCO Wines Co., Ltd., then air-dried and pressed to remove 90% of the oil. The resulting residue was collected for subsequent experiments.

LAB strains, including Lactobacillus plantarum CICC21790 (L.P90), Lactobacillus casei CICC6117 (L.C17), Lactobacillus fermentum mucus CICC22704 (L.F04) and Lactobacillus fermentum mucus CICC24575 (L.C75), were procured from China Center of Industrial Culture Collection (CICC). The other 4 LAB strains, including Lactobacillus plantarum LWCC3001 (L.P), Lactobacillus plantarum ACCCC11095 (L.P 95), Lactobacillus casei LWCC3002 (L.C) and Lactobacillus rhamnosus LWCC3004 (L.R), were purchased from Luwei Biological Technology Co., Ltd. in Shanghai, China.

De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) broth medium and MRS agar medium were purchased from Huasheng Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). 2,2’-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) reagents were purchased from Titan (Shanghai, China). Ferric ion reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) kits and Folin-Ciocalteu’s phenol reagent were purchased from Solarbio Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Gallic acid standard and rutin standard were purchased from TMRM (Beijing, China). All other analytical reagents were purchased from Aladdin Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of LAB starter

The preparation of LAB starter was slightly modified based on the methods of others (Li, Dong, et al., 2021; Li, Gao, et al., 2021; Li, Guo, & Ding, 2021; Li, Huang, et al., 2021; Li, Jiang, et al., 2021). All strains were cultured in MRS broth at 37 °C until reaching 6.00 log CFU/mL. Then harvested by centrifugation at 3500× g for 5 min at 4 °C. After discarding the supernatant, the pellets were washed twice with 10 mL of normal saline (NS), followed by centrifugation under identical conditions. Finally, the strains were washed with 5 mL of NS and resuspended as an inoculum of FPB.

Preparation of fermented FPB

The method for FPB preparation was modified based on previous studies by Zhao, Mu, et al. (2025). The grape seed residue, which were cleanly peeled and free of molds, were dried in an oven (DHG-9245A, Shanghai Huitai Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 55 °C for 8 h, then pulped in a juicer (JX3362, Jinxiong Co., Ltd., Fuzhou, Fujian, China) with distilled water at 1:20 (w/w). Sterilization of the grape seed pulp was subsequently carried out at 75 °C for 15 min. Sterilised FPB was first tempered in a thermostatic water bath (Photosynthesis, Hunan Frontier Technology Co., Ltd, China) and then cooled to 25 °C. Each suspension was inoculated with 11% (v/v) of an individual LAB strain and incubated without agitation at 37 °C for 10 h in an HZQ-X100 incubator (Shanghai Jingsheng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd, China). After fermentation, the culture was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 2 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected as the final fermented FPB. The un-inoculated control group (CK).

Physicochemical properties analysis of fermented FPB

Fermentation endpoint pH

After 10 h of FPB fermentation, the pH of the fermentation endpoint was measured using a pH meter (Wang et al., 2024). Briefly, 10 mL of fermentation broth was accurately weighed into a beaker, and the electrode of the pH meter was immersed below the liquid surface of the FPB, followed by recording the stabilized pH value.

Total phenol content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC) analysis

The TPC was determined by the Folin phenol method, and gallic acid was used as the standard control (Abdel-Hamid & Newair, 2016). Briefly, the sample (200 μL) was mixed with 2.5 mL of 1 mol/L Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 2.5 mL of 15% (w/v) Na2CO3 solution. The mixture was then diluted to 10 mL with distilled water and incubated in a water bath at 40 °C for 60 min. Absorbance was measured at 778 nm and the total polyphenols content was quantitatively expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per mL. The TFC was determined by the spectrophotometry method, and rutin was used as the standard control (Li, Huang, et al., 2021). Briefly, 1 mL of sample was mixed with 4 mL of 70% (v/v) ethanol and 0.5 mL of 5% (w/v) NaNO2. After 5 min of reaction, 0.5 mL of 10% (w/v) Al (NO3)3 was added, and the mixture was allowed to react for an additional 5 min. The reaction was then terminated by adding 2 mL of 1 mol/L NaOH, and the volume was adjusted to 10 mL with 30% (v/v) ethanol. Absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV-2100, Unico Instrument Co., China). The content was quantitatively expressed as mg rutin equivalents (RE) per mL.

Antioxidant activity analysis

The FRAP was determined by previous method (Li, Cheng, et al., 2025) with minor modifications. Briefly, FPB samples were centrifuged at 3500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for analysis. An aliquot of 30 μL of the supernatant was mixed with 900 μL of freshly prepared FRAP working reagent. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 10 min, and absorbance was measured at 593 nm. Vitamin C (0.1 mg/mL) was used as a positive control and subjected to the same procedure. Where Y is the absorbance at 593 nm, x is the standard solution ion concentration required for evaluating the antioxidant capacity of the sample (μmol/mL), V is the total volume of the reaction solution (mL), V1 is the volume of sample (mL).

The ABTS free radical scavenging rate was conducted as previously reported with slight modification (Shi et al., 2023). Briefly, stock solutions of ABTS (2 mmol/L) and K2S2O8 (2.45 mmol/L) were prepared separately, mixed in equal volumes, and allowed to react in the dark for 24 h. The resulting solution was then diluted to an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm to obtain the ABTS working solution. An aliquot (1 mL) of FPB supernatant was added to 5.5 mL of the ABTS working solution, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 734 nm using a spectrophotometer. Vitamin C (0.1 mg/mL) was used as a positive control and subjected to the same procedure. where A0 is the absorbance without fermentation sample, and A1 is the absorbance with fermentation sample.

Viable cell counts analysis

The viable LAB cell counts (VCC) in the FPB before and after fermentation were determined using the plate count method (Liang, Zhang, et al., 2024). Specifically, 0.1 mL of FPB was subjected to serial dilution with 0.9 mL of normal saline. Aliquots of 0.1 mL from appropriate dilutions were spread onto MRS agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Colonies numbering between 30 and 300 were enumerated, and results are expressed as log colony-forming units per milliliter (log CFU/mL).

Free amino acids (FAAs) analysis

The FAAs were extracted as previously described (Gu et al., 2021). Take 0.2 mL FPB sample, add 100 μL 0.1 mol/L PITC-acetonitrile solution, 100 μL 1 mol/L triethylamine-acetonitrile solution, vortex for 1 min, then water bath at 25 °C for 40 min. Add 1.5 mL n-hexane, vortex for 1–2 min and leave for 5 min. Centrifuged at 1000,0 rpm for 5 min and extracted twice. The lower layer was aspirated with a syringe, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and transferred to a vial for analysis by liquid chromatography as (Kuang et al., 2022; Yang, Yao, et al., 2025) stated, and some modifications were made. The detailed buffer combination and elution procedure are shown in Supplementary Materials Table S2.

An electronic nose (E-nose) analysis

E-nose analysis was conducted utilizing the PEN3 (Airsense Analytics Inc., Schwerin, Germany). Initially, 5 mL of FPB was introduced to the headspace bottle for 30 min and then analysis. Prior to sample detection, air was purged for 120 s and the probe was inserted for 90 s (Yang, Liu, et al., 2025). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the data using the Win Muster software. The description of sensor performance is presented in Table S1.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was conducted using a 9-point hedonic scale to assess the quality of fermented and non-fermented FPB. Fermented samples (3 mL each) were presented in tasting cups to 12 trained panelists (6 males, 6 females; aged 20–40 years), who evaluated acidity, color, aroma, mouthfeel smoothness, astringency, clarity, aftertaste, and overall acceptability. Samples were served in a randomized monadic sequence, coded with three-digit random numbers. Ethical approval was not required for this sensory study; all participants provided written informed consent.

GC-IMS analysis

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in FPB were analyzed using headspace gas chromatography coupled with ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS). A 1 mL sample was placed into a 20 mL headspace vial and equilibrated at 60 °C with agitation at 500 rpm for 15 min. Then, 100 μL of the headspace vapor was automatically injected at 85 °C in spitless mode.

Separation was carried out on an MXT-WAX capillary column (30 m × 0.53 mm × 1.0 μm; Restek, USA) held at 80 °C for 30 min. The carrier gas flow was programmed as follows: initial flow of 2 mL/min held for 2 min, increased to 10 mL/min over 8 min, then raised to 100 mL/min over 10 min, and finally maintained at 100 mL/min for an additional 10 min. The IMS (FlavourSpec, GAS, Dortmund, Germany) was operated at 45 °C with a constant drift gas flow of 150 mL/min.

Retention indices (RIs) were calibrated using ketone standards (C4-C9). VOC identification was based on matching both retention indices and drift times with reference standards in the GC-IMS library (G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany), according to the method described by Mora-Villalobos et al. (2020). The relative content of each compound was quantified based on average peak areas.

Statistical analysis

All experimental results are presented as mean ± standard deviation of triplicate measurements. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test was used to deter mine significant differences between means at a 95% confidence level (P < 0.05). Multivariate statistical analyses, namely partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), were performed with SIMCA 14.1 (Umetrics, Sweden). Variables with a variable importance in projection (VIP) value exceeding 1 were considered potential biomarkers. PCA biplots derived from GC-MS data were also generated using SIMCA. Bar plots and additional visualizations were created with Origin 2024.

Results and discussion

Effect of single bacteria on fermented FPB

pH changes of FPB fermented by different LAB

Grape seeds contain small amounts of free sugars (approximately 5.2%-7.8%), with glucose and fructose as the primary components (Milinčić et al., 2020). These sugars can be metabolized by LAB to produce organic acids. It has been demonstrated that LAB differ in their acidification capacity (Khushboo et al., 2023), pH is recognized as a key indicator for assessing probiotic fermentation performance on grape seeds. The effects of different LAB on the pH of FPB are shown in Fig. 1A. Relative to CK (4.92), all inoculated fermentations exhibited significantly lower pH values (P < 0.05), indicating that LAB can grow using grape seeds as a substrate. LAB exhibited strain-specific differences in both the utilization of FPB and their acid-producing capacity. L.P showed the highest acidogenic activity, achieving a pH drop from 4.92 to 4.10, significantly below the values recorded for other strains (P < 0.05). Conversely, L.P90 showed the weakest acidogenic activity, with pH decreasing only marginally from 4.92 to 4.80, yielding a final pH significantly higher than those of other strains (P < 0.05). Fermentation with the eight single strains resulted in pH reductions of approximately 0.1–1.0 units, yielding final pH values ranging from 3.8 to 5.0, which fall within the typical acceptable pH range for many fermented beverages (Echresh et al., 2025). The observed acidification possibly reflects utilization of the seed’s residual free sugars as a carbon source (Mahajan et al., 2025) and LAB-mediated production of organic acids (e.g., lactic acid, acetic acid, and formic acid) alongside proteolytic release of peptides and amino acids, which can further lower pH (Alrosan et al., 2023; Mohapatra et al., 2024). Different LAB exhibit inherent variations in cell envelope protease activity and intracellular peptidase profiles. E-nose and sensory evaluation of FPB fermented by different LAB

Flavor is one of the most crucial factors for consumers to accept food (Abouelenein et al., 2023). E-nose is an analytical instrument designed for odor evaluation, capable of detecting and distinguishing both simple and complex volatile compounds (Zhang et al., 2020). In this study, the volatile profiles of FPB fermented with eight single bacterial strains were assessed by electronic nose, and the corresponding radar plot is presented in Fig. 1C. E-nose sensors exhibited strong responses from W5S, W2W, and W1W and W1S, indicating the possible presence of short-chain alkanes, inorganic sulfides, aromatic compounds, and nitrogen oxides following grape seed fermentation. These compounds are representative volatile metabolites typically generated during grape seed fermentation (Fan et al., 2023). Fig. 1D presents the radar plot of electronic nose analysis, revealing that fermentation with all eight strains resulted in varying increases in the responses of sensors W5S, W2W, W1W, and W1S relative to the uninoculated CK, indicating that the flavor characteristics have undergone significant changes after fermentation (Wang, Ouyang, et al., 2021).

In addition, the effects of fermentation with different strains on the sensory profiles of FPB were further studied, and the changes in acidity, color, aroma, mouthfeel, taste and clarity of FPB after fermentation with various strains were analyzed (Fig. 1B). Sensory evaluation of the FPB fermented with eight strains illustrated that the organic acids produced by fermentation increase the acidity of the sample, bringing the astringency and sour taste of FPB into a more coordinated sensory balance, thereby optimizing the overall taste experience. The taste score of FPB fermented by L.R was the highest, followed by that fermented by L.C, and the astringency score of FPB fermented by L.R was the lowest. Importantly, the overall acceptability of fermented FPB was all higher than that of the uninoculated CK group. In short, fermentation could improve the mouthfeel of FPB and enhance consumers’ acceptability (Liang, Bai, et al., 2024).

TPC and TFC of FPB fermented by different LAB

Phenolic compounds, as important bioactive constituents, can improve the sensory attributes of foods and have demonstrated health-promoting potential (Chen et al., 2023; Li, Jiang, et al., 2021). In the present study, fermentation generally promoted TPC increases in most groups (Fig. 1E), except for L.P, where TPC (31.36 mg/L) was significantly lower than the control (35.27 mg/L) (P < 0.05). The remaining strains all significantly enhanced FPB's TPC (P < 0.05). This finding aligns with previous studies showing elevated total phenolic levels in fermented watermelon juice (Li, Tian, et al., 2025; Zhang, Gu, et al., 2025; Zhao, Mu, et al., 2025). The changes in TFC of FPB fermented with different LAB are presented in Fig. 1F. LAB fermentation significantly increased the TFC of FPB (P < 0.05), with marked strain-specific variations. The CK exhibited the lowest TFC, at approximately 34 mg/L. Following fermentation with strain L.R, TFC reached its peak value (approximately 39 mg/L), which was significantly higher than those obtained with strains L.P, L.F04, and L.P90 (P < 0.05). The TFC of the L.F75-fermented group (37 mg/L) did not differ significantly from that of the L.R group (P > 0.05), but was significantly higher than those of groups fermented with L.C, L.P95, and others (P < 0.05). This observed enhancement in TPC and TFC may be attributed to the metabolic activities of LAB. Grape seeds are naturally rich in diverse bioactive compounds, including polyphenols and flavonoids (Tang et al., 2018). Enzymes such as glycosidases and phenolates produced by LAB can hydrolyze bound phenolic compounds, releasing free phenolics (Curiel et al., 2015). These strains may also cleave flavonoid ester linkages to liberate free flavonoids, thereby contributing to the elevated TPC and TFC.

Antioxidant activity of FPB fermented by different LAB

Beverages enriched with antioxidants provide multiple health benefits by enhancing various physiological functions (Wootton-Beard & Ryan, 2011). It has been demonstrated that fermentation could increase antioxidant activity significantly in plant-based substrates (Nie et al., 2022). In the present study, the antioxidant capacity of FPB was evaluated using the FRAP and ABTS free radical scavenging rate. Fig. 1G and H demonstrate that fermentation significantly boosted the antioxidant activity of FPB compared to CK. The FRAP results (Fig. 1G) identified L.R and L.C as the most effective strains. The FRAP antioxidant activity of vitamin C at the same concentration was 2.35 μmol/mL. The L.C17 fermentation group achieved a FRAP value of 1.67 μmol/mL, equivalent to 71.06% of the vitamin C activity, while the L.R group reached 1.60 μmol/mL, corresponding to 68.08% of the vitamin C activity Additionally, the L.R and L.C17 groups exhibited significant increases of 0.18 μmol/mL and 0.21 μmol/mL, respectively, compared to CK (P < 0.05). No significant difference was observed between L.C17 and L.R in FRAP antioxidant activity (P > 0.05), indicating their comparable performance as superior strains. After 10 h of fermentation, all inoculation groups showed significantly enhanced ABTS free radical scavenging rate (Fig. 1H) (P < 0.05), with L.R and L.C achieving the highest rates. Vitamin C exhibited 92.40% ABTS free radical scavenging rate at the same concentration. The L.R fermentation group reached 83.66%, corresponding to 90.54% of the vitamin C activity, while the L.C group achieved 82.79%, equivalent to 89.60% of the vitamin C activity. Both were significantly higher than CK (P < 0.05). Consistent with these findings, Li, Gao, et al. (2021) reported that the antioxidant activity of pear juice progressively increased during fermentation with Lactobacillus acidophilus CH-2.

Based on the comprehensive evaluation of sensory quality and bioactivity of FPB fermented with different single strains, L.R exhibited the best overall performance. Among all strains, L.R achieved the highest sensory acceptability, the highest total phenolic content (45.61 mg/L) (P < 0.05), the highest total flavonoid content (39.01 mg/L) (P < 0.05), and the highest ABTS radical scavenging rate (83.66%) (P < 0.05). Although L.R ranked fourth in FRAP value, it did not differ significantly from L.C17 (P > 0.05), which ranked first. Therefore, L.R was selected as the primary strain for subsequent mixed-strain fermentation with other LAB.

Effect of mixed bacteria on fermented FPB

Previous studies have demonstrated that Lactobacillus strains utilize nutrients to produce organic acids-such as lactic, acetic, and formic acids-thereby lowering pH and increasing acidity (Wang, Wu, et al., 2021). As expected, all mixed-strain fermentation groups significantly reduced the pH of FPB (Fig. 2A) (P < 0.05). E-nose and sensory evaluation of FPB fermented by different mixed LAB

Mixed-strains fermentation can alter the types or levels of volatile aroma compounds in beverages (Fang et al., 2023), while E-noses enable rapid and objective assessment of a sample's volatile characteristics (Wang et al., 2025). Fig. 2C shows the E-nose results after co-cultivation fermentation of strain L.R with each of the other seven LAB. Fig. 2D quantifies the differential responses of four key sensors from these mixed-culture fermentations. Compared to CK, the L.R + L.C combination exhibited significantly elevated responses in sensors W5S, W2W, and W1W, followed by L.R + L.P90. These sensors are specific to nitrogen oxides, sulfur-containing compounds, and aromatic volatiles, respectively, indicating that L.R + L.C co-fermentation enhanced the production of these volatile classes through synergistic metabolic interactions. This finding is consistent with Li, Sun, et al. (2025), who used E-nose analysis to demonstrate similar effects of Lactobacillus fermentation on the overall flavor of Cordyceps militaris juice.

Sensory evaluation (Fig. 2B) assessed eight attributes, including aroma intensity, smoothness, and overall acceptability. Among all mixed-culture fermentation groups, the L.R + L.C combination received the highest overall acceptability score and the lowest astringency score. This finding aligns with previous reports demonstrating that mixed-culture fermentation enhances sensory quality (Lan et al., 2023).

TPC and TFC of FPB fermented by different mixed LAB

Fig. 2E and Fig. 2F present the TPC and total TFC, respectively, of the beverage co-fermented with L.R and each of the seven other LAB. The TPC and TFC in all co-fermentation treatment groups were significantly higher than those in CK (P < 0.05). TPC results showed (Fig. 2E) that the L.R + L.C17 and L.R + L.F04 combinations exhibited the highest performance (50 mg/L), followed by the L.R + L.C and L.R + L.P95 combinations (48 mg/L). The significant increase in TPC after co-fermentation (Fig. 2E) may be attributed to enzymes and carboxyl groups generated during fermentation disrupting cell structures and releasing phenolic compounds (Dey et al., 2016). For the TFC (Fig. 2F), the L.R + L.C combination achieved the maximum value (39 mg/L), significantly higher than other treatment groups (P < 0.05). This can be attributed to the synergistic action of β-glucosidase and α-rhamnosidase (Li, Guo, & Ding, 2021). It is known that Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus can specifically cleave the β-1,4-glycosidic bond between the aglycone and the sugar moiety of flavonoid compounds (Landete et al., 2014). L.C likely created a conducive metabolic niche characterized by adequate nutrient availability and pH buffering (Vidana Gamage et al., 2021), thereby promoting the expression and stability of β-glucosidase produced by L.R.

Antioxidant activity of FPB fermented by different mixed LAB

Grapes are known to be rich in polyphenolic compounds, with 60–70% of the total polyphenols concentrated in the seeds (Krasteva et al., 2023). During fermentation, probiotic metabolism converts these polyphenols into low-molecular-weight phenolic acids, which directly enhance probiotic antioxidant enzyme activity and synergistically scavenge free radicals (Zeng et al., 2020). The specific enzymes produced by LAB vary, influencing their ability to release bioactive antioxidants (Kieliszek et al., 2021). Fig. 2G presents the FRAP results, demonstrating that all co-culture groups had significantly higher ferric reducing antioxidant power than CK (1.4 μmol/mL) (P < 0.05). This confirms that LAB co-fermentation effectively improves the antioxidant capacity of FPB. Notably, the L.R + L.C co-culture exhibited the highest FRAP value, reaching approximately 1.65 μmol/mL. The ABTS free radical scavenging rate (Fig. 2H) revealed that all co-culture groups had significantly higher radical scavenging activity than CK (74%) (P < 0.05). The L.R + L.P and L.R + L.C combinations exhibited the strongest activity, with scavenging rates of approximately 85%. No significant difference was found between these two groups (P > 0.05), whereas both were significantly superior to the L.R + L.P95 and CK (P < 0.05), demonstrating their equivalent synergistic potential in radical scavenging. The strain-specific differences in antioxidant activity are attributed to the complementary catalytic capacities of the different co-culture systems, with the L.R + L.C combination exhibiting the highest overall antioxidant capacity due to its optimal enzymatic synergy. The hydrolysis of flavonoid glycosides and phenolic acid esters is initiated by β-glucosidase and carboxylesterases from L.R (Li, Guo, & Ding, 2021), L.C complements this by secreting elevated levels of esterases and phenolic acid decarboxylases (PADs) (Li et al., 2017). These enzymes subsequently cleave ester bonds and transform hydroxycinnamic acids into vinyl phenols, which mitigates glycosidase feedback inhibition and promotes continuous phenolic release (Sreerama et al., 2012). The superior antioxidant performance of L.R + L.C can be attributed to the synergistic action of their enzyme systems, which facilitated the production of diverse bioactive phenolics, including free flavonoids that elevated FRAP values (Zhao, Yang, et al., 2025). Concurrently, the generation of small-molecule phenolic acids and vinyl phenols enhanced ABTS free radical scavenging rate. This comprehensive antioxidant improvement aligns with earlier studies demonstrating that mixed-culture fermentation enhances antioxidant potential (Estanech et al., 2024). In contrast, L.R + L.P95 exhibited high FRAP values but only moderate ABTS radical scavenging activity, likely due to its esterase activity, which preferentially releases phenolic acids rather than activating flavonoids. Meanwhile, the L.R + L.P90 co-culture exhibited the lowest antioxidant activity, suggesting that strains such as L.P90 generally possess weak enzymatic activity.

Based on the comprehensive evaluation of sensory quality and bioactivity of FPB fermented with different mixed-strain combinations, L.R + L.C was identified as the optimal co-culture. This combination achieved the highest sensory acceptability, the highest total flavonoid content (38.52 mg/L) (P < 0.05), the highest ABTS radical scavenging rate (84.78%), and the highest FRAP value (1.63 μmol/mL) (P < 0.05). Therefore, L.R + L.C was selected for further investigation in mixed-strain fermentation.

Comparison of fermentation characteristics between single and mixed LAB

pH and biological changes during fermentation process

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the physicochemical and biological changes occurring during FPB fermentation, variations in pH, TPC, TFC, antioxidant capacity and the VCC were monitored throughout the fermentation process. It was found that pH declined significantly as the fermentation time increased (P < 0.05). Specifically, the pH of the FPB fermented with the L.R and L.C combination decreased from 4.93 at the beginning to 4.12 at the end of fermentation, while FPBs fermented with individual strains exhibited a similar trend (Fig. S1A).

As shown in Fig. S1(B-E), the total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), and antioxidant activities (FRAP antioxidant activity and ABTS free radical scavenging rate) of CK, L.R, L.C, and L.R + L.C groups increased significantly over time during 0–10 h of fermentation (P < 0.05). The increase observed in CK can be attributed to the presence of naturally occurring enzymes in grape seeds, such as cellulase and pectinase (Stanek-Wandzel et al., 2024). These enzymes catalyze the degradation of the lignocellulosic cell wall and cleave the glycosidic bonds of bound phenolic and flavonoid compounds (Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2025). Concurrently, the incubation temperature of 37 °C provided an optimal condition that facilitated the passive release of these phytochemicals while avoiding enzyme denaturation (Xavier Machado et al., 2021). Furthermore, incubation at 37 °C induced hydration and swelling of grape seed cell wall polysaccharides (cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin), increasing porosity while disrupting non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions) between phenolics/flavonoids and macromolecules (Karim et al., 2024). These physical-chemical changes promoted the leaching of pre-existing soluble phenolics into the fermentation supernatant, resulting in elevated TPC and TFC. However, the L.R + L.C co-culture amplified this process through synergistic enzymatic actions and metabolic regulation.

Consequently, after 10 h of fermentation, the TPC and TFC of the L.R + L.C group were 30%-40% higher than those of CK (P < 0.05). Regarding antioxidant activity, FRAP values increased steadily over time in all groups, while ABTS free radical scavenging rate exhibited more pronounced increases during the early fermentation stage. The L.R + L.C co-culture demonstrated significantly higher FRAP values from 4 h onward and superior ABTS free radical scavenging rate starting at 6 h compared to other treatment groups (P < 0.05). At 10 h, this co-culture achieved FRAP values of 1.82 μmol/mL and an ABTS free radical scavenging rate of 85.1%. This trend is similar to the research trend of Ryu et al. (2019). While the CK group exhibited increased antioxidant activity through passive enzymatic release, LAB fermentation actively enhanced this process. The L.R + L.C co-culture, in particular, secreted glycosidases that transformed low-activity phenolics, including proanthocyanidin dimers, into potent antioxidants such as gallic acid and ellagic acid (Wang, Wu, et al., 2021). This active metabolic enhancement underscores the advantage of L.R + L.C co-fermentation in developing beverages with high antioxidant potential.

Although a gradual decline in VCC was observed as the fermentation progressed (Table 1), the final counts remained well above the national standard requirements. Sensory quality and biological activity change during storage

To investigate changes in sensory quality and bioactive properties of the FPB during storage, dynamic variations in VCC, pH, TPC, TFC, antioxidant activities (ABTS free radical scavenging rate and FRAP antioxidant activity) and sensory quality were systematically monitored over 15 days of refrigerated storage at 4 °C. The results are presented in Table S3, and sensory quality data are illustrated in Fig. S2.

During refrigerated storage, no viable cells were detected in CK, while the VCC in the probiotic-inoculated groups gradually declined over time. This phenomenon can be attributed to the progressive depletion of carbon sources in grape seeds, which led to partial death of lactic acid bacteria as storage time extended. Among all groups, the L.R + L.C co-culture exhibited the highest viability stability during refrigerated storage. At the initial stage (days 1–3), the VCC of the single-strain groups (L.R and L.C) ranged from 0.94 to 1.03 × 108 CFU/mL, higher than that of the co-culture group (0.65 × 108 CFU/mL). By day 15, the VCC of L.R and L.C had decreased to 0.24 × 108 CFU/mL and 0.26 × 108 CFU/mL, respectively, while the L.R + L.C group maintained a significantly higher count of 0.27 × 108 CFU/mL (P < 0.05). To be effective as probiotics, the viability of probiotic strains in food products should generally be maintained within the range of 106–107 CFU/mL or CFU/g (Min et al., 2017). At the end of refrigerated storage, the VCC in all three fermented FPB remained high, exceeding 107 CFU/mL, which is consistent with the findings of Bai et al. (2024).

During storage, all groups exhibited declining trends in TPC, TFC, ABTS free radical scavenging rate, and FRAP values. In the CK group, which contained no viable cells, the pH remained stable. Due to the absence of hydrolases secreted by probiotics, bound phenolics could not be released, and free phenolics were susceptible to oxidation. Consequently, this group showed the most rapid decreases in TPC, TFC, and antioxidant activities. In the inoculated groups, the pH rapidly decreased to 3.94–4.06 during early storage, establishing a stable acidic microenvironment. Simultaneously, β-glucanase and esterases secreted by LAB hydrolyzed bound phenolics and flavonoids into their free forms, thereby increasing the initial content of bioactive compounds (Landete et al., 2014). As a result, these groups exhibited slower declines in TPC, TFC, and antioxidant activities during subsequent refrigerated storage (Kim et al., 2022). Among all groups, the L.R + L.C co-culture exhibited the slowest decline in bioactive compounds during storage. This can be attributed to the interspecific synergy between L.R and L.C, which promoted the production of catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD). These enzymes effectively scavenged reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby mitigating oxidative damage to bioactive components (Escudero-López et al., 2013). Additionally, small peptides derived from probiotic metabolism can form an antioxidant network with phenolic compounds, synergistically enhancing overall antioxidant activity (Li, Liu, et al., 2025). The decline in antioxidant activity and phenolic content during refrigerated storage observed in this study aligns with the findings of Wu et al. (2023) in fermented apple juice. However, it diverges from the report by Prieto-Santiago et al. (2025), who noted increased antioxidant levels post-fermentation with no significant changes during storage.

We also evaluated changes in sensory quality during storage. As shown in Fig. S2, inoculation with probiotics significantly delayed sensory deterioration throughout the 15-day storage period. Throughout the 15-day storage period, the L.R + L.C co-culture consistently received significantly higher scores for color, aroma, and overall acceptability compared to other groups. Its astringency and smoothness ratings remained within the optimal range, demonstrating superior sensory stability. This phenomenon can be attributed to the mildly acidic environment established by organic acids (e.g., lactic and acetic acids) produced during fermentation, which effectively inhibited the activities of polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and lipoxygenase (LOX), thereby reducing the formation of browning substrates and off-flavor volatiles (Zhou et al., 2020). In contrast, the CK group, lacking active microbial regulation, underwent spontaneous oxidation and enzymatic browning, leading to rapid deterioration of sensory quality. This further validates that microbial co-fermentation serves as a core technological strategy for simultaneously enhancing both functional properties and sensory attributes.

It should be noted that the sensory evaluation in this study was conducted by a trained panel of 12 individuals. While this panel size was adequate for initial differentiation of sensory attributes among fermentation treatments, it inherently limits the generalizability of the findings to broader consumer populations. Future studies should address this limitation by expanding the panel size and including consumers from diverse backgrounds in hedonic testing to better capture population-level preferences.

Lactobacillus rhamnosus primarily promoted the accumulation of tryptophan, lysine, isoleucine, and leucine, whereas Lacticaseibacillus casei exhibited advantages in enhancing asparagine and valine formation. These differences reflect the substrate preferences and metabolic characteristics of each strain. Sensorially relevant amino acids were grouped into four functional classes, i.e. sweet, bitter, umami, and aroma, according to their principal flavor contributions (Chen, Cui, et al., 2025). Across all samples, the total concentrations of flavor-active amino acids followed the descending order: sweet > bitter > aromatic > umami. Sweet amino acids include asparagine, glycine, alanine, proline, and threonine. Particularly, in the mixed fermentation group (L.R + L.C), the content of sweet amino acids reached 187.14 μg/mL, with Asn being particularly abundant. Compared with the CK group, the total sweet amino acid content in the mixed fermentation group (L.R + L.C) increased significantly by 36 μg/mL. Umami amino acids mainly include glutamic acid and aspartic acid (Zhang et al., 2013). Both components increased significantly after fermentation (P < 0.05). These changes directly enhanced the umami intensity of the beverage. They improved the overall taste fullness and acceptability.

It is worth noting that Asn, which contains an amide functional group, can undergo deamidation to form aspartic acid, a key precursor in glutathione biosynthesis (Fan et al., 2023). This transformation may enhance the antioxidant defense system by promoting glutathione synthesis (Luo et al., 2025). Furthermore, after fermentation, the Asp content increased by approximately 4.10 μg/mL, which is consistent with the change in the content of fresh flavor amino acid. This increase not only enhances the flavor of the FPB, but also supports energy metabolism through the role of Asp in the tricarboxylic acid cycle. The contents of other amino acids such as Glu, Thr, Val, and Phe were elevated to varying degrees. These amino acids contribute to flavor and may possess some anti-inflammatory effects (Sartor et al., 2021). In conclusion, LR + LC co-fermentation effectively increased both the amino acid content and the flavor enrichness of FPB, demonstrating synergistic improvements in sensory quality and potential bioactivity.

The fingerprint plot

In this study, fingerprint profiles of these volatile compounds were established to visualize the dynamic changes during FPB fermentation (Fig. 4C). Each point corresponds to a signal peak, with color intensity reflecting relative abundance (brighter shades indicate higher concentrations). As shown in Fig. 4B, the spectra can be clearly divided into 4 different regions: region a (significant increase in content after fermentation), region b (slight decrease in content after fermentation), region c (increase in content after inoculation with L.C), and region d (insignificant change in content before and after fermentation).

GC-IMS detected 11 alcohols in FPB. Alcohols endow FPB with a mild and pleasant odor (Hu, Zhao, et al., 2022).In the fingerprint chromatogram (Fig. 4B), region a shows an increase in certain alcohols after mixed fermentation, aligning with findings from other studies (González-Manzano et al., 2004).The alcohols that showed a more substantial increase in LRLC group were 1 -hexanol (increased from 681.52(CK) to 3375.65(LRLC)), 2-Methyl-1-propanol (increased from 468.99(CK) to 983.11(LRLC)), and 2-butanol (increased from 114.16(CK) to 427.32(LRLC)), with 1 -hexanol presenting a mildly sweet scent to FPB, and 2-Methyl-1-propanol and 2-butanol. This may be due to LAB utilizing amino acids as nitrogen and carbon sources, producing small amounts of higher alcohols such as Isopentanol, Isobutanol, and active amyl alcohol (Liu et al., 2024). These higher alcohols typically exhibit fruity, floral, and wine-like aromas, contributing to the flavor profile development of FPB (Carpena et al., 2020).

Ketone compounds, generated via amino acid degradation or the Maillard reaction, help reduce undesirable fishy odors in fermented beverages (Seesaard & Wongchoosuk, 2022). The fingerprint chromatogram (Fig. 4B) shows that the content of all ketone substances in region a significantly increased after fermentation. Notably, 2-octanone content increases approximately 6-fold after fermentation (increased from 484.59(CK) to 2534.95I(LRLC), as LC fermentation of FPB directly produces this compound, imparting frankincense and cheese aromas to FPB. Fermentation increases the contents of 2-butanone, 3-hydroxy (increased from 867.28(CK) to 3179.92(LRLC)) and 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one (increased from 473.42(CK) to 1549.70(LRLC)) approximately 4-fold, imparting buttery and citrus flavors to FPB. Other ketones, such as Pentan-2-one (increased from 71.26 to 106.86) with apple flavor and 2-Butanone (increased from 76.15(CK) to 125.17(LRLC)) with creamy aroma, approximately doubled in amount after fermentation. Fermentation-induced ketone enrichment is primarily attributed to Lactobacillus casei LC's robust acidification capacity, efficient lipolytic enzyme system, and phenolic conversion efficiency (Kieliszek et al., 2021). These properties promote fatty acid release from grape seed oil and their incomplete oxidation-mainly via β-oxidative decarboxylation-generating ketones (e.g., 2-octanone, 2-butanone) (Ruiz-Rodríguez et al., 2017). This establishes LC as the dominant ketone-synthesizing strain, while LR synergistically enhances the process through substrate liberation and metabolic precursor provision. The increase of ketones adds fruity and creamy aromas to the flavor of FPB.

Aldehydes mainly originate from the hydrolysis of proteins and the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids during the fermentation process (Hidalgo & Zamora, 2016). Phenylacetaldehyde presents floral and cherry aroma. In FPB samples, the content of Phenylacetaldehyde after fermentation (LRLC) (increased from 55.77 to 223.61) was more than 3 times after fermentation that before fermentation (CK), giving FPB richer aroma. 1-Nonanal takes on a sweet orange aroma. In the FPB samples, the content of nonanal after fermentation (LRLC) (increased from 730.25 to 1053.75) was approximately 1.6 times that before fermentation (CK), possibly due to fermentation promoting the catalytic oxidation of 1-Nonanol. GC-IMS analysis revealed elevated aldehyde content in FPB, arising from grape seeds’ rich unsaturated fatty acids and proteins, enhanced by LRLC co-fermentation synergy (Chen et al., 2024). Notably, Propanal and Hexanal have unpleasant and pungent odors (Varlet et al., 2007). Fermentation reduces undesirable pungent aldehydes (Propanal (decreased from 290.13 to 119.50), Hexanal (decreased from 2445.73 to 810.16) et al.). Consequently, elevated levels of flavor-enhancing aldehydes impart FPB with a distinctive sweet-floral-fruity aroma profile, thereby improving its overall flavor quality.

Esters are generated through esterification between alcohols and fatty acids during fermentation (Holland et al., 2005). They have both lower flavor thresholds and fruity flavors (Plotto et al., 2008). 10 esters were detected in the FPB samples. Ethyl isobutyrate, imparting fruity and creamy aromas, showed a 5-fold concentration increase in the LRLC co-fermentation group (from 89.52 to 419.45) compared to the control (CK), significantly enhancing FPB flavor. This may be due to the formation of proteins in grape seeds after fermentation by microbial decomposition and degradation (Nan et al., 2022). The second-highest highest content was tetrahydrofurfuryl acetate (increased from 294.21(CK) to 1123.86(LRLC)), which was about 4-fold higher than before fermentation. It gave the FPB samples a fruity and winey aroma, providing the basis for improving the flavor of FPB. GC-IMS analysis indicated the highest total ester content post co-fermentation, attributable to LRLC synergy and alcohol esterification, aligning with prior reports (Yaqoob et al., 2024). After LRLC co- fermentation, the increase in ester content brings fruity aroma to FPB and enriches its flavor.

With exceptionally low odor thresholds, trace pyrazines significantly intensify flavor perception (Fayek et al., 2023). During fermentation, they synergize with other flavorants (e.g., esters, aldehydes) to modulate sweet-acid balance and inhibit off-flavor formation (Cherniienko et al., 2022). The concentration of 2-ethyl-5-methylpyrazine (imparting a coffee flavor) increased from 92.36 (CK) to 1160.82 (LRLC), representing an approximately 12-fold increase after LRLC co-fermentation. Meanwhile, 2-ethyl-3-methylpyrazine (contributing a nutty flavor) rose from 189.53 (CK) to 3253.85 (LRLC), showing an approximate 17-fold increase and making a significant contribution to FPB flavor. Acids originate from multiple pathways such as bacterial metabolism and amino acid degradation (Pan et al., 2022). After fermentation, two acids, acetic acid and crotonic acid, were detected in the FPB samples. Moreover, 1,1-diethoxy ethane was detected, which has a sweet flavor. Although species of acids and pyrazines detected in FPB samples were less, they still played a role in shaping the flavor of FPB.

Multivariate statistical analysis

The partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) model was applied to reduce the dimensionality of GC-IMS-detected VOCs data. This approach facilitates the identification of potential marker compounds and clarifies key differences in volatile profiles associated with fermentation changes (Lubes & Goodarzi, 2017). The load graph (Fig. S3C) results of the PLS-DA model of GC-IMS explain 76.3% of the total variance, indicating a high reliability in evaluating VOCs in aroma. In addition, 200 replacement tests were conducted to evaluate the fitting quality of the model. R2Y= 0.92, Q2= 0.777 (Fig. S3B), which are lower than the initial values (R2Y and Q2 are in the upper right corner). It indicates that the constructed model has good predictability. As shown in Fig. S3C, the aromas of LR and LRLC are similar, while those of LC and LRLC are different. After fermentation, they showed significant differences from CK, indicating a considerable change. The closer the compounds are to the samples in the loading diagram, the greater their contribution (Cai et al., 2024). In the PLS-DA model, the variable importance of projection (VIP) can be adopted to further distinguish and identify VOCs in FPB. The higher the VIP score of VOCs, the greater its importance in differentiating samples. Fig S3D highlights ten variables whose VIP scores exceed 1, reinforcing the notion that probiotic fermentation distinctly upgrades FPB aroma and sets a clear benchmark for follow-up studies. To refine our selection criteria, we employed a dual-threshold approach based on ROAV and VIP values (both > 1.0). This approach pinpointed 10 compounds in Table S5 as putative key odorants, including: 2-ethyl-3-methylpyrazine, 1-hexanol, 3-hydroxy-2-butanone, 2-octanone, hexanal, methyl heptenone, 2-ethyl-5-methylpyrazine, tetrahydrofurfuryl acetate, 2-methyl-1-propanol, and furfuryl propionate. These compounds markedly differentiated the co-fermented (LRLC) samples from those produced with individual LR or LC strains, indicating their potential role as key aroma-active compounds in FPB.

Conclusions

This article developed a FPB through mixed LAB strains co-fermentation with LR + LC. Notably, the antioxidant activity of FPB increased by 10% after fermentation. Notably, the asparagine content increased significantly by about 20 times (1.91–38.33 μg/mL). Additionally, GC-IMS identified 46 VOCs in FPB. The content of 2-Ethyl-5-methylpyrazine increased approximately 11-fold post-fermentation, while 2-Ethyl-3-methylpyrazine increased approximately 15-fold. This study provides a novel approach for reusing grape seed resources. Despite the findings presented above, certain limitations should be acknowledged. In this study, we have selected a LAB strain combination suitable for grape seed fermentation with enhanced bioactivity and flavor characteristics. However, probiotic resources are not limited to LAB, and other microbial strains with superior performance may exist. Future research should explore microbial strains or consortia with higher nutrient utilization efficiency to achieve value-added utilization of grape seed resources.
