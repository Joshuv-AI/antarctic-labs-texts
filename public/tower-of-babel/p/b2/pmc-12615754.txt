Intramuscular epinephrine in cardiac arrest: A systematic review
Authors: Reem Alshaikh, Adeel Sheikh, Courtney Fleming, Facundo Garcia-Bournissen, Janice A. Tijssen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpinephrine administered by intravenous (IV) or intraosseous (IO) route is the first-line medication for cardiac arrest and is associated with improved survival. Intramuscular (IM) epinephrine is currently not indicated for cardiac arrest but may be faster and easier to administer and may result in improved patient outcomes.AimTo evaluate the evidence for IM epinephrine compared to IV or IO epinephrine in, and animals with cardiac arrest.MethodsThis systematic review followed the PRISMA guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42021259729). Databases were searched for studies comparing IV, IO, and IM epinephrine administration in cardiac arrest up to June 2, 2025. Studies in children, adults, and animals with cardiac arrest were included. Studies involving neonates and unpublished studies (such as conference abstracts and trial protocols) were excluded. Two investigators reviewed studies for relevance, extracted data and assessed bias of individual studies using the ROBINS-I and OHAT tools. Certainty of evidence was evaluated using GRADE methodology.ResultsOne observational adult out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) study, five animal studies, and one narrative review were included. For all studies, the risk of bias was moderate and certainty of evidence was low. In the one human before-and-after study of 1405 adults with OHCA, IM epinephrine was associated with improved survival (11.0 % vs 7.0 %; aOR 1.73, 95 % CI 1.10–2.71) and neurologically favourable survival (9.8 % vs 6.2 %; aOR, 1.72, 95 % CI 1.07–2.76) compared to IV/IO epinephrine. Animal studies in both adults and children had heterogeneous methods and results were mixed.ConclusionA limited number of studies have compared IM epinephrine to IV/IO or no epinephrine in cardiac arrest, including one human trial which showed improved neurological survival for IM epinephrine. Further studies, particularly randomized controlled trials in humans, to explore IM epinephrine for cardiac arrest are justified.

Introduction

Epinephrine (adrenaline) is the first line medication for cardiac arrest. It increases the coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure thereby helping to achieve return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and preserve circulation to the brain.1, 2 The earlier epinephrine is administered, the greater the likelihood of survival and favorable neurological outcomes.3, 4, 5, 6 The recommended route of epinephrine administration in cardiac arrest is intravenous (IV) or intraosseous (IO).7 However, these routes of delivery can be challenging to establish and lead to delays in administration of epinephrine, particularly in the out-of-hospital setting.8 Securing IV access is challenging in patients who are poorly perfused, particularly in patients who have relatively more subcutaneous fat or smaller veins. Furthermore, the time spent establishing IV access can detract from valuable chest compressions. The IO route is a potential alternative and can be faster to implement. However, multiple studies have failed to demonstrate an advantage of IO in comparison to IV route.7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13

Intramuscular (IM) is an alternative route for epinephrine administration and is commonly used in the treatment of anaphylaxis, even by untrained providers because it is easy, fast, and safe.14 IM epinephrine is typically injected into the vastus lateralis muscle and is rapidly absorbed into the systematic circulation.15 Despite these potential benefits, IM epinephrine is not recommended for cardiac arrest, under the unproven assumption that it can lead to suboptimal effects.

We performed this systematic review to evaluate the published data on IM epinephrine use in cardiac arrest in adults and children.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol for this systematic review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD42021259729. The protocol review adhered to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The PRISMA checklist is provided (Appendix 1).

Study question

The study question was framed using the population, intervention, comparator, and outcome (PICO) approach: In patients of all ages who suffer a cardiac arrest in any setting (P), did the IM route of epinephrine administration (I) compared to IV/IO (C) result in improved patient and/or process outcomes (O)? Patient outcomes included ROSC, survival, and neurologically favourable survival. Process outcomes included administration of epinephrine, time to epinephrine, and accuracy of dosing.

Information sources and search strategy

A systematic literature search was performed on June 2, 2025, and included all years since inception across online databases including OVID Embase and Medline, Scopus, CINAHL, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and Web of Science. Keywords included “epinephrine (adrenaline)”, “intramuscular (IM)” “intraosseous (IO)”, “intravenous (IV)”, and “cardiac arrest”, along with different combinations and indexing search terms (Appendix 2). We also searched the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (https://www.who.int/clinical-trials-registry-platform/the-ictrp-search-portal) to identify ongoing trials.

Study selection

Systematic reviews, randomized control trials (RCTs) and non-RCTs (interrupted time series, controlled before-and-after studies, cohort studies) were included. Because we hypothesized that human data would be limited, animal studies were also included but analyzed separately. Simulation studies were included for process outcomes. We included all years and only those with an English abstract. Studies involving neonates and unpublished studies (such as conference abstracts and trial protocols) were excluded. Neonates were excluded due to significant differences in resuscitation steps and medication administration.

Pairs of reviewers (JT, AS, RA, CF), using pre-defined screening criteria, independently screened all titles and abstracts retrieved by the systematic search. Any discrepancies regarding inclusion and exclusion of articles were resolved by discussion between two reviewers (JT and 2nd available). Those articles retained for full-text assessment were then reviewed in duplicate and a final set of full-text reports was identified for data abstraction. Any discrepancies in the extracted data were identified and resolved via discussion and consensus.

Data collection

Data from each study was assessed using a predefined standardized data extraction form (https://training.cochrane.org/). Two reviewers independently extracted data from individual studies and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Adjusted odds ratios were extracted for human studies, including for sensitivity analyses.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

The risk of bias was assessed using the Robins-I Tool for non-randomized studies, while the OHAT Risk of Bias Tool was applied to animal studies. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used to assess the certainty of the body of evidence (https://www.gradeworkinggroup.org/).

Results

Study selection

Eight studies met our inclusion criteria. All studies included in this systematic review were published in the last decade (2013–2025) except for one from 1967. Of these, five were in animal models of cardiac arrest, including three in pediatric models. One included study was a systematic review. The two human studies were conducted in adults with OHCA. These studies were conducted within one EMS system with the larger study16 covering all years from the smaller study17 and additional years. The larger study included all the smaller study patients (as per the study authors). Thus, this SR will only report on the larger study. No studies included the human pediatric population or those with IHCA. There were no simulation studies. No additional studies were identified from a review of the reference lists or grey literature. Study characteristics

For the critical outcomes of survival and neurologically favourable survival, 1 observational study was included.16 This before-after interventional OHCA study involved 1405 adults aged 18 years and over with non-traumatic cardiac arrest from a single EMS system in the USA between 2010 (pre-2019: before) and 2024. The patients received a single IM dose while IV or IO was secured, then IV or IO epinephrine every 3–5 min. IM epinephrine was associated with improved survival to discharge (11.0 % vs 7.0 %; aOR 1.73, 95 % CI 1.10–2.71) and neurologically favourable survival (9.8 % vs 6.2 %; aOR, 1.72, 95 % CI 1.07–2.76) compared to IV/IO alone. The per-protocol analyses were adjusted for age, sex, bystander CPR, witnessed arrest, interval from emergency call to EMS arrival, public location of the arrest, and initial cardiac rhythm. Two sensitivity analyses were performed to address the fifty-two (3.7 %) patients who received IV/IO epinephrine instead of IM epinephrine in the ‘after’ period, first by intention-to-treat and then these patients were excluded. Both sensitivity analyses were consistent for survival and neurologically favourable survival (aOR 2.06 (1.33–3.20) and aOR 2.16 (1.36–3.43) for ITT and aOR 1.91 (1.20–3.03) and 1.95 (1.19–3.17), respectively).

There were no human studies for the important outcome of ROSC.

For the process outcomes, there was one human adult OHCA study that reported rate of administration of epinephrine and time to epinephrine.16 In this study, paramedics adhered to the early IM adrenaline protocol in 89 % of cases (420 of 472). The median time from the Public Safety Answering Point (PSAP 911) emergency call to the first dose of IM adrenaline was 12 min (IQR 9.9–14.3) compared to 15.3 min (IQR 12.5– 18.5) in the control IV/IO group. The interval from EMS arrival to the first adrenaline dose was 4.3 min (IQR 3–6) in the IM cohort compared to 7.8 min (IQR 5.8–10.4) in the IV/IO cohort. There were no human studies that reported accuracy of dosing.

Animal studies reported ROSC, survival, and plasma epinephrine concentration. A summary of methods is reported in Table 2. Models included piglets (i.e., pediatric),19, 20, 22 mature rats18 and dogs21 (i.e., adults). Cause of arrest and epinephrine dosing, timing and route of administration varied across the animal studies. In piglets, Mauch et al. found similar ROSC rates across the IV (8/8), IM (7/8), and control (4/8) groups, where p = 0.077 for IV vs NS groups.20 In another piglet study of arrest induced by asphyxia, 10/28 piglets had ROSC from CPR alone, prior to the planned epinephrine. The remaining groups became unbalanced following the dropouts. The authors reported the median increases in plasma epinephrine concentration 3 min after epinephrine was administered (IV and IM, only) was 138, 134, and 29 nM in group IV, IM, and control, respectively.19 O’Reilly et al. reported comparable rates of ROSC and time to ROSC between IM and IV epinephrine in a juvenile piglet model equivalent to a 1-month old human infant.22 In the dog model, IM epinephrine yielded 40 % ROSC, while IV epinephrine resulted in 100 % ROSC when administered 6 min after the onset of cardiac arrest (p-values not provided).21 In the adult rat study, IV was superior to IM, which was superior to placebo for rate of ROSC (no p-value provided).18 Furthermore, there were no significant differences in the mean arterial pressure between the IV and IM Discussion

In this living systematic review, we identified one adult OHCA study that evaluated IM epinephrine compared to IV/IO epinephrine in cardiac arrest and found improved survival, however this study had a moderate risk of bias and low certainty of evidence. Animal studies had heterogeneous methods and results.

Epinephrine is classified as an adrenergic agent and cardiac stimulant. It acts upon both the alpha and beta receptors, favouring beta receptors of the heart, vascular and other smooth muscles. Administration facilitates a rapid rise in blood pressure through three mechanisms of action: stimulation of cardiac muscle leading to increased strength of ventricular contractions, increasing heart rate; and arterioles constriction. Based on these properties, epinephrine is recommended for the treatment of cardiac arrest.

Epinephrine administration in patients with cardiac arrest has been studied extensively. The PARAMEDIC-2 Trial, which randomized 8014 adult OHCA patients to receive epinephrine or placebo found that epinephrine tripled the chance of survival to hospital admission (24 % vs. 8 %) and increased survival to 30 days approximately 40 % (3.2 % vs. 2.4 %) but there was no difference in neurologically favourable survival.24 In a subgroup analysis, increased time to epinephrine administration had reduced benefit for survival. In a meta-analysis with over half a million patients with cardiac arrest, the use of epinephrine in the first 10 min was associated with survival and neurologically favourable survival compared to administration after 10 min.25 However, the IV/IO routes of delivery can be challenging to establish and lead to delays in administration of epinephrine, particularly in the out-of-hospital setting.3, 8, 24 Securing IV access is challenging in patients who are poorly perfused, particularly in patients who have relatively more subcutaneous fat or smaller veins. Furthermore, the time spent establishing IV access can detract from valuable chest compressions. IO access is advocated as a fast alternative to IV; however, it carries a risk of malposition. A post-mortem study in pediatric cadavers demonstrated that IO needles were malpositioned in 47 % of infants and 39 % of children.24 In adults, not limited to those with cardiac arrest, the success rate of IO placement in the tibia or humerus was 91 %.26 Studies of patients with cardiac arrest have not demonstrated improved outcomes with IO epinephrine compared to IV epinephrine.7, 10, 11

As IM epinephrine administration does not rely on vascular access, it could provide a more efficient route for initial epinephrine administration. IM epinephrine has been effectively used for the treatment of anaphylaxis, which also often involves poor circulation. In anaphylaxis, pump failure is not the primary concern; rather, hypotension, widespread vasodilation, and high vascular permeability contribute to reduced blood flow. By contrast, in cardiac arrest, the complete loss of cardiac output is the main issue, though high-quality chest compressions achieve 30–40 % of normal cardiac output.27, 28, 29 The cardiac output generated by cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) may be sufficient to allow for proper absorption and distribution of IM epinephrine from the peripheral injection site to target sites. Human pharmacokinetics data for IM epinephrine show epinephrine peaks approximately 5–10 min after IM administration, which coincided with increased heart rate and blood pressure.30, 31, 32 Peak plasma concentrations after 0.3 mg IM epinephrine were 2.1 ng/ml.32 These concentrations are similar to those observed during infusion with 0.1 µg/kg/min epinephrine,33 but approximately 10 % of those expected after an IV epinephrine bolus with 0.01 mg/kg IV epinephrine.34 These findings informed the human OHCA study presented in this SR.16 In a recent literature review, Dreborg et al. concluded that plasma epinephrine concentration follows a dose dependent pattern, in which progressively higher doses of epinephrine per IM injection leads to higher plasma epinephrine concentration.15 Furthermore, IM administration may result in elevated epinephrine concentration for up to two hours,15, 35 which could be beneficial in the setting of cardiac arrest, particularly in austere pre-hospital environments, where early post-cardiac arrest hypotension is common and associated with poor outcomes.36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41 However, the additional risk of promoting arrhythmias and/or diastolic dysfunction is an important consideration, particularly in adults, that needs to be explored ideally in the context of a clinical trial.

The only human study of IM vs IV/IO epinephrine for cardiac arrest reported improved survival and neurologically favourable survival at hospital discharge in a large cohort of adults with OHCA.16 Multiple sensitivity analyses supported the finding. The study also showed improved time to epinephrine administration in the IM group. However, the investigators could not control for temporal confounders, such as changes in EMS training, equipment, protocols, or hospital care between the two study periods (2010–2019 and 2019–2024). Furthermore, since groups were not randomized, they risked being unbalanced. There were higher rates of bystander CPR in the IM epinephrine group for which adjustments were made, however, there may be residual confounders. The Hawthorne effect may have also existed, leading to more aggressive or attentive care when the new IM protocol was introduced. Nevertheless, this study is novel, and the findings are encouraging but not definitive. While outcomes from OHCA remain poor, it is imperative that we continue to explore novel and simple interventions that may improve them.

The five animal studies which evaluated IM epinephrine also provide some insight into this therapy. Two studies demonstrated equivalency between IM and IV routes of administration for ROSC.20, 22 Two studies showed that the IV route was superior to the IM route, which was superior to placebo.18, 21 One study showed increased epinephrine concentration following IM19 and 2 studies showed similar responses in mean arterial pressure following IM and IV administration.18, 22 Nevertheless, these results should be interpreted with caution as animal studies often fail to integrate standard of care post-arrest therapies and do not report neurological outcomes.42 In addition, time to drug administration in animals does not reflect the human clinical experience, where epinephrine administration is often delayed compared to animal studies.43

Our systematic review has several strengths. Our team included content experts and systematic reviewers and was assisted by a research librarian with prior experience running systematic review searches for the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation sanctioned systematic reviews. We used a narrow PICOST, had prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria, and evaluated the quality and strength of the evidence. Unfortunately, our review is limited by the deficiency of human data and the need to include animal studies.

Conclusion

This review highlights the scarcity of high-quality evidence regarding the administration of IM epinephrine in cardiac arrest. While the evidence is limited, it suggests that IM epinephrine may have a role in cardiac arrest management. It may improve time to epinephrine administration and access to epinephrine in the pre-hospital setting. These may lead to improved neurologically favourable survival. Further studies, particularly randomized controlled trials in humans, like the PRIME Trial (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05166343), are needed to better understand the potential clinical benefits of IM epinephrine in cardiac arrest.
