Job Loss and Early-onset Dementia Risk in Middle-aged Adults: A Longitudinal Study☆
Authors: Heejoo Park, Jian Lee, Youngsun Park, Juho Sim, Byungyoon Yun, Jin-Ha Yoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly-onset dementia poses a significant public health challenge, particularly due to its profound impact on working-age individuals (under 65 years). This study investigated the association between job loss and early-onset dementia in middle-aged adults in Republic of Korea.MethodsUsing National Health Insurance Service data in the Republic of Korea, we analyzed participants aged 45–54 years who were employed by private companies and who underwent health checkups between 2009 and 2010. Early job loss was defined as involuntary unemployment within two years of the 2011 index date. The primary outcome was incident dementia, identified using both International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10) codes and antidementia medication use. Adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. Subgroup analyses were conducted by age, household income, residential region, company size, and industrial sector.ResultsAmong 1,130,665 participants (mean age 49.2 ± 2.82 years, follow-up of 14.02 years), early-onset dementia occurred in 3,748 individuals. Of these, 580 cases were from the job loss group (n = 98,504) and 3,168 cases were from the remaining employed group (n = 1,032,161). Job loss was significantly associated with an increased risk of early-onset dementia in both males (adjusted HR: 1.75; 95% confidence interval, 1.57–1.95) and females (adjusted HR: 1.51; 95% confidence interval, 1.27–1.78). Subgroup analyses identified a higher dementia risk among low-income individuals, particularly in males under 50, females over 50, and those employed in the primary industrial sector.ConclusionJob loss was significantly associated with an increased risk of early-onset dementia, particularly among males, after adjusting for multiple demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related covariates.

Introduction

Early onset dementia, affecting individuals under 65 years, is a major global public health challenge involving neurodegenerative diseases [1]. Its prevalence among those aged 30–64 years ranges from 54.0 to 163.0 per 100,000, with an annual incidence of 13.2 per 100,000 [2]. This condition is devastating due to its chronic, progressive nature and economic burden, impacting working-age individuals [2]. Medical costs and lost productivity strain families and healthcare systems, often increasing dependency on social services. For example, Harvey et al reported an annual care cost of £7,868 per person in the UK [3].

Early-onset dementia includes two prevalent subtypes: early-onset Alzheimer's disease and early-onset vascular dementia, which account for approximately 60% and 20% of cases, respectively [4]. Early-onset Alzheimer's disease is characterized by memory impairment, alongside more pronounced linguistic deficits, executive dysfunction, visuospatial impairments, behavioral symptoms, and faster cognitive decline compared to late-onset Alzheimer's [5,6]. For early-onset vascular dementia, elevated blood pressure in middle age is a key risk factor [4,7]. Additional risk factors for early-onset dementia include genetic predisposition, comorbid conditions such as diabetes and osteoporosis, as well as socioeconomic factors like low income and unhealthy lifestyle behaviors [8].

Among these socioeconomic factors, financial difficulties in middle age, often tied to job loss or unstable employment, can foster unhealthy lifestyle behaviors that exacerbate dementia risk [9,10]. Job loss, frequently driven by economic downturns, company restructuring, or closures, represents a significant psychosocial stressor, particularly for individuals nearing retirement [11]. Its health impacts are well-documented, with increased risks of severe stress, depression, anxiety, stress-related illnesses, and suicidal behaviors [12,13]. These chronic stress and depressive states may further contribute to neuroinflammation and neurodegenerative processes, potentially acting as precursors to cognitive decline [14]. However, while research has extensively explored general or late-onset dementia [15,16], the specific link between job loss and early-onset dementia remains largely unexamined, highlighting a critical gap in understanding how socioeconomic stressors influence this condition.

Given the profound impact of early-onset dementia and the potential role of job loss as a risk factor through its associated chronic stress and depression, further investigation is essential. Although the connection between job loss and general health outcomes is well-established, few studies have explored its specific association with early-onset dementia. In particular, research utilizing longitudinal data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (KNHIS) is scarce. To address this gap, this study aims to examine the association between job loss and early-onset dementia, while accounting for various socioeconomic factors, using KNHIS data.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

This retrospective study tracked participants from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database from January 2006 to December 2023 and covered more than 97% of the South Korean population [17]. Participants aged 45–54 years who underwent a national health checkup in 2009 or 2010 at private companies and were employed from 2006 to 2011 were enrolled in this study. Demographic data, biochemical test results, and personal, socioeconomic, and lifestyle data were collected through structured questionnaires, while medical records, such as outpatient visits or hospitalizations, were obtained using diagnostic codes. The index date was January 2011. Participants were excluded if they had: (1) missing data or outliers in the health checkup results; (2) a history of diagnosed dementia prior to the index date; (3) a history of diagnosed mental diseases (F00–F99); (4) developed dementia or died within 2 years after the index date; (5) employment in industries classified as “T (Activities of households as employers; undifferentiated goods and services producing activities of households for own use)” or “U (Activities of extraterritorial organizations and bodies)” (Table S1).

This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Severance Hospital (approval number: 4 2024 0398). The need for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study.

Main outcomes

The primary outcome was newly developed cases of dementia. Dementia was diagnosed based on the prescription records of antidementia drugs (donepezil, rivastigmine, memantine, or galantamine) and International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD 10) codes (F00, F01, F02, F03, G23.1, G30, or G31) [18,19]. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality, Alzheimer disease (F00 and G30), vascular dementia (F01), and dementia associated with other diseases (F02, F03, G23.1, and G31) [18]. Participants were followed up until the primary outcome was reached, death, or December 2023, whichever occurred first.

Independent variables and covariates

Early job loss, the independent variable, was defined as job loss in 2012, 2013, or both, within two years from the 2011 index date. In South Korea, the vast majority of fixed-term contracts are renewed biennially in practice, although the legal maximum duration is two years. Following this predominant two-year cycle, we defined early job separation as departure within two years from the index date [20]. Covariates included demographic (age, sex), socioeconomic (household income quartiles, residential region [Seoul, metropolitan, others], company size [<5, 5–49, 50–299, ≥300 employees]) [21], and health-related factors (obesity, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, Charlson Comorbidity Index, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity). Obesity was classified by body mass index (BMI): normal (<23 kg/m2), overweight (23–24.9 kg/m2), obese class I (25–29.9 kg/m2), obese class II (≥30 kg/m2) [22]. Hypertension was diagnosed either by prescriptions for antihypertensive medication associated with ICD 10 codes I10–I15 or a systolic blood pressure (BP) ≥140 mmHg and/or a diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg [23]. Diabetes was diagnosed based on prescriptions for antidiabetic medications associated with ICD 10 codes E11–E14 or fasting blood sugar (FBS) level ≥126 mg/dL [24]. Dyslipidemia was diagnosed based on prescriptions for lipid-lowering medications associated with ICD 10 code E78 or total cholesterol ≥240 mg/dL [25]. The updated Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated using the diagnostic codes of each disease category, requiring at least three hospital visits (inpatient or outpatient) within 2 years prior to the index date [26]. However, diabetes was excluded from the CCI calculation, as it was treated as a covariate. Smoking history was divided into three categories: nonsmokers, ex-smokers, and current smokers. Alcohol consumption was classified according to weekly intake as mild consumption (<210 g for males and <140 g for females), moderate consumption (210–420 g for males and 140–350 g for females), and severe consumption (>420 g for males and >350 g for females) [27]. Physical activity levels were measured in Metabolic Equivalent of Task (METs) minutes per week, based on reported participation in vigorous exercise (7 METs), moderate exercise (4 METs), and walking (2.9 METs) [28]. Physical activity levels were grouped into four categories: <500, <1000, <1500, and ≥1500 METs min/week [29].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were stratified according to sex. To minimize reverse causation, a two-year landmark period was applied, and participants who developed dementia during this period were excluded from the analysis. Baseline characteristics of the participants were evaluated using chi square (χ2) tests for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables. Adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for both primary and secondary outcomes were calculated using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models.

To validate the results, four different models were established: 1) Model 1 was adjusted for age; 2) Model 2 was adjusted for age, residential region, household income, company size, and industry sector; 3) Model 3 was adjusted to further include CCI group, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, in addition to Model 2; and 4) The final model was adjusted to further incorporate lifestyle-related variables, in addition to Model 3.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, subgroup analyses were performed based on age, household income, residential region, company size, and industrial sector to evaluate the risk of dementia. Household income was categorized into high income (high or high-middle income) and low income (low middle or low income) groups. Company size was defined as companies with ≤50 employees or companies with >50 employees. Second, interactions between subgroups (household income, alcohol consumption, obesity, and job loss) and early-onset dementia were evaluated using an additive interaction approach, specifically by calculating the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) [30]. Third, the association between re-employment and the risk of early-onset dementia was analyzed. Re-employment was defined as participants who experienced job loss in 2012 or 2013, re-entered employment after the two-year landmark, and maintained employment in both 2014 and 2015. To ensure accurate follow-up, participants who developed dementia or died between 2014 and 2015 were excluded. Fourth, a complementary log–log transformation of the Kaplan–Meier survival curves was employed to examine the cumulative risk of dementia according to job loss. Fifth, to address potential surveillance bias due to varying access to medical evaluations across employment groups, we included health checkup participation status from 2011–2012 as an additional covariate. Sixth, to assess the robustness of our outcome definition, we conducted two sensitivity analyses [1]: redefining dementia using International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10) codes recorded as either the primary or secondary diagnosis, irrespective of antidementia medication prescriptions, and [2] applying a stricter definition of dementia based solely on prescriptions of antidementia medications (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, or memantine) with ICD-10 codes recorded as the primary diagnosis.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software version 8.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline participant characteristics

Among the 1,823,685 participants tracked, 1,130,665 individuals (856,223 males and 274,442 females; median age [interquartile range], 49 years [47–52 years]) were included in the final analysis after applying the exclusion criteria (Figure S1). When stratified by sex, 7.42% (n = 63,508) of males and 12.75% (n = 34,996) of females experienced job loss. The overall cohort selection process, including inclusion and exclusion criteria, the index date, the 2-year landmark period, and the classification into stable employment, job loss, and re-employment groups, is illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1.

Job loss participants were older than those stably employed (p < 0.001), except for female physical activity (p = 0.028). Males with job loss had lower income, worked in smaller companies and secondary industries, were more obese, smoked more, and were less active. Among males in the job-loss group, the risks of Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, dementia associated with other diseases, and all-cause mortality were significantly elevated compared to the stable employment group (aHR 1.60, 95% CI, 1.41–1.81; aHR 2.46, 95% CI, 1.92–3.16; aHR 2.35, 95% CI, 1.78–3.11; aHR 2.48, 95% CI, 2.40–2.56, respectively). Re-employment after job loss significantly reduced dementia risk in males compared to those who remained unemployed (aHR 0.37, 95% CI, 0.28–0.49), but this effect was not statistically significant in females (Table S3). After adjusting for health checkup participation, the association between job loss and early-onset dementia remained significant for both males (aHR 1.67, 95% CI, 1.49–1.86) and females (aHR 1.50, 95% CI, 1.26–1.78).

In sensitivity analyses, redefining dementia using ICD-10 codes as either the primary or secondary diagnosis, regardless of medication use, confirmed a significant association with job loss (males: aHR 1.10, 95% CI, 1.05–1.15; females: aHR 1.08, 95% CI, 1.04–1.13). Applying a stricter definition based solely on antidementia medication prescriptions (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, or memantine) with ICD-10 codes as the primary diagnosis strengthened the association (males: aHR 1.87, 95% CI, 1.65–2.11; females: aHR 1.47, 95% CI, 1.20–1.78) (Table S4).

Discussion

Middle-aged individuals with job loss had a higher dementia risk than those employed. Subgroup analyses showed increased risk in males aged <50 years with low income and females aged >50 years with high income. In males, job loss interacted additively with income, alcohol, and obesity to elevate dementia risk. Re-employment reduced dementia risk in males but not females. Job loss may elevate dementia risk by disrupting routines, causing sleep disturbances, chronic stress, and depression, which promote beta-amyloid accumulation, reduce physical activity, and limit healthcare access, thereby accelerating cognitive decline and vascular damage [31].

Early-onset dementia's complex causes in middle-aged individuals delay diagnosis, emphasizing early detection [32]. Our study found job loss increased dementia risk in males aged <50 years and females aged >50 years, aligning with sex- and age-specific patterns [33]. Social role theory suggests males face stress as breadwinners postjob loss [34], while females over 50 experience compounded stress from career disruption and menopausal estrogen declines [35]. These highlight sex- and age-specific factors in dementia risk.

Previous studies have indicated that lifestyle factors such as obesity and alcohol consumption are worse in males compared to females. Among males, income and obesity interacted with job loss, significantly increasing the risk of dementia due to income instability and stress [36,37]. Obesity negatively affects health and increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases, mental health issues, and all-cause mortality. A previous study identified a significant link between job instability and obesity in males aged >50 years, but not in females [38]. Similarly, our study found a significant RERI value for the interaction between obesity and job insecurity in males, suggesting a higher risk of dementia. Consistent with these results, a previous study showed that low socioeconomic status and an unhealthy lifestyle significantly increased the risk of dementia in males, but not in females [39]. In our study, males who experienced job loss, especially those with low income levels, had an increased risk of dementia. This can be attributed to the psychological burden associated with traditional gender-based roles, whereby job loss can lead to stress, depression, and increased dementia risk [40]. These differences in lifestyle factors and chronic conditions between males and females contribute to disparities in dementia risk according to sex. While stroke and vascular dementia risk factors, such as atrial fibrillation, heart failure, hypertension, atherosclerosis, obesity, and diabetes, are more prevalent in males, studies have reported that females are disproportionately affected by dementia due to these factors [41]. However, in our study, the risk for vascular dementia was higher in males than in females. This finding suggests that the observed sex differences in dementia risk may be influenced by the differential impact of job loss on males and females. Specifically, the unique social and psychological burden experienced by males after job loss may explain their increased vulnerability to vascular dementia.

In the Republic of Korea, all workers are required to undergo biennial health checkups, typically facilitated by employers through paid leave to promote participation [42]. These checkups include body measurements, blood tests, and cancer screenings, with employers facing fines for noncompliance. However, individuals who experience job loss lose access to this system, potentially neglecting health management. Re-employment reintegrates individuals into the health checkup system, supporting consistent health monitoring, cognitive reserve through skill acquisition, social engagement, structured routines, and financial stability. Our findings suggest that re-employment among middle-aged individuals postjob loss may significantly reduce dementia risk by enhancing cognitive function and promoting an active, structured lifestyle [43]. Notably, re-employment significantly lowered dementia risk in males, but the smaller number of re-employed females compared to males may have contributed to observed gender differences. This disparity likely reflects the Republic of Korea's gender-specific labor market, where men are often primary earners and maintain continuous workforce engagement. Social role theory suggests that traditional gender norms influence employment behaviors and re-employment opportunities, potentially explaining these sex-specific differences [34].

The main strength of this study is that it compared the risk of early-onset dementia among middle-aged individuals who experienced job loss with individuals who remained employed. All analyses were conducted according to sex, and the risk of dementia was estimated through a stratified analysis based on various socioeconomic and clinical characteristics to identify high-risk subgroups. Interaction analyses between factors that increased dementia risk and job loss were performed to identify key contributing factors to dementia risk. Furthermore, this study included 97.2% of the South Korean population, representing an economically active population.

This study had several limitations. First, some participants' employment statuses may have been inaccurate, as they were inferred from their insurance type, particularly for individuals at both extremes of the income spectrum. Second, unmeasured confounding factors, such as lifestyle habits and subjective health conditions, may exist within the NHIS data. Third, owing to the lack of cause-of-death data, we were unable to analyze cases of suicide or accidental death. Fourth, because this was an observational study, we could not establish a causal relationship between job loss and the risk of dementia. Fifth, the lack of mental health indicators, such as depressive symptoms, limited exploration of pathways linking unemployment to dementia via mental health deterioration. Sixth, relying on insurance eligibility to infer employment status may have introduced misclassification, especially for freelancers. Finally, the findings should be interpreted cautiously when generalizing due to the varying socioeconomic contexts and characteristics across different countries.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates a significant association between job loss and increased early-onset dementia risk, particularly among males and individuals with low income, obesity, or heavy alcohol consumption. The association remained robust after adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors. Re-employment was linked to reduced dementia risk in males, suggesting potential reversibility under certain conditions. These findings highlight the critical role of employment stability and occupational health monitoring in mitigating cognitive decline within working-age populations.
