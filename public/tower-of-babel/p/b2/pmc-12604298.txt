Aligning family medicine residency learning outcomes with societal needs: a nominal group study
Authors: Keith J Todd, Robson Rocha de Oliveira, Sima Zahedi, Amrita Sandhu, Sandra Fournier, Charo Rodríguez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent evidence suggests that family physicians, who have traditionally provided a wide range of services across various settings, are choosing to reduce their scope of practice. This can have negative effects on communities that rely on family physicians to meet their diverse primary care needs. The College of Family Physicians of Canada (CFPC) spearheaded a curriculum review and renewal of postgraduate residency training to support broad-scope family medicine and specifically address six areas of evolving societal need: (i) home and long-term care; (ii) addiction and mental health; (iii) Indigenous health; (iv) health equity and anti-racism; (v) virtual care and health informatics; and (vi) leadership, advocacy, and scholarship. This study answered the following research question: Which learning outcomes for the six areas of societal need identified by the CFPC should be prioritized in family medicine postgraduate education, according to local stakeholders? MethodsAs part of a broad investigation, we conducted a descriptive study using the nominal group technique. Participants—researchers, clinical educators, and residents affiliated with a Canadian university—first generated ideas for learning outcomes and rated each idea’s importance on a five-point Likert scale. Then, they selected the five ideas per area of societal need that they believed to be of highest priority.Results Fifty-nine participants took part in six sessions in the fall of 2023. They generated and prioritized 227 learning outcomes, emphasizing topics related to patient-centred care and to personal and professional skills in care delivery. They also highlighted the need for both culturally safe care and a better understanding of the history of inequity in health care for marginalized populations.Conclusions This study generated and prioritized many learning outcomes relevant to the six areas identified by the CFPC. Combined with national-level work and international consultations, this work provides actionable learning outcomes for family medicine residency training programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08114-6.

Introduction

Family medicine is a broad discipline whose practitioners work in many care settings and provide care for people of all ages. Additionally, family medicine has been changing apace with medicine at large: medicine’s increasing complexity [1] and an emphasis on patient-centred care [2] have brought about new realities for family physicians. To enable physicians to meet these demands, their training must be adapted accordingly [3–5].

The social accountability of medical schools and family medicine programs to meet the needs of their populations is central to educational mandates internationally [6–9]. This social accountability mandate is outlined by international bodies that guide training standards in family medicine [10, 11]. To address the changing realities of family medicine, multiple countries have reviewed and even extended their training programs or have explored educational innovations in an effort to ensure family physicians are prepared to deliver full-spectrum primary care [12–19]. Numerous jurisdictions have also noted changing practice patterns and a lack of physician preparedness [15, 20, 21].

In Canada, although the residency training is two years long, family physicians provide care across a broad spectrum of care environments (labour and delivery wards, emergency departments, primary care offices, hospital wards, palliative and intensive care units). In line with the findings mentioned above, recent studies on Canadian family physicians have demonstrated declining comprehensiveness across all career stages [22, 23]. As a result, health care teams can face challenges in meeting the diverse needs of the communities they serve [24, 25].

To address changing societal needs in Canada, the College of Family Physicians of Canada (CFPC) undertook a national consultation on the postgraduate training program, and, in 2022, published a report on their findings [26]. While this report called for larger health system reform, it also named six areas of societal need that required more attention in the residency curriculum: (i) home and long-term care; (ii) addiction and mental health; (iii) Indigenous health; (iv) health equity and anti-racism; (v) virtual care and health informatics; and (vi) leadership, advocacy, and scholarship. The CFPC identified these areas of need but did not define learning outcomes—this task was left to each medical school. In our program, we decided to use empiric methods to generate learning outcomes that could be applied both in our local context and in similar milieux worldwide. Since this reform was contentious in Canada, we took a collaborative approach to developing learning outcomes, engaging with international experts [27] as well as with local stakeholders. The work we present here reports the results of these consultations with local stakeholders, which were guided by the following research question: Which learning outcomes for the six areas of societal need identified by the CFPC should be prioritized in family medicine postgraduate education, according to local stakeholders?

Methods

The reporting of this study follows the ACCORD reporting guideline [28].

Study design and methods

We conducted a descriptive study using the nominal group technique (NGT) [29, 30] and engaged with local stakeholders to generate learning outcomes for the six areas of societal need mentioned above. We held six NGT sessions between October and December 2023, one for each priority area. These sessions were guided by KJT, RRO, SZ, and CR, who are all physicians with experience in qualitative and quantitative methods, along with consensus methods.

Participants

The study was conducted in the Department of Family Medicine of a Canadian university. To ensure a diverse range of participants, we selected local stakeholders from three categories:

Academics: researchers with over five years of experience in an area relevant to postgraduate family medicine curricula.

Academics: researchers with over five years of experience in an area relevant to postgraduate family medicine curricula.

Clinical educators: physicians with more than two years of experience teaching or mentoring residents.

Clinical educators: physicians with more than two years of experience teaching or mentoring residents.

Residents: family medicine postgraduate trainees who had successfully completed at least one full year of residency.

Residents: family medicine postgraduate trainees who had successfully completed at least one full year of residency.

We recruited participants in September 2023, ensuring that at least one participant from each of the three categories was present in each session (Table 1). To include stakeholders based in remote locations, all sessions were conducted both in-person and online [31].

NGT process and data collection

Each group was led by a trained facilitator who used a standardized script to ensure consistency. We collected data on prepared questionnaires (supplemental data, Appendix 1). Three members of our research team (RRO, AS, and KT) attended all sessions to support facilitators and take field notes, which were then incorporated into the discussion phase. At the start of each session, KT provided a concise overview of the study, after which the facilitator presented the discussion question—With regards to [area of societal need], graduates of the family medicine residency program should be able to …?—and guided participants through the four core NGT steps [30]:

Analysis of learning outcomes

We first analysed the results of step 4a (round one). For each learning outcome, we calculated the median score and, based on this, assigned a level of importance —low (median of 1 or 2); medium (median of 3); or high (median of 4 or 5). Next, for the ideas of high importance, we determined whether consensus was reached: If 70% of participants assigned a score of 4 or 5, then consensus was reached [32]. Finally, we calculated the sum of the Likert ratings and normalized the values to assign an importance score to each learning outcome [33, 34].

The results of step 4b (round two) allowed us to generate a cumulative top-five priority list for each area of societal need. First, we totalled all the participant votes for each idea and assigned 5 points for the highest-ranked priority, 4 points for the second-highest-ranked priority, and so on, with the lowest-ranked priority receiving 1 point. Next, we calculated the sum total points for each idea to generate the top priorities for each area overall [33]. In the case of a tie among the top five points values, we included all associated learning outcomes in the priority list. As a result, some ‘top-five’ lists contained up to seven learning outcomes.

In addiction and mental health, 19 of 30 ideas (63%) were deemed to be of high importance, and 16 of the 19 (84%) reached consensus. Items with the highest ranks are listed in Table 2. In round two, participants in the addiction and mental health group prioritized six learning outcomes, and only three were among the most highly ranked in the first round of voting. Although ‘providing trauma-informed care’ was prioritized (Table 3), this learning outcome did not reach consensus in the first round of voting.

In health equity and anti-racism, high importance was attributed to 56 of 68 learning outcomes (82%), and 31 of these 56 (55%) reached consensus. Top learning outcomes covered ideas such as ‘racism in health care’ and ‘barriers for marginalized populations’ (Table 2). Of the learning outcomes that appeared on the highest priority list (Table 3), only two also appeared on the list of highest importance (Table 2). Of note, ‘trauma-informed care’ was prioritized by the participants in both the addiction and mental health and the health equity and anti-racism groups.

The home and long-term care nominal group generated 28 learning outcomes, of which 20 (71%) were deemed highly important; of these 20, 19 (95%) reached consensus. All participants felt it was important that residents learn how to evaluate patients in the home where resources are limited (Table 2); this outcome was also prioritized (Table 3). Participants prioritized the provision of palliative care and practice management as well. All prioritized learning outcomes reached consensus in round one.

Participants in the Indigenous health group deemed 33 of 36 learning outcomes (92%) to be of high importance, and, of these 33 highly important ideas, reached consensus on 28 (85%). All participants (100%) agreed on nine of the learning outcomes generated (Table 2). While all prioritized learning outcomes reached consensus, none of them appeared on the ‘most important’ list.

In the area of leadership, advocacy and scholarship, 26 of 44 learning outcomes (59%) were deemed highly important; of the 26, consensus was reached for 17 (65%). Important learning outcomes included the use of evidence-based medicine, the evaluation of guidelines, and the practice of reflection to enable continued improvement (Table 2). Three of the learning outcomes that were unanimously deemed important were also prioritized in round two. One of the prioritized learning outcomes did not reach consensus (Table 3).

In virtual care and health informatics, high importance was attributed to 13 of 21 learning outcomes (62%), and 8 of the 13 (62%) reached consensus. Learning outcomes were related to awareness of available technologies (including their limitations and appropriate use), medicolegal implications, and ways of improving efficiency (Table 2). All of the prioritized learning outcomes except ‘safe use of technologies’ reached consensus in round one (Table 3).

Discussion

The local stakeholders who took part in our nominal group study generated 227 learning outcomes around the CFPC’s six areas of societal need. To interpret these learning outcomes, we will first ground them in the four principles of family medicine as defined by the CFPC [35]. Second, we will look across the six areas to identify similarities in the generated learning outcomes, and will share some considerations for implementation.

Contextualizing learning outcomes in the four principles of family medicine

Though the focus of our study was the Canadian system and the CFPC report [26], we note that similar medical education priorities were highlighted in the Edinburgh Declaration, underscoring their relevance internationally [36]. Furthermore, international family medicine education bodies such as WONCA [10] endorse many of the larger themes identified by the CFPC and examined in our study. Additionally, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) called earlier this year for continued improvement to the delivery of culturally safe, patient-centred care [37], pointing to the broad relevance of our findings.

The four principles of family medicine are foundational ideas that shape the discipline of family medicine: (1) the family physician is a skilled clinician; (2) family medicine is a community-based discipline; (3) the family physician is a resource to a defined practice population; (4) the patient-physician relationship is central to the role of the family physician [35]. For the purpose of this discussion, we will look first at the learning outcomes that speak to the foundation of medicine (medical expertise, the first principle). Then we will discuss the learning outcomes related to the more social aspects of being a family physician (principles two to four). Learning outcomes related to the first principle of family medicine were generated in the addiction and mental health, home and long-term care, leadership, advocacy and scholarship, and virtual care nominal group sessions. Given the contemporary concerns surrounding mental health (e.g., the crisis of addiction and suicide) [38–40], the fast-changing world of informatics (e.g., AI-aided health care), and our aging population, it is not surprising that many learning outcomes in the associated areas of societal need speak to the fact that family physicians must be skilled clinicians. Learning outcomes prioritized in leadership, advocacy and scholarship emphasized the skills clinicians need in order to evolve with the rapid changes in medicine. Many of the highly important and prioritized learning outcomes generated in the area of virtual care and health informatics related to the delivery of safe, high-quality virtual care [41]. By highlighting the specific clinical skills required to meet new and challenging areas of health care, our study underscores the centrality of the principle that the ‘family physician is a skilled clinician’.

Second, health equity and anti-racism and Indigenous health share many similarities and relate to the remaining three principles of family medicine: ‘being community-based’, ‘being a resource for a defined population’, and ‘the patient-physician relationship being the cornerstone of the practice’ [35]. The generated learning outcomes point to ongoing health care inequities and to the marginalization of some populations, which lead to poorer health outcomes. By building curriculum content to address foundational knowledge gaps and to highlight ongoing racial inequities in health care systems, physicians will be better prepared to address system-level change [42].

Alongside the four principles of family medicine, other frameworks, including CanMEDS, also support educational programming in medicine [43]. While the medical expert role underpins the profession of medicine, recent work to revise the CanMEDS framework [44] aligns with calls from the OECD [37] and the CFPC to adjust training so that physicians might be catalysts for societal change and meet the diverse needs of the communities they serve.

Implementing curricular changes is challenging and heavily dependent on local context [45]. However, other published work has demonstrated successful implementation of a structural racism curriculum using co-design approaches, such as engaging with community members [46]. Given programs’ diverse needs, it can be helpful to use a structured curriculum development framework like Kern’s six-step approach [47].

Themes and implications

We now shift our perspective to look across all six areas of societal need in order to identify similarities in the generated learning outcomes. We thus discern two ‘clusters’ of learning outcomes — patient-centred care and professionalism — which we will discuss below. We will close this section by comparing the outcomes of the two rounds of voting (steps 4a and 4b).

Participants prioritized learning outcomes that cluster around patient-centred care (including cultural competence). These outcomes were generated in four of the six areas of societal need: health equity and anti-racism, Indigenous health, home and long-term care, and addiction and mental health. Ultimately, the learning outcomes and skills required to meet these areas of societal need would support care delivery in family medicine and other disciplines [44]. These learning outcomes are consistent with existing research showing that cultural competence and patient-centred training can help improve health care quality for diverse and vulnerable populations [48, 49].

We also find that learning outcomes from four of the six areas form a cluster around professionalism. Professionalism-related learning outcomes were prioritized in leadership, advocacy and scholarship, virtual care and health informatics, Indigenous health, and health equity and anti-racism. The professionalism cluster is broad, and includes awareness of personal biases and ongoing learning needs, as well as commitment to ongoing learning and improvement. Self-reflective practices amongst physicians are brought to the fore by the heightened focus on diverse societal needs and by an awareness of ongoing inequities [44, 49]. The importance of learning outcomes around personal reflection such as these is supported by existing professionalism literature [50, 51]. Professionalism training in medical curricula must be adapted to the local medical school. In addition, schools must ensure that faculty development supports this professionalism training [50]. In future curriculum development, it will be important to identify educational interventions that are most impactful at reducing health inequities and improving care for marginalized groups [49, 52].

Finally, we note that learning outcomes prioritized in round two diverged from those deemed most important in round one of voting. In Indigenous health, for instance, none of the six prioritized learning outcomes had the highest importance scores. Given the scope of our study, we did not ask participants why they prioritized the items that they did. One consideration is the small size of the groups. Prioritization lists can be heavily influenced by two individuals who believe an item is of high priority. While some items received votes from many and sometimes all participants, other learning outcomes on the list reflect the views of just a couple of individuals. This is likely the case for the four prioritized learning outcomes that did not reach consensus in round one voting.

A second consideration emerges from the difference in the characters of the two rounds of voting: It is understandable that participants would assign theoretical importance to many of the learning outcomes individually. Yet, when they are forced to choose which learning outcomes to include in a new curriculum (and which to forego), their focus shifts to reflect feasibility and impact.

Overall, the cross-area view we have taken in this section can help sharpen the focus of curriculum developers as they face decisions around prioritization.

Limitations

Although the societal needs addressed in this study have been recognized internationally [10, 37], this research was rooted in the Canadian context, which may limit its usefulness for some. Family physicians and academics working in academic family medicine also have particular views on these issues that may not always be transferable. Moreover, the majority of our participants were clinician educators; therefore, while we used the standard NGT process, which provides equal opportunity for discussion, the clinician educator perspective is naturally more heavily weighted in our outcomes. We designed our nominal groups to be conducted both online and in person to enhance generalisability and to enable broad participation, including from those in rural or remote locations. This hybrid design, however, may have favoured individuals who participated in person. While we attempted to provide equal opportunity for all, there may have been instances when online participants were not as readily able to engage.

Conclusion

Our study presents the first stakeholder-driven list of CFPC-aligned learning priorities, generating 227 learning outcomes across the six areas of societal need. The local focus of this work is complemented by our Delphi study, which provides an international perspective [27]. Taken together, these results address globally relevant societal needs. Despite contextual variations, implementation of our findings could support universal principles in family medicine, such as comprehensiveness and continuity; implementation could also help address concerns raised in the 2025 OECD report on patient-reported outcomes [37]. Future research should examine any changes to family medicine residency curricula in terms of their effects on training and on patient-oriented outcomes.
