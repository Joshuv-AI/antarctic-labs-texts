Characterization, in vitro release, and antioxidant activity of glutenin hydrolysate encapsulated in liposome‐loaded uni‐axial and co‐axial electrospun fibers
Authors: Nagihan Kalintas Caglar, Ahmet Furkan Caglar, Fatih Bozkurt, Feyzanur Izciler, Osman Sagdic, Canan Yagmur Karakas, Ayse Karadag
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDBioactive peptides derived from protein hydrolysates provide various health benefits; however, their practical application is limited by low gastrointestinal stability, enzymatic degradation, and poor intestinal absorption. Overcoming these challenges remains a key bottleneck for oral peptide delivery. This study aimed to develop and systematically compare uni‐axial and co‐axial electrospun pullulan/carboxymethylcellulose fibers incorporating liposome‐encapsulated glutenin hydrolysate (GH) to enhance its stability, mucoadhesion, and controlled release along the gastrointestinal system.RESULTSGH (7.5 mg mL−1) was encapsulated into lecithin–phytosterol (1:0.5, w/w) liposomes, yielding an average size of 76 nm and an encapsulation efficiency of 57.52%. These liposomes were successfully embedded into nanofibers, showing homogeneous distribution and GH loading efficiencies of 61.04–85.22%. Compared with free GH, liposomal systems preserved the antioxidant activity (ABTS and FRAP values) of GH during gastrointestinal digestion, while the non‐hybrid formulation demonstrated reduced preservation. Liposome‐loaded nanofibers exhibited markedly lower GH release under gastric conditions (21.05–25.85%) than free‐GH fibers (42.69%), while co‐axial fibers provided the most sustained intestinal release. Additionally, liposomal incorporation significantly enhanced mucoadhesive properties.CONCLUSIONThe hybrid liposome–nanofiber approach integrates protective and controlled‐delivery mechanisms, resulting in enhanced preservation of antioxidant activity and sustained release compared with conventional fibers. This food‐grade strategy shows strong potential for oral delivery of bioactive peptides in functional food and nutraceutical applications requiring gastrointestinal stability. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Bioactive peptides – protein‐derived fragments released during enzymatic hydrolysis – exhibit a wide range of physiological functions and have attracted increasing attention as functional food ingredients and nutraceuticals. 1 , 2 Among these, antioxidant peptides and protein hydrolysates are of particular interest due to their ability to scavenge free radicals and mitigate oxidative stress. 3 , 4 , 5 Despite their promising benefits, the practical application of bioactive peptides remains limited by low bioavailability and poor stability during food processing and gastrointestinal (GI) transit. 3 Exposure to acidic gastric conditions, digestive enzymes, bile salts, and the mucus layer covering the intestinal epithelium can significantly reduce peptide integrity and absorption, representing a major challenge for effective oral delivery of peptide‐based nutraceuticals. 5 , 6

To address these limitations, various delivery systems have been explored to protect bioactive peptides and improve their gastrointestinal performance. Liposomes, which can encapsulate hydrophilic, hydrophobic, and amphiphilic compounds, have emerged as promising carriers for enhancing oral peptide absorption by facilitating transit across the intestinal epithelium. 7 , 8 , 9

However, conventional liposomes suffer from several inherent drawbacks, including thermodynamic instability, premature leakage of encapsulated compounds, and high susceptibility of the phospholipid bilayer to digestive enzymes and bile salts. 10 , 11 These limitations necessitate the development of advanced formulation strategies that can enhance liposome stability while maintaining their delivery properties under GI conditions.

Electrospinning, a simple and widely utilized technique in drug delivery for producing fibers, faces the primary challenge of controlling the rapid release of the encapsulated compound. 12 Incorporating intact liposomes into electrospun fibers has been proposed as an effective approach to mitigate burst release and enhance protection of sensitive bioactives. 10 , 13 , 14 Furthermore, co‐axial electrospinning, which produces core–shell nanofibers through the simultaneous extrusion of separate core and shell solutions, has also been utilized to sustain prolonged release profiles. 15 Previous studies reported enhanced stability and controlled release of salmon calcitonin when pectin‐coated liposomes were integrated into uni‐axial and co‐axial electrospun polyvinyl acetate (PVA) and sodium alginate fibers. 16 , 17

Despite these advances, the application of integrated liposome–nanofiber systems in food‐related contexts remains limited, particularly for colon‐targeted peptide delivery, 16 , 17 packaging, 18 , 19 , 20 and oral delivery. 21 While synthetic polymers such as polyethylene oxide (PEO), PVA, and polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) 16 , 19 , 22 dominate nanofiber production, limited studies have utilized food‐grade polymers like zein, chitosan, pullulan, and pectin. 20 , 21 , 23

Pullulan (PUL) is a favored hydrophilic biopolymer composed of repeating maltotriose units linked by α‐(1→6) and α‐(1→4) glycosidic bonds, providing flexibility, water solubility, and excellent fiber‐forming ability. 24 Carboxymethylcellulose (CMC), although unsuitable for electrospinning alone, is water‐soluble, mucoadhesive, and compatible with pullulan. 25 Hydrogen bonding between the COO− groups of CMC and the —OH groups of pullulan improves the mechanical and structural properties of the resulting fibers, enhancing their suitability for electrospinning. 26 Mucoadhesive polymers are favored in oral delivery systems by prolonging retention at mucosal surfaces, 27 and liposome entrapment has been shown further to enhance mucoadhesion in fibers and films. 21 , 28 , 29

In this context, the present study aimed to develop a hybrid delivery system by integrating glutenin hydrolysate (GH)‐loaded liposomes into uni‐axial and co‐axial electrospun PUL/CMC nanofibers to enhance peptide stability and controlled release during simulated gastrointestinal digestion. GH, previously demonstrated by our group to exhibit antioxidant, antidiabetic, and anticancer activities, 30 was used as the model bioactive peptide. Free GH, nanofiber‐encapsulated free GH, and liposomal GH‐loaded nanofibers were fabricated and systematically characterized, and all formulations were evaluated under simulated GI conditions to assess antioxidant retention and GH release behavior, providing insight into their potential application in functional food and nutraceutical systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Ethanol, sodium azide, NaOH, HCl, TPTZ (T1253), FeCl3.6H2O, K2S2O8, ABTS, Triton X‐100 (T8787), α‐amylase (A1031), mucin (M2378), pancreatin (P7545), pepsin (P7012), and Tris–HCl were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). Membrane filter (14629‐47‐D, PES, 5.000 MWCO), AmiconUltra filters (UFC905024), and BCA Protein Assay Kit and bile salt were obtained from Sartorius (Göttingen, Germany), Millipore (UK), Thermo Fisher (Walton, MA, USA), and Biolife (Milan, Italy), respectively. Phytosterol (Vitasterol S‐80 WDP (900 g kg−1); Supporting Information, Table S1), lecithin (Lipoid S75, Lipoid GmbH, Ludwigshafen, Germany), pullulan (Prinova Nagase Group, Itasca, IL, USA), carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, METHOCEL E4M) and Flavourzyme (500–1000 LAPU g−1, Novozymes, Bagsvaerd, Denmark) were kindly donated from the related companies.

Methods

Preparation of wheat GH and characterization

The hydrolysis procedure was adopted from a previously published study by our research group, 30 and consistent with methods reported in the literature for wheat gluten hydrolysis aimed at producing bioactive peptides. 31 , 32 Gluten was extracted from wheat flour (AACC Method 38‐10), 33 washed with aqueous ethanol (700 mL L−1) to isolate glutenin, dried (45 °C), and stored at −20 °C. Hydrolysis was conducted using Flavourzyme (10 g L−1, enzyme/substrate ratio) in phosphate buffer (10 mmol L−1, pH 7) containing sodium azide (0.2 g L−1) at 50 °C for 4 h, followed by enzyme inactivation (90 °C, 10 min). The hydrolysate was centrifuged and filtered through a 5 kDa membrane; 30 the permeate (<5 kDa) was freeze‐dried (Martin Christ, Osterode am Harz, Germany) and stored at 4 °C until analysis.

The degree of hydrolysis (DH%) was measured by the pH‐stat method, 34 calculated as the ratio of cleaved peptide bonds (h) to the total number of bonds (h tot) per unit weight of protein: (1) DH%=BNbMpahtot×100 where B is the volume of base spent (mL), N b is the normality of the base (N), M p is the protein mass (g), a is the average degree of dissociation of α‐NH2 groups, and h tot represents the total of peptide bonds (mEq g−1 protein).

The antioxidant activity of GH was assessed using ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) and 2,2′‐azino‐bis(3‐ethylbenzothiazoline‐6‐sulfonic acid (ABTS+) assays. FRAP results were expressed as μg ascorbic acid equivalents (AAE) mg−1 GH), while ABTS+ scavenging activity was reported as IC₅₀ values (mg GH mL−1). 30 The calibration curve for FRAP assay ranged from 1 to 5 μg AAE mL−1 with the equation of y = 0.0927x − 0.0022 (R 2 = 0.997).

Production of liposomes and characterization

Liposomes were prepared by thin‐film hydration, followed by sonication. 35 Lecithin (20 g L−1) and phytosterols (1:0.10–1:0.75, w/w) were dissolved in ethanol, evaporated at 40 °C under reduced pressure to form a lipid film, and hydrated with GH in phosphate buffer (10 mmol L−1, pH 7). The dispersion was heated (60 °C, 30 min) and sonicated (10 cycles, 60% amplitude; UP400S, Hielscher, Teltow, Germany).

Particle size, zeta potential (ζ), and polydispersity index (PDI) were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Nano ZS, Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK), and morphology was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; model HT7800, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) after negative staining.

Encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of GH was determined by separating free GH using Amicon ultra‐centrifugal filters (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) at 5000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C. 36 The protein content in the filtrate was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit. 37 Briefly, 25 μL of sample was mixed with 200 μL reagent, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and cooled to room temperature for 10 min; absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan SkyHigh, Thermo Fisher). A calibration curve was constructed using GH concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 3 mg mL−1 (y = 0.2544x + 0.1994; R 2 = 0.999), where x and y represent GH concentration and absorbance, respectively. EE% was calculated according to Eqn (2): (2) EE%=A−BA×100 where A is the amount of GH loaded into liposomes and B is the amount of free GH detected in the filtrate.

Production of fibers

Polymer concentrations and ratios were optimized in preliminary studies (Supporting Information, Figs S1 and S2). PUL (250 g L−1) and CMC (30 g L−1) were prepared in phosphate buffer (10 mmol L−1, pH 7) and blended at 80:20 (v/v). For uni‐axial fibers, empty or GH‐loaded liposomes were added at 10:90 (v/v). Electrospinning (HO‐NFES‐0434 Unit, Holmarc Opto‐Mechatronics Pvt Ltd, Kerala, India) was carried out at 15.8 kV with a 0.35 mL h−1 flow rate and 10 cm needle‐to‐collector distance (25 °C, 50% relative humidity).

For co‐axial fibers, the core phase contained PUL (250 g L−1) with liposomes (85:15, v/v), and the shell phase consisted of the PUL/CMC blend. Co‐axial electrospinning was performed at 18.8 kV with outer/inner flow rates of 0.30/0.05 mL h−1 and a 12 cm collection distance (Table 1).

Loading efficiency of GH in fibers

Fibers (100 mg) were dissolved overnight in phosphate buffer (10 mmol L−1, pH 7.0), then mixed (1:1, v/v) with Triton X‐100 (5 g L−1) to disrupt liposomal structures. 38 Triton X‐100 is a well‐characterized non‐ionic surfactant known to efficiently solubilize phospholipid bilayers while minimizing protein denaturation. 39 , 40 , 41 The same extraction and quantification procedure was applied to all formulations to ensure methodological consistency during loading efficiency (LE%) determination. The mixture was sonicated for 5 min, incubated for 15 min, and centrifuged at 13 600 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered (0.45 μm), and GH content was quantified as described under ‘Production of liposomes and characterization’, above.

Morphology of fibers

Fiber morphology was analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; FEI Quanta 450 FEG, FEI Co., Vienna, Austria), and average diameters were calculated from >100 fibers using ImageJ. Confocal images were obtained with a Zeiss LSM 780 microscope. Core–shell structures of co‐axial fibers were confirmed by field emission SEM (Apreo 2 S LoVac STEM detector, Thermo Scientific).

Thermal behavior of fibers

The fibers were examined using thermogravimetric and differential thermal analysis (TG‐DTA) and a TA Q100 differential scanning calorimeter (Thermo DSC Q2, TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) in a nitrogen environment (20 mL min−1). The samples were heated from 20 to 500 °C at 10 °C min−1.

Fourier transform infrared spectra of fibers

Infrared spectra of the samples were acquired using a Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrophotometer (ATR‐FTIR, Tensor 27, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany). The spectra were obtained in absorption mode at a resolution of 4 cm−1 in a wavelength range of 4000–500 cm−1.

In vitro and ex vivo mucoadhesiveness of fibers

In vitro mucoadhesion was evaluated using porcine mucin discs on a texture analyzer (TA‐XT2Plus, Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, UK) in adhesive mode. 42 Fibers were attached to a P/10 probe (10 mm), and hydrated mucin discs (0.1 mol L−1 phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4, 60 s) were fixed to the holder. The probe operated at 0.5 mm s−1 under a 5 g force.

Ex vivo mucoadhesion was performed as described by Kalintas Caglar et al. 21 Lamb abomasal mucosa was rinsed (0.1 mol L−1 PBS, pH 7.4), sectioned (~2.5 × 2.5 cm), and equilibrated in enzyme‐free simulated intestinal fluid (pH 7, 37 °C) for 15 min. The membrane was mounted at 37 °C, and measurements were conducted under the same probe conditions. At least six replicates were analyzed per sample.

In vitro GI digestion assay

In vitro simulated digestion was performed according to Minekus et al. 43 using simulated salivary (SSF), gastric (SGF), and intestinal fluids (SIF) (Supporting Information, Table S2). Fibers (0.75 g) or free GH (~3.5 mg) were sequentially subjected to oral (α‐amylase, 2 min, pH 7), gastric (pepsin, 2 h, pH 3), and intestinal phases (pancreatin and bile, 2 h, pH 7) at 37 °C under shaking conditions. Digestion was terminated at 85 °C for 10 min. 9 The antioxidant activity values reported for GH‐loaded samples were obtained after subtraction of the corresponding blank controls, which were subjected to digestion under identical conditions. Samples were centrifuged (13 600 × g, 10 min), cooled, lyophilized, redissolved in phosphate buffer (10 mmol L−1, pH 7), and analyzed for antioxidant activity (see ‘Preparation of wheat GH and characterization’, above).

In vitro release of GH

GH release was evaluated at 30 min intervals during simulated digestion conditions. At each time point, 100 μL of digesta was collected and replaced with fresh medium. Samples were centrifuged (13 600 × g 10 min, 4 °C) and cooled on ice, and GH content in the supernatant was quantified as described under ‘Production of liposomes and characterization’, above. For low‐concentration samples, 25 μL of digesta was mixed with 200 μL BCA reagent and incubated at 45 °C for 30 min. GH concentration was calculated using a calibration curve (0.05–2 mg mL−1; y = 0.0003x − 0.0262, R 2 = 0.998). The cumulative release rate (%) of GH was plotted over time according to Eqn (3): (3) Cumulative release%=MtM0×100 where M 0 is the initial amount of GH, and M t is the cumulative quantity of GH released from digested samples.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were done three times in triplicate samples, and the data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. SPSS Statistics 25 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's test. In addition, Student's t‐test was used to evaluate differences between pre‐ and post‐digestion values within each formulation. For all results, a P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Characterization of GH

Flavourzyme – a mixture of endoproteases and exopeptidases produced by Aspergillus oryzae – is commonly used to hydrolyze proteins to generate antioxidative or radical scavenging peptides. 44 In the present study, the DH% of GH was 5.45 ± 0.48%, which is comparable to the value of GH (5%) reported in Bozkurt et al. 30 and to the hydrolysate of canola protein (6.33%) produced using Flavourzyme. 45 The antioxidant activities determined by FRAP and ABTS+ assays were 0.436 ± 0.02 μg AAE mg−1 GH and an IC50 value of 1096.13 ± 13.17 μg GH mL−1, respectively. The antioxidant activity of protein hydrolysates is primarily dependent on the peptide size, amino acid sequence, and the hydrolysis conditions, including enzyme type, enzyme‐to‐substrate ratio, temperature, and pH. 46

Characterization of GH‐loaded liposomes

The size of blank liposomes (without GH) was around 60 nm and remained relatively unaffected by increasing phytosterol content up to a certain lecithin:sterol ratio. At higher inclusion levels (1:0.75, w/w), the size was increased to around 73 nm (Table 2), likely due to sterol occupation within the lipid membrane. 47 When GH was loaded, vesicle size exhibited slight decreases or increases depending on the lecithin:sterol ratio. For example, loading GH into plain lecithin liposomes (without phytosterol) enlarged the vesicle size (P > 0.05). At an inclusion ratio of 1:0.1 (w/w), the size decreased (P < 0.05) to approximately 55 nm, whereas at a ratio of 1:0.5 (w/w) the size slightly increased to about 67 nm. Wong 48 suggested that low sterol concentrations may enhance bilayer fluidity, leading to a reduced vesicle size, while higher sterol levels might diminish membrane fluidity, induce inter‐lipid spacing, and result in membrane expansion and increased size. 49 , 50 TEM images confirmed a spherical morphology with a monolayer structure, consistent with vesicle sizes ranging from 53 to 66 nm (Fig. 1), in agreement with DLS measurements.

Phytosterol incorporation increased the zeta potential of GH‐loaded liposomes but largely unaffected blank liposomes (Table 2), suggesting altered peptide–phospholipid interactions at higher sterol levels. 51 , 52 Most liposomes showed a low PDI (<0.3), indicating a uniform particle size distribution (Table 2), except for GH‐loaded plain liposomes.

A high EE% is desirable for bioactive delivery systems, with reported values for protein hydrolysate‐loaded liposomes ranging from 28% to 92%. 51 , 53 The diverse peptide composition in different protein hydrolysates complicates an understanding of their affinity for liposome structures. EE% of GH (5 mg mL−1) in plain liposomes (without phytosterol) was low (17.46 ± 1.37%), but increased substantially to 66.09 ± 1.21% with phytosterol incorporation up to 50% of lecithin concentration (w/w), before decreasing at higher sterol levels (Table 2). Similar increases in EE% upon phytosterol inclusion have been reported for other bioactives such as quercetin and ascorbic acid. 54 , 55

Sterol molecules embedded in the phospholipid bilayer interacts with fatty acid chains through the sterol tail at C17, while the hydroxyl group at C3 simultaneously interacts with the hydrophilic head groups of the phospholipids. 56 These interactions fill bilayer gaps, strengthen membrane integrity, and reduce leakage by limiting the permeation of encapsulated compounds. 57 However, at higher sterol levels, increased membrane rigidity restricts the lipid rearrangement during encapsulation, limiting the bilayer's ability to accommodate peptide molecules and promoting GH expulsion or leakage from vesicles. Similar observations showed that excessive sterol incorporation reduces lipid chain mobility and induces membrane defects, ultimately reducing encapsulation efficiency. 58

Increased GH concentrations (0–10 mg mL−1) in liposomes with a fixed lecithin:phytosterol ratio (1:0.5, w/w) resulted in a gradual increase in particle size from approximately 60 to 90 nm (Table 3), consistent with findings by Hosseini et al. 51 The higher zeta potential of GH‐loaded liposomes (−35.93 to −39.73 mV) compared to blank liposomes (−32.97 mV) (Table 3) suggests adsorption or partial insertion of charged peptide at the liposome surface. 35 While PDI values showed no significant differences (P > 0.05) at most GH concentrations, the slight increase observed at 10 mg mL−1 may reflect colloidal heterogeneity induced by excess charged peptides. EE% decreased significantly from 66.09 to 32.02% as GH loading increased from 5 to 10 mg mL−1 (Table 3), indicating saturation effects consistent with prior studies. 51 , 59

Characterization of empty, GH‐ and liposomal GH‐loaded fibers

Morphological analysis of fibers

The size of empty uni‐axial (Fig. 2(A)) and co‐axial fibers (Fig. 3(A)), without liposome or GH, were approximately 188 and 195 nm, respectively. Incorporating free GH increased the diameter of uni‐axial fibers to 216 nm (Fig. 2(B)), whereas a slight reduction was observed for co‐axial fibers (181 nm, Fig. 3(B)). GH‐loaded liposomes (7.5 mg mL−1; lecithin:phytosterol, 1:05, w/w) and their blank counterparts were then employed to produce liposome‐loaded fibers. Incorporation of blank and GH‐loaded liposomes increased uni‐axial fiber diameters to around 254 nm (Fig. 2(C)) and 241 nm (Fig. 2(D)), respectively. Co‐axial fibers containing GH‐loaded liposomes exhibited an average diameter of 219 nm (Fig. 3(C)) – slightly larger than the empty co‐axial fibers (195 nm). Co‐axial electrospinning consistently produced thinner fibers across all formulations, which can be attributed to the presence of shell fluid, which moderates rapid solvent evaporation, stabilizes jet formation, and enables extended stretching. 60

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images of liposomal GH‐loaded fibers (Fig. 4), in which both liposomes and polymers were previously dyed, revealed a uniform dispersion of liposomes (green‐yellow) as small, spherical vesicles embedded within red threads of PUL/CMC fibers. Overlay CLSM images confirmed liposome incorporation into both uni‐axial and co‐axial fibers (Fig. 4(C),(F)). Co‐axial fibers showed lower liposome fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4(E)), accompanied by stronger fiber signals (Fig. 4(D)), which can be attributed to shielding of the liposome‐containing core by the surrounding shell layer. In CLSM analysis, fluorescence intensity typically decreases with increasing sample depth due to absorption or scattering of both excitation and emitted light by upper layers, leading to reduced signal detection from deeper regions. 61 Consistently, filed emission SEM images (Fig. 4(G)) clearly demonstrated a distinct core–shell morphology, with contrast differences between inner and outer regions confirming successful core–shell formation.

(A–F) Confocal laser scanning microscopy images of liposomal glutenin hydrolysate (GH)‐loaded nanofibers. (A, D) Liposomes stained with Nile Red. (B, E) Polymers stained with Fast Green. (C, F) Overlay images. (G1, G2) STEM images of core–shell structure of co‐axial fiber.

All fibers exhibited uniform, defect‐free morphology, except for GH‐loaded uni‐axial fibers, which displayed random bead‐like structures (Fig. 2(B)). Bead formation during electrospinning is commonly associated with imbalances in solution viscosity and conductivity that disrupt jet stability. 16 Although these parameters were not directly measured in the present study, incorporation of charged peptide molecules such as GH into the uni‐axial spinning solution may influence jet stability. Increased charge density in uni‐axial systems could enhance coulombic repulsion forces within the jet, potentially destabilizing the Taylor cone and leading to bead formation, as previously reported for pullulan‐based systems containing charged additives. 62

In contrast, the absence of bead formation in liposome‐containing uni‐axial fibers may be related to the presence of lecithin. As a zwitterionic phospholipid, lecithin can reduce surface tension and act as a plasticizing surfactant, thereby improving spinnability. 63 Lecithin has been reported to enhance electrospinnability in alginate/PEO and pullulan/zein systems by modifying rheological properties, increasing solution viscosity, and promoting more elastic network behavior, resulting in uniform fiber formation. 64 , 65

In co‐axial electrospinning, separate core and shell streams form a compound Taylor cone in which the sheath flow governs jet stability. 66 , 67 The electrospinnable shell can stabilize non‐spinnable or charged core components, limiting their influence on the outer solution. Accordingly, confinement of GH within the core phase reduces its impact on spinning parameters. Wang et al. 68 further demonstrated that the shell phase buffers electrohydrodynamic instabilities induced by charged core components, resulting in uniform, bead‐free fibers.

Structural characterization

The FTIR spectra of individual components and electrospun nanofibers are given in Fig. 5. PUL and CMC exhibited characteristic bands at 3000–3700 cm−1 corresponding to O—H stretching vibrations, 800–950 cm−1 associated with α‐glucopyranoside units and α‐(1→6) glycosidic bonds of PUL, and at 1148 cm−1 attributed to glycosidic bond stretching. 69 Additional bands observed at 950–1250 cm−1 were related to C—C, C—O, and C—H stretching vibrations, 70 while peaks in the range of 1413–1600 cm−1 corresponded to symmetric COO− vibrations of CMC. 26 In the GH spectrum, absorption bands between 1404 and 1588 cm−1 were assigned to CN stretching and NH bending vibrations in the amide II region, which are characteristic for peptide structures. 71

E: empty with no liposome or GH; GH, BL and GHL: loaded fibers with GH, blank liposome and liposomal GH, respectively.

The spectra of empty uniaxial (U‐E) and coaxial (C‐E) fibers showed shifts in carbohydrate‐related peaks, including a shift of pullulan from 925 to 1018 cm−1 and CMC from 1022 to 1020 cm−1, indicating intermolecular interactions between PUL and CMC. 72 These shifts are mainly attributed to hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl groups of PUL and carboxylate groups of CMC, leading to the formation of a polymeric complex within the nanofiber matrix, consistent with previous reports on pullulan‐based electrospun systems. 73 , 74

Upon incorporation of GH, blank liposomes, or liposomal GH into the fibers (Fig. 5(B),(C)), slight but consistent shifts and reductions in band intensities were observed in both the amide II region and the carbohydrate‐related regions. The reduced intensity and partial masking of GH‐specific amide bands suggest that GH was not present in a free molecular state but was effectively entrapped within the polymer–liposome network. In liposomal GH‐loaded fibers, these changes were more pronounced (U‐GHL: 1018 to 1015 cm−1; C‐GHL: 1020 to 1017 cm−1), suggesting additional interactions between peptide functional groups and the phospholipid bilayer, along with secondary interactions with the surrounding PUL/CMC matrix. 74 These findings are indicative of hydrogen bonding and possible electrostatic interactions, confirming the formation of GH–liposome–polysaccharide complexes. 69

The molecular interactions identified by FTIR analysis were further supported by thermal analysis. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) thermograms of PUL and CMC revealed endothermic peaks at 136.40 and 128.08 °C, respectively, which shifted to approximately 102 °C in both U‐E and C‐E fibers, (Fig. 6), consistent with the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds between the polymer chains. 69 Incorporation of blank liposomes (U‐BL) and liposomal‐GH (U‐GHL) into uni‐axial fibers resulted in peak shifts to 112.72 and 102.02 °C, respectively. In co‐axial fibers, the corresponding endothermic peaks appeared at 94.41 and 98.81 °C for blank liposome and GH‐liposome loaded samples, respectively. These shifts could be attributed to water loss and formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds between polysaccharides and liposomes, 75 indicating structural interactions within the fiber matrix.

Thermogravimetric analysis further revealed that incorporation of blank liposomes, free GH, or liposomal GH did not negatively affect the thermal stability of PUL/CMC nanofibers (Fig. 7). All samples exhibited two main weight‐loss stages. The first stage, observed up to ~140 °C, corresponded to moisture evaporation. Pure pullulan showed a weight loss of 3.69%, while higher losses were observed for PUL/CMC uni‐axial (U‐E, 5.85%) and co‐axial (C‐E, 4.70%) fibers. This increase was associated with altered water‐binding behavior in fibers resulting from intermolecular hydrogen bonding between polymers. The higher moisture loss observed in uni‐axial fibers suggests stronger polymer–polymer interactions compared to co‐axial structures. 26

The second major weight‐loss stage, occurring near 300 °C, corresponded to polymer degradation. 76 In the empty uni‐axial nanofiber (U‐E), the maximum decomposition rate was observed at 311.6 °C, which slightly decreased upon loading free GH (308.3 °C), blank liposome (308.7 °C), and liposomal GH (307.1 °C). These minor shifts can be attributed to the presence of additional organic components, which may locally disrupt polymer chain packing without compromising the overall thermal integrity of the nanofibers. 74 In contrast, empty co‐axial nanofibers (C‐E) showed a maximum degradation temperature of 311.3 °C, which remained nearly unchanged after loading (310.4, 311.8, and 311.1 °C for C‐GH, C‐BL and C‐GHL, respectively), indicating that the core–shell structure provides structural confinement and limits the thermal influence of loaded components.

At approximately 600 °C, liposomal GH‐loaded fibers exhibited a higher residue compared to empty fibers, with values of 18.11% and 14.76% for U‐GHL and C‐GHL, exceeding those of U‐E (13.51%), C‐E (11.14%), U‐BL (17.65%), and C‐BL (12.80%). The increased residual mass suggests the formation of a more compact and interconnected network driven by polymer–liposome and peptide–polymer interactions. 76 , 77 Similar thermal behavior has been reported for liposome‐loaded pullulan‐based films and nanofibers, confirming that incorporation of liposomal bioactives preserves or enhances thermal stability. 74 , 76 Such interactions promote restricted chain mobility and enhanced resistance to thermal decomposition, which is consistent with the hydrogen bonding and complex formation identified by FTIR and DSC analyses.

Loading efficiency of fibers

The loading efficiency (LE%) of GH into nanofibers was calculated by comparing the total peptide content in the fibers to that present in the initial feed solution. Triton X‐100 was used to disrupt the structure of the liposomes when determining the amount of peptide in the fibers. The use of Triton X‐100 to achieve release of encapsulated proteins and peptides from liposomal systems has been widely reported in the literature. 78 , 79 , 80 , 81

The LE% values were 85.22 ± 3.15% for U‐GH, 77.72 ± 3.88% for U‐GHL, and 61.04 ± 0.82% for C‐GHL, indicating a clear dependence of peptide loading on fiber architecture and loading strategy. Kirbas and Altay 82 reported a 77.97% peptide loading into PUL/alginate/CaCl2 uni‐axial fibers; Soto et al. 83 achieved 80.82–95.41% loading of nisin in amaranth protein/ PUL uni‐axial nanofibers; and Rajanna et al. 84 observed 72.95–86.04% loading of casein‐derived peptides into uni‐axial PUL nanofibers.

For bioactive compounds incorporated into nanofibers via uniaxial electrospinning, a loading efficiency theoretically approaching ⁓100% may be anticipated, as the peptide is directly blended with the polymer solution prior to fiber formation. However, deviations from the theoretical maximum are commonly reported and may arise from unavoidable systematic losses during electrospinning, including solution retention within the syringe and needle (dead volume), deposition losses during jet travel, handling‐related losses during fiber collection and extraction. 85

Among formulations, uni‐axial fibers containing free GH (U‐GH) exhibited the highest LE% (85.22 ± 3.15%), reflecting efficient incorporation under the applied conditions. In uni‐axial electrohydrodynamic systems, the bioactive compound is distributed throughout the bulk polymer phase without compartmentalization, and loading behavior is primarily governed by polymer‐bioactive interactions and solution properties such as viscosity and chain entanglement. 86 Incorporation of GH in liposomal form resulted in lower LE% values within a comparable range (61.04–77.72%), including co‐axial fibers (C‐GHL), where GH was first encapsulated within the liposomes and subsequently integrated into a core–shell fiber structure.

Mucoadhesive properties of fibers

Results from in vitro and ex vivo mucoadhesion assays indicated that loading liposomal GH into the fibers enhanced their mucoadhesive properties compared to both empty fibers and those loaded with free GH (Fig. 8). Consistent with these findings, our previous studies showed that loading liposomal propolis and liposomal mulberry extract into gelatin–zein and pullulan–pectin nanofibers increased mucoadhesion. 21 , 28 Enhanced mucoadhesion has also been reported after incorporating liposomal shrimp peptides into sodium caseinate films, attributed to the plasticizing effect of liposomes, which increases polymer flexibility and promotes interaction with the mucus matrix. 29

The mucoadhesion work of liposomal GH‐loaded co‐axial fibers was lower than that of uni‐axial counterparts, likely due to liposome confinement within the fiber core (Fig. 8(B)). However, in the in vitro assay, co‐axial empty and GH‐loaded fibers showed higher mucoadhesion, which may be attributed to the core–shell architecture promoting more uniform CMC surface distribution and enhanced hydrogen bonding with mucin. The differences observed between in vitro and ex vivo assays may arise from the different interaction mechanisms between the fibers and mucin compared to those occurring at the sheep mucosal membrane. 25

In vitro antioxidant stability of GH in simulated digestion model

Changes in the antioxidant activity of GH during in vitro digestion were assessed using ABTS+ and FRAP assays (Table 4). After digestion, free GH retained only 35.27% of its initial ABTS+ radical scavenging activity. Antioxidant peptides are typically short sequences (2–10 residues), and their activity strongly depends on amino acid sequence, particularly the presence and positioning of histidine, tryptophan, and proline. 87 During GI digestion, these peptides may undergo further hydrolysis into different fragments, potentially leading to reduced antioxidant capacity. 88 , 89

In vitro release of GH in simulated digestion model

Evaluation of GH release profiles (Fig. 9) revealed a rapid initial release of 17.03% within the first 30 min of gastric digestion from uni‐axial GH‐loaded fibers (U‐GH), which is likely related to the high water solubility of pullulan. 91 A similar burst release was reported for uni‐axial amaranth protein/pullulan fibers, showing rapid initial curcumin release during gastric digestion. 76 In the present study, GH release from liposome‐loaded fibers at the end of the gastric phase (21.05% for U‐GHL; 25.85% for C‐GHL) was approximately half that of U‐GH (42.69%). CLSM images of gastric digesta confirmed preserved liposome structures, supporting delayed GH release (Fig. 9(B),(C)).

During the intestinal digestion, GH was continuously released from U‐GH fibers, reaching 78.53% of its initial content. Comparable high intestinal release levels (82–99%) have been reported for quercetin and ferulic acid encapsulated in amaranth protein/PUL uni‐axial fibers. 90 In contrast, liposome‐loaded fibers exhibited lower GH release (57.34% for U‐GHL and 50.54% for C‐GHL), with C‐GHL showing the most sustained release. Under intestinal conditions, bile salt penetration and phospholipid hydrolysis likely disrupted liposomal membranes, consistent with the elongated oblong vesicle features (white arrows) observed in the intestinal digesta of U‐GHL fibers (Fig. 9(B),(C)). In liposomal GH‐loaded fibers, active components initially diffused through the liposome membrane, then across the PUL/CMC sheath. 12 , 21 Incorporation of liposomes into uni‐ and co‐axial PVA/PEO‐alginate fibers has similarly been shown to reduce burst release of salmon peptides and improve sustained release behavior. 16

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates that incorporating GH‐loaded liposomes into electrospun PUL/CMC nanofibers effectively enhances the stability, mucoadhesion, and gastrointestinal performance of bioactive peptides. Phytosterol incorporation significantly improved encapsulation efficiency, with an optimal lecithin‐to‐phytosterol ratio of 1:0.5 (w/w), while higher peptide loading reduced efficiency due to bilayer saturation. Liposomes were uniformly distributed within both uni‐axial and co‐axial fibers, confirming successful integration. FTIR and thermal analyses supported structural interactions within the hybrid system. Liposome‐loaded fibers showed improved mucoadhesion, retained higher antioxidant activity during simulated digestion, and exhibited reduced gastrointestinal release, particularly in co‐axial formulations. These findings indicate that liposome‐loaded electrospun nanofibers represent a promising food‐grade delivery platform for sensitive bioactive peptides. Future studies should focus on scalability and in vivo validation to further support practical applications.
