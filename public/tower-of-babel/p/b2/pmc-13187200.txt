Next-generation digital twin model with unobtrusive RF multi-sensing for AI-based human monitoring
Authors: Sagheer Khan, Aaesha Alzaabi, Imran M Saied, Usman Anwar, Kiran Khurshid, Tughrul Arslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn healthcare, Digital Twin (DT) model can create real-time digital representations of patients’ physiological states, enabling intelligent, data-driven decision-making for continuous monitoring and personalized treatment-without the invasiveness of traditional methods. This study presents a next-generation RF-powered DT model, integrating AI-driven analytics with multi-vital human sensing to establish a virtualized, self-learning, and adaptive framework for health monitoring. Leveraging unobtrusive Radio Frequency (RF) sensors, advanced signal processing, and Artificial Intelligence (AI), the proposed DT model facilitates continuous, non-contact assessment of respiration and exhaled hydration, ensuring a scalable, cost-effective, and efficient approach to real-time physiological monitoring. This work integrates unobtrusive ESP32 Wi-Fi sensors with Channel State Information (CSI) for respiration tracking and flexible UWB RF sensors for hydration monitoring. Enhanced signal processing achieves 100% accurate estimation of Breaths Per Minute (BPM) within the accepted inter-observer variability threshold (±5 BPM) from raw respiration data. To overcome the challenge of limited real-world respiration and hydration datasets, novel statistical augmentation methods are employed to generate synthetic data, validated through cross-correlation techniques (Pearson, Kendall, and Spearman). AI models, including supervised and semi-supervised Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL), are implemented for binary and multi-class classification of respiration and hydration data. Random Forest achieves top accuracies of 88% (binary) and 69% (multi-class) in semi-supervised classification, while Decision Tree attains 89% and 83% accuracy in supervised exhaled hydration classification. Additionally, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) achieves 93% (binary) and 89% (multi-class) accuracy in semi-supervised hydration classification.Keywords: Artificial intelligence (AI), Digital twin (DT), Hydration monitoring, RF sensing, Respiration monitoringSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Healthcare 4.0 (H4.0) represents the fourth healthcare revolution, expanding the horizons of innovation within the field. The core concept of H4.0 focuses on the development of efficient and tailored healthcare services for individuals. This marks a notable shift from the conventional Healthcare 1.0 (H1.0) model, which requires in-person visits for diagnosis. Healthcare 2.0 (H2.0) brought remote communication and medical records via an online portal to interact with healthcare professionals remotely at their convenience. The progression continued with Healthcare 3.0 (H3.0) incorporating remote monitoring technologies and telemedicine for medical care access to patients at home. H4.0 takes the integration of digital healthcare technologies to an entirely new level. It is accomplished through the integration of digital healthcare technologies like the Internet of Things (IoT), big data, Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS), and Artificial Intelligence (AI), to establish a personalized and efficient healthcare system. H4.0 has the potential to offer real-time monitoring, enhance surgical strategies for patients, enhance treatment outcomes, and ensure precise diagnoses. The patient data becomes invaluable for healthcare providers, facilitating the recognition of analytics in health outcomes. This information can contribute to the creation of innovative treatments and advancements in the medical field. The notion of a Digital Twin (DT) model, a dynamic portrayal of a physical system encompassing all its attributes and components, has the potential to revolutionize healthcare by constructing a digital model that mirrors a patient’s physiological and pathological processes. The construction of the DT model is formulated through the integration of various technologies, including IoT1,2, Cloud Computing (CC)3,CPS4,5, and AI6,7. Initially, DT was seen as an advanced simulation tool.8,9. However, efforts were made to align the model closely with the physical system10,11. These technologies enable a link between the tangible and virtual realms. Leveraging these digital models allows the simulation of novel innovations and treatments within a secure environment12,13. This empowers healthcare professionals with the capability to make well-informed decisions regarding patient care14–16. GE Healthcare created a ”Capacity Command Center” incorporating simulation capabilities and decision support, and this has been implemented at John Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore17. Siemens Healthineers optimized John Mater Private Hospital (MPH) in Dublin using DT technology18. The application area of the DT in healthcare is represented in Fig. 1.

Related work and research problem

The median lifespan has risen, touching into the sixties. There is a worldwide increase in the elderly population. The growing population, linked with heightened requirements for healthcare services, is exerting pressure on the healthcare system and exhausting its resources19–21. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) projections, one in six individuals will be 60 years of age or older by 2030, and by 2050, the proportion will increase to double digits22. This demographic shift poses challenges for the healthcare system in providing prompt and efficient care23,24. Multiple health problems of joint pain, depression, breathing irregularity, strokes, and cataract errors e.t.c., can affect individuals of any age, not just the elderly. Breathing vital can provide the status of one’s health most accurately. According to the authors of25, approximately 5% population of wealthy countries have a condition marked by sluggish breathing - bradypnea. By contrast, the study found that signs of various breathing issues were present in 30% of patients who were in their sixties. Traditional methods of capnography and impedance pneumography for continuous breathing monitoring will possibly become less utilized in the future of healthcare. The future trend leans towards unobtrusive sensing, advanced data processing, and the integration of ML and DL for more intelligent and effective respiratory monitoring. These include precise medication administration, improved patient monitoring, accurate diagnoses, decision support, and cost-effective healthcare, among other significant aspects3.

To effectively analyze the communication link between a transmitter and receiver, leveraging the Channel State Information (CSI) of Wi-Fi sensors is key. CSI captures environmental influences on signal characteristics, enabling the extraction of data like respiration rates. Wireless standards like LTE, Wi-Fi, and WiMAX use Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing (OFDM) to maintain high data rates in frequency-selective channels36. In OFDM, the total spectrum is divided into narrowband orthogonal subcarriers, mitigating channel fading caused by large delay spreads through the Inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFFT). By analyzing CSI, it is possible to extract information about the environment that is encoded in Wi-Fi signals. This can thus be detected by CSI. N. Bao et. al.37 provide a discussion on the phase and amplitude changes toward detection of such micro respiration movements. Mathematically, the signal at the receiver can be stated as a function of the transmitted signal38.

Recently, there has been a surge in studies exploring the potential of antennas for inconspicuously monitoring breathing patterns. Specifically, the Respiratory Rate (RR) is gauged by assessing phase alterations induced by the subject on the transmission signal. Various radar systems have been proposed in literature for vital sign detection, including Continuous-Wave (CW) and Ultra-Wideband (UWB) radars39. Within CW radar technology, investigations have delved into single-tone, Frequency-Modulated Continuous-Wave (FMCW), and Stepped Frequency-Modulated Continuous-Wave (SFCW) approaches for RR monitoring40–44. While these endeavors have demonstrated promising outcomes in terms of accuracy and sensitivity, they have primarily concentrated on validating antennas for sensing only one aspect of breathing. Consequently, they have not explored additional parameters relevant to respiration, such as tidal volume or exhalation water volume. Nonetheless, researchers have explored RR alongside other non-breathing-related parameters such as heart rate and sleep patterns45,46. As Wi-Fi-based sensors are widely available and reasonably priced, their value in detecting patient vital signs is becoming more widely acknowledged. The widespread use of Wi-Fi-based sensors simplifies the implementation of health monitoring systems by reducing the need for specialized equipment and setup costs, while their continuous and unobtrusive operation enables seamless integration into everyday environments. They can efficiently monitor vital signs like heart rate and respiration rate because they can detect minute variations in signal propagation caused by human motions, which adds to their usefulness and affordability in practical applications.

RF based vital sign monitoring, while promising for non-contact healthcare sensing, faces several intrinsic challenges before clinical translation. RF propagation in real environments is dominated by multipath and clutter, which can distort channel measurements and complicate the isolation of true physiological motion signatures. For this, specialized signal processing and array techniques are required to mitigate such effects for reliable vital sign extraction47,48. Motion artifacts from movements and caregiver activity introduce RF interference that can overlap with respiration and heartbeat signatures, reducing robustness in clinical environments. Multi-person scenarios further complicate sensing due to overlapping reflections and RF entanglement. In addition, coexistence with dense hospital wireless infrastructure degrades sensing reliability through interference in Wi-Fi and radar bands49.

Novel contribution

This research describes the pilot single subject design of a novel Digital Twin (DT) model based on unobtrusive RF sensors and AI-powered for continuous multi-vital monitoring, integrating advanced signal processing and decision-support analytics. The proposed system leverages an ESP32 Wi-Fi Channel State Information (CSI) sensor for non-contact respiration rate monitoring and an Ultra-WideBand (UWB) RF sensor (0.8GHz-3GHz) for detecting exhaled hydration. To enhance accuracy, an improved signal processing pipeline is implemented for denoising and precise Breaths Per Minute (BPM) estimation from both experimental and synthetic respiration datasets. Additionally, a novel statistical frequency-domain augmentation technique is introduced to generate larger, high-quality synthetic hydration datasets, addressing data scarcity challenges. The ML and DL models, for supervised and semi-supervised classification, are employed for binary and multi-class decision support, providing real-time physiological insights to healthcare professionals. The experiments are in a lab controlled environment and the proposed model is not clinically generalizable. In this research, the DT will be the subject’s respiration estimation, hydration information, and classification decision support provided by the ML/DL.

As presented in Fig. 2, the proposed system is implemented as a closed-loop, bidirectional DT rather than a one-way sensing and AI pipeline. In the forward direction, unobtrusive RF sensors continuously acquire respiration and exhaled-hydration data, which are processed, augmented, and mapped into a latent physiological state through SAE/AE feature extraction and ML classifiers, forming the evolving digital representation of the patient. In the reverse direction, the DT actively influences the physical monitoring process via a clinician-in-the-loop feedback pathway. AI-derived respiration and hydration classifications, confidence measures, and trend estimates are presented to physician as real-time decision support, enabling the clinician to adapt monitoring intensity, sensor prioritization, alert thresholds, and clinical attention based on the DT state. These actions directly affect subsequent sensing and patient management, thereby modifying the physical system that the DT observes. Simultaneously, newly acquired and augmented data are used to periodically update the latent models and classifiers, allowing the DT to learn patient specific patterns over time. This continuously physical-to-digital and digital-to-physical coupling establishes true bidirectional adaptation and learning, distinguishing the proposed framework from a static AI inference pipeline and realizing a clinically meaningful DT.

Experimental setup

Figure 3 provides a generic block diagram representation of the experimental setup. The experiment was conducted inside a controlled laboratory environment with a single subject consisting of Vector Network Analyzer (VNA), ESP32 Wi-Fi sensors (transmitter and receiver), and UWB antenna. The subject performed three distinct breathing scenarios with Respiratory Rates (RR) set at 12 BPM for low, 20 BPM for moderate, and 28 BPM for high breathing intensity. A metronome was used to guide the subject’s breathing pattern and served as the ground truth for validating RR estimated from Wi-Fi sensor data. For each breathing scenario, the subject maintained the assigned breathing rate for a duration of 2 minutes. During this period, the following data were collected: (1) continuous signal data from Wi-Fi sensors with precise timestamps; (2) 10 scans taken at 10-second intervals using a UWB antenna connected to a vector network analyzer (VNA); and (3) 10 manual recordings of exhaled breath’s Relative Humidity (RH) using a digital psychrometer (Protmex PT6508), measured at specific points during exhalation77.

Block diagram of experimental setup.

An ESP32 Wi-Fi sensor with CSI characteristics and an RF sensor is implemented to acquire the subject’s respiration rate and exhaled hydration respectively. The subject is seated 0.5m perpendicular to the direct Line-Of-Sight (LOS) between the ESP32 transmitter (Tx) and receiver (Rx) in a 3m x 3m room. The transmitter and receiver are positioned 1 meter apart, directly facing each other to ensure a clear LOS. As illustrated in Fig. 4, this lab-controlled setup allows the sensors to detect subtle changes in signal properties, like CSI, which are indicative of the subject’s physiological parameters. The subject is in quasistatic position to replicate the behaviour of an elderly patient sitting in chair, sleeping, stroke patients or being a static position for a period of time.

Respiration data extraction

While Wi-Fi sensing has promising applications, most consumer devices lack native access to CSI data without hardware modification, as Wi-Fi is primarily designed for communication. However, tools like the Intel 5300 and Atheros NICs enable partial or full CSI extraction. In contrast, ESP32 microcontrollers provide direct access to all 52 subcarriers, supporting low-cost, standalone sensing. For this research, two ESP32-DevKitC-VE modules were used—a transmitter and a receiver—utilizing built-in 2.4 GHz omnidirectional antennas for CSI-based Wi-Fi sensing.

In this setup, the chest’s expansion and contraction of the subject during breathing perturbs the Wi-Fi signal propagation environment. These small chest perturbations are detected as changes in the Wi-Fi CSI data78. The respiration data acquired through the Wi-Fi CSI-based non-invasive sensor from the experiments will be utilized to create a multi-vital DT model. Table 2 provides the details of the experiment and the subject. The ESP32 Wi-Fi sensor operates using the IEEE 802.11n standard, which is based on Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM). It captures data across 52 subcarriers, in line with the OFDM principle. For this experiment, the sampling duration is set to 120 seconds (2 minutes), with a rate of 120 packets per second, resulting in a total of 14,400 packets. However, it is important to know that in real-world scenario, there will be packet losses. To monitor patient respiration, we focus on the Channel State Information (CSI), which captures variations in the Wi-Fi signal across these subcarriers. The selected breathing rates were chosen to represent clinically meaningful respiration states. A normal adult resting respiratory rate typically lies between 12 and 16BPM, making 12 BPM a baseline of normal breathing. Rates above this range show abnormal or stressful respiration, so 20BPM and 28BPM were selected to reflect progressively elevated and clinically relevant abnormal conditions. This range allows the proposed signal processing and DT framework to be evaluated across both normal and abnormal respiration states, rather than being tuned to a single narrow operating point.

Breathing exhaled hydration data extraction

The Ultra-wideband (UWB) sensor utilized for detecting breathing exhaled hydration operates at a frequency range of 0.8GHz to 3GHz. The sensor is fabricated with conductive textile material of Shieldex Zell and adaptable substrate material of RS-PRO viscose wool felt to provide flexibility in the placement of the sensor79. This will allow to detection of breathing abnormality through exhaled hydration, while maintaining an unobtrusive approach to capturing the exhaled hydration parameter. Authors of80,81 utilized this sensor for the detection of early-stage Alzheimer disease. The ESP32 Wi-Fi sensor remains effective in detecting respiration even with slight patient movements. The authors detailed work on the suitability of Wi-Fi and Hydration RF sensor for patient vital data collection is presented77.

The core principle of UWB sensing is leveraged for detecting exhaled water vapour by positioning the sensor near the mouth. As a person breathes, the dielectric properties in this region fluctuate. During inhalation, the intake of air and moisture results in lower dielectric properties, while exhalation introduces a burst of humid air, causing a sharp increase. These dynamic changes allow the UWB antenna to capture variations linked to breathing, providing an additional parameter for assessing respiratory quality. The UWB antennas operate over a wide frequency range, enabling high data rates and precise target localization. In this study, their broad bandwidth allowed detection of exhaled water vapour during breathing. Their low-power, unobtrusive nature also makes them suitable for safe medical applications82.

To capture exhaled breath data, a Vector Network Analyzer (VNA) was used in conjunction with a UWB antenna. The experiment was controlled through a host PC, which communicated with the VNA via a GPIB interface to initiate signal generation and record reflection data using the instrument’s built-in software. The VNA employed in this study was the HP 8753, featuring a frequency range of 300 kHz to 3 GHz and a dynamic range of up to 100 dB. RF sensors were connected to the 50-ohm ports of the VNA using SMA cables to ensure signal integrity82. For optimal detection of exhaled breath without interfering with the Wi-Fi sensor system, the UWB antenna was positioned 0.75m in front of the subject, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

Methodology for respiration monitoring

Modified respiration pre-processing methodology

The subject’s respiration rate is one of the two vitals considered for the proposed fused DT model. Authors of75, presented the signal processing methodologies estimating patient’s BPM from raw respiration dataset. Results from these methodology is represented in Figs. 9, 10, 11, 12 and 13. However, the methodology implemented in our case study provided an inaccurate estimation of BPM. It was observed that the subject’s data had underlying random noise acquired by the Wi-Fi sensor during experimentation. The solution was to enhance the methodology by placing a low pass filter (2Hz). Following is a brief explanation of the proposed signal processing methodology:

Removal DC: Subtracting the mean value from the signal is the process of removing the DC component. This constant offset, or DC component, has the potential to skew the analysis. By removing it, the focus can be moved toward the fluctuations in the signal that hold the relevant information.

Moving Average Filter: By averaging every data point with its surrounding points, a moving average filter smoothes the signal. Through the reduction of random noise and fluctuations, the underlying trends in the data become more apparent.

Low Pass Filter: The filter attenuates higher frequency transmissions while allowing signals below 2 Hz to pass through. This cutoff was chosen because all meaningful respiration related variations occur well below 1Hz. For example, breathing rates between 12BPM and 28BPM correspond to approximately 0.20-0.47Hz. In contrast, higher frequency components in WI-Fi CSI and UWB signals are mainly caused by RF noise, packet jitter, multipath effects, and small involuntary movements rather than actual breathing. Using a 2Hz cutoff therefore preserves the actual respiratory signal and its low-order harmonics while effectively removing non-physiological RF disturbances83.

Band Pass Filter: The frequency region of interest, which for respiration rate signals is normally between 0.15Hz and 0.6Hz84,85, is isolated using a bandpass filter. Frequencies below 0.15Hz are mostly associated with slow baseline drift and posture changes, while frequencies above 0.6Hz are dominated by motion artifacts and RF noise. By restricting the signal to the physiologically meaningful band, the filter ensures that the extracted CSI and UWB variations are driven mainly by actual breathing motion, leading to more stable and reliable BPM estimation for the DT model76.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA): A statistical method called PCA is used to minimize the dimensionality of data while retaining the majority of the variation86. The data is converted into a new coordinate system in which the highest variation is captured by the first Principal Component (PC), the second greatest variance is captured by the second PC, and so on. This stage helps to pinpoint the signal’s most important characteristics. In Wi-Fi CSI sensing, individual OFDM subcarriers experience different multipath fading and noise levels and therefore do not contribute equally to respiration induced channel variations. Many subcarriers are highly correlated and dominated by static reflections, while only subset carriers’ strong dynamic information caused by chest motion. The PCA is employed to transform the multivariate subcarrier space into an orthogonal basis ordered by explained variance, enabling redundancy removal and concentration of respiration related dynamics into a small number of components. After DC removal and band-pass filtering in the respiration frequency band, the dominant structured source of variance within the physiological respiration band is periodic breathing motion. Consequently, the first PC (corresponding to the largest eigenvalue) represents the signal with the highest respiration-related variance and the strongest synchronized modulation across subcarriers. Selecting this component therefore maximizes the detectability of the respiration waveform and yields a more stable and accurate dominant spectral peak for subsequent PSD-based breathing-rate estimation. Because respiration-induced CSI variations are spatially correlated across subcarriers, whereas motion artifacts and packet losses tend to be less correlated, PCA naturally suppresses these disturbances by concentrating on respiratory information into the dominant component. Importantly, PCA orders components based on variance magnitude rather than frequency content. Therefore, changes in breathing rate shift the spectral peak location but do not affect the relative strength of respiration-driven fluctuations within the filtered band. Experimental observations confirmed that across all tested breathing rates, the first principal component consistently exhibited the clearest respiration-related periodicity, validating its stable selection.

Methodology for hydration monitoring

Breathing exhaled hydration

In this research, the purpose of this RF sensor is to detect changes in breathing exhaled hydration. Analyzing the exhaled hydration in the breathing levels can provide additional insight into the status and the quality of the respiratory system, including the lungs. The hydration in the breath can influence the composition of exhaled breath, and changes in these levels might be indicative of various physiological processes, including those related to lung function. For example, dehydration could potentially impact the mucous membranes in the respiratory tract and affect the properties of exhaled air88. Monitoring breath for hydration markers could be a non-invasive way to gather information about the respiratory system and, indirectly, the condition of the lungs.

Human breath includes gases such as carbon dioxide, nitrogen, and oxygen in addition to water vapour. The respiratory system, which consists of the lungs and airways, is the source of the moisture in exhaled air. Water vapor, a major component of exhaled breath, significantly influences the interaction with RF signals due to its relatively high dielectric constant, particularly in its pure form. However, the water content in human breath is typically not pure and may contain dissolved ions and other biological compounds, which can introduce conductive properties. As a result, the RF waves emitted from the sensor are affected by both the dielectric and conductive characteristics of the exhaled moisture, leading to signal variations based on hydration and respiratory activity. With the higher concentration of water vapours in exhaled breathing, it can interact more strongly with the RF signals, leading to greater loss of RF signals and vise versa. Scattering parameter (S-parameters) are defined as the input wave that strikes one of the ports89. This proposition is not exhaustive. Reflection and transmission occur with waves, and the plots specifically depict the quality of reflected waves, shedding light on the absorption or loss of RF waves in the environment. In our research, the water content from the exhaled breathing results in more RF waves being lost. Higher loss of the RF waves could indicate that the quality of the breathing is better as there is more denser air, which is rich in hydration, being exhaled out of the human body. This would provide an indication to how well the person’s respiratory system is functioning.

The S-parameters for 12 BPM, 20 BPM, and 28 BPM breathing, representing the exhaled breath hydration effects on the RF UWB sensor, are shown in Fig. 7. The analysis of variations across different breathing rates reveals a significant impact of respiration on RF signal behavior. As the BPM increases from 12 BPM to 28 BPM, the deviation from the free condition becomes more pronounced, particularly in the higher frequency range (above 1 GHz). The red dashed lines in each case indicate stronger fluctuations and deeper dips in , suggesting increased RF wave scattering and absorption effects due to water content in exhaled breath. Higher RF loss in exhaled breath correlates with higher water content in the exhaled air. This behavior has potential applications in non-contact respiration monitoring, biomedical sensing, and body-centric wireless communication systems for analyzing patient hydration levels. The observed trends highlight the feasibility of using RF-based approaches to track physiological changes, reinforcing the potential for real-time health monitoring.

Frequency domain statistical data augmentation

Authors of76 presented a statistical time series data augmentation method for generating larger synthetic respiration datasets of 12BPM to 25BPM. Time series data augmentation methodologies cannot be implemented on frequency domain datasets. Exhaled hydration acquired through the UWB antenna is in the frequency domain with magnitude in Decibels(dB). According to76, AugDT-H is the best novel proposed methodology for generating a larger synthetic respiration time series dataset. However, modifications to AugDT-H are made and presented in this research to allow the generation of larger synthetic exhaled hydration datasets in the frequency domain.

In the novel proposed augmentation methodology of S-parameter data, the original dataset is first divided into two equal halves. For each half, the technique generates new synthetic data points by applying a random perturbation to the existing values. Specifically, for every data entry, a random number uniformly drawn from the interval [0,1] is added to the original value, effectively shifting it slightly while preserving the underlying distribution. This process is repeated independently for both halves of the data, ensuring variability while maintaining the statistical structure of the original dataset. The augmented halves are then concatenated to produce an expanded dataset, which can be used to enhance the robustness and generalization capability of machine learning models during training.

The initial dataset was just a single subject data which was increased to 100 patient data with 13000 data points each. This was achieved with AugDT-F data augmentation methodology. Figure 8. represents the AugDT-F methodology in the frequency domain for the subject’s exhaled hydration data augmentation. To avoid distorting physiologically meaningful spectral features, the AugDT-F method introduces only small, bounded perturbations to real measured spectra instead of adding independent noise components. This approach preserves the dominant respiration-related attenuation patterns and the natural relationships between frequency points, while still increasing variability in a controlled manner. The high cross-correlation values observed between the original and synthetic datasets further support this, confirming that the augmentation maintains the underlying RF spectral structure rather than amplifying noise-dominated components.

This section summarizes how the proposed DT performs when signal processing, data augmentation, and AI are combined. Although many models were evaluated, clear trends emerge. This allows statistical features with a Decision Tree to perform very well in supervised learning, while SAE(32-16-32) with KNN achieves the higher accuracy in the semi-supervised case. Limitation & future work

The proposed DT model demonstrates the feasibility of multi-vital, non-contact physiological monitoring using heterogeneous RF sensors. However, as this is a single subject pilot study, several limitations must be acknowledged before clinical deployment and large-scale translation.

The experimental evaluation is based on single-subject data collected under controlled laboratory conditions. Although this is appropriate for proof-of-concept validation of the sensing, signal processing, and AI pipelines, it does not yet capture inter-patient variability arising from differences in age, body composition, respiratory mechanics, or disease state.

Conclusion

This study presents a pioneering investigation into the development of a first-of-its-kind, multi-vital Digital Twin (DT) model for real-time, non-contact physiological patient monitoring. The system integrates unobtrusive ESP32 Wi-Fi sensors with Channel State Information (CSI) for respiration tracking and Ultra-Wideband (UWB) RF sensors for exhaled hydration assessment. The proposed RF-powered DT framework offers a scalable, cost-effective, and non-invasive alternative to traditional monitoring systems, with strong potential applications in chronic disease management, elder care, and remote patient monitoring.

Through advanced signal processing techniques-including DC removal, smoothing, low-pass and bandpass filtering, and Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The system achieves 100% accurate estimation of Breaths Per Minute (BPM) for target rates of 12,20, and 28 BPM within the accepted inter-observer variability threshold (BPM). To enhance decision-making support, both supervised and semi-supervised AI models were applied for binary and multi-class classification of respiration and hydration data. Using a Stacked Autoencoder (SAE) with a 32-16-32 structure, Random Forest achieved classification accuracies of 88% (binary) and 69% (multi-class) for respiration. For hydration assessment, the RF sensor data required no additional pre-processing, enabling direct classification: Decision Tree reached 89% (binary) and 83% (multi-class) accuracy in supervised learning, while K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) achieved 93% and 89% accuracy in semi-supervised learning.

To overcome the challenge of limited real-world datasets, novel statistical augmentation methods were employed to generate synthetic respiration and hydration data. These synthetic samples were rigorously validated using cross-correlated techniques, including Pearson, Kendall, and Spearman correlation coefficients, confirming their alignment with real signal patterns and supporting robust model training.
