Optimizing human FcRn mouse models to improve pharmacokinetic evaluation of antibody drug candidates
Authors: Gregory Christianson, Zachary M. Howard, Samantha Kenney, Emily Lowell, Nijaguna Bethur, Derry Roopenian, Elena Gonzalo-Gil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe use of animal models that can reliably predict drug performance in human patients is critical to antibody therapeutic development. Along with assessing toxicity and efficacy, determining the pharmacokinetic (PK) properties of therapeutics in Tg32 and Tg276 mice is essential to preclinical characterization. While Tg32 mice have been well established as indispensable in their ability to model the PK properties of antibody therapeutics, their intact immunity leaves them capable of mounting anti-drug antibody responses that interfere with PK interpretation. Here, we demonstrate the negative impact anti-drug responses can have on PK parameters derived from Tg32 mice, and provide strong evidence to support the use of immunodeficient Tg32 SCID mice as an equivalent means to model human PK. In addition, we investigate one possible cause for reduced FcRn function observed in Tg276 mice when compared to Tg32 mice in spite of evidence that their FcRn protein levels are actually higher. We also introduce NSG Tg32 mice and our attempts to block off-target binding of human IgGs to their high-affinity Fc gamma receptors, which failed to recover FcRn function similar to that observed from Tg32 mouse controls, dramatically limiting their utility for PK analysis. Taken together, our results provide a comparison of these preclinical animal models, so they can be used to improve human PK predictions of antibody therapeutic candidates in development.

Introduction

When developing antibody therapeutics, one essential goal is the accurate prediction of their human pharmacokinetic (PK) properties prior to proceeding to clinical trials. Tg32 and Tg276 mouse models (B6.Cg-Fcgrttm1Dcr Tg(FCGRT)32Dcr/DcrJ and B6.Cg-Fcgrttm1Dcr Tg(CAG-FCGRT)276Dcr/DcrJ, respectively) uniquely express human FcRn and have consistently made accurate human PK estimates for antibody therapeutics. Due to their lower cost and reduced ethical concerns compared to using nonhuman primates (NHP), preference for using mice that express Tg32 and Tg276 transgenes is justifiably enabling innovation in this class of therapeutics.1–5

A key aspect of the mouse models used for preclinical PK analysis is their expression of human neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn). FcRn binds pinocytosed Immunoglobulin G (IgG) within acidic endosomes and recycles it back into circulation, thereby protecting it from lysosomal degradation. Due to amino acid sequence differences between species, IgG and FcRn affinities also differ between species, leading to wildtype mouse FcRn recycling human IgG with clearance rates that are incongruent with human data.6–8 Multiple mouse models have been developed to serve the unique requirements for preclinical PK and pharmacodynamic (PD) characterization of FcRn-based therapeutics, including Tg32,9 Tg276,10 Tg32 AlbKO,11 Tg32 SCID,12 Tg32 hFc,13 NSG Tg32, and Tg32 hAlb mice. Tg32 mice feature a human genomic FCGRT transgene driven by the human promoter combined with a targeted knockout of the mouse Fcgrt gene.14 Tg32 mice have an FcRn tissue expression pattern similar to wildtype mice and human,15 more FcRn protein expressed when homozygous (hom) than when hemizygous (hemi) across all tissues,16 and FcRn function that translates well to the clinic.2,3 Tg276 mice also feature a human FCGRT transgene, except its expression is driven by the ubiquitous CAG promoter (cytomegalovirus early enhancer element/chicken beta actin promoter/rabbit beta-globin splice acceptor gene), resulting in expression in all tissues at levels higher than humans,15,17 but with comparatively less FcRn function compared to Tg32 mice 9,10 that still correlates well to human and NHP data.6

Therapeutic antibodies encompass a diverse array of formats, including conventional monospecific monoclonal antibodies (mAbs),18 bispecific antibodies,19 antibody-drug conjugates (ADC),20 and Fc-fusion proteins,21 which all bind therapeutic targets while exhibiting PK profiles needed for efficacy. Advances in molecular engineering have enabled development of IgG Fc mutations such as YTE (M252Y/S254T/T256E) or RL (Q311R/M428L), which extend half-life in circulation,22 or IHH (I253A/H310A/H435A), which reduces half-life.23 These innovations, coupled with the emergence of next-generation antibody formats, are transforming the therapeutic landscape by better matching drug exposures to patient needs. However, accurate preclinical assessment of therapeutic candidates can be complicated by anti-drug antibody (ADA) responses in immunocompetent mouse models, limiting PK interpretation and translation to the clinic. When ADA occurs in human patients, it can frequently be managed to maintain efficacy.24 In preclinical studies, ADA can appear in circulation as early as 4–7 d post exposure and accelerate clearance of therapeutics.12 ADA responses have the potential to be exacerbated by engineered therapeutic constructs as they deviate more from self-protein sequence/structure.25 Tg32 SCID mice (B6.Cg-Fcgrttm1Dcr Prkdcscid Tg(FCGRT)32Dcr/DcrJ), which have the human FCGRT Tg32 transgene and mouse Fcgrt knockout in addition to the Prkdcscid mutation, were introduced in concept10 and study 12,26,27 as a means to remove ADA interference from preclinical PK analysis by preventing mouse ADA responses.

The development of NSG mice (NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ) occurred just as antibody-based cancer therapeutics were emerging, allowing preclinical assessment of these drug candidates in NSG mice engrafted with human immune cells,28 human cancers,29 and both human immune cells and cancers to screen immunotherapeutics.30 NSG mice express unique alleles of Fc gamma receptors, FcγRI and FcγRIV, that have high affinity for human IgG, resulting in its accelerated clearance.31 The off-target binding of therapeutic antibody candidates to FcγRs in NSG mice can diminish efficacy when modeling cancer antibody therapeutics.32 Increasing therapeutic antibody doses in NSG mice can saturate off-target FcγR binding, allowing surplus therapeutic to bind its intended target.33 Attempting to improve the NSG mouse model for antibody PK and efficacy study, we developed NSG Tg32 mice (NOD.Cg-Fcgrttm1Dcr Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl Tg(FCGRT)32Dcr/J), which uniquely express human FcRn. We characterized the function of human FcRn transgene in NSG Tg32 mice by assessing the PK of trastuzumab in combination with polyclonal human IgG (IVIG) to block off-target binding.

Here, we investigated FcRn function differences between Tg32 and Tg276 mice, compared PK data sets between Tg32 and Tg32 SCID mice to support their equivalence, and introduced NSG Tg32 mice for modeling human antibody PK. While the CAG promoter used to drive expression in Tg276 mice is a strong ubiquitous promoter in mice34 and results in quite high FcRn protein expression,15 FcRn function rated in terms of half-life of administered human IgG antibodies is actually lower in Tg276 mice when compared to Tg32 mice. To test the premise that expression of FcRn in tissues not participating in IgG recycling can act as antibody sinks, we investigated the PK of trastuzumab in Tg32xTg276 F1 mice to determine if the hemizygous co-expression of both transgenes in the same mice would cause Tg276 transgene expression to diminish Tg32 transgene function.

Finally, we compared the PK of Tg32 and Tg32 SCID mice to investigate the advantages of using immunocompromised Tg32 SCID mice to avoid ADA, and provide PK data establishing Tg32 SCID mice as equivalent to Tg32 mice for making predictions of human PK performance. The overarching goal of these studies was to gain a better understanding of these preclinical animal models, so they can be used to improve human PK predictions of antibody therapeutic candidates in development.

Results

Tg32 mice model human PK with ADA-dependent variability

To investigate the influence of the homozygous and hemizygous transgene states (also referred to as zygosity differences) in Tg32 and Tg276 mice, we analyzed the PK properties of three US Food and Drug Administration-approved therapeutics displaying a broad range of clearance in humans: belatacept (8.5 d),35 ipilimumab (14.7 d),36 and pembrolizumab (22 d).37 Belatacept is an Fc-fusion protein composed of the extracellular domain of CTLA-4 linked to the Fc region of human IgG1 used for the prophylaxis of organ rejection in adult kidney transplant recipients. Ipilimumab and pembrolizumab are conventional monospecific mAbs. Ipilimumab (IgG1) targets CTLA-4 and is primarily used in the treatment of metastatic melanoma, while pembrolizumab (IgG4) targets PD-1 and is used in the treatment of various cancers, including melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer. Plasma concentrations of each therapeutic were measured longitudinally using a human IgG enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) from Tg32 homozygous (hom), Tg32 hemizygous (hemi), Tg276 hom, Tg276 hemi, and FcRn knockout (FcRn null) mice. The data are represented as the percentage of therapeutic remaining in plasma compared to day 1 to more clearly show elimination trends (Figure 1(A)), while plasma concentration–time data are also provided (Supplementary Figure S1(A)). PK analysis was performed using Phoenix WinNonlin software, where additional parameters besides half-life are included in Supplementary Table S1. The resulting data showed a trend of half-lives with hom mice consistently exceeding half-lives of hemi mice, although the significance of these differences varied. While only pembrolizumab half-life was significantly different between Tg32 hom and hemi mice, Tg276 hom and hemi mice discriminated significant half-life differences for all three therapeutics (Figure 1(B)). This distinction suggests that the different levels of FcRn expressed by hom and hemi Tg276 mice resulted in functional FcRn differences that were better suited to differentiate PK differences. Despite this Tg276 advantage, Tg32 hom clearance rates for these therapeutics correlated exceptionally well (Pearson r = 0.999) to published human rates of clearance (Figure 1(C)). Having established that zygosity of Tg276, and often Tg32, affects FcRn function so that the higher expression in hom mice frequently yields longer half-lives than the lower expression in hemi mice, it begs the question as to why Tg32 mice protect FcRn ligands better than Tg276 mice. The Tg276 transgene is driven by the CAG promoter, which was recently described as the strongest ubiquitous promoter to date.34 This description is corroborated by Li et al. who found that Tg276 mice express higher levels of human FcRn than either humans or Tg32 mice.17 One possible reason Tg276 mice recycle human Immunoglobulin G (hIgG) to a lesser degree than Tg32 mice is that the Tg276 transgene expresses in tissues lacking appropriate IgG recycling pathways. This “inappropriate” tissue expression might cause some fraction of IgG in Tg276 mice to bind to nonfunctional FcRn and elevate IgG clearance compared to Tg32 mice. To resolve if the ubiquitous expression of FcRn is the cause of increased clearance in Tg276 mice, we mated Tg32 hom females with Tg276 hom males to produce F1 offspring that were hemizygous for both Tg32 and Tg276. If ubiquitous Tg276 expression causes increased clearance, then Tg276 expression in F1 mice should also reduce the function of their co-expressed Tg32 transgene. F1 mice were intravenously (IV) injected with trastuzumab at 5 mg/kg along with Tg32 hom, Tg32 hemi, Tg276 hom, and Tg276 hemi mice with the goal of comparing the half-life yielded from F1 mice to the half-lives yielded from the controls. The mice were blood sampled at 2, 4, 7, 9, 11, 14, 22, and 28 d. The data are represented as the percentage of trastuzumab remaining in plasma compared to day 1 to show the elimination phase clearly (Figure 1(D)). Plasma concentration–time data was also graphed (Suplementary Figure S1(B)). Instead of diminishing half-life compared to Tg32 hom mice, the Tg32 hemi and Tg276 hemi transgene functions in F1 mice yielded a trastuzumab half-life (11.08 ± 2.54 d) that was not significantly different from the half-life yielded from Tg32 hom mice (8.94 ± 0.90 d). This strongly suggests that ubiquitous expression of FcRn is not the cause for Tg276 mice having lower FcRn function despite having higher expression than Tg32 mice. While Tg32 and Tg276 mice showed consistent differences for trastuzumab half-life with Tg32 hom longer than Tg32 hemi and Tg276 hom longer than Tg276 hemi, these differences were not significant. It is worth noting that between the two different transgenes, Tg32 hom half-life for trastuzumab was significantly longer (p < 0.05) than the half-life yielded from Tg276 hom mice (3.60 ± 0.09 d) by one way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (Figure 1(E)).

When protein-based therapeutics are PK characterized in animal models with a full complement of immunity, it is possible that some animals will develop immune responses against the therapeutics in the form of ADA. When ADA arises, the immune complexes formed are rapidly cleared by phagocytic mononuclear cells in the liver and spleen, in addition to the clearance that occurs when antibodies fail to bind FcRn within the recycling pathway. ADA augmented clearance greatly complicates PK analysis of the affected therapeutics. ADA interference of PK interpretation was exemplified when conventional IgG monospecific antibodies (mAb-1 and mAb-2) were IV injected into immunocompetent Tg32 mice at either 3 or 10 mg/kg (Figure 2(A–D)). Plasma concentrations of mAb-1 were more rapidly cleared due to ADA for 6 out of 8 mice when dosed at both 3 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg (Figure 2(A,B), respectively). ADA response onset began as early as 7 d at the lower dose, and later at 21 d at the higher dose (Figure 2(E,F), respectively). This sample size was too small to distinguish whether tolerance was induced at the higher dose for mAb-1, or the diminished ADA was a matter of chance. mAb-2 yielded plasma concentrations with less ADA, though both 3 and 10 mg/kg doses resulted in 1 out of 4 mice displaying late onset of ADA at 21–28 d (Figure 2(C,D), respectively). The weak indications of ADA in the plasma concentration–time plots for mAb-2 were confirmed by the drug-specific ADA activity found at the coincident sample timepoints (Figure 2(G,H)). Plasma concentration averages from all mice showed that, due to its immunogenicity, mAb-1 yielded nonlinear clearance profiles compared to mAb-2 (Figure 2(I)). The variable clearance and half-lives yielded from the reduced number of ADA-free mice forced drug development decisions for mAb-1 to be based on data from only 2 of 8 mice (Figure 2(J), Supplemental Figure S1(C)). Tg32 SCID mice recapitulate Tg32 pharmacokinetics

Development of ADA significantly alters PK profiles in both Tg32 and Tg276 mouse models (regardless of zygosity), and elimination of the adaptive immune system was a means to prevent both ADA formation and its impact on PK assessment. To achieve this goal, we developed the Tg32 SCID mouse model, which both preserves homozygosity of the human Tg(FCGRT)32Dcr transgene to facilitate allometric scaling and carries the spontaneous scid (severe combined immune deficiency) mutation of the Prkdc gene. SCID mice are defective in the recombinase responsible for the assembly of antigen receptor genes, which causes an absence of mature T and B cells and prevents antibody responses such as ADA. T and B cell deficiency is not expected to affect FcRn function since the Tg32 transgene is not expressed by these cells.15 To evaluate PK outcomes between Tg32 and Tg32 SCID mice, either trastuzumab (Herceptin®, 10 mg/kg) or etanercept (Enbrel®, 20 mg/kg) were IV injected and serial blood samples were periodically collected throughout 28 or 30 d, respectively. Trastuzumab is a human IgG1 antibody used as a therapeutic to bind HER2 receptors on cancerous cells to slow their replication, while etanercept is an Fc-fusion protein approved to treat various autoimmune diseases by inhibition of the signaling from tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). Both trastuzumab and etanercept were cleared at nearly identical rates in Tg32 or Tg32 SCID mice (Figure 3(A,C)), yielding trastuzumab half-lives of 18.5 d (Tg32) and 19.6 d (Tg32 SCID) and etanercept half-lives of 3.8 d (Tg32) and 4.2 d (Tg32 SCID) with no significant differences (Figure 3(B,D)). The PK uniformity between Tg32 and Tg32 SCID mice for these diverse therapeutics strongly suggests that any PK data yielded from either of these two human FcRn mouse stocks can be considered equivalent. Other derived PK parameters are highly similar between the two mouse models as well (Supplementary Table S1). Taking a step further, it is reasonable to use the allometric scaling exponents derived from Tg32 mouse data2,3 to scale PK parameters yielded from Tg32 SCID mice. Allometric scaling was applied to Tg32 and Tg32 SCID half-lives, and the predicted human values were compared to human data proportionally (Table 1). Both the Tg32 and Tg32 SCID predicted human half-lives for trastuzumab were accurate, having ratios of 0.93 and 0.99, respectively. To pilot use of Tg32 SCID mice to model human PK using allometric scaling, three bispecific antibodies were characterized. Mosunetuzumab (Lunsumio®, IgG1), glofitamab (COLUMVI®, IgG1), and talquetamab (Talvey®, IgG4) are used to treat lymphomas and myelomas by engaging CD3+ T cells and either CD20 (mosunetuzumab, glofitamab) or GPRC5D (talquetamab) on cancer cells to effect cancer elimination. Mosunetuzumab (1 mg/kg), glofitamab (2 mg/kg), or talquetamab (1 mg/kg) were IV dosed into Tg32 SCID male mice, and mice were serially blood sampled over 21 d. The percentage of bispecific antibody remaining in plasma compared to day 1 was used to more clearly compare elimination trends (Figure 3(E)), while plasma concentration–time data are also provided (Supplementary Figure S1(D)). The half-lives ranged from mosunetuzumab at 8.9 d to talquetamab at 6.1 d, with glofitamab in the middle at 6.7 d (Figure 3(F)). Despite its novel 2:1 structure with bivalency for CD20 and monovalency for CD3, glofitamab managed to persist in circulation slightly better than talquetamab (p < 0.05) with its more standard IgG1 structure. Tg32 SCID clearance rates for these bispecifics were found to be very highly correlated (Pearson r = 0.939) to human clearance, providing strong support for use of Tg32 SCID mice in place of Tg32 mice to make predictions of therapeutic antibody PK performance in the clinic in the absence of ADA concerns (Figure 3(G)).

Blocking off-target binding in NSG Tg32 mice

To assess antibody recycling in the newly established NSG Tg32 mouse model, both NSG Tg32 and Tg32 mice were IV dosed with trastuzumab (10 mg/kg, 0 d, n = 5) with or without being preloaded with intravenous IgG (IVIG, −1 d, 10 mg/kg). Mice were serially blood sampled, and trastuzumab plasma concentrations quantified by ELISA. The percent trastuzumab remaining from 1 d showed better persistence in NSG Tg32 mice that was IVIG preloaded compared to no preload due to IVIG binding/blocking off-target FcγR1. This allowed a larger portion of trastuzumab dosed 1 d later to bypass FcγR1 and be FcRn recycled. In stark contrast, trastuzumab persisted better in Tg32 mice that were not preloaded compared to preloaded Tg32 (Figure 4(A)). Likely, IgG recycling was impeded in IVIG preloaded Tg32 mice due to FcRn saturation. The impact on half-life from FcRn saturation was evident between Tg32 mice with (9.9 d) and without IVIG preload (16.8 d), but was assumed to have occurred in NSG Tg32 treatment groups as well. While trastuzumab half-lives from NSG Tg32 without IVIG preload were shorter (4.1 d) than when preloaded (6.1 d), both these values were greatly reduced compared to the half-lives yielded from the well-established Tg32 mouse model that expressed the same Tg (FCGRT)32Dcr transgene (Figure 4(B)). This strongly suggested that, besides off-target binding to FcγR1, FcRn saturation may have also contributed to reduced FcRn recycling of trastuzumab in NSG Tg32 mice. The values represent the mean ± SD. Unpaired two-tailed t-tests were used for statistical analysis, where ns = not significant.

While trastuzumab persistence was improved in IVIG preloaded NSG Tg32 mice, FcγR1 turnover and de novo synthesis could have also provided a renewable supply of unbound FcγR1 over the period of the study, fueling augmented clearance of trastuzumab. To investigate whether improved methods of dosing might additionally improve trastuzumab PK, multiple modes of dosing were used in both NSG mice expressing mouse FcRn and NSG Tg32 mice expressing human FcRn. All NSG Tg32 and NSG treatment groups were dosed with trastuzumab at 0 d (10 mg/kg). The first NSG Tg32 and NSG treatment groups received no other doses (n = 5). The second treatment groups were preloaded one time with IVIG (10 mg/kg; −1 d; n = 5). The third treatment groups were preloaded 1-time with IVIG (10 mg/kg; −1 d; n = 5) and weekly reloaded with trastuzumab (10 mg/kg; 7, 14, 21 d; n = 5). The fourth treatment groups were preloaded one time with IVIG (10 mg/kg; −1 d; n = 5) and weekly reloaded with IVIG (10 mg/kg; −1, 6, 13, 20 d) and trastuzumab (10 mg/kg; 7, 14, 21 d; n = 5). A simple preload of IVIG 1 d prior to trastuzumab dosing significantly lengthened trastuzumab half-life from 3.6 d to 5.6 d in NSG Tg32 mice (Figure 4(C)), and from 5.7 d to 7.2 d in NSG mice (Figure 4(E)). To best assess advantages of multiple dosing, data from multi-dose treatment groups were analyzed by comparing PK data yielded from the first week (when FcγR1 might have been more unbound with IVIG/trastuzumab) and the fourth week (when FcγR1 was more likely to be saturated with IVIG/trastuzumab). NSG Tg32 mice dosed with IVIG (−1 d) and trastuzumab (0, 7, 14, 21 d) showed a 4.0-d half-life early (week 1) and a 9.4-d half-life later (week 4), while NSG Tg32 mice dosed with IVIG (−1, 6, 13, 20 d) and trastuzumab (0, 7, 14, 21 d) showed a similar 4.7-d half-life early (week1) and a 9.1-d half-life later (week 4). The 4–5 d half-life improvement for trastuzumab yielded from both multi-dose treatment modes indicated that repeat dosing was a means to significantly improve trastuzumab PK. Though trastuzumab half-life improved to 9 for multi-dosed NSG Tg32 mice, this was still short of the 16.8 d half-life yielded from Tg32 mice on the B6 background that were not IVIG preloaded. This half-life difference was likely due to FcRn saturation, an unavoidable outcome of the multi-dose regimen, which limited the capacity of FcRn to recycle trastuzumab within week 4 of this study. The data are represented as the percentage of trastuzumab remaining in plasma compared to day 1 to facilitate comparison of elimination trends (Figure 4(D,F)). Steady states were not achieved within the duration of multi-dose studies due to the trastuzumab half-lives exceeding the period of dosing.

Discussion

FcRn functionality is strongly influenced by transgene zygosity where homozygous transgene expression generally leads to better protection of IgG compared to hemizygous expression.9,10 It has been observed that higher levels of FcRn expression that resulted from transgene homozygosity increased membrane receptor density, augmented FcRn capacity to bind and recycle IgG, and extend antibody half-life. The inverse relationship between a saturable IgG recycling receptor and IgG half-life was first proposed more than 60 y ago by Brambell,40 and again several years later by Waldmann.41 This principle was challenged by observations that Tg276 mice, despite expressing more FcRn via their strong CAG promoter, consistently exhibited greater clearance rates than Tg32 mice, whose FcRn transgene was driven by the human promoter.10 One possible explanation for this discrepancy was the ubiquitous expression of FcRn in Tg276 mice, which included tissues that do not contribute to IgG recycling.15,17 In this context, could FcRn expressed on nonfunctional tissues bind IgG without contributing to its return to circulation and counteract FcRn recycling in functional tissues? If true, this would result in a net loss of IgG and accelerated clearance.

When trastuzumab was administered to Tg32 × Tg276 F1 (hemi, hemi) mice it yielded a half-life that was not significantly different from the half-life yielded from Tg32 hom mice, and dispelled the concept that ubiquitous expression of FcRn from the Tg(CAG-FCGRT)276Dcr transgene in non-recycling tissues would act as an IgG sink and diminish overall FcRn function. The control mouse stocks included in this same study generally recapitulated the dogma that higher FcRn expression led to higher function, when we showed that Tg32 mice protected IgG significantly better than Tg276 mice at the hom level, but lacked significance at the hemi level for each transgene. The observation that Tg276 mice exhibited faster IgG clearance than Tg32 mice despite literature showing higher FcRn expression in Tg276 mice 15 suggests that the previous expression data was flawed or that IgG recycling was not dependent on FcRn expression alone, and possibly also depended on the sufficient expression/function of downstream proteins in the IgG recycling pathway. The FCGRT gene promoter binds regulatory transcription factors (Sp1, Sp2, Sp3, c-Fos, c-Jun, YY1, and C/EBPβ or C/EBP δ) to enable tissue-specific expression of FcRn in humans 42 and Tg32 mice. But was the replacement of this native promoter with the CAG promoter in Tg276 mice the cause of their diminished FcRn function? If so, the promoter expressed from the Tg32 transgene in F1 mice might have acted as both a cis-acting factor to induce tissue-specific expression from the Tg32 transgene and as a trans-acting factor to upregulate tissue-specific expression from the Tg276 transgene. Or, as a trans-acting factor, the promoter might have also upregulated expression of downstream proteins in the IgG recycling pathway to augment the function of FcRn receptors expressed from both transgenes in F1 mice. However, we are not aware of evidence in the literature that promoters trans-activate distant genes. This leaves the question of why Tg32 mice protect IgG better than Tg276 mice unresolved. Future studies might investigate whether the CAG promoter in Tg276 mice causes expression in cellular compartments to be unable to properly fold or localize FcRn, reducing its functional capacity. Possibly confocal microscopy of Tg32 and Tg276 tissues could be used to cross compare functional binding of properly folded FcRn (which binds labeled mouse anti-FcRn and labeled human IgG at pH 6) verses misfolded FcRn (which binds labeled mouse anti-FcRn only and leaves labeled human IgG unbound at pH 6). At the same time, FcRn trafficking studies within FcRn-expressing monocytes from Tg276 and Tg32 mice might be able to tease out whether Tg276 FcRn trafficking was bottle-necked somewhere within the endosomal recycling pathway, indicating that FcRn from the Tg276 transgene was interacting less efficiently with the mouse endosomal pathway and causing FcRn recycling to be reduced when compared to recycling in Tg32 monocytes.

As therapeutic antibodies and their derivatives are developed across a wide range of disease indications, there is an increased need for accurate, cost-effective preclinical modeling of PK to support the accelerated pace of these drug development programs. While the Tg32 mouse model has been validated repeatedly to predict human PK,1–4 the paucity of Tg32 SCID mouse data in the literature suggests that these mice continue to be under-utilized. Valente et al. encouraged the use of Tg32 mice in place of NHP to lower costs from ADA impacted data, but use of Tg32 SCID mice could be an even better solution. Tg32 SCID mice were specifically developed to augment the Tg32 model by the inclusion of immunodeficiency to allow preclinical characterization in the absence of ADA. ADA responses lead to immune complexes that clear test articles rapidly from circulation and can obscure the true PK behavior of an antibody or Fc-containing therapeutic.43–47 Complicating matters further, ADA responses are extremely variable from mouse to mouse. Our findings underscore the importance of evaluating both plasma concentration–time profiles and ADA activity on a per-animal basis as a means to avoid generating PK data skewed by ADA. In a recent JAX Preclinical Services (JAX PCS) PK characterization study, Tg32 mice dosed with the human IgG1 reference antibody HuLys11 (2 mg/kg, IV) showed ADA responses from 11 of 60 mice (18%, data not shown). Retrospective analysis of several hundred antibody therapeutic candidates that were PK characterized in Tg32 mice by JAX PCS showed that ADA responder frequencies ranged from approximately 0–50% of mice for each study (data not shown). When the frequency of animals with ADA was low in the retrospective studies, PK data compromised by ADA somewhat limited the statistical analysis needed to identify lead candidates, but when the ADA frequency was high preclinical PK characterization was not possible. In our PK analysis of etanercept and trastuzumab, we provided strong evidence that the use of Tg32 SCID mice in place of Tg32 mice when avoiding ADA is desired, and that allometric scaling exponents derived from Tg32 mice can be used for scaling Tg32 SCID PK data to make human PK predictions. Together, the Tg32 and Tg32 SCID models form a versatile and complementary platform for evaluating Fc-containing biologics. When Tg32 mice are used to predict human PK performance, one must take into account that ADA responses observed in these mice in no way predict responses that might occur in the clinic.48

Use of the Tg32 SCID mouse model enables preclinical PK evaluation across a broad range of disease indications, including oncology, infectious diseases, metabolic diseases, autoimmune and inflammatory disorders, and organ transplantation. Their use can also expand the range of therapy formats that can be reliably PK evaluated to include multispecific antibodies (such as the T-cell engagers analyzed here), Fc fusion proteins (such as the belatacept analyzed here in Tg32 hom and hemi mice, yielding 3 out of 8 mice with ADA), ADCs, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as the pembrolizumab analyzed here, yielding 4 out of 8 Tg32 hemi mice with ADA), all of which are prone to ADA responses.

Despite human FcRn expression, NSG Tg32 mice exhibited significantly faster clearance of trastuzumab compared to Tg32 mice. This accelerated clearance was only partially corrected by IVIG preloading, suggesting that the unique FcγR1 allele with its very high affinity for human IgG exerted a counteracting influence on antibody disposition. IVIG preloading modestly increased trastuzumab half-life in NSG Tg32 mice, likely by transient saturation of FcγRs and reduced clearance. Note that IVIG preloading in Tg32 mice caused partial saturation of FcRn and reduced the half-life of trastuzumab. Tg32 SCID mice were not preloaded with IVIG to assess FcRn saturation as part of this study, but it is reasonable to assume that FcRn function within Tg32 SCID mice would respond similarly in how their FcRn saturated with IgG since no significant differences were measured in half-life for trastuzumab or etanercept, and the mouse IgG circulating in Tg32 mice has no affinity for human FcRn, and so has no relevance for FcRn saturation. While repeated trastuzumab dosing in NSG Tg32 mice extended the post-last dose half-life, administering IVIG prior to each dose did not confer additional benefit. This suggested that trastuzumab engagement of FcγR alone in multi-dosed NSG mice may have reached a saturation threshold and limited the utility of preloading IVIG as an approach to normalize PK to levels achieved by Tg32 mice. Additionally, when FcγRs undergo ligand-induced ubiquitination and degradation, they are replaced with newly synthesized unbound FcγRs and off-target clearance capacity is maintained.49,50 Furthermore, trastuzumab and etanercept half-lives were equivalent between Tg32 and Tg32 SCID models, indicating that enhanced clearance in NSG Tg32 mice is not attributable to their lymphocyte deficiency. The similarity in the trastuzumab PK profiles from both NSG Tg32 and NSG mice was consistent with the shared FcγR1 allele, rather than the species’ origin of their FcRn, showing FcγR1 as the primary driver of altered trastuzumab clearance in these mice. It was notable that the trastuzumab half-life was longer in NSG mice expressing mouse FcRn than the half-life yielded from NSG Tg32 mice, consistent with the generalized higher affinity observed between mouse FcRn and humanized antibodies.7,51 These findings underscore that the NSG Tg32 mouse model is greatly limited in its current state. While possibly being useful for efficacy studies that would benefit from its ability to both express human FcRn and be repopulated with human immune cells when antibody therapeutics are being characterized, NSG Tg32 mice will not provide meaningful human PK predictions despite their human FcRn Tg32 expression. Work is in progress at JAX to replace the presumably culpable Fcgr1 mouse gene in NSG mice with the human FCGR1A homolog as a means to eliminate the FcγR1 antibody sink problems and allow the human FcRn expressed in NSG Tg32 mice to protect human IgG more similar to the Tg32 mouse model.

The unique characteristics of the human FcRn mouse models we have described here, along with our in-house experience, suggest that guidance for their use may be beneficial. These guidelines should be considered as general rules and should be tailored as needed for each test article as its FcRn affinity is optimized to benefit efficacy. Tg32 mice are best used to characterize therapeutic candidates that are expected to have low to average FcRn affinity and have guesstimated half-life values from a few days to 20 d in humans. When characterized in Tg32 mice, test articles engineered to have extended half-life (20 d or longer) may yield less accurate estimates of human PK unless study periods are extended to 2 or 3 times the expected half-life. If there is reason to believe test articles might be immunogenic, if related molecules have a history of being immunogenic, or the avoidance of potential ADA responses is highly valued, then Tg32 SCID mice should be used in place of Tg32. If test articles are expected to have higher than average or very high affinity for FcRn and expected to yield extended half-life of 20–30 d or more in humans, then Tg276 hemi mice should be used. Use of Tg276 hemi mice to characterize the PK of especially long-lived test articles will greatly shorten the period of study and has greater potential to accurately estimate PK performance in humans. The same considerations for when to use Tg32 SCID mice in place of Tg32 mice also apply for using Tg276 hemi SCID mice in place of Tg276 hemi mice, such as when test articles are expected to be immunogenic, related molecules are immunogenic, or avoidance of ADA is highly valued. Studies that will allow Tg276 hemi or Tg276 hemi SCID PK data to be scaled to accurately predict human PK are being actively pursued.

Materials and methods

Mouse models and genetic backgrounds

A range of genetically engineered mouse models was used to evaluate the PK of mAbs and Fc-containing therapeutics. All animal studies were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health’s Office of Laboratory Animal Welfare guidelines and were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at The Jackson Laboratory. All the mice were maintained under standard conditions with a 12/12 h (on: 6 am, off: 6 pm) light–dark cycle. Animals were housed in individually and positively ventilated polysulfonate cages with HEPA filtered air at a density of 3–4 mice per cage. Filtered tap water, acidified to a pH of 2.5 to 3.0, and normal rodent chow were provided ad libitum. Animals were checked weekly for welfare and mice presenting more than 15% of maximum body weight loss were considered at humane endpoint and euthanized.

The B6.Cg-Fcgrttm1DcrTg(FCGRT)32Dcr/DcrJ (JAX stock #014565), also known as Tg32, carries a targeted deletion of the endogenous mouse Fcgrt gene and a transgene expressing the human FCGRT gene under the control of its native human promoter (B6.Cg-Fcgrttm1Dcr Tg(FCGRT)32Dcr/DcrJ). This model was used in both hemizygous (Tg32 hemi) and homozygous (Tg32 hom) states to assess zygosity-dependent FcRn function. For experiments not specifying transgene zygosity, homozygous mice were utilized. The B6.Cg-Fcgrttm1Dcr Tg(CAG-FCGRT)276Dcr/DcrJ (JAX stock #004919), also known as Tg276, expresses the human FCGRT gene under the control of the strong, ubiquitous CAG promoter and also lacks the mouse Fcgrt gene (B6.Cg-Fcgrttm1Dcr Tg(CAG-FCGRT)276Dcr/DcrJ). Tg276 mice were used in both hemizygous and homozygous forms to evaluate promoter-driven differences in FcRn expression and function. FcRn− mice (B6.129X1-Fcgrttm1Dcr/DcrJ; JAX stock# 003982) are homozygous for a targeted deletion of the Fcgrt gene and served as negative controls for FcRn-mediated IgG recycling (B6.129X1-Fcgrttm1Dcr/DcrJ). F1 hybrid mice were generated by crossing Tg32 homozygous females with Tg276 homozygous males; these offspring were hemizygous for both transgenes and were used to assess potential synergistic effects of dual FcRn transgene expression. The B6.Cg-Fcgrttm1Dcr Prkdcscid Tg(FCGRT)32Dcr/DcrJ, also known as Tg32 SCID model (JAX stock# 018441) is an immunodeficient variant of Tg32 that retains homozygosity for the human FCGRT transgene and carries the Prkdcscid mutation (B6.Cg-Fcgrttm1Dcr Prkdcscid Tg(FCGRT)32Dcr/DcrJ), allowing for PK evaluation in the absence of adaptive immune responses. NSG Tg32 mice were generated by backcrossing Tg32 mice onto the NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) background. These mice (JAX stock# 028615) are homozygous for the human FCGRT transgene and immunodeficient, but retain NSG-specific FcγRI and FcγRIV alleles, which are known to influence antibody clearance. The parental NSG strain (JAX stock# 005557) lacks mature T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells, and is commonly used for human immune system engraftment and xenograft studies. All animals were genotyped to confirm the presence and zygosity of relevant transgenes and mutations, and age- and sex-matched animals were used for each experimental group to ensure consistency across studies.

Test article formulation and intravenous tail vein administration

All biologics formulated in this study–including pharmaceutical-grade belatacept (10 mg/kg), ipilimumab (10 mg/kg), pembrolizumab (10 mg/kg), trastuzumab (5 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg), etanercept (20 mg/kg), laboratory-grade glofitamab (MedChemExpress; 2 mg/kg), mosunetuzumab (MedChemExpress; 1 mg/kg), talquetamab (MedChemExpress; 1 mg/kg), conventional IgG monospecific antibody mAb-1 (proprietary source; 3 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg), and mAb-2 (proprietary source; 3 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg)–—were diluted prior to IV administration. IV injections were performed by a trained technician under an approved IACUC protocol. Preloading was performed subcutaneously using pharmaceutical grade polyclonal human IgG at 10 mg/kg (Privigen). Mice were warmed using a heating lamp in a HEPA-filtered vivarium space, then placed in a restrainer for injection via the lateral tail vein using a 27 G needle (BD; 305109) and a 1 mL slip-tip syringe (BD; 309659) at a volume of either 5 mL/kg or 10 mL/kg. Following injection, animals were monitored briefly to ensure no adverse effects before being returned to their housing.

Retro-orbital bleeding and plasma processing

Retro-orbital blood collection was performed by a trained technician under an approved IACUC protocol. Proparacaine hydrochloride (Sandoz; AK2D15DS-2), a topical anesthetic solution, was applied to the eye designated for blood collection and allowed to take effect for approximately 5 min. A 25 µL capillary tube was then inserted into the medial canthus to collect blood, which was immediately mixed with 1 µL of 250 mM K3-EDTA (Sigma; 03690). In accordance with animal welfare guidelines, no more than 1% of the total blood volume was collected within any 2-week period. Samples were placed on ice immediately after collection to prevent hemolysis. Following collection, blood samples were centrifuged at 14,000 × g at 4°C to separate plasma from cellular components. The resulting plasma was diluted 1:10 in 50% glycerol and stored at −20°C until further analysis.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Human IgG ELISA

An hIgG ELISA was performed using high-binding 96-well microplates (Costar; 9018) coated with 100 µL per well of mouse anti-human IgG (Mabtech; MT145) capture antibody diluted 1:250 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). The plates were sealed and incubated overnight at 4°C. On the following day, plates were washed three times with 300 µL per well of ELISA wash buffer (PBS +0.05% Tween-20) and blocked with 250 µL per well of diluent (wash buffer containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA)) for 1 h at room temperature while dilutions of mouse plasma samples and test articles (standard curves) were prepared. After 1 h, blocking buffer (diluent) was removed from the plates and 100 µL of diluted plasma samples and test articles were transferred to the assay plates, which were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature. At the completion of this incubation period, the plates were washed three times with 300 µL per well of ELISA wash buffer and 100 µL per well of alkaline phosphatase (AP)-conjugated mouse anti-human IgG (Mabtech; MT78) detection antibody diluted 1:1000 in diluent was added to the plates to incubate for 1 h at room temperature. Following incubation, plates were washed three times with 300 µL per well of ELISA wash buffer, then 100 µL per well of 1 mg/mL p-nitorophenyl phosphate substrate buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific; 34047, 34064) was added to each well. The plates were protected from light and incubated at room temperature until optimal color development was reached. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a SpectraMax i3x spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices).

Anti-drug antibody ELISA

An ADA ELISA was performed using high-binding 96-well microplates that were coated with 100 µL per well of test articles used in the study that were diluted to 5 µg/mL in PBS. The plates were sealed and incubated overnight at 4°C. On the following day, the plates were washed three times with 300 µL per well of ELISA wash buffer (PBS +0.05% Tween-20) and blocked with 250 µL per well of diluent (wash buffer containing 1% BSA) for 1 h at room temperature, while dilutions of mouse plasma samples and naïve mouse plasma samples were prepared. Each test article had two assay plates prepared: one experimental plate and one control plate using naïve Tg32 plasma to account for background binding. Following the incubation, blocking buffer (diluent) was removed from the plates and 100 µL of the diluted plasma samples were transferred to the assay plates which were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After 1 h, the plates were washed three times with 300 µL per well of ELISA wash buffer and 100 µL per well of goat anti-mouse kappa-AP (Southern Biotech; 1050-04) diluted 1:1000 in diluent was added to each well to incubate for 1 h at room temperature. The plates were once more washed three times with 300 µL per well of ELISA wash buffer and 100 µL of 1 mg/mL p-nitorophenyl phosphate substrate buffer was added to each well. The plates were protected from light and incubated at room temperature until optimal color development was reached. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a SpectraMax i3x spectrophotometer. For ADA normalization, negative absorbance values were observed in samples lacking ADA due to background subtraction from the control plate.

Trastuzumab anti-Idiotype ELISA

A trastuzumab anti-idiotype ELISA was performed using high-binding 96-well microplates that were coated with 100 µL per well of mouse anti-Herceptin (BioRad; HCA168G, clone AbD18018) diluted 1:2000 in 1X PBS. The plates were sealed and incubated overnight at 4°C. On the following day, the plates were washed three times with 300 µL per well of ELISA wash buffer (PBS +0.05% Tween-20) and blocked with 250 µL per well of diluent (wash buffer containing 1% BSA). The plates were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature, while mouse plasma samples and trastuzumab dilutions (standard curves) were prepared. After 1 h, the blocking buffer (diluent) was removed from the plates and 100 µL of the diluted plasma samples and trastuzumab was transferred to the assay plates. The plates were then incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Following this incubation, the plates were washed three times with 300 µL per well of ELISA wash buffer and 100 µL per well of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated mouse anti-human FC (BioRad; MCA647P) diluted 1:500 in diluent was added to each well. After the addition of this solution, the plates were incubated for 1 h at 37°C before once more being washed three times with 300 µL per well of ELISA wash buffer. Following the final wash, 100 µL of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (ThermoFisher Scientific; 34028) was added to each well and plates were protected from light and developed for 30 min. Development was stopped after 30 min by adding 50 µL per well of ELISA stop solution (Invitrogen; SS04). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax i3x spectrophotometer.

Human CD3d/CD3e capture ELISA

A human CD3d/CD3e capture ELISA was performed using 96-well streptavidin-coated plates (ACRO Biosystems; 50-201-9443). Human CD3d/CD3e-biotin (SinoBiological; CT038-H2508H-B) was added at 100 µL per well at a concentration of 50 ng/well as the capture antibody. The plates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature and then washed three times with 300 µL per well of ELISA wash buffer (PBS +0.05% Tween-20). Following this wash, the plates were blocked using 250 µL of diluent (wash buffer containing 1% BSA) per well and incubated for 1 h at room temperature, while dilutions of the mouse plasma samples and test articles (standard curves) were prepared. At the completion of the incubation, blocking buffer (diluent) was removed from the plates and 100 µL of diluted plasma samples and test articles were transferred to the assay plates. The plates were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature and washed three times with 300 µL per well of ELISA wash buffer afterward. Anti-human IgG (H+L) HRP conjugate (Promega; W4031) was diluted 1:10000 and 100 µL per well was added to the plates to incubate for 1 h at room temperature. The plates were then washed three times with 300 µL per well of ELISA wash buffer, and 100 µL of TMB substrate was added to each well for development. The development was stopped by adding 50 µL of ELISA stop solution after optimal color development was reached. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax i3x spectrophotometer.

Pharmacokinetic modeling (Phoenix-WinNonlin) and allometric scaling

PK parameters were calculated using non-compartmental analysis (NCA) in Phoenix WinNonlin (Certara, version 8.5.4). Plasma concentrations of mAbs were measured longitudinally via ELISA at predefined timepoints post-dose and entered into the software as µg/mL. Bleed times were recorded in hours relative to IV bolus administration, and dosage (mg/kg) was included to calculate clearance (CL), defined as dose divided by the area under the concentration–time curve (AUC). NCA was performed using the log-linear trapezoidal method, with the terminal elimination phase manually defined from Day 1 to the last quantifiable timepoint, supported by regression diagnostics (adjusted R2 > 0.9). Key PK parameters included terminal half-life (t1/2), CL, Cmax, and volume of distribution (Vd). Concentration values with coefficients of variation (CV) >20%, or outside the quantification limits (LLOQ/ULOQ), were excluded from analysis. Allometric scaling was utilized to predict human PK parameters, including CL using the following equation4: CLpredhuman=CLobsanimal×BMhumanXBManimal

Predicted human CL (CLpred(human)) was estimated using the observed CL in animals (CLobs(animal)), adjusted for body mass differences (BManimal). Human body mass (BMhuman) was assumed to be 75 kg. An allometric scaling exponent(X) of 0.90 was applied to CL to account for interspecies physiological differences. Predicted human half-lives were determined by applying a scaling factor of 0.63.3

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.3.1. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two independent groups, a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons involving two independent variables, a two-way ANOVA was conducted, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons post-hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and significance levels were indicated as ns (not significant) or by thresholds (e.g., p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001).
