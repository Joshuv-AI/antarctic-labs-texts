First-week absolute lymphocyte count dynamics and 28-day mortality in older adults with sepsis
Authors: Esra Yakışık Aktekin, Behiye Deniz Kosovalı, Şerife Gökbulut Bektaş, Deniz Erdem, Firdevs Tuğba Bozkurt Biçer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis is a syndrome in which immune recovery is impaired and the clinical course becomes more severe, particularly in older patients. Although lymphopenia is an easily monitored indicator of sepsis-associated immune dysfunction, the clinical significance of daily lymphocyte changes during the first week in older intensive care patients remains unclear. This study evaluated associations between absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) dynamics, 28-day mortality, and a culture-based secondary microbiological outcome in patients aged ≥ 65 years with sepsis.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort, patients aged ≥ 65 years admitted from the emergency department to the ICU with sepsis between January 1, 2022 and December 31, 2025 were included. Daily ALC during days 1–7 was categorized as no lymphopenia (≥ 1000 cells/mm³), moderate lymphopenia (500–999 cells/mm³), or severe lymphopenia (< 500 cells/mm³). The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality; the secondary outcome was 28-day nosocomial culture positivity.ResultsAmong 566 patients, the mean age was 80.7 ± 8.0 years, 48.2% were female, and 28-day mortality was 41.0%. ALC was available for all patients through ICU day 3 and for 514/566 patients (90.8%) on ICU day 7; later non-availability reflected shortened observation time rather than random laboratory omission. Severe lymphopenia declined from 33.9% on day 1 to 24.7% on day 7. Mortality was highest in severe lymphopenia, with FDR-corrected significance on day 2 and days 4–7. In the prespecified day-4 multivariable model, severe lymphopenia was independently associated with mortality (aOR 1.84, 95% CI 1.08–3.14; p = 0.025), whereas moderate lymphopenia was not. Increasing severe-lymphopenia burden was also associated with mortality during days 1–4 (aOR 1.16; p = 0.033) and days 1–7 (aOR 1.13; p = 0.013). Nosocomial culture positivity showed unadjusted late-week associations, but not after adjustment.ConclusionIn older ICU patients with sepsis, day-4 severe lymphopenia and first-week severe-lymphopenia burden were associated with 28-day mortality. The culture-based secondary outcome was not confirmed after adjustment and should be considered exploratory. First-week ALC monitoring may aid bedside risk stratification, but prospective multicenter validation is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13688-7.Keywords: Sepsis, Elderly patient, Lymphopenia, Intensive care, Mortality, Nosocomial culture positivity

Introduction

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection and remains one of the most challenging clinical conditions in intensive care practice [1]. At the global level, the burden of sepsis is still very high; the reporting of approximately 48.9 million cases and 11 million sepsis-related deaths for 2017 clearly demonstrates the weight of this syndrome in modern medicine [2].

Advanced age is one of the main variables that markedly changes the clinical course of sepsis. In older patients, when comorbidity burden, loss of physiological reserve, frailty, sarcopenia, malnutrition, and immunosenescence converge, susceptibility to sepsis increases while the capacity for recovery decreases. Therefore, in the older patient with sepsis, the issue is not only the presence of infection, but also the extent to which the host response to it is sustainable [3].

The current approach has moved away from explaining sepsis solely through hyperinflammation. It is now accepted that, for many patients, what is decisive is immunosuppression that develops together with inflammation and is often prolonged. Increased myeloid-derived suppressor cells, imbalance in T-cell subsets, lymphocyte apoptosis, T-cell exhaustion, and weakening of the adaptive immune response are major components of this process [4, 5]. One of the findings most easily reflected in clinical practice is lymphopenia. An absolute lymphocyte count below 1000 cells/µL is generally accepted as lymphopenia, and below 500 cells/µL as severe lymphopenia; however, which threshold and which timing best reflect clinical outcomes is still debated [6].

The relationship between persistent lymphopenia and poor outcomes in sepsis has been shown in different studies. Drewry et al. reported that a lymphocyte count that remained low on day 4 after the diagnosis of sepsis was associated with both 28-day and 1-year mortality, and also increased the risk of secondary infection [7]. In later studies as well, persistent lymphopenia was found to be associated with a prolonged clinical course, in-hospital death, and worse outcomes in critical illness [8, 9]. Therefore, lymphopenia is now regarded not only as a concomitant laboratory abnormality, but as a clinically meaningful sign suggesting that immune recovery is inadequate in sepsis [6].

In older patients, this picture becomes even more pronounced. Inoue et al. showed that older patients with severe sepsis had a reduction in immunocompetent T cells and markedly prolonged lymphopenia, and that lymphocyte depletion persisted for weeks, particularly in those who died [10]. Reviews addressing changes in immune cell number, phenotype, and function in older patients with sepsis have also highlighted PD-1-positive T cells, regulatory T cells, and patterns of functional exhaustion [11, 12]. In more recent older cohort studies, lymphocyte/T-cell patterns that failed to recover on days 3 and 7 were shown to be associated with 28-day mortality, and risk prediction was strengthened when frailty and immune parameters were evaluated together [13, 14].

On the other hand, lymphocyte-based prediction is not limited to the absolute lymphocyte count alone. While baseline lymphopenia in patients with septic shock has been reported to be associated with 30-day mortality [15], recent large database studies have suggested that the neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio may also predict prognosis in older patients with sepsis [16, 17]. Nevertheless, the parameter that is still the easiest and most universally accessible in daily practice is the absolute lymphocyte count.

Most of the existing literature either evaluates the entire adult sepsis population together or addresses lymphocyte level at a single time point. However, in the older intensive care patient, the key clinical question is often not whether the lymphocyte count is low at baseline, but whether it recovers during the first week. In this study, we investigated the relationship of daily first-week lymphocyte dynamics with 28-day mortality and 28-day nosocomial culture positivity, used as a conservative culture-based secondary microbiological outcome, in patients aged 65 years and older with sepsis.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This study is a single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study conducted in a tertiary adult intensive care unit. The study included patients aged 65 years and older who were admitted to the intensive care unit from the emergency department with a diagnosis of sepsis between January 1, 2022 and December 31, 2025, and who were 65 years of age or older on the date of ICU admission. If the same patient had more than one intensive care admission, only the first admission was evaluated.

Definition of sepsis

Sepsis was defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. The SOFA score was used to assess organ dysfunction, and eligible cases were evaluated according to the Sepsis-3 definition [1].

Data collection

Data were obtained retrospectively from the hospital electronic record system and intensive care patient follow-up files. During the study period, 3,497 ICU admissions were screened. Of these, 742 belonged to patients aged 65 years and older with sepsis and met the initial screening criteria. Because the first-week lymphocyte course cannot be meaningfully evaluated in hyperacute fatal presentations, 78 patients (10.5%) who died within the first 24 h of ICU admission were excluded. In addition, 98 more patients (13.2%) with missing core study data were excluded during cohort construction (Fig. 1). Thus, the final analytic cohort included 566 ICU admissions, corresponding to 76.3% of the older sepsis admissions initially found eligible. Available variables were age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), APACHE II, SOFA, sepsis source, need for mechanical ventilation (MV) during 28 days, need for renal replacement therapy (RRT), ICU and hospital length of stay, 28-day mortality, 28-day nosocomial culture positivity status, and daily lymphocyte categories for ICU days 1–7.

Sepsis source and microbiological classification

The source of sepsis was classified as lung, abdomen, urinary tract, bloodstream, and other according to clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings. The main pathogens identified at admission were categorized as Gram positive, Gram negative, yeast/viral, culture negative, and polymicrobial. The secondary microbiological outcome was defined as 28-day nosocomial culture positivity and was used as a pragmatic surrogate for culture-positive secondary infection. Admission pathogens and cultures obtained at ICU presentation were not counted as secondary events. Follow-up culture positivity was considered nosocomial when it occurred after the index ICU admission and was not attributable to persistence of the admission pathogen or the initial infection focus. When the available records indicated colonization or contamination without clinical evidence of infection, these episodes were not interpreted as definitive secondary infection. Because formal blinded clinical adjudication and full prospective application of CDC/NHSN surveillance criteria were not feasible in this retrospective dataset, this outcome was analyzed and interpreted as exploratory.

Definition of lymphopenia and lymphocyte categories

Patients’ absolute lymphocyte counts were evaluated daily during the first 7 days. Daily lymphocyte status was classified into three categories: no lymphopenia (≥ 1000 cells/mm³), moderate lymphopenia (500–999 cells/mm³), and severe lymphopenia (< 500 cells/mm³). These thresholds were prespecified on clinical and pragmatic grounds because ALC < 1000 cells/mm³ is commonly used to define lymphopenia and ALC < 500 cells/mm³ to define severe lymphopenia. A categorical approach was retained to facilitate bedside interpretability rather than to derive cohort-specific data-driven cut-offs. In the exploratory nosocomial culture positivity analysis, a binary classification was also used, and in this approach, lymphopenia was defined as an absolute lymphocyte count < 1000 cells/mm³, corresponding to either moderate or severe lymphopenia.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median [IQR] according to distribution. In baseline comparisons, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables and the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. In day-specific descriptive analyses, only patients who were alive, under observation, and had an ALC measurement available on that ICU day contributed to the denominator. Later non-availability of ALC values primarily reflected shortened observation time due to death, discharge, or transfer rather than random laboratory omission. These non-available values were not imputed and were not assumed to represent lymphocyte recovery. Day-specific denominators were reported explicitly. To describe attrition, we summarized day-specific ALC availability across ICU days 1–7. Sensitivity analyses were performed by comparing available-case estimates with complete-case estimates restricted to patients with ALC categories available for all ICU days 1–7. Multiple imputation was not performed because later non-availability primarily represented informative attrition rather than missing-at-random laboratory omission. Crude associations between daily lymphocyte category and 28-day mortality were examined separately for days 1–7; p values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery rate. The same approach was also used in exploratory daily analyses of binary lymphopenia and 28-day nosocomial culture positivity.

Adjusted models

The primary multivariable logistic model evaluated day-4 lymphocyte category in patients with a measurement on day 4 and used the no-lymphopenia group as the reference category. Day 4 was selected a priori because it balances two clinically important situations: by this time, the transient early decline in lymphocytes should have started to recover in at least some patients; on the other hand, declining day-specific observation time was still limited compared with later days. In other words, day 4 offers a reasonable time window in which inadequate immune recovery may begin to become apparent without sacrificing too much cohort size. Because patients were required to be alive, under observation, and to have an available day-4 ALC measurement to enter the day-4 model, this analysis also functioned as an operational day-4 landmark analysis. An adjusted day-7 model was used as a sensitivity analysis. We also examined the cumulative number of severe-lymphopenia days during days 1–4 and days 1–7. Covariates were selected on clinical grounds and included age, sex, CCI, APACHE II, sepsis source, MV, and RRT. In the primary adjusted models, APACHE II was preferred instead of SOFA in order to limit redundancy among severity measures. As a sensitivity analysis, the primary day-4 model was repeated after substituting SOFA for APACHE II.

ICU and hospital lengths of stay were treated as downstream clinical outcomes rather than baseline confounders, because both are influenced by survival status and the subsequent ICU course. Therefore, they were not included in the mortality models. Instead, among patients who survived 28 days and had a day-7 measurement, ICU and hospital lengths of stay were investigated separately according to day-7 lymphocyte categories using Kruskal-Wallis tests and gamma-log generalized linear models adjusted for the same covariates. No imputation was performed. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ankara Bilkent City Hospital on 21 January 2026 (decision no. TABED 2/1849/2026), after completion of the retrospective study period from 1 January 2022 to 31 December 2025. Approval was obtained for retrospective analysis of routinely collected, anonymized clinical data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective design of the study and the use of routinely collected clinical data, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Among the 3,497 ICU admissions screened during the study period, 742 older adults with sepsis were initially eligible. After excluding 78 patients who died within 24 h after ICU admission and 98 patients with missing core study data, 566 ICU admissions remained for the final analysis. In the analytic cohort, the mean age was 80.68 ± 7.97 years; 273 patients (48.2%) were female and 301 patients (53.2%) were aged 80 years or older. The most common source of sepsis was lung infection (54.2%), followed by urinary tract infection (24.9%). Admission cultures were negative in 37.5% of cases; the next most frequent category was Gram negative organisms (25.8%). Mechanical ventilation was used in 62.2% of patients and RRT in 25.1%. Twenty-eight-day mortality was 41.0%. CCI, APACHE II, and SOFA scores were higher in non-survivors than in survivors; by contrast, age, sex, sepsis source, and microbiological category at admission were similar between groups. ICU and hospital lengths of stay were shorter in non-survivors than in survivors, consistent with early death shortening total length of stay (Table 1).

First-week lymphocyte dynamics

Day-specific ALC availability was complete through ICU day 3 (566/566, 100.0%). Thereafter, the denominator decreased progressively to 557 on day 4, 547 on day 5, 531 on day 6, and 514 on day 7, primarily because patients no longer contributed ICU observation time after death, discharge, or transfer. Thus, later non-availability should be interpreted as attrition related to shortened clinical follow-up rather than simple laboratory omission. Among available measurements, the proportion of severe lymphopenia declined from 33.9% on day 1 to 24.7% on day 7, whereas the proportion of patients without lymphopenia increased from 28.6% to 36.2% (Table 2; Fig. 2). Moderate lymphopenia remained the most frequent daily category throughout the week.

Crude association with 28-day mortality

Across all analyzed days, crude mortality was numerically highest in the severe lymphopenia group. After false-discovery-rate correction, the association between daily lymphocyte category and 28-day mortality remained significant on day 2 and on days 4–7, whereas day 3 no longer met the adjusted threshold value (Table 3). Mortality in the severe lymphopenia category ranged from 45.3% on day 1 to 52.6% on day 6, whereas this rate ranged from 27.4% to 35.8% in patients without lymphopenia.

Adjusted mortality analyses

In the prespecified adjusted day-4 model, severe lymphopenia was independently associated with higher 28-day mortality compared with no lymphopenia (aOR 1.84, 95% CI 1.08–3.14; p = 0.025); no such association was found for moderate lymphopenia (Table 4; Fig. 3). In the day-7 sensitivity model, severe lymphopenia maintained its directional association with mortality, but narrowly missed the conventional threshold for statistical significance (aOR 1.74, 95% CI 1.00–3.03; p = 0.052). This pattern is still clinically important: the point estimate remained above 1 and stayed close to the day-4 result, indicating attenuation rather than reversal. Burden analyses showed a consistent dose-response pattern; each additional severe-lymphopenia day was associated with higher mortality both during days 1–4 (aOR 1.16, 95% CI 1.01–1.33; p = 0.033) and during days 1–7 (aOR 1.13, 95% CI 1.03–1.24; p = 0.013).

In sensitivity analyses, the main findings were materially unchanged. In complete-case analyses restricted to patients with ALC categories available for all ICU days 1–7, day-4 severe lymphopenia remained associated with 28-day mortality (aOR 1.96, 95% CI 1.12–3.43; p = 0.019), and the days 1–4 severe-lymphopenia burden remained significant (aOR 1.21 per additional severe day, 95% CI 1.04–1.40; p = 0.011). Substituting SOFA for APACHE II did not materially change the main finding; day-4 severe lymphopenia remained associated with 28-day mortality (aOR 1.75, 95% CI 1.03–2.97; p = 0.038) (Supplementary Table S1).

Exploratory nosocomial culture positivity analyses

In exploratory analyses of 28-day nosocomial culture positivity, crude associations between binary lymphopenia (ALC < 1000 cells/mm³) and culture positivity emerged in the later part of the week. After false-discovery-rate correction, days 6 and 7 remained significant in unadjusted analyses (Table 5). However, when disease severity and organ support were taken into account, the association no longer reached statistical significance in the adjusted day-7 model (aOR 1.40, 95% CI 0.92–2.12; p = 0.112). Taken together, this culture-based signal appears biologically plausible but is not sufficiently stable for a strong causal or prognostic interpretation and should be considered exploratory.

Secondary analyses of ICU and hospital length of stay

Because total ICU and hospital length of stay are downstream variables shortened by early death, length-of-stay analyses were restricted to patients who survived 28 days and had a day-7 lymphocyte measurement. In this survivor-only subset, late lymphopenia categories were unadjustedly associated with longer lengths of stay (Table 6): median ICU length of stay was 17 [10–32] days in patients without lymphopenia on day 7, 24.5 [13–48.5] days in those with moderate lymphopenia, and 27 [13–44.5] days in those with severe lymphopenia (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.002). Median hospital length of stay was 26 [16.5–42] days, 34 [18.8–58.3] days, and 37.5 [18.3–50.8] days, respectively (p = 0.018). After multivariable gamma-log adjustment, neither moderate nor severe day-7 lymphopenia remained independently associated with ICU length of stay (moderate ratio 1.10, 95% CI 0.93–1.30; severe ratio 1.03, 95% CI 0.84–1.27) or hospital length of stay (moderate ratio 1.12, 95% CI 0.95–1.31; severe ratio 1.09, 95% CI 0.90–1.33).

Sensitivity analysis for repeated identifiers

A sensitivity analysis excluding repeated national identification numbers and retaining only the first of each repeated record produced a materially similar estimate for the primary exposure: day-4 severe lymphopenia remained associated with 28-day mortality (aOR 1.78, 95% CI 1.03–3.07; p = 0.038), supporting the robustness of the main finding.

Discussion

The data from this study suggest that, in the older patient with sepsis, interpreting the lymphocyte count as a single baseline laboratory value is not sufficient. In our cohort, the clearest divergence emerged not on the first day, but within the first week, when the course of illness began to take shape. Especially in patients with severe lymphopenia, the higher mortality suggests that in the older patient it may be not the “low lymphocyte count” alone but rather the “non-resolving lymphocyte response” that is clinically meaningful.

The isolated loss of statistical significance on day 3 after FDR correction should be interpreted cautiously. The direction of effect remained consistent, and the q value narrowly exceeded the adjusted threshold (q = 0.058). This may reflect transient early immune fluctuation, treatment-related variation during resuscitation, or limited power after correction for multiple daily comparisons rather than a true reversal of the overall trajectory.

This finding is consistent with current knowledge of the immunopathogenesis of sepsis. Sepsis is now understood not only as hyperinflammation but also as a state of prolonged immunosuppression. Increased myeloid-derived suppressor cells, imbalance in T-cell subsets, lymphocyte apoptosis, and T-cell exhaustion are important components of this picture [4, 5, 18, 19]. From a clinical perspective, lymphopenia is the simplest and most accessible reflection of this complex process. Our data suggest that lymphopenia that does not recover and worsens, especially during the first week, may represent the bedside counterpart of ongoing immune insufficiency in sepsis.

The relationship between persistent lymphopenia and poor outcomes has previously been shown in different studies. Drewry et al. reported that an absolute lymphocyte count remaining low on day 4 after the diagnosis of sepsis was associated with both 28-day and 1-year mortality and also increased the risk of secondary infection [7]. Jing et al. and Adigbli et al. also associated persistent lymphopenia with a higher risk of death in critical illness [8, 9]. Studies by Jiang, Cilloniz, and Chung showed that low lymphocyte levels were accompanied by adverse outcomes in different sepsis and severe infection cohorts [20–22]. The contribution of our study to this literature is that the same pattern was demonstrated specifically in older intensive care patients and through daily monitoring over the first week.

It has long been known that the immune response is more fragile in older patients with sepsis. Inoue et al. showed that older patients with severe sepsis had a significant reduction in immunocompetent T-cell counts and that lymphopenia lasted for at least 21 days, especially in those who died [10]. Later work by the same group showed that persistent inflammation and T-cell exhaustion may be more pronounced in older sepsis patients [12]. He et al., in turn, emphasized that in older sepsis patients not only cell counts but also cell phenotypes and functions are altered, with immunosuppressive profiles such as PD-1-positive T cells and regulatory T cells becoming prominent [11]. For this reason, it seems more appropriate to interpret our findings not simply as a hematologic variable, but as a simple indicator of delayed immune recovery in the older patient.

Recent older sepsis cohorts also support this interpretation. In studies conducted in older and octogenarian patients, lymphocyte/T-cell patterns that failed to recover on days 3 and 7 were reported to be associated with 28-day mortality [13]. Similarly, in a multicenter study in which frailty-laboratory index and lymphocyte patterns were evaluated together, both frailty burden and lymphocyte patterns were shown to independently reflect short-term mortality risk in older sepsis patients [14]. Our data are important in showing that a similar clinical message can be produced with daily complete blood count measurements, without requiring advanced immune phenotyping.

On the other hand, baseline lymphopenia cannot be said to be entirely unimportant. There are studies showing that baseline lymphopenia in patients with septic shock is associated with 30-day mortality [15]. However, in our cohort, clear differentiation emerged not at presentation but during follow-up. This suggests that, in the older patient, immune recovery capacity over the first few days may be more decisive than a single baseline value.

Another dimension of the lymphocyte-based prognostic approach is the neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio. In two recent studies based on large databases, high NLR values in older sepsis patients were reported to be associated with 28-day mortality and to increase predictive power when used together with clinical scores such as qSOFA [16, 17]. Although NLR was not evaluated in our study, it is noteworthy that clear separation was detected even with the daily course of absolute lymphocyte count alone. This shows that lymphocyte dynamics can carry a strong clinical signal even without more complex hematologic indices.

Our findings regarding the secondary microbiological outcome should be interpreted cautiously. In our study, the association between binary lymphopenia and 28-day nosocomial culture positivity was not evident on days 1–5; significance emerged on days 6 and 7 in unadjusted analyses. This timing is biologically plausible because lymphopenia persisting into the second half of the first week may reflect delayed immune recovery. However, the adjusted model did not confirm a statistically significant association, and the outcome was based on retrospective culture positivity rather than formal clinical adjudication. Therefore, this finding should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence that lymphopenia independently predicts secondary infection. The demonstration by Drewry et al. of the relationship between persistent lymphopenia and secondary infection [7] and the discussion by Wang et al. of lymphopenia as a meaningful immune marker for secondary infection [6] support the biological plausibility of this signal, but they do not remove the need for prospective validation in older ICU cohorts.

Therefore, the clinical value of lymphocyte count should be considered primarily in the context of serial immune monitoring and mortality risk stratification. The expert consensus report prepared for sepsis-associated immunosuppression recommends initiating immune monitoring within the first 48 h after the diagnosis of sepsis and continuing it as dynamically as possible; in this monitoring, peripheral lymphocyte count and mHLA-DR are listed among the main indicators [23]. The same document identifies patients aged 65 years and older as a high-risk group [23]. The review by Liu et al. and the clinical guide by Cidade et al. also emphasize that the immune response should be assessed through serial rather than one-time measurements [24, 25]. Data from Hohlstein et al. on lymphocyte subsets in critical illness also support this approach [26]. Our study is consistent with these recommendations in showing that daily lymphocyte monitoring is usable in routine practice in older sepsis patients.

The treatment-related aspect of lymphopenia should not be overlooked either. Studies with IL-7 have shown that lymphocyte counts, especially CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, can increase and that sepsis-associated lymphopenia can be partially reversed [27, 28]. These treatments are not yet part of routine practice; however, our findings suggest that older patients who still have persistent severe lymphopenia in the middle and at the end of the first week may represent a more suitable subgroup for future immunomodulatory approaches.

The epidemiologic background of our study is also consistent with the literature on sepsis in older adults. Sepsis is more common in older adults, symptoms may be more subtle, and mortality is higher [29, 30]. Recent cohorts have shown that sepsis outcomes worsen with increasing age, that the need for organ support increases at very advanced age, and that immunosenescence contributes meaningfully to this picture [31–33]. In our cohort, the predominance of lung and urinary tract foci and the high mortality rate are consistent with this general picture. In contrast, the lack of a significant association of sepsis source and admission pathogen category with mortality suggests that, at least in this age group, the host immune-response pattern may be more informative than the infection focus.

Another notable point is that lymphocyte recovery may be relevant not only for short-term but also for longer-term outcomes. A recent study in septic shock reported that lymphopenia was associated with long-term mortality and that lymphocyte correction might have prognostic value [34]. Although our study focused on 28-day outcomes, the possibility that immune recovery during the first week may also be important for later clinical outcomes in the older patient with sepsis should not be overlooked.

This study has several strengths. First, the entire cohort consisted of sepsis patients aged 65 years and older; this reduces the heterogeneity caused by having older and younger adults in the same analysis. Second, lymphocyte course was based not on a single measurement but on daily follow-up over the first 7 days. Third, the biomarker used is derived from a routine complete blood count available in every center and does not require advanced laboratory infrastructure. Finally, the study also explored a culture-based secondary microbiological outcome, although this analysis should be interpreted cautiously and as hypothesis-generating.

However, the study also has some limitations. The single-center and retrospective design limits causal interpretation and cannot completely exclude selection bias. Immune status was evaluated only through total lymphocyte count, and more detailed parameters such as lymphocyte subsets or mHLA-DR were not studied. Although the thresholds used are clinically understandable and applicable, the optimal cut-off may vary across different populations, and cohort-specific ROC-derived thresholds were not the aim of this pragmatic analysis. Day-specific and burden models may be affected by survivor or immortal-time effects because patients who die, are discharged, or are transferred early contribute fewer observation days. We attempted to mitigate this by excluding deaths within the first 24 h, interpreting the day-4 analysis as an operational landmark analysis, explicitly describing day-specific ALC non-availability and attrition, and comparing available-case with complete-case estimates; nevertheless, residual time-dependent bias cannot be fully excluded in a retrospective design. The secondary microbiological outcome was based on retrospective clinical records and culture positivity rather than formal blinded adjudication; therefore, some culture-positive episodes may have represented colonization or persistent infection rather than true secondary infection. For this reason, we used the conservative term 28-day nosocomial culture positivity and interpreted this outcome as exploratory. Finally, because the data were collected from routine clinical care, treatment-related factors that could influence lymphocyte recovery were not fully captured.

In conclusion, in the older patient with sepsis, the clinically important issue is not simply that the lymphocyte count is low; the key determinant is that this low count persists and especially that it evolves into a severe form. This pattern, which becomes more evident toward the middle and end of the first week, has implications for mortality risk stratification. The late-week culture-positivity signal is biologically plausible but was not confirmed after adjustment and should be interpreted cautiously. Daily absolute lymphocyte count may therefore provide a practical and accessible adjunctive monitoring indicator in older patients with sepsis, but it should be interpreted together with clinical severity and requires prospective validation.

Conclusion

In ICU patients with sepsis aged 65 years and older, severe lymphopenia persisting through day 4 and a higher burden of severe lymphopenia during the first week were independently associated with 28-day mortality. The association with 28-day nosocomial culture positivity was weaker after adjustment and should be considered exploratory. ICU and hospital lengths of stay should be interpreted as downstream clinical outcomes rather than baseline adjustment variables. Therefore, routine lymphocyte monitoring during the first week may provide a simple bedside indicator of delayed immune recovery in older patients with sepsis, but prospective multicenter validation is needed before this approach is used as an independent decision-making tool.
