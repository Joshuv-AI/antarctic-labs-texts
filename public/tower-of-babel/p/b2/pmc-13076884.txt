Comparing effective and functional connectivity
Authors: Shigeru Shinomoto, Yasuhiro Tsubo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInformation processing in the brain is thought to result from the coordination of large-scale neuronal activity. However, this activity occurs within fine neural circuits. In this study, we investigate the relationship between neuronal wiring and coherent activity. First, we estimate monosynaptic connectivity by applying an advanced analysis method to spike trains recorded with high-density microelectrodes and confirm that the estimated effective connectivity is broadly in line with neuroanatomical and neurophysiological evidence. Second, we estimate functional connectivity or the temporal correlation of calcium imaging signal between the same set of neurons, and confirm that the estimated functional connectivity is susceptible to influence of shared inputs and population synchronization on slower timescales. Notably, even with unrealistically fast time scale, the functional connectivity defined as the synchronous correlation, is only partially consistent with the effective connectivity estimated using advanced analytical techniques that pursue monosynaptic connections. These findings suggest the complementary roles for effective and functional connectivity: the former provides circuit-level specificity, while the latter reflects emergent system-wide patterns of activity. We propose that an integrative approach combining both perspectives is essential for understanding circuit-level computation in the brain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42580-2.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Neuroscience

Introduction

With the development of large-scale recording techniques, there has been a surge of interest in capturing the communication that takes place between different parts of the brain. Researchers have proposed capturing the “functional connectivity” (FC), by defining it as significant temporal correlations in activity, with the hope of understanding how the brain supports cognition and behavior, or capturing brain dysfunction when it occurs1–4. Recently, two-photon microscopy technology is rapidly evolving to allow parallel recording of a large number of neurons, opening up the possibility of drawing a huge detailed map of neuronal FC5–10. Despite these advances, it is still poorly understood how such neuronal co-activity emerges on the underlying hard-wired neuronal circuitry.

It seems difficult for the two-photon microscopy technology to elucidate neuronal hardware wiring because calcium dynamics on the order of 100 ms are much slower than monosynaptic signal transmission between neurons, which occurs in a few ms. In contrast, microelectrode technology, which can determine neuronal firing with sub-millisecond accuracy, has the potential to reveal the causal antecedents and sequelae of neuronal activation. There has been a long-standing effort to identify the actual monosynaptic connections that each neuron exerts on another, based on recorded spike trains11–28. Since the inference is still imperfect, we refer to the estimated connections as “effective connectivity” (EC), following the terminology used in the references2,3. The number of recordable neurons is increasing due to the development of high-density electrodes such as Neuropixels29,30.

In this study, we examine the relationship between FC and the underlying neuronal wiring structure estimated as EC. To this end, we first estimate the monosynaptic wiring between neurons using a state-of-the-art method for estimating EC. Of the various estimation methods, we selected ShinGLMCC28 because it currently provides the most reliable proxy for the monosynaptic connectivity27. By analyzing a large number of spike trains recorded with high-density microelectrodes, we examine the consistency of the EC with neuroanatomical and neurophysiological evidence.

Second, we compute FC between the same set of neurons by simulating two-photon imaging signals from the spike trains. To do this, we convolve a kernel representing calcium influx for each spike and the subsequent pumping. Since calcium signals can identify neuronal firing with a temporal resolution of 100 to 1000 ms, it is difficult to infer the monosynaptic impact of a delay of a few ms between neurons. FC is intended to capture simultaneous activity correlation. Since we can simulate a case in which the optical signal has a shorter time resolution, we are interested in whether FC, as defined by simultaneous correlation, correlates with EC, and in particular, whether FC can ultimately represent EC at the shortest timescale.

Third, we perform a simulation of an artificial neural network consisting of the estimated synaptic connections to determine the extent to which internal connections contribute to FC. This should be contrasted with the real FC, wherein recorded neurons not only interact with each other, but also receive external stimuli. To examine the network’s susceptibility to external stimuli, we apply weak stimuli to a subset of neurons in the simulation.

Results

Estimating the EC

Given a set of neuronal spike trains, the EC analysis method determines whether any pair of neurons are monosynaptically connected based solely on their spike timing information, without relying on any other knowledge such as the distance between the recorded neurons. This is a difficult task, as if we are trying to infer the relationship between any pair of people from their chat times alone, without using other cues such as their social community or where they live. Although conventional algorithms have made many incorrect estimates, subsequent innovations have greatly improved the accuracy of the estimates15–25. While efforts are still underway to improve and compare the performance of analysis methods26,27, we adopt our latest algorithm ShinGLMCC28 and analyze spike trains recorded with a single probe consisting of high-density electrodes, Neuropixels29,30.

While ShinGLMCC determines monosynaptic connections without reference to the recorded positions, the estimated connections turned out to be clearly well localized to each brain region. In addition, the patterns of neuronal connectivity differ significantly between different brain regions. Interneuronal connections in the thalamus are fewer and more localized than in the hippocampus and cortex, consistent with neuroanatomical and neurophysiological knowledge31–33. To see if the ECs are subject to statistical error, we divide the 3,600-second time series into two halves and estimate ECs from each half. The connection matrices are shown in Supplementary Figure 1. Their similarity confirms the stability of the estimation.

ShinGLMCC also distinguishes between excitatory and inhibitory connections. Figure 1(a) displays them in magenta and light blue, respectively. Outgoing connections from each neuron are aligned vertically in the matrix. The estimated connections tend to be the same color, reflecting the neurophysiological fact that each neuron’s output connections are uniquely polarized as either excitatory or inhibitory. This is sometimes referred to as Dale’s principle, which was originally proposed as to the neuronal chemical transmitters.

Figure 1(b) magnifies the hippocampus and a part of the visual cortex to focus on their individual intersubregional projections. There are prominent excitatory projections from the dentate gyrus (DG) to the CA in the hippocampus and from superficial layers to deeper layers in the visual cortex. These unidirectional projections are consistent with neuroanatomical knowledge34–36. To investigate whether the intersubregional connections in the hippocampus and visual cortex are subject to statistical fluctuations, we tested whether the estimated ECs disappear when the significance level is decreased. As shown in Supplementary Figure 2, the intersubregional connections remained stable even when the significance level was drastically decreased from 0.001 to 0.000001.

Figure 1(c) depicts parts of the hippocampus and visual cortex to demonstrate the reciprocal connections between inhibitory interneurons and excitatory pyramidal neurons. This circuit structure supports the idea of a common inhibition mechanism in which a “winner-takes-all” mechanism operates37–39. In the hippocampus, when a mouse is at some location, place cells specific to that location activate inhibitory cells to suppress other place cells. When the mouse moves to another location, other place cells activate the same inhibitory cells to suppress others. This is consistent with the knowledge that inhibitory cells are not place selective. In the visual cortex, inhibitory neurons also have reciprocal connections between many excitatory cells, but the number of connections is not as large as in the hippocampus40.

Estimating the FC

Next, we investigate the relationship between FC and EC for the same set of neurons. To do this, we artificially generate two-photon signals from the spike trains that we used to estimate monosynaptic connections. The fluorescence signal represents the calcium concentration in each neuron; when it fires, calcium immediately enters the cell and is then slowly pumped out. We represent this process by convolving an exponential kernel function, for , at each spike time (Figure 3(a)).

Given a set of fluorescence signal time series, we compute the functional connectivity in terms of the Pearson correlation coefficient between a pair of neurons’ time series. Under the aforementioned linear hypothesis, the Pearson correlation can be obtained by convolving a kernel, , with the cross-correlation of the original spiking time series.

According to the practical approach adopted in FC research, we select the top fraction of pairs with the highest correlation. In the current study, we also examine pairs with the lowest correlations, which we refer to as negative FC to distinguish it from positive connectivity. Comparison of FC and EC

Figure 3(b) shows FC matrices estimated for the neurons recorded from the thalamus, hippocampus, and visual cortex, in reference to EC matrices. Because the fluorescence signals are artificially generated, we can examine how the FC varies with the timescale of the optical signal. Here we varied the time scale of a convolving kernel from 1000, 100, to 10 ms. First, we observe that the positive FCs are more localized in three brain regions than the negative FCs. Second, the positive FCs become more localized as the time scale becomes shorter. The average distances between positive FC pairs, in terms of matrix indices, are 55.4, 42.4, and 17.1 for 1000, 100, and 10 ms, respectively, while those for negative FC pairs are 79.8, 74.9, and 68.9. The average distance between EC pairs is even shorter: 14.9.

Figures 3(c) and (d) show the adjacency graphs of the positive and negative FCs, with the nodes of three brain regions colored differently, in reference to the excitatory and inhibitory ECs. Neurons of three regions are relatively well clustered with positive FC when the timescale is unrealistically short, ms. However, the clustering features are still not as clear as those for excitatory and inhibitory ECs estimated by ShinGLMCC, as shown on the rightmost. We tested an even shorter time scale ms, but the situation is similar and the FCs do not approach the ECs.

Because neuronal function can occur on a given circuit, it is naturally expected that neuronal co-activation, or functional connectivity, will occur for a pair of neurons that are monosynaptically connected. To see their relationship, we computed the conditional probability that each pair of neurons is connected as EC, given that they are functionally connected (Figure 3(e)). The conditional probability turned out to be low on the realistic timescale of calcium dynamics of about ms. On the timescale of 100 ms, which may be the shortest possible timescale in reality, the conditional probability of an inhibitory EC can be as high as 0.4 for pairs with very strong top FC. In other words, a pair of neurons showing strong coactivation could be monosynaptically connected, but more likely have an inhibitory connection. Because we are making artificial optical signals, we can go to even shorter timescales, such as ms, which does not seem feasible with current technology. Then the conditional probabilities get higher. For example, pairs with highly selective positive FC are likely to be monosynaptically connected in terms of EC. It is also true that the probability of an inhibitory EC is higher than that of an excitatory EC. However, this does not mean that the FCs are similar to the ECs, because the conditional probabilities become lower for the larger population of weaker FC such as or 0.1.

We estimate an association between FC and EC in terms of the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), which measures the balance of binary variables by taking into account their true and false positives and negatives43. Here we simplify the classifications into the presence or absence of connections, ignoring their signs, such as positive or negative FCs and excitatory or inhibitory ECs. The MCC varies with the probability p of selecting FCs (Figure 3(f)). For high probabilities such as , the MCC is low because the less selective FC may not be associated with EC. On the contrary, for low probabilities such as , the pairs with strong FC tend to have EC, but the other majority lose the association. As a result, the MCC peaks between and . The peak value of the MCC increases as the timescale is decreased from ms to ms, indicating that the FC is closer to the EC. Note, however, that the value is still much less than the perfect match, or , indicating that FC cannot represent EC even at the short timescale of ms. We also tested the shortest timescale of ms, but the situation did not change significantly. Thus, the FC does not represent the EC, even in the limit of the shortest timescale.

Numerical simulation of a network of estimated connections

While the recorded neurons are still a tiny fraction of the neurons in the brain, their ECs exhibit specific features consistent with neuroanatomical knowledge. To investigate the extent to which the FCs can be reproduced by the network of recorded neurons alone, here we perform a simulation of an artificial neural network consisting of the estimated connections.

A numerical simulation is performed using the spiking neuron model, the Multi-timescale Adaptive Threshold (MAT) model44. Synaptic connections between model neurons were provided by the connection matrix estimated from the real data set shown in Figure 1(b). In addition to these intranetwork connections, we added noise representing inputs from invisible neurons so that the firing rate of each neuron is equal to the recorded firing rate. This simulation has only one free parameter that multiplies the ECs to determine the strength of the intranetwork connectivity relative to the inputs from external invisible neurons. We chose this parameter so that the ECs estimated from simulated spike trains become similar to the original ones (METHODS). As a result, these EC matrices showed good agreement, as can be seen by comparing the right ends of Figs. 3(b) and 4(a).

Three left panels of Fig. 4(a) show the resulting FCs for the timescales 1000, 100, or 10 ms. Although these FCs of an artificial neural network do not reproduce ECs either, FCs of 100 and 10 ms are more similar to EC than those of the real spike trains shown in Fig. 3(b), implying that the FC of the simulated spike trains tends to reflect the monosynaptic connectivity relatively more faithfully than the real spike trains. This may be because the simulation is performed with the isolated network, while the real network receives correlated inputs from external invisible neurons whose states change over time.

To see how the FC is affected by external stimulation, we ran simulations in which weak stimulation was applied to the thalamus or visual cortex. Namely, we repeatedly increased the excitatory noise to neurons in each area for 1 second in every 20 seconds (Figs. 4(b) and (c)). The applied stimulus is quite modest, so that the firing rates of the stimulated neurons increased by about 1.5 times for the short period of time. Such a small perturbation has induced a very strong FC among these stimulated neurons, particularly for the case of a realistic timescale of 1000 or 100 ms. This implies that the FC of the fluorescent calcium signal is sensitively influenced by common inputs to neurons.

On the contrary, the ECs were hardly affected by the stimulation. The MCCs between the ECs of the unstimulated system (Fig. 4(a)) and stimulated systems (Fig. 4b) and (c)) were greater than 0.98. This fact supports the robustness of the EC estimation method, ShinGLMCC.

Discussion

In the present study, we analyzed high-density recording data to estimate monosynaptic and functional interneuronal connectivity. First, we estimated direct neuronal wiring using the advanced analysis method. Second, we simulated calcium imaging signals and calculated FC. Third, we simulated a neural network consisting of the estimated connections. From these analyses, we learned that the state-of-the-art analysis method accurately captures the direct interaction between neurons by extracting the synaptic influence with a delay of a few ms, while the FC is strongly influenced by shared inputs, indirect interactions, and population synchronization. In particular, we found that FC at the time resolution of the calcium fluorescence signal is only partially consistent with the underlying EC. Even at unrealistically fast time resolutions, FC does not represent the interneuronal wiring.

We have shown that the latest monosynaptic estimation method, ShinGLMCC, not only makes inferences consistent with neuroanatomical and neurophysiological evidence, but also reveals new information about individual brain regions, such as the extensive innervation of inhibitory neurons in the hippocampus. A more important contribution of this estimation method may be its ability to provide concrete values for interneuronal connections, allowing realistic network simulations. These results are also due to the latest recording technology, which has made it possible to accurately monitor a large number of spike trains. Note that there still seem to be errors in spike sorting. We expect that the signal-to-noise ratio of the measurement and the sorting algorithm will continue to improve. We have confirmed that the latest connection estimation algorithm is a fairly reliable analysis tool, but we also expect that the algorithm to continue to improve.

EC is estimated by judging whether the synaptic influence with a delay of a few ms is statistically significant in the cross-correlogram. This statistical inference requires a large number of spikes to determine the existence of a monosynaptic connection. Currently, we are using data from one-hour measurements. It would be desirable to have longer time measurements that would allow the estimation of even weaker connections. Note that the need for a long monitoring time is not a technical requirement of a specific analysis method, but essentially necessary to obtain statistical confidence from a finite number of spikes, as was given in equation (3) or Table 1 in the reference23.

Next, we estimated FC between the same set of neurons and examined its relationship to EC, because we expected that neuronal co-activation, or FC, would appear on top of the network of connected neurons and might correlate with EC. However, we found that FC at the time resolution of calcium fluorescence signals is a weak reflection of the underlying neuronal circuitry and does not approach EC even at the shortest time resolution.

Throughout this analysis, we have adopted the standard definition of FC as the co-activity of a pair of neurons, or the Pearson correlation of calcium dynamics. There are many variants of FC that aim to capture the directed flow of information in the brain, in particular by detecting the causal interaction or activation delay between nodes45–47. While these measures may add information about the dynamic aspect of the brain, it still seems difficult to relate them to the underlying connectivity structure as long as data are given with the calcium signal on a timescale of the order of 100 ms, because it cannot resolve the monosynaptic signal transmission of a few ms between neurons.

Here, we generated two-photon signals from the recorded spike trains by linearly convolving an exponential kernel function at each spike time. It has been reported that the fluorescence signals may exhibit nonlinearity with respect to the accumulated signals48,49. Thus, it might be worthwhile to investigate whether the correlation between FC and EC could be strengthened by taking the nonlinearity into account.

While monosynaptic estimation is becoming more informative, particularly with the rapid development of high-density electrode techniques, FC analysis may also retain its importance because it may represent the influence of the invisible majority of neurons in the brain and also depend on behavioral contexts. The capabilities of two-photon recording technology seem to be developing even more rapidly, allowing the monitoring of a much larger number of neurons than microelectrode technology. Accordingly, an approach that integrates both monosynaptic and FC will influence future research design. This approach may reveal the conditions under which co-activation reflects underlying structure and how network dynamics modulate this relationship.

Methods

ShinGLMCC algorithm for estimating ECs

EC between a pair of neurons is detected by estimating the influence of the firing of one neuron on the firing of another neuron with a synaptic latency of a few ms. It has been proposed to construct a cross-correlation histogram or cross-correlogram and to detect a significant deviation from the flat distribution11–13. While the original theory took into account the statistical fluctuations in spike counts, in vivo neuronal firings show much larger fluctuations. There have been many efforts to resolve these confounding factors14–28.

One of the authors and others have applied the generalized linear model to estimate EC23. This algorithm, GLMCC, introduces a function a(t) to absorb the large undulation in a cross-correlation and fits the function to the cross-correlogram. Here, represents the possible monosynaptic impact of the short-duration function f(t) from jth neuron to ith neuron. To make the fitting function a(t) represent a smooth undulation, the model penalizes large gradients with a prior distribution, , where W is the time window of a CC and is a hyperparameter representing the flatness of a(t).

Although the GLMCC greatly improved the estimation accuracy, we found cases in which even this algorithm produced dubious inferences, particularly when the cross-correlation exhibited a cusp at the origin. We showed that the cusp is caused by nondifferentiable fluctuations in the brain, and revised the analysis algorithm to ShinGLMCC28. Namely, we revised the GLMCC to ShinGLMCC by allowing the function a(t) to bend at the origin. Specifically, we separated a single integral in the prior distribution into two half-lines and changed the first-order derivative to the second-order derivative: , with the constraint . This means that a(t) is requested to be smooth in each half-region except at the origin.

Simulation of a large-scale network of neurons

We performed a numerical simulation of a network of neurons using the Multi-timescale Adaptive Threshold (MAT) model44. When we performed network simulations to test the ShinGLMCC in the reference28, we chose interneuronal connections uniformly at random, taking into account neurophysiological knowledge such as balanced input, fractions of excitatory and inhibitory neurons, and lognormally distributed excitatory connectivity. In contrast, here we have adopted the connections estimated from the real data set. The peculiarity of the estimated connections is that they are clearly spatially localized, and that inhibitory neurons tend to have reciprocal feedback connections from excitatory neurons in each brain region (Fig. 1).

Stimulation to a part of a network

To see how FC is affected by external inputs, we performed network simulations by applying weak stimulation to a small group of neurons. This was done by increasing the excitatory noise to the neurons by a factor of 1.1 for 1 second every 20 seconds. As a result, the firing rates of the stimulated neurons increased by about 1.5 times for that 1 second. The stimulated neurons were selected from the thalamus (from number 1 to 76) or from the superficial layer of the visual cortex (from number 195 to 235). By recording spikes from all 242 neurons for 3,600 s, we estimated monosynaptic and FCs as in the unstimulated case.
