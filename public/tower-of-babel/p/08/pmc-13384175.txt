Early-life body mass index and adult fat distribution: a life-course Mendelian randomisation study

Introduction

Obesity-related risk is highly heterogeneous. Individuals with similar body mass index (BMI) may differ substantially in cardiometabolic risk, and regional fat distribution is now recognised as a major determinant of this heterogeneity.1 2 In particular, visceral adipose tissue (VAT) is more strongly associated with adverse metabolic traits and future disease than several subcutaneous depots, whereas gluteofemoral adipose tissue (GFAT) is often considered comparatively metabolically favourable.13 The role of abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue (ASAT) is more complex and may depend on the analytical framework and population under study.4 MRI-derived BMI-adjusted adiposity traits, namely, ASAT, GFAT and VAT adjusted for BMI (ASATadj, GFATadj and VATadj, respectively), provide a useful framework for studying fat partitioning independently of overall body size.5

Childhood BMI is not a static phenotype. Longitudinal studies have shown that BMI follows distinct developmental phases, including the infancy adiposity peak, subsequent decline in early childhood and adiposity rebound in later childhood.6 7 Time-resolved genome-wide association studies (GWASs) and longitudinal genetic modelling indicate the genetic architecture of BMI changes across development, demonstrating that BMI from infancy to adolescence is influenced by age-varying and partially distinct genetic profiles.8 9 This developmental specificity implies that BMI measured at different ages should be evaluated as separate exposures rather than as repeated measurements of a single homogeneous trait.8 9 Such a distinction is biologically plausible because the timing of adiposity gain may be as critical to long-term health outcomes as its overall magnitude.7

Observational studies suggest that BMI gain during specific developmental windows may differentially relate to adult fat depots.10 11 Earlier adiposity rebound has repeatedly been linked to later obesity and more central adiposity, and cohort data suggest that BMI gain in adolescence may be more strongly associated with adult visceral fat, whereas subcutaneous fat may reflect cumulative growth across multiple periods.1214 These observations raise a key life-course question: does genetically predicted BMI at different ages exert distinct downstream effects on adult fat distribution? Recent Mendelian randomisation (MR) studies using Norwegian Mother, Father and Child Cohort Study (MoBa) age-specific BMI instruments have enabled more detailed assessment of BMI from birth to 8 years in relation to later outcomes such as colorectal cancer and cholelithiasis.15 16 However, to our knowledge, no previous study has evaluated age-specific early-life BMI in relation to adult MRI-derived BMI-adjusted regional fat depots.

MR can strengthen causal inference by using genetic variants as instrumental variables, thereby reducing confounding and reverse causation under specific assumptions.17 In a life-course setting, univariable MR (UVMR) estimates the total effect of BMI at a given age on later outcomes, including effects that may operate partly through subsequent body size or other intermediate pathways, whereas multivariable MR (MVMR) can jointly model early-life BMI and adolescent BMI to estimate the association of early-life BMI with the outcome conditional on later body size.18 19 Therefore, a two-sample MR study was conducted using age-specific BMI instruments from birth to 8 years and adolescent BMI to investigate their associations with adult MRI-derived BMI-adjusted fat distribution traits (ASATadj, GFATadj and VATadj). We hypothesised that the associations would vary across developmental stages and that some later-childhood associations might persist after accounting for adolescent BMI.

Methods

Study design and reporting

A summary-level, two-sample MR study was conducted. In the first stage, UVMR was used to estimate the total association of BMI at each early-life time point with adult fat distribution traits. In the second stage, MVMR jointly modelled BMI at the early-life time point and adolescent BMI to estimate associations conditional on later body size. The study was planned and reported with reference to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) framework for MR studies.20 The overall design is shown in figure 1.

Age-specific childhood BMI traits were evaluated as exposures, adult MRI-derived BMI-adjusted ASAT, GFAT and VAT as outcomes, and adolescent BMI as the later-life body-size exposure in multivariable Mendelian randomisation. Data sources

Summary statistics for early-life BMI were obtained from MoBa, which provides GWAS data at 12 time points: birth; 6 weeks; 3, 6 and 8 months; and 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 5, 7 and 8 years.6 11 MoBa provides measured BMI data across detailed early-life developmental windows, enabling time-resolved assessment from birth to later childhood. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. After quality control in the source GWAS, BMI at each age-specific time point was z-score-transformed before genetic association analysis. Therefore, the genetic effect estimates for early-life BMI correspond to a 1-SD higher BMI at the relevant childhood age.

Adolescent BMI genetic association data were obtained from the Early Growth Genetics Consortium repeated-measures GWAS of BMI, including cohorts such as ALSPAC, CHOP, NFBC1966 and NFBC1986. The adolescent BMI area-under-the-curve phenotype was selected because it captures longitudinal BMI during adolescence and was suitable for representing later body size in the MVMR models.8

Outcome summary statistics were derived from UK Biobank MRI-based GWASs of adipose depots.5 The original study quantified VAT, ASAT and GFAT volumes in up to 38 965 genotyped participants of predominantly European ancestry. This GWAS provided direct measures of regional adiposity, whereas the BMI-adjusted traits allowed us to assess fat partitioning independent of overall adult body size. BMI-adjusted local adiposity traits were generated by regressing each depot on age, age squared, BMI and height in sex-stratified models and extracting residual variation. The present analysis used VATadj, ASATadj and GFATadj to evaluate regional fat distribution independent of overall adult adiposity.

The exposure and outcome GWAS datasets were derived from distinct cohorts, and material sample overlap was not expected. A summary of data sources is provided in online supplemental table S1.

Instrument selection and harmonisation

For each early-life BMI exposure, single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with BMI at genome-wide significance (p<5×10-8) were selected as instrumental variables. Independent variants were identified using linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping (r2<0.001; clumping window 10 000 kb). Across the 12 early-life BMI time points, the number of retained variants ranged from 1 to 14. For adolescent BMI, a more permissive threshold (p<5×10-7) was used to retain sufficient variants for multivariable modelling, followed by the same clumping procedure.16 21 22

For MVMR, variants associated with the early-life BMI exposure and adolescent BMI were combined, re-clumped and harmonised across exposures and outcomes so that effect estimates corresponded to the same effect allele. Palindromic variants were handled according to standard harmonisation rules implemented in the MR workflow.21

Mendelian randomisation assumptions and instrument strength

The main analyses relied on the three core instrumental-variable assumptions: relevance, independence and exclusion restriction (figure 1).20 The relevance assumption was evaluated using pre-specified SNP selection criteria, LD clumping and instrument-strength assessment. In the univariable setting, instrument strength was assessed for each SNP using the F-statistic, calculated as F = (βexposure/SEexposure) ², with values >10 considered to indicate sufficiently strong instruments.17 In MVMR, conditional F-statistics were calculated to assess exposure-specific instrument strength after accounting for adolescent BMI. The independence assumption was considered by using predominantly European-ancestry, covariate-adjusted GWASs where available and independent LD-clumped variants. The exclusion restriction assumption was evaluated through pleiotropy and sensitivity analyses, as described below.

Univariable and multivariable Mendelian randomisation analyses

For UVMR, the primary estimator was random-effects inverse-variance weighted (IVW) MR when multiple independent SNPs were available.23 When only one SNP was available, the Wald ratio was used.24 Effect estimates are reported as beta coefficients for the SD change in the adult fat distribution trait per genetically predicted 1-SD higher BMI at the corresponding age.5 6 11

MVMR was used to evaluate whether early-life BMI associations were independent of adolescent BMI. In this framework, adolescent BMI was treated as a later-life exposure rather than a conventional confounder. Accordingly, MVMR estimates represent associations of BMI at the earlier developmental stage conditional on adolescent BMI.18 19 Conditional F-statistics were calculated for MVMR models to evaluate exposure-specific instrument strength after accounting for the other exposure in the model. Conditional F-statistics were interpreted as adequate when ≥10, borderline when 5 to <10 and weak when <5. MVMR estimates with low conditional F-statistics were interpreted cautiously because of potential weak-instrument bias.22 Given the correlation between BMI measured at adjacent developmental stages, MVMR findings were interpreted as conditional genetic associations rather than definitive evidence of isolated developmental effects at a single age.

Sensitivity analyses

Where the number of variants was sufficient, sensitivity analyses included weighted median, MR-Egger, simple mode and weighted mode estimators.2527 Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran Q statistics, directional horizontal pleiotropy using the MR-Egger intercept, influential variants using leave-one-out analyses and directionality using MR Steiger testing.28 MR-Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier was used where applicable to detect potential pleiotropic outliers.29 Sensitivity analyses requiring multiple variants were not applicable to single-SNP exposure–outcome pairs.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in R V.4.5.1 using TwoSampleMR, MR, MVMR and related packages. Nominal statistical significance was defined as p<0.05. To account for multiple testing across age-specific exposures and three adiposity outcomes, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied. Both nominal and FDR-adjusted results are reported.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Instrument characteristics

Genetically predicted BMI at 12 early-life time points, from birth to 8 years, was evaluated in relation to adult MRI-derived BMI-adjusted fat distribution traits. In UVMR, the number of instrumental SNPs differed substantially across age-specific BMI exposures, ranging from 1 to 14. Unless otherwise stated, beta estimates represent the SD-unit change in the adult BMI-adjusted fat distribution trait per genetically predicted 1-SD higher BMI at the corresponding childhood age. Instrument characteristics and F statistics are summarised in online supplemental table S2.

Abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue adjusted for body mass index

For ASATadj, UVMR showed age heterogeneity. Higher genetically predicted BMI at 1.5 years was associated with higher adult ASATadj (IVW beta=0.123, 95% CI 0.044 to 0.203, p=0.002), and a similar association was observed at 2 years (IVW beta=0.182, 95% CI 0.061 to 0.303, p=0.003). At 5 years, when only one SNP was available, the Wald ratio suggested a positive association (beta=0.322, 95% CI 0.128 to 0.517, p=0.001). These associations remained significant after FDR correction. BMI at 8 years was nominally associated with higher ASATadj (IVW beta=0.166, 95% CI 0.036 to 0.296, p=0.012) but did not survive FDR correction (figure 2; online supplemental table S3).

ASAT, abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue; ASATadj, abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue adjusted for body mass index; BMI, body mass index; MR, Mendelian randomisation; p.ad, FDR adjusted p value; SNPs, single-nucleotide polymorphisms.

Gluteofemoral adipose tissue adjusted for body mass index

For GFATadj, no robust or consistent evidence of association was found across early-life BMI time points. Nominal findings were limited to sparse-instrument settings: BMI at 6 weeks showed a borderline inverse association based on a single SNP (Wald ratio beta=−0.232, 95% CI −0.465 to −0.000, p=0.050), whereas BMI at 5 years showed a borderline positive association (Wald ratio beta=0.193, 95% CI 0.000 to 0.386, p=0.050). No other time point showed a significant association in the primary analyses (figure 3; online supplemental table S4).

Univariable Mendelian randomisation estimates for age-specific early-life BMI and adult GFATadj. Estimates are beta coefficients per genetically predicted 1-SD higher BMI at the corresponding age. GFAT, gluteofemoral adipose tissue; GFATadj, gluteofemoral adipose tissue adjusted for body mass index; MR, Mendelian randomisation; p.ad, FDR adjusted p value; SNPs, single-nucleotide polymorphisms.

Visceral adipose tissue adjusted for body mass index

VATadj showed the clearest age-specific pattern. Higher genetically predicted BMI in later childhood was associated with lower adult VATadj. At 5 years, the Wald ratio suggested an inverse association (beta=−0.392, 95% CI −0.584 to −0.199, p=6.51×10−5), and at 7 years, the IVW estimate showed a robust inverse association (beta=−0.263, 95% CI −0.387 to −0.140, p=2.94×10−5). Both associations remained significant after FDR correction. BMI at 8 years was also nominally associated with lower VATadj (IVW beta=−0.262, 95% CI −0.476 to −0.049, p=0.016), but this did not remain significant after FDR correction (figure 4; online supplemental table S5).

BMI, body mass index; MR, Mendelian randomisation; p.ad, FDR adjusted p value; SNPs, single-nucleotide polymorphisms; VAT, visceral adipose tissue; VATadj, visceral adipose tissue adjusted for body mass index.

Multivariable Mendelian randomisation conditional on adolescent body mass index

After adolescent BMI was included in the MVMR models, most associations observed in UVMR attenuated substantially. For ASATadj, the positive associations observed at 1.5, 2, 5 and 8 years were no longer nominally significant. For GFATadj, the borderline associations at 6 weeks and 5 years also disappeared. For VATadj, the later-childhood inverse pattern was attenuated but not completely abolished: BMI at 5 years remained inversely associated with VATadj (beta=−0.254, p=0.029), and BMI at 7 years retained a nominally significant inverse association (beta=−0.208, p=0.046). However, the corresponding conditional F-statistics were low (2.60 and 2.67, respectively), indicating weak conditional instrument strength. These MVMR findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory conditional signals rather than definitive evidence of independent age-specific effects. The estimate at 8 years remained negative but was no longer statistically significant (beta=−0.147, p=0.142) (figure 5; online supplemental table S6).

Multivariable Mendelian randomisation estimates for early-life BMI and adult VATadj after conditioning on adolescent BMI. Estimates represent associations of BMI at the corresponding early-life time point conditional on adolescent BMI. BMI, body mass index; VATadj, visceral adipose tissue adjusted for body mass index.

Sensitivity analyses

Where sufficient variants were available, MR-Egger, weighted median and mode-based analyses were broadly used to assess robustness and possible horizontal pleiotropy. Sensitivity evidence was strongest for the 7-year BMI association with VATadj. Findings based on single-SNP instruments, particularly the 5- year VATadj association, should be interpreted as exploratory because pleiotropy-robust estimators were not applicable. MVMR conditional instrument strength diagnostics are incorporated into online supplemental table S6. Detailed sensitivity results are provided in online supplemental table S7 and diagnostic plots are shown in online supplemental figures S1–S5.

Discussion

In this life-course MR study, the associations between genetically predicted BMI across early life and adult BMI-adjusted fat distribution traits were clearly age-specific rather than uniform across development. Among the three adiposity outcomes examined, ASATadj showed positive associations at selected early-childhood time points, GFATadj showed no reproducible pattern and VATadj displayed the most consistent signal, with inverse associations emerging mainly in later childhood. After conditioning on adolescent BMI, most associations observed in UVMR attenuated substantially, suggesting that many of these signals are more likely to reflect continuity of body size across development than strong independent effects of adiposity at a specific early-life stage. By contrast, the inverse association between later-childhood BMI and adult VATadj persisted at nominal significance, particularly around 5–7 years, raising the possibility that this developmental window may be relevant to later visceral fat partitioning independent of overall adult adiposity.18 19 30

These findings support the view that BMI measured at different ages should not be treated as a single homogeneous exposure. Longitudinal and time-resolved genetic studies have shown that the genetic architecture of BMI changes markedly from infancy to later childhood and adolescence, with infant BMI differing from BMI in later developmental periods in both genetic determinants and correlation with adult body size.611 Our findings are concordant with this developmental framework and further suggest that the downstream consequences of higher BMI depend not only on magnitude but also on developmental timing.

The attenuation of most early-life signals after the inclusion of adolescent BMI is particularly informative from a life-course MR perspective. In this setting, UVMR estimates represent total effects, whereas MVMR helps distinguish earlier direct effects from those operating through later adiposity.18 19 The marked weakening of the ASATadj associations after conditioning on adolescent BMI therefore suggests that these signals are more compatible with long-term tracking of body size than with a strong developmental imprint established during infancy or early childhood. Given the greater expandability of subcutaneous adipose tissue, adult ASATadj may reflect cumulative adiposity burden across the life course more than a narrowly time-specific developmental signal.31 32

By contrast, GFATadj did not show a stable age-specific pattern. This may indicate that gluteofemoral fat partitioning is more strongly shaped by later pubertal maturation, hormonal influences and sex-specific developmental remodelling than by BMI variation during infancy or early childhood.2 3 It is also possible that BMI is an imperfect proxy for the biological processes most relevant to GFAT, particularly because GFAT has often been linked to a comparatively favourable metabolic profile.3 5 Accordingly, the null GFATadj findings should not be overinterpreted as evidence of no developmental relevance but rather as indicating that depot-specific biology may not be fully captured by age-specific BMI instruments alone.

The most notable finding was the inverse association between later-childhood BMI and adult VATadj. Because the outcome was BMI-adjusted VAT, this result should not be interpreted as implying that higher childhood BMI is beneficial overall. Rather among adults with similar overall body size, a higher genetic propensity to greater BMI around 5–7 years may be associated with a lower tendency towards preferential visceral fat accumulation later in life. This distinction is important because VAT is more strongly linked to adverse cardiometabolic traits and future disease than several other fat depots.1 2 33 34 Our results therefore appear to relate to fat partitioning rather than to the total burden of adiposity.

One biologically plausible explanation involves the developmental period around adiposity rebound. The 5–7-year window identified in our analysis broadly overlaps with the period surrounding adiposity rebound, which has long been associated with later obesity and metabolic risk.12 35 It is conceivable that BMI during this stage partly indexes inter-individual differences in adipose tissue development, storage capacity and partitioning efficiency. Under the adipose tissue expandability framework, individuals with greater capacity for relatively safe lipid storage may be less prone to later visceral or ectopic fat accumulation despite comparable total adiposity.32 36 From this perspective, a higher genetically predicted BMI in later childhood might mark a developmental tendency toward more favourable adipose tissue buffering, which subsequently manifests as lower VATadj conditional on adult BMI. This interpretation is biologically plausible and directionally consistent with prior work suggesting that childhood adiposity may show apparently favourable associations with selected adult metabolic traits after accounting for later BMI.37

Our findings also have implications for nutritional and developmental research. From a life-course obesity perspective, this study does not support the simplistic interpretation that higher childhood BMI is desirable; rather, it suggests that the timing of adiposity gain may carry biologically distinct implications for later body composition.12 33 38 This reinforces the importance of considering growth tempo and developmental trajectories, rather than relying exclusively on single cross-sectional BMI values, when evaluating long-term metabolic risk. The validity of these findings should be considered in light of the MR assumptions. The relevance assumption was generally supported by instrument selection and F-statistics, but the independence and exclusion restriction assumptions cannot be fully proven in summary-level MR. Therefore, we considered the multi-SNP UVMR association between BMI at 7 years and VATadj as the most robust finding, whereas single-SNP estimates and MVMR estimates with low conditional F-statistics should be interpreted as exploratory.

The study has several limitations. First, the number of instrumental variants differed across age windows, and some estimates, especially those at 5 years and 6 weeks, were based on a single SNP. These findings should therefore be considered exploratory. Second, in MVMR, some nominally significant conditional estimates, particularly those for VATadj at 5 and 7 years, had low conditional F-statistics. These findings may be susceptible to weak-instrument bias and should be interpreted as exploratory signals requiring confirmation in larger age-specific GWAS datasets or independent cohorts. Third, the major GWAS datasets were derived predominantly from European-ancestry populations, limiting generalisability to other populations. Fourth, although MR can reduce confounding and reverse causation, residual horizontal pleiotropy cannot be fully excluded. Fifth, the outcomes were BMI-adjusted adiposity traits; therefore, the results relate to regional fat partitioning conditional on adult BMI rather than to absolute fat mass or obesity burden. Sixth, because this study used summary-level MR, we could not directly account for pubertal onset, life-course weight change or exercise, all of which may influence adipose tissue deposition and modify adult regional fat distribution. Finally, the study provides genetic epidemiological evidence but does not include direct biological or independent clinical validation.

In conclusion, this study suggests that genetically predicted BMI across early life shows stage-specific associations with adult BMI-adjusted fat distribution traits. Most associations appear to reflect continuity of body size across the life course, while later childhood, particularly the association between BMI at 7 years and VATadj, may merit further investigation as a possible developmental period related to adult visceral fat partitioning. These findings support a life-course view of obesity biology, but should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of instruments for some age windows and the exploratory nature of the conditional MVMR estimates.
