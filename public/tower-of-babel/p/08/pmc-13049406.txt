May-Thurner syndrome disguised as pulmonary embolism: A case report
Authors: Amha Weldehana, Swati Chand, Syed Haidry, Hasan Ahmad, Arun Goyal, Junichi Shimamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn 80-year-old female patient presented with intermediate-risk pulmonary embolism with underlying etiology called May-Thurner syndrome—a contralateral right common iliac artery compressing the left common iliac vein against the lumbar vertebra and causing a cascade of endothelial changes, stenosis, and thrombi formation. It is common among young women with deep vein thrombosis or chronic venous insufficiency symptoms over their left leg. Diagnosis is confirmed by intravascular ultrasound examination. This patient was successfully treated through a multidisciplinary team approach, which included percutaneous mechanical embolectomy, inferior vena cava filter, stenting of the left common iliac vein, and systemic anticoagulation.Keywords: May-Thurner syndrome, Pulmonary embolism, Venous thromboembolism

May-Thurner syndrome (MTS), also known as iliac vein compression or Cockett's syndrome, is an under-recognized cause of venous thromboembolism (VTE).1 Iliac vein compression can occur at either side, but MTS results from compression of the left common iliac vein by an overlying right common iliac artery against the lumbar spine, leading to venous stasis, intimal hyperplasia, and thrombosis.2,3 MTS typically affects young women with unexplained chronic left leg pain, swelling, or deep vein thrombosis (DVT), but may be overlooked or misclassified as unprovoked DVT.4, 5, 6 Diagnosis is often incidental during imaging for unrelated indications.

We report a case of MTS in an elderly woman undergoing inferior vena cava (IVC) filter placement for extensive DVT and percutaneous mechanical thrombectomy for a pulmonary embolism (PE).

Case report

An 80-year-old woman with diabetes (hemoglobin A1c of 6.0%), hypertension, and chronic kidney disease (baseline serum creatine of 2.40 mg/dL) presented with 1 week of dyspnea. Three weeks earlier, she had been hospitalized for pneumonia and discharged in stable condition. She denied chest pain, cough, or leg swelling. Examination revealed only hypoxia requiring 2 L/min of nasal oxygen.

Laboratory studies showed normal blood counts, mild troponin elevation, and raised B-type natriuretic peptide. ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. Echocardiography revealed right ventricular (RV) dilation and strain with RV dilation-free wall akinesis with sparing of the RV apex (McConnell's sign), prompting a computed tomography angiogram, which confirmed a saddle PE (Fig 1), and intravenous heparin was initiated. Doppler ultrasound examination identified large left femoral and popliteal vein thromboses.

Multidisciplinary discussion was undertaken with a PE response team. Given her intermediate-risk PE and persistent RV dysfunction after systemic anticoagulation, the interventional cardiology performed percutaneous mechanical thrombectomy using 24F FlowTriever system, achieving near-complete acute clot resolution.7 Preprocedural and postprocedural right heart catheterization data are presented in the Table. To prevent recurrent embolization, an IVC filter was placed via the right femoral vein by vascular interventional radiology. Venography during IVC filter placement revealed extensive thrombosis and free-floating thrombus extending into the infrarenal IVC (Fig 2).

Vascular surgery performed mechanical thrombectomy of the IVC and left common iliac vein using a Penumbra CAT 16F catheter to prevent IVC occlusion and sequalae of postphlebitic syndrome.8 Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) examination was performed, which confirmed external compression of the left common iliac vein by the right common iliac artery and extensive thrombus—diagnostic of MTS (Fig 3, A).9 Balloon angioplasty and venous stenting with a 16 × 100 mm Zilver Vena stent of the left common ilia vein was performed (Figs 3, B and 4). The patient was transitioned to apixaban and discharged in stable condition. At 2 and 14 months follow-up, Doppler ultrasound examination confirmed stent patency of the left iliac and femoral veins.

Discussion

This case highlights a presentation of MTS in an elderly woman, manifesting as extensive DVT and PE.10 The incidental identification of left iliac vein compression during IVC filter placement was pivotal in uncovering the etiology of her thromboembolic disease, which otherwise might have been attributed to recent immobility.

Epidemiology and pathophysiology

MTS most often affects women aged 20 to 40 years, with anatomical compression in ≤25% of the population and symptomatic disease in 1% to 5%.11,12 This case underscores that anatomical venous compression can remain silent for decades and manifest later in life, particularly when additional risk factors for thrombosis coexist, such as decreased mobility or systemic inflammation. The underlying mechanism involves chronic pulsatile compression of the left common iliac vein by the right iliac artery against the lumbar vertebra. This persistent pressure causes endothelial injury, fibrosis, and intimal spur formation, which promote venous stasis and thrombogenesis.3,6,11,13 In our patient, these changes remained subclinical until provoked by acute illness and relative immobility, culminating in DVT and PE. This sequence reinforces multifactorial nature of VTE—where anatomical substrate such as MTS interacts with transient risk factors to produce clinically significant thromboembolism.

Diagnostic considerations

The diagnosis of MTS requires a high index of suspicion, particularly in patients with unilateral left-sided DVT, recurrent thrombosis despite anticoagulation, or unexplained chronic venous insufficiency. In this case, the absence of leg swelling and the patient's advanced age initially lowered suspicion.6,11,13 However, the finding of iliac vein stenosis on venography prompted further assessment with IVUS examination, confirming the diagnosis.

Although IVUS examination remains the gold standard, noninvasive imaging modalities have become increasingly important. Computed tomography or magnetic resonance venography can delineate venous compression, luminal narrowing, and collateral circulation. IVUS examination, with its high sensitivity (∼96%), is invaluable for both diagnosis and procedural planning, providing precise measurements of stenosis and guiding stent deployment.13,14 Doppler ultrasound examination, although limited for visualizing pelvic veins, remains useful for follow-up evaluation of stent patency and detection of recurrent DVT.

Management strategies

The management of MTS depends on the clinical presentation and symptom severity. Asymptomatic patients or those with incidental anatomical compression may be managed conservatively. However, symptomatic patients with DVT or PE require prompt and multidisciplinary management.15 Our patient's treatment involved coordination between the cardiology, interventional radiology, and vascular surgery teams.

Treatment goals include removing the thrombus, relieving the venous obstruction, and preventing recurrence. The therapeutic options range from systemic anticoagulation to catheter-directed thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy, angioplasty, and stenting.16 Although no randomized controlled trials directly compare anticoagulation alone with endovascular interventions, several case series and retrospective studies suggest superior long-term patency and symptom relief when mechanical obstruction is corrected.17 Ongoing clinical trials aim to clarify whether stenting provides a durable benefit over medical therapy alone.18

Mechanical thrombectomy vs thrombolysis

Mechanical thrombectomy performed in this case allowed rapid mechanical aspiration of embolic material without thrombolytics, thus reducing the bleeding risk. In ATTRACT trial (Acute Venous Thrombosis: Thrombus Removal with Adjunctive Catheter-Directed Thrombolysis), catheter-directed thrombolysis did not significantly decrease post-thrombotic syndrome rates or improve long-term outcomes compared with anticoagulation alone and was associated with higher bleeding complications.19,20 For elderly or high-risk patients, mechanical thrombectomy offers a safe and effective alternative, especially when performed in centers with multidisciplinary expertise.

Postprocedure follow-up and outcomes

After stenting, patients require risk factor modification and serial imaging to monitor stent patency. Patency rates vary widely, with primary patency reported between 32% and 98% and secondary patency between 66% and 96%.21 Early reintervention may be necessary for in-stent restenosis, thrombosis, or mechanical complications.21

Patients with venous stents for nonthrombotic venous lesions should receive anticoagulation; however, the optimal duration remains uncertain.22 Most clinicians recommend ≥3 to 6 months of anticoagulation, extended for patients with persistent risk factors or incomplete thrombus resolution. Direct oral anticoagulants such as apixaban are increasingly favored for their efficacy and convenience. Long-term success requires collaboration between vascular surgery and hematology, emphasizing the value of structured postprocedural surveillance.

Clinical implications

This case emphasizes several important clinical lessons. First, MTS can occur in older adults and should not be dismissed based on age alone. Second, incidental findings during unrelated procedures—such as IVC filter placement—can reveal significant anatomical contributors to thromboembolism. Finally, a coordinated multidisciplinary strategy ensures comprehensive management, from diagnosis through intervention and follow-up.

This case also underscores the evolving role of endovascular techniques in managing VTE. With advances in imaging and device technology, minimally invasive strategies such as mechanical thrombectomy and venous stenting are now practical, safe, and effective options even in elderly or high-risk populations.

Conclusions

MTS can present at any age and may underlie otherwise unexplained DVT or PE. Recognition of this condition is crucial, as addressing the mechanical compression in addition to anticoagulation reduces recurrence and improves outcomes.

This case illustrates that a multidisciplinary approach—combining advanced imaging, endovascular intervention, and systemic anticoagulation—offers excellent clinical results. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for MTS in patients with left-sided DVT or unexplained PE, regardless of demographic profile.
