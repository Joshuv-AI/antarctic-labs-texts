Naturalistic driving behavior among older adults with and without epilepsy: A pilot study
Authors: Jonathan P. Williams, Yiqi Zhu, Ganesh M. Babulal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Insufficient data exist for driving risk for people with epilepsy (PWE). This longitudinal, retrospective case-control study examines the differences in driving behaviors among older adults with/without epilepsy history using a novel naturalistic driving datalogger.Methodology:Eligible participants were cognitively normal ([CDR] = 0) or had mild cognitive impairment (MCI) ([CDR] = 0.5), age ≥ 65, self-reported epilepsy history, and had a global positioning system datalogger installed in their vehicle, which collected data every 30 s during driving trips. Driving data captured between January 2023 and January 2025, aggregated monthly, were included. Linear mixed-effects (LME) models assessed longitudinal driving behavior data as a function of epilepsy status using a matched (age, sex, and education) case-control design.Results:In this pilot study, epilepsy history had a significant effect on the y-intercept/baseline in models for driving parameters related to driving distance and speed. Longitudinal differences in LME models were shown for driving parameters associated with in-vehicle time, driving distance, and speed. PWE drove slower, less frequently (e.g., fewer trips), and spent more non-driving time in vehicles.Conclusion:Important differences in driving behaviors may exist between drivers with and without epilepsy. New, effective methods to unobtrusively measure longitudinal driving behavior of PWE are feasible and can improve clinical management at the patient level. More studies are needed to elucidate the relationship between specific driving behaviors and driving risk in PWE.

Introduction

Driving is a privilege and a primary means of transportation for employment, social engagements, and essential tasks that support independent living [1,2]. Older drivers are among the fastest-growing segments in the United States (US) population (25 % by 2050), and the rates of collision-related injuries and deaths rise in this demographic up to 20 times that of younger drivers due to health and age-related changes in cognition [3]. This group may face driving restrictions due to impairments (cognitive or functional) from neurological conditions like dementia or epilepsy. Epilepsy, a disabling disease characterized by recurrent spontaneous seizures, affects nearly 50 million people worldwide, with an incidence rate that steadily increases after age 50 years [4-7]. Epilepsy-related cognitive impairment (CI) results from both underlying brain pathology and the dynamic effects of seizures, chronic abnormal brainwaves (e.g., interictal spikes), or side effects from anti-seizure medication (ASM) use [8]. Seizures that impair awareness and induce convulsions cause functional impairment and are most associated with motor vehicle crashes (MVCs), but other subtypes may influence MVC risk [9]

Currently, driving fitness for PWE is assessed by the time since the last seizure, in the absence of other types of longitudinal data [9]. In the US, where driving restrictions irrevocably impact quality of life, most states require an average seizure-free interval of 3–12 months before drivers with epilepsy are eligible for driving privileges [9]. However, ~5% of 447 participants with epilepsy reported having seizures while driving before their epilepsy diagnosis, with 26.1 % reporting this seizure type as their first lifetime seizure [10]. This data casts a high beam on the clinical importance of driving safety in PWE and its blind spots. There is insufficient high-level data for driving risk counseling for people with epilepsy (PWE) [11]. This longitudinal, retrospective case-control study examines the feasibility of using a novel naturalistic driving datalogger to detect differences in driving behaviors among older adults with and without a history of epilepsy. The significance of new, effective methods to measure longitudinal driving behavior of PWE can improve clinical management at the patient level.

Methods

Participants

Prospective participants were enrolled in ongoing longitudinal driving studies at Washington University and the Driving Real World In-Vehicle Evaluation System (DRIVES) Project. The baseline visit included a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) assessment based on a standardized open-ended interview [12]. All participants were either cognitively normal ([CDR] = 0) or had mild CI (MCI) ([CDR] = 0.5), were aged ≥ 65, had a valid driver’s license, and drove at least weekly. Participants completed neuropsychological testing that resulted in a Preclinical Alzheimer Cognitive Composite (PACC) score, which is a more sensitive measure of early, subtle signs of cognitive dysfunction that can occur in the preclinical stage of AD before symptoms are noticeable. The PACC scores, although not specific to epilepsy, can give additional information on episodic memory, executive function, and global cognition, with lower scores indicating more cognitive dysfunction. Participants were administered the Driving Habits Questionnaire (DHQ), which is a clinical measurement of driving abilities based on self-report information from the prior year [13]. Participants agreed to have dataloggers installed in their personal vehicles as part of The DRIVES Project [14,15]. Study protocols were approved by the Washington University Human Research Protection Office. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants (n = 20) were identified from The DRIVES Project and divided into two groups, those with (n = 10) or without (n = 10) an epilepsy/seizure history based on self-report health history. Cross-validation of epilepsy history or features (e.g., seizure types, epilepsy subtype, last seizure date) with external medical records or diagnostic testing was not performed with data collection in the primary studies, and data were not available. No participants were excluded based on uncertain epilepsy history. The groups were matched on age, sex, and years of education. No participants with clinically diagnosed dementia were included in this study. Both groups underwent the same cognitive testing procedures despite their epilepsy history.

Driver monitoring and driving behaviors

A global positioning system data logger (Azuga Inc.) was plugged into a vehicle’s onboard diagnostics-II (OBD-II) port and collected data every 30 s during a trip. A “trip” was defined as data gathered from the period of ignition ON to ignition OFF. Trip details included date, distance, time spent traveling, and route taken. The DRIVES methodology has been published previously [14]. To accurately capture participants’ driving behaviors, each was given a unique Bluetooth beacon that registered with the data logger when nearby, which aided in isolating participant driving data from other drivers in their household using a shared vehicle, if applicable. There were no systemic issues with the datalogger or Bluetooth beacon function in the study period.

Aggressive (or risky) driving behaviors (e.g., speeding, sudden acceleration, hard braking) were also logged during each trip [16,17]. Speeding was recorded when the vehicle speed was at least 6 MPH higher than the posted speed limit. Longitudinal participant driving data, captured between January 2023 and January 2025 and aggregated at the monthly level, were included. Monthly data points were selected as the unit of time to increase sensitivity of detecting changes in driving behavior related to seasonality (weather, duration of daylight, etc.) while minimizing missing data points or quiescent driving periods. Despite the initial eligibility criteria (e.g., drives at least weekly), participants were not withdrawn from the study if they did not drive within a 7-day period given the heterogeneity in human behavior and need to focus of ecological data collection. The study team did not intervene to motivate participants to drive to avoid changing natural driving behaviors. Driving behaviors were categorized as related to speed, distance, or/and in-vehicle time. Due to rolling enrollment, the baseline start date/number of data points varied for participants.

Statistical analysis

Data collection and curation were managed in REDCap. Linear mixed-effects (LME) models assessed longitudinal driving behavior data as a function of epilepsy status using a matched case-control design. The model fixed effects included the predictors/covariates: epilepsy status, study month, age, sex, and an interaction term for epilepsy/study month. The model random effects included participant identification number and study month.

LME models generated the intercept and slope difference between groups with and without epilepsy history for each driving behavior parameter. The difference in intercept represented the main difference at the baseline between the two groups when controlling for demographic variables. To better approximate the contributions of each covariate in the model, the constant term was suppressed. The difference in slope represents the rate of change over time. Driving variable descriptions are located in the appendix. The LOESS nonparametric technique used local weighted regression (default 0.8 bandwidth) to fit a smoothed curve through data points, resulting in graphical representations of driving behavior models over time. Missing datapoints were not omitted. Additional analyses included a chi-square test of independence and a t-test of two groups. Statistical analyses were conducted in SAS with a significance level of 0.05.

Results

Participants were primarily female, well-educated, and White (Table 1). No MVCs occurred among participants in either group during the study period. Epilepsy history had a significant effect on the y-intercept (baseline) models for 15 driving variables. These driving parameters fell into two interrelated categories—driving distance and speed (Table 2). Distance-based parameters showed that the PWE group took fewer trips at baseline, controlling for demographic variables. Epilepsy history was a statistically significant predictor in the model for trip count (M = 123.43, SE = 104.69 vs M = 82.66, SE = 104.17, p = 0.007), total trip count between 1–5 miles (M = 38.84, SE = 52.08 vs M = 15.25, SE = 51.77, p = 0.001), trip count between 10–20 miles (M = 1.15, SE = 0.40 vs M = 0.97, SE = 0.39, p = 0.005), and total nighttime trips (M = 68.29, SE = 92.61 vs M = 34.24, SE = 91.57, p = 0.003). Speed-based driving parameters showed that the epilepsy group drove slower trips. For variables measuring speed (e.g., average maximum speed; M = 94.60, SE = 19.82 vs M = 86.84, SE = 19.61, p = 0.002) and compliance with posted speed limit (e.g., average speed when compliant with the posted speed limit; M = 78.89, SE = 18.30 vs M = 73.51, SE = 18.13, p = 0.03), epilepsy was a statistically significant predictor in the models.

The differences in slopes were significant between groups for three driving parameters characterized by in-vehicle time, driving distance, and driving speed (Table 2). Participants with a history of epilepsy significantly increased their in-vehicle time (e.g., idle time) compared to the control group (M = 0.56, SE = 0.59 vs M = 1.58, SE = 0.64, p = 0.03). Baseline idle time was not significantly different. The epilepsy group tended to drive longer trips over time. The distance-based parameter, trip count between 10–20 miles, showed longitudinal differences between the two groups (M = 0.00, SE = 0.01 vs M = 0.01, SE = 0.01, p = 0.02). This parameter alone was associated with significant differences at both baseline and longitudinally between the two groups (Fig. 1). The speed-based parameter, which measured the ratio of trips with speeding to trips with compliant speed, was significantly different between the two groups. This appeared most influential for those without epilepsy (M = −0.00, SE = 0 vs M = 0.00, SE = 0, p = 0.02), with older adults without epilepsy tending to have a higher ratio of trips speeding relative to trips not speeding.

Discussion

This retrospective, longitudinal pilot study outlines distinct differences in daily driving behaviors between older adults with and without a history of epilepsy, matched on age, sex, and education. Compared to controls, PWE drove slower and less frequently (e.g., lower speeds and with fewer trips) at baseline. Longitudinal data indicated differences between the two groups on a narrow set of parameters. While PWE generally drove less, they were more likely to take longer trips (e.g., trips between 10–20 miles) over time.

Intriguingly, PWE were significantly different from controls by engaging in less risky driving behaviors (e.g., fewer nighttime trips or less time speeding) primarily at baseline between the groups. There were no significant group differences in aggressive driving parameters that connote higher driving risk (e.g., hard braking, sudden acceleration, or hard cornering). In this study, PWE, who are eligible to drive, may be hypervigilant regarding driving safety. They inherently represent drivers at increased risk for harm from driving, but our results did not uncover any increased risky driving behaviors. Two DHQ domains—as surrogates for dependency on other drivers and confidence in driving—were not significantly different between the two groups at baseline. It is noteworthy that these individuals are not representative of all PWE, as their seizures are presumed to be well-controlled despite limited information on seizure type. These results still provide novel insights into actual driving behaviors, utilizing a passive, unobtrusive naturalistic methodology.

Several findings warrant further study. For example, PWE spent more idle time in their vehicles, which could be a marker of CI from seizures or seizure-related factors. Seizure-related factors (e.g., ASM) have been understudied in driving despite evidence of the adverse cognitive effects associated with ASMs [18]. Data on ASMs were not included in this study due to limited medical records on epilepsy; however, future prospective studies should be designed to investigate the role of ASMs in driving impairment among a robust group of PWE. Notably, the epilepsy group had lower PACC scores and a higher baseline CDR score, indicating the presence of either preclinical cognitive dysfunction or baseline MCI without prodromal dementia symptoms compared to the control group. Additional longitudinal studies with a robust group of PWE can increase the representativeness of the study and generalizability of the findings.

Since motor vehicles became accessible to the public in the 1890s, epilepsy was grouped with medical conditions posing an increased crash risk, and PWE were routinely excluded from driving [19]. The advent of more ASM in the 1940s created the possibility of seizure-freedom for PWE and, consequently, driving privileges [20]. Yet, estimates for increased risks for PWE vary across studies [9]. One study found that PWE were 1.13–2.16 times more likely to have a MVC than controls [2]. Another study of drivers presenting to the emergency department after an MVC found that PWE were 7 times more likely to be in an MVC than controls [21]. PWE are twice as likely to die within 24 h of a crash compared to drivers without epilepsy [10,22]. While only 0.2 % of deaths and up to 1 % of MVCs annually are ascribed to seizures, these figures account for drivers diagnosed with epilepsy at the time of MVC. Given that MVCs are rare for PWE, even in the literature, it is unsurprising that no MVCs occurred at all among the included participants. Driving behaviors have the potential to track vital changes in epilepsy and subsequent risk from disease-related sequela.

This study and others demonstrate the possibilities of passively obtaining high-quality longitudinal data by objectively monitoring the driving behaviors of key populations such as PWE [23-26]. Although participants did not report ongoing seizures, their driving behaviors were significantly distinct from the control group in some domains. Longitudinal monitoring via dataloggers can measure dynamic changes in longitudinal driving and contextualize the influence of medications and prognostic factors on driving safety. More prospective, longitudinal studies of drivers with seizures, using spatio-temporal, continuous data, are needed to identify important prognostic signs and inform proactive strategies to mitigate harm.

This study has several strengths, including an established longitudinal data collection methodology with repeated measures and robust baseline measures for all participants. There are some limitations. First, this pilot study has a small, non-random sample, which limits the power and generalizability. Selection bias influences who participate in a study (e.g., local driving restrictions or seizure control). The number of PWE among total prospective participants was low, which limits generalizability to other PWE. It will be of paramount importance to investigate these behaviors in other groups of PWE, considering age, sex, and other aspects of identity. As a first-of-its-kind study, no prior data existed to inform a priori effect size calculations. Epilepsy history was self-reported and did not include specific details of seizures that affect driving behavior/risk [27]. Furthermore, the inability to cross-validate or characterize epilepsy history status against objective data (e.g., medical records, diagnostic tests) is a limitation for subgroup analyses (e.g., focal versus generalized epilepsies) in this study. This retrospective analysis does not control for unmeasured, potentially relevant variables. For example, given variable study start times, we cannot robustly account for differences in driving behavior due to seasonality. In Missouri, it can be presumed that all PWE were seizure-free > 6 months before starting the study (local regulations), but we cannot exclude the possibility that participants had seizures during the study period but continued driving. Future studies with seizure diaries or other seizure detection devices could address this limitation.

Conclusions

This retrospective two-year longitudinal pilot study reveals distinct differences in the driving behaviors between two groups of older adult drivers with and without a history of epilepsy. As a group, epilepsy had a significant effect (p < 0.05) in LME models on baseline driving behaviors related to driving distance and driving speed. Longitudinally, changes were observed between the two groups across in-vehicle time, driving distance, and driving speed. Compared to controls, the epilepsy group appeared to drive slower, less often, and spent more non-driving time in vehicles. This study demonstrates the feasibility of obtaining high-quality longitudinal driving data in key populations such as those with epilepsy using passive monitoring. Future, inclusive prospective, longitudinal studies of drivers with seizures are needed to understand important prognostic signs and inform proactive strategies to enhance driver safety.
