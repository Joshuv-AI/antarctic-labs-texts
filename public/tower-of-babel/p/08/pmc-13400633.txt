Damage mechanisms in St. Bartholomeus Church under gravity, snow, and seismic loads
Authors: Memduh Karalar, Buse Sertkaya, Gülşah Çufalı, Barış Erdil, Fuat Korkut, Namık Yaltay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTürkiye is home to numerous historical masonry structures that reflect its rich cultural heritage. Understanding the structural behavior of such buildings under various loading conditions is crucial for identifying damage mechanisms and supporting effective conservation strategies. While previous studies have primarily focused on the effects of earthquakes, the impact of continuous vertical loads, such as gravity and snow, has been relatively overlooked. This study investigates the structural behavior of the St. Bartholomeus Church under static and dynamic loading conditions. After conducting material characterization and structural assessments, three-dimensional finite element model was developed using SAP2000 v20 and ANSYS v19 to identify the difference in the results. The structure was analyzed under its own weight, snow load, and seismic excitations to evaluate its response and identify potential sources of damage. The results indicate that the structure exhibits significant tensile and shear stress concentrations even under its own weight. When snow loads are included, these stress levels increase significantly, particularly in the roof and west wall. This suggests that damage could initiate under vertical loading conditions. Seismic effects could further exacerbate these effects and contribute to the progression of damage. These findings indicate that dead and snow loads can significantly contribute to the deterioration of abandoned historical masonry structures, and that these factors must be considered alongside seismic effects in structural assessments. Accordingly, structural reinforcement measures may be necessary to enhance stability and resilience, particularly in the event of a future earthquake.Keywords: Ancient masonry structure, Dynamic effects, FEM, Time history analysisSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Determining the structural safety of historical masonry buildings presents numerous challenges due to the materials used in their construction and the inherent characteristics of the structures themselves1–3. The design and construction of these buildings have been shaped by a combination of empirical knowledge and common industry practices4. Monumental and public buildings, particularly religious structures, exhibit distinctive architectural and engineering elements that have persisted to the present day. These structures were generally accepted by the majority of the population during the period of their construction, as well as through subsequent maintenance and long-term use. The resilience of these structures can be attributed to significant structural interventions carried out over the years.

Historic buildings are subjected to various adverse loads throughout their lifetimes. Extreme events such as earthquakes, landslides, floods, and conflicts can generate intense loads over short periods, while environmental effects such as snow accumulation, wind, freeze-thaw cycles, wetting-drying processes, and gradual load changes contribute to long-term deterioration5,6. The interaction between these short-term and long-term effects can lead to progressive damage accumulation and increased structural vulnerability.

Seismic behavior and vulnerability of historic masonry churches, addressing topics such as structural irregularities, material properties, and dynamic response characteristics have been studied by several researchers7–13. Numerical modeling techniques, particularly the finite element method, have been widely used to simulate the structural response of such buildings under seismic loading14–20. These studies have significantly contributed to understanding earthquake-induced damage mechanisms and developing appropriate retrofitting strategies. Recent studies have also employed advanced finite element modeling approaches to evaluate masonry structures under complex loading conditions, further emphasizing the importance of numerical simulations in understanding structural behavior21.

The mechanical behavior of masonry structures, according to the stress-free theory proposed by Heyman22, is generally characterized by negligible tensile strength. Within this framework, masonry structures primarily resist compressive forces, while tensile stresses cause cracking without contributing to the load-carrying capacity. In recent years, this concept has been expanded through advanced numerical formulations, including finite element applications capable of simulating stress-free behavior and crack development more realistically23–25. In contrast, the present study adopts a linear elastic modeling framework. Although this approach does not explicitly apply stress-free conditions, it allows for the identification of regions where tensile stresses exceed the material’s capacity. These regions can be interpreted as potential crack initiation zones. Therefore, while the results do not capture stress redistribution or post-crack behavior, they can be interpreted conceptually in a manner consistent with stress-free theory. Recent studies have also highlighted the differences between linear, nonlinear, and stress-free formulations in predicting masonry behavior by comparing various modeling approaches21,26.

Despite these advances, the role of non-seismic loads—particularly gravity and snow loads—as primary contributors to structural damage have not been sufficiently investigated. While most existing studies attribute damage in historic masonry churches primarily to seismic effects, the potential impact of continuous vertical loads is largely overlooked27–47. This shortcoming becomes particularly critical for abandoned structures, where a lack of maintenance allows cumulative damage resulting from environmental and gravity-induced effects to develop over long periods of time.

In this context, the St. Bartholomeus Church Complex, which represents a typical example of Armenian church architecture, provides an important case study. The structure, which has been abandoned for nearly a century, is located in a region characterized by severe climatic conditions, including both a high risk of earthquakes and significant snow accumulation. While previous studies and historical records generally attribute the structure’s current state of damage to past earthquakes, the relative contribution of gravitational and environmental loads has not been systematically evaluated.

Numerous studies have investigated the structural and seismic behavior of Armenian masonry churches using numerical and experimental approaches. For example, the Tekor Church in Eastern Türkiye was examined using FEM-based macro-modeling and time-history analysis, highlighting the vulnerability of such structures to seismic effects48. Similarly, the medieval Armenian church in Gazimağusa, Cyprus, was evaluated using a nonlinear modeling approach combined with in-situ dynamic characterization techniques, providing valuable insights into its structural response characteristics49. However, these studies primarily focus on seismic behavior and do not explicitly examine the role of continuous vertical loads, such as snow accumulation, in the damage development of Armenian church structures.

In addition to seismic effects, numerous studies have emphasized the importance of environmental and continuous loads—such as snow accumulation, freeze-thaw cycles, and long-term gravitational effects—in the deterioration of masonry structures. These effects can lead to progressive damage, reduce material strength, and significantly impact structural performance over time4,5,50–52. However, compared to seismic loads, the combined effects of gravity and snow load on historic masonry structures have received much less attention in the literature.

This study aims to address the gap in the field by investigating the structural behavior of the St. Bartholomeus Church Complex under gravitational, snow, and seismic loads using finite element modeling. Unlike traditional approaches that primarily focus on seismic performance, this study demonstrates that critical stress levels exceeding material capacity can develop not only under vertical loading conditions but also under gravity and snow loads, and highlights that these loads can play a significant role in damage development. Additionally, the interaction between pre-existing damage caused by vertical loads and subsequent seismic effects is examined. To achieve this, a detailed three-dimensional finite element model of the structure was developed using SAP2000 and ANSYS software, incorporating material characterization and realistic loading scenarios. The structure was analyzed under its own weight, snow load, and multiple earthquake records, enabling a comparative assessment of damage mechanisms under different loading conditions.

The structural assessment of historic masonry structures is typically conducted within internationally recognized frameworks, such as the ICOMOS/ISCARSAH recommendations and relevant guidelines on the conservation of cultural heritage53. These frameworks emphasize the importance of accurate modeling, material characterization, and the consideration of both short-term and long-term loading effects. In particular, they highlight the necessity of accounting for cumulative and continuous effects, which is directly relevant to the scope of the present study.

Properties of the Monastery of St. Bartholomeus

The St. Bartholomeus Monastery is situated on an elevated terrain, offering a panoramic vista of the expansive Zap Valley and located within the geographical confines of Albayrak village in the Başkale district of Van province in Türkiye. The structure is located at a distance of 23 km from the district of Başkale and 110 km from the province of Van (Fig. 1).

History and architectural properties

A plethora of conjectures have been advanced concerning the history of the structure. The precise origins of the monastery remain a subject of historical debate, with various sources attributing its establishment to either the fourth or fifth centuries. However, the first explicit reference to the monastery is documented in a thirteenth-century manuscript, thereby establishing its historical presence in the medieval period. However, this perspective is not universally accepted, as some historians have proposed an alternative interpretation. These scholars contend that the monastery was erected on the site of a pre-existing structure, thereby challenging the prevailing view. Notwithstanding the acceptance of the 13th century as a valid period, the Monastery of St. Bartholomeus, which is regarded as one of the most substantial monasteries of medieval Armenia, has endured to the present day, albeit in a state of considerable damage. Despite the monastery having been known by different names prior to the 13th century, it was first referred to as St. Bartholomeus in 1316. During the late 19th century, the monastery functioned as the administrative center of several villages, in addition to extensive pastures, fields, and forests. The site was of significant religious importance, serving as a prominent pilgrimage destination55–58.

To the west of the main monastery is the narthex (Fig. 2). The narthex is characterized by the presence of a skylight dome in its central area. West of the narthex, a tower with a small bell was situated. The façades of the monastery and the narthex were decorated with pilasters. On the western façade of the narthex was a large portal with a statue believed to be the Apostle Bartholomeus on horseback slaying a dragon. The monastery’s architectural composition consists of a church and a jamatun (church house55). Jamatuns, which are not seen in the religious architecture of Christians such as Byzantine, Georgian, Assyrian and Nestorian, are unique to Armenian architecture. There is a divergence of opinion regarding the origin, definition and functionality of these jamatun structures. Some researchers define jamatuns as a large hall added to church buildings. In contrast, some posits that the provenance of jamatuns can be traced back to the aesthetic of Islamic-Byzantine palace architecture.

Geometric properties

Externally, the monastery was surrounded by a perimeter wall that had the appearance of a fortress with high battlements and bastions. A small village settlement known as Deir developed around the buildings. Of the edifices constituting the monastery, solely the Church of Saint Bartholomeus and the jamatun, erected contemporaneously with the church, have endured to the present day, bearing the characteristic destruction of their upper covers. The remaining edifices have been completely destroyed. The upper cover had collapsed, and the interior was filled with the rubble and stones from the walls and the pavement. The rectangular jamatun and the church, which have a common façade on the exterior and measure 17 × 31 m. in the east-west direction, are divided into two parts by a wall built in the middle. The eastern section of the site is occupied by the church, while the western section is home to the jamatun. The architectural design of the building is delineated by a configuration of a closed Greek cross, accompanied by a modest upper covering that is situated upon the corner walls. The dome that covers the central square space is supported by wall buttresses that have been carried forward from the walls in the east, as well as pointed arches that rise on cruciform buttresses. These cruciform buttresses are connected by arches in three directions in the west. The dome was covered with yellow and black stone slabs in the form of a pyramidal cone. The central square space was extended in four directions by cross arms and covered with barrel vaults. In the interior, it was not possible to combine the four rectangular corner rooms with the main space, and the integrity of the latter was compromised. The easternmost section of the structure is characterized by a five-cornered apse, the depth of which is measured at 4 m. The apse is surmounted by a half-dome, and is illuminated by a diminutive crenellated window55–61. It is noteworthy that there are vaulted rooms built as a second floor above the apse and postaforium rooms. These vaulted sections have endured to the present day (Fig. 2).

The building complex consisting of the jamatun and the church is hereafter referred to simply as the church complex in this study. The thickness of the exterior walls on the east, north, and south sides of the church complex measures 2.10 m, while the outer wall on the west side is 2.60 m when viewed in its entirety. The dome drum is positioned at an elevation of 3.5 m and is equipped with four windows. The dome, situated at an elevation of 17.5 m above ground level, is designed as a dodecagonal pyramid cone clad in black and yellow stone plates externally. The construction is positioned on a stone platform, the height of which from ground level is 1.30 m. The entrance gate is 8 m high and decreases in height as one goes inward. The aforementioned crown gate, measuring 5 m in width, undergoes a narrowing as one progresses inward55–58. The primary wall of the church complex’s main section measures 9.2 m in height, with the total plan dimensions measuring 17 × 31 m. St. Bartholomeus Church Complex was constructed in accordance with the Closed Greek Cross plan. The architectural configuration of churches of the Closed Greek Cross type is characterized by their foundation on a rectangular plan, accompanied by the presence of a domed section, featuring a pendant passage at its core. These edifices predominantly repose upon four supports, thereby ensuring structural stability and integrity. The cross arms on the vertical axes of the dome are covered with vaults, and the corner units on the diagonal axes are covered with vaults or domes55–61.

Structural condition and seismicity

The church complex may have had several structural deficiencies related to material use and load carrying system. For example, the extensive repair covering the rebuilding of the wall and upper cover and placing of black and colored stones at the horizontal bands was stated to date back to 1633. The reason for the repair may be attributed to the earthquake since two extensive earthquakes, 1715 and 1860, were reported to affect the church complex significantly resulting in the collapse of the domes55–61. The repair and reconstruction of the damaged parts took many years. Some repair marks were visible from the pictures taken by58 in 1913 when the structure was still in use. As previously outlined, the church complex is afflicted with considerable structural deficiencies. This is primarily due to its prolonged disuse, spanning approximately a century, and the absence of any maintenance or repair initiatives. The church complex’s structural integrity has been compromised by several factors, including seismic activity, the harsh winter climate of the region, and the adaptation of its structural components for diverse purposes by the local populace. Additionally, there have been incidents of unauthorized treasure hunting activities, further exacerbating the church complex’s dilapidated state. The aforementioned issues have collectively resulted in the church complex’s current state of disrepair and dilapidation55,57.

As demonstrated in Figs. 2 and 3, the church complex’s current state is of significant concern. It is evident that the exterior cladding stones have been completely removed from the north-western façade wall of the church complex. Cracks and deterioration are observable extending from the roof to the foundation in this section, with a maximum width of 10 cm in certain areas. In this section, it was also found that the wall at the gate section, where the door was located, had completely collapsed or been deliberately dismantled. A subsequent examination of the east wall’s exterior façade revealed a crack that extended from the roof to the foundation in the north-eastern corner. Additionally, the stones at the foundation level of the building from south to north exhibited signs of damage and spillage in certain locations. Old repair marks, comprising black and different colored stones, are observed on the north wall. Furthermore, it was noted that there are fissures in this section, located towards the right corner, and that deterioration is evident at the foundation level. Besides, a diagonal crack was observable near the west corner. A thorough examination of the south façade revealed numerous issues, including cracks in the height of the wall, spills in the exterior cladding stones towards the east corner, horizontal cracks and collapses at the roof level in other sections, coating damage and material deterioration at the foundation level.

Current damage state of the exterior walls.

A thorough investigation into the damage conditions of the structural elements within the church complex revealed the presence of more significant structural issues. In the jamatun section, the dome has completely collapsed, and the arched load-bearing wall that separates the church from the jamatun and extends in the north-south direction has also collapsed. The arches extending in the east-west direction are in relatively good condition, but the outer coatings of the columns on which these arches rest have fallen. Furthermore, the roof arch on the west façade exhibits substantial deformations in the vertical direction, as illustrated in Fig. 4. An evaluation of the church section revealed that the roof, in the form of barrel vaults, had been completely destroyed, the cladding stones were missing, and the mortar had deteriorated significantly. This indicated a severe vulnerability of the walls that had survived. In addition, it was determined that the wall connections were fractured, and diagonal cracks were observable on the walls.

Material properties

Easily accessible limestone, basalt and other stones of volcanic origin were used as the main masonry materials for the construction of the church complex. As mentioned before, the cladding of the walls is made of smoothly cut stones, while the interior is made of rubble. Samples were taken and analyzed to determine the binding material.

FTIR analysis was conducted within the wavelength range of 450–4500 cm− 1 (with a resolution of 4 cm− 1) utilising the PerkinElmer One Spectrum model instrument. The XRD analysis was conducted utilising the RIGAKU D/max-2200 device, the purpose of which was to facilitate the identification of the crystal structure of the binding materials. Thermogravimetric analysis was conducted within the temperature range of 25 to 1000 °C using the Seteram/Labsysevo Thermo-ICE3000 Series device.

Following a thorough examination of the mortar samples obtained from various sections of the structure (Fig. 5), it was determined that the vibrations observed within the 1000–1100 cm− 1 range are attributable to the CSH configuration65. Vibrations within the range of 1250–1550 cm− 1 have been shown to be indicative of carbonate ions and calcite66. In addition, the bands observed at 712 and 873 cm− 1 have been identified as the characteristic bands of calcium carbonate67. It is assumed that the bands between 3668 and 3687 cm− 1 were formed due to the presence of moisture in the body, and that the bands between 2968 and 2992 cm− 1 are caused by various organic compound carbohydrates68. The presence of CSH structure is indicative of the use of natural pozzolan and slaked lime as hydraulic binders. The presence of CaCO3 may be attributable to two potential factors: firstly, carbonation within the structure, and secondly, the incorporation of limestone into the binder utilized.

FTIR spectra of the mortar samples from different regions of the building.

The results of the XRD and phase analysis demonstrate the presence of minerals including hydroxycancrinite, montmorillonite, halloysite, trabzonite, sodium magnesium silicate, aluminian, scawtite, and magnesite (MgCO₃), calcite (CaCO₃), and quartz (SiO₂). These findings indicate the presence of a variety of crystal structures (Fig. 6). The presence of calcite (CaCO3) and quartz (SiO2) has been demonstrated to support the FTIR results.

XRD patterns for the mortar samples from different regions of the building.

The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) results indicate that the investigated historical mortars are predominantly lime-based and exhibit varying degrees of carbonation depending on their location within the structure. The mass loss observed in the 600–900 °C range, associated with CaCO₃ decomposition, is consistently the most significant across all samples, with higher values in the foundation and exterior façades, suggesting more advanced carbonation due to prolonged exposure to environmental conditions. Conversely, lower CO₂ losses in the roof sample indicate relatively limited carbonation, likely due to reduced moisture interaction. The mass loss in the 120–400 °C range, attributed to dehydration of hydrated phases such as C–S–H, points to the presence of minor hydraulic components, possibly arising from pozzolanic reactions or the use of siliceous aggregates. Additionally, the relatively low Ca(OH)₂ decomposition peaks (400–550 °C) confirm that most portlandite has been transformed into calcite over time, which is typical for aged lime mortars. Slight variations in low-temperature mass loss (25–120 °C) further suggest differences in porosity and moisture retention among the sampled zones. (Table 2) Overall, the results reflect a heterogeneous material influenced by both original composition and long-term environmental exposure, consistent with findings reported in previous studies on historic lime mortars69–71. As demonstrated by TGA results (Fig. 7), the elevated incidence of reductions occurring within the temperature range of 600 °C to 800 °C, a finding corroborated by FTIR and XRD experimental results, indicates the presence of CaCO371,72.

Modeling

Masonry structures are inherently complex systems due to their heterogeneous material compositions and irregular load-transfer mechanisms. This complexity makes the numerical modeling of historic masonry buildings particularly challenging, especially when attempting to capture their overall structural behavior under varying loading conditions.

Due to the deteriorated condition of the structure, a full performance-based assessment is beyond the scope of this study. Instead, the focus has been on identifying load-induced damage mechanisms and critical stress zones. The objective of this study is to determine the loading conditions responsible for the observed damage and to identify key considerations for future reconstruction or restoration efforts. Many restoration or reconstruction projects are carried out without a comprehensive understanding of the structural fragility inherent in such historic structures2,4,12,13,19,75,76. Despite the countless adverse effects these buildings have endured over time, many have survived to the present day. However, the underlying causes of their structural damage often remain unclear. Therefore, it is crucial to understand the structural behavior of these buildings and their responses to different loading conditions. In this study, the church complex is analyzed in its assumed undamaged state, and three-dimensional numerical models are developed accordingly.

In this study, a macro-modeling approach was adopted in which masonry is represented as an equivalent homogeneous continuum. In this approach, masonry units and mortar are not modeled separately. Instead, they are idealized as a single material with average mechanical properties. This approach is widely used in large-scale structural analyses due to its computational efficiency. Although micro-modeling approaches can capture local failure mechanisms more accurately, they require detailed material characterization and significantly higher computational effort, making them less suitable for large and complex historic structures.

In order to provide an adequate depiction of the behavior, the church complex was modelled in three dimensions due to the intricacy of its shape. The modelling was carried out using ANSYS v1977 and the SAP2000 v2078 software, and the outcomes were then compared. The pedestal and dome portions of the church on the roof are treated as shell parts, while the load-bearing walls, arches, vaults, and roof are depicted as solid. The displacements of the church’s foundation nodes at ground level in the three axes (x, y, and z) were established as a fixed support while implementing the boundary conditions in the model. The x-direction is located through the longitudinal direction (east-west direction) of the church complex, as illustrated in Fig. 8. In a similar manner, the y-direction is aligned in the transverse direction, while the z-direction is aligned through the height. A total of 18,391 nodes, 10,648 solid elements, and 257 shell elements were employed in the modelling process.

Cross-sections of the church complex (east-west and north-south sections)58.

In ANSYS v1977, the accuracy of finite element method (FEM) predictions of stress and strain values is contingent on the mesh size and shape employed. In order to ascertain the appropriate mesh concentration in the St. Bartholomeus Church Complex, the mesh dimensions were modified, and each case’s studies were conducted again. Due to its reduced number of nodes and enhanced measurement dispersion across the FEM, the tetrahedron meshing option was selected79. The objective of this study was to evaluate the degree of separation of the contact surfaces between the components of the church complex. The efficacy of a face-to-face contact method, wherein the contact surface may vary, was also the subject of the study. This interaction is recognized as the phenomenon in which the outermost layer of one entity meets the outermost layer of another entity80. The establishment of a pair of interaction faces is achieved through the designation of the target component as the other face and the interaction component as the first77. To illustrate the interaction and departure between two faces, the CONTA-174 component and a corresponding TARGE-170 component are detailed for the church complex interaction interfaces77. The decision has been taken to establish a bond of contact between the structure and its members.

The church complex was modelled on the basis of the architectural drawings prepared by Bachman58 (Figs. 2 and 8) and the on-site measurements. The most arduous aspect of the modelling process pertained to the delineation of the interior structural system’s layout and its subsequent representation within the model. It is evident that the drawings under consideration and the on-site investigations fail to provide unequivocal identification of the structural system layout in the church section. In order to overcome this problem, photographs were used and 3D models as close as possible to the real structural features were created as shown in Fig. 9. Although ANSYS v19 is more capable of modelling the solid materials and connections (or interactions), relatively simple model (even though it has solid and shell sections) SAP2000 v20 was also utilized to understand the differences between the results.

3D models of St. Bartholomeus Church Complex.

During the modeling process, various simplifying assumptions have been adopted, including the alignment of wall intersections, the use of homogeneous material properties, the neglect of secondary structural elements such as small openings and small architectural components, and the assumption of linear elastic material behavior. Although these assumptions are generally used for computational convenience, their combined effect can influence the local stress distribution and the overall stiffness of the model. These simplifications may lead to a stiffer structural response and affect the magnitude and distribution of stress. However, they are not expected to alter the general trends in damage localization, which is the primary focus of this study. For example, neglecting openings may lead to an overestimation of stiffness, while assuming homogeneous material properties may mask local weaknesses. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with these limitations in mind. The analysis aims to provide a global understanding of structural behavior and damage trends rather than a precise prediction of local failure mechanisms.

It should be noted that this study adopts a linear elastic modeling approach, which represents a simplified model of the actual nonlinear behavior of historical masonry structures. Wall materials exhibit highly nonlinear behavior, particularly under tensile stresses, where cracking and stress redistribution occur at relatively low stress levels. Therefore, stress values exceeding the tensile strength should be interpreted not as indicators of collapse, but as regions where cracking is likely to begin. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as indicators of the onset of damage rather than ultimate failure. The primary objective of this study is not to predict collapse mechanisms, but to identify critical regions susceptible to damage under different loading conditions. In this context, linear elastic analysis provides a useful first-order approximation for comparing the relative effects of gravity, snow, and seismic loads. However, nonlinear modeling approaches (e.g., pushover or stress-free models) will provide a more realistic representation and are recommended for future studies.

The finite element model developed could not be validated against experimental or in-situ dynamic identification data due to the structure’s severely damaged condition. Reliable measurements of natural frequencies or material properties were not available for direct calibration. However, qualitative validation was performed by comparing the locations of high stress concentrations with observed damage patterns. Areas exhibiting high tensile and shear stresses align well with the actual damage zones observed in the structure, such as the roof, the west façade, and wall intersections. Furthermore, consistent results obtained from two independent numerical platforms (SAP2000 and ANSYS), while not replacing experimental validation, further support the robustness of the modeling approach.

Loading

In order to understand the reason for the damage the church complex was analyzed under gravity loading including self-weight and snow load, and gravity+lateral earthquake force. Below each loading was explained in detail.

Gravity loading

Since simplified macro modeling strategy was followed during the modelling process stone and mortar layers were combined and their mechanical properties were assigned to the equivalent solid elements. As given in Table 1 unit weight of a solid element in the model was taken as 19 kN/m3 which represents both stone and mortar. Therefore, self-wegiht of the structure was directly measured by the software.

The district of Başkale is characterized by high snowfall levels, which are attributable to its altitude of 2,400 m. It has been documented that the maximum snow thickness recorded in the district is 120 cm (Fig. 10). Consequently, TS498 asserts that Başkale is among the regions subjected to the highest snow load81. Given the district’s altitude, the snow load is estimated to be 1.84 kN/m2. Following the abandonment of the church, which occurred more than a century ago, the edifice fell into a state of disrepair and was no longer utilized. Consequently, during the winter months, snow accumulates on the roof, which persists until the advent of spring. In order to consider this situation, the snow load was increased to 2 kN/m2.

The church complex is subject to a constant cycle of freezing and thawing, as well as wetting and drying, due to the high number of days with snowfall, significant temperature variations, and ongoing precipitation during other seasons. The extent to which these effects damage mortar and stone materials remains to be ascertained. However, the church complex is being gradually and irreversibly damaged by constant and regular environmental impacts. In this study, environmental effects are disregarded; the focus is instead placed on the effects of vertical loads and horizontal earthquake loads.

Earthquake records and their characteristics

It is important to note that, in addition to the effect of the masonry elements’ self-weight, seismic activity may be a factor contributing to the damage. It can therefore be concluded that in order to differentiate between damage resulting from lateral and gravitational loads, an evaluation of the seismic performance of the structure must be conducted.

In this section of the research, earthquake data were selected and utilized to conduct dynamic analysis in the time history domain. In order to conduct a time history analysis of the structure, 11 seismic records were selected. The acceleration data were obtained from two sources: the AFAD Türkiye Acceleration Database and Analysis System (TADAS)83 and the Pacific Earthquake Engineering Research Center (PEER)84. The initial two seismic events occurred in close proximity to the church complex, which may have exerted a direct influence on the edifice. The church complex was initially analyzed under the gravity load, which incorporates the self-weight and the snow load, as illustrated in Fig. 12. It is evident that the regions where the tensile stress is intense are located on the roof cover, which is now in a state of disrepair and disintegration. The analysis indicated that the roof cover, which has not survived to the present day, is under high strains even under vertical loads (Fig. 12). The present condition of the component is illustrated in Fig. 13b. The analysis of vertical loads suggests that the damage to the roof cover is attributed to high tensile stresses, while the damage to the entrance is attributed to shear stresses. As previously indicated, the church complex encountered various structural issues throughout its existence. It has been documented that the structure’s substantial roof has experienced repeated instances of partial collapse, necessitating reconstruction in response to either seismic events or gravitational forces55,57. Consequently, the hypothesis that the roof’s failure was precipitated by the convergence of tensile and shear stresses, acting in conjunction with gravitational loads at the roof level, is being postulated. This is believed to have resulted in the observed damage to the cladding also.

In Fig. 14, the stress distributions of the church complex during the Van earthquake are demonstrated. It has been established that the standard stress distributions corresponding seismic forced acting in the x and y directions yield similar patterns. The stresses in both directions approach the tensile strength limit and are distributed uniformly throughout the entire structure. A subsequent analysis of the shear stress distributions reveals that the stress distributions on the east façade are similar, while the right bearing wall on the west façade is at the maximum shear strength limit when the earthquake acts in the y direction. The shear stresses were found to be generally similar to the vertical load results. Consequently, it was determined that the roof, which exhibited some loss of strength under vertical loads and suffered partial damage, having been exposed to an earthquake having similar characteristics as the Van earthquake, the damage had likely increased. Although the arch and vault structures within the building had suffered some damage from both vertical loads and an earthquake similar to the Van earthquake, the probability of total collapse was considered low. It was determined that the wall cladding on the west façade may have been damaged without the need for larger earthquakes. This was due to the fact that it was subjected to significant shear demands, both in vertical loads and earthquake loading on the outer surface.

Despite the church complex having not been exposed to an earthquake larger than the Van earthquake, which occurred in proximity to the church complex in 2011, other earthquake loadings were analyzed in order to be taken into account in the event of any restoration or reconstruction work. The objective of this study is to ascertain how the church complex would respond in the event of future major seismic events if restoration or reconstruction works were executed in accordance with the original design, without incorporating any structural enhancements. In order to comprehend this phenomenon, the church complex was subjected to analysis under a range of earthquake loadings. As the most deleterious effects were observed in the Düzce and Kahramanmaraş Pazarcık earthquakes, the following section presents information on the behavior of the two aforementioned earthquakes.

It has been determined that even when the structure is supported by its own weight, it experiences significant strain. The shear stress distribution on the exterior façade of the church, obtained from SAP2000 and ANSYS under the combination of earthquake load and gravity loads acting in the x direction of the Düzce Earthquake, is given in Figs. 15 and 16. As demonstrated by the figures, it can be posited that an earthquake exhibiting characteristics analogous to those of the Düzce Earthquake may have been a contributing factor to the collapse of the roof and the damage to the walls previously documented in55,57. Moreover, it can be posited that the adverse effect of the heavy roof and snow, when accompanied by an earthquake force, might have resulted in total collapse of the roof, since shear forces and tensile forces were found to be high at the roof level.

The comparable structural response obtained from SAP2000 and ANSYS indicates that the numerical models reasonably capture the system’s overall behavior. However, this agreement should be interpreted as an indication of consistency between the modeling approaches rather than as a direct validation of the results.

The roof of the church as well as the vault and arch structures within the church exhibit severe tensile strains when examining the distribution produced by the normal stress under gravity loading. This very high tensile stress is beyond the tensile strength. The load-bearing walls of the church experienced a moderate amount of tensile stress, although it did not surpass the limit value. This stress was found in the lower and middle sections of the walls which are similar to the present damage state (Fig. 19). Similarly, when the maximum shear stress distribution under gravity loading was examined, intense shear stresses occurred on the west wall close to north-west corner. The shear strength limit of the structure was exceeded in this region. It was demonstrated that the structure experiences very high strains even while it is supported by its own weight, and in some places, these extreme stresses surpass the material’s strength limitations. It is seen that there are similarities between the present state and the high stress areas in the model where stress concentration occurs. Since the church complex has been abandoned for more than a century, damage induced by gravity loading and/or earthquakes has not been repaired, leaving the structure vulnerable to environmental effects such as wetting–drying and freeze–thaw cycles, which may have further contributed to material deterioration5,6.

The results obtained from the numerical analyses should be interpreted in light of the modeling assumptions and limitations discussed in Chap. 3. In particular, the use of a linear elastic material model implies that the identified stress concentrations represent potential damage initiation regions rather than definitive indicators of structural failure. Accordingly, the findings of this study aim not to identify definitive failure mechanisms but to highlight the relative effects of different loading conditions on the structural response. In this context, the results indicate that gravity and snow loads may play a significant role in the development of structural damage, particularly when combined with long-term environmental effects.

Conclusion

This study provides a detailed examination of the evaluation of the structural performance of St. Bartholomeus Church Complex through both static and dynamic analyses. A finite element model of the structure was developed using ANSYS v19 and SAP2000 v20, and its response under gravity, snow, and seismic loading conditions was investigated. Based on the findings of the study, the following conclusions can be drawn:

The analysis of maximum normal stress under self-weight indicates the presence of significant tensile stresses in the roof vault. Accompanying snow loads which reaches considerable level in the region, this high tensile tension exceeded strength limits.

Due to its massive roof system and large span (17 m), the structural configuration of the vaults and arches appears to be vulnerable under sustained loading conditions. This is evidenced by the numerous instances of damage and collapse that occurred during the church complex’s operational period. Even under its own weight, the structure experiences elevated tensile and shear stresses that exceed the material’s strength in certain regions.
