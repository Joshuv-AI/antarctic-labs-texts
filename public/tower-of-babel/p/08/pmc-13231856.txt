A scoping review on cardiometabolic risk factors among Pacific Island populations and implications on future research in the region
Authors: Tristan V C Paulino, Bwenaua Biiri, Elizabeth Matisoo-Smith, Anna L Gosling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndigenous populations in the Pacific are disproportionately burdened by cardiometabolic diseases compared to other populations, yet understanding of the underlying factors contributing to these health disparities remains limited. A combination of genetic and non-genetic risk factors contributes to cardiometabolic disease outcomes, but current reviews involving Pacific Island peoples are limited by geographical and demographic constraints. To address this, we conducted a scoping review that identified risk factors, with an emphasis on genetic factors associated with cardiometabolic diseases among Pacific Island populations (Polynesians, Melanesians, and Micronesians). We used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses framework for scoping reviews, utilizing MEDLINE (Ovid) and Web of Science (all databases). From March 2023, we identified population-based studies published in the last decade. A total of 1887 abstracts were screened, 114 full-text articles were reviewed, and a final 58 articles were included in our review. Within the last decade, genetic studies outnumbered non-genetic studies. Obesity, diabetes, and gout were the most prominent conditions researched. Melanesian and Micronesian populations were underrepresented in cardiometabolic disease research compared to Polynesians. The combination of non-genetic and genetic research has implications for developing equitable Pacific-centric interventions and treatment in the future.

Introduction

Non-communicable cardio-metabolic diseases rank among the top leading causes of death worldwide. When stratified by ethnic group, Pacific Island populations are disproportionately affected by cardio-metabolic diseases compared to other populations. Within Aotearoa New Zealand, the prevalence of gout among Māori and other Polynesians (many of whom were migrants from other Polynesian islands) is double that of non-Māori and non-Pacific communities.1,2 Within the US territory of Guam, CHamoru, and other Micronesians have higher rates of diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular disease than US national rates.3‑5 The Melanesian islands of the Solomons and New Caledonia continuously rank among the highest prevalence of type 2 diabetes worldwide.6 Comprehending prevalent variables involved in cardiometabolic disease risk is thus necessary to address health inequities in the Pacific region.

Historically, changes in dietary habits and lifestyle resulting from westernization during the early to mid-20th century have been regarded as the main drivers of high burden of metabolic disease within the Pacific.7 These changes consisted of a shift from traditional dietary norms of fruits, root crops, and seafood to diets often rich in highly processed red meat, saturated fats, and refined sugars.8 In addition, a more active lifestyle transitioned into a sedentary one. As a result, previous research primarily focused on non-genetic factors contributing to metabolic disease. However, the development of genomic technology and the resulting decrease of sequencing cost over the last 2 decades have created the opportunity to understand disease burden from a genetic perspective worldwide.9‑11 As such, more evidence has indicated a genetic component to these diseases. Within Pacific Island populations, genetic variation in single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) has been identified as influential on disease status across different metabolic conditions.12,13 Additionally, genetic variants that are amplified among Pacific Island populations and rarely found in other populations have been identified to show a combination of risk and protective effects across different conditions.14

Previous reviews in this space have been limited to either highlighting mostly behavioral or lifestyle variables (ie, non-genetic risk factors)15 or highlighting research within Polynesian populations (ie, Samoan, Maori, Tongan, etc.).16 They do not grasp the current scope of genetic and non-genetic research among other Pacific Island peoples such as Micronesians or Melanesians. Thus, a pan-Pacific review would provide a better understanding of cardiometabolic disease across all Pacific Island peoples. To address the circumstance, our objective was to conduct a scoping review to identify and describe the current work with a particular emphasis on genetic research associated with metabolic diseases among all Pacific Island populations in the last decade.

Methodology

The literature review team consisted of 2 independent reviewers. A subject librarian from the University of Otago consulted with the review team on developing the key terms and search strategy. The review methodology followed the scoping review guidelines for the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyses (PRISMA) framework for scoping reviews.17 Compared to a traditional systematic review, a scoping review is a method that more broadly assesses the literature and knowledge gaps of a specific topic and question without assessing biases from individual articles. This review aimed to identify adult population-based studies (cross-sectional, case–control, and cohort) identifying risk factors of cardio-metabolic diseases among Pacific Island populations. Pacific Island populations or Pacific peoples were defined as individuals who self-identified as belonging to a population originating from an island group, a part of the three oceanic groups of Polynesia, Melanesia, and Micronesia, as shown in Figure 1. Studies from countries with prominent Pacific Island populations such as the United States were considered. The list of metabolic diseases included in our study is based on the list of metabolic diseases listed by Merriman and Wilcox16 such as obesity, cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, kidney disease, and gout. Additional conditions like dyslipidemia and hypertension were also included in the current review as they were deemed by the research team as prevalent metabolic issues among Pacific Island populations as observed in the literature.18,19

Cardiometabolic research across the Pacific region.

Starting March 2023, the review team inputted the list of search terms (Table S1) into MEDLINE (Ovid) and Web of Science (all databases) databases. Terms included a list of all Pacific Island chains, countries, and territories, along with all metabolic conditions previously described. Searches were limited to English-only original research articles published in the last 10 years—January 2013 to March 2023. Reviews, ethnographies, and testimonials were excluded from the respective search engines. Duplicate articles were removed using EndNote. Collected articles were uploaded to Rayyan.ai, an online bibliographic tool used to assist in the review process.20 Each reviewer in the team independently scanned titles and abstracts to determine eligibility for further full-text review. Disagreements about eligible articles were resolved through discussion. Upon completion of the initial scan of article abstracts and titles, a full-text review of the filtered articles was conducted. Articles not found within our criteria after full-text review were excluded. Articles in our review that were previously identified in Merriman & Wilcox16 were included.

Results

As described in Figure 2, 1886 articles were collected from our initial search with 236 of them from MEDLINE and the remaining 1651 from Web of Science (all databases). Around 52 duplicate articles were removed. About 114 articles were further identified based on the review of the abstract and title. A total of 58 (100%) articles were selected after a full-text review with 16 (33.3%) articles for obesity, 12 (25.0%) articles for diabetes, 12 (25.0%) for gout, 1 (2.1%) for cardiovascular disease, 1 (2.1%) for hypertension, 5 (10.4%) for dyslipidemia, and 1 for kidney disease (2.1%) as seen in Table 1. Fifteen articles were not detected in our search, but articles from prominent research groups in this space were recommended by the review team. Genetic risk factors among Pacific Island populations

Obesity

Obesity is a complex condition characterized by excess body fat accumulation, closely linked with metabolic conditions like diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and hypertension. Genetic research has focused on the CREBRF genetic variant rs373863828, with the minor A allele associated with higher BMI, body fat, abdominal and hip circumference, and lipid levels.14 Despite this link to increased adiposity, rs373863828 is paradoxically associated with lower odds of diabetes and gestational diabetes. This trend holds across multiple Pacific populations including Micronesians in Guam and Saipan,21 Tongan,22 Māori,23,24 and Native Hawaiians.25 Notably, each additional A allele corresponds to a 1.4 kg/m2 increase in BMI.14

Research on the pleiotropic effects of rs373863828 has suggested that the variant may promote insulin secretion rather than improve insulin sensitivity.26 A meta-analysis of Samoan and Māori/Polynesian cohorts showed both direct and indirect effects of the A allele on type 2 diabetes and fasting glucose, mediated through its positive effect on BMI.27 Further studies have explored the impact of rs373863828 on body composition.28 For example, studies in Samoans found an association between rs373863828 and height but no links to body fat percentage or visceral adiposity.29 However, a connection to muscle mass was observed, suggesting tissue-specific effects.30

Genetic studies outside of CREBRF have focused on variants previously linked to obesity in other populations. The FTO locus, a major player in global BMI studies, showed no significant effect on BMI in Pacific Island populations due to small sample sizes.31,32 A larger meta-analysis in Aotearoa New Zealand and Samoan cohorts using Bayesian modeling confirmed no significant relationship between FTO rs9939609 and BMI.33 The ADRB2 locus, associated with energy expenditure, showed some association with obesity in Tongan and Solomon Island communities.34,35 Functionally, the rs34623097 variant may affect BMI, but its molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. As with other loci, the impact of ADRB2 variants has shown variability across different populations.36 Thus, population-specific genetic variations, such as CREBRF, may play a more significant role in Pacific obesity than shared variants with other populations.

Diabetes

Type 2 diabetes is characterized by elevated blood sugar levels resulting from the body’s impaired ability to utilize or produce insulin effectively. Impairment of insulin has significant consequences in carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism. Identified behavioral factors mainly derive from multi-ethnic cohort studies based in Hawaii. Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander is the technical term for categorizing all Pacific communities in these studies. Other Pacific Islanders in this category primarily comprise Polynesians (Samoans, Tongans, etc.) and Micronesians (CHamoru, Chuukese, Marshallese, etc.).37

Genetic studies in diabetes outside CREBRF have been fairly limited in scope but have focused on identifying the proportion of Pacific ancestry as an exposure on diabetes outcome. CREBRF articles identified in our review were grouped in the obesity article count and not reflected in the diabetes article count. However, this is not indicative of undermining the connection of CREBRF to both conditions. In the Hawaiian multi-ethnic cohort, a 10% increase in Polynesian/Hawaiian genetic ancestry corresponds to an 8.6% and 11.0% increase in diabetes risk. This study also pinpoints variants congregating in the chromosome 6 region (rs370140172) as distinctive Hawaiian variations associated with diabetes status.38 However, this study failed to come to similar findings when replicated on a smaller Samoan cohort.

A cross-sectional study documented in a conference proceeding revealed that a greater proportion of CHamoru and Micronesian ancestry was linked to higher odds of diabetes.39 Though these studies offer valuable insight into risk among Pacific communities, they also caution against the risk of exclusively using ancestral proportions as markers for disease risk. Such oversimplification could have far-reaching consequences, from inadvertently reinforcing racial stereotypes to influencing medical decision-making and treatment disparities based on perceived genetic predispositions.

There is a single gestational diabetes study involving the multi-ethnic cohort, which found a protective effect from SNPs MTNR1B and SLC30A8 on Native Hawaiian and other Pacific Island women.40 MTNR1B encodes melatonin receptors, and carriers of the rs10830963 allele exhibit heightened expression of MTNR1B in pancreatic beta cells, leading to compromised insulin secretion.41 However, further research is necessary to understand the potential metabolic pathways of these specific variants.

Gout

Gout is a chronic condition characterized by joint inflammation caused by monosodium urate crystals, which form due to elevated serum urate levels (hyperuricemia).42 This inflammatory response is driven by the NLRP3 inflammasome, which produces proinflammatory cytokines.43 Most investigations into gout among Pacific Island populations were initiated within Polynesian communities residing in Aotearoa, New Zealand, as epidemiologic evidence indicated ethnic-specific disparities in gout prevalence.2

From a genetics perspective, a few hundred loci have been found to have an effect on serum urate with a fraction of those influencing gout.44 Common genetic variants, including those in ABCG2, SLC2A9, SLC16A9, GCKR, SLC2A11, SLC2A12, SFMBT1, VEGFA, and PPARGC1B have been replicated in Pacific Island populations.45‑51 Other loci such as TLR4, PPARGC1B, CARD8, IL1B, PDZK1, MICA, and CD14, which are also associated with gout risk and serum urate, are more connected with the inflammation pathway.52‑54 Mitochondrial DNA genetic variation was also found to increase gout risk among Māori and other Polynesians.55 One study outside of New Zealand reported relative frequencies of some of these variants among some Native Hawaiians56—but no downstream association analyses were done.

There is variation in the effects of these loci on populations. Variants like SLC2A9 rs16890979 increase gout risk similarly in both Polynesians and Europeans.47,57 However, TLR4 rs21493 increases gout risk in Europeans but offers protection in Polynesians.58 ABCG2 rs2231142 increases risk among Western Polynesians but not Eastern Polynesians.45 Population-specific variants associated with gout risk have been identified in Polynesians. The P1036L variant of ABCC4 reduces uric acid transport and increases gout risk.59 Likewise, rs752113534 in IL-37 was associated with an increased risk of gout in hyperuricemic Polynesians.60 A variant in APOC3 elicited a protective effect on gout status.61 Alcohol consumption interacts with the GCKR rs780094 and influences gout risk.62 Specifically, alcohol exposure eliminates the genetic risk effect of GCKR on gout among Europeans in Aotearoa, New Zealand. In Polynesians, no significant interaction between alcohol and gout risk or hyperuricemia. Research on the interaction between genetics, environment, and epigenetics in gout is ongoing.

Hypertension

Genetic work in this space has identified rs5049 in the angiotensinogen (AGT) locus with hypertension among Melanesians residing in the Solomon Islands.63 Angiotensinogen is a critical component of the renin–angiotensin system, which influences components of blood pressure, including blood vessel contraction, sodium and water balance, and cell development in the heart.64

Dyslipidemia

Like our gout section, these genetic studies have focused on identifying common genetic variants with specific lipid biomarkers such as total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and triglycerides.65 A GWAS study from a Samoan cohort has identified risk variants in multiple loci such as APOA1, CETP, and APOE which negatively affect profiles like triglycerides, LDLs, and HDL.66 Similarly another locus of interest was BTNL9.67 Another study utilizing data from a Samoan cohort and Māori/Polynesian cohort from New Zealand found a variant (rs1597000001) in the CTEP locus that is associated with serum lipid change (higher HDL, lower LDL) and a potential protective effect on cardiovascular disease.68 CETP modifies lipid levels by mediating the bidirectional transfer of cholesterol esters from atheroprotective HDL-C to atherogenic very low-density cholesterol in exchange for triglycerides.68 Each minor allele copy of rs1597000001 contributes to higher HDL by 0.24 mmol/L and lower LDL by 0.13 mmol/L. Additionally, this variant was shown to decrease CETP activity by 27.9%.68 This variant elicits a very large effect as compared to other SNP variants. Lower levels of CETP have been associated with decreased cardiovascular disease risk. While CETP inhibitors have not definitively reduced cardiovascular risk in clinical trials, recent analyses suggest potential cardiovascular and metabolic benefits with long-term CETP inhibitor use.

A cross-sectional study in Tonga identified the ADRB2 risk variant rs34623097 as associated with increased triglyceride levels with each copy of the A allele increasing serum triglyceride by 0.79 mmol/L.35 There was no observed effect of the allele on other lipid parameters such as total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, or low-density lipoprotein cholesterol. No other studies have found similar results in other Pacific communities or populations.

Non-genetic risk factors among Pacific Island populations

Most behavioral studies within the last 10 years utilize data derived from ongoing cohort studies in Hawai’i, Samoa, and Aotearoa New Zealand and mostly highlight behaviors that have previously been identified to influence metabolic disease status in other populations. In the Multi-Ethnic Cohort, longer sleep duration (>9 h) was weakly associated with increased diabetes risk,69 while moderate chocolate consumption (up to four times a week) showed a protective effect among Native Hawaiians.70 Extended sleep duration may be indicative of potential indicative of other health complications that can be linked to impaired glucose metabolism and insulin resistance,71 while flavonoids have been shown to enhance glucose metabolism and reduce oxidative stress.72 Meanwhile, in the Aotearoa New Zealand Polynesian cohort, dietary triggers for gout included alcohol73 and tomatoes.74 Notably, tomato consumption was identified as a novel dietary trigger for gout flares among New Zealand Whites and Polynesians.75 Additionally, ferritin levels76 and high intake of sugar-sweetened beverages (four servings daily) were strongly associated with gout.77 Sugar-sweetened beverage consumption elicited similar effects on obesity among Marshallese populations residing in Arkansas78 with self-efficacy toward exercise also influencing BMI.79 Higher cellphone use correlated with elevated BMI in ni-Vanuatu.80 Data from the Samoan cohort showed that a mixed modern diet (ie, diet consisting of processed foods and some traditional island foods like seafood and root crops) had measurable impacts on lipid profiles. Specifically, it was associated with increased triglyceride levels and elevated HDL cholesterol,81 indicating that dietary changes from westernization affect metabolic health in complex ways.

Beyond diet, environmental variables and cultural practices also seemed to influence metabolic outcomes. Based on geo-spatial data, proximity to fruit and vegetable stores was associated with lower diabetes rates in New Zealand,82 possibly reflecting underlying socioeconomic dynamics and access to resources. Kava, a traditional beverage made from Piper methysticum, seemed to have an increased effect on BMI among ni-Vanuatu women but a decreasing BMI effect ni-Vanuatu men.83 With the limited research and sample size in this ni-Vanuatu study—additional research should be conducted to understand sex-specific effects observed from kava use. In the same study, tobacco use was also linked to a lower BMI—which has also been found in previous studies in other populations. Within Native Hawaiians, methamphetamine use was associated with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction and heart failure.84 Social deprivation, which is the absence of social interaction, support, or participation in normal aspects of society, significantly increased the odds of kidney disease among Polynesians in New Zealand.85

Discussion

Over the past decade, genetic studies (n = 38, 65.5%) have outpaced non-genetic ones (n = 20, 34.5%) in the Pacific, as seen in Table 1. Three key factors have driven this increase in genomic research: the significant disease burden in these communities, the availability of more affordable genetic sequencing techniques, and the discovery of disease-risk effects from common and population-amplified genetic variants. While genetic variation in metabolic disease is a non-modifiable risk factor, increased knowledge can inform targeted interventions using precision medicine. However, precision medicine–based interventions for metabolic conditions have yet to be realized86 and accessible to ethnic minorities, including Pacific Island populations, and remain an ongoing challenge.87 A major obstacle behind such efforts, in particular, is the lack of replication among the identified studies. With the exception of the CREBRF variant, no other variants connected to any condition have replicated findings consistently across different Pacific communities. This may be either due to a lack of comprehensive data among Pacific Island populations or that the effect of these genetic variants is truly different between groups. Differences in genetic effects may be due to the variation in population structure among Pacific Island populations, which is sometimes not accounted for in previous studies.

Cardiometabolic research has notably underrepresented Melanesian and Micronesian populations, accounting for only 15.5% of total research articles (Table 1). Some studies involving these groups are often grouped under broader “Other Pacific Islander” categories in Polynesian studies, such as those in Hawaii88 and Aotearoa New Zealand. This underrepresentation has limited our understanding of the unique genetic, cultural, and environmental factors affecting these populations’ cardiometabolic health. Given the close historical, ethnolinguistic, and cultural ties among Pacific peoples,13,89 these underrepresented communities may share genetic predispositions or possess genetic variation that influences disease risk differently.

Obesity, diabetes, and gout have received the most attention in research (n = 40, 83.3%), while other metabolic conditions such as hypertension, kidney disease, and dyslipidemia remain understudied. As these conditions also pose significant issues in the region, more research attention is needed to address them.

Enhancing participation from underrepresented Pacific communities in research faces challenges stemming from historical practices in Indigenous research.90,91 These issues often arise from researchers from high-income nations outside of the Pacific region conducting studies in low- to middle-income regions without ensuring that local communities benefit.92 To address health disparities, current research models must prioritize equitable outcomes and ensure that data is protected and owned by the communities.92,93 Indigenous-focused ethical research frameworks have emerged to promote equitable outcomes, focusing on transparency in consultation, culturally sensitive methods, community involvement, and capacity building for Indigenous scholars.94 Investing in academic institutions within the Pacific with emphasis on areas with low- to middle-income countries (Figure 1, Table S4) is essential for enhancing research capacity, community engagement, and managing data repositories at the local.91 Larger academic institutions engaged in research in the Pacific or that partner with academic institutions in the Pacific should share responsibility for investing in these communities to address cardiometabolic disparities in the region.

Limitations

This review excluded oceanic areas beyond the 3 specific geographical regions in the Pacific. However, it is worth noting that further exploration of these areas may be warranted, particularly considering the high burden of metabolic diseases. This review did not discuss the comorbidities of metabolic diseases, metabolic syndrome, or their underlying molecular mechanisms. The limited availability of information on the molecular mechanisms contributing to the manifestation of these comorbidities and their interplay presents an ongoing challenge and should be considered in future reviews.95

Due to time and resource constraints, we were only able to analyze the metabolic disease research and Pacific Island populations within a decade’s time span, utilizing 2 databases (MEDLINE and World of Science). Our review may have missed informative studies prior to 2013—which may have influenced the proportion of non-genetic studies identified in our review. The incorporation of additional databases looking at a larger timeframe in our analysis may have yielded additional results. Although it is possible, such articles would have been previously discussed in prior reviews,15,16 and that we would see a similar proportion of research being done between Pacific Island populations. A PRISMA scoping review checklist can be found in Table S5.

During our review, we utilized the terms Melanesian, Micronesian, and Polynesian to categorize the diverse regional populations within the Pacific. Nevertheless, it is crucial to recognize the limitations in applying these terms. It is important to note that these regional designations, while providing a broad understanding from a biomedical perspective, should also be contextualized, as many Pacific communities have a stronger identification with their specific island chain of origin.96,97
