Dynamic single‐cell sequencing unveils the tumor microenvironment evolution of gastric cancer abdominal wall metastases during radiotherapy
Authors: Qianqian Mao, Zhenzhen Wu, Yonghong Lai, Ling Wang, Qiongzhi Zhao, Xi Xu, Xiansheng Lu, Wenjun Qiu, Zhihua Zhang, Jiani Wu, Gaofeng Wang, Rui Zhou, Jianhua Wu, Huiying Sun, Na Huang, Xiatong Huang, Luyang Jiang, Yiran Fang, Yuyun Kong, Li Liang, Jianping Bin, Yulin Liao, Min Shi, Wangjun Liao, Dongqiang Zeng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough the combination of immunotherapy and radiotherapy (RT) for the treatment of malignant tumors has shown rapid development, the insight of how RT remodels the tumor microenvironment to prime antitumor immunity involves a complex interplay of cell types and signaling pathways, much of which remains to be elucidated. Four tumor samples were collected from the same abdominal wall metastasis site of the patient with gastric cancer at baseline and during fractionated RT for single‐cell RNA and T‐cell receptor sequencing. The Seurat analysis pipeline and immune receptor analysis were used to characterize the gastric cancer metastasis ecosystem and investigated its dynamic changes of cell proportion, cell functional profiles and cell‐to‐cell communication during RT. Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescent staining and bulk RNA sequencing were applied to validate the key results. We found tumor cells upregulated immune checkpoint genes in response to RT. The infiltration and clonal expansion of T lymphocytes declined within tumors undergoing irradiation. Moreover, RT led to the accumulation of proinflammatory macrophages and natural killer T cells with enhanced cytotoxic gene expression signature. In addition, subclusters of dendritic cells and endothelial cells showed decrease in the expression of antigen present features in post‐RT samples. More ECM component secreted by myofibroblasts during RT. These findings indicate that RT induced the dynamics of the immune response that should be taken into consideration when designing and clinically implementing innovative multimodal cancer treatment regimens of different RT and immunotherapy approaches.Keywords: gastric cancer, immunomodulation, radiotherapy, single cell sequence, tumor microenvironment

INTRODUCTION

It is generally considered that gastric cancer (GC) patients with high‐risk factors for locoregional recurrence after radical D2 resection, such as insufficient dissection distance from tumor margin, vascular tumor thrombus, perineural invasion, and N3 or metastatic lymph node ratio >25%, are the potential beneficiaries of postoperative radiotherapy (RT). 1 In the nonmetastatic setting, several important trials evaluating the efficacy of chemoradiotherapy (CRT), scheduled either before or after surgery, have been recently reported or are currently ongoing. 2 , 3 Radiotherapy is also a useful tool in alleviating some clinical symptoms of late‐stage GC and can improve the patients' general condition and quality of life. 4 Palliative RT is a quick, inexpensive, and effective way for metastatic cancer to reduce tumor size and symptoms, which can improve quality of life with limited side‐effects. 5 , 6 However, the insight of how RT induces metastatic tumor microenvironment (TME) dynamic changes remained unclear. Moreover, RT has shown synergistic effect on immunotherapy by enhancing the host immune response and inhibiting cancer cell immune escape in preclinical studies. 7 , 8 The results of several clinical trials investigating RT–immunotherapy combinations are also encouraging. 9 , 10 , 11 Despite this promising clinical progress, the time of introduction and the type of immunotherapy is still not well understood.

It is well‐documented that radiation not only inactivates and kills cancer cells through extensive DNA damage but also provokes dual effects on the TME. Ionizing radiation can activate vascular endothelial cells and triggers immune responses through the increased production of cytokines, chemokines, and cell adhesion molecules that induce immune cell recruitment and extravasation. 12 , 13 However, it can also induce cancer‐associated fibroblast (CAF) senescence and enhance tissue remodeling, 14 infiltration of immunosuppressive cells, 15 expression of immune checkpoints (e.g., PD‐L1), and consequently T cell exhaustion and tumor immune tolerance. 16 Furthermore, previous studies have revealed the significant role of TME in prognosis and immunotherapy efficacy for patients with GC based on the analysis of a large number of clinical samples. 17 , 18 Thus, understanding the response of tumor cells and TME to RT would provide valuable insights into the processes underlying a successful response to cancer treatment.

Recently, the utilization of single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) combined with T‐cell receptor sequencing (scTCR‐seq) in studying TME has provided valuable insights into the complex responses of the tumor ecosystem to treatment. 19 , 20 However, this powerful technique has yet to be applied to longitudinally elucidate the effect of RT on tumor cells and TME during treatment. 21 , 22

In this study, we collected four paired biopsies of abdominal wall metastasis tumors from a patient with GC before treatment, on treatment, and after fractionated RT. Through scRNA‐seq and TCR‐seq analysis, we characterized the GC metastasis ecosystem and investigated its dynamic changes of cell proportion, cell functional profiles, and cell‐to‐cell communication during RT in order to shed some light on improving the RT‐immunotherapy combination procedure.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient enrollment and sample collection

The adult patient enrolled in the study was diagnosed with GC and underwent laparoscopy‐assisted total gastrectomy with D2 lymphadenectomy in 2020 along with adjuvant CRT (oxaliplatin and capecitabine). The postoperative pathological sections were diagnosed with poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma of the stomach. The pathological diagnosis was T4a, N2, M0, and stage IV. Re‐examination 10 months after the operation indicated that the tumors had metastasized in the peritoneum and lower right abdominal wall by PET/CT. However, following 14 courses of palliative chemotherapy (carilizumab, apatinib, and irinotecan), another lower left abdominal wall mass appeared and was confirmed by biopsy and diagnosed as gastric adenocarcinoma by pathology. The chemotherapy regimen changed to albumin paclitaxel. Unfortunately, imaging examination showed multiple bone metastases throughout the body and an enlarged abdominal wall mass. In order to prevent further enlargement of the abdominal wall mass and improve the life quality of life for the patient, palliative radiation therapy to 45 Gy (15 fractions over 3 weeks) was given. Four pairs of tissue samples were obtained from the lower left abdominal wall metastasis site by biopsy at four distinct time points: 7 days before RT, and 7 days, 15 days, and 26 days after the first day of RT. The patient received three courses of immunotherapy and targeted therapy (pembrolizumab and lenvatinib) after RT. Nevertheless, the disease still progressed. Paraffin sections from tumor tissue samples were collected for immunochemistry and immunofluorescence.

Preparation of single‐cell suspensions

Tumor tissues were cut into pieces (<1 mm3) in DMEM (Gibco) with 10% FBS (Gibco) and digested using a MACS tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec) for 30 min on a rotor at 37°C. The digested mixture was filtered using a 70 μm cell strainer (BD Falcon) in DMEM to obtain dissociated cells. Dissociated cells were centrifuged at 330 g at 4°C for 10 min. After removing the supernatant, cells were treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (Solarbio) for 15 min on ice. After washing twice with PBS, cells were resuspended in sorting buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% FBS). Finally, cells in 10 μL suspension were counted using an inverted microscope and a hemocytometer. Cell viability was assessed using 0.1% Trypan blue staining.

Single‐cell RNA‐seq library and TCR‐seq library preparation and sequencing

Single cells were run on a 10x Genomics Chromium Controller Instrument, and barcoded single‐cell RNA‐seq libraries were prepared using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Kit version 2 from 10x Genomics to generate single‐cell gel‐beads in emulsions (GEMs). Within GEMs, barcoded cDNA was produced through reverse transcription, and GEMs were then broken. The remaining cDNA was fragmented, end‐repaired, A‐tailed, ligated with adaptors, and amplified by PCR. Amplified cDNA was then used for both 5′ gene expression library construction and TCR V(D)J targeted enrichment was carried out with the Chromium Single Cell V(D)J Enrichment Kit, Human T cell (10x Genomics), followed by V(D)J library construction. For RNA‐seq library construction, amplified cDNA was fragmented and end‐repaired, double‐sided size‐selected, PCR‐amplified with sample indexing primers, and double‐sided size‐selected. For TCR‐seq library construction, TCR transcripts were enriched from amplified cDNA by PCR. Subsequently, enriched PCR product was fragmented and end‐repaired, size‐selected, PCR‐amplified with sample‐indexing primers, and size‐selected. Libraries prepared according to the manufacturer's user guide were then purified and profiled for quality assessment. Finally, every library was sequenced using a NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform (Illumina). Raw data were processed using CellRanger (version 6.0) to generate gene‐barcode matrices. The RNA‐seq and TCR data analysis methods, including quality control, integration, clustering, and downstream bioinformatic analysis, are described in Appendix S1. It is worth mentioning that the signature gene list for T/natural killer (NK) cells, 23 myeloid cells, 24 fibroblasts, 25 and endothelial cells have been previously described (Tables S1–S4).

Bulk RNA‐seq data download and processing

Bulk RNA‐seq data for locally advanced rectal cancer pre‐ and post‐therapeutic (neoadjuvant CRT) resections were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under accession number GSE94104 and GSE190826. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was undertaken using the GSVA R package 26 to estimate pathway activity of samples. Signature scores for samples were calculated using the calculate_sig_score function in the IOBR package. 27 The EaSIeR R package 28 was adopted to the evaluation of cellular interactions based on ligand–receptor pair and gene expression patterns. Bulk RNA‐seq datasets for GC (Asian Cancer Research Group [ACRG]/GSE62254, GSE15459, and The Cancer Genome Atlas Stomach Adenocarcinoma [TCGA‐STAD]) were obtained from the GEO database and TCGA (UCSC Xena; https://gdc.xenahubs.net/). The Kaplan–Meier method was used to generate survival curves for the subgroups in each dataset, and the log‐rank (Mantel–Cox) test was used to determine if they were statistically different. The hazard ratios for univariate analyses were calculated using a univariate Cox proportional hazards regression model.

Immunocytochemistry

The immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was carried out according to the standard protocol procedure. The biopsy tissue samples were paraffin‐embedded. Sections (5 μm thick) were deparaffinized by xylene and rehydrated. Heat‐mediated antigen retrieval prior to staining was followed by cooling at room temperature for 30 min. Following antigen retrieval, samples were incubated with primary Ab overnight at 4°C. The next day, the sections were incubated with secondary Ab for 1 h and diaminobenzidine, followed by hematoxylin staining. The sections were scanned using SQS‐120P (TEKSQRAY). The following primary Abs were used: anti‐CD3 (1:1000, Cat. ab16669; Abcam), anti‐CD4 (1:1000, Cat. ab133616; Abcam), anti‐CD8 (1:1000, Cat. ab237709; Abcam), anti‐HLA‐A (1:500, Cat. ab52922; Abcam), and anti‐collagen type 1 alpha 2 chain (COL1A2) (1:1000, Cat. a5786; Abclonal).

Immunofluorescence staining

Biopsy specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature followed by dehydration and paraffin embedding. Serial sections were cut as 5 mm and hydrated before staining. Sections were subjected to immunofluorescent (IF) staining by overnight incubation at 4°C with Abs raised against C D44 (1: 200, Cat. 60224; Proteintech), EpCAM (1: 1000, Cat. 21050; Proteintech), and COL1A2 (1:1000, Cat. a5786; Abclonal). The antigen retrieval was carried out in citric acid solution (pH 6.0). Fluorescent images were acquired on Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk normality test tested the normality of the variables. The unpaired or paired Student’s t‐test were used to estimate the significance of difference between two groups. Statistical significance for normally distributed variables, and non‐normally distributed variables were analyzed by the Mann–Whitney U‐test (Wilcoxon rank sum test). For comparisons of more than two groups, the Kruskal–Wallis and one‐way ANOVA tests were used for nonparametric and parametric methods, respectively. The correlation coefficient was computed by the Spearman and distance correlation analysis. All statistical analyses were carried out using R version 4.2.1 (https://www.r‐project.org/), and the p values were two‐sided. p values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The adjusted p value for multiple testing was calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction.

RESULTS

Case history

A patient with GC was treated at Southern Medical University Nanfang Hospital for postoperative recurrence and metastases throughout the body. A more detailed description about disease progression and treatment regimen are shown in Materials and Methods. The lower left abdominal wall tumor metastasis of the patient received palliative RT of 45 Gy in 15 fractions of 3 Gy each in 3 weeks without additional treatment. We obtained biopsy samples from the same abdominal wall metastasis tumor site at four distinct time points: 7 days before and 7, 15, and 26 days after the first RT session, termed the pre‐RT, post‐RT‐1, post‐RT‐2, and post‐RT‐3 tumor samples, respectively (Figure 1A). In the comparison of abdominal CT images before and after RT, there was the regression trend of pre‐existing tissue mass on the lower left abdominal wall, however, a new tissue mass on the lower right abdominal appeared (Figure S1A). Serum tumor markers showed increased cancer antigen (CA)24‐2 and CA19‐9 concentrations after RT, hinting that local RT did not prevent the progression of disease (Figure S1B). Nevertheless, we found that there were almost no tumor cells in the last biopsy sample through pathological examination (Figure S1C).

Single‐cell transcriptome identified GC abdominal wall metastasis landscape dynamics during RT

For each biopsy specimen, we isolated single cells without prior selection for cell types and used the 10x Chromium platform to generate scRNA‐seq data and scTCR‐seq data. In total, 20,236 cells were collected, and data from 15,093 single cells were further analyzed after quality control, with an average of 2514 genes, 10,022 unique molecular identifiers, and only approximately 3.5% mitochondrial genes per cell detected (Figure S1D–F). The number of cells from each biopsy is provided in Table S5. Unsupervised clustering revealed cell clusters representing nine major cellular lineages according to common cell type genes (Figure 1B and S1G). All nine clusters were present across pre‐, and post‐RT‐1, ‐2, and ‐3 abdominal wall metastasis samples (Figure 1C). We next analyzed the changes in the main cellular composition of specimens during RT (Figure 1D). Consistent with clinical presentation, epithelial cells were almost eliminated after the completion of RT. The proportion of myeloid cells displayed an elevated trend during RT, indicating the recruitment of myeloid cells and their infiltration into the tumors while the proportion of T cells substantially declined during treatment. In addition, we noticed the large influx of neutrophils after 3 weeks of RT, possibly associated with acute tissue inflammation caused by cumulative radiation dose. Consistent with the scRNA‐seq results, T cells including CD4+ cell and CD8+ cells were enriched in the Pre‐RT sample (Figure 1E). Together, our results described the original cellular composition of GC abdominal wall metastases site and the dynamic changes in their response to RT.

Diverse RT sensitivity existed among epithelial cell subtypes

Next, we focused on the alteration of epithelial cells during RT. We extracted data of epithelial cells for further clustering, which yielded three subclusters, named Epi_1, Epi_2, and Epi_3 (Figure 2A). We observed that the proportion of the Epi_2 and Epi_3 clusters continuously decreased but the proportion of the Epi_1 cluster increased significantly during the treatment (Figure 2B,C), suggesting different RT sensitivity of epithelial cell subtypes, and the Epi_ 1 cluster seemed to be resistant to RT. Our results also showed that all epithelial clusters were copy number‐unstable, which usually characterized tumor cells (Figure S2A,B). Heatmaps illustrated the top differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each subcluster (Figure S2C). Pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs based on Gene Ontology, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactom databases indicated that: (1) focal adhesion, cadherin binding, and ECM interaction characterized Epi_1; (2) aerobic metabolism signaling pathways were hallmarks of Epi_2; and (3) DNA replication and a high S/G2M ratio were typically seen in Epi_3 (Figures 2D–F and S2D), which was consistent with the results of Hallmark gene signature score evaluation (Figure S2E).

We further examined the regulatory network of epithelial cells. The activity of TFAP2C and FOXC1 regulons were remarkably higher in Epi_1, whereas the activity of E2F1, E2F8, and EHZ2 regulons related to cell cycle was remarkably higher in Epi_3 (Figure S2F). Cell trajectory analysis of tumor cells identified the transition starting from Epi_3 to Epi_2 and then to Epi_1, which corresponded to the real time (Figure 2G–I). Along the pseudo‐time, proliferating markers, including MKI67, TOP2A, TK1, and PCAN were significantly downregulated while apoptosis‐related genes such as ATF3, BMP2, CDKN1A, and EMP1 and cell adhesion genes, involving LAMB3, LAMC2, and NECTIN4 were significantly upregulated in the cell fate ending (Figure 2J). In addition, the expression of CD44 marked stem cells was downregulated in overall epithelial cells during RT regardless of clusters, which was verified by IF staining of paired biopsy samples (Figure 2K–M). In keeping with pseudo‐time results, GSVA of tumor hallmark signals showed upregulated expression of apoptosis and apical junction pathways and downregulated expression of glycolysis and E2F targets pathways in irradiated tumor cells (Figure S2G). It has been reported that CD44 is the key modulator of glucose metabolism molecule in cancer cells. 29 Furthermore, RT can induce the upregulation of immune checkpoint gene expression in tumor cells as an immune escape mechanism. 30 In our study, the expression of LGALS3, CD47, CD276, LGALS9, and VSIR showed a continuous increased trend in response to RT (Figure S2H). Together, on one hand, RT inhibited cell proliferation and glycolysis and promoted apoptosis to kill tumor cells. On the other hand, the remaining tumor cells enhanced apical junctions, cell adhesion, and immune evasion in response to radiation damage in our study.

Radiotherapy induced cytotoxic gene expression in NKT clusters but impaired T response

T cells and NK cells are most renowned for their cytotoxic activity. In our data, we undertook unsupervised clustering of all T cells and NK cells, and based on gene markers of cell types we identified 10 clusters: four CD4+ T cell clusters, three CD8+ T cell clusters, one NKT cell clusters, and two NK cell clusters (Figure 3A,B). Each cluster was observed across four samples (Figure 3C). CD4_c1_ IL7R clusters expressed high levels of naive markers, such as IL7R and CCR7; CD4_c2_HSPA1A was characterized by high heat shock gene expression (HSPA1A and HSPA1B), and stress response signature. The regulatory T cell (Treg) cluster showed a classical Treg gene signature FOXP3, CTLA4, and TIGHT (Figures 3D and S3A). For CD8+ T cells, we found that CD8_c1_GZMK showed elevated expression of C‐C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5), a canonical marker of effector‐memory T cells (TEMs) and downregulated activation signature. The CD8_c2_IFNG cluster was identified with activated TEMs for GZMA, GZMK, CCL4, CCL5, and IFNG positive with high effector function and TCR signaling (Figures 3D and S3A). The metallothionein family of genes including MT1X, MT2A, and MT1E are relevant to the dysfunctional phenotype and the state of inactivation, and are highly expressed in both CD4_c2_MT1 and CD8_c3_MT1 clusters. The NKT_FGFBP2 and NK_c2_FCGR3A cluster expressed a clear cytotoxic signature (Figure S3A), whereas the NK_c1_XCL1 cluster showed high expression of XCL1 and XCL2 (Figure 3D). By examining the paired TCR repertoires in all T cells, we found that CD8+ T and NKT cells contained more shared clonotypes (Figure S3B,C).

Subsequently, we studied changes of T/NK cells in the relative abundance and expression of gene signatures following RT. The proportion of NKT_FGFBP2 and NK_c2_FCGR3A clusters presented an increased trend in 2 weeks of RT (Figure 3E); it has been reported previously in cervical cancer and pancreatic cancer that RT induced the accumulation of CD16+ (encoded by FCGR3A) NK cells. 19 , 31 In addition, the score of cytotoxicity of all T cells was upregulated after 3 weeks of RT, particularly in the NKT_FGFBP2 cluster, whereas the score of exhaustion was downregulated at the early stage of RT (Figures 3F,G and S3D,E). Moreover, the expansion of TCRs relatively decreased in the first 2 weeks of RT, but then recovered to and exceeded the pretreatment levels after the completion of RT (Figure 3H), which was in contrast to the trend of TCR diversity calculated by the Shannon index (Figure 3I). In addition, the clonal overlap was obviously low between the untreated T cells and irradiated T cells from either time point (Figure 3J). These findings suggested that the majority of tumor‐resident T cells were depleted during radiation and subsequently replaced by circulating T cells after RT, which was recently reported in irradiated brain metastasis. 21 Taken together, we found that RT induced NKT/NK subclusters to express genes encoding cytotoxic molecules, which demonstrated the activation of innate immunity whereas T cell responses were impaired during the RT.

Radiotherapy induced infiltration of C1q+ macrophages with enhanced proinflammatory capacity

We next explored the myeloid cells' landscape and their dynamic changes during RT. Based on the expression of marker genes, we categorized the myeloid cell population into five heterogeneous subclusters: two subtypes of macrophages and two populations of dendritic cells (DCs) and one monocyte subtype (Figure 4A,B). Each cluster was observed across all samples (Figure S4A,B). Regarding macrophages, the Mac_c1_C1QB cluster charactered by C1Q complement genes and highly expressed APOE, HLA‐DRB1, MRC1, FOLR2, and TREM2 (Figure S4C,D), was identified as C1q+ macrophages, which correlated with T cell exhaustion and poor prognosis in numerous cancers. 32 The Mac_c2_FABP5 cluster expressed genes encoding free fatty acid transporters such as FABP4 and FABP5, and lipid receptors LGALS3 and TREM2 (Figure S4C,D), similar to lipid‐associated macrophages defined by Liu et al. 33 We further quantified M1 and M2 gene signature scores in macrophage subclusters and found that they showed coexistence of M1 and M2 functional phenotypes (Figure S4E–G). Additionally, we used the combined GC cohort to evaluate the association between the macrophage subcluster gene signature and prognosis, finding that patients with high gene signature of the Mac_c1_C1QB subtype tended to have poor prognosis (Figure S4H,I). Dendritic cells are the main participants for initiating antigen‐specific immune responses in tumor immunity. Three distinct subsets in classic DCs (cDCs) were identified in across cancer types including two classical cDC subsets (CLEC9A+ cDC1s and CD1C+ cDC2s) and a mature cDC subset (LAMP3+ cDC). 24 In our data, we observed the latter two subtypes and named them cDC2_CD1C and cDC3_LAMP3, respectively.

The overall composition of the myeloid cell compartment was modified following RT (Figure 4C). Notably, the Mac_c1_C1QB cluster was mainly in unirradiated tumor and its relative proportion increased during RT while the Mac_c2_FABP5 cluster dominated in the post_RT_3 sample, suggesting the infiltration of lipid‐associated macrophages after the completion of RT. Moreover, there was an obvious decreasing trend in the relative proportion of cDC2_CD1C clusters. Next, function scores were applied to explore functional changes in each myeloid subtype during RT. The Mac_c1_C1QB cluster showed the higher anti‐inflammatory, phagocytosis, and antigen‐presenting capacity while The Mac_c2_FABP5 cluster showed higher proangiogenic function (Figure 4D). Remarkably, the proinflammatory score of all macrophage subclusters increased at 2 weeks of RT with no other obvious functional change (Figure 4E). However, RT downregulated the antigen‐presenting capacity of DC cells, which explained the impaired T cell responses to some extent (Figure 4F). In addition, IHC staining confirmed the downregulation of MHC‐I genes (HLA‐A) in post‐RT samples (Figure 4G). Together, RT induced the infiltration of C1q+ macrophages with enhanced proinflammatory capacity while it reduced resident DC subsets with declined antigen‐presenting capacity.

Stromal cell remodeling during RT with increased myofibroblastic CAF polarization and activation of endothelial cells

We examined the population of stromal cells and defined 11 subclasses, comprising four fibroblast, four endothelial cell (EC), and three smooth muscle cell clusters (Figure 5A,B). Each stromal cluster was distributed evenly across samples (Figure S5A). To better feature the heterogeneous states of CAFs, we calculated the expression of gene signatures of inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) and myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs). 25 The Fibro_c1_DPT cluster displayed dominant myCAF signatures whereas the Fibro_c2_MGST1 cluster showed high expression of iCAF‐related signatures (Figure 5C). In addition, cells in the Fibro_c3_POSTN cluster presented high transforming growth factor β (TGFβ)‐myCAF signature score and highly expressed INHBA (Figures 5C and S5C), relevant to CAFs and unfavorable survival in GC. 34 Consistently, the gene signature of the Fibro_c3_POSTN cluster was strongly associated with poor prognosis in the combined GC cohort (Figure S5D). Four subclusters of ECs displayed distinct transcriptional features (Figure S5B,E): (1) Endo_c1_KDR, which highly expressed vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) receptor genes, FLT1, KDR, and FLT4, likely reflective of variations in tumor‐specific VEGF signaling; (2) Endo_c2_ACKR1, which was identified as venous ECs (ACKR1 and SELP), enrich genes related to antigen presentation (HLA‐DRA); (3) Endo_c3_SRGN, which expressed marker genes of arterial ECs such as FBLN5 and GJA5; and (4) Endo_c4_RBP7, which refers to pericytes with high expression of RGS5 and CD36. The KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the Endo_c2_ACKR1 cluster was enriched for cell adhesion molecules as well as antigen processing and presentation and the Endo_c3_SRGN cluster was enriched for leukocyte transendothelial migration (Figure S5F).

Next, we compared the population of stromal subpopulations during RT. The relative proportion of the Fibro_c3_POSTN and Fibro_c4_NEAT1 clusters obviously increased while the proportion of the Fibro_c2_MGST1 cluster decreased after 2 weeks of RT (Figure 5D), suggesting polarization of myCAFs induced by RT. When evaluating the expression of signature gene sets in fibroblast subclusters, the Fibro_c4_NEAT1 cluster showed the highest collagen production and angiogenesis capacity, which were both enhanced on treatment of RT (Figure 5E,F). In addition, the proportion of the Endo_c3_SRGN cluster was elevated with enhanced leukocyte transendothelial migration function after 2 weeks of RT, hinting that activation of ECs contributed to the infiltration of myeloid cells. However, RT also declined the antigen‐presenting capacity in all endothelial clusters (Figure 5E,G). Moreover, both IHC and IF staining validated that the collagen (encoded by COL1A2) increased after RT, especially at the point of Post‐RT‐3 (Figure 5H,I).

Cellular cross‐talk during RT

To better depict the cell–cell interaction network during RT, we combined the post_RT_1, post_RT_2, and post_RT_3 sample as post_RT samples. Using the CellChat R package 35 to compare the differences in signaling pathways between pre_RT and post_RT samples, we found there was an increase except CD4 and CD8 T cells in both interaction strength and number in post_RT samples (Figure 6A,B). Additionally, we presented the detailed changes of signaling pathway in pre_RT and post_RT samples (Figure S6A). Notably, ECM‐related interactions involving COLLAGEN, FN1, and THBS pathways were upregulated in response to RT (Figure S6B–D). Yao et al. recently have verified that ECM–receptor interaction was enriched in postradiation breast cancer tissues through bulk RNA‐seq data. 36 CX3CR1 is known to be expressed by NK cells, 37 and the CX3C signaling increased between endothelial cells and NK/NKT cells (Figure S6E), which was responsible for recruiting NKT/NK cells. Moreover, various NK cell‐activating signaling pathways elevated in post_RT samples such as CD266, CD48, and PVR signaling (Figure S6A), of which PVR signaling from tumor cells and endothelial cells to NKT cells may be associated with the enhanced cytotoxicity in NKT cells induced by RT (Figure S6F).

Next, the iTALK package 38 was used to identify alterations of ligand–receptor (LR) interactions among cell clusters during RT. Consistent with the results of Cellchat, the CCL2/CCL3/CCL4– > CCR1 pair presented more active in macrophages in post_RT samples, which was responsible for the recruitment of them into irradiated tumor (Figures 6C and S6G). Of various cellular growth factor LR pairs, endothelial cells most actively interacted with other TME cells via the VEGFC– > ITGB1 pair (Figure 6D). Regarding immune checkpoint LR interactions, we found LGALS9– > HAVCR2 LR pair between endothelial cells and myeloid cells, and TNFSF14– > TNFRSF14 LR pair between CD8+ T cells were more active in post_RT samples (Figure 6E,F). LGALS9 encodes the galectin‐9 (Gal‐9) protein, which is essentially an immune system inhibitor and drives macrophage M2 polarization by activating its receptor Tim‐3. 39 In contrast, tumor necrosis factor superfamily member 14 (TNFSF14) otherwise known as LIGHT, serves as an important antitumor immune activity ligand primarily expressed on activated T cells and activated NK cells. 40 Overall, RT affected interactions between cell clusters, which related to ECM remolding, vascular endothelial activation, and immunomodulation.

Verification in locally advanced rectal cancer cohorts with CRT

To our knowledge, there are currently no paired sample data before and after RT for GC. Thus, bulk RNA‐seq from two locally advanced rectal cancer cohorts including 52 paired pre‐CRT and post‐CRT samples (GSE94104, n = 80; GSE190826, n = 24), were applied to verify our findings above. Like the results of GSVA of hallmark pathways on epithelial cells (Figure S2H), G2M checkpoint, E2F targets, and glycolysis pathways were enriched in pre‐CRT samples while myogenesis, hypoxia, and apical junction pathways were highly expressed in post‐CRT samples (Figure S7A,B). We also compared the score of functional gene signatures between pre‐ and post‐CRT groups. Collagen and leukocyte trans‐endothelial migration score were significantly elevated after CRT, which were consistent with our results (Figure S7C,D). However, anti‐inflammatory, exhaustion, and phagocytosis scores were upregulated in post‐CRT samples, which was distinct to our analysis. Furthermore, we observed that LR pairs involving the collagen pathway, FN1 pathway, THBS pathway, and VEGF pathway were enhanced after CRT, which also validated the results of our single cell data (Figure S7E,F). There are many factors associated with immunological impact of RT, including the nature of the tumor (e.g., its mutational burden), the specific tissue or organ and the precise RT delivery regimen (e.g., the dose per fraction, total dose, time between fractions, and overall treatment time). In total, RT has certain commonalities in its impact on the TME, regardless of the type or location of the cancer.

DISCUSSION

There is still no strong evidence how tumor cells and TME dynamically evolute at single cell resolution during RT. One study indicated that GC cells can be endowed with stemness traits in response to RT. 41 However, that study did not examine the response of cancer cells to RT in vivo nor investigate other TME cells. Chow et al. depicted tumor and immune remodeling following RT in renal cell carcinoma at single cell resolution. 22 However, the statistical analyses were carried out on unpaired samples and did not include on‐treatment samples. Herein, using scRNA‐seq and scTCR‐seq in tumor samples from the same abdominal wall metastasis site from a patient with GC at baseline and during fractionated RT, we are able to characterize the variants of epithelial, immune, and stromal cell subclusters during RT at high resolution. We hoped that these findings would provide novel insights into the understanding of tumor ecosystem remodeling in response to RT, granting clues for the design of more effective combinatory therapies, especially immunotherapy, thus achieving long‐term improvement in disease control.

Consistent with previous reports, proliferative tumor cells are susceptible to radiation, which undergo apoptosis, especially immunogenic cell death to trigger immune activation. 8 In contrast, quiescent tumor cells are insensitive to RT, and upregulate expression of immune checkpoints such as CD47 and LGALS3 in our study. Fan et al. recently reported that tumor cell‐derived GAL‐3 (encoded by LGALS3) can upregulate CD47 expression, thus not merely inhibit macrophage phagocytosis but induce an immunosuppressive microenvironment. 42 In addition, a previous study has reported that radiation induces the expression of CD47 as a mechanism of RT resistance in colorectal cancer. 30

In terms of cellular composition alternation, we primarily observed infiltration of myeloid cells and decline of T cells, which was associated with activation of endothelial cells and enhanced leukocyte trans‐endothelial migration induced by RT. Additionally, it has been reported that immune cells presented distinct sensitivity to radiation that T cells exhibited higher sensitivity than myeloid cells, 43 which partially explained the phenomenon. Furthermore, different radiation fractionation regimens may impact the types of infiltrating cells in the tumor. In the preclinical study, whereas the 1 × 16.4 Gy and 3 × 8 Gy hypofractionated schemes induced a lymphoid response, the 18 × 2 Gy so‐called “conventionally fractionated” pattern induced a myeloid response such as myeloid‐derived suppressor cells and tumor‐associated macrophages. 44 The later radiation scheme was similar to that undertaken in our study.

In the cell functional analysis, we observed that the enrichment of NKT subpopulations with enhanced cytotoxic activity, which was associated with upregulated chemokine CX3RL1 and NKALs such as PVR and NECTIN2 secreted by irradiated tumor cells and endothelial cells. In keeping with findings in cervical cancer, 19 RT may activate innate immunity rather than adaptive immunity in our study. Further supporting evidence was that the antigen presentation function of DC cells and endothelial cells decreased along with the accumulation of RT dosage, which prevented the activation of adaptive immunity. Moreover, multiple cell types including epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts upregulate pro‐angiogenesis abilities in response to the hypoxic environment induced by RT.

Thus far, few studies have included a time‐series analysis of RT regimens in patient tissue. Oyoshi et al. recently reported that RT induced infiltration of macrophages expressing immunosuppressive genes into tumor in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. 45 They also undertook scRNA‐seq in in‐situ tumor biopsy samples and obtained “pre‐RT,” “during RT,” “just after RT (7 days after RT),” and “after RT (~1 month after RT)”. In line with our results, the increase in the population of myeloid cells was most notably observed at the “during RT” time point with the decrease of T cells. In a further analysis of subpopulations of myeloid cells, they found that coinhibitory and inflammatory genes peaked during RT, which was consistent with our findings. However, the HLA gene expression gradually increased and peaked after RT, which was opposite to our results of single‐cell transcriptome analysis and IHC staining.

Our time‐series analysis revealed distinct expression patterns in each phase after RT. We observed there was no significant improvement in the immune microenvironment compared to pre‐RT samples at the first time point. C1q+ macrophages showed the strongest proinflammatory capacity at the second time point, while T cells showed the highest cytotoxicity function at the third time point. Considering that in our case, RT mainly involved infiltration of myeloid cells and upregulation of the CD47 and GAL‐3 immune checkpoint, dual inhibition of CD47 and GAL‐3 with RT concurrently primed may be a promising treatment approach. Furthermore, as T cell response declined during RT but recovered at the end, administration of immune checkpoint inhibitors following radiation may improve tumor‐specific T cell infiltration into irradiated tumors. This is consistent with previous results showing that patients with non‐small‐cell lung cancer receiving durvalumab after concurrent CRT demonstrated better overall survival and progression‐free survival than those receiving placebo. 46

However, our study has some limitations that warrant consideration. First, the limited number of patients might lead to bias in our findings and more patients are needed in future studies. Second, the microenvironment of the metastatic site in the abdominal wall might not represent the microenvironment characteristics of the primary lesion or other metastatic sites, thus it will be necessary to obtain samples from multiple sites. Third, due to the lack of a validation cohort comprising paired pre‐RT and post‐RT GC samples, we could only perform validated testing on two locally advanced rectal cancer cohorts at the transcriptome level. Based on our results, several studies using multi‐omics data in the future may unveil the entire dynamic landscape in tumor during RT and thereby achieve the better application of RT in the management of cancer. The RT regimen must be improved and adjusted to maximize immune stimulation, to enable successful combination with other treatment methods, including immunotherapy.
