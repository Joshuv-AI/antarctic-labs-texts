Refractory ventricular tachyarrhythmias following entrectinib initiation in metastatic lung adenocarcinoma: a case report
Authors: Lili Tang, Weiwei Wan, Hongtao Zhang, Xiaoyue Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEntrectinib is a targeted therapy that has been approved for the treatment of ROS1-positive non-small cell lung cancer. However, this therapy is associated with known but rare cardiotoxic risks.Case presentationWe report a case of ventricular tachyarrhythmias occurring on day 4 after entrectinib initiation in a 37-year-old Han Chinese man with ROS1-positive metastatic lung adenocarcinoma. The arrhythmias was refractory to pharmacologic and electrical therapy, resulting in hemodynamic collapse and the need for venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. The diagnostic workup excluded other causes, and therapeutic plasma exchange led to rhythm stabilization and cardiac recovery. The patient was successfully weaned from extracorporeal membrane oxygenation by day 5.ConclusionsThis case highlights a potentially fatal early-phase cardiac complication of entrectinib and supports the use of early electrocardiographic monitoring and therapeutic plasma exchange as management and rescue strategies.Keywords: Entrectinib, Ventricular tachyarrhythmias, Cardiotoxicity, Therapeutic plasma exchange

Background

Entrectinib is a multitarget tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) that has been approved for the treatment of ROS1-positive non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK) fusion-positive solid tumors. Entrectinib is generally well-tolerated, with previous clinical trials reporting mainly mild adverse events such as cognitive impairment, fatigue, and gastrointestinal symptoms [1]. Some reports have shown that entrectinib may induce serious cardiac events, including myocarditis [2], heart failure [2, 3], and life-threatening arrhythmias [4, 5]. Herein, we present a rare case of refractory ventricular tachyarrhythmias (VT) induced by entrectinib in a patient with ROS1-positive lung adenocarcinoma. This case highlights an underrecognized but potentially fatal cardiac complication and emphasizes the importance of early arrhythmia surveillance during entrectinib therapy.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old Han Chinese male with stage IV ROS1-positive lung adenocarcinoma was admitted to the emergency department with acute chest tightness and dyspnea, followed by a 10-minute episode of coma. The patient presented with shock, marked by tachycardia (127 beats per minute), hypotension (70/32 mmHg), muffled heart sounds, and mottled skin. Targeted therapy with 600 mg of entrectinib once daily was initiated 4 days earlier. The patient had a history of deep vein thrombosis managed with an inferior vena cava filter and rivaroxaban. Initial electrocardiography (ECG) revealed VT with remarkable convex ST elevation (shark sign) in V1–V5 (Fig. 1A), followed by polymorphic VT (Fig. 1B). Laboratory tests revealed mild troponin elevation, metabolic acidosis, and normal electrolytes. Despite comprehensive resuscitation, including cardioversion, amiodarone, lidocaine, β-blockers, and vasopressors, the patient remained refractory to VT with persistent hypotension (Fig. 1C). Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was initiated emergently. Coronary angiography revealed no significant stenosis (Fig. 2), and transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated diffuse ventricular hypokinesis with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 27% and no structural abnormalities. Entrectinib was discontinued following hospital admission, and therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) was initiated the following day. After TPE, the patient’s cardiac rhythm reverted to the sinus rhythm (Fig. 1D), accompanied by rapid recovery of cardiac function, which allowed successful weaning from VA-ECMO on day 5. The patient was discharged in an improved condition on day 13.

Discussion

Entrectinib is a novel, orally administered, multitarget TKI that received accelerated US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval in 2019 because of its potent antitumor efficacy and distinctive central nervous system activity. Although entrectinib was well-tolerated in phase I‒III trials, 3.6‒4.9% of patients experienced grade 3‒4 cardiac adverse events, including myocarditis, heart failure, and QTc prolongation [1]. Cardiotoxicity is the leading cause of the discontinuation of entrectinib treatment [1]. Although no fatal arrhythmias have been reported in clinical trials, two real-world cases have been documented: one with VT and Brugada-like ECG changes [4], and another with fulminant myocarditis triggering an electrical storm [5].

The mechanisms underlying entrectinib-induced malignant arrhythmias remain unclear. Oncological studies have indicated a significant association between TKIs and an increased risk of serious ventricular arrhythmias. Large-scale clinical studies on TKIs revealed that the incidence of ibrutinib-associated malignant arrhythmias was nearly ten times higher than expected, at approximately 6.0–7.9 cases per 1000 person-years [6]. Notably, these events were not significantly associated with preexisting cardiovascular disease. The development of QTc prolongation or ventricular arrhythmias associated with TKIs may be driven by several underlying mechanisms [7]: (1) delayed cardiac repolarization caused by off-target blockade of hERG potassium channels; (2) drug interactions with potassium channel chaperone proteins may lead to protein misfolding, thereby impairing the proper assembly of hERG channels; (3) suppression of PI3K/Akt signaling may lead to aberrant sodium and calcium currents, disturbed calcium cycling, and consequently abnormal electrical activity; or (4) TKIs can trigger signaling pathways leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) depletion, and cell apoptosis, ultimately worsening cardiomyocyte injury and apoptosis. Additional real-world and preclinical studies are needed to clarify the associations between entrectinib and malignant arrhythmias, as well as the underlying mechanisms involved.

The occurrence of entrectinib treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) may be influenced by a temporal effect. According to FDA-approved prescribing information, entrectinib reaches steady-state plasma concentrations within 5 days of once-daily 600 mg dosing [8]. Malignant arrhythmia occurred on the fourth day of entrectinib administration in this case. Real-world reports have indicated that adverse cardiac events typically occur within 3–5 days after the initial full-dose administration of entrectinib, suggesting that the heart may be a key early target organ of entrectinib toxicity. Therefore, during the early phase of entrectinib treatment, clinicians should consider continuous or repeated ECG monitoring to help prevent or promptly detect malignant arrhythmias. Previous studies have indicated that the median onset of entrectinib-TRAEs is 12 days [9], with the timing varying across organ systems. This finding highlights the importance of targeted monitoring of different organ functions at specific phases of treatment.

TPE may serve as an effective intervention for managing severe entrectinib TRAEs. Pharmacokinetic studies have indicated that entrectinib is 99% bound to plasma proteins, with an elimination half-life of approximately 20 hours, and its active metabolite M5 has a half-life of approximately 40 hours [8]. Previous studies have shown that the QTc prolongation induced by entrectinib may persist for an average of 7.8 days after discontinuation and that the neurotoxic effects may last for approximately 3.4 months [10]. The patient in this case showed rapid clinical improvement after undergoing TPE, with no further episodes of arrhythmia. However, symptoms gradually resolved over a period of 7–43 days following drug discontinuation in four other reported cases without plasma exchange. Given its high plasma protein binding and long half-life, conventional treatments such as supportive medications, hemofiltration, or hemoperfusion may be insufficient for rapidly and effectively reducing drug levels in life-threatening entrectinib-TRAEs. Consequently, plasma exchange may represent a more effective and necessary intervention in the management of severe entrectinib-TRAEs. Real-world data on the use of entrectinib remain limited, with most existing studies conducted in predominantly North American populations. Further real-world research is essential to better assess drug safety and to inform effective strategies for the prevention and management of entrectinib-TRAEs.

Conclusion

Entrectinib may increase the risk of malignant arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death. Clinicians are advised to consider continuous or repeated ECG monitoring during the early phase of treatment to prevent or promptly detect these potentially fatal events. Plasma exchange may serve as an effective therapeutic option for life-threatening entrectinib-TRAEs.
