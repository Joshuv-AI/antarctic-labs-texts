Mechanisms and precision interventions in sarcopenia and osteoarthritis comorbidity: A narrative review
Authors: Jun Ma, Chen Chen, Hao Jiang, Mengran Xi, Wei Luo, Renwen Wan, Mi Zou, Tianqi Wang, Xinyi Zhang, Shiyi Chen, Gang Chen, Zhiwen Luo, Nirong Bao, Patrick Shu-Hang Yung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSarcopenia and osteoarthritis are two common musculoskeletal disorders that often coexist, posing significant challenges in clinical management. These conditions share underlying mechanisms such as chronic inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and biomechanical stress, which accelerate musculoskeletal degeneration. Sarcopenia, characterized by muscle loss and weakness, and osteoarthritis, marked by cartilage degradation and joint dysfunction, frequently overlap, leading to increased disability and reduced quality of life. The presence of both conditions creates a detrimental cycle where muscle weakness exacerbates joint pain, and joint dysfunction further weakens muscles. This comorbidity complicates treatment, requiring integrated strategies that address both muscle and joint pathology. Advances in personalized therapies, driven by multi-omics technologies, are crucial in targeting these interconnected conditions more effectively. Precision medicine, using genetic, metabolic, and physiological data, can help tailor treatments and improve patient outcomes. Furthermore, the integration of adaptive interventions, digital twin models, and real-time feedback systems will play key roles in refining individualized treatment plans. Multi-omics integration and real-world evidence are also transforming how clinical trials are designed and how we assess disease progression, moving away from structural markers to functional and patient-centered outcomes. Effective interventions must involve early detection, personalized approaches, and interdisciplinary care, aiming to break the cycle of degeneration and improve patients' functional independence and quality of life.The translational potential of this articleThis review identifies the biomechanical, metabolic, and epigenetic “vicious cycles” that drive the comorbidity of sarcopenia and osteoarthritis. By integrating multi-omics data, digital twin modeling, and real-time biomechanical feedback, this work provides a framework for clinical precision medicine. The clinical significance lies in identifying a “mechanical window of opportunity” for early intervention—using personalized exercise and nutritional strategies to restore joint homeostasis and prevent irreversible functional decline.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Osteoarthritis, Inflammation, Metabolic dysfunction, Personalized therapy, Precision medicine

Introduction

Concept of comorbidity and clinical context

The comorbidity of sarcopenia and osteoarthritis refers to the simultaneous occurrence of progressive skeletal muscle atrophy and degenerative joint disease in an individual. Although these conditions differ in their primary pathophysiology, they interact in complex, bidirectional ways, influencing the onset, progression, and clinical manifestation of each other. Clearly defining this comorbidity is essential, as it ensures a precise understanding that distinguishes true co-occurrence from mere symptom overlap or shared etiological factors. This clarity is crucial for accurate clinical diagnosis, as signs like muscle weakness, limited mobility, and functional decline can arise from either condition independently or from their combined effects. The latter often accelerates deterioration beyond what would be expected from either condition in isolation.

Patients who suffer from both sarcopenia and osteoarthritis face a complicated healthcare journey, frequently requiring multiple referrals across primary care, rheumatology, orthopedics, and rehabilitation services [1]. This process is often characterized by fragmented communication and redundant interventions. Clinical deterioration tends to occur more rapidly at certain junctures, including the onset of joint pain leading to activity limitation, the measurable decline in muscle mass or strength, and sudden functional decline following acute events such as falls, joint inflammation, or surgery [2,3]. Identifying these critical points can inform the development of early-warning systems and tailored intervention algorithms that match the patient's stage in the disease continuum.

The population affected by this comorbidity is diverse. While the highest prevalence is observed in older adults, early-onset cases are increasingly recognized, particularly in individuals with obesity, metabolic syndrome, prolonged sedentary behavior, or chronic systemic inflammation. This expanding at-risk demographic highlights the need for more refined risk stratification models that consider factors such as age, body composition, genetic predisposition, and lifestyle. A structured clinical-mechanistic matrix could serve as a key tool in this effort. Such a matrix should integrate unresolved diagnostic uncertainties, gaps in understanding muscle-joint interactions, the roles of metabolic and endocrine modulators, and the long-term functional outcomes that influence patient independence.

Epidemiological patterns and health burden

From a population health perspective, the combined burden of sarcopenia and osteoarthritis is a growing concern [4]. This challenge is exacerbated by the global aging population, changes in lifestyle, and the prolonged survival of individuals with chronic diseases. Epidemiological surveys show increasing prevalence for both conditions, with osteoarthritis affecting more than 10% of older adults worldwide, and sarcopenia affecting 5% to 20% depending on the diagnostic criteria. While osteoarthritis has long been associated with older age, sarcopenia is now being detected more frequently in middle-aged individuals, particularly in societies undergoing rapid transitions in nutrition, urbanization, and mechanization, which all contribute to reduced physical activity [5].

In China, studies at both national and regional levels suggest that the prevalence of sarcopenia and osteoarthritis is similar to that in Western countries, although significant interregional variations exist [6]. These differences are influenced by factors such as socioeconomic status, dietary habits, healthcare access, and opportunities for physical activity. Such disparities complicate direct epidemiological comparisons and emphasize the need for context-specific strategies for prevention and intervention.

The health burden associated with these conditions extends far beyond mere prevalence rates. Both sarcopenia and osteoarthritis independently contribute to mobility impairment, pain, and reduced quality of life; however, their combined effect is far more severe. Rather than a simple summation of effects, their interaction leads to a synergistic worsening of disability. Patients often experience rapid declines in gait velocity, decreased postural stability, and an increased risk of falls and fractures. These physical limitations often lead to earlier and more prolonged dependence on caregivers and institutional support, thereby escalating the societal and familial impact of these diseases.

Economic analyses further underscore the significant financial burden of sarcopenia-osteoarthritis comorbidity [7]. Direct medical costs include repeated imaging studies, pharmacological treatments, physical therapy, assistive devices, and surgeries such as joint replacements. Indirect costs, which often exceed direct medical expenses, encompass productivity losses, early retirement, prolonged caregiving, and opportunity costs associated with reduced participation in community and family roles. Vulnerable groups such as rural populations, postmenopausal women, and individuals with coexisting chronic diseases like diabetes or cardiovascular disorders are disproportionately affected. This pattern highlights the intersection of biological susceptibility and social determinants of health, reinforcing the need for integrative public health approaches that address both medical and structural inequities.

Clinical phenotypes and staging framework

The clinical presentation of sarcopenia-osteoarthritis comorbidity is best understood as a continuum of composite phenotypes, wherein pain, muscle weakness, and restricted range of motion coexist with varying intensity and in different sequences. In osteoarthritis, chronic joint pain typically initiates compensatory movement patterns, which in turn lead to disuse-related muscle atrophy and further declines in neuromuscular coordination. Conversely, in sarcopenia, intrinsic muscle weakness and loss of power contribute to joint instability, abnormal loading patterns, and accelerated degenerative changes. This cycle exacerbates pain and mechanical stress [8]. The clinical spectrum ranges from pain-dominant profiles with secondary muscle weakness to weakness-dominant forms where pain develops later, necessitating phenotype-specific assessment and treatment strategies.

Recent clinical studies have highlighted the significant overlap of these conditions. For instance, the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) reported that the prevalence of sarcopenic obesity in osteoarthritis patients is significantly higher than in the general population, correlating with reduced grip strength and slower gait speed [9]. Similarly, data from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI) indicated that lower thigh muscle specific strength is a strong predictor of symptomatic knee osteoarthritis progression [10]. A meta-analysis of Asian populations further revealed that patients with sarcopenia have a 2-fold increased risk of developing knee osteoarthritis compared to non-sarcopenic controls, emphasizing that muscle quality is a critical determinant of joint health [11].

A comprehensive understanding of the temporal relationship between joint damage and functional decline is essential for precision management. In many cases, structural joint changes such as cartilage degeneration, subchondral bone remodeling, and synovial inflammation precede noticeable muscle wasting [12]. However, in certain patients, particularly those with metabolic or neuromuscular vulnerabilities, muscle strength loss may occur earlier, altering joint mechanics and initiating a cascade of degenerative processes. In high-risk individuals, these factors may progress concurrently, leading to a self-perpetuating loop of dysfunction. Identifying the dominant sequence of these processes in individual patients is vital for targeting modifiable factors at the appropriate stage.

The staging of this comorbidity benefits from a dichotomous framework that separates the potentially reversible early stage from the progressive, largely irreversible phase. The early stage is marked by mild to moderate functional deficits, modest reductions in muscle mass, and incipient structural joint changes. At this stage, interventions such as progressive resistance exercise, neuromuscular training, anti-inflammatory therapies, and nutritional support can halt or even partially reverse functional decline. In contrast, the progressive phase is characterized by advanced joint destruction, severe sarcopenia, and significant disability, where management focuses on pain relief, mobility preservation, and quality of life maintenance.

An effective staging system should employ multimodal assessment tools. High-resolution imaging techniques, such as magnetic resonance imaging and musculoskeletal ultrasound, offer detailed evaluations of both articular structures and muscle architecture [13]. Laboratory biomarkers that reflect systemic inflammation, protein metabolism, hormonal status, and cartilage degradation provide additional insights into the underlying pathophysiology [14]. Functional performance tests, including grip strength, gait speed, and sit-to-stand assessments, establish a tangible link between structural and biochemical findings and the patient's functional capacity. The integration of these parameters enables the creation of a multidimensional disease map, guiding personalized intervention strategies and long-term monitoring.

This review aims to provide a clinically relevant synthesis of the pathophysiological and functional interplay between sarcopenia and osteoarthritis within an integrative framework that encompasses anatomy, biomechanics, and systemic physiology. It will explore the mechanisms through which muscle and joint pathologies interact at molecular, cellular, tissue, and organismal levels, informing interventions that are both mechanistically robust and clinically applicable. Based on rigorous evidence appraisal, the review will use a graded evaluation approach to assess various study designs, focusing on observational research for epidemiological validity, randomized controlled trials for methodological rigor, and mechanistic studies for translational relevance. The synthesis will integrate both quantitative and qualitative methodologies, applying meta-analytic techniques when homogeneity allows and narrative integration when heterogeneity is present. Sensitivity analyses, evaluation of publication bias, and subgroup analyses across demographic, clinical, and methodological dimensions will also be included. The goal is to provide actionable insights into the modulation of muscle-joint inflammatory pathways, optimization of neuromuscular recruitment, and targeted rehabilitation strategies, thus advancing scientific understanding and improving patient outcomes in the intersecting domains of sarcopenia and osteoarthritis.

Biomechanical interwoven mechanisms and translation

Load imbalance and joint microenvironment alterations

The biomechanical relationship between loading patterns and joint integrity is a critical intersection in the study of osteoarthritis and sarcopenia [15]. Deviations from normal loading, including excessive load and force maldistribution, are key contributors to the development of pathological shear and compressive stress profiles, which erode both the mechanical and biological resilience of joints [16]. Such abnormal loading disrupts the homeostasis of articular tissues, accelerating degeneration, especially in high-demand joints like the knee and hip. Shear forces cause abrasion of the articular cartilage surface, leading to microstructural disruption and fibrillation. Excessive compressive stress deforms cartilage, diminishes its viscoelastic properties, and triggers maladaptive remodeling of the subchondral bone [17]. The concurrent presence of shear and compressive forces sets the stage for a chronic degenerative cycle, characteristic of osteoarthritis, where intrinsic repair mechanisms fail to keep pace with cumulative structural damage (Fig. 1).

The integrity of load-distributing structures, such as menisci and ligaments, is fundamental to mechanical balance. Damage or laxity in these tissues disrupts the uniform transmission of forces across joint surfaces, leading to focal overloading and reduced mechanical stability. This not only accelerates localized cartilage wear but also compromises proprioceptive feedback mechanisms, which are essential for precise neuromuscular control. Loss of proprioceptive acuity has become increasingly recognized as a biomechanical amplifier of osteoarthritis progression, linking structural breakdown to impaired movement coordination. As instability evolves, patients often adopt compensatory gait strategies that further distort load distribution, perpetuating a cycle of mechanical and neuromuscular decline.

Gait and postural deviations are particularly pronounced in individuals with both sarcopenia and osteoarthritis. The interplay of muscle weakness, axial misalignment, and altered movement patterns changes joint reaction force vectors, creating regions of concentrated stress alongside areas of underloading within the same joint. This stress heterogeneity promotes compartment-specific degeneration and undermines overall joint function. Evidence from interventional gait retraining programs suggests that re-establishing balanced load distribution can interrupt maladaptive cycles, highlighting the importance of precision motion analysis for early intervention planning.

Mechanical underloading is another critical factor contributing to joint deterioration, often driven by pain avoidance or reduced physical activity [18]. Physiological cyclic loading is essential for synovial fluid circulation, nutrient exchange in avascular cartilage, and the maintenance of chondrocyte metabolic activity. Prolonged unloading, commonly seen in sedentary individuals or those with advanced disease, leads to matrix dehydration, proteoglycan depletion, and loss of mechanical stiffness [19]. These observations underscore the dual nature of mechanical load as both a necessary stimulus for tissue maintenance and a potential driver of degeneration when excessive or insufficient, emphasizing the need to identify optimal loading thresholds for joint preservation.

Decline in muscle quality and loss of dynamic stability

The progressive deterioration of muscle quality that accompanies sarcopenia has significant implications for joint stability, locomotor control, and overall biomechanical efficiency [20]. One of the most prominent features of this decline is the shift in muscle fiber composition, from type II fast-twitch fibers, which are specialized for rapid and high-power contractions, to type I slow-twitch fibers, which support endurance but generate lower peak force. This phenotypic transition is associated with decreased contractile speed, reduced maximal torque, and a diminished capacity to meet sudden mechanical demands. As a result, the ability to generate rapid corrective forces in response to perturbations is impaired, compromising dynamic joint stabilization during activities such as walking on uneven surfaces or adjusting to sudden changes in direction. Clinically, quadriceps weakness has been associated with anterior knee instability and accelerated cartilage wear, while weakening of the rotator cuff musculature leads to subtle yet functionally significant microinstability of the shoulder, contributing to tendon degeneration and joint capsule laxity over time.

Age-related degeneration of the neuromuscular junction further exacerbates muscle performance decline [21]. Structural changes, such as postsynaptic fragmentation, reduced acetylcholine receptor density, and cycles of motor neuron denervation followed by incomplete reinnervation, disrupt efficient signal transmission. These changes lead to reduced motor unit recruitment, asynchronous firing patterns, and delayed contraction onset. In biomechanically challenging situations, such as rapid balance recovery after tripping, these neuromuscular deficits reduce the speed and precision of reactive stabilization, thereby increasing the risk of falls, secondary joint injury, and cumulative musculoskeletal strain. These deficits are not solely due to reduced muscle strength; they also reflect a loss of the temporal coordination necessary for maintaining joint alignment under dynamic loading.

The decline of deep stabilizing muscle groups, including the transversus abdominis, multifidus, and deep hip rotators, is another critical factor in the loss of dynamic stability (Fig. 1) [22]. These muscles play a vital role in fine-tuning joint positioning, controlling accessory motion, and providing segmental stability, rather than contributing to gross limb movement. Their selective atrophy or neuromuscular inhibition, often exacerbated by pain, disuse, or altered movement patterns, reduces the ability to resist translational and rotational forces at the joint. This impairment alters load trajectories, leading to uneven stress distribution across articular surfaces and accelerating degenerative cartilage changes. Furthermore, the weakening of these deep stabilizers initiates a self-reinforcing degenerative cycle in which instability modifies joint loading patterns, abnormal loads compromise muscle activation efficiency, and progressive muscle atrophy exacerbates mechanical imbalance.

Age-related changes in connective tissue composition further amplify these muscular deficits [23]. Tendons exhibit reduced collagen fiber crimp, lower water content, and increased collagen crosslinking, which diminishes their elasticity and capacity for efficient strain–energy storage. This reduces their role as mechanical springs, increasing the reliance on active muscle contraction to absorb and release mechanical energy. In parallel, fascial tissues become stiffer and less adaptable, impairing force transmission between muscle groups and across kinetic chains. The loss of fascial compliance disrupts the smooth coordination of multi-joint movements, increasing energy expenditure and reducing movement efficiency [24]. These tissue-level changes exacerbate repetitive joint loading, contributing to overuse injuries, particularly in weight-bearing joints, where cumulative microtrauma accelerates structural degeneration.

Together, the interplay of fiber-type transition, neuromuscular junction degeneration, deep stabilizer atrophy, and connective tissue stiffening leads to a multifaceted decline in the neuromuscular system's ability to maintain dynamic stability. This decline not only impairs movement performance but also shifts joint loading toward mechanically unfavorable patterns, accelerating the onset of degenerative joint diseases. Effective intervention requires an integrated approach that targets both contractile and connective tissue components, combining neuromuscular activation strategies, targeted resistance training, and biomechanical retraining to restore coordinated force generation and optimize load distribution across the musculoskeletal system [25].

Mechanobiological signaling pathways

Mechanobiological signal transduction plays a fundamental role in how cells within the musculoskeletal system detect, interpret, and respond to mechanical stimuli, thereby maintaining tissue integrity and function [26]. This process provides the molecular foundation for understanding how physical forces shape the structural and biochemical state of tissues, influencing both physiological adaptation and pathological degeneration. At the cellular level, specialized mechanoreceptors serve as primary sensors of mechanical information [27]. Among these, integrins act as critical transmembrane adhesion receptors, establishing physical and biochemical continuity between the extracellular matrix and the intracellular cytoskeleton. By forming focal adhesion complexes, integrins connect extracellular matrix components to actin filaments, creating a conduit for tensile, compressive, and shear forces to be transmitted to intracellular signaling pathways. These complexes activate various signaling intermediates, including focal adhesion kinase, Src family kinases, and Rho family GTPases, which regulate cell shape, motility, and differentiation. Integrin-mediated pathways preserve the architectural stability of musculoskeletal cells and integrate mechanical inputs with biochemical signals from growth factors and cytokines, coordinating cellular responses with the fluctuating physical environment [28].

The cytoskeleton plays a decisive role in converting mechanical load into transcriptional outcomes. The actin cytoskeleton, along with microtubules and intermediate filaments, functions as a dynamic tension-bearing scaffold that remodels in response to changes in load magnitude and direction. This remodeling is closely linked to the activity of the Hippo pathway effectors YAP and transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif TAZ, which act as key mechanotransducers in the nucleus. Changes in cytoskeletal tension determine the localization of YAP and TAZ, allowing them to shuttle between the cytoplasm and the nucleus. When mechanical tension is high, YAP/TAZ accumulate in the nucleus, promoting the transcription of genes involved in extracellular matrix synthesis, cell proliferation, and anabolic tissue remodeling [29]. In contrast, reduced mechanical engagement, such as in immobilization or microgravity, retains YAP/TAZ in the cytoplasm, suppressing anabolic programs and favoring catabolic or quiescent states [30]. This tension-sensitive regulation allows musculoskeletal cells, such as chondrocytes, osteoblasts, and tenocytes, to finely adjust their metabolic and regenerative responses to mechanical conditions. Abnormalities in this pathway, resulting from chronic overload or disuse, are increasingly implicated in the pathogenesis of degenerative musculoskeletal disorders.

In articular cartilage, chondrocytes exemplify the interplay between mechanical stress sensing and metabolic regulation. These cells operate in a balanced environment, where cyclic loading sustains extracellular matrix turnover and mechanical competence. However, abnormal stress profiles, such as excessive compression, repetitive shear, or asymmetric loading, can disrupt mechanotransduction, shifting the balance between anabolic and catabolic processes [31]. This leads to the overproduction of matrix-degrading enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinases and aggrecanases, and a reduction in the synthesis of collagen and aggrecan. Persistent exposure to damaging loading conditions triggers pro-inflammatory pathways, elevates oxidative stress, and induces mitochondrial dysfunction, compromising chondrocyte viability and repair capacity. In this setting, the extracellular matrix loses structural and functional integrity, accelerating the progression of osteoarthritis [32]. Conversely, appropriately tuned mechanical stimulation activates anabolic pathways in chondrocytes, upregulating cartilage-specific matrix gene expression, and promoting metabolic resilience. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of biomechanical interventions designed to maintain or restore cartilage health.

At the tissue-system level, the musculoskeletal apparatus, comprising bone, muscle, tendon, and joint structures, functions as a mechanically coupled network governed by bidirectional feedback loops [33]. Mechanical loading of bone induces strain-driven fluid flow through the lacunar–canalicular system, which osteocytes sense and translate into biochemical signals like prostaglandins, nitric oxide, and sclerostin. These signals affect osteoblasts and osteoclasts, modulating bone remodeling in response to mechanical demands. Similarly, skeletal muscle contractions generate tensile forces that are transmitted through tendons to joint structures, influencing cartilage mechanobiology and load distribution. Disturbances in one component of this network, such as muscle weakness due to sarcopenia or bone fragility due to osteoporosis, can alter joint loading patterns, changing the mechanical cues perceived by cartilage and synovium. This interdependence creates a self-reinforcing cycle, in which pathological changes propagate across tissues, amplifying degeneration and functional decline. Recognizing and characterizing these systemic mechanobiological feedback loops is essential for developing therapeutic strategies that address multiple tissue types. Approaches that integrate muscle strengthening, bone health optimization, and joint load modulation offer promising avenues for restoring mechanical balance and mitigating progressive musculoskeletal deterioration [34].

Specifically, the Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways represent critical shared mechanobiological hubs in both muscle and cartilage. In sarcopenia, mechanical unloading downregulates PI3K/Akt signaling, leading to FoxO activation and subsequent muscle atrophy via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway [35]. Similarly, in chondrocytes, the suppression of PI3K/Akt signaling under abnormal loading conditions compromises cell survival and matrix synthesis [36]. Furthermore, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway exhibits a dual role, while essential for osteoblast differentiation and muscle repair, its aberrant hyperactivation in cartilage has been linked to hypertrophic differentiation and osteoarthritis progression [37]. These shared pathways highlight that mechanical signals are transduced through conserved molecular cascades, yet they result in tissue-specific fates that collectively drive the comorbidity.

Mechanical basis for assessment and intervention targets

Translating biomechanical principles into clinical practice is crucial for optimizing management strategies for sarcopenia and osteoarthritis [38]. Recent advances in wearable gait and load-monitoring technologies allow for the high-resolution capture of spatiotemporal parameters, joint kinetics, and load distribution during daily activities. By enabling longitudinal, real-world monitoring, these systems facilitate the detection of subclinical deviations from normal loading patterns, which often precede radiographic or symptomatic deterioration. The ability to characterize individual biomechanical signatures supports precision-targeted interventions aimed at preserving structural integrity before irreversible damage occurs.

Exercise prescription remains central to conservative management, though its therapeutic efficacy depends on the precise regulation of mechanical load [39]. The relationship between exercise intensity, volume, and recovery intervals must be carefully calibrated to induce adaptive musculoskeletal remodeling while avoiding excessive joint stress. Incorporating real-time biomechanical feedback into training programs can help refine load progression and improve adherence [40]. Targeted activation of key muscle groups enhances dynamic joint stability, while weight-bearing and cyclical loading activities promote cartilage health by activating mechanotransductive pathways that regulate chondrocyte metabolism and extracellular matrix turnover.

Orthotic interventions serve as mechanical modulators that reshape joint loading. Unloader knee braces effectively redistribute tibiofemoral forces away from degenerative compartments, reducing nociceptive stimuli and potentially decelerating structural progression. Similarly, custom foot orthoses correct malalignment, improve kinetic chain efficiency, and mitigate compensatory stress in proximal and distal segments. Emerging additive-manufacturing techniques enable the development of patient-specific orthoses that integrate pressure mapping data, offering a new level of personalization in mechanical offloading.

In the surgical continuum, perioperative rehabilitation informed by biomechanical optimization is essential for restoring symmetrical, energy-efficient movement patterns. Preoperative conditioning that enhances muscular strength, proprioception, and neuromuscular coordination has been shown to improve postoperative gait mechanics and load-sharing efficiency. After surgery, progressive loading regimens tailored to individual tissue healing timelines can reestablish joint homeostasis, minimize maladaptive compensation, and reduce the likelihood of recurrence or secondary joint involvement [41]. The concept of a “mechanical window of opportunity” emphasizes the importance of timing interventions to coincide with optimal tissue responsiveness and remodeling capacity.

An integrated framework that combines mechanical assessment, targeted interventions, and continuous functional monitoring addresses the multifactorial interplay between sarcopenia and osteoarthritis. This biomechanically grounded strategy not only preserves joint health and mitigates structural degeneration but also sustains functional independence and quality of life throughout the aging process.

The interwoven mechanisms of metabolic inflammation

Immune metabolic imbalance and inflammatory aging

The relationship between immune metabolic imbalance and aging-induced inflammation has become increasingly recognized as a cornerstone in the pathophysiology of chronic diseases, particularly osteoarthritis [42]. Aging is often accompanied by a progressive shift in immune cell profiles, notably the transition from pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages to anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages (Fig. 1). This shift plays a central role in the persistence of inflammation, particularly within synovial tissues. Chronic synovitis, or long-term inflammation of the synovial membrane, is sustained by the continuous activation of immune cells, often triggered by repeated stress or injury [43,44]. This persistent immune activation leads to the continuous secretion of inflammatory mediators, such as cytokines, interleukins, and tumor necrosis factor, further exacerbating joint degeneration. These pro-inflammatory cytokines not only amplify the immune response but also inhibit the regenerative mechanisms of cartilage and bone, thereby accelerating joint tissue degeneration in osteoarthritis.

In parallel with immune changes, mitochondrial dysfunction and the consequent accumulation of reactive oxygen species play a significant role in the inflammatory phenotype of aging [45]. Mitochondria, the central energy producers within cells, are key regulators of inflammation, yet their functionality declines with age [46]. This dysfunction results in a marked increase in reactive oxygen species production, triggering the activation of key inflammatory signaling pathways such as NF-kB and MAPK. These pathways amplify the inflammatory response, further accelerating tissue degradation. Reactive oxygen species-induced oxidative stress also promotes cellular senescence, a process characterized by irreversible cell cycle arrest and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. As senescent cells accumulate, they perpetuate a chronic, low-grade inflammatory state, which accelerates tissue degeneration and contributes to functional decline, particularly in musculoskeletal tissues [47]. Crucially, these senescent cells develop a Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype (SASP), characterized by the robust secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6), chemokines, and matrix-degrading proteases [48]. In the joint microenvironment, chondrocyte-derived SASP factors can induce senescence in neighboring synoviocytes and subchondral bone cells via paracrine signaling. Simultaneously, SASP factors released from aging skeletal muscle enter systemic circulation, potentially exacerbating synovial inflammation [49]. This ‘senescence contagion’ suggests that SASP acts as a biochemical bridge, transmitting degenerative signals between muscle and joint tissues.

Moreover, aging induces a metabolic shift in muscle tissues that exacerbates muscle mass degradation. Aging muscle exhibits a preference for glycolytic metabolism over oxidative phosphorylation, leading to decreased mitochondrial efficiency and increased reliance on less efficient energy production pathways. This shift activates catabolic signaling pathways, particularly the ubiquitin-proteasome system, resulting in increased muscle protein degradation. This change diminishes muscle mass and strength, impairs overall musculoskeletal function, and contributes to frailty, reduced mobility, and increased vulnerability to falls and fractures. Notably, this muscle degradation is not only a hallmark of aging but also a critical factor in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis, where muscle weakness directly influences joint stability and accelerates disease progression.

Myokines, adipokines, and organ crosstalk

A central feature of metabolic inflammation is the interplay between muscle and adipose tissue through the secretion of myokines and adipokines [50]. Myokines, cytokines produced by muscle cells during physical activity, regulate various physiological processes, including bone homeostasis and cartilage metabolism [51]. Myokines such as irisin have been shown to influence osteoblast and osteoclast activity, promoting bone formation and inhibiting bone resorption, thus maintaining bone integrity. These molecules also impact cartilage metabolism, with some myokines exerting protective effects against cartilage degradation. Additionally, myokine secretion facilitates crosstalk between tissues and organs, influencing metabolic pathways in distant tissues such as the liver, adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle. This signaling network is crucial for maintaining metabolic health and tissue homeostasis [52].

In contrast, adipokines, signaling molecules produced by adipose tissue, play a prominent role in driving systemic inflammation, particularly in the context of obesity. Key adipokines such as leptin, adiponectin, and resistin significantly impact the inflammatory environment within the synovium, contributing to cartilage degradation and the progression of osteoarthritis [53]. These adipokines activate matrix metalloproteinases and other catabolic enzymes that degrade extracellular matrix components in cartilage, further exacerbating joint degeneration. The low-grade inflammation driven by adipokines disrupts cartilage homeostasis, promoting destructive processes in the synovial fluid. Therefore, the imbalance between muscle-derived myokines and fat-derived adipokines creates a complex and dynamic network regulating metabolic and inflammatory processes, with far-reaching implications for joint health. In the context of obesity-induced osteoarthritis, the crosstalk between infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP) adipocytes and articular chondrocytes is particularly detrimental. Adipocytes within the IPFP secrete high levels of leptin and resistin, which diffuse into the synovial fluid and bind to receptors on chondrocytes [54]. This interaction upregulates the expression of MMP-13 and ADAMTS-5, directly accelerating type II collagen and aggrecan degradation [55]. Moreover, obesity-related systemic inflammation exacerbates this local crosstalk, creating a feed-forward loop where adipocyte-derived inflammatory signals further sensitize chondrocytes to mechanical stress, thereby linking metabolic overload with structural joint failure [56].

The bidirectional communication between muscle and adipose tissue is particularly evident in the paracrine signaling between these tissues. For example, myostatin, a negative regulator of muscle growth, suppresses adipogenesis, while adiponectin released from adipose tissue modulates muscle metabolism, influencing muscle protein synthesis and function [57]. This interaction is crucial for regulating systemic energy balance and maintaining tissue homeostasis. In aging individuals, the imbalance between muscle wasting (sarcopenia) and excessive adiposity (obesity) exacerbates metabolic disturbances, creating a vicious cycle of increased inflammation, reduced muscle strength, and further fat accumulation. This cycle accelerates the onset and progression of age-related diseases, such as osteoarthritis and metabolic syndrome, making the muscle-adipose tissue interaction a key target for therapeutic intervention.

Additionally, the gut microbiome is emerging as a critical player in regulating metabolic inflammation. Dysbiosis, an imbalance in the composition of the gut microbiota, has been implicated in systemic inflammation and metabolic disturbances, including insulin resistance and increased fat deposition. Short-chain fatty acids, metabolites produced by gut microbiota, interact with immune cells, modulating their activation and cytokine production [58]. These microbial metabolites can either exacerbate or alleviate systemic inflammation, depending on the composition of the microbiome. The close relationship between gut microbiota, immune function, and metabolic health highlights the importance of considering the gut-skeletal muscle-adipose tissue axis in the pathophysiology of metabolic inflammation.

Key nodes in energy metabolism pathways

Energy metabolism in musculoskeletal tissues is regulated by a complex network of molecular regulators, with mTOR and AMPK serving as pivotal control nodes. mTOR acts as a master regulator of anabolic processes, promoting protein synthesis, ribosomal biogenesis, and cellular growth [59]. AMPK, on the other hand, is activated during energy-deficient states to stimulate catabolic pathways, restoring energy balance through enhanced fatty acid oxidation and glucose uptake [60]. The dynamic balance between mTOR and AMPK activity is essential for preserving cellular function, tissue integrity, and metabolic stability. Disruption of this equilibrium, such as sustained mTOR hyperactivation or chronic AMPK suppression, has been closely linked to age-related musculoskeletal disorders, including osteoarthritis. Excessive mTOR signaling promotes aberrant extracellular matrix turnover and tissue hypertrophy, while inadequate AMPK activation impairs autophagy, mitochondrial biogenesis, and the repair capacity of chondrocytes and myocytes.

Insulin resistance, a common metabolic alteration in aging, is a central driver of energy imbalance [61]. By impairing glucose and lipid utilization at the cellular level, insulin resistance reduces ATP availability and fosters the accumulation of metabolic by-products, such as advanced glycation end-products and lipid intermediates. These compounds serve as potent activators of inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB, which accelerates muscle protein breakdown, promotes ectopic fat deposition within muscle fibers, and disrupts chondrocyte metabolism, exacerbating cartilage matrix degradation. Additionally, impaired insulin signaling alters the responsiveness of bone and cartilage cells to anabolic growth factors, shifting the balance toward catabolic dominance and structural deterioration.

Amino acid signaling, particularly through branched-chain amino acids like leucine, is crucial for regulating protein synthesis via mTOR activation [62]. Adequate amino acid availability ensures sustained anabolic processes, supporting muscle hypertrophy and connective tissue maintenance. Conversely, diminished amino acid supply or defective nutrient sensing, as seen in aging and chronic inflammation, reduces translational efficiency and increases proteolysis, contributing to sarcopenia and the weakening of periarticular structures. Emerging evidence also suggests that the amino acid composition, not merely the total quantity, influences collagen synthesis and cartilage turnover, indicating potential for targeted nutritional interventions in osteoarthritis prevention.

The metabolic plasticity of adipose tissue, especially the browning of white adipose depots into thermogenically active brown-like adipocytes, represents an additional regulatory dimension [63]. This transformation, mediated by factors such as irisin and PGC-1α, enhances mitochondrial density and uncoupled respiration, increasing total energy expenditure. In aging individuals, promoting adipose tissue browning may reduce visceral fat burden, lower systemic pro-inflammatory cytokines, and improve insulin sensitivity. These effects could indirectly protect joint structures by mitigating the low-grade inflammation and metabolic strain associated with obesity. Integrating strategies that modulate mTOR-AMPK signaling, improve insulin sensitivity, optimize amino acid availability, and enhance adipose tissue browning may provide a multifaceted approach to preserving musculoskeletal health and counteracting metabolic drivers of osteoarthritis.

Metabolic inflammation phenotype and clinical correlation

The metabolic inflammation phenotype is closely linked to clinical outcomes, particularly in aging-related diseases such as osteoarthritis. Increased inflammatory activity, reflected by elevated cytokine levels and other inflammatory markers, correlates strongly with the intensity of pain and progression of joint damage in osteoarthritis [64]. The higher the levels of systemic inflammation, the greater the degree of cartilage degradation, joint stiffness, and functional limitations, all of which contribute to a significant decline in quality of life. As a result, inflammatory markers are increasingly used as prognostic tools to predict disease progression and guide therapeutic strategies. The correlation between inflammatory activity and clinical outcomes underscores the importance of targeting inflammatory pathways in managing chronic diseases.

The decline in muscle mass and strength, hallmarks of sarcopenia, is directly associated with an increased risk of falls and fractures, particularly in individuals with osteoarthritis [65]. Muscle weakness, combined with joint instability due to cartilage loss, heightens the risk of falls, leading to fractures that further exacerbate functional decline. The loss of muscle strength also impairs tissue regeneration, making it a critical factor in determining disease outcomes.

Imaging studies that assess inflammation and structural damage offer valuable insights into osteoarthritis progression [66]. MRI and other imaging techniques detecting synovial inflammation provide non-invasive means of monitoring disease progression and evaluating intervention efficacy. When combined with clinical and laboratory data, these imaging biomarkers offer a comprehensive understanding of disease status, which is essential for patient stratification in clinical trials.

Laboratory markers of systemic inflammation, such as C-reactive protein and interleukin levels, have proven to be reliable indicators of disease activity in aging and osteoarthritis. These markers not only assess the current inflammatory state but also help stratify patients based on their risk of disease progression. The increasing recognition of inflammatory markers as prognostic tools underscores the growing role of metabolic inflammation in the clinical management of aging-related diseases.

Epigenetics and cross-layer integration

DNA methylation and susceptibility

The identification of muscle- and cartilage-specific methylation loci has emerged as a pivotal area in understanding the molecular basis of tissue susceptibility to degeneration. High-resolution genome-wide methylation profiling has revealed CpG sites with marked tissue specificity, which may define the intrinsic resilience of musculoskeletal cells to mechanical strain, oxidative injury, and chronic inflammatory challenges. These methylation signatures act as dynamic regulatory nodes, orchestrating transcriptional programs that govern extracellular matrix assembly, sarcomere stability, and reparative capacity. Systematic mapping of these epigenetic landscapes across homeostatic, aging, and pathological states offers the potential to discover early predictive biomarkers, facilitating timely interventions before structural integrity is irreversibly compromised. Moreover, integrating methylation mapping with single-cell multiomics could uncover previously unrecognized subpopulations of progenitor cells whose susceptibility patterns influence overall tissue robustness [67].

Recent studies emphasize the correlation between methylation-derived biological age and functional decline within the musculoskeletal system. Biological age, inferred from methylation algorithms, often diverges from chronological age, reflecting the cumulative impact of environmental insults, metabolic stress, and systemic inflammation [68]. In both skeletal muscle and articular cartilage, accelerated epigenetic aging is associated with reduced anabolic signaling, impaired regenerative potential, and diminished load-bearing capacity [69]. This relationship positions DNA methylation as a sensitive molecular marker of tissue vitality, capable of revealing subclinical degenerative processes long before clinical symptoms manifest. Notably, longitudinal studies suggest that the gap between epigenetic and chronological age may serve as a quantitative index to evaluate the efficacy of therapeutic interventions aimed at delaying musculoskeletal decline.

Environmental and lifestyle factors, including nutrient imbalances, repetitive overloading, sedentary behavior, and exposure to pollutants, can induce site-specific methylation changes, many of which are reversible [70]. Experimental models have demonstrated that the targeted removal of detrimental stimuli, the application of demethylating agents, structured physical training, and nutraceutical interventions can partially restore methylation patterns at key regulatory loci (Fig. 2). This reversibility underscores the plasticity of the musculoskeletal epigenome and supports the potential for “epigenetic conditioning” strategies to reprogram at-risk tissues toward a more youthful molecular architecture. Such approaches could redefine preventive orthopedics by incorporating epigenetic monitoring into rehabilitation protocols.

The development of advanced risk stratification tools integrating methylation biomarkers with biomechanical loading history, genetic predisposition, and systemic health parameters holds promise for precision orthopedics. These predictive models, leveraging machine learning, could enhance early detection, optimize rehabilitation timelines, and guide surgical decision-making with unprecedented accuracy. Incorporating longitudinal methylation tracking into clinical workflows could reduce the incidence of catastrophic degenerative events, mitigating individual disability and alleviating the socioeconomic burden associated with musculoskeletal disorders.

Epigenetic mechanisms ultimately exert their effects by modulating the accessibility of chromatin to key Transcription Factors (TFs). In cartilage, SOX9 is the master regulator of chondrogenesis, and its downregulation via histone deacetylation is a hallmark of osteoarthritis [71]. Conversely, in skeletal muscle, the myogenic regulatory factors MyoD and Myf5 are essential for muscle repair, their recruitment to target promoters is often hindered by age-related chromatin condensation [72]. Furthermore, the inflammatory transcription factor NF-κB is constitutively activated in both tissues due to epigenetic loss of repression, driving the expression of catabolic genes [73]. Targeting the epigenetic enzymes that regulate the binding accessibility of these TFs represents a precision strategy to restore anabolic homeostasis.

Histone modifications and chromatin accessibility

Histone modifications are central determinants of chromatin architecture, influencing the transcriptional regulation of genes essential for musculoskeletal homeostasis [74]. Detailed mapping of histone acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination patterns in key anabolic and mechanosensitive loci has elucidated the molecular logic by which tissues adapt to both physiological and pathological stimuli. These modifications alter nucleosome stability, thereby modulating the capacity of muscle and cartilage cells to respond transcriptionally to growth factors, inflammatory mediators, or mechanical stress. Constructing high-resolution histone modification atlases for musculoskeletal tissues may reveal novel “epigenetic checkpoints” that govern the balance between repair and degeneration.

Changes in chromatin accessibility are central to the transcriptional reprogramming observed in osteoarthritis, sarcopenia, and disuse atrophy. Chromatin remodeling events selectively activate catabolic pathways while suppressing anabolic gene networks, accelerating tissue deterioration [75]. High-throughput techniques such as ATAC-seq and ChIP-seq now allow for unprecedented resolution in mapping these accessibility changes, linking structural chromatin states to functional transcriptomic alterations [76]. This expanded knowledge base provides a framework for therapeutically reconfiguring chromatin states to restore regenerative potential.

The interface between mechanical loading and epigenetic regulation has become an increasingly recognized factor in tissue fate determination. Mechanotransduction pathways, mediated by integrins, cytoskeletal scaffolds, and the nuclear lamina, can directly modulate histone modification patterns and chromatin compaction, effectively encoding a “mechanical memory” within the epigenome [77]. This mechano-epigenetic coupling offers a compelling explanation for why tissues subjected to chronic overload or underload develop distinct molecular phenotypes. Understanding these processes could lead to therapies that restore physiological epigenetic states through controlled mechanical interventions, such as precision rehabilitation protocols or biomechanically optimized orthotics.

Therapeutic targeting of histone-modifying enzymes is an emerging translational opportunity. Small-molecule modulators of histone acetyltransferases, deacetylases, and methyltransferases have demonstrated the ability to skew gene expression toward regenerative or anti-inflammatory states in preclinical models [78]. Rational drug design, leveraging structural insights into enzyme-substrate interactions, may yield selective compounds capable of fine-tuning epigenetic landscapes without broadly disrupting cellular homeostasis, thereby expanding the pharmacological toolkit for musculoskeletal medicine.

Non-coding RNA and intercellular communication

MicroRNAs are pivotal post-transcriptional regulators of protein turnover, extracellular matrix organization, and inflammatory signaling in muscle and cartilage. Altered expression of specific microRNAs is strongly associated with pathological conditions such as muscle wasting and cartilage degradation, positioning them as promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets [79]. Studies involving miRNA mimics and inhibitors have demonstrated their potential to restore anabolic-catabolic balance, suggesting clinical applicability in restoring musculoskeletal homeostasis. Additionally, recent advancements in miRNA-based therapeutic delivery systems have opened new avenues for targeted interventions, highlighting the translational promise of miRNA manipulation in orthopedic care [80]. Specific miRNAs have emerged as dual regulators in this comorbidity. For instance, miR-140 is a cartilage-specific miRNA whose deficiency leads to early-onset osteoarthritis and growth defects, while its overexpression protects against cartilage degradation [81]. In skeletal muscle, the 'myomiRs' (miR-1, miR-29, miR-133, and miR-206) are crucial for myogenesis [82,83]. Notably, elevated serum levels of miR-206 have been correlated with both muscle atrophy in sarcopenia and inflammatory severity in osteoarthritis [84]. Additionally, miR-29 family members function as key regulators of extracellular matrix fibrosis in both muscle and cartilage, suggesting that targeting these shared epigenetic modulators could offer therapeutic benefits for both tissues simultaneously [85].

Long non-coding RNAs and circular RNAs significantly expand the regulatory landscape of musculoskeletal biology [86]. These molecules act as scaffolds, decoys, or molecular sponges, regulating complex transcriptional and post-transcriptional events through interactions with microRNAs, transcription factors, and chromatin remodelers. Network analyses have revealed that certain long non-coding RNAs and circular RNAs function as central “hub” molecules within disease-related regulatory networks, exerting profound control over tissue fate decisions. For example, specific long non-coding RNAs have been shown to modulate inflammation and matrix degradation in cartilage, making them attractive targets for therapeutic modulation. This hierarchical regulatory structure underscores the potential for targeting key non-coding RNAs to reprogram disease progression, offering new strategies for managing musculoskeletal diseases.

Extracellular vesicles, such as exosomes and microvesicles, serve as critical mediators of intercellular communication by transporting microRNAs, long non-coding RNAs, and other regulatory molecules across tissue boundaries [87]. These vesicles facilitate crosstalk between muscle, bone, cartilage, and immune cells, enabling adaptive responses to mechanical, inflammatory, and metabolic challenges. Understanding the molecular mechanisms governing vesicle cargo selection, secretion, and uptake is crucial for developing targeted delivery systems for therapeutic nucleic acids. This intercellular communication mechanism regulates tissue integrity and joint homeostasis while holding promise for restoring tissue function in degenerative diseases.

Liquid biopsy techniques that analyze circulating non-coding RNAs and vesicle-associated biomarkers in blood or synovial fluid provide a minimally invasive approach to assess the epigenetic landscape of musculoskeletal tissues. These real-time biomarkers could offer early indications of pathological changes, facilitating more accurate disease staging and monitoring. Furthermore, incorporating liquid biopsy biomarkers into clinical decision-making could enhance diagnostic precision, enable early intervention, and allow dynamic adjustment of treatment regimens, ultimately optimizing patient outcomes in musculoskeletal care [88].

Multi-omics integration and computational modeling

The convergence of single-cell transcriptomics with spatial omics has significantly transformed musculoskeletal tissue research [89]. These integrative methodologies capture not only the transcriptional profiles of individual cells but also preserve their spatial context within the tissue microenvironment (Fig. 2). By mapping cellular compositions in regenerative niches, inflammatory infiltrates, and degenerative lesions, this approach provides a comprehensive view of musculoskeletal pathology. This spatially resolved data shifts the focus from traditional, pathway-centric models to a more holistic, ecosystem-level understanding of tissue dysfunction, revealing previously unrecognized cellular interactions and spatial dynamics that underlie disease progression.

Integrating proteomics and metabolomics further enriches our understanding by offering functional insights that complement genomic and transcriptomic data [90]. Proteomics enables detailed quantification of structural and enzymatic proteins, post-translational modifications, and protein interaction networks, facilitating the identification of key molecular players in tissue homeostasis and degeneration [91]. Meanwhile, metabolomics captures the biochemical fingerprint of cellular metabolic states, shedding light on metabolic processes that drive tissue adaptation or failure. The coupling of proteomic and metabolomic data can uncover critical metabolic-structural interactions and identify vulnerable points within these pathways, which could serve as therapeutic targets.

Advanced computational approaches, combining genomic, epigenomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data, now enable the dissection of causal mechanisms within complex biological systems. Causal inference frameworks, integrated with multimodal machine learning algorithms, generate predictive models capable of identifying disease subtypes and forecasting individual therapeutic responses [92]. These models can prioritize candidate interventions based on data-driven insights, creating an iterative feedback loop that can be refined through experimental validation. Such computational tools are poised to revolutionize personalized medicine by identifying the most effective treatment strategies for musculoskeletal diseases.

For these computational models to be seamlessly integrated into clinical practice, they must be interpretable and transparent. The ability to explain the decision-making process behind patient stratification and treatment recommendations will enhance clinician trust and facilitate the adoption of these models in real-world settings. By bridging molecular profiling with actionable clinical pathways, multi-omics integration has the potential to fundamentally redefine the prediction, prevention, and treatment of degenerative musculoskeletal diseases, transforming them into more personalized, precise, and targeted interventions [93].

Evaluation of classification and intervention pathways

Diagnostic criteria and risk stratification reconstruction

The refinement of diagnostic criteria and risk stratification for musculoskeletal disorders, particularly sarcopenia and osteoarthritis, is essential for advancing precision medicine. Establishing standardized diagnostic criteria across diverse populations and clinical settings improves the accuracy and consistency of diagnoses, enabling more effective management. While sarcopenia and osteoarthritis are both multifaceted conditions, their diagnosis often relies on clinical judgment and subjective assessments, leading to variability in care. To address this, universally accepted diagnostic criteria that encompass both functional and structural components are needed. Key parameters, such as muscle strength, physical performance, advanced imaging technologies, and emerging biomarkers, should be incorporated to predict disease progression. By establishing evidence-based criteria, healthcare systems can detect these disorders at earlier stages, enhancing treatment outcomes and cost-effectiveness [94]. Furthermore, such criteria will improve musculoskeletal health management and reduce the economic burden of these conditions on healthcare infrastructures.

A novel three-dimensional classification system, integrating structural, functional, and symptomatic aspects of musculoskeletal health, offers a significant advancement. This system enables clinicians to assess anatomical impairments alongside functional declines and pain, which are critical factors affecting patient quality of life. Structural changes in muscle, bone, and joint tissues often coincide with functional impairments, including loss of strength and mobility, as well as the onset of symptoms such as pain and discomfort. This multidimensional approach provides a comprehensive view of the patient's condition, leading to more accurate assessments [95]. It also allows for personalized and dynamic treatment approaches, improving the management of sarcopenia and osteoarthritis. Additionally, this classification framework facilitates the tracking of disease progression, ensuring timely intervention and potentially reducing the need for invasive treatments later.

The integration of biomechanical and metabolic-inflammatory models further refines risk stratification. By combining biomechanical evaluations with metabolic and inflammatory markers, a more accurate and dynamic risk scoring system can be developed [96]. This integrated framework enables healthcare professionals to predict disease progression and tailor interventions effectively (Fig. 2). Real-time monitoring provides detailed insights into the mechanisms driving disease progression, identifying potential therapeutic targets. These models can track disease evolution, offering adaptive risk assessments and enabling proactive interventions. Such flexibility is invaluable in making early, evidence-based decisions that mitigate the impact of these chronic conditions.

Furthermore, stratifying disease progression risks and identifying reversibility indicators are essential for guiding clinical decision-making. Clinicians need to differentiate between those at high risk of irreversible damage and those whose conditions may still be treatable. For instance, joint degradation, muscle wasting, and specific inflammatory biomarkers signal irreversible damage, necessitating urgent intervention. Conversely, metabolic disturbances and early inflammatory responses may be reversible with targeted treatments. Risk stratification empowers healthcare providers to personalize treatment regimens, selecting the most appropriate interventions based on each patient's risk profile [97]. This differentiation is crucial in preventing disease escalation, improving patient outcomes, and reducing the long-term healthcare burden.

Exercise and nutritional interventions

Exercise and nutrition remain central to the management of musculoskeletal disorders, with growing scientific evidence validating their roles [98]. Combining resistance training with aerobic exercises is an effective strategy for addressing both sarcopenia and osteoarthritis. Resistance exercise, particularly strength training, is critical for maintaining muscle mass and strength, which are essential for physical function in aging individuals. Stronger muscles improve mobility and provide joint stability, particularly beneficial for individuals with osteoarthritis. Aerobic exercises, on the other hand, enhance cardiovascular health and overall metabolic function, which help manage osteoarthritis by reducing inflammation and promoting joint health. The concurrent implementation of both resistance and aerobic exercises through periodized or parallel training ensures that multiple aspects of physical health are addressed, maximizing benefits. For optimal results, exercise regimens should be tailored to individual abilities, ensuring long-term adherence and sustained improvement. Additionally, integrating exercise with personalized goals enhances patient engagement, improving the likelihood of long-term adherence [99].

Nutrition, particularly protein intake, plays a vital role in muscle maintenance and repair [100]. Protein, especially when combined with leucine, an essential amino acid, stimulates muscle protein synthesis and counteracts the effects of sarcopenia. Leucine activates key signaling pathways, such as the mTOR pathway, that are involved in muscle growth and repair. Furthermore, the timing of protein intake, particularly the distribution of protein consumption across several meals, can enhance muscle anabolism and help preserve muscle mass, especially in individuals with sarcopenia or those undergoing rehabilitation. Combining nutritional strategies with appropriate exercise regimens significantly amplifies the benefits of both, contributing to greater improvements in muscle preservation, function, and rehabilitation outcomes [101].

The synergistic effects of vitamin D and anti-inflammatory nutrients like omega-3 fatty acids are gaining attention in musculoskeletal disease management. Vitamin D is essential for bone health and plays a critical role in muscle function [102]. Research consistently shows that low levels of vitamin D are associated with muscle weakness, increased fall risk, and worsened functional outcomes in older adults. Supplementing vitamin D in deficient populations can reduce these risks and improve physical performance. Similarly, omega-3 fatty acids, known for their anti-inflammatory properties, reduce chronic inflammation, a key factor in osteoarthritis pathogenesis. By incorporating these nutrients into a balanced diet, individuals with musculoskeletal conditions can experience reduced joint pain, improved mobility, and better overall health outcomes. This integrated approach emphasizes not only addressing mechanical aspects of musculoskeletal health but also considering nutritional factors influencing muscle and joint function.

Patient adherence to exercise and nutritional interventions remains one of the most significant challenges in managing musculoskeletal diseases. Developing personalized care plans, implementing educational initiatives, and integrating technology, such as wearable devices and mobile health platforms, are essential to overcoming this barrier. Digital tools that track progress and remind patients of their exercise schedules can enhance patient engagement, ensuring the sustainability of the treatment regimen. Home-based interventions, facilitated by technology, offer patients the flexibility to follow prescribed regimens without frequent clinic visits, improving long-term adherence. Creating accessible, patient-centered pathways for integrating exercise and nutrition into daily routines will enhance treatment efficacy, improve health outcomes, and reduce the need for more invasive interventions.

Targeted medications and regenerative strategies

The development of pharmacological treatments targeting the inflammatory and catabolic pathways in musculoskeletal diseases has led to promising therapeutic options. Combination therapies addressing both inflammation and tissue degradation are gaining traction in managing osteoarthritis and sarcopenia (Fig. 2). Drugs targeting pro-inflammatory cytokines or enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinases, can slow joint destruction [103]. Simultaneously, anti-catabolic agents counteract muscle wasting, improving joint function and muscle strength. Integrating these treatments provides a comprehensive approach that addresses the systemic and localized inflammation driving musculoskeletal degeneration. Molecular profiling and genetic insights enable personalized treatments, tailoring drugs to an individual's specific molecular makeup and disease stage [104].

Anabolic agents and growth factors are also showing promise in promoting tissue synthesis and repair. Myostatin inhibitors, anabolic steroids, and other growth factors stimulate muscle growth, enhance bone mineral density, and promote cartilage repair. These agents activate anabolic signaling pathways, enhancing protein synthesis and facilitating tissue regeneration. Additionally, cell-based therapies, such as stem cell injections and gene therapies, are opening new frontiers in regenerative medicine [105]. These therapies have the potential to repair damaged tissues at the cellular level, offering long-term solutions for conditions previously difficult to treat. Combining cell-based therapies with pharmacological interventions addresses both symptoms and root causes of musculoskeletal diseases.

Moreover, bioactive materials like scaffolds, hydrogels, and biodegradable implants are gaining attention in regenerative therapies. These materials provide structural support to damaged tissues, promoting cell attachment and facilitating tissue growth. When combined with stem cell therapies or growth factors, bioactive materials can significantly enhance the regeneration of cartilage, bone, and muscle. This integrated approach, combining cellular therapies, biomaterials, and pharmacological treatments, represents a powerful strategy for musculoskeletal degeneration, offering patients a comprehensive treatment solution that targets both the structural and functional aspects of disease.

Weight management and metabolic modulation also play essential roles in managing musculoskeletal diseases. Medications regulating metabolism, such as GLP-1 receptor agonists, aid in weight control, reducing joint burden and improving function [106]. Weight management is particularly important in osteoarthritis, where excess weight exacerbates joint stress and inflammation. Metabolic modulators also enhance muscle metabolism, promoting anabolic pathways and slowing sarcopenia progression. When integrated into a broader treatment plan, metabolic regulators significantly improve outcomes for patients with both metabolic and musculoskeletal disorders, making them an integral part of a holistic therapeutic strategy.

Community prevention and continuous care

Community-based interventions are crucial for preventing and managing musculoskeletal diseases, especially in high-risk populations, such as older adults and individuals with a family history of osteoarthritis or sarcopenia [107]. Regular screening programs in these groups enable early detection, allowing timely interventions that mitigate disease progression. Early interventions, including exercise prescriptions, dietary modifications, and pharmacological treatments, improve long-term health outcomes and reduce severe musculoskeletal degeneration [108]. A tiered referral system, directing high-risk individuals to specialized care units, ensures appropriate care and minimizes delays, enhancing treatment effectiveness [109].

Home-based exercise prescriptions and remote monitoring platforms represent a paradigm shift in musculoskeletal disease management. These approaches empower patients to engage in treatment regimens from home while allowing healthcare providers to track progress and adjust interventions as needed. Digital health tools, such as mobile apps and wearable devices, provide real-time feedback and reminders, ensuring patient adherence to prescribed regimens [110]. Remote monitoring also allows clinicians to assess disease progression outside clinical settings, enhancing care continuity and patient engagement [111].

A multidisciplinary approach is essential for managing complex musculoskeletal conditions. Combining the expertise of orthopedic surgeons, rheumatologists, physiotherapists, nutritionists, and other healthcare professionals creates a comprehensive care model. This collaborative approach ensures that all aspects of disease prevention and rehabilitation are addressed. Furthermore, continuous care networks extending beyond the clinic provide ongoing support, ensuring patients remain engaged and continue receiving appropriate care throughout their journey.

Finally, cost-effectiveness assessments and policy integration are crucial for making advanced musculoskeletal interventions accessible to all [112]. Governments and healthcare systems must evaluate the economic impact of these interventions, considering long-term savings from early detection, prevention, and efficient treatments. Integrating these interventions into national health policies can optimize care for populations at risk, ultimately improving public health outcomes and reducing healthcare costs.

Conclusion and future prospects

Core insights

Sarcopenia and osteoarthritis are two prevalent musculoskeletal disorders that frequently coexist in aging populations, presenting significant clinical challenges [113]. Both conditions share common underlying mechanisms, such as chronic low-grade inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and biomechanical stress, which collectively contribute to the progressive degeneration of musculoskeletal structures. Sarcopenia, characterized by the loss of muscle mass and strength, and osteoarthritis, defined by cartilage degradation and joint dysfunction, often overlap in patients, complicating their management. The coexistence of these disorders accelerates functional decline, increasing disability and diminishing quality of life. Muscle weakness exacerbates joint pain, while joint dysfunction places additional strain on muscles, creating a detrimental feedback loop that accelerates the progression of both conditions [114].

The interplay between sarcopenia and osteoarthritis presents unique challenges in clinical practice. Patients with both conditions experience a vicious cycle in which the presence of one disorder worsens the prognosis of the other, impeding functional capacity and increasing the risk of severe disability. This dual burden complicates the development of effective treatment regimens, as traditional approaches tend to focus on each condition independently. Consequently, there is a pressing need for more integrated, comprehensive treatment strategies that address both muscle and joint pathologies simultaneously.

Recent advancements have focused on integrated, multimodal therapeutic approaches that target both sarcopenia and osteoarthritis. These strategies often involve a combination of pharmacological treatments, physical therapy, exercise regimens, and nutritional interventions. Emerging research has emphasized the importance of personalized care models that take into account the complex interrelationship between muscle function, joint health, and metabolic factors. Personalized treatments aim not only to prevent further deterioration but also to enhance overall functional outcomes, reducing the need for invasive interventions and improving quality of life. These advancements suggest that the future of treating these comorbidities will revolve around individualized care plans tailored to each patient's unique physiological, biomechanical, and metabolic profile.

Personalized precision interventions have become a critical focus for future research. The integration of genomics, epigenomics, and other omics technologies, along with advances in machine learning and artificial intelligence, offers exciting potential for creating predictive models of disease progression [115]. These models will enable clinicians to tailor treatments based on each patient's unique genetic, metabolic, and physiological characteristics. The shift toward precision medicine will improve the efficacy of interventions, allowing for earlier detection and more targeted treatments to prevent or slow disease progression. This paradigm shift is expected to lead to more accurate diagnostic tools, better prognostic models, and more effective therapeutic options than current, one-size-fits-all approaches.

Looking ahead, the field must continue to integrate precision medicine, multi-omics technologies, and interdisciplinary research. The combination of insights from molecular biology, bioinformatics, clinical medicine, and other fields will advance our understanding of the complex mechanisms underlying sarcopenia and osteoarthritis. This approach will not only deepen our understanding of these diseases but also provide novel biomarkers for early diagnosis and facilitate the development of more targeted therapeutic strategies. Collaborative research across scientific disciplines will be key to enhancing both the prevention and treatment of these debilitating conditions.

Multi-omics-driven precision intervention

Multi-omics-driven precision interventions have gained significant traction in musculoskeletal medicine. Through the application of cutting-edge omics technologies, including genomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and transcriptomics, researchers are advancing our understanding of the molecular underpinnings of sarcopenia and osteoarthritis. These technologies enable the identification of specific biomarkers that differentiate disease subtypes, facilitating more precise and personalized therapeutic interventions. By classifying patients according to their molecular profiles, clinicians can ensure that individuals receive the most effective treatments tailored to their unique disease characteristics.

One of the most promising advancements in precision medicine for these conditions is the use of adaptive interventions, particularly through N of 1 trials. N of 1 trials allow for real-time treatment adjustments based on an individual's response to therapy. This patient-centered approach ensures that treatment regimens remain relevant to the patient's evolving condition, providing a dynamic feedback loop between the clinician, the treatment protocol, and the patient. As the field progresses, adaptive interventions are likely to become central to the development of flexible, individualized treatment plans that optimize outcomes for patients with sarcopenia and osteoarthritis.

Another innovative approach is the development of digital twin models, which replicate an individual's physiological state in a virtual environment [116]. These models integrate multi-omics data, including genetic, metabolic, and environmental factors, to simulate how a patient's body will respond to various treatments. By testing different therapeutic options within the digital twin framework, clinicians can predict the most effective course of treatment without relying on trial-and-error in clinical settings. This capability significantly enhances decision-making by providing a tailored, risk-minimized approach to treatment. By incorporating dynamic simulations of disease progression and treatment responses, digital twin technology has the potential to revolutionize how clinicians approach the management of musculoskeletal disorders, particularly in the context of comorbidities like sarcopenia and osteoarthritis.

Real-time feedback systems, supported by wearable devices and digital platforms, are another critical component of precision medicine [117]. These technologies allow continuous monitoring of a patient's activity levels, muscle function, and joint mobility, providing both patients and healthcare providers with valuable insights into treatment efficacy and adherence. By creating a real-time feedback loop, these systems enable healthcare providers to make immediate adjustments to treatment regimens, ensuring that the patient's care remains aligned with their evolving needs. Integrating these data-driven insights into a learning healthcare system will ensure that treatment plans are continuously optimized, enhancing patient outcomes and satisfaction.

Clinical trials and real-world evidence

As musculoskeletal medicine evolves, the role of clinical trials and real-world evidence in shaping treatment strategies for sarcopenia and osteoarthritis has become increasingly important. While traditional randomized controlled trials have been instrumental in establishing treatment efficacy, they often fail to capture the complexity of clinical practice in real-world settings. To address this limitation, innovative trial designs, such as platform trials and adaptive randomization, have emerged. Platform trials enable the simultaneous evaluation of multiple interventions, enhancing the efficiency of testing and allowing researchers to compare various treatment strategies in real time. Adaptive randomization further improves these trials by dynamically adjusting the allocation of patients to different treatment arms based on real-time data, ensuring that patients receive the most promising therapies available. This flexibility allows trials to remain responsive to emerging data, maximizing the chances of identifying effective treatments.

Real-world evidence, derived from patient cohorts and wearable device data, provides critical insights into how treatments perform in everyday clinical settings. Wearable devices that track physical activity, joint function, and muscle mass offer continuous data that can supplement clinical trial findings. This integration of real-world data allows clinicians to refine treatment protocols, ensuring they are grounded in actual patient experiences. By incorporating real-world evidence, researchers can develop more comprehensive, personalized treatment strategies, improving both patient outcomes and clinical decision-making.

Moreover, there is a shift in the focus of outcome measures from structural markers to functional and patient-centered endpoints [118]. Imaging techniques traditionally used to assess joint degeneration or muscle loss do not always correlate with patients' actual functional abilities or quality of life. Future clinical trials are increasingly likely to incorporate functional assessments such as strength, mobility, pain, and overall quality of life as primary outcomes. This shift ensures that treatments are not only effective at halting or reversing structural damage but also improve daily functioning, which is the ultimate goal of any intervention. Additionally, patient-reported outcomes will become increasingly integral to evaluating treatment success, providing valuable insights into the patient's perspective on their health and quality of life.

The integration of vast datasets from clinical trials and real-world studies requires robust data-sharing frameworks that prioritize privacy protection. As healthcare systems collect and analyze larger datasets, ensuring patient confidentiality while facilitating research collaboration becomes essential. Clear guidelines for data sharing and compliance with privacy regulations will promote transparency, enabling broader access to data and fostering more robust analyses across research institutions. These frameworks will be pivotal in enhancing the use of real-world data to improve treatment strategies and patient outcomes.

Breaking the vicious cycle and implementation strategies

To break the vicious cycle of muscle and joint degeneration, a multifaceted approach is required, encompassing both an understanding of the underlying mechanisms and the implementation of effective interventions. Recent research has focused on identifying molecular targets for modulating the progression of sarcopenia and osteoarthritis. Inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and impaired tissue regeneration have been identified as key factors driving disease progression, and therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways hold significant promise. For example, anti-inflammatory agents, metabolic modulators, and tissue regeneration therapies could help restore balance to the musculoskeletal system, preventing further degeneration and improving overall function.

Early intervention plays a crucial role in preventing the escalation of these conditions. Targeting disease processes at the “window of opportunity,” when interventions are most likely to have a significant impact, has the potential to halt or even reverse the course of disease. Early-stage interventions, including exercise regimens, nutritional support, and pharmacotherapy, are critical in managing sarcopenia and osteoarthritis. Secondary prevention strategies, such as routine screening and monitoring of at-risk populations, can identify individuals before they develop severe disease, reducing the overall burden on healthcare systems and improving long-term outcomes [119].

The integration of medical engineering and smart technologies into musculoskeletal care represents another critical strategy for breaking the cycle of degeneration. Devices that track muscle strength, joint mobility, and other functional parameters can provide real-time feedback to both patients and healthcare providers, enabling timely adjustments to treatment protocols. Innovations such as robotic exoskeletons, biofeedback devices, and smart rehabilitation equipment offer more precise, individualized treatments. These technologies allow for better control over treatment delivery and empower patients to manage their conditions more effectively, enhancing functional recovery and improving quality of life.

Finally, international collaboration and sustainable strategies are essential for advancing the prevention and treatment of sarcopenia and osteoarthritis on a global scale. Through the exchange of knowledge, resources, and research findings, countries can collaborate to address the growing burden of these diseases. Implementing sustainable healthcare strategies that prioritize education, infrastructure, and research will ensure that innovative treatments are accessible to diverse populations worldwide. By fostering international partnerships, we can develop a more equitable healthcare system that supports the prevention and treatment of musculoskeletal diseases globally.
