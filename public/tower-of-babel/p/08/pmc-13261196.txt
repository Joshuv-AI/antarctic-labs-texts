Hybrid “respect or resect” mitral valve repair techniques.

Central Message

The “respect” and “resect” techniques should be regarded as complementary components of the surgical armamentarium, enabling individualized mitral valve repair strategies based on the valve's morphology and underlying pathologies.

Contemporary mitral valve repair techniques for mitral regurgitation (MR) attributable to posterior mitral leaflet prolapse involve leaflet resection or preservation. Traditionally, resection has been the preferred method to address prolapsed segments with the removal of excess tissue, sometimes at the expense of leaflet mobility.1,2 However, the “respect” approach uses neochordae to reshape the posterior leaflet into a supportive structure. This preserves the natural leaflet structure, with the aim to enhance coaptation surface.3, 4, 5 This manuscript focuses specifically on posterior leaflet pathology, with all 3 cases involving P2 prolapse, providing a controlled framework for comparing repair strategies across a consistent morphologic pattern.

Methods and Patients

Three patients with severe degenerative MR underwent “respect” and/or “resect” repairs. The first patient, a 67-year-old man, had severe MR from Barlow disease. Preoperative transthoracic echocardiography showed mild leaflet sclerosis, a 10.7-mm vena contracta, a 6.3-mm coaptation defect, and anteriorly directed jet with P2-P3 prolapse. The second and third patients, aged 67 and 62 years, respectively, had severe MR from ruptured P2 chordae with myxomatous disease. The second patient had 3/4 MR with an eccentric anteriorly-directed jet, and a mitral valve area of 3.8 cm2 with a severely dilated left atrium. The third patient had myxomatous leaflets with 3/4 MR with an anterior excentric jet, a mitral valve regurgitant volume of 50 mL, and a mean gradient of 2 mm Hg. Despite similar disease presentations, repair approaches varied among them. Institutional review board approval was not applicable. The requirement for patient informed consent was waived due to the anonymized and educational nature of this video case submission.

Procedures

For the first patient, a minimally invasive right minithoracotomy was performed. Because of the patient’s Barlow disease and the enlarged annulus, neochordae was used to achieve optimal coaptation in the setting of a diffusely redundant posterior leaflet. Optimal leaflet coaptation was achieved using premeasured 24 mm polytetrafluoroethylene artificial chordae. These were measured using a caliper, from the head of the papillary muscle selected for chordae attachment to a near-by valve segment with normal length chordae. Given the annular dilatation associated with Barlow disease and the need for enhanced anteroposterior stabilization, a 42-mm semirigid, saddle-shaped complete annuloplasty ring was implanted to reinforce the annulus. This procedure included a left atrial appendage exclusion and cryoablation and required 76 minutes of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and 64 minutes of aortic crossclamp time. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed good results without a residual leak and a mean gradient of 3 mm Hg with satisfactory coaptation length of 5 mm and no systolic anterior motion (SAM) (see Video 1 for TEE and intra-operative images of the three cases).

In the second patient, excess P2 tissue was identified and deemed suitable for resection. A minimally invasive approach involved a triangular resection of the excess P2 tissue, because the prolapsed segment represented roughly one third of the free margin. The height of the prolapsed portion slightly exceeded the base of the free margin, and the resection geometry was tailored to maintain a posterior leaflet height under 15 mm to reduce the risk of SAM. Because annular pathology was limited and anterior annular integrity was preserved, annular remodeling was achieved using a 34-mm semirigid, double-saddle annuloplasty ring to restore annular geometry. The procedure required 48 minutes of CPB and 39 minutes of aortic crossclamp time. Intraoperative TEE showed a mean gradient of 3 mm Hg, trace MR, and no SAM.

The third patient had a ruptured P2 chordae and underwent a minimally invasive procedure involving a triangular resection, with residual MR on TEE and incomplete coaptation. To ensure appropriate coaptation with a height of approximately 6 mm, the decision was made to add neochordae after limited resection. Free-hand polytetrafluoroethylene neochordae implantation was performed to lower P2, using adjacent chordae as a reference by pulling on them with the hook and measuring the required length, anchored to the anterolateral papillary muscle. Given the combined leaflet pathology and the need for stable annular support after a hybrid repair, a 36-mm semirigid, double-saddle annuloplasty ring was implanted. This procedure required 52 minutes of CPB and 42 minutes of aortic crossclamp time. Intraoperative TEE showed no residual MR, a mean gradient of 2 mm Hg, and no SAM. This mixed approach demonstrates that neochordae can complement resection to achieve optimal results when sufficient leaflet tissue is available.

Discussion and Conclusions

In our experience, focal excess tissue favors resection, diffuse redundancy favors neochordae, and insufficient coaptation after limited resection favors a hybrid approach. Postoperative results are comparable, effectively enhancing leaflet coaptation. These approaches are successfully performed via right minithoracotomy, with excellent outcomes, reasonable duration, and no residual MR. The “resect” technique is preferred when there is excess leaflet tissue amenable for removal, whereas the “respect” technique is favored for functional prolapse of degenerative valves. The choice depends on leaflet condition and the surgeon's experience.
