Dissecting inequity: global systematic review of trauma clinical guidance
Authors: Gabriela Zavala Wong, Maclean S Panshin, Tina Samsamshariat, Mohamed Albirair, Jakob E Gamboa, Colby G Simmons, Ashley D Farley, Lacey N LaGrone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEfforts to strengthen healthcare systems have led to the development of clinical practice guidance, defined as clinical decision-making aids built on scientific evidence, experiential knowledge, and ideally, patient values. This review evaluates the accessibility, relevance, and quality of existing trauma guidance globally.MethodsA systematic review evaluated trauma-related clinical guidance sources published from 2016 to 2023, searching in English across eight databases and 28 professional society websites. Using a combination of Medical Subject Headings terms, a series of protocols, guidelines, position articles, reviews and consensus documents were assessed using the National Guideline Clearinghouse Extent Adherence to Trustworthy Standards (NEATS).ResultsOut of 986 records, 108 met review criteria, excluding unretrievable (13), outdated (25), non-trauma (110), and not fitting a guidance resource definition (730). Almost 90% of trauma guidance resources featured a first author from a high-income country (HIC). When categorizing trauma guidance resources by the first author’s region, 44% came from North America, 8% from South America, 34% from Europe, 13% from Asia and Pacific and 1% from Africa. 82% of clinical guidance was public access with no registration required, and the remaining 18% had an average cost of US$45.7 (13.38 SD) the majority of which was generated by US trauma professional organizations. Regarding quality standard adherence, the mean quality score of all sources was 3.81 (scale 1–5), 77% disclosed the source of funding, 91% involved a multidisciplinary group and 54% explicitly mentioned the inclusion of a methodological expert. On logistic regression, the only factor predictive of a high (≥4) NEATS quality score was the presence of a methodological expert. There was no observed association between quality and language, author origin, multidisciplinary group, and professional society endorsement.DiscussionRetrieved articles were mostly published in English and predominantly produced by HIC authors. Low- and middle-income country (LMIC) researchers relied on collaboration for most publications. Most resources were open access, however, the average cost for those which require paid access (US$45.7) may be cost-prohibitive to LMIC providers. Generally, clinical practice guidance resources have a fair quality score when evaluated with the NEATS scorecard. Nonetheless, methodological expert involvement is often overlooked, despite its impact on guidance resources quality. Limitations of this review include the exclusive use of English for search terms, which may have led to the under-representation of sources in other languages and those from LMIC regions.ConclusionThe reviewed trauma clinical guidance published between 2016 and 2023 predominantly featured authors from HICs, with limited representation from LMICs, despite LMICs bearing a higher injury burden. Only 14% of guidance considered pediatric populations, although trauma is the leading cause of death among this group. Most guidance was published in English, with over 80% being open access. The mean quality score of included guidance, assessed by the NEATS scorecard, was 3.81 out of 5. Enhancing trauma guidance impact may require promoting LMIC authorship, fostering multiregional collaborations and addressing barriers such as cost, registration and language.

Introduction

Injuries are responsible for more than four million deaths per year worldwide and are the leading cause of death among people under 45 years of age.1 2 This represents a major economic and social burden,35 especially for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) which represent more than three-quarters of the world population and where most traumatic injuries occur.6

Clinical practice guidance plays a crucial role in optimizing patient care by helping healthcare providers to make evidence-based decisions promptly.7 These promote interventions of proven benefit and advise against potentially harmful practices, ultimately improving disease outcomes, and providing consistency of care.79 We used the term “guidance” throughout the review to reference all clinical guidance resources (guidelines, protocols, algorithms, best practice management and others).

The World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Association for Trauma Surgery and Intensive Care (IATSIC) developed guidelines for essential trauma care in 2004, and this product has yet to undergo update.10 According to a 2016 systematic review, only 14% of LMICs showed some level of uptake.11 Additionally, prestigious international professional societies do not always include guidelines that consider low-resource settings limitations11 and further, only 28% of all scholarly publications are available without cost.12

In addition, there is a rising concern regarding clinical guidance quality.13 Although guidelines are developed via an extensive systematic review of existing evidence and their recommendations are based on that evidence, the scientific rigor with which they are overseen is diverse.13 14 A systematic review that evaluated 161 clinical practice guidelines based on the Appraisal of Guidelines Research and Evaluation Instrument (AGREE-II) found unfavorable ratings in the following domains: patients’ values, policy values, alignment of values and local applicability.14 Furthermore, higher scores in the AGREE-II (higher quality) were found in guidelines sponsored by government organizations in comparison to the ones produced by professional societies.13 Nonetheless, quality is not the only aspect to consider when evaluating a clinical guidance resource; design, format, end-user experience and practicality are relevant in any clinical setting, especially during time-sensitive scenarios inside an emergency room.15

Motivated by these findings, the present systematic review intends to evaluate the relevance (guidance production per region, guidance topic and target population), accessibility (affordability and end-user experience), and quality of the existing trauma clinical guidance to identify key areas for improvement in development and in dissemination efforts.

Methods

This systematic review and its protocol (PROSPERO: CRD42021292381) followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (see online supplemental file 1: PRISMA checklist) and Methodological Expectations of Cochrane Intervention Reviews (MECIR) standards.16 17 There was no source of funding to conduct this study. Authors do not have any competing interests.

Search strategy

Eight electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, ProQuest, Ovid Medline, Scielo, Google Scholar, and Global Index Medicus) were searched to identify existing trauma clinical guidance resources (books, booklets, consensus statements, protocols, algorithms, and guidelines) using a combination of English Medical Subject Headings and Entry Terms or equivalent (descriptors, keywords, index terms and others). Additionally, we searched the publicly available websites of 28 professional societies registered in the IATSIC (online supplemental file 2: Search Strategy). When English was not available in these webpages, the Google Translate webpage extension was used to translate the content.

Eligibility criteria

We included resources whose content was aligned with the definition from the Institute of Medicine (IOM) of a clinical practice guideline, which describes it as a systematic aid provided for clinical decision making based on scientific evidence, clinical experiential knowledge, and patient values.7 Although, given the trauma community considers protocols, clinical practice guidelines, practice management guidelines and similar as different forms of guidance in general, we used the term “guidance” throughout the article to reference all clinical guidance resources. Eligible resources covered any injury-related topic with the exclusion of prehospital care. Abstracts not associated with a full-text publication (inexistent publication) were excluded. Although there were no language exclusions, search terms were conducted exclusively in English.

In full-text resources that were not available in English, materials were translated to English using online free-access platforms such as Google Translate or Online Doc Translator. We searched for resources published within 5 years of the initial search date (2016–2021). This decision followed the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommendations, which suggested guidelines have a lifespan of 60 months. After the MECIR standards,18 the search strategy was run again in January 2024, as our review was yet to be completed. This allowed for guidance published in 2023 to be included.

Study selection process

All results were imported to Zotero, a citation management software, where duplicates were deleted. Citation files were then exported to Rayyan reference management software. Three authors independently performed the first screening of titles and abstracts (GZW, TS, MSP), with tabulation of the reason for exclusion. All included resources subsequently underwent full-text screening by at least two independent reviewers (GZW, TS, MSP, JEG, CGS). Any discrepancies during screening were resolved by deliberation among at least two authors.

Data collection process

The information extracted from each of the included resources included: (1) Title, (2) Journal, (3) Year, (4) Accessibility (which included languages, cost, and registration needed), (5) Surgical specialty, (6) Clinical problem, (7) Medical complication (8) Target population, (9) Regional collaboration, (10) Multiregional collaboration, (11) Level of trauma center per author, (12) Institution of corresponding author, (13) Region clinical guideline applied, (14) World Bank classification by income, (15) Country by author, (16) Guideline affiliation with a professional society, (17) Quality metrics of a guideline based on the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s National Guideline Clearinghouse Extent Adherence to Trustworthy Standards (NEATS) Instrument,19 (18) Correspondence address, (19) Corresponding author’s email address, (20) resource site link, and (21) Need for translation (online supplemental file 3: Data dictionary). NEATS quality scorecard metrics included: (1) Disclosure of guideline funding source, (2) Presence of multidisciplinary group, (3) Presence of a methodologist, (4) Disclosure of conflicts of interest, (5) Patient and public perspectives, (6) Use of a systematic review-synthesis of evidence, (7) Grading the quality of evidence, (8) Benefits and harms of recommendations, (9) Evidence supporting recommendations, (10) Rating the strength of recommendations, Specific and (11) Unambiguous articulation of recommendations, (12) External review (scientific and clinical experts, organizations, patients, and representatives of the public), and (13) Updating plans. Given that we included a variety of resource types, and not all items were required to include these elements in their published versions, the “use of a systematic review- study selection” and “use of a systematic review- search strategy” were excluded (online supplemental file 4: NEATS quality metrics).

Statistical analysis

Extracted data was presented in frequency tables and a density map. Stata (StataCorp 2023, College Station, Texas, USA) was the software used for data analysis. Pearson’s χ² test was employed to compare categorical variables of the NEATS scorecard and analysis of variance was used to compare NEATS score means among guidance resources in high-income country (HIC), middle-income country (MIC) and low-income country (LIC).

Results

Relevance

Guidance Production

A total of 986 sources were reviewed, and 108 were included (figure 1: Flow Chart). More than 80% of trauma guidance originated from HICs (see figure 2). When classifying published resources by first author’s region, 44% came from North America, 34% from Europe, 13% from Asia and Pacific, 8% from South America and 1% from Africa. More than half of reviewed trauma guidance resources, 64% of 108, had at least one author from the USA. The most represented country from Asia and Pacific was Australia with at least one Australian author in 23% of 108 resources. European participation came mostly from the UK with 20% of 108 resources where at least one author came from the UK. Brazil had the largest production in South America with at least one Brazilian author in 20% of the resources. African countries had fewer than 9% published guidance resources where at least one author was from this region (see figure 2).

Trauma guidance production by World Bank Country Income Classification.

After classifying guidance resource production by World Bank country income classification, HICs had at least one author contributing in 93% of the resources published between 2016 and 2023. MICs and LICs had a smaller involvement, participating in 32% and 6% clinical resources respectively. LMIC authors participated always in collaborative initiatives with HIC authors with the exception of 6% of resources that were created exclusively by MIC representatives. LIC authorship participation in the 6% of guidance resources was all part of a multiregional collaboration along with HIC and MIC authors. When reviewing the authors of all 108 resources, more than 85% of first authors, correspondence authors, and senior authors came from HICs. Of those, 69% were from the USA and Europe. Only 12% first authors, 11% correspondence authors and 10% were senior authors from MIC. All LIC representatives were middle authors. Among the 108 guidance resources, 14% were global collaborations. However, authorship was predominantly from HICs. Only one global collaboration had a first author from a MIC, Argentina. Most world collaboratives feature less than ten MIC collaborators per guidance. Again, LIC authors’ participation represented just 1% and all were middle authors.

81% of trauma guidance resources were sponsored by at least one professional society. Out of those 87 professionals society-endorsed clinical guidance resources, 36% resources involved a multiregional professional society collaboration, most frequently (17%) led by the World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES). 38% of trauma guidance publications were endorsed by North American professional societies, where all of them, except 1% (Ontario Trauma Group) came from US professional societies with the highest representation from the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma (EAST) with 20% guidance resources. European professional societies published 11% of trauma guidance resources, where only three (3%) were regional collaborations and the rest of them single-country professional societies publications that included the UK, Ireland, Austria, Germany, Denmark, Italy, Norway, Spain, and Sweden. Asian professional societies published 10% of trauma guidance resources and these included societies from China, India, Japan, South Korea and Saudi Arabia. Finally in South America, only 6% of trauma clinical guidance resources were published by professional societies, 1% was a regional collaboration and 5% between Argentina, Brazil, Chile and Colombia.

Guidance topics and target populations

Trauma guidance topics encompass resources from multiple disciplines, reflecting the multidisciplinary nature of trauma care and the involvement of providers across several surgical specialties. The majority of topics focused on general surgery and neurosurgical trauma-related conditions; whereas the remaining 11% covered trauma-related conditions in orthopedic, thoracic, urologic and ophthalmologic surgery (see figure 3).

The target population of trauma guidance resources was mainly adults. 15 out of 108 guidance documents were dedicated exclusively to the pediatric population and only 1% to geriatric patients (see figure 4).

Quality metrics

When evaluating the 108 trauma guidance resources according to the NEATS scorecard we identified that 77% explicitly disclosed the funding sources. Most of them were developed by a multidisciplinary group (91%), however only 54% of guidance resources mentioned the presence of a methodological expert during the development process. The other quality metrics of the NEATS scorecard were scored from 1 to 5, 1 being the lowest score and 5 the highest. The mean NEATS score of all 108 guidance resources was 3.81, median 4.0 and mode of 4.1 (95% CI 3.67 to 3.96). When comparing the categorical variable of NEATS scorecard among guidance resources from HICs, MICs and LICs no significant difference was found (table 2). The lowest mean NEATS scores were on considering patient and public perspectives and updating plans explicitly described in trauma guidance resources. No significant difference was found when comparing means (table 2).

Predictive factors of a high NEATS score

A multiple regression analysis was used to estimate the contribution of reviewed variables on the likelihood of resources achieving a mean NEATS score equal to or greater than 4. In this model, we included the English language, whether resources were open access, guidance production classified by income group, declaration of funding resources in guidance, presence of a multidisciplinary group, presence of a methodological expert and whether the guidance resource was endorsed by a professional society. On logistic regression, the only variable that reached significance (p<0.05) was the presence of a methodologist. It was associated with almost a 3-fold increase in obtaining a NEATS score equal to or greater than 4 (table 3).

Discussion

This review highlights the troubling global disparities in the production of trauma clinical guidance, predominantly stemming from HICs and mostly published in English, a language that is not official in the majority of LMICs (89%). Although over 80% of these guidance resources are open access and designed for ease of use, financial barriers persist, with paywalled documents costing an average of US$45.76. The findings reveal significant variances in methodological rigor, with guidance produced by methodological experts achieving higher quality scores. Despite the overall quality of trauma guidance falling into the middle-points field,21 it surpasses that of other medical fields such as dermatology and COVID-related guidance. 22 23 A significant amount of guidance resources covered trauma-related neurosurgical conditions, such as traumatic brain injury; these are relevant because head injuries are among the top three most frequently affected body regions in trauma.24 25 Yet, a gap remains: only 14% of the guidance addresses the urgent needs of the pediatric population, despite unintentional injuries being the leading cause of death among children aged 1–19.26 27 This study’s novelty lies in its thorough examination of the accessibility, relevance, and quality of global trauma guidance, emphasizing the critical need for targeted improvements (methodological rigor, target populations, LMIC participation and others).

It is well established that academic research, and consequently the production of guidance, is more frequently conducted in high-resource settings where there are ample opportunities, ranging from infrastructure to proper methodological training. To address the existing gap, it is essential to recognize the importance of multiregional collaborations, particularly those that include mentorship programs pairing experienced researchers from HICs with investigators from LMICs. This support can aid LMIC institutions in developing independent research initiatives over time. This approach is part of the “capacity building” concept, which is even used by non-profit organizations such as the WHO to enhance the implementation of guidance and foster community practices in health at national, regional, and global levels.28 Fogarty International serves as an excellent example of HIC mentorship in LMIC research initiatives, and a similar structure could be applied to the development of guidance.29 For instance, the WSES has established the National Delegate Project, which identifies key local partners (delegates) with diverse backgrounds from various countries around the world. These delegates are tasked with reporting on local circumstances and helping to refine WSES guidelines, position articles, and consensus conferences.30

Authors from LMICs are seldom first authors and are more commonly found in middle author positions, whereas HIC authors frequently take the lead. This disparity is even evident in “multiregional” or “world” guidance documents. Various factors contribute to this imbalance, including disparities in research infrastructure, funding access, and opportunities for leadership training.

First and foremost, authorship order must be guided by ethical standards that accurately reflect the contributions and leadership of each researcher involved. We recognize that LMIC researchers often face constraints that limit their ability to lead research efforts despite their expertise in local health challenges. Addressing this issue requires ethical authorship frameworks that reflect contributions fairly and proactively support LMIC researchers in assuming first-author positions in studies that directly impact their communities.

Collaboration between HIC and LMIC researchers remains crucial for global health research, and HIC researchers provide valuable contributions, including access to resources and methodologies not always available in LMICs. It is vital that we prioritize equitable knowledge-sharing over a traditional, top-down approach. This includes structured mentorship programs, increased access to leadership training, and establishing independent research infrastructure in LMICs to reduce reliance on external institutions. Nonetheless, HIC support is not always possible or available. To address this, we propose some strategies: (1) advocating for open-access research tools (courses, workshops, and diplomas) and databases, (2) fostering partnerships with institutions that prioritize resource-sharing, and (3) creating regional networks where LMIC researchers can collaboratively share “successful methodologies/models” that could be replicated in similar settings. By integrating these approaches, we can aim to shift toward a more sustainable and autonomous research landscape in LMICs.

Existing trauma guidance is often formulated in HICs with assumptions of access to advanced medical infrastructure and healthcare personnel, making many recommendations impractical for lower-resource settings. Understandably, HICs may not prioritize resource-tailored recommendations. However, local resources are dynamic, influenced by factors such as weather, socio-political conflicts, pandemics, and technology disruptions, affecting reliability—even within HICs. Although guidance adaptations are mostly documented in LMICs, there’s a growing recognition that underserved communities in HICs, like the USA, also face similar resource challenges. For instance, rural non-trauma centers do not have the same equipment as academic urban level 1 trauma centers, yet both provide care to trauma patients (referral is not always possible). If guidance suggests activating a massive transfusion protocol but none exists, it can leave providers feeling morally distressed and unable to deliver the best care. To improve trauma care, we need diverse and inclusive authorship that accounts for varying resource settings, including non-urban centers in HICs. We recommend a more collaborative and inclusive knowledge exchange in guidance development (identify shared needs between LMICs and underserved communities in HICs), feasibility testing of guidance before its implementation and dissemination, and acknowledging the dynamic nature of local resources in guidance development rather than assuming static availability. Considering local context, including staff attitudes and available resources, avoids the failure of guidance fitting the reality of patients and providers.31 Full implementation cannot be achieved if it is not affordable. Studies revealed that guidance tailored to local context was more effective in implementing terms.3234

Many trauma guidance resources are locked behind expensive paywalls, limiting access for clinicians and researchers, especially in LMICs. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the advantages and importance of open-access shared guidance. Many guidance resources are still published in peer-reviewed journals that incur operating costs, making it difficult for some researchers to afford the high fees associated with open-access journals. This open-access model creates a paradox: although it allows readers to access content, it places a financial burden on authors, favoring those who can afford publication fees. When these fees go unpaid, it reduces costs for the authors but results in paywalls for the audience.

The scientific publishing industry profits significantly—often at nearly 40% margins—whereas researchers and editors do not receive payment for their contributions.35 Additionally, advancement in research careers depends on the quantity and quality of published work, measured by the journal impact factor.36 This model is problematic and controversial, as the majority of “free labor” from researchers and editors generates substantial profits for publishing companies.35 Although this model is far from ideal and very complex and unique in nature, there are strategies that could improve the situation. Institutions, non-profits and government agencies could negotiate article-publishing cost discounts. Alternative publishing models such as preprint servers and institutional repositories could provide free dissemination alternatives. Moreover, we urge for more transparent pricing, fair open-access practices, and journals to enhance accessibility, especially for LMIC researchers.

Limitations

Although eight databases were searched, search term language was English exclusively. Even database operators (eg, AND/OR/NOT) are designed to be run in English. There are no standardized multilingual operators across search engines. The research team acknowledged this as a language bias, and translated the search terms into other languages like Spanish and results obtained were not even close to the more than 1000 results retrieved in the first screening process and most of them were not trauma-related. Some search engines may not even retrieve results for certain Spanish terms. This issue could also be applied to other languages such as Chinese, French, Italian, German and more. Strict comparability across different languages is difficult as search engines are unfamiliar with certain non-English vocabularies and may not yield relevant results. Future research is needed to understand how language influences search results, access to information and creates potential biases.

Additionally, two of the major Latin America search engines (Scielo and LILACS) were not included in this review. This exclusion could also lead to an under-representation of trauma guidance production in this region, as these platforms are significant repositories for publications originating from Latin American countries. The Internet Protocol (IP) address was not taken into consideration when running the search strategy and the effects of it on the retrieved results are unknown.

In essence, this caused underreporting of guidance resources in other languages and under-represented our global sample of trauma guidance. Focusing only on English-language studies may contribute to perpetuating inequities in research recognition and dissemination of non-English speakers’ scientific contributions, including guidance resources. In the effort to include the production of non-English researchers we searched for guidance resources in all webpages of the 28 surgical societies affiliated with IATSIC.

Additionally, the 108 guidance resources come mainly from HICs, LMICs underrepresentation (small sample) could influence the analysis of results. In the logistic regression model, the pseudo R2 value was 0.20 (1 highest value), meaning our regression model does not explain entirely the variability of the dataset (NEATS score ≥4) and therefore, there might be room for additional determinants (independent variables) in the model that could help us predict a high NEATS score. Future studies are needed to validate the presented model.

Conclusions

Current trauma clinical guidance largely features authors from HICs with minimal representation from LMICs, despite LMICs bearing a higher injury burden. Guidance topics are relevant to the burden of trauma conditions, such as the example of the amount of guidance dedicated to traumatic brain injury. However, the review guidance failed to consider more informed recommendations for the pediatric population. These were only covered in 14% of the retrieved guidance even though trauma is the leading cause of death in this group. More than 80% of guidance was open access, whereas the average cost of remaining paid-access guidance was US$45.76. The overall quality of the guidance resources included, as measured by the NEATS score, had a mean of 3.81 out of 5 falling slightly over the middle points for quality adherence. Promoting LMIC authorship and multiregional collaborations is essential to recognize the value of cultural perspectives and local expertise in resource allocation for increasing relevance in future guidance development. Improving clinical guidance impact could involve expert methodological input and addressing accessibility barriers such as cost, registration and language.
