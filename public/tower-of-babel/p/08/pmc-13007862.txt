Multi-system inflammatory syndrome in a child with Crohn's disease- a diagnostic dilemma
Authors: Barkha Dhanjani, Shivangi Tetarbe, Dhruv Gandhi, Ira Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-covid multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) can cause life-threatening inflammation in children, particularly in those with underlying conditions like Crohn’s disease (CD). The overlap of symptoms between CD flares and MIS-C can create diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, necessitating high-level clinical suspicion and aggressive management when both conditions coexist. We present a 14-year-old boy, with a 3-year history of CD, who presented with diarrhea, abdominal pain, and weight loss. Investigations revealed anemia, thrombocytosis, hypoalbuminemia, and elevated inflammatory markers and fecal calprotectin. He was treated with methylprednisolone and methotrexate. On day 25 of admission, he developed altered sensorium, inability to ambulate, and hypoxia. He was found to have left ventricular dysfunction, non-hepatic hyperammonemia and cerebral sinus thrombosis. Work-up revealed a pro-inflammatory state with elevated serum ferritin, interleukin-6, d-dimers and positive anti-Covid IgG, and led to the diagnosis of MIS-C overlapping with CD flare. He was treated with intravenous immunoglobulin and recovered.

Introduction

Crohn’s disease(CD) is a chronic relapsing inflammatory bowel disease(IBD) characterized by transmural granulomatous inflammation of any part of the gastrointestinal tract, commonly affecting the ileum or the colon [1]. While patients typically present with gastrointestinal symptoms, they may also present with extra-intestinal manifestations involving the musculoskeletal, ophthalmic, neurological, cardiovascular, cutaneous, and hepatobiliary systems [2]. In the post-Covid era, there has been a significant increase in the occurrence of multi-system inflammatory syndrome in children(MIS-C), the presentation of which closely resembles acute exacerbations of CD. We present a 14-year-old boy, with a 3-year history of CD, with flares from poor medication adherence. In the current presentation, he developed gastrointestinal symptoms suggestive of a Crohn’s flare, however, the presence of rare complications, including non-hepatic hyperammonemia, axonal motor neuropathy, and cardiac dysfunction, in conjunction with positive anti-Covid IgG led to the identification of post-Covid MISC.

Case report

A 14-year-old boy, diagnosed with CD in May 2021, after presenting with abdominal pain and vomiting, was initially treated with oral prednisolone and mesalamine. He presented a year later with worsening symptoms attributed to poor medication adherence. He was treated with intravenous methylprednisolone, followed by oral prednisolone and azathioprine. However, he was lost to follow-up again within 3 months of this presentation. In May 2024, after six months without treatment, he presented with weight loss of 10 kg for 6 months and diarrhea and abdominal pain for 1 month. On presentation, his height was 139 cm (<3rd percentile as per Indian Academy of Pediatrics(IAP) charts) and weight was 23.06 kg (<3rd percentile as per IAP charts). On examination, he had generalized abdominal tenderness, but bowel sounds were normal. Other general and systemic examinations were normal. Investigations of the patient are shown in Table 1. Stool examination found 5–6 pus cells/high power field and stool calprotectin was > 1000 μg/gm. Abdominal ultrasound showed terminal ileal thickening, concentric thickening of the caecum, ascending colon, and proximal transverse colon, omental caking, mesenteric lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly with fatty infiltration, and moderate ascites. Magnetic resonance enterography(MRE) showed multiple areas of mucosal irregularity in the terminal ileum, ileo-caecal junction, and caecum, and asymmetrical and circumferential areas of mural thickening (Fig. 1A). In terminal ileum, 3 focal short segmental areas of bowel thickening with circumferential enhancement and near-complete luminal narrowing were seen. Between areas of luminal narrowing, pseudosacculations were seen on the antimesenteric border (Fig. 1B). The Pediatric CD Activity Index was 42. He was started on intravenous methylprednisolone (2 mg/kg/day) and methotrexate (15 mg/m2/week). On day 6 of intravenous methylprednisolone, the gastrointestinal symptoms resolved, and he was shifted onto oral prednisolone (2 mg/kg/day). On day 25 of admission, he developed unexplained hypoxia in supine position. Chest X-ray was normal, and echocardiography showed left ventricular dysfunction (ejection fraction of 48%) and left ventricular dilatation, for which he was started on enalapril. He also developed acute-onset altered sensorium, urinary incontinence, inability to ambulate, and was found to have fluctuations in heart rate (90–130/min) and blood pressure (varying between 50th–95th percentile for age). Lower limb power was 3/5 bilaterally, deep tendon reflexes were absent, flexor plantar reflex was present, and there was right-sided lower motor neuron facial palsy. Plasma ammonia was 223 mmol/l and in view of non-hepatic hyperammonemia, lactulose and rifaximin was started. Cerebrospinal fluid examination was normal. Brain MR imaging showed diffuse cerebral atrophy and hydrocephalus with mild periventricular edema. MR venography revealed partial chronic thrombosis of confluence of sinuses, right transverse and sigmoid sinus with partial recanalization for which subcutaneous low molecular weight heparin was started. Nerve conduction studies suggested axonal motor neuropathy with proximal involvement. Coxsackie IgM was positive and anti-Covid IgG was positive (>5284BAU/ml). He was treated with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and was able to walk within 48 h. His plasma ammonia normalized in 24 h (98 mmol/l). He was discharged on day 35 of admission on oral prednisolone (2 mg/kg) and methotrexate.

Discussion

This case highlights the multisystem involvement in a pediatric patient of CD, exacerbated by poor treatment adherence, leading to significant complications. The patient's growth retardation, malnutrition, and severe anemia reflect the chronic nature of his disease and its impact on nutrient absorption. Elevated fecal calprotectin, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) indicate active inflammation, while imaging confirmed extensive gastrointestinal involvement with terminal ileal and colonic thickening, a common finding in CD [1]. His deterioration on day 25 was consistent with MIS-C. Sweeny et al. [3] described a 16-year-old boy with CD and overlapping MIS-C features. His presenting symptoms included progressive bloody diarrhea and abdominal pain preceded by rhinorrhea two weeks prior. His laboratory results revealed elevated inflammatory markers, anemia, hyperferritinemia, and elevation of D-dimers with positive anti-Covid IgG, as in our patient. The patient was treated with intravenous methylprednisolone but because of no response to therapy, he received IVIG. Unlike our patient who responded well to IVIG, their patient continued to have bloody diarrhea that only responded to infliximab [3]. Krawiec et al. [4] described a 14-year-old boy who experienced a CD flare complicated by MIS-C. He presented with fever and diarrhea and was found to have anemia, lymphopenia, thrombocytosis, elevated CRP, ESR, and d-dime, similar to our patient. IVIG administration improved his condition, like in our patient [4].

Non-hepatic hyperammonemia in MIS-C may be linked to the prolonged hyperinflammatory state, heightened vulnerability to bacterial infections, or a hypercoagulable state. MIS-C hypercoagulability could result in micro-thromboemboli in the intestines, leading to mucosal and flora alterations, which in may increase the likelihood of infections by urease-producing bacterial [5]. Neurological manifestations in IBD are reported in 3% cases [6]. Lossos et al. [6] reported acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy, mononeuritis multiplex, bibrachial plexopathy, myasthenia gravis, recurrent strokes, cerebral venous thrombosis, and myelopathy in IBD patients. Peripheral neuropathy was mostly reported in ulcerative colitis while myelopathy and myopathy was reported in CD [6]. The neurological involvement in CD can be attributed to several factors, of which immunological derangement and hypercoagulation from MIS-C is relevant in our patient [7].

A key aspect in this case is differentiating MIS-C from IBD flares. Both entities present with elevated inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP, ferritin, d-dimer) [8, 9]. However, MIS-C typically has cardiac and neurological involvement, as in our case, while IBD flares rarely display neuro-cardiac involvement [2, 8]. Additionally, MIS-C is associated with either positive Covid-19 serologies or polymerase chain reaction results. Another differential to consider is Kawasaki Disease which may also present with elevated inflammatory markers but is more likely to have coronary artery aneurysms rather than the ventricular dysfunction seen commonly in MIS-C [8].

Conclusion

Our patient's initial presentation was highly suggestive of a severe CD exacerbation, driven by chronic inflammation and treatment non-adherence. However, the subsequent development of rare complications including non-hepatic hyperammonemia, axonal motor neuropathy, facial palsy, and cardiac dysfunction suggested a distinct, overlapping pathology. The presence of strongly positive anti-Covid IgG antibodies was pivotal in identifying the underlying trigger as MIS-C. The patient's dramatic improvement following IVIG highlights the immune-mediated nature of these conditions and affirms the importance of a high index of suspicion for MIS-C in children with IBD who develop atypical systemic symptoms.
