Introduction

ADPKD occurs in approximately 1/400 to 1/1000 live births, and nearly half of affected patients progress to end‐stage renal disease by the age of 60 years [1]. In Japan, ADPKD accounted for 124 of the 1 551 living‐donor kidney transplants (8.0%) and 9 of the 211 deceased‐donor kidney transplants (4.3%) in 2024 [2].

During the pretransplant evaluation of patients with ADPKD, native nephrectomy may be performed when there is insufficient space in the iliac fossa for kidney allograft placement [3]. IVC thrombosis associated with ADPKD due to mechanical compression by the liver or renal cysts has previously been reported [4, 5]; however, postoperative thrombosis of the IVC or iliac vein following native nephrectomy has not been described. In kidney transplant recipients, thrombosis of the IVC or iliac vein is particularly significant because it may lead not only to fatal pulmonary embolism but also to renal allograft vein thrombosis and subsequent graft loss [6, 7].

Herein, we report two cases of postoperative IVC thrombosis following bilateral native nephrectomy in patients with ADPKD.

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 50‐year‐old woman underwent bilateral native nephrectomy to create space for a kidney allograft. She was receiving maintenance dialysis and had a daily urine output of approximately 200 mL. Her medical history included hypertension and four episodes of renal cyst infection. She had no history of thrombosis and no known risk factors for venous thromboembolism associated with urological surgery [8]. She had markedly enlarged kidneys, with volumes of 2 167 and 2 325 mL on the right and left, respectively. In addition, she had multiple large liver cysts, and the longest anteroposterior diameter of the liver was measured to be 190 mm. Considering the history of recurrent renal cyst infections and cosmetic preference, bilateral nephrectomy was performed. The procedure was carried out in the supine position through a midline abdominal incision. The operation time was 394 min, and the estimated blood loss was 1 758 mL.

During the procedure, CVP was maintained between 5 and 10 mmHg to avoid hypovolemia. The patient received mechanical prophylaxis for VTE using intermittent compression devices over graduated compression stockings and was able to ambulate on POD 2. Although she had no symptoms suggestive of DVT, her D‐dimer level increased to 11.6 μg/mL. CECT on POD 4 revealed an IVC thrombus with mechanical compression of the IVC by the liver, accompanied by caudal dilatation of the IVC (Figure 1). Anticoagulation therapy with heparin was immediately initiated and subsequently transitioned to warfarin at discharge on POD 24. PT‐INR was controlled between 1.5 and 2.5 by a vascular surgeon, and anticoagulation was continued for seven months, until complete resolution of the thrombus was confirmed. Eight months after nephrectomy, living‐donor kidney transplantation was performed uneventfully with mechanical prophylaxis and pharmacologic prophylaxis using enoxaparin sodium, and no recurrence of DVT was observed during 12 years of follow‐up without anticoagulation therapy.

Case 2

A 46‐year‐old man was receiving maintenance dialysis with a daily urine output of approximately 1 000 mL. His medical history included a left renal injury treated with transarterial embolization; however, he had no history of thrombosis and no known risk factors for perioperative VTE. While awaiting native nephrectomy due to insufficient space for placement of a kidney allograft for living‐donor kidney transplantation, he developed renal cyst hemorrhage and infection, and therefore underwent bilateral native nephrectomy. The kidney volumes were 2 632 and 1 221 mL on the right and left, respectively, and the longest anteroposterior diameter of the liver was 198 mm. Surgery was performed in the supine position through a midline abdominal incision. The operation time was 295 min, and the estimated blood loss was 1 040 mL. During surgery, the CVP decreased to below 5 mmHg; therefore, a total of 1 500 mL of extracellular fluid was administered until POD 1 to correct hypovolemia after surgery. Perioperative VTE prophylaxis consisted of mechanical measures only, and the patient was able to ambulate on POD 3. Postoperatively, his D‐dimer level increased to 21.6 μg/mL on POD 6 without clinical signs of DVT. CECT revealed an intravascular thrombus in the IVC and common iliac vein despite caudal dilatation of the IVC from the liver (Figure 2). Anticoagulation therapy with warfarin was initiated, and PT‐INR was controlled between 1.5 and 2.5 by a vascular surgeon. Complete resolution of the thrombus was confirmed four months after surgery, at which point anticoagulation was discontinued. Living‐donor kidney transplantation was successfully performed six months after nephrectomy with mechanical prophylaxis of VTE. The pharmacologic prophylaxis, including anticoagulation therapy, was not administered because of a prior liver cyst hemorrhage before kidney transplantation.

Discussion

Prophylaxis, early detection, and prompt intervention of postoperative DVT are essential to prevent fatal pulmonary embolism. After urological surgery, early ambulation and pharmacologic and/or mechanical prophylaxis are recommended depending on the individual risk stratification [6]. Current guidelines strongly recommend perioperative mechanical thromboprophylaxis and suggest pharmacologic prophylaxis according to individual risk stratification in kidney transplantation recipients who have undergone open nephrectomy [8]. Although evidence is limited, mechanical prophylaxis is also recommended during and after kidney transplantation [9]. Typical symptoms of DVT include lower‐extremity pain and edema; however, patients may remain asymptomatic until a life‐threatening embolic event occurs [10]. When DVT develops, early detection by CECT or US and immediate initiation of anticoagulation therapy are crucial to reduce mortality [10].

This is the first report describing IVC thrombosis following native nephrectomy in patients with ADPKD. Both patients were asymptomatic in the present cases, and elevated D‐dimer levels led to the diagnosis of IVC thrombosis by CECT. Successful anticoagulation therapy allowed both patients to subsequently undergo living‐donor kidney transplantation. As previously mentioned, prophylaxis is essential for postoperative venous thrombosis, but there is no established evidence for prophylaxis specifically targeting IVC thrombosis. In this context, monitoring D‐dimer levels may be a simple and useful method for the early detection of postoperative IVC thrombosis after nephrectomy. The utility of D‐dimer testing for DVT is currently under investigation [11, 12, 13]. In addition, D‐dimer levels help elucidate the need for prolonged anticoagulation because patients with elevated D‐dimer levels after the discontinuation of anticoagulant therapy have an increased risk of recurrence [14]. For definitive diagnosis of IVC and iliac vein thrombosis, CECT is considered more useful than US [15].

Both patients in this report were receiving maintenance dialysis with residual urine output. Performing bilateral native nephrectomy in such patients results in anuria, necessitating ultrafiltration during dialysis until kidney transplantation. Recent consensus statements recommend unilateral nephrectomy because bilateral nephrectomy is associated with a higher risk of complications [3]. In particular, given the increased risk of bleeding associated with anticoagulation therapy, bilateral nephrectomy should generally be avoided. However, bilateral nephrectomy has been reported to provide better physical quality of life after surgery and at long‐term follow‐up [16]. Therefore, the indication for bilateral nephrectomy should be carefully determined based on the risk of complications, the patient's history of cyst hemorrhage or infection, and patient preferences regarding quality of life [3].

In addition, the optimal timing of nephrectomy is a major concern in patients with ADPKD. From the perspective of renal function, simultaneous nephrectomy with kidney transplantation is feasible for well‐selected patients in experienced institutions [3]. Particularly, in patients at high risk of thrombosis, pre‐transplant nephrectomy should be avoided whenever possible because anticoagulation therapy in dialysis patients carries a substantial bleeding risk. When pretransplant nephrectomy is unavoidable, nephrectomy is generally recommended to be performed one to three months before kidney transplantation [3].

In the present two cases, anticoagulation therapy was continued for four to seven months until complete resolution of the thrombus, and kidney transplantation was performed one to two months after thrombus resolution. Although this report includes only a small number of cases, these treatment intervals may provide a practical reference for clinical practice.

In the present cases, we hypothesized that right nephrectomy exacerbated mechanical compression of the IVC by the liver, based on our CECT findings. In fact, previous care reports describing DVT occurred by mechanical compression of the IVC by the liver [4, 5, 17, 18], and IVC obstruction leading to Budd‐Chiari Syndrome after bilateral nephrectomy [19]. Therefore, we focused on the preoperative volume of the right kidney, calculated using the ellipsoidal method (π/6 × length × width × depth), and the longest anteroposterior diameter of the liver. Compared with the other seven patients with ADPKD who underwent nephrectomy at our institution, the two present cases showed larger right kidney volumes (> 2 000 mL), longer anteroposterior liver diameters (> 190 mm), and longer operative time (Table 1). In cases with markedly enlarged kidneys and liver, bilateral nephrectomy in a staged manner may be an optional choice to reduce operative time compared with the simultaneous procedure. Furthermore, renal embolization may be an alternative treatment option, as it can reduce kidney volume by approximately 35%–50% [20]. We hypothesize that preservation of residual right kidney volume may help prevent mechanical compression of the IVC by the liver and avoid prolonged operative time. The major limitations of this study are the small number of cases, which precludes statistical analysis, and the lack of CT volumetry, which would enable more precise measurement of the kidney and liver volumes.

Conclusion

We presented two cases of IVC thrombosis following native nephrectomy in patients with ADPKD. For early diagnosis of IVC thrombosis, measurement of D‐dimer levels may be useful, and CECT is recommended. To investigate the risk factors for thrombosis, as well as prevention strategies and treatment options, further accumulation of cases is necessary.
