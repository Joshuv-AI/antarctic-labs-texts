Essential and multifunctional mpox virus E5 helicase-primase in double and single hexamer
Authors: Yunxia Xu, Yaqi Wu, Yuanyuan Zhang, Kaiting Gao, Xiaoying Wu, Yaxue Yang, Danyang Li, Biao Yang, Zhengyu Zhang, Changjiang Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
An outbreak of mpox virus in May 2022 has spread over 110 nonpandemic regions in the world, posing a great threat to global health. Mpox virus E5, a helicase-primase, plays an essential role in DNA replication, but the molecular mechanisms are elusive. Here, we report seven structures of mpox virus E5 in a double hexamer (DH) and six in single hexamer in different conformations, indicating a rotation mechanism for helicase and a coupling action for primase. The DH is formed through the interface of zinc-binding domains, and the central channel density indicates potential double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), which helps to identify dsDNA binding residues Arg249, Lys286, Lys315, and Lys317. Our work is important not only for understanding poxviral DNA replication but also for the development of novel therapeutics for serious poxviral infections including smallpox virus and mpox virus.

INTRODUCTION

DNA replication is required to separate the double-stranded (ds) DNA to the single-stranded (ss) DNA as a template for synthesis of daughter dsDNA, which is a fundamental biological process for almost all living organisms (1–7). This includes the ori recognition, DNA melting, and unwinding, carried out by DNA helicases (1, 8). On the basis of structural motifs, the DNA helicases are classified into six superfamilies (1, 8, 9).

The eukaryotic replicative helicases belong to the subfamily VI adenosine triphosphatase (ATPases) associated with diverse cellular activities (AAA+) helicases, which compose of six minichromosome maintenance (MCM) subunits 2 to 7 (4, 10–12). The initiation of DNA replication could be divided into two stages. The first stage is that the ori recognition complex and loading factors Cdc6 and Cdt1 recruit two ring-shaped MCM helicases that form an inactive double hexamer (DH) around the duplex DNA, while the second stage is the DH activation that requires the recruitment of two firing factors, Cdc45 and Go-Ichi-Nii-San (GINS), to form the active Cdc45-MCM-GINS holo-helicase (12–14). The DNA replication is strictly regulated by only once per cell division, where the holo-helicase composes of at least 11 proteins (15, 16). In bacteria, such as Escherichia coli, at least three proteins (DnaA, DnaB, and DnaC) are required to form the replicative helicase at the ori (17, 18).

Small DNA viruses—papillomavirus (PV), simian virus 40 (SV40), and adeno-associated virus (AAV) use only a single protein for ori recognition, DNA melting, and unwinding (19–21). The PV E1, SV40 large T-antigen, and AAV Rep belong to the helicase superfamily III (SF3) (9). Crystal structures of some domains of these proteins were reported in the presence or absence of ssDNA, and the viral helicases unwind dsDNA in 3′-5′ direction (22–24). The E1 and large T-antigen were reported to form DH and single hexamer for their functionality (25, 26). More recently, a cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the primase-helicase C-terminal part D5323–785 of the vaccinia virus, a member of poxviridae family, was determined to 4.1 Å (6). African swine fever virus (ASFV), one of nucleocytoplasmic large DNA viruses, belongs to the Asfivirus genus in the family Asfarviridae. ASFV C962R protein was reported to have DNA polymerization and helicase activities and form a DH by two ring-shaped hexamers via the DNA polymerization domains (27). Although great progresses have been achieved in understanding structure and function of viral helicases, there are much fewer studies on full length of viral primase-helicases in DH and single hexamer, which greatly hampers the understanding of mechanisms of viral DNA replication and the development of novel drugs.

The outbreak of mpox in May 2022 poses a great challenge to the global health (28). Mpox virus is a large enveloped dsDNA virus, belonging to Orthopoxvirus genus in the family Poxviridae (29, 30). The viral genomic DNA contains around 197 kilobase pairs and encodes about 182 proteins (30). The mpox virus infects human cells and forms a cytoplasmic viral replication factory, where mpox virus DNA replication proteins form a DNA replication machinery to efficiently and coordinately synthesize viral DNA entirely in the cytoplasm.

Mpox virus DNA polymerase catalytic subunit F8 and heterodimer processivity factor E4 and A22 form the DNA replication machinery (31). In contrast to small DNA viruses, mpox virus E5 performs not only ori binding, dsDNA melting, and unwinding (21, 32–35) but also RNA primer synthesis (36). Mpox virus E5, a DNA helicase-primase, contains 785 amino acids, which can be divided into five domains, primase domain (residues 1 to 237), zinc-binding domain (residues 238 to 322), collar domain (residues 323 to 403), AAA+ helicase domain (residues 404 to 705), and C-terminal domain (residues 706 to 785) (Fig. 1A) (6, 8, 33), suggesting that mpox virus E5 is a multifunctional helicase. To understand E5’s mechanism, we captured different states of the DNA helicase-primase structure.

Here, we identified that mpox virus helicase E5 exists in the DH and single hexamer forms, and we report mpox virus E5 cryo-EM structures in DH in complex with potential dsDNA, hexamer in complex with or without ssDNA (ssDNA hexamer and apo hexamer), hexamer in complex with adenylyl-imidodiphosphate (AMPPNP) and ssDNA (AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer), hexamer in complex with ssDNA-1ATP-3ADP (1ATP-3ADP-ssDNA-hexamer), hexamer with AMPPNP-ssDNA-zinc and primase domains (AMPPNP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer), hexamer with ADP-ssDNA-zinc, and primase domains (ADP-ssDNA -primase-hexamer), which reveal mpox virus E5 mechanism in dsDNA melting, unwinding, and DNA replication. Our work provides the high-resolution structure of a viral DH in potential dsDNA trapped from the expression cells, as well as full-length DNA helicase-primase structures, which provides a structural basis for understanding mechanisms of poxvirus helicase-primases and has a great potential for the development of therapeutics. In addition, this work has a great impact for understanding other DNA viruses’ DNA replication.

RESULTS

Purified mpox virus E5 in double and single hexamer

Mpox virus E5 was cloned into a pFastbac1 vector and expressed in high-five insect expression system (Materials and Methods). The protein was purified by nickel affinity and size exclusion chromatography (Fig. 1B and Materials and Methods), which showed that mpox virus E5 was purified. The negative staining of the purified E5 suggests that E5 is in two forms, the hexamer and DH (fig. S1B). We prepared the cryo-EM samples and performed two-dimensional (2D) classifications, which proved that E5 indeed exists in hexamer and DH forms (fig. S2).

ATPase activity of mpox virus E5

Mpox virus E5 is a family III helicase that uses adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) as an energy resource to unwind dsDNA into ssDNA. To test the ATPase activity (17, 37), E5 was incubated with 1 mM ATP and the released phosphates were detected, which showed that E5 hexamer has ATPase activity with 0.0578 ± 0.0042 μM ATP·s−1 at a concentration of 75 nM (fig. S1A), suggesting that the purified E5 is active.

dsDNA binding activity of mpox virus E5

To check whether purified E5 binds dsDNA, we selected biotin-labeled dsDNA to determine the E5 binding affinity using biolayer interferometry (BLI) (Materials and Methods) (27, 38), which showed that the purified E5 can bind dsDNA (Fig. 1C) with a KD (dissociation constant) of 10.6 ± 0.17 nM, indicating that the purified E5 has dsDNA binding activity.

Helicase activity of mpox virus E5

To check whether E5 has helicase activity, the wild-type E5, or E5323–785, or E5 mutant Lys286Ala was incubated with the fork DNA (39) at 4°C for 30 min, while the ssDNA and the fork DNA as the marker (Materials and Methods). The results showed that the wild-type mpox virus E5 and E5 mutant Lys286Ala did not show a detectable helicase activity, while E5323–785 showed helicase activity (fig. S1C).

Primase activity of mpox virus E5

To check whether E5 has the primase activity, the E5 was incubated with an ssDNA and nucleoside triphosphate (NTP) or deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate (dNTP) individually (Materials and Methods) (36), while an ssDNA (5′ FAM-TCACACATTTTTTTCTAGACACTAAATAAAATAGTAAAAT 3′) and a dsDNA (5′ FAM-TCACACATTTTTTTCTAGACACTAAATAAAATAGTAAAAT 3′; 5′ATTTTACTATTTTATTTAGTGTCTAGAAAAAAATGTGTGA 3′) as a marker. Only new nucleotide was synthesized using the NTP as substrates. The newly generated double-stranded nucleotide suggested that the E5 can synthesize RNA primer using the ssDNA as template (Fig. 1D), indicating that E5 has the primase activity.

Structure determination of mpox virus E5

Purified mpox virus E5 in hexamer and DH states was concentrated to 2 and 1.5 mg/ml, respectively, and frozen to copper grids (Materials and Methods). The DH was picked and structure-determined from the native expression sample by insect cells (fig. S2). The apo hexamer and ssDNA hexamer were classified from the same purified sample (fig. S3). AMPPNP-ssDNA-primase hexamer and AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer were obtained by incubating E5 and AMPPNP for 2 hours (figs. S4 and S5). The 1ATP-3ADP-ssDNA-hexamer and ADP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer were obtained by incubating E5 with ATP and Mg2+ for 1 hour (fig. S6) before the sample was blotted on copper grid and frozen for data collection. The cryo-EM structures of DH, apo hexamer, ssDNA hexamer, AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer, AMPPNP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer, 1ATP-3ADP-ssDNA-hexamer, and ADP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer were determined to 3.22-, 2.87-, 3.03-, 2.51-, 2.91-, 2.90-, and 3.27-Å resolutions (Fig. 2, figs. S2 to S6, and table S1), respectively, which reveal that mpox virus E5 contains five domains, primase, zinc binding, collar, AAA+ helicase, and C-terminal domains, from the N terminus to the C terminus (Figs. 2 and 3 and fig. S7).

DH in complex with potential dsDNA

The DH structure shows that the zinc-binding domains form the interface of the DH, while low-resolution density of primase domain is observed, circling around the zinc-binding domains as a belt (Fig. 2, A to D), suggesting that the primase domain is relatively flexible. The interface residues Asn310, His312, Ser313, and Arg288 from the zinc-binding domain of each protomer have some contacts with the opposite protomer residues, forming the interactions between the two hexamers (Fig. 2E). The second hexamer is not at the mirror position of the first hexamer but twisted a little bit where the zinc-binding domain from one hexamer moves to the middle of two zinc-binding domains of another hexamer (Fig. 2, A and B). The DH has the dumbbell shape with an appropriate size of 113.7 Å in length, 113.7 Å in width, and 268.8 Å in height (Fig. 2, A and B). The zinc-binding domain composites of three antiparallel β strands and two α helices (Fig. 2G). The collar domain consists of four α helices and three antiparallel β strands with the helices at the surface. The AAA+ helicase domain consists of a sandwiched structure that forms by five α helices and two short hairpins (consisting of one α helix and two antiparallel β strands) at the top, five antiparallel β strands at the middle and four short α helices, two antiparallel β strands, and four loops at the bottom. The C-terminal domain extends away at the bottom and contains five α helices connected by two antiparallel β strands (Fig. 2G). The E5 protomer assembles “L” shape with the C-terminal domain at the bottom (Fig. 2G).

There are extra densities in the central channel of the DH, where the densities in the channel of the zinc binding domain are the clearest and become less clear in the collar domain. The densities are identified as potential dsDNA (Fig. 2, C and D), but the resolution is low, and we are unable to assign the sequence of the dsDNA due to the dsDNA from the H5 insect expression cells and therefore heterogeneity. The density at the AAA+ helicase domain is identified as ssDNA (Fig. 2F). We consider that the two hexamers’ interaction with a low binding affinity is hard to form the DH, but the potential dsDNA in the channel might play an important role (Fig. 2, D and E).

To check whether potential dsDNA can be bound in the channel, we measured whether the channel is wide enough to host the dsDNA. The channel radius of the DH and hexamer at the narrowest and widest positions were measured using the HOLE program (40). The channel radius of the zinc-binding domain is more than 12 Å (fig. S8A), while the channel is a highly positively charged channel, consisting of residues Lys286 and Arg249 (Fig. 2H). We speculate that this channel binds potential dsDNA. At the collar domain, the channel radius is larger than 12 Å at the widest position near the N terminus with highly positively charged residues Lys315 and Lys317 and 7.5 Å at the narrowest position near the C terminus with highly negatively charged residues (fig. S8A), suggesting that the collar domain might bind potential dsDNA, but conformational changes are required to allow dsDNA to go through collar domain to the helicase domain. The narrowest radius of the helicase domain is around 2.5 Å with highly hydrophobic and positively charged residues (Fig. 2H and fig. S8A). The radius of the dsDNA is reported to be 10 Å (41). To facilitate dsDNA binding, the radius of the channel has to be larger than 10 Å, which suggests that the zinc-binding domain channel binds potential dsDNA. The potential dsDNA densities are clear in the narrow chamber, but the densities become less clear at the widest chamber, suggesting that the dsDNA is flexible (Fig. 2, C and D). The top channel of the collar domain has some density for potential dsDNA, but no density is observed in the bottom chamber. The helicase domain has a density with radius less than 10 Å, which suggests that the helicase domain binds ssDNA (Fig. 2F). To confirm the potential dsDNA binding residues, we generated single mutant (Lys286Ala) and quadruple mutant (Arg249Ala/Lys286Ala/Lys315Ala/Lys317Ala) of mpox virus E5 and determined their dsDNA binding affinities using BLI. The single mutant reduced dsDNA binding activity with a KD of 91.5 ± 1.5 nM, while the quadruple mutant greatly reduced the dsDNA binding activity with a KD of 171 ± 2.5 nM, suggesting these residues Arg249, Lys286, Lys315, and Lys317 are involved in dsDNA binding (Fig. 2, I and J).

Different conformations of the DH

From the 2D classifications of the DH, we observed that most of the populations of the two hexamers are aligned relatively well, but small portions of them do not (Fig. 2K). Some of them start to bend at the interface between the two hexamers, and some of them adopt obviously conformational changes by bending at a large angel of 30° to 40°. Some of them show that one hexamer is moving away from another. Unfortunately, these small portion particles are not enough for high-resolution structural determination. Nevertheless, these small portion particles might indicate that the DH could be separated into two hexamers.

Hexamer with and without DNA

The hexameric E5 from the purified E5 is sorted into two classes, the apo and ssDNA binding (figs. S3 and S9). In the apo structure, only densities of the collar, helicase, and the C-terminal domains with the approximate dimensions of 120 × 107 × 99 Å are observed, suggesting that the primase and zinc-binding domains are flexible (fig. S9, A to C). The radius of collar and helicase domains at the narrowest positions is 10 and 5 Å (fig. S8B), respectively. At the helicase domain, densities for the DNA binding loop Leu583-Asn594 are not observed, suggesting that this DNA binding loop is disordered, which confirms that the apo structure does not bind any DNA. Between helicase domains, the gaps between protomers are not equal. However, the gap is closed at the bottom by the C-terminal domains. The hexamer is formed by collar domain residues Asn395, Phe327, Thr391, Asp398, and Val401; C-terminal domain residues Arg762, His763, Lys765, and Val711 from one protomer interaction with collar domain residues Leu384, Pro386, Arg389, Thr365, Lys366, and Asn352; and helicase domain residues Ser564, Cys563, Tyr606, Tyr645, and His629 from another neighbor protomer using hydrogen bonds, salt bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals interactions (fig. S9G).

In the purified hexameric sample, there is another class of structure, ssDNA binding hexamer. There is an extra density at the helicase domain, which is identified as ssDNA (figs. S3 and S9, A to F). As the DNA is from the insect cell expression system and is heterogeneous, we could not identify the sequence of the DNA and modeled DNA sequence “CCCC” in the density (Fig. 4).

Compared to the apo hexamer structure, obviously structural changes were observed in the ssDNA binding hexamer, where the DNA binding loop of the helicase domain in the protomers 1, 2, and 4 is involved in the DNA binding, and their densities are visible (Fig. 4C), while density of the DNA binding loop Leu583-Asn594 in the protomers 5 and 6 is still not observed, indicating that these loops are flexible. Coincidently, two-helix hairclip Gly521-Leu549 in protomers 1, 2, 3, and 4 adopts a conformational change in ssDNA binding hexamer, but the hairclip in protomers 5 and 6 has a minor or no conformational change by comparison with the apo hexamer (fig. S12B). Accordingly, Arg585 and Phe588 from protomers 1, 2, 3, and 4 interact with deoxyribose (C2, C3, and C4), phosphate (C3, C4, and C5), and cytosine (C3, C4, and C5) of ssDNA (Fig. 4C), respectively, with the staircase manner. The helicase domain gaps become closer for the ssDNA binding protomers. The diameter of the collar domain in the ssDNA binding hexamer is very similar to that of the apo hexamer, while the diameter of helicase domain and C-terminal domain has some changes (fig. S8), respectively. Although the ssDNA binding causes the conformational changes, where the protomers 1, 2, 3, and 4 have more conformational changes than other protomers, the overall ssDNA binding structure resembles the apo structure with an root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 1.898 Å over 463 aligned residues (fig. S12B).

Hexamer in complex with AMPPNP and helicase active site

Hexameric E5 has two forms in the presence of AMPPNP and Mg2+ ion, one with clear density for the collar, helicase, and C-terminal domains, and another with additional densities for zinc and primase domains for protomers 5 and 6 (Fig. 3 and figs. S4 and S5). In the first form structure, the densities for the helicase and C-terminal domains in protomer 6 become less clear than other domains, suggesting that these two domains are more flexible than others (Fig. 3 and fig. S10), which result in almost invisibility of AMPPNP in protomer 6 and the β and γ phosphates of AMPPNP in protomer 1. The AMPPNP is bound by helicase domains between two protomers, where the adenosine is sandwiched by hydrophobic residues Phe630 and Leu655; surrounded by Ile464, Asp656, and Thr511; and anchored by Asp467 (fig. S11A). The ribose interacts with Leu651 and Thr511, while phosphate groups interact with Thr511, Lys509, Ser510, and Asn605 from the same protomer as the residues for the adenosine binding and Arg620 and Arg619 from a neighboring protomer for phosphate group binding. The Mg2+ ion is bound by the β and γ phosphates of AMPPNP and the Ser510 (fig. S11A).

There is a clear density for ssDNA, which allows us to assign ssDNA (CCCCC). Except protomers 5 and 6, all DNA binding residues are observed from other protomers (Fig. 4B and fig. S11, E to H). Arg585 and Phe588 of protomers 1, 2, 3, and 4 form salt bridges with the phosphate and stack against the deoxyribose and cytosine, respectively, which show the staircase phenomenon interactions (Fig. 4B). The density for the ssDNA is clear enough to identify the directionality of the ssDNA in the 3′-5′ from the C-terminal domain to the N-terminal domain (fig. S10D). This is consistent with other helicase superfamily III helicases (9). The helicase gaps between protomers become even smaller when AMPPNP binds. The radius of collar and helicase domains at the narrowest points is around 10 and 4.5 Å, respectively (fig. S8D). Comparing with the ssDNA-hexamer structure, although the AMPPNP-ssDNA hexamer structure is similar with an RMSD of 1.005 Å over 463 aligned residues, the structure with the additional AMPPNP binding causes protomers to pack closer, especially in gaps between protomers 3 and 4 and 4 and 5 (fig. S3 and fig. S12). This two-structure superimposition also shows that the conformational changes for AMPPNP and ssDNA binding residues occur by AMPPNP binding (fig. S11D). Especially, AMPPNP binding residues adopt conformational changes, while three protomers (1, 2, and 3) and four protomers (1, 2, 3, and 4) are involved in ssDNA binding in ssDNA-hexamer structure and AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer, respectively (Fig. 4 and fig. S11D). There is an additional ssDNA nucleotide observed toward to the C-terminal domain in the AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer (Fig. 4B).

Primase and the potential active site

Another form of AMPPNP bound hexamer E5 structure, a full-length structure of E5 is observed at near-atomic resolution in protomer 5, and four domains (zinc binding, collar, helicase, and C-terminal domains) are shown in the protomer 4, while only three domains are seen in the rest four protomers as the class one AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer structure (Fig. 3D and fig. S5). This form of AMPPNP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer overlaps the first form of AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer structure in collar, helicase, and the C-terminal domains. The primase domain folds a sandwiched structure (Fig. 4E and fig. S12E). At the top, there are four antiparallel β strands connected by two α helices, and a β sheet consisting of three β strands is at the middle, while four α helices are formed at the bottom (fig. S12, E and F).

The potential catalytic site of the primase is identified by superimposing human primase-polymerase in complex with dsDNA [Protein Data Bank (PDB): code 7JKP], where residues Phe53, Thr179, Lys130, Arg131, and Lys187 are potentially involved in the incoming NTP binding, and residues Asp70, Asp72, and His134 are potential catalytic residues. Residues Arg176 and Arg175 potentially bind template DNA, while Asp170 is potentially involved in primer RNA binding (Fig. 4, E and F).

E5 in complex with ADP and Mg2+

To track the E5 reaction in vitro, we incubated E5 with ATP and Mg2+ for 1 hour. We only observed hexameric particles, suggesting that the DH is turned to hexamer in the presence of ATP and Mg2+.

In the 1-hour incubation sample, there are hexameric structure with zinc and primase domains observed as well as hexameric structure without seeing densities for zinc and primase domains (Fig. 3 and fig. S7). Two zinc and one primase domains are resolved in ADP-ssDNA-primase hexamer, while there is poor density for another primase domain located at the center hole of the collar domain, but we do not build this in our model for caution. Five ADP molecules are identified in the ATP binding site, while one ssDNA molecule is visible in the helicase domains (figs. S7 and S11B). Except one protomer DNA binding motif Gly584-Asn590 disordered, all other protomers are involved in ssDNA binding. For the 1ATP-3ADP-ssDNA hexamer structure, three ADP and one ATP molecules in the ATP binding site as well as one ssDNA molecule (fig. S11, C, I, and J, and table S1) in the helicase domains are observed, whereas only four protomers involved in the ssDNA binding (Figs. 4D and 3F and fig. S7F). Comparing with the AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer, the 1ATP-3ADP-ssDNA-hexamer shows that it rotates in the clockwise direction (fig. S12D). To show the reaction processes, we used all the E5 particles to generate a movie using the CryoSPARC (42), which reveals the protomer movements with the coupling with the primase (fig. S13 and movie S1).

DISCUSSION

DNA replication is an essential and fundamental process, which requires helicase machinery to separate dsDNA into ssDNA as templates. Mpox virus is a large dsDNA virus, which encodes all the proteins for its DNA replication. Mpox virus E5 is the helicase-primase for viral DNA replication. We identified that the poxviral helicase, mpox virus E5, forms the DH. Although PV E1 and SV40 large T-antigen were reported to form DH, there are no high-resolution structures of these viral DH (22, 26, 34). We determined the DH to a subatomic resolution, which shows that the DH binds potential dsDNA. We also observed that the DH is in different conformations in the 2D classification (Fig. 2K). Further work is required to study detailed interactions and the DH function. When ATP and Mg2+ are added into the purified mpox virus E5, all DH molecules become hexamers (fig. S6), suggesting that E5 uses ATP to drive dsDNA to ssDNA. We reported high-resolution structures of apo hexamer, ssDNA-hexamer, AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer, and 1ATP-3ADP-ssDNA-hexamer, which show that the helicase DNA binding loop Gly584-Asn590 is disordered in apo hexamer, and three and four helicase DNA binding loops are ordered and bind ssDNA in ssDNA-hexamer and ADP-ssDNA-hexamer, while four helicase DNA loops are visible and bind ssDNA in AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer (Fig. 4B). Superimposition of these structures shows that these protomers rotate in the counterclockwise direction from the apo, ssDNA hexamer, and AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer, where the hexamers pack tighter to drive the ssDNA through the helicase (fig. S12, A to D). However, superimposition of AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer and 1ATP-3ADP-ssDNA hexamer suggests that these protomers rotate in the clockwise direction from AMPPNP-ssDNA hexamer to 1ATP-3ADP-ssDNA-hexamer, indicating that the hexamer might be starting to release and return to the apo form (fig. S12, A to D).

In AMP-PNP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer and ADP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer structures, five AMPPNP or five ADP molecules are identified in their structures with ssDNA at the helicase domain in the 3′-5′ direction from the C-terminal domain to the N-terminal domain, respectively (Fig. 4, fig. S11, and table S1). There are clear densities for one primase domain and two zinc-binding domains (Fig. 3 and fig. S12, E and F). The active site of the primase is at the side of the hexamer, which we propose that the primase binds the DNA to synthesize the RNA primer (Fig. 4, E and F, and fig. S12, E to G). We used the 1-hour incubation particles of E5, ATP, and Mg2+ to generate a movie (movie S1 and fig. S13), which might show E5 reaction processes. On the basis of the DH structure, we identified that the DH binds potential dsDNA in the central channel composed of zinc-binding domain and the collar domain, while the helicase domain binds potential ssDNA. The mutation of the potential dsDNA binding residues Arg249, Lys286, Lys315, and Lys317 obviously reduces the dsDNA binding affinity (Fig. 2, I and J). Recently, the DH structure of the ASFV helicase and DNA polymerase C962R was reported (27). In contrast to mpox virus E5 DH, the C962R DH is formed via the interface between polymerization domains, which are related to the primase domains of mpox virus E5, suggesting that the C962R DH is formed in a different way from that of mpox virus E5. In addition, unlike the mpox virus E5 DH, the central channel of the C962R DH (PDB code: 8IQH) does not show dsDNA density, suggesting that C962R does not bind dsDNA from the expression bacteria. It is worth noting that the C962R has the DNA polymerase activity rather than the primase activity of mpox virus E5, and the C962R is not essential in ASFV, suggesting that C962R might play different roles from that of the mpox virus E5 (27). When this manuscript was under review, mpox virus E5 hexamer structures have been published (39), which showed seven states of mpox virus E5 hexamer and reported helicase activities of the wild-type and mutant E5. The mpox helicase activities we obtained are consistent with this report (39). However, this published work did not report the E5 DH and dsDNA binding residues in the zinc and collar domains.

We therefore propose an mpox DNA replication mechanism. The DH is formed to bind dsDNA at the DNA replication ori, while the DH uses ATP to unwind dsDNA to ssDNA by the E5 helicase binding, leading DNA strand and extruding the lagging DNA strand out. The hexamer E5 synthesizes RNA primer, and the DNA polymerase complex F8-A22-E4 uses the DNA as a template to synthesize daughter DNA continuously (Fig. 5). The E5 continuously uses the ATP hydrolysis to separate the dsDNA into ssDNA as templates for DNA synthesis until the DNA replication is finished. The limitation of this study is that although we observed different conformations of mpox virus E5 DH in 2D classifications, we could not get enough single particles to obtain high-resolution structures of mpox virus E5 DH in different states. E5 consists of primase, zinc binding, collar, helicase, and C-terminal domains and plays multifunctional roles. Our work sheds important light in the structure and function of mpox virus E5, and further studies are required to explore the E5 functions in cell and animal models.

The potential mechanism of E5.

The E5 binds dsDNA at the ori to form DH. The DH uses ATP to melt the ori by grasping 3′-5′ ssDNA (C-terminal to N-terminal domains of E5). E5 separates dsDNA into ssDNA to form replication forks. E5 binds the 3′-5′ ssDNA and extrudes another ssDNA for bidirectional DNA replication. E5 uses the cycles of ATP binding, hydrolysis, and ADP release to unwind dsDNA to ssDNA.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cloning, expression, and purification of E5

The coding sequence of mpox virus E5 (UniProt: A0A7H0DN89) was synthesized and codon-optimized for SF9 cell. The sequence of e5R and variants including Lys286Ala (single mutant) and Arg249Ala/Lys286Ala/Lys315Ala/Lys317Ala (quadruple mutant) was inserted into a pFastbac vector and fused an N-terminal 8 × His tag and a tobacco etch virus cleavage site. The recombinant baculoviruses were generated by SF9 cells followed by the user manual of Bac-to-Bac Baculovirus Expression System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). High-Five cells were infected with baculovirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 and grown in IB905 serum-free medium (Yishengke Biotechnology) for 72 hours at 27°C. High-Five cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3000g for 10 min. The harvested cells were resuspended and sonicated in a lysis buffer containing 25 mM Hepes (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 10 mM imidazole, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and complete protease inhibitors (TargetMol). The clarified supernatants were separated by centrifugation (38,000g, 4°C, 30 min) and loaded onto 5 ml of Ni-NTA beads (Cytiva). The beads were washed by a lysis buffer with the addition of 30 mM imidazole. The bound protein was eluted with 300 mM imidazole in lysis buffer. The eluted protein was concentrated and loaded onto a gel filtration column (Cytiva, Superdex 200 Increase 10/300) with a buffer containing 25 mM Hepes (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP). The protein fractions were concentrated and stored at −80°C until use.

Purification of E5 for BLI and primase activity assay

To determine the binding activity and primase activity of E5, we purified the E5 protein with no DNA. The harvested High-Five cells were resuspended and sonicated in a lysis buffer containing 25 mM Hepes (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 10 mM imidazole, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and complete protease inhibitors. The clarified supernatants were separated by centrifugation (38,000g, 4°C, 30 min) and loaded onto 5 ml of Ni-NTA beads. The beads were washed by the lysis buffer with the additional 30 mM imidazole and 1 M NaCl. The bound protein was eluted with 300 mM imidazole in the lysis buffer. The eluted protein was added 10 μM ZnSO4 and loaded onto the gel filtration column with a buffer containing 25 mM Hepes (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP. The protein fractions were concentrated and stored at −80°C until use.

Primase activity assay

The primase activity was detected by a gel mobility assay. E5 protein (0.5 μM) was incubated with 1 μM FAM-labeled ssDNA (5′ FAM-TCACACATTTTTTTCTAGACACTAAATAAAATAGTAAAAT 3′) in a reaction buffer [25 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.0), 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM TCEP] with the addition of 1 mM NTP or dNTP at 37°C for 30 min. The positive control was only dsDNA (5′ FAM-TCACACATTTTTTTCTAGACACTAAATAAAATAGTAAAAT 3′; 5′ ATTTTACTATTTTATTTAGTGTCTAGAAAAAAATGTGTGA 3′) in the reaction buffer. The reaction was quenched by heating at 95°C for 5 min. Samples were loaded and separated on a 15% polyacrylamide gel containing 5 M urea in 0.5× tris-borate EDTA (TBE) buffer with 2× TBE urea loading buffer. The gel was visualized on a fluorescence imager (Tanon MINI Space 3000).

ATPase activity assay

The ATPase activity was determined by detecting the rate of ATP hydrolysis by E5 following the manufacturer’s instruction (BioAssay Systems, DATG-200). Standard curve of phosphate, the hydrolysate of ATP, were made by the phosphate concentrations ranging from 5 to 40 μM. All phosphate concentration determinations were performed in triplicates. Hexamer E5 (71.5 nM) and ATP (1 mM) were mixed in the reaction buffer and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. A total of 200 μl of malachite green reagent, samples, and standard substance were added and then incubated for another 30 min at room temperature. Phosphate from the ATP hydrolysis was measured by optical density at 620 nm on a plate reader (Molecular Devices, Spectra MaxiD3). The ATP hydrolysis rates of hexamer E5 were calculated according to the standard curve of phosphate and performed in triplicates.

Biolayer interferometry

The BLI assay was performed on a ForteBio Octet RED96 machine to determine the binding affinity. For the E5 dsDNA binding assay, 5 μM biotin-labeled dsDNA (5′ Biotin-GGCTTGTATTTCACACCGCACCTCAGCGCG 3′; 5′ CGCGCTGAGGTGCGGTGTGAAATACAAGCC 3′) was loaded on streptavidin (SA) sensor in a BLI buffer of pH 8.0 containing 25 mM Hepes, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM TCEP, and 0.02% Tween 20. After baseline, the sensor-labeled DNA was dipped into different concentrations of wild-type, single mutant, or quadruple mutant E5 for association and then dipped into the BLI buffer for dissociation. The control well did not add protein E5. Data analysis was performed on the Octet software DataAnalysis11.

Helicase activity assay

The helicase activity was detected by a gel mobility assay. An oligonucleotide (5′ AGCTACCATGCCTGCACGAATTAAGCAATTCGT-AATCATGGTCATAGCT-FAM 3′) (39) and an oligonucleotide (5′AGCTATGACCATGATTACGAATTGCTTGGAATCCTGACGAACTGTAG 3′) were annealed to generate a fork DNA by heating to 95°C for 5 min. Protein (5 μM; apyrase digested) and fork DNA (2 μM) were incubated at 4°C for half hour in a buffer containing 25 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 4 mM MgCl2, and 5 mM ATP. Samples were loaded and separated on a 20% polyacrylamide gel in 0.5× TBE buffer. The gel was visualized on a fluorescence imager (Tanon MINI Space 3000).

Negative-stain electron microscopy

The E5 protein was performed on 300-mesh copper grids with carbon film. The copper grids were glow-discharged for 50 s at 15 mA using a PELCO easiGlow system. A total of 5 μl of E5 protein (12.5 μg/ml) was loaded onto copper grids for 1 min and then removed excess samples by filter paper. A total of 5 μl of 2% uranyl acetate was blotted the copper grids twice for 30 s, and the excess uranyl acetate was removed by filter paper. Images were recorded on a Talos L120C G2 transmission electron microscope operating at 120 kV.

Cryo-EM sample preparation

For the apo hexamer and ssDNA hexamer structure, the E5 protein was concentrated to 2 mg/ml for cryo-EM grid preparation. E5 (3 μl) was applied onto a glow-discharged copper grid (300 mesh; QUANTIFOIL Cu R1.2/1.3) by a FEI Vitrobot (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The grids were blotted for 5 s with a force of 4 under 100% humidity at 8°C. All grids were flash-frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen.

For the AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer, the E5 was supplemented with 1 mM AMPPNP on ice for 2 hours in a reaction buffer containing 25 mM Hepes (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, 1 mM EDTA, 5% glycerol, and 1 mM MgCl2. For the AMPPNP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer structure, 10 μM ZnSO4 was added to the E5 protein purified from Ni-NTA and loaded onto the gel filtration column. All Cryo-EM sample grids were prepared using the same procedure as the apo E5 protein.

For the 1ATP-3ADP-ssDNA-hexamer and ADP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer structures, the E5 was supplemented with 1 mM ATP in the reaction buffer on ice for 1 hour. Cryo-EM sample grids were prepared using the same procedure as the apo E5 protein.

For the DH structure, 10 μM ZnSO4 was added to the E5 protein purified from Ni-NTA and then loaded onto the gel filtration column. The protein fractions were collected and prepared for the Cryo-EM samples. Sample E5 was concentrated to 1.5 mg/ml and loaded onto the copper grid, while sample E5 was diluted to 0.4 mg/ml to a copper grid coated with graphene oxide (400 mesh; EM Resolutions, R1.2/1.3).

Cryo-EM data collection

Before data collection, all the sample grids were checked using the 200-kV Glacios cryo-electron microscope equipped with a Ceta-D complementary metal-oxide semiconductor camera. The checked frozen grids were transferred to a 300-kV cryo-electron microscope (Titan Krios G4, Thermo Fisher Scientific) that equipped with a Gatan K3 director electron detector and a GIF BioQuantum energy filter for data acquisition. Images were recorded at a magnification of 105,000, and movies were automatically collected by Thermo Fisher Scientific EPU software in counting mode, with a nominal defocus ranging from −0.8 to −2.5 μm, a total electron dose of 50 e/Å2, and a pixel of 0.84 Å.

Cryo-EM structure determination

Micrographs (7705) for the apo E5 sample were collected, and the motion correction and contrast transfer function (CTF) were carried out using CryoSPARC (v4.1) live (42). The preprocessed micrographs were used to pick particles using the blob picker, and initial 14,830,372 particles were used for 2D classification. After three rounds of particle extraction, 2D classification, and 2D selection, 1,009,710 particles were selected for 3D ab initio reconstruction and hetero-refinement into five classes. The class 4 of 552,220 particles is used to generate the apo hexamer map of 2.87 Å after homo-refinement and nonuniform refinement. The class 5 of 263,224 particles is used to generate the apo ssDNA binding map of 3.03 Å.

Micrographs (7655) for the E5 with AMPPNP-incubated sample were collected, and the motion correction and CTF were carried out using CryoSPARC (v4.1) live. The preprocessed micrographs were used to pick particles using the blob picker, and initial 15,018,657 particles were used for 2D classification. After three rounds of particle extraction, 2D classification, and 2D selection, 2,433,067 particles were selected for 3D ab initio reconstruction and hetero-refinement into six classes. The classes 3 and 5 of 1,106,716 particles in total were used to generate the AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer map of 2.51 Å after homo-refinement and nonuniform refinement.

Micrographs (8222) for the E5 with 1 mM AMPPNP 2-hour incubated sample were collected, and the motion correction and CTF were carried out using CryoSPARC (v4.1) live. The preprocessed micrographs were used to pick particles using the blob picker, and initial 13,240,812 particles were used for 2D classification. After three rounds of particle extraction, 2D classification, and 2D selection, 1,341,105 particles were selected for 3D ab initio reconstruction and hetero-refinement into four classes. Duplicated particles from class 1 were removed. The rest 384,020 particles of the class 1 were classified into another three classes by 3D ab initio reconstruction and hetero-refinement. The class 1 of 134,528 particles is used to generate the AMPPNP and ssDNA bound E5 with clear primase domain map of 2.91 Å after homo-refinement and nonuniform refinement.

Micrographs (8214) for the E5 with 1 mM ATP for 1-hour incubation sample were collected, and the motion correction and CTF were carried out using CryoSPARC (v4.1) live. The preprocessed micrographs were used to pick particles using the blob picker, and initial 8,479,127 particles were used for 2D classification. After three rounds of particle extraction, 2D classification, and 2D selection, 1,246,933 particles were selected for 3D ab initio reconstruction and hetero-refinement into three classes. Duplicated particles from class 2 were removed. The rest of 310,181 particles of the class 1 were classified into another three classes by another round 3D ab initio reconstruction and hetero-refinement. The class 1 of 107,462 particles is used to generate the ADP and ssDNA bound E5 with clear primase domain map of 3.27 Å after homo-refinement and nonuniform refinement. The class 2 of 185,355 particles is used to generate the 1ATP-3ADP-ssDNA-hexamer map of 2.90 Å.

Micrographs (15,432) for the DH E5 were collected, and the motion correction and CTF were carried out using CryoSPARC (v4.1) live. The preprocessed micrographs were used to pick particles using the blob picker, and initial 8,479,127 particles were used for 2D classification. After three rounds of particle extraction, 2D classification, and 2D selection, 215,801 particles were selected for 3D ab initio reconstruction. After homo-refinement and nonuniform refinement, the E5 DH structure was determined to 3.22-Å resolution.

Model building and refinement

The E5 was modeled using the AlphaFold 2 server and divided into five domains for model building, namely, the primase domain, the zinc-binding domain, the collar domain, the AAA+ helicase domain, and the C-terminal domain. Five domains were individually docked into cryo-EM maps manually for all the six hexamers and one DH structures by Chimera (43). ssDNA copurified with E5 from expression host cell H5 could be visible from six structures reported in this study, namely, ssDNA hexamer, AMPPNP-ssDNA-hexamer, AMPPNP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer, ADP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer, 1ATP-3ADP-ssDNA-hexamer, and the structure of dsDNA bound DH E5. Because the DNA was copurified from the host cells, we could not identify the sequence of the DNA, and poly C was used for model building and refinement. Structure refinement was performed using the real-space refinement in PHENIX (1.20.1-4487) (44). Coot was used to improve the models manually (45). For the DH structure, although density for potential dsDNA is observed, we do not add dsDNA molecule in the structure for caution. In the AMPPNP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer and ADP-ssDNA-primase-hexamer structures, two zinc-binding domains and one primase domain are built in the structures, while weak density for second primase domain located at the center channel of the collar domain is observed, but we did not add the second primase domain in both structures for caution. For 1ATP-3ADP-ssDNA-hexamer, the densities for the ATP and ADP were checked and the occupancy for the ATP is not 100%. For the flexible parts of structures, we used different occupancies for the flexible regions of structure. The Coot (4) or Chimera (2) programs were used for superimposition of different structures.
