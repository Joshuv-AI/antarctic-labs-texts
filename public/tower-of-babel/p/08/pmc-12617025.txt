Waste‐Free Closed‐Loop‐Recyclable and Thermally Superinsulating Polyimide Aerogels Utilizing Diels‐Alder Chemistry
Authors: Yufei Xiong, Chang‐Lin Wang, Jadwiga Poniatowska, Kitty Nijmeijer, Nicholas Leventis, Željko Tomović
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrganic aerogels are an advanced class of lightweight materials with ultralow thermal conductivity and high porosity, widely used in applications such as thermal insulation, energy storage, and aerospace engineering. Among them, polyimide aerogels stand out for their excellent thermal stability, mechanical strength, and chemical resistance. However, their permanently cross‐linked networks make them non‐recyclable, posing a significant sustainability challenge. Here, a novel closed‐loop recycling approach for polyimide aerogels based on dynamic Diels–Alder chemistry is presented, utilizing an aerogel–sol–aerogel (ASA) process. Matrimid, a commercial polyimide, is post‐functionalized with furfurylamine to form a furan‐modified poly(amide‐imide) (PAI‐FU), which reacts with bismaleimide via a Diels–Alder reaction to create a thermally reversible cross‐linked aerogel network. The resulting poly(amide‐imide) aerogels (PAIAs) exhibit low bulk density (≈ 0.15 g cm−3), high porosity (> 87%), ultralow thermal conductivity (16.0 mWm−1K−1), excellent thermal stability (T d5% ≈ 400 °C), and remarkable mechanical strength (≈ 3.4 MPa). More importantly, PAIAs can be depolymerized at elevated temperatures into soluble oligomers that can be reconstructed into fresh aerogels without catalysts or purification steps. The recycled PAIAs retain their nanostructural morphology and key aerogel‐specific properties, demonstrating efficient closed‐loop recycling. This approach paves the way for advanced and sustainable thermally superinsulating materials.Keywords: aerogels, Diels‐Alder chemistry, polyimide, recycling, sustainability, thermal insulation

Introduction

Aerogels, identified by IUPAC as one of the top ten emerging technologies in chemistry, present a promising solution for energy conservation.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] Organic aerogels in particular exhibit ultralow thermal conductivity, reaching values as low as 20 mWm−1K−1, surpassing the insulating performance of conventional insulators, such as polystyrene foams, polyurethane foams, and glass wool.[ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] These exceptional thermal properties, combined with the ultralow weight and high open porosity of organic aerogels, make them extremely promising materials for reducing energy consumption and curbing greenhouse gas emissions.

Among synthetic organic aerogels, polyimide aerogels stand out as high‐performance materials due to their exceptional thermal stability, chemical resistance, and mechanical strength.[ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 ] These properties render them excellent candidates for applications in thermal insulation, high‐temperature thermal protection, and energy storage.[ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ] Despite alternative routes mainly through reaction of amines and isocyanates,[ 13 , 14 ] the typical synthetic protocol of polyimide aerogels follows the established route to polyimide,[ 20 ] and involves preparation of a poly(amic acid) (PAA), followed by gelation‐inducing imidization.[ 21 , 22 ] Extensive research has been conducted to enhance the properties of these aerogels by modifying the chemical structure and the cross‐linking mode of the polyimide backbone.[ 12 , 15 , 23 , 24 ] However, polyimide aerogels are yet to be made sustainable, since their chemically stable cross‐linked network makes them also non‐recyclable. To overcome this limitation, the lifecycle management of thermally insulating polyimide aerogels should incorporate recycling protocols that are waste‐free, energy‐efficient, and atom‐economical, thereby significantly contributing to a circular economy.

Closed‐loop chemical recycling of aerogels allows for the recovery of molecular feedstocks, representing a pioneering approach in the development of sustainable organic aerogels. Previous studies have shown that organic aerogels containing imine or hexahydrotriazine linkages can undergo complete depolymerization via acid‐catalyzed hydrolysis, providing monomeric building blocks with high purity that can be reused immediately for the synthesis of a next‐generation aerogel.[ 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 ] However, the additional isolation and purification steps required to separate the monomers after depolymerization result in waste generation. To alleviate these labor‐intensive processes and minimize the loss of valuable chemical resources, we have recently introduced a novel recycling strategy for organic aerogels, referred to as the aerogel‐to‐sol‐to‐aerogel (ASA) process.[ 29 ] This innovative approach is designed to harness the dynamic nature of the covalent bonds within organic aerogel networks. Specifically, using the exchange reactions between imine and additional amines, we have demonstrated the depolymerization of cross‐linked polyimine aerogels into soluble oligomer mixtures, which can then be repolymerized into fresh aerogels with the introduction of an appropriate amount of aldehydes. To date, polyimine has been utilized as an example for this strategy, though the true potential of the ASA process has yet to be fully explored. In that regard, a drawback of the ASA process implemented with polyimines is that it requires the addition of extra chemical feedstocks of amines and aldehydes to enable the depolymerization and repolymerization processes. Namely, each next generation of aerogel requires more starting materials to initiate and complete the recycling process, and that poses a limit to the content of every new generation of aerogel in recycled material.

To fully realize the potential of the ASA process in a waste‐free and resource‐efficient fashion, we introduce an alternative class of dynamic covalent bonds based on Diels‐Alder chemistry, which enables quantitative closed‐loop recycling of aerogels. Diels‐Alder adducts, prepared from cycloaddition of furan (diene) and maleimide (dienophile) at ≈ 50 °C, have been reported to be thermally reversible at elevated temperatures (≈ 100 °C).[ 30 , 31 , 32 ] This unique characteristic has been utilized extensively to develop materials for various applications, such as self‐healing and shape‐memory thermosets, and recyclable membranes.[ 31 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] By leveraging the thermal reversibility of Diels‐Alder adducts, these materials can be thermally recycled without the need for additional chemical feedstocks. This makes them an ideal candidate for the ASA process, fully aligned with the waste‐free/resource‐efficient concept.

In this work, we present the first example of thermally closed‐loop recyclable poly(amide‐imide) aerogels (PAIAs) based on Diels‐Alder chemistry using the ASA process (Figure 1 ). To incorporate the Diels‐Alder adduct into PAIAs, we selected a commercially available polyimide polymer, Matrimid® 5218,[ 39 ] and functionalized it with furfurylamine to form a furan‐modified poly(amide‐imide) (PAI‐FU). PAI‐FU acts as a precursor for the formation of Diels‐Alder adducts by subsequently reacting with bismaleimide via cycloaddition to form a cross‐linked network. Followed by solvent‐exchange and supercritical CO2 drying, a series of PAIAs were successfully prepared. These aerogels exhibit outstanding performance, having low bulk density (≈ 0.15 g cm−3), high porosity (> 87%), large specific surface area (up to 278.6 m2 g−1), pronounced thermal stability (T d5% ≈ 400 °C), enhanced mechanical performance (E ≈ 3.4 MPa), and intrinsic hydrophobicity. They also attain ultralow thermal conductivity (16–21 mW m−1K−1), serving as high‐performance thermally superinsulating materials. More importantly, however, closed‐loop recycling of PAIAs can be performed through the ASA process: triggering the reverse Diels‐Alder reaction at elevated temperatures enables the depolymerization of PAIAs into a mixture of soluble oligomers, which can be fully cross‐linked again at 50 °C. Without any additional catalyst or extra purification steps, PAIAs convey the full potential of the ASA process by fulfilling recyclability in a resource‐efficient fashion. The recycled PAIAs preserve their nanostructural morphology and related aerogel‐specific properties. Overall, this work provides a novel pathway for the synthesis of circular insulating materials based on Diels‐Alder chemistry featuring outstanding thermal and mechanical performance.

Results and Discussion

Design and Fabrication of Poly(Amide‐Imide) Aerogels (PAIAs) Based on Diels‐Alder Chemistry

To enable efficient Diels‐Alder adduct formation, a properly designed polymer with either furan or maleimide moieties is required. To achieve this, Matrimid, a commercially available linear polyimide, was post‐functionalized through a reaction with furfurylamine.[ 38 ] This reaction partially opened the imide rings of Matrimid, forming diamide structures with amide linkages to grafted furan groups. This modified polymer is abbreviated as PAI‐FU. PAI‐FU is a statistical copolymer in which approximately 25% of the imide groups are substituted with furan moieties, as confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figure S1, Supporting Information). To investigate the Diels‐Alder/retro‐Diels‐Alder reaction between PAI‐FU and maleimide, a model reaction was conducted using PAI‐FU and N‐phenylmaleimide, a mono‐functional maleimide. PAI‐FU was first reacted with an equimolar amount of N‐phenylmaleimide (relative to the furan groups) in N,N‐dimethylacetamide (DMAc) at 50 °C for 24 h, followed by an additional 24 h at room temperature (Figure 2 ; Figure S2, Supporting Information). 1H NMR spectroscopy was used to monitor the reaction progress by analyzing aliquots taken at various time points. The formation of Diels‐Alder adducts was evidenced by the appearance of new characteristic signals at 6.6 and 5.2 ppm, and the final conversion of the Diels‐Alder reaction reached 68%. Afterward, the reaction mixture was heated in an oven at 97 °C to induce the retro‐Diels‐Alder reaction. According to the NMR spectrum, the dissociation of Diels‐Alder adducts was completed after 3 h. This was confirmed by the disappearance of the characteristic signals at 6.6 and 5.2 ppm. The model reaction results demonstrate the successful incorporation of furan moieties into the polyimide backbone and confirm the reversible nature of the Diels‐Alder reaction in the PAI‐FU. Hence, PAI‐FU was further utilized for the synthesis of recyclable aerogels based on Diels‐Alder adducts.

a) Reaction scheme illustrating the Diels‐Alder reaction and retro‐Diels‐Alder reaction between PAI‐FU and N‐phenylmaleimide. b) 1H NMR spectra (400 MHz, 25 °C, DMSO‐d 6) of the Diels‐Alder and retro Diels‐Alder reaction between PAI‐FU and N‐phenylmaleimide monitored at different points in time.

To synthesize the cross‐linked organic aerogel based on Diels‐Alder adducts, we applied the same reaction conditions for the synthesis of PAIAs, using 1,1′‐(methylenedi‐4,1‐phenylene)bismaleimide (BMI) as a suitable maleimide crosslinker and PAI‐FU as polyfunctional furan‐based precursor (Figure 3a). The poly(amide‐imide) organogels were prepared by mixing BMI and PAI‐FU in DMAc at 50 °C for 24 h, followed by aging at room temperature for another 24 h (Figure 3b). Subsequently, the solvent of the organogels was exchanged to methyl ethyl ketone (MEK). Consistent with the previous model study, the Diels‐Alder reaction reached 68% conversion, and the unreacted BMI during the gelation step was removed during the solvent‐exchange process. The solvents used during the solvent‐exchange process were collected by distillation under reduced pressure, subsequently yielding high‐purity unreacted BMI, which can be reused for synthesizing another batch of aerogels (see Figure S3, Supporting Information). After the solvent exchange process, the organogels in MEK were dried using supercritical CO2. With the intention of investigating the correlation between initial concentration and materials properties of the final aerogels, three different precursor concentrations (0.08, 0.10 and 0.12 g cm−3) were employed for the gelation process (Table S1, Supporting Information). The aerogel samples are labeled accordingly as PAIA‐A, PAIA‐B, and PAIA‐C, respectively.

a) Reaction scheme illustrating the cycloaddition reaction of PAI‐FU with BMI to form a cross‐linked poly(amide‐imide) network. b) Schematic representation of the aerogel synthesis protocol. The precursor solutions are mixed and allowed to gel at 50 °C for 24 h. The formed organogel is aged at room temperature for 24 h, followed by solvent exchange with MEK and drying with supercritical CO2 to yield PAIA.

PAIAs exhibited exceptional mechanical robustness. Their mechanical behavior was assessed via uniaxial compression testing, with stress–strain curves revealing that the materials could withstand compressive strains exceeding 70% without structural failure (Figure 4a). In addition, the compressive modulus (E) showed a positive correlation with bulk density, attaining values up to 3.40 MPa. This performance outperforms various other organic aerogels at comparable densities (see also Figure S4a, Supporting Information).[ 25 ]

For long‐term deployment in the field, it is important to assess the water resistance of aerogels as water absorption can significantly reduce their superinsulating performance.[ 8 ] The hydrophobicity of the PAIAs was quantified by measuring the water contact angle, θ, on flat aerogel surfaces. The values of θ of the three PAIAs of this study were found in the range of 114°−126° (Figure 4b). By comparison to typical dense (non‐porous) polyimides like unmodified Kapton films (θ = 76.6° ± 3.9°)[ 40 ] and Matrimid 5218 film (θ = 75° ± 1°),[ 41 ] PAIAs exhibited significantly higher hydrophobicity without any pre‐ or post‐ hydrophobic treatment,[ 42 , 43 ] which was attributed to their texture.[ 44 ] Additionally, the PAIAs displayed a minimal amount of water uptake (≈ 5%) after being fully immersed in water for 24 h, further highlighting their strong hydrophobic nature, which renders them ideal thermally insulating materials.

Microstructural and Morphological Properties of PAIAs

The pore architecture and corresponding skeletal morphology of the PAIAs were investigated using nitrogen physisorption porosimetry and scanning electron microscopy (SEM), respectively. The adsorption–desorption isotherms of all samples (Figure 4c) can be classified as Type IV according to IUPAC guidelines, indicative of mesoporous materials. However, several features of the isotherms, such as the delayed onset of adsorption at high relative pressures (P/P0 > 0.85), the absence of well‐defined plateaus, and the presence of narrow hysteresis loops, suggest the coexistence of a relatively broad range of mesopores combined with microporosity.[ 45 ] This interpretation was supported by the pore size distributions derived via the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method, which revealed broad profiles across all samples (Figure 4d). Furthermore, comparison of the total specific pore volumes (VTotal), calculated from bulk and skeletal density measurements, was 5 to 50 times larger than the pore volumes (VBJH) probed by nitrogen sorption measurements. This substantial difference confirms that the majority of porosity in PAIAs arises from macropores, which are undetectable via nitrogen physisorption (Table 2 ).

Specific surface areas (σ) were determined using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method. As the initial precursor concentration increased, σ values rose more than threefold, ranging from 87.7 to 278.6 m2 g−1 (Table 2). Average pore diameters (Φ) were calculated from the total specific pore volume (V Total) and surface area using the relation Φ = 4 × V Total/σ, revealing a decrease from 314 to 73 nm with increasing initial concentration (Table 2). These results express the structural shift from more open, coarser frameworks toward denser, finer skeletal architectures at higher initial concentrations of PAIA. Additionally, this structural evolution is consistent with the trends observed in the BJH‐derived specific pore volumes (V BJH), which also varied with initial concentration. Notably, PAIA‐C exhibited the highest values of both σ and V BJH (0.57 cm3 g−1), indicating a comparatively more intricate pore network within its skeletal framework. Alongside the isotherm analysis and pore size distribution data, these results conclude that the PAIAs are predominantly macro‐mesoporous, with their textural properties tunable via precursor concentration.

Furthermore, SEM analysis confirmed that all PAIAs exhibited predominantly macroporous architectures, consisting of 3D, web‐like networks with interconnected polymeric branches. At higher magnification, these branches were observed as strings of fused nanoparticles. Image analysis revealed that increasing the initial concentration from 0.08 to 0.12 g cm−3 resulted in a decrease in the average width of the skeletal branches from 54 ± 14 nm to 26 ± 5 nm (Table 2). These values are in line with the average particle diameters calculated from specific surface area (σ) and skeletal density (ρ s) data, which decreased from 51 to 16 nm over the same concentration range (Table 2). This indicates that variations in the initial concentration of precursors influence the structural morphology, aligning with qualitative SEM observations that reveal the branches are composed of nanoparticle clusters. This observed trend, in which higher initial concentrations lead to smaller particles, resulting in increased specific surface area and mesopore volume, has been previously observed. The proposed gelation kinetics suggest that higher initial precursor concentrations produce smaller but more numerous particles, allowing the percolation threshold to be reached more quickly.[ 46 ]

To further quantify the structural differences among the PAIAs beyond particle size considerations, the K‐index calculation proposed by Taghvaee et al was employed.[ 44 ] Defined as the ratio of the water contact angle (θ) to porosity (Π), the K‐index serves as an integrated metric reflecting both surface texture and structural openness.[ 44 ] The K‐index increased from 1.27 to 1.42 with increasing initial concentration, indicating a modest reduction in the aspect ratio of the skeletal branches within the aerogel network.[ 44 ] This suggests that the decrease in particle size is accompanied by a shortening of the branch segments between interconnects, an observation confirmed by detailed SEM analysis. These findings are consistent with the control of nanostructure by gelation kinetics.

Thermal Properties of PAIAs

Due to the small pore sizes, PAIAs should be expected to show a strong Knudsen effect[ 47 , 48 ] and superior thermal insulation properties. Qualitatively, the thermal insulation performance of PAIAs was visualized with an infrared (IR) camera (Figure 5a). Aerogel samples were placed on heating and cooling stages with temperatures of 100 °C and −25 °C, respectively. The temperature at the center of the surface of the specimen, opposite to the one heated or cooled, was recorded at 0 min and after 10 min on the temperature stage. As shown by the IR images, the surface temperature of PAIAs did not increase or decrease drastically; instead, it remained close to room temperature, demonstrating the effective prevention of heat transfer.

To further evaluate the thermally insulating properties of PAIAs, thermal conductivity measurements were conducted with a heat flow meter according to the ASTM C518 standard. All PAIA samples showed impressively low thermal conductivity values between 16.0 and 20.7 mWm−1K−1, which fall in the range of the best thermal insulators in comparison with other related insulation materials (Table 3 ; Figure S4b, Supporting Information).[ 25 ] Especially in comparison to conventional polyimide aerogels, PAIAs demonstrate superior thermal superinsulating performance,[ 10 , 49 , 50 , 51 ] suggesting that the chemical composition of the polymer backbone could have played an important role in reducing the through‐the‐solid heat conduction λ s, a component of the total (measured) thermal conductivity, λ Total.[ 52 ] Unlike the backbone of conventional polyimides such as Matrimid, the PAIAs consist of a poly(amide‐imide) network with molecular‐level Diels‐Alder‐based crosslinks that might contribute to scatter phonons, therefore maintaining the intrinsically low thermal conductivity of the polymer. In addition, as shown by SEM (Figure 4f), the solid network at the nanoscopic level consists of linear segments that, at first approximation, facilitate heat transfer, and nodes (interconnects) that will scatter heat. The calculated values of λ g and λ s, along with the experimental values of λ Total, are included in Table S2 (Supporting Information), and are plotted as a function of bulk density, ρ b, in Figure 5b. It is noted that λ Total decreases with increasing ρ b, initially at a steep rate before remaining at approximately constant values. This behavior is rather unusual and can be understood by examining the individual contributions from λ g and λ s. The gaseous component, λ g, follows expected trends, decreasing monotonically with ρ b, reflecting the reduced Knudsen effect with smaller pore size (Φ, see Table 2). In contrast, the solid thermal conduction, λ s, increases only marginally. Given the consistent chemical composition across all PAIA samples, the subtle increase in λ s with ρ b can be attributed to an interplay of three morphological factors that effectively counterbalance one another: a reduction in branch length tends to enhance thermal conductivity, whereas a simultaneous decrease in particle size and a corresponding increase in the number of interparticle contacts act to suppress it, leading to low solid thermal conductivity. Overall, both the Knudsen effect and the morphology change with increasing bulk density work synergistically toward almost record‐low thermal conductivity for polymer aerogels.

Now, owing to their high imide, amide, and aromatic content, the PAIAs of this study are expected to display great thermal stability and flame resistance.[ 54 , 55 ] The thermal stability was assessed using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Figure 5c). Initially, PAI‐FU showed a lower decomposition temperature at 5% weight loss (316 °C, T d5%) than Matrimid (493 °C, Table 3). This could have resulted from the functionalization with furfurylamine, whereas the highly heat‐resistant imide content of Matrimid was partially replaced by amide moieties with reduced thermal stability. On the contrary, in comparison with PAI‐FU, PAIAs exhibited higher T d5% values (405−414 °C), suggesting that the thermal stability of PAIAs originated not only from the leftover imide and the aromatic moieties, but also from the high cross‐linking density induced by Diels‐Alder adducts.

To further investigate the flame resistance of PAIAs, we conducted initial burning tests by exposing PAIA‐C to an open flame for 10 s (Figure 5c; Movie S1, Supporting Information). The combustion of aerogel was extinguished shortly after initial ignition, leaving a char layer on the surface, which prevented further burning. The fact that the combustion was extinguished in a short time attests to the pronounced flame resistance of PAIAs. The incorporated imide backbones are known for their intrinsic flame resistance,[ 56 ] thereby suppressing the combustion and accelerating its char formation.

Closed‐Loop Recycling of PAIAs

The retro‐Diels‐Alder reaction serves as the key mechanism for the recyclability of PAIAs. To demonstrate the closed‐loop recycling of PAIA, PAIA‐C was chosen as an ideal candidate due to its outstanding insulation performance, and two cycles of recycling were conducted. By treating PAIA‐C in the presence of the solvent DMAc at elevated temperatures, the polymeric network breaks down due to the dissociation of Diels‐Alder adducts into furan and maleimide moieties. Figure 6a illustrates the aerogel recycling protocol. To initiate depolymerization, PAIA‐C fragments were dispersed in DMAc and heated in an oven at 97 °C. After 3 h, a clear orange solution containing PAI‐FU and BMI was obtained. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis confirmed that the depolymerization process produced an oligomer mixture with comparable molecular weight and broader distribution compared to those of the original polymer (Table S3, Supporting Information). Subsequently, 1H NMR spectroscopy reveals that both precursors were present in the mixture (Figure 6c; Figure S5, Supporting Information). By calculating the molar ratio of furan moieties (PAI‐FU) to maleimide moieties (BMI), it was determined that approximately 31 mol% of BMI was absent from the mixture, suggesting that the Diels‐Alder reaction during gelation achieved a conversion rate of 69%, which aligns with the conversion observed in the model reaction. Conversely, the depolymerized mixture of PAIA‐C was then immediately used for recycling. To achieve this, 0.31 equivalents of fresh BMI were introduced, forming a stable organogel (Table S4, Supporting Information). This was followed by solvent exchange with MEK and supercritical CO2 drying. Remarkably, 100% of the initial chemical resources were retained in the regenerated aerogel.

a) Schematic representation of the thermal recycling of PAIAs. b) Reaction scheme illustrating the depolymerization of PAIAs. c) 1H NMR spectra of the depolymerized mixture of PAIA‐C, BMI, and PAI‐FU. d) GPC curves of PAI‐FU and the depolymerized mixture of PAIA‐C. e) SEM micrographs of PAIA‐C, recycled PAIA‐C 2nd generation, and recycled PAIA‐C 3rd generation with different magnification ratios. f) Radial graphs depicting the aerogel properties of original, recycled PAIA‐C 2nd generation, and recycled PAIA‐C 3rd generation, including bulk density, porosity, compressive strength at 10% deformation, water contact angle, and thermal conductivity.

Throughout two recycling cycles, the recycled PAIA‐Cs displayed nearly identical properties to the original aerogel, including low bulk density, high porosity, large specific surface area, and hydrophobicity. Notably, the recycled aerogels maintained their low thermal conductivity (15.9−16.9 mWm−1K−1), preserving their classification as a thermally superinsulating material (Table S5, Supporting Information). The nanostructural morphology of the recycled PAIA‐Cs was further validated by SEM (Figure 6e), which confirmed that their microstructure remained comparable to the original material. Additionally, TGA (Figure S6, Supporting Information) and stress‐deformation curves (Figure S7, Supporting Information) demonstrated that the recycled aerogel retained both thermal stability and mechanical strength, underscoring the reproducibility of its performance.

Conclusion

In this study, we present a synthetic protocol for thermally recyclable PAIAs through dynamic Diels‐Alder chemistry, enabling efficient closed‐loop recycling via the ASA process. Through post‐functionalization of Matrimid, a PAI‐FU is prepared and used as a polyfunctional furan to react with bismaleimide, forming thermally reversible cross‐linked networks. The resulting PAIAs display ultralow thermal conductivity (16.0−20.7 mWm−1K−1) and flame resistance with low bulk density (≈ 0.15 gcm−3), high porosity (> 87%), excellent mechanical strength (≈ 3.4 MPa), and hydrophobicity, making them ideal for long‐term thermal insulation applications. Furthermore, PAIAs demonstrate outstanding thermal stability and flame resistance. More importantly, these PAIAs are designed to be closed‐loop recycled via the ASA process, where they depolymerize at elevated temperatures, producing soluble oligomers that can be directly reused to regenerate fresh aerogels with identical properties. This method eliminates labor‐intensive, waste‐producing purification steps while preserving 100% of the original chemical building blocks in the recycled material. Overall, this work marks a major step toward the development of high‐performance, recyclable aerogel materials, contributing to a more resource‐efficient and sustainable polymer economy.
