Preparation of composite calcium phosphate cement scaffold loaded with Hedysarum polysaccharides and its efficacy in repairing bone defects
Authors: Lianggong Zhao, Bo Wang, Shilan Feng, Huifang Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt’s imperative to create a more ideal biological scaffold for bone defect repair. Calcium phosphate bone cements (CPC) could be used as a scaffold. Some ingredients and osteogenic factors could be added to improve its poor mechanical properties and biological activity. As a macromolecule extracted from traditional Chinese medicine, Hedysarum polysaccharides (HPS) would significantly promote the osteogenic activity of bone biomaterials. Zirconium oxide and starch were added to the solid phase and citric acid was added to the liquid phase to optimize CPC. HPS was loaded onto the scaffold as an osteogenic factor, and the prepared CPS + HPS was characterized. Further, the cytocompatibility of CPS + HPS was assessed according to activity, differentiation, and calcification in neonatal rat calvarial osteoblasts, and the biosafety of CPS + HPS was evaluated according to acute toxicity, pyrogen, sensitization, and hemolysis. The success of CPS + HPS in repairing bone defects was evaluated by using a rabbit femur implantation experiment. After optimization, CPS-20-CA-5 containing 10% starch and 5% citric acid displayed the highest mechanical strength of 28.96 ± 0.03 MPa. HPS-50 was demonstrated to exert the best osteogenic effect. The combination of CPS + HPS achieved HPS-loaded CPC. Material characterization, cytocompatibility, biosafety, and femoral implantation experiments indicated that CPS + HPS possessed better pressure resistance and improved osteogenic ability in bone defect repair.CPS + HPS demonstrated effective pressure resistance and superior osteogenic ability, which may be of great significance for bone defects and bone tissue engineering to promote bone regeneration and repair.Graphical Abstract The composite CPS loaded with HPS-50 is an excellent scaffold with fine load-bearing ability and sustained release of osteogenic factors to repair bone defects.

Introduction

Bone defects, resulting from fractures and bone disease, have tremendous socioeconomic impacts on personal and occupational disability and relate health care costs. For clinical treatment of large bone-defect, autografts remain the gold standard. Although these biological grafts exhibit high healing rates, approximately 20–30% of patients who received autograft treatment-experienced chronic pain and morbidity at the donor site, while >30% of allograft procedures were complicated by fracture, non-union and infection [1]. Bone scaffolds which are convenient to be used and relatively cheap [2] could continuously supply nutrients including calcium and phosphorus for bone repair [3]. Biological bone scaffolds have attracted significant interest due to their distinctive biocompatibility and ability to enhance bone regeneration [4–6]; There were two artificial bone scaffolds, calcium phosphate bone cement (CPC) and polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), were widely used in bone-defects repair process. However, PMMA has gradually being replaced as a result of its poor degradability, despite its excellent mechanical properties [7]. Calcium ions [8] can contribute to osteoblast differentiation [9]; thus, CPC has attracted significant attention as a bone graft substitute due to its structural similarity with bone minerals and excellent bone integration [10], bone conductivity, and degradability [11, 12]. Nevertheless, the poor mechanical properties and high brittleness of CPC have limited its development. Accordingly, researchers have been committed to improve CPC by strengthening the mechanical properties via the addition of ingredients, or focusing on loading effective factors onto the scaffold, such as bone-related factors, plant extracts, polysaccharides, to obtain better osteogenic properties [13]. Studies have confirmed that the addition of zirconium oxide [14] or zirconia [15] to hydroxyapatite [16] or β-tricalcium phosphate [17] could improve their crystallinity and mechanical properties [16]. Starch, typically corn starch [18], has been used successfully in bone scaffolds, bone cement [19], and drug delivery systems [20], to increase mechanical strength [21]. Moreover, as a calcium binder [22], citric acid has been successfully added to the liquid phase to improve the compression strength [23] and durability parameters [24] of bone cement [25]. Based on these previous findings, we focused on improving both the solid and liquid phases of bone cement with a view to reforming its structure and improving the mechanical properties.

It is necessary to load an osteogenic factor in/on bone scaffolds for bone repair [12]. It has been suggested that polysaccharides, biological macromolecules, may be excellent bone regeneration factors [26] and bioactive supplement for CPC [13]. It has been demonstrated that osteoblast activity can be significantly improved by reinforcing CPC with cellulose [27], chitosan [28], and hyaluronic acid [29]. CPC containing konjac glucomannan and guar gum displays good biocompatibility and is expected to be successfully used in bone defects repair [30]. Radix Hedysari (RH), the dry root of Hedysarum polybotrys Hand.-Mazz, promotes blood circulation and is thought to regulate the bone microenvironment, which is enriched in hematopoietic and vascular networks [31]. Hedysarum polysaccharides (HPS) are the principal active fraction of RH. Polysaccharides participate in the improvement of bone homeostasis via the BMP/SMAD/RUNX2 and OPG/RANKL/RANK signaling pathways, apoptosis pathway, and transcription factor activity [32]. In previous studies, we discovered that HPS stimulates osteoblast differentiation by increasing the activity of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and promoting the expression of Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) and Osterix (Osx), the two master genes of osteoblast differentiation, to promote bone regeneration [33]. The active polysaccharides have been extracted from RH using a complex method combining cellulose/papain (1:1) enzyme digestion and ultrasound (105 W for 60 min, pH 5) to obtain HPS-50 and HPS-80 fractions, pertaining to the presence of 50% and 80% ethanol, respectively; however, their application to CPC for bone repair has not yet been studied [34].

This article aimed to design a multifunctional composite CPC loaded with HPS as a bone regeneration scaffold for bone defects repair. Zirconium oxide, starch, and citric acid were added to optimize CPC [10, 35]. A starch network nanostructure was formed by gelatinization to improve its mechanical properties, and HPS was loaded onto the scaffold as an osteogenic factor to improve bone healing ability. The optimal CPC loaded with HPS was subjected to cytocompatibility testing, biosafety testing and rabbit femur implantation experiments to demonstrate the dual advantage of a material scaffold possessing weight-bearing capacity and targeted, sustained release of osteogenic factors for the promotion of bone healing.

Materials and methods

Optimization of bone cements

Bone cement materials

Analytical grade dicalcium phosphate dehydrate (CaHPO4·2H2O, DCPD, SINOPHARM, SH, CHN), hydroxyapatite (HAP/HA, YUANYE, SH, CHN), corn starch (Angel, Yichang, HB, CHN), tricalcium phosphate (TCP, Ron, SH, CHN), and zirconium oxide (ZrO2, Ron, SH, CHN) were used after filtration through an 80-mesh screen. Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA, Ron, SH, CHN), anhydrous di-potassium hydrogen phosphate (K2HPO4, BJ, CHN), citric acid (CA, Jianxin Chemical, SH, CHN), and methyl methacrylate (MMA, Energy Chemical, SH, CHN) were used in the production of bone cement.

Preparation of bone cements

Bone cements were determined by three parameters: solid phase, liquid phase, and liquid-solid ratio (L/S). The calcium/phosphorus molar ratio (3:2) of human adult bone was used [36, 37]. TCP:HA: DCPD was used at a ratio of 8:1:1 [38] with the addition of 10% ZrO2 as the contrast agent. PMMA was used as a comparison of compression strength. The liquid phase of CPC was 0.5 mol/L K2HPO4 [10] with 0%, 5%, or 10% citric acid prepared with distilled water [22]. PMMAs uniformly contained MMA. According to the results of pre-experiments, an L/S of 0.7 mL/g was optimal for the preparation of the paste; however, the L/S with starch was 1–1.3 mL/g since of gelatinization. To prepare the CPCs, the solid and liquid phases were thoroughly mixed according to the established L/S using a magnetic stirrer (if containing starch, the temperature was set to 80 °C), quickly poured into a syringe, and injected into different molds for shaping. The CPCs were cured in a programmable temperature and humidity tester (Maijie, SH, CHN) at 37 °C and 60% humidity for 72 h and then dried at 60 °C for 24 h.

Integrity testing of bone cements

The integrity of the bone cements was evaluated according to anti-washout, injectability, and degradability.

The bone cement slurry and the samples after curing were placed in a Petri dish containing 10 mL deionized water. Anti-washout was evaluated according to the degree of diffusion seen in the images, and it was graded as 1, 2, or 3, corresponding to high, medium, or low anti-washout, respectively. The bone cement slurry was poured into a 2 mL syringe, and a 1 kg load was applied vertically from above to evaluate the injectability. The initial mass M0 of the syringe, the total mass M1 of the slurry and the syringe, and the mass M2 after ejection of the slurry were recorded. The injection coefficient (IC) = (M1–M2)/(M1–M0)×100 with three times [39]. The degradation rate of the CPCs was explored in the simulated body fluid Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS). Considering the bone repair cycle, a period of 8 weeks was chosen. The samples, which had a diameter and height of 5 mm and 3 mm, respectively [6], were placed in a 5 mL centrifuge tube containing 3 mL HBSS buffer and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 30 rpm. The initial mass m0 and the mass mn (n indicates the week) were recorded. The degradation rate (%) was calculated as (m0–mn)/m0 × 100 [40].

Compression strength testing of bone cements

According to the mechanical requirements, pieces were prepared possessing a diameter of 9 mm and a height of 12 mm, with five pieces in each group. The CPC specimens after curing immediately were placed on the fixture of a universal material testing instrument (CSS-44100, Changchun Experimentation Machine Academy, Changchun, JL, CHN) to evaluate the compression strength.

Structural characterization of bone cements

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were used to characterize the structure of the representative bone cements samples.

The infrared spectra of CPC powder prior to curing and CPC-1, CPS-20-CA-5, and CPPA-75 samples were measured on a Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer (NEXUS670, Nicolet, USA) in the range of 400–4000 cm−1. The X-ray diffractometer (D/Max-2400, Rigaku, JPN) was used with the following parameters: rotating target; tube pressure, 40 kV; tube flow, 100 mA; and 2θ continuous scanning from 10° to 60° at a rate of 10°/min. CPCs were observed by SEM (JSM-6701F, JPN) to evaluate pore diameter and structure. CPC-1, CPS-20, CPS-20-CA-5, and CPPA-75 samples were fixed for observation and image acquisition.

Screening of HPS as the osteogenic factor

Cells and animals

Neonatal rat calvarial osteoblasts (ROBs) and rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (rBMSCs) were separated from neonatal suckling SD rats and Wistar rats, respectively, obtained from the Animal Experiment Center of Lanzhou University with SCXK[GAN]2018-0002 [41]. Alizarin red staining was used to identify the calcification degree with a view to verifying that the ROBs and rBMSCs were osteoblasts. The MTT assay was used to determine cell viability.

ROBs and rBMSCs viability assay

The MTT assay was used to evaluate the viability of ROBs and rBMSCs following the addition of HPS-50 and HPS-80. The concentrations of HPS were 0 μg/mL, 10 μg/mL (low), 100 μg/mL (medium), and 1000 μg/mL (high) [42]. Cells were seeded at 1 × 104/well on 96-well plates, with six wells, for 24 h. The medium was replaced with HPS for 24 h, 48 h, or 72 h. Subsequently, MTT was added for 4 h, and the absorbance (A) was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (BIO-RAD Model 680, USA) after the addition of 100 µL DMSO. The viability was calculated as A (Experimental group)/A (Control) × 100.

In Alamar Blue (Yisheng, SH, CHN) assay, cells were seeded at 1 × 105/mL on 96-well plates, with three wells, for 24 h. 10 μL Alamar Blue was added for 4–8 h. The fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (Synergy Neo2, BIOTEK, USA) at an excitation of 530 nm and an emission of 590 nm. Cell viability (%) was calculated as (F590 experimental – F590 negative control)/(F590 positive control – F590 negative control) × 100, where F590 = fluorescence value at 590 nm.

Alkaline phosphatase activity assay

Osteogenesis induction medium consisted of DMEM or DME/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, 1000 U/mL streptomycin, 1 × 10−7 mol/L vitamin C, 1 × 10−7 mol/L dexamethasone, and 10 mmol/L β-sodium glycerophosphate (Solarbio).

Evaluation of cell differentiation was performed using an alkaline ALP detection kit (Jiancheng, Nanjing, JS, CHN). Primary ROBs and rBMSCs were seeded on 24-well plates. The following day, the medium was replaced with HPS-containing osteogenesis induction medium. The ALP activity was measured after 3, 6, and 9 days of induction.

Calcium deposition assay

Primary cells were seeded on 6-well plates. The next day, it was induced by osteogenic medium containing HPS. After 14 days, Alizarin Red staining solution (0.2%, pH 8.3, Solarbio) was added. Calcified nodules were photographed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (VERT1, Zeiss, USA), and the images were analyzed using the Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

PCR of osteogenic genes

Primary ROBs and rBMSCs were seeded on 6-well plates and induced for 7 days. Total RNA was extracted according to the TRleasyTM Total RNA Extraction Kit (Yisheng, SH, CHN). After quantification, the cDNA template was obtained according to Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (gDNA digester plus). NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) was used for β-actin (beta-actin), Runx2 (runt-related transcription factor 2), OCN (osteocalcin), and COL1 (collagen I). The PCR primers were synthesized by Beijing Bomeide Gene Technology Co., Ltd (Table 2). Realtime fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed according to Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Low Rox Plus). Preparation and evaluation of the CPS + HPS scaffold

Preparation of CPS + HPS

HPS was extracted from RH as previously described [34]. The physicochemical properties and monosaccharide composition of HPS have been previously studied [43]. Subsequently, HPS-50 was dissolved in the liquid phase to prepare CPS + HPS: C1, C2, C3, C4, C5, and C6 (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 mg HPS-50/CPS, respectively) according to 2.1.2.

Characterization of CPS + HPS

CPS + HPS was characterized by anti-washout, injectability, degradability, compression strength, FTIR, XRD, and SEM. C1 and C4 were photographed by SEM. The specific methods are described in 2.1.3–5.

Cytocompatibility of CPS + HPS

The CPS + HPS was sterilized at 121 °C for 30 min [44], and soaked in complete medium at 37 °C for 72 h to get its extraction solution.

MTT, Alamar Blue, ALP activity, and calcium deposition assays of CPS + HPS were carried out in ROBs to evaluate cytocompatibility according to the methods in 2.2.2–4.

Biosafety of CPS + HPS

The biosafety evaluation of CPS + HPS mainly includes acute toxicity, pyrogen test, sensitization experiment, and hemolysis test according to the 2020 edition of Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China (Ch.P). Its extraction solution was prepared with 0.9% normal saline for 72 h according to the ratio of 3 cm2/mL.

Acute toxicity: Kunming mice (16–28 g) were randomly divided into 5 groups, respectively Control, CPC, PMMA, CPS, and CPS + HPS (6 mg HPS-50/g CPS) group. The extraction solution was injected subcutaneously in the proportion of 50 mL/kg. Routine blood, and biochemical indexes were examined. The visceral index=Visceral wet weight/Body weight × 100, and the visceral samples were stained by H&E.

Pyrogen test: The 5 groups same as acute toxicity were designed with 3 rabbits each group, not less than 1.7 kg. The extraction solution was injected through rabbit’s auricular vein in the proportion of 0.5 mL/kg. The temperature was measured every 30 min, a total of 6 times. Then, it was judged to be qualified if the temperature rise was lower than 0.6 °C, and the total temperature rise was lower than 1.3 °C.

Sensitization experiment: The 5 groups same as acute toxicity were designed with 6 guinea pigs (250–350 g) each group. The 0.5 mL extraction solution was injected intraperitoneally every other day for total 3 times, and 1 mL was injected intravenously for challenge on 14th and 21st days. Concurrently, observed whether there were allergic reaction symptoms within 30 min.

Hemolysis test: In view of the close relationship between CPS + HPS and blood circulation, hemolytic test was conducted on 14 CPCs and CPS + HPS. The positive P was distilled water, and the negative N was normal saline. Hemolysis rate (%) = (OD of experience group – ODN) / (ODP – ODN) × 100 with 3 times. If the hemolysis rate was less than 5%, it was judged to be qualified.

Efficacy of CPS + HPS in repairing rabbit femoral defects

We carried out implantation experiments on rabbit femoral bone defects to evaluate the bone healing ability of CPS + HPS in bone tissue engineering.

Rabbits, weighing 2–3.5 kg and routinely raised for 7 days, were provided by the Animal Experiment Center of Lanzhou University with SCXK[GAN]2018-0002. Following anesthesia with 10% chloral hydrate (intravenous injection at the ear margin, 2 mL/kg) and Su-mian-xin II (subcutaneous injection, 0.1 mL/kg), the rabbit was fixed on the operating table in the supine position. A longitudinal incision was made on the medial side of the femur of the hind leg and the intramuscular space was stripped to expose the periosteum and femur. A hole with a diameter of 5 mm and a depth of 5 mm was made on the medial side of the femoral ankle of the hind leg using a bone drill. The control group was directly sutured after drilling, and the experimental groups were sutured after implantation of the CPC, CPS, or CPS + HPS (6 mg HPS-50/g CPS), as described in Table 3. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM, USA). Data are expressed as the mean ± SD. Differences between the groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was set as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01.

Discussion

Bone regeneration scaffolds do not only require excellent weight-bearing capability but also bone regeneration capability, with broad application prospects in bone defects repair. Accordingly, we designed a composite calcium phosphate cement loaded with Hedysarum polysaccharides (CPS + HPS) to repair bone defects.

Primarily, to improve the release performance and mechanical strength of CPC, Zirconium oxide and corn starch were added to the solid phase, and citric acid was added to the liquid phase of CPC. The anti-washout, injectability, degradability, and mechanical indexes were optimized, and PMMA was used as the reference for mechanical indexes. Despite the addition of ZrO2 and 10% starch, the anti-washout remained poor (grade 3); however, the addition of too much corn starch led to low anti-washout (grade 3), while the improved CPC showed medium anti-washout (grade 2), as did CPS-20-CA-5. Meanwhile, lower injection difficulty and higher injection rate were required for CPC, and CPS-20-CA-5 and CPS-30-CA-5 had relatively consistent injection performance. The mechanical strength of CPC containing ZrO2, starch, and citric acid (CPS-20-CA-5) was improved from 5.25 ± 0.02 MPa to 28.96 ± 0.03 MPa. However, the improvement in compression strength was largely due to the starch nanonetwork. According to the characterization, CPS-20-CA-5 displayed excellent anti-washout, injectability, and better mechanical strength, which was used in subsequent experiments (the follow-up CPS).

Simultaneously, we focused on the effects of HPS-50 and HPS-80 on osteogenic ability of ROBs and rBMSCs. Absorbance (MTT) and fluorescence (Alamar Blue) were employed to evaluate cell viability [48]. Moreover, ALP activity was used as a marker of osteogenic differentiation [49], and Alizarin Red staining was used to determine calcified nodules and evaluate the mineralization degree of osteoblasts. HPS-50 had a superior effect on the proliferation, viability, differentiation, and mineralization of ROBs and rBMSCs as compared with HPS-80. In summary, HPS-50 maintained superior osteogenic ability, suggesting its suitability as an osteogenic factor in following experiments.

Subsequently, HPS-50 was loaded onto CPS to prepare CPS + HPS, which was characterized according to anti-washout, injectability, degradability, compression strength, FTIR, XRD, and SEM. The addition of HPS-50 had a positive effect on CPS structure, and the compression strength increased in an HPS-50 concentration-dependent manner. Moreover, CPS + HPS displayed similar mechanical properties to ideal biological bone scaffolds, with a compression strength of 50–151 MPa [45]. The cytocompatibility tests confirmed that CPS + HPS (6 mg HPS-50/g CPS) has a greater osteogenic ability to promote bone regeneration. Meanwhile, the biosafety evaluation of acute toxicity, pyrogen test, sensitization experiment, and hemolysis test according to Ch.P perfectly verified that CPS + HPS (6 mg HPS-50/g CPS) was provided with compliant biosafety.

Furthermore, the ability of CPS + HPS to repair bone defects was evaluated by using femoral implantation experiments in rabbits. The change in calcium levels was closely related to bone defects, and the levels of inorganic phosphorus were higher at 2 and 4 weeks post-surgery, indicating the bone healing period. At 8 weeks post-surgery, the state of the bone defects was improved and the levels of inorganic phosphorus had returned to normal at 8 weeks, suggesting stable bone formation [50, 51]. General observation, H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining indicated that the viscera appeared no inflammatory reaction or pathological changes, further illustrating the biosafety of CPS + HPS. X-rays and H&E staining of femoral samples showed successful construction of the bone defect models. At 2 weeks post-surgery, bone healing was not observed and a number of inflammatory cells were present, the levels of which had decreased by 4 weeks post-surgery, and new bone had begun to form. At 8 weeks post-surgery, there were no inflammatory cells present and the bone tissue had started to become connected. In comparison with those in the CPS, CPC, and control groups, the bone defects in the CPS + HPS group were essentially completely healed at 8 weeks post-surgery, demonstrating that CPS + HPS possessed outstanding bone healing ability. The CPS + HPS group contained significantly larger amounts of new bone than the CPS or CPC groups at 8 weeks post-surgery, indicating the promoting effect of CPS + HPS on new bone formation and maturation. CPS + HPS produced calcium and phosphate ions, which promoted new bone formation through osteoinduction, and converted HA also stimulated the endogenous expression of osteogenic factors, such as HPS, and enhanced ALP activity, which is particularly important in the early mineralization process associated with bone formation [52]. Therefore, we confirmed that CPS + HPS not only played a role in bone support and weight-bearing in bone defects but also promoted bone regeneration and healing as a scaffold for bone tissue engineering.

In conclusion, we successfully synthesized a multi-functional CPS + HPS as a bone regeneration scaffold. The compression strength of CPC was improved by the starch nanonetwork structure. CPS + HPS displayed excellent cytocompatibility and slowly released active components into the bone microenvironment, improving the ability in repairing bone defects. This study is of great significance for bone diseases, especially bone defects, and opens a new research avenue for osteogenic factor-loaded scaffolds. Obviously, CPS + HPS requires further validation, such as pharmacokinetics studies, which is the direction of our future research.

However, there are still some potential limitations of the study. The effects of HPS on osteoclast activity, the effects of CPS + HPS on both osteoblast and osteoclast need more studies. The need for further in vivo studies to confirm the effectiveness of CPS + HPS in clinical settings, such as the pharmacokinetics of CPS + HPS in rabbits; the efficacy of CPS + HPS in repairing different types of bone defects, such as in skull and in tibia.
