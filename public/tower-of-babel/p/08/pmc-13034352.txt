Targeted immune response gene expression profiles linked with necrosis and mortality in preterm infants with surgical necrotizing enterocolitis
Authors: Parvesh Mohan Garg, Robin Riddick, Pengbo Zhang, Jeffrey Shenberger, Neha Varshney, David Sawaya, Padma Garg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe sought to determine changes in the expression of immune response-related genes occurring in surgical necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) tissues from infants with necrosis severity and survival status.MethodsTargeted RNA sequencing of a select panel of 395 immune response genes was performed on RNA isolated from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded intestinal tissue samples (n=36). DESeq2 was used to analyze differential expressions between infants with mild to moderate and severe necrosis and with respect to survival status after correcting for RNA integrity.ResultsThirty-five genes were differentially expressed (FDR-adjusted p<0.1) between mild-moderate necrosis and severe necrosis. Principal component analysis identified alternations in genes involved in host defense, natural killer (NK) cell signaling and development, and apoptosis which were overexpressed in severe necrosis (IGJ, GZMA, TNFSF10, KLRB1, and CD160). Expression of leukocyte antigens (ITGAM, ITGAX) and cytokine and chemokine receptors (such as IL1A, IL1B, CCL2, CCL3) was increased in patients with mild necrosis. Six genes were significantly differentially expressed (FDR-adjusted p<0.1) between survivors and the non-survivors. Genes related to chemokine neutrophil attractants (CXCL1, GBP, PTGS2, CXCL11, CXCL9, and CXCL10) were upregulated in non-survivors.ConclusionSevere necrosis and non-survival in NEC infants were associated with differential gene expression related to host defense, NK cell signaling and development, and apoptosis. Understanding the role of these pathways in severe NEC may guide the development of prognostic and treatment pathways.Keywords: Neonatology

Introduction

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a devastating acute gastrointestinal illness of the neonatal period, affecting about 5%–10% of premature infants with birth weights of ≤1500 g.1 2 Despite major advances in neonatal intensive care and reductions in all-cause mortality among premature infants, NEC continues to be associated with mortality rates of 25%–40%.3,7

Histopathologically, NEC lesions are marked by coagulative necrosis, inflammation, bacterial overgrowth, and reparative tissue changes.8,15 A recent study correlated outcomes in NEC with the depth of bacterial invasion and the severity of inflammation in the resected bowel. In addition, the presence of NEC lesions in the resected margins, presumably indicating incomplete removal of necrotic bowel, is also associated with mortality.16 Evidence suggests that inclusion of genetic information is helpful in predicting disease outcomes in NEC. Twin studies indicate that genetic factors may account for up to 50% of the risk of NEC.17 While certain single nucleotide polymorphisms, such as those in carbamyl phosphate synthetase (T1405N),18 IL12 (p40 promoter CTCTAA/GC),19 VEGF (C-2578A),20 and NFKB1(g.-24519delATTG),21 have been found to be associated with NEC, genome-wide association studies for NEC are few.22

The challenge for providers is to prevent NEC or detect clinical manifestations as soon as possible in an effort to mitigate severity. Discovering biomarkers, or predisposing factors, which increase risk or susceptibility for severe disease has the potential to stratify care and treatment plans aimed at lowering risk and improving outcomes. As an initial step, we sought to determine changes in immune response genes associated with different severities of bowel necrosis in preterm infants with NEC. Additionally, we aimed to identify differential gene expression patterns occurring in survivors.

Methods

This study was conducted at the neonatal intensive care unit at University of Mississippi Medical Center (UMMC), which is a regional referral center for infants with surgical NEC. We reviewed the medical and pathology records to identify patients with advanced NEC23 who underwent an exploratory laparotomy and surgical resection of the bowel during 15 years (January 2000 to December 2015). Patients with spontaneous intestinal perforation were excluded.

Demographic characteristics, including birth weight, gestational age, gender, race or ethnicity, mode of delivery, and outborn status, were retrieved from the patients’ medical records. Maternal data, including pregnancy-induced hypertension, chorioamnionitis, and antenatal steroid use, were recorded. Infant characteristics, including mode of delivery, small for gestational age status, Apgar score at 5 min, age of NEC onset, mode of clinical presentation (bloody stools, abdominal distension, feeding intolerance), persistent patency of the ductus arteriosus (PDA), use of non-steroidal agents for medical treatment of PDA, blood culture-positive sepsis, and duration of antibiotics, were recorded. The region of affected bowel and length of bowel resected was noted. We also collected data on the length of hospital stay and mortality. Mortality was defined as death due to NEC or NEC-associated sepsis. The length of stay was defined as the total length of stay from the day of admission until discharge. To assess postoperative morbidity, we recorded the duration of postoperative ileus, days of parenteral nutrition, development of short bowel syndrome (SBS), and time to achieve full feeds (feeds at 120 mL/kg/day). SBS was defined as infants who were still requiring total parenteral nutrition more than 90 days following the NEC surgery. Days of parenteral nutrition were defined as the interval between postoperative day 0 until full enteral feedings were achieved (defined as 120 mL/kg/day). Surgical morbidity was classified as surgical site infections (including dehiscence and abscesses), strictures, fistulas, adhesions, and perforations.

Histopathological evaluation

Specimens of surgically resected small intestine bowel tissue from the patients with NEC were identified in pathology archives. The samples for analysis were taken from the most affected part of the small intestine identified by the pathologist. H&E-stained resected bowel tissue sections were recorded for the area of necrosis on the slide (percent), scored as 0, 1 (0%–25%), 2 (26%–50%), 3 (51%–75%), and 4 (76%–100%); maximum depth of necrosis, scored as 0, 1 (mucosa), 2 (submucosa), 3 (muscularis propria), and 4 (transmural); severity of inflammation, scored as 0 (no inflammatory cells), 1 (up to 50 inflammatory cells/high power field (hpf)), 2 (51–200 inflammatory cells/hpf), 3 (>201 cells/hpf); depth of inflammatory infiltrates, scored as 0, 1 (mucosa), 2 (submucosa), 3 (muscularis propria), and 4 (transmural).16 Infants with necrosis scores of 0, 1, and 2 were classified as having mild-moderate necrosis, whereas those with scores of 3 and 4 were classified as having severe necrosis.

RNA sequencing methods

RNA was isolated from 10 µm sections of NEC formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) human intestine specimens (n=36) obtained following laparotomy. Targeted RNA sequencing using the AmpliSeq Illumina Immune Response Panel was used for targeted quantitative expression of genes linked to immune system interactions, which included 400 genes. Raw reads were de-multiplexed and aligned to human reference genome assembly (hg19) using RNA Amplicon Application (along with custom panel manifest) available on Illumina BaseSpace Computing Platform (DRAGEN APP; http://basespace.illumina.com/). Aligned reads for each gene were normalized for sequencing depth and RNA composition using DESeq2’s median of ratios method and used for downstream differential expression analysis in survivors and the non-survivors while accounting for other covariates such as necrosis. An adjusted p value < 0.1 was used for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed; categorical data were summarized as frequencies (absolute and relative) and any simple bivariate comparisons were performed using Fisher’s exact tests or χ2 tests.Continuous data were presented as either means with standard deviation (SD), when underlying distributions would be roughly symmetric, or medians with first and third quartiles when skew was noted. Differences in the continuous data were tested using a Student’s t-test or if symmetry was in question, a Mann-Whitney U test. A p value <0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Twenty-three infants were males, and 13 infants were female. The cohort had 23 infants who belonged to African-American ancestry and 10 infants were Caucasian. Within the cohort, 21 infants (21/36, 58.3%) had mild-moderate necrosis and 15 infants (15/36,41.6%) had severe necrosis (score 3–4). Out of 36 infants with surgical NEC, 11 infants (11/36, 30.5%) died, and 25 infants (25/36, 69.4%) survived.

Gene expression in infants with mild-moderate necrosis and severe necrosis

RNA-Seq analysis and identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

The principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear sample separation supported by differential expression results, which identified 25 upregulated and 9 downregulated genes after low count filtering (figure 1A). The mean–average (MA) plot demonstrated stable fold change distributions with significant genes trending upward. The volcano plot (figure 1B) highlighted a focused set of transcripts exhibiting both substantial effect sizes and strong statistical evidence of differential expression, underscoring a coherent transcriptional signature underlying the PCA-defined groups.

(B) Volcano plot analysis for necrosis differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The X axis represents the log2 transformed difference fold value, and the Y axis represents the −log10 transformed significance value. FC, fold change.

Differential expression analysis was used to investigate the association with stage of necrosis and gene expression in preterm infants with NEC. The analysis showed 35 genes were differentially expressed (p<0.1) between mild-moderate necrosis (score 0–2) and severe necrosis (score of 3–4). A majority of the amplicons included in the panel were detected. Genes related to altered host defense, natural killer (NK) cell signaling and development, apoptosis, etc., were overexpressed in severe necrosis (IGJ, GZMA, TNFSF10, KLRB1, CD160). Expression of leukocyte antigens (ITGAM, ITGAX) and cytokines and chemokine receptors (IL1A, IL1B, CCL2, CCL3) were upregulated in patients with mild-moderate necrosis. Gene expression in survivor and non-survivor

RNA-Seq analysis and identification of DEGs

Differential expression analysis showed six genes being significantly differentially expressed (FDR-adjusted p<0.1) between survivors and the non-survivors. Genes related to chemokines attracting neutrophils (Chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand, CXCL1), guanylate binding protein−1 (GBP1, role in cell-autonomous immunity) and prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 gene (PTGS2, encodes for the cyclo-oxygenase-2 (COX2) enzyme, needed for prostanoid biosynthesis involved in inflammation and mitogenesis), CXCL11 (cellular response to lipopolysaccharide; chemokine-mediated signaling pathway; and neutrophil chemotaxis), CXCL9 (leukocyte chemotaxis, recruitment, and differentiation), and CXCL10 (recruits immune cells like macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), NK cells, and Th1 cells to sites of inflammation) were all increased in non-survivors. Discussion

Our study investigates gene expression patterns underlying severe necrosis and mortality in preterm infants with surgical NEC by using mRNA sequencing from FFPE intestinal tissue samples. Overall, our results show that severe necrosis, as defined by intestinal pathology, is associated with increased expression of genes involved in host defense, NK cell signaling and development, and apoptosis. In addition, survivors of surgical NEC display distinct immune gene expression profiles characterized by increased expression of leukocyte antigens and cytokine receptors, indicating a more active immune response relative to non-survivors. Conversely, non-survivors exhibit increased inflammation and chemotactic signaling. Furthermore, the degree of necrosis was associated with additional DEGs, underscoring its central role in shaping immune regulation.

These findings are consistent with prior research. Tremblay et al.24 analyzed RNA expression in ileal samples from preterm infants with NEC and compared them to samples from infants with other diseases. They found that NEC intestines showed over-representation of pathways related to innate immunity, such as altered T and B cell signaling, granulocyte adhesion and diapedesis, B cell development, and pattern recognition receptor roles. Moreover, Topp Cluster analysis revealed that NEC was marked by increased lymphocyte and leukocyte migration, chemotaxis, and adhesion, while functions related to lipid metabolism were downregulated, defining a distinct biological signature for NEC containing severe necrosis, relative to the others.24 The investigative group reported the upregulation of CXCL10, TLR4, TLR10, DEFA5, REG3A, LCN2, and TFF3 and downregulation of HBA2 and HBG2 expression in NEC. They also reported a high degree of similarity between NEC and Crohn’s disease gene expression.24 Similarly, our study identified genes related to altered host defense, NK cell signaling and development, and apoptosis as being overexpressed in surgical NEC infants. Notably, in our cohort, CXCL11 (cellular response to lipopolysaccharide; chemokine-mediated signaling; neutrophil chemotaxis), CXCL9 (leukocyte chemotaxis, recruitment, and differentiation), and CXCL10 (recruitment of macrophages, dendritic cells, NK cells, and Th1 cells to inflammatory sites) were all increased in non-survivors. These shared chemokine-driven patterns further support the association between escalating inflammatory signaling and poorer clinical outcomes in NEC.

Further supporting the immunologic basis of NEC, Xie et al. analyzed RNA-Seq data from intestinal tissues of 12 preterm infants with NEC, NEC self-control, and normal controls, detecting 34 712 genes and identifying 7463 DEGs.25 Gene Ontology analysis showed these DEGs were mainly involved in chemokine receptor binding, transporter activity, and growth factor binding, while Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis indicated significant enrichment in toll-like receptor signaling, Th17 cell differentiation, and cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathways. Immune infiltration profiles differed notably among the three tissue groups.25 The author’s results reinforce our findings, particularly the strong involvement of chemokine-mediated signaling and innate immune activation. Additionally, the broader transcriptomic dataset supports the biological relevance of the immune pathways associated with severe NEC and non-survivors.

A recent study analyzed 129 formula-fed preterm piglets diagnosed with NEC at necropsy on day 5, comparing subgroups of NEC (n=20) and control piglets (n=19) via whole-blood transcriptome analysis.26 Lesions were scored on a 1–6 scale, with scores ≥3 indicating NEC. Severe cases (scores 5–6) showed transmural necrosis or pneumatosis intestinalis.26 A total of 344 DEGs were identified between NEC and control groups, highlighting systemic immune responses and inflammation. Validation confirmed AOAH, ARG2, FKBP5, PAK2, and STAT3 as genes significantly altered in severe NEC cases, detectable in both whole blood and dried blood spots (DBS).26 In summary, blood gene expression changes occurred before severe clinical manifestations, suggesting potential for early NEC detection. DBS sampling emerged as a feasible method for small-volume blood collection in infants, enabling use as a potential biomarker.26 Overall, the piglet data complement our findings by showing that immune dysregulation and inflammatory activation are not limited to intestinal tissue but are systemically detectable and may precede overt clinical disease. Although the species and sample type differ, both studies converge on the idea that escalating inflammation is associated with more severe NEC.

Oxidative stress (OS) may also contribute to the development of NEC. Xiong et al. explored OS mechanisms in NEC focusing on OS-related genes, key signaling pathways, macrophage subtypes, and potential therapeutic targets.27 This work underscores how OS and macrophage heterogeneity shape the NEC immune microenvironment. By comparison, our study focuses on gene expression patterns linked to necrosis severity and survival in surgical NEC. We found that severe necrosis and non-survival are associated with heightened inflammatory and chemotactic signaling, while survivors show a more regulated immune response. Although the two studies examine different aspects of NEC biology, both consistently demonstrate that dysregulated immune activation, whether driven by OS pathways or chemokine-mediated inflammation, is strongly associated with more severe disease. Together, they highlight complementary molecular mechanisms contributing to NEC pathogenesis.

Comparison of our surgical NEC transcriptomic analysis with the publicly available GSE46619 dataset reveals the multifaceted molecular landscape of NEC.28 The GSE46619 study, which compared NEC tissues with surgical controls, identified extensive transcriptomic alterations dominated by metabolic pathway disruptions, including tryptophan, fatty acid, and arachidonic acid metabolism, and denoted several hub genes such as AGT, IL8, KNG1, ACACB, and CAT as potential contributors to NEC pathogenesis.28 In contrast, our targeted immune-focused RNASeq study provides a severity and outcome-stratified profile of NEC, demonstrating that immune activation and chemokine signaling are tightly linked to histopathologic necrosis and mortality. Taken together, these findings suggest a complementary model in which broad metabolic disturbances characterize early or global NEC susceptibility. The physiologic changes may escalate immune dysregulation, particularly among genes involved in chemokine-mediated neutrophil recruitment and innate immune activation, driving the progression to severe necrosis and mortality in infants with surgical NEC.

A major strength of this study is the direct analysis of human intestinal tissue from preterm infants with surgical NEC, providing biological insight into disease pathophysiological mechanisms. By stratifying infants according to necrosis severity and survival status, the study provides a unique ‘within-NEC’ comparison that highlights clinically relevant transcriptional differences. Linking transcriptomic patterns with histopathologic necrosis scores further strengthens biological interpretation. Additionally, the identification of gene expression signatures associated with survival offers potential prognostic value and aligns with findings from independent human and animal NEC transcriptomic studies, reinforcing the validity of the observed pathways.

Despite these important findings, our study possesses several important limitations. The samples were obtained from a single center, and the cohort was relatively small (n=36), which reduces statistical power and raises concerns about generalizability and reproducibility. The long accrual period for sample collection may also introduce biological and clinical management variability that may impact external validity. In the absence of non-NEC or healthy intestinal controls, it is difficult to determine if observed expression patterns are specific to NEC or represent generalized inflammation. Since intestinal tissues were collected at the time of surgery, it is unclear whether changes in immune gene expression reflect causal mechanisms or secondary responses to established necrosis. The lack of adjustment for clinical variables may also be viewed as a weakness, although our goal was to assess immune-regulating genes’ association with varying degrees of NEC.

Use of FFPE samples, which often contain degraded RNA, may bias results toward stable transcripts despite normalization for RNA quality. While the use of a targeted panel of 400 immune-related genes enables deep interrogation of pathways central to host defense, inflammation, and immune regulation, it may overlook upstream contributions of epithelial, metabolic, or vascular-related genes to NEC severity. Furthermore, since we did not independently validate gene expression (quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), immunohistochemistry, protein assays), our associations are preliminary. Finally, the observational design precludes any ability to infer causality, leaving it uncertain whether observed expression differences reflect primary disease processes or secondary systemic responses. Together, these shortcomings increase the likelihood of false-positive and false-negative findings, and therefore our results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

In conclusion, this study identifies DEGs in intestinal tissue samples from premature infants with NEC and highlights molecular patterns that may be of clinical interest. Survivors and non-survivors with surgical NEC exhibit distinct immune gene expression profiles, reflecting differing immune environments at the time of surgery. Non-survivors demonstrate increased expression of genes involved in inflammation and chemotactic signaling, including several neutrophil-attracting chemokines, consistent with a heightened proinflammatory state. In contrast, survivors show higher expression of leukocyte antigens and cytokine receptors, suggesting an immune profile associated with active or regulated host response rather than implying a direct protective mechanism. Overall, this study provides exploratory insight into the immune landscape of surgical NEC and underscores the relevance of inflammatory and immunoregulatory pathways in shaping clinical outcomes. By identifying gene expression signatures associated with necrosis severity and survival, this study lays the groundwork for future research aimed at validating these biomarkers and clarifying their mechanistic roles. A deeper understanding of these pathways, combined with broader genomic profiling and independent validation, may ultimately guide the development of targeted interventions to modulate inflammation and improve outcomes for infants with NEC.
