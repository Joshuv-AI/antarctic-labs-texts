Mapping the structure of an emerging field: A scientometric decoding of large language model applications in the dental field
Authors: Yue Lin, Yiseul Choi, Wonse Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo map the knowledge structure of large language model (LLM) applications in the dental field (LADF) through a dual-database scientometric study that highlights trends, collaborations, hotspots, and future directions.Materials and methodsThe Web of Science and Scopus were searched on August 1, 2025. LLM-related articles in dentistry were screened. After removing duplicates, 311 English articles and reviews were included in the study. Bibliometrix (v5.0) was used for characteristic and citation analyses. CiteSpace (v6.4.R1) was used for keyword analysis.ResultsThe number of publications surged from 24 (2023) to 182 (Jan–Aug 2025). Among all publications, 90% were original research articles. Two-thirds were open-access, funded mainly by governments. As for the collaboration network, author and institutional networks were fragmented. National-level collaboration was stronger. High-income countries dominated the LADF output. Keyword clustering revealed a hub-and-spoke structure that included three research frontiers: 1. broadening applications, 2. deepening clinical use, and 3. comparative evaluation, with accuracy and quality as central themes.ConclusionsLADF has expanded rapidly, but research remains fragmented. Shared datasets, stronger global collaboration, and the development of standardized evaluation metrics, particularly for diagnostic and question-answering proficiencies, are necessary to address the central themes of accuracy and quality.

Introduction

The recent proliferation of large language models (LLMs), as exemplified by public-facing commercial tools, such as ChatGPT, is changing numerous aspects of healthcare [1]. Dentistry is a field with complicated diagnostics, clinician-patient communication, and extensive educational demands, with substantial opportunities for the application of advanced AI technologies [2]. Current evidence indicates that LLMs have potential utility in diverse areas, from assisting in the differential diagnosis of oral lesions to generating personalized patient education materials and serving as interactive training tools for dental students [3], [4]. This burgeoning interest is reflected in the rapid growth of academic publications on the LLM applications in the dental field (LADF) since late 2022 [5], [6]. Notably, LADF output surged from 24 articles in 2023 to 182 in the as of August 1, 2025, statistically signaling the emergence and exponential expansion of a novel research area in dentistry.

The rapid expansion has led to a fragmented and academically chaotic research landscape [7]. Several critical problems have emerged. A clear map of the field's intellectual structure, emerging research hotspots, and key collaborative networks is missing. The efforts of new researchers risk redundancy, funding agencies may struggle to identify priority areas for strategic investment, and clinicians are left without evidence-based guidance on the true capabilities and limitations of these emerging tools [8], [9]. This lack of a global data-driven perspective critically hinders the development of LADF, making it hard to find the correct frontier.

To address this critical gap, we conducted the first comprehensive and systematic scientometric analysis of LLMs in dentistry. Traditional narrative reviews do not provide an objective and comprehensive understanding of the overall development and core trends of LADF. Therefore, we adopted a scientometric analysis approach to quantitatively and visually examine all published literature. Using this method, the study aimed to offer insights to guide clinical translations by uncovering trends, research gaps, and strategic priorities that cannot be identified through individual studies. A key methodological strength of this study is the integration of two premier scholarly databases, namely, the Web of Science and Scopus. This dual-database approach, the best practice for rigorous scientometric studies, enables unparalleled depth and breadth of coverage. This approach aimed to capture a more comprehensive view of the LADF structure.

The objectives were (1) to examine publication trends and identify the most influential authors, institutions, and countries, as well as to reflect their collaboration networks; (2) to explore research hotspots and structures through keyword analysis; (3) to investigate highly cited papers to uncover the intellectual foundations of the field; and (4) to propose strategic, data-driven recommendations for future research based on the results. By quantifying research outputs and creating intellectual maps, this study provides a panoramic view of the current research landscape and offers a crucial reference for researchers, clinicians, and policymakers to contribute to the future development of this field.

Materials and methods

Data sources and screening process

In the identification phase, a literature search was conducted on August 1, 2025, using the Web of Science (WoS) (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) and Scopus (Elsevier, Amsterdam, Netherlands) databases. The initial search, based on the query in Table 1, identified 1188 records, comprising 453 articles from WoS and 735 articles from Scopus. The study identification and screening processes were designed to be systematic and reproducible. The PRISMA guideline principles were applied. These two sets of search records were kept separate and screened independently. In this study, LADF was defined as the body of research that investigates the development, validation, and application of LLMs in dentistry, including dental education, clinical practice, and research contexts, ranging from LLM-assisted radiographic interpretation and differential diagnosis to personalized patient education. A three-stage screening process was implemented (Fig. 1). The screening of WoS and Scopus datasets was conducted independently in three steps. Step 1: Filtering according to document characteristics. An initial filter was applied to both sets to include only peer-reviewed “articles” or “reviews”, thereby ensuring a focus on substantive peer-reviewed research. The language was restricted to English to maintain consistency in subsequent scientometric analyses. As non-English publications constituted less than 5% of the initial results (9 of 453 in WoS, 1.98%; 31 of 735 in Scopus, 4.21%), this restriction was not expected to introduce a significant bias. This round yielded 402 and 601 records from WoS and Scopus, respectively.Fig. 1The identification, screening, and inclusion process.

The identification, screening, and inclusion process.

Step 2: Screening the relevance of titles and abstracts. Two authors independently screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining records in each dataset to assess their relevance to dentistry. The inter-rater reliability for this process was substantial (Cohen's Kappa of WoS=0.803, 95% CI=0.76–0.85; Cohen's Kappa of Scopus=0.779, 95% CI=0.71–0.84). A third senior author resolved all the disagreements. Following this stage, 259 articles from WoS and 294 articles from Scopus were retained for full-text review.

Step 3: Full-text review. This step was performed on the articles retained in the previous stage. This final eligibility assessment was crucial for confirming that the LLM was a central element of the study, which was often absent in the abstract. We further excluded articles that involved general machine learning or deep learning, but did not specifically address LLMs. This resulted in 253 and 273 articles from WoS and Scopus, respectively.

In the inclusion phase, two sets of eligible articles (253 from WoS and 273 from Scopus) were merged to create a pool of 526 articles. Through a rigorous deduplication process, 215 duplicate records were removed. After a verification process, 311 articles were included in the scientometric analysis. Research tools

The titles, abstracts, keywords, and related metadata were downloaded from WoS and Scopus. All extracted materials were saved as CSV files in Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) and as TXT files (Microsoft, Redmond, WA).

We implemented a hybrid analytical approach to create a detailed knowledge landscape. We applied static analysis using Bibliometrix (version 5.0; K-Synth Srl, Academic Spin-Off of the University of Naples Federico II, Naples, Italy) to identify the main theoretical basis and characteristics of LADF [16]. To analyze the field’s development, research structures, and study hotspots, we conducted a trace using CiteSpace (version 6.4.R1, Advanced; Chaomei Chen, Drexel University, Philadelphia, PA, USA) [17]. This combination of static and dynamic tools ensured a thorough and high-quality analysis.

Research process

CiteSpace (slice length = one year) was used to conduct network analysis. When profiling the LADF literature, we adopted CiteSpace’s g-index strategy because of its wide uptake in scientometric studies to facilitate cross-study comparability. This strategy reduced the node set, thereby lowering the network density, improving visual clarity, and preserving representativeness [18].

To determine the optimal k value, a sensitivity analysis was performed by testing various thresholds. After setting the node type to “keyword”, we found that the relative size of the largest connected component (LCC) peaked at 83% when k was set at 20. Beyond 20, the proportion began to decrease (e.g., 80% at k = 30), indicating a decreased structural integrity. When k was set at 15, the network became relatively sparse, and did not adequately reflect the LADF structure. Consequently, a g-index with k = 20 was selected as the final parameter to ensure that our analysis was based on the most coherent and representative core knowledge network, while avoiding irrelevant connections [18].

The other parameters of CiteSpace were as follows. 1. Time slicing was configured from January 2023 to August 2025, with one year per slice. 2. For the term sources, we selected titles, abstracts, author keywords, and Keywords Plus. 3. The link strength was set to “cosine”, and the link scope was set as “within slices”. 4. No pruning methods were applied. 5. The visualizations were generated using the “cluster view – static” and “show merged network” options. Bibliometrix was selected for the citation analysis. Microsoft Excel and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) were used for charting and descriptive statistics. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Characteristic analysis

The identified LADF publications highlighted the novelty of the field and its recent explosive growth. A scientometric analysis of 311 publications revealed a sharp increase in output from 24 articles in 2023 to105 in 2024, and 182 in 2025 (as of August). The corpus was predominantly composed of original research articles (90.35%), with a small fraction of reviews (9.65%). The Bradford's law analysis identified a core set of journals driving this discourse [19]. Following the standard Bradford distribution model, journals were ranked by publication volume and divided into three zones, with each zone accounting for approximately one-third of the total articles. The first zone (Zone 1) was identified as the core set. The three most productive journals were BMC Oral Health (24 articles), Cureus Journal of Medical Science (15 articles), and the European Journal of Dental Education (13 articles). The identification of these core journals, as dictated by Bradford's law, provides a starting point for clinicians, educators, and researchers who wish to access the most impactful and cutting-edge LADF research. Moreover, the disciplinary focus of these core journal sources aligned closely with the interdisciplinary nature of LADF. The key focus was primarily rooted in “dentistry, oral surgery, and oral medicine” (49.9% in Scopus and 66.4% in WoS). Other areas that featured included “social sciences/education” (10.6% in Scopus and 10.4% in WoS) and “computer science/medical informatics” (3.9% in Scopus and 4.40% in WoS). Collectively, the literature accrued an average of 8.57 citations per publication, indicating an emerging scholarly impact (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Application of Bradford's Law to the LADF literature.

Funding and access analysis

Among the 311 articles analyzed, 55 explicitly acknowledged financial support. The funding statements were classified by sponsoring entities, and the supporters were ranked by frequency. The leading sponsor was the National Natural Science Foundation of China (6 acknowledgments), followed by the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science, and Technology (MEXT, Japan), the Japan Society for the Promotion of Science, and the National Research Foundation of Korea (4 acknowledgments each). Open access (OA) status was declared in 199 papers. The most common was Gold OA (145 papers), followed by Green OA (108 papers) and gold-hybrid (43 papers). Green OA, also known as self-archiving, refers to the practice of depositing a specific version of a manuscript (usually the accepted manuscript), into a public repository, such as an institutional archive or PubMed Central. The accepted manuscripts have been peer-reviewed and accepted for publication, but have not yet undergone final copyediting and typesetting. This practice is a common practice for complying with funder mandates (e.g., the NIH Public Access Policy) and is generally permitted by publishers (often following a specified embargo period). Consequently, some articles were counted in several OA categories, such as those appearing simultaneously in Gold OA and Green OA [20].

Research network analysis

From this scientometric analysis, the three most productive authors were Ana Suarez Garcia (Freq = 8), Yolanda Freire Mancebo (Freq = 8), and Hossein Mohammad-Rahimi (Freq = 7). Yolanda Freire Mancebo and Ana Suarez Garcia are affiliated with the Universidad Europea de Madrid, where their work centers on artificial intelligence and patient safety in dentistry [21]. Hossein Mohammad-Rahimi is from the Department of Dentistry and Oral Health, Aarhus University, Denmark and focuses on assessing LLM performance in dentistry [22]. Two tightly knit groups were prominent in the LADF authorship network, namely the Kyushu Dental University group (degree = 8) and the Universidad Europea de Madrid group (degree = 6). Nevertheless, nearly all authors exhibited zero betweenness centrality (only three reached 0.01), highlighting the weak connections between different research communities (Fig. 3).Fig. 3The scientometric analysis of authors.

The most connected institutions were Charité Universitätsmedizin Berlin (degree = 12, betweenness centrality = 0), Saveetha Institute of Medical and Technical Sciences (degree = 10, centrality = 0.01), and Ajman University (degree = 8, betweenness centrality = 0.07). Institutions with relatively high degrees (more than 10) were modest in their bridging roles (betweenness centrality < 0.1), indicating a globally sparse network with several small components (Fig. 4). Keyword analysis

Using CiteSpace, we generated a keyword co-occurrence map (Fig. 6). To improve the accuracy, synonymous terms with different spellings (e.g., “artificial intelligence” and “AI”) were merged. Specific instances that represent particular meanings (e.g., ChatGPT) were retained as independent nodes from the broader category to capture the specific influence of these nodes and avoid semantic dilution. The network of 169 nodes and 520 links resulted in a relatively sparse overall density of 0.0366. Nevertheless, its largest connected component encompassed 141 nodes (83%), indicating the emergence of a tightly interlinked core knowledge structure.Fig. 6The scientometric analysis of keyword co-occurrences and the burst terms.

The scientometric analysis of keyword co-occurrences and the burst terms.

We conducted an analysis using keyword-frequency data. The top 21 words with the highest co-occurrence frequency were divided into two categories: First, foundational words that indicate the article's attributes, including: artificial intelligence, large language models, natural language processing, generative artificial intelligence, dentistry, machine learning, article, comparative study, health, deep learning, and information. Second, the specific research elements included dental education, humans, ChatGPT, oral surgery, performance, medical education, patient education, education, diagnosis, and students (Fig. 6).

To identify bursting research frontiers by year, we used Kleinberg's burst detection algorithm, with the Gamma (γ) parameter set to 0.6 and the minimum duration set to one. This relatively high value acted as a conservative filter, increasing the cost of the state transitions within the model. As a result, only keywords with the most significant and intense growth in frequency were detected. Minor hotspots were excluded. This deliberate choice guaranteed that the 12 resulting burst keywords were robust indicators of the field's core intellectual shift. The burst term 'article' was excluded from the final interpretation as it served as a document descriptor rather than a conceptual keyword (Fig. 6).

For the keyword co-occurrence network, we identified salient thematic clusters and evaluated their quality using modularity (Q) and silhouette (S) statistics. The modularity value of 0.5623 exceeds the widely recognized threshold of 0.3, suggesting well-defined community boundaries. The mean silhouette score of 0.8684 surpassed the “highly convincing” benchmark of 0.7; meanwhile, every individual cluster exhibited a silhouette score greater than 0.80, showing great internal cohesion and clear separation among clusters. The harmonic mean of Q and S is 0.6828, further confirming the robustness and interpretive reliability of the clustering results.

We used CiteSpace's LLR algorithm to generate the initial cluster labels and then manually corrected them based on the core content of the literature within each cluster to ensure accuracy [30]. The original LLR cluster labels were as follows: #0. oral health (18.79, 1.0E−4); #1. Precautionary anecdotes (36.63, 1.0E−4); #2. Transformative healthcare (61.38, 1.0E−4); #3. Medical Diagnostic (21.53, 1.0E−4); #4. Microsoft Bing (111.72, 1.0E−4); #5. Using Open-source Software (34.18, 1.0E−4); #6. assessing ChatGPT (100.64, 1.0E−4); #7. Pediatric dentistry (86.77, 1.0E−4); #8. Oral Surgery (52.05, 1.0E−4).

The LLR algorithm often tended to “overfit”. It typically extracted statistically prominent and highly specific terms (e.g., a specific product such as “Microsoft Bing” or an obscure phrase such as “precautionary anecdote”) from the citing articles. These terms often fail to capture the academic thematic clusters. It is recognized that in scientometric methodology, purely algorithmic labels sometimes fail to “converge to a focused message” and lack overall accuracy [17], [31]. Therefore, manual relabelling based on a thorough reading of the included documents is a necessary standard practice [32].

To ensure the accuracy and representativeness of cluster labels, an expert review process was implemented to replace the initial labels generated using CiteSpace. For each of the nine clusters, 15 experts, including five senior academics at professorial level or director level, five clinical specialists with more than 8 years of clinical experience, and five researchers who will conduct/conducted LLMs-related research, independently reviewed the cluster's keywords, and the titles and abstracts of the five major citing articles, to create a representative thematic label (Table 3, Table 4). Descriptive labels contained fewer than five words for each cluster. The authors then determined the final label by collecting all 15 proposals, grouping them by semantic themes, and selecting the most frequently nominated category. The most cited paper was by Balel, who discussed the possibility of using ChatGPT in the oral and maxillofacial domains. Discussion

This is the first comprehensive scientometric analysis of LADF. Our findings highlighted the rapid growth in LADF, but also the fragmentation of this emerging field. Research has increased since late 2022 and reflected a strong interest in a wide range of applications. Using the Web of Science and Scopus, we provide a panoramic view of key contributors, research hotspots, and influential works, offering a foundation to guide future scholarly and clinical efforts in this domain.

Analyzing LADF characteristics, the most significant finding was the near-explosive growth in publications since 2023. This surge aligned closely with the launch and popularization of ChatGPT (GPT-3.5, released in November 2022). Although it is simple to attribute this growth to a single event, the increased accessibility of general-purpose LLMs and the maturation of their associated toolchains have significantly lowered the barrier to their use [42]. This, in turn, has spurred the rapid application in various dental domains and ignited widespread interest in and resource investment from the dental academic community. The high average citation rate of 8.57 citations per publication in the past two years served as evidence.

LADF research is not only being published in large quantities but also being actively read and cited by peers, which is a symbol of the rapid formation of an academic community [43]. Furthermore, the corpus was predominantly composed of original research articles (over 90%), with few reviews. This is a typical characteristic of a field in its early phases, indicating that the primary research task is to establish theoretical and practical LADF frameworks [44]. Consequently, the need for a systematic synthesis (i.e., reviews) has not yet become prominent. Finally, the distribution of publications across a diverse range of journals highlighted the need for interdisciplinary collaboration to expand LLM applications in dentistry. Taken together, these trends depict the field as being dynamic but still at an early exploratory stage, accompanied by rapid expansion and efforts to produce theoretical and practical work.

Regarding funding, our analysis identified three main sponsor categories: national/public government funds (e.g., MEXT), university funds (e.g., Hiroshima University), and hospital grants (e.g., National Taiwan University Hospital). Notably, sponsorships were most frequently reported by entities at both the national and institutional levels in China and Japan, reflecting a significant interest in applying LLMs in dentistry in these countries. However, funding from corporations and professional societies was limited. This reliance on public funds may indicate that researchers emphasize public welfare objectives rather than commercial targets [45]. The focus on public goods is also reflected in the dissemination of research. We found that nearly two-thirds of the articles were open access (OA). This trend is likely influenced by the nature of the core technology. Recent advancements in this field have been largely propelled by highly accessible and versatile LLMs from major technological companies. Consequently, the low barrier to entry for these tools indicates that the research findings may be relevant to dental specialists, scholars from other disciplines, and the general public. Authors may therefore prefer the OA model to ensure easier access to their work, aiming to accelerate LADF [46].

The scientometric analysis indicated that as an emerging research field, LADF's collaboration network exhibited significant structural differences across various levels. First, at the author level, a productive and influential core group of authors has yet to be formed. According to Price’s law, authors with three or more publications are defined as core authors (Ana Suarez: eight papers, yielding a threshold of 2.12). However, this group accounted for only 9.61% of the total output, reflecting a relatively low contribution. Structurally, the collaboration network is similarly sparse, with a vast majority of authors showing near-zero betweenness centrality, symbolizing an early stage of development. While small, internally cohesive research teams were identified, such as those at Kyushu Dental University and Universidad Europea de Madrid, they lacked effective connections with one another, leading to distinctly fragmented author networks [25], [47].

Second, collaborative networks exhibited a similarly fragmented landscape at the institutional level. The network density was extremely low (density = 0.0298), with each institution collaborating with only about 3.4 partners on average (among the 116 institutions, we detected 199 collaborative links). The entire network was divided into multiple small components, with the largest connected component comprising only 44 institutions (37%). This is further corroborated by Price's law analysis, which showed that only 37.9% of the institutions meet the core productivity threshold. Collectively, these data indicate that inter-institutional collaboration is sparse and disconnected and that a dominant core group of institutions is still in the early stages of formation [48].

In contrast, the national-level collaboration network demonstrated greater consolidation and a core-periphery structure. Based on Price’s law (with a threshold of 6 publications, according to the highest production, the USA), the identified core group of countries contributed 78.7% of the total output, indicating a high degree of production concentration. The core groups included the USA, Turkey, China, India, Saudi Arabia, Germany, Brazil, Japan, Spain, Iran, the United Kingdom, Australia, the United Arab Emirates, Canada, Switzerland, Jordan, Pakistan, Greece, Cyprus, Italy, and South Korea. In terms of network connectivity, a large connected component that includes 80% of all countries was formed. More importantly, nations, such as Germany, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Saudi Arabia, served as crucial “bridges” in multinational collaborations, as evidenced by their high centrality scores. This may be related to the national policies in these countries, such as the NSDAI policy in Saudi Arabia and the Aktionsplan Künstliche Intelligenz policy in Germany. Nevertheless, the overall network density remained low (density = 0.1104), suggesting that the deeply integrated global network had not yet fully matured. This contrast between countries, institutions, and authors reflected a typical evolutionary pattern in which the aggregate output of a few research-intensive countries created a core group. For widespread institutional collaboration to occur, a truly integrated network is necessary. Such an integrated network takes time to develop.

A notable paradox in our findings is the discrepancy between the publication volume and network centrality, particularly in Turkey and China. Despite being the second (Freq = 60) and third (Freq = 31) most productive countries, both exhibited unexpectedly low betweenness centrality (Turkey = 0; China = 0.05). In scientometrics, centrality measures a country's role as a bridge to a global network. Turkey’s high output was primarily driven by domestic research or isolated bilateral ties (Degree = 1, mainly with the USA), which kept it at the periphery of the global collaborative web. Similarly, China possesses a self-sufficient domestic research ecosystem. While major funding bodies like the National Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC) provide substantial support, their funding preferences often favor focused research within single institutions or specific teams, which may contribute to localized clusters and the relatively low betweenness centrality observed in our analysis. These findings highlight the critical need for highly productive nations to actively expand their international collaborations.

We further classified the countries based on the World Bank's annual income data. According to the data, LADF involved 1 low-income country, 10 lower-middle-income countries, 14 upper-middle-income countries, and 38 high-income countries [49]. A chi-square goodness-of-fit test was performed. The results indicated a highly significant deviation from the hypothesized uniform distribution, X2(df = 3, N = 498 publication frequencies) = 382.35, p < 0.001. The effect size for this disparity was very large (Cohen's w = 0.88), indicating a profound imbalance in the research output. A post-hoc analysis of the standardized residuals, using a Bonferroni-corrected significance level of α = 0.0125, was conducted to identify the specific sources of this imbalance. The analysis revealed that high-income countries published significantly more articles than expected (standardized residual = +15.01). Conversely, lower-middle-income and low-income countries published significantly fewer than expected (standardized residuals = −5.78 and −10.98, respectively). Publication frequency in upper-middle-income countries did not deviate significantly from the expected value. These findings indicate that research on LADF is predominantly driven by high-income nations, as reflected by the constituents of the core group of countries. This dominance by high-income countries is likely attributable to several factors. First, these countries generally have more advanced medical education systems, which train and sustain large numbers of students and researchers, providing a solid talent base for frontier exploration. Second, substantial research funding from governments and universities enables local medical research institutions to purchase expensive AI equipment or make unrestricted use of LLMs provided by commercial companies [50]. In addition, a well-developed digital infrastructure may further consolidate the leading research positions of developed countries. In addition to these fundamental advantages, the effect of policy as a catalyst is important. Countries such as Germany have approved corresponding policies to accelerate AI development, which consists of LLMs and their applications in multiple fields. Without improvement, this limitation may exacerbate the digital divide as the development and validation of LADF could concentrate on data from populations in high-income countries. This carries a potential risk of bias in the questions currently being addressed in the LADF, the datasets being employed, and the ethical frameworks that are inevitably shaped by the context of the “Global North”. As a result, future AI tools would hold the risk of not only acting less effectively in solving the oral health problems of diverse populations worldwide, but also creating algorithmic bias, such as misdiagnosing conditions that are known to be more prevalent in certain groups (e.g., noma) or recommending treatments that are unavailable or unsuitable in resource-limited areas.

In our scientometric analysis of keywords, a high degree of overlap was found, which strongly suggests that LADF is an emerging and interdisciplinary area. As research is still in its early exploratory stage, no fixed research paradigm has yet been established, and intellectual exchange is currently mainstream [30]. Explanations of the research frontiers.

Researchers are working to broaden the applications of LLMs in dentistry. This is reflected in clusters 2 (Application extensibility) and 5 (LLM and software integration). The frequent appearance of the keywords, “dental education”, “medical education”, and “patient education”, confirmed that researchers have been actively trying to extend LLM technology from purely clinical tasks to education, patient instruction, and other non-clinical support domains. For example, using LLMs to generate scripts for data storage or acting as examination assistants to dental educators [51], [52]. Another possible application is the generation of personalized patient communication materials, such as postoperative instructions [34].

Regarding vertical deepening, research is advancing toward greater specialization and depth in specific clinical subfields. Cluster #7 (Domain-Specific Applications) was the most representative, focusing on the application of LLMs in particular specialties, such as pediatric dentistry and periodontology. Oral surgery is currently one of the most concentrated areas of vertical exploration, as reflected by the high frequency of “oral surgery” as a keyword. In this context, “depth” refers to the transition of LLMs from general medical knowledge retrieval to analyzing specialized clinical tasks. Examples include evaluating the reliability of LLMs in managing complex pediatric cases and facilitating the prediction of specific anatomical and pathological conditions during oral and maxillofacial surgeries [40], [53].

From the comparative analysis, it is clear that this is a key paradigm for evaluating the capabilities of LLMs and determining their value. It can be further divided into two levels: the first one is human–machine comparison, as shown in Cluster #0 (comparison with human), researchers compare the performance of LLMs with that of human experts (reflected by the keyword “humans”) to assess reliability; the second one is model–model comparison, which could be reflected by Cluster #4 (chatbot comparison), whose main characteristic is comparing different LLMs (e.g., ChatGPT, Gemini) against one another.

To advance these three research frontiers, the scientific and rational evaluation of LLM performance is crucial. Our analysis reveals two core criteria of evaluation. For clinically relevant tasks, “diagnostic capability” (Cluster #3, represented by the high-frequency keyword “diagnosis”) may serve as the gold standard, while for non-clinical tasks (such as answering academic questions), “question answering proficiency” (Cluster #6, represented by the high-frequency keyword “performance”) is the main criterion. However, among all clusters, the underlying standard, which could be described as the “core”, is accuracy and quality (Cluster #1). This explains why Cluster #1 emerged as the central cluster connecting multiple research themes (Fig. 7).

The observed significant overlap is not a limitation. Rather, it is indicative of the multifaceted nature of this emerging field. For example, a study evaluating the performance of LLMs may employ chatbot comparison (Cluster #4) and also compare chatbots to human experts (Cluster #0). This complex interconnectedness serves to verify and ensure the accuracy and quality of technology (Cluster #1) [37]. The consensus levels for clusters #0, #7, and #8 were relatively low, indicating that the conceptual boundaries of the field remained blurred at this stage. Even for domain experts, for example, a study comparing ChatGPT with human performance could be classified either as “comparison with human” or “clinical performance evaluation”. Such divergence among experts reinforces our view that the field is characterized by conceptual fluidity and an as-yet-immature intellectual structure.

Burst terms were divided into two phases. Phase I (2023) focused on foundation and validation. The burst terms in this phase centered on three fundamental questions: “what it is”, “where to use it”, and “is it good”. For definition, “artificial intelligence in dentistry” (strength 1.26, lasting through 2025) emerged as the core term that underpins the field, while “machine learning” (1.10) highlighted the emphasis on its technical foundation. In terms of application, “dental care” and “clinical practice” (both 1.22) were directly applied to real-world clinical settings. “Patient information” (1.06) indicated an early focus on communication and information generation. And “adult” (0.89) suggested that early studies deliberately concentrated on adult populations, avoiding the ethical and data-related complexities of pediatric research. Regarding evaluation, “evidence-based dentistry” and “data accuracy” (both 1.22) appeared simultaneously, reflecting a strong orientation toward rigorous assessment and reliability rather than uncritical enthusiasm.

Phase II (2024–2025) focused on expansion and specialization. Once feasibility was established, research advanced in two directions: horizontal expansion and vertical deepening. On the horizontal side, “health care” (1.60) was the strongest burst term, signaling a decisive shift of perspective—from viewing AI applications as isolated dental tools to situating them within broader health systems, including integration with systemic health. The appearance of “internet” (1.19) further marked the movement into open digital environments, such as evaluating the quality of dental information provided by public LLMs and exploring remote consultation. On the vertical side, “oral surgery” (0.91) stood out as evidence of the field moving into specialized and higher-risk clinical areas. Given its complex diagnosis and surgical planning, oral surgery provides an ideal test bed for examining its practical capabilities. These two phases aligned closely with the patterns identified in the cluster analysis, consolidating the coherence of thematic evolution.

It is worth mentioning that fundamental search terms, such as “large language models” and “ChatGPT”, were not identified as burst keywords despite their high overall frequencies (67 and 31, respectively). Methodologically, this is an expected result of the Kleinberg burst algorithm. These terms defined our initial search queries and their frequencies were consistently high from the outset. They lack the sudden relative surge required to trigger statistical bursts. However, this phenomenon reflected field-specific nomenclature preferences. The sustained high frequency of “ChatGPT” suggests that dental researchers exhibit a strong tendency to explicitly name the specific commercial models they evaluate in their clinical contexts, rather than relying solely on the broader term “large language models”.

The “hub-and-spoke” structure uncovered in this research, combined with the marked fragmentation at the author and institutional levels, provides evidence that proves that the LADF is currently in what Thomas S. Kuhn described as a “pre-paradigmatic science” stage. In this phase, an emerging research field has not yet formed a unified theoretical framework or dominant paradigm. Specifically, the characteristic of “un-unified” is shown in the low-density and weakly connected author and institutional collaboration networks, which aligns closely with Kuhn's description of multiple “competing schools” possessing different methods. Simultaneously, the “unified” is reflected in the fact that all research frontiers point toward the core theme of “accuracy and quality”. This indicates that, despite diverse research paths, the entire scholarly community is exploring a common and fundamental scientific problem.

This structure is a classic manifestation of a burgeoning pre-paradigmatic science; the central problem attracts multidimensional exploration from different research traditions, and these parallel efforts are gradually creating theoretical and practical foundations for the future establishment of a mature LADF paradigm.

Future research and limitations

As the volume of original research in the LADF continues to grow, there is an urgent need for high-quality systematic reviews and meta-analyses over the next one to two years. Such research should aim to synthesize existing evidence and delineate current limitations and research gaps to guide future studies. In addition to feasibility, the clinical and educational impacts of LADF should be a primary research focus. This includes investigating whether LLM-assisted diagnostic or treatment-oriented tools can improve patient outcomes and whether LLM-based educational tools can enhance students' clinical skills and knowledge more effectively than traditional methods. As LLMs transition towards practical applications, the related ethical problems, accountability, and regulatory challenges become increasingly prominent [54]. To further broaden the different application types of LLMs, authors should contact private enterprises or professional associations to explore potential research sponsorships with market-driven demands, in addition to seeking funding from public institutions.

Our findings demonstrate that the LADF is characterized by “micro-level fragmentation and macro-level consolidation”. Although this pattern is typical of an emerging discipline, it presents specific risks and opportunities. Current fragmentation at the institutional level may lead to redundant research efforts and a lack of standardized evaluation criteria. Furthermore, the high concentration of research output within a few core countries raises concerns regarding potential biases in future AI models trained on geographically limited data. To overcome these challenges, we recommend the following actions: 1. the establishment of shared, open-source clinical datasets and validation platforms to unify research standards; 2. Proactive leadership from the identified 'bridge' nations and institutions (e.g., Germany, the USA) in organizing international workshops, and the collaboration in the partnership with other income countries; 3. There is a call for funding agencies to create grant programs that specifically incentivize multi-institutional and international partnerships. Following these steps, the LADF community can bridge structural gaps and accelerate its growth to become a more robust and global field.

For future research pursuing horizontal expansion, vertical deepening, or comparative analysis, it is recommended that the assessment of “diagnostic capability” or "question answering proficiency" remains the central focus. Ultimately, these evaluations should be benchmarked against unwavering criteria of high accuracy and quality. Importantly, future research must actively engage with the rapid developments in LLMs and deep learning to maintain an up-to-date knowledge base. Researchers should consistently monitor and incorporate findings from the wider LLM literature.

This study has several limitations. First, the analytical tools used in this study have inherent limitations. Although CiteSpace and Bibliometrix are powerful scientometric software packages, their algorithms and functions evolve continuously. More sophisticated methods may emerge in the future, potentially providing more accurate predictions of research trends and revealing new knowledge connections within the field. Second, we focused on the literature inclusion strategy. This is reflected in our selection of databases (Web of Science and Scopus), languages (English only), and document types (articles and reviews). We acknowledge that this approach may have excluded unique publications from other databases and non-English literature, such as studies from Korea and China. However, considering that the target of this research was to identify critical works that were important in LADF and had been thoroughly peer reviewed, not selecting all relevant publications was acceptable. Future research should select a broader set of data sources to provide a more comprehensive view. Finally, this study presented a snapshot of the research landscape up to the final search date. Given the explosive growth in research on LLMs, there is an unavoidable publication lag, and cutting-edge research may not have been captured.

Conclusion

LLM applications in the dental field (LADF) is a rapidly expanding yet structurally fragmented domain. To advance this field, three critical areas must be addressed: First, the current core research questions center on safely broadening LLM applications and deepening their specialized clinical use, while maintaining the fundamental prerequisite of “accuracy and quality”. Second, researchers should prioritize developing standardized evaluation metrics, establishing shared open-source datasets, and fostering multinational collaborative networks to overcome institutional fragmentation and prevent geographic algorithmic bias. Finally, clinicians should utilize LLMs as supportive tools benchmarked against human experts. Concurrently, educators must integrate AI literacy into modern curricula, teaching students to critically evaluate LLM outputs and navigate their inherent limitations. Overall, progress in LADF will depend on balancing innovation with rigorous validation and responsible implementation.
