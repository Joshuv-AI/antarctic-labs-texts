Effects of antenatal depression on adverse birth outcomes among women attending antenatal care at Jimma University Medical Center, Ethiopia: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Bacha Workineh, Desta Workineh, Girma Beressa, Derese Eshetu, Mangistu Abera, Aberash Beyene Derribow, Bayisa Bereka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Existing evidence indicated an increasing burden of antenatal depression on adverse birth outcomes in low- and middle-income countries. However, the evidence regarding the effect of antenatal depression on adverse birth outcomes is not well understood in the study area. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the effect of antenatal depression on adverse birth outcomes at Jimma University Medical Center, Southwest Ethiopia. A facility-based prospective cohort study was conducted among 255 pregnant women at Jimma University Medical Center from May to September 2023. A convenient sampling technique was employed to select study subjects. Adverse birth outcomes were measured by having one or more negative newborn outcomes. Antenatal depression was assessed using the Edinburgh Postpartum Depression Scale. A pretested, interviewer-administered, structured questionnaire was used to collect the data. A bivariable and multivariable log-binomial model was used to examine the association between antenatal depression and adverse birth outcomes. A statistically significant association was declared at a P value less than 0.05. Results In this study, the estimated cumulative incidence of adverse birth outcomes was 53% and 29% among women with and without antenatal depression, respectively. The proportion of antenatal depression was 20%. Physically inactive women (ARR = 1.54;95%CI:1.22,1.94), exposed to tobacco (ARR = 2.64;95%CI:1.90,3.67), chronic medical illness (ARR = 2.14; 95%CI:1.68,2.72), induced labor (ARR = 1.47; 95%CI:1.17,1.85), and antenatal depression (ARR = 1.58; 95%CI:1.26, 1.95) were the predictors of the risk of adverse birth outcomes. The incidence of adverse birth outcomes among women with and without depression at Jimma University Medical Center was 53% and 29%, respectively. Physically inactive women, exposed to tobacco, chronic medical illness, induced labor, and particularly antenatal depression, were statistically significantly associated with newborn adverse birth outcomes. Thus, to minimize the incidence of adverse birth outcomes, early anticipation, screening, and management of antenatal depression are recommended during antenatal care follow-up.

Introduction

Depression is a continuous poor mood or loss of interest in pleasurable activities for at least two weeks, distinct from temporary sadness or stress1. Antenatal depression affects 10% women globally, with higher rates in low and middle-income countries1,2. The pooled prevalence of antenatal depression ranged from 22.7% to 34% in middle and low-income countries, which is considered a common mental disorder during pregnancy3,4. Antenatal depression has significant consequences for newborns’ adverse birth outcomes5. The Pacific Island region has a mean prevalence of 13% and 12% of newborn adverse birth outcomes (preterm births and Low birth weight6.

Sub-Saharan Africa has a pooled incidence of 29.7%, and South Africa has a 14.6% incidence2,7. In Ethiopia, the prevalence of antenatal depression is estimated at 24.2%, with a study in Jimma town revealing a rate of 16.6%8,9. Similarly, the prevalence of adverse birth outcomes in Ethiopia is 26.8%, with the highest rates being preterm birth (8.76%) and low birth weight (10.06%)10. Northeast Ethiopia has 32.5% adverse birth outcomes, whereas southeast Ethiopia’s Bale zone has 21%11,12.

The study findings indicated that certain factors are associated with adverse birth outcomes, affecting both newborns and their overall health13–16. Prenatal depression, multiple pregnancies, preterm membrane rupture, rural women, insufficient prenatal care, pregnancy-induced hypertension, age over 35, and complicated pregnancies are all associated with adverse newborn outcomes. Preterm delivery occurs before 37 weeks of pregnancy, is the most prevalent adverse birth outcome, that an associated with antenatal depression10,17.

The World Health Organization(WHO) defines preterm birth as the birth of a baby before 37 weeks of gestation, which affects more than 15 million newborns and their mothers18.

In Ethiopia, neonatal mortality is 33 per 1,000 live births, with preterm birth being the leading cause, accounting for 13.8% of cases in Jimma town19,20.

Antenatal depression can result in low birth weight (LBW), defined as a newborn weighing 2,500 g or less, with an estimated prevalence of 14.6% in Ethiopia and 7.8% in Jimma21–24. Stillbirth is a significant adverse birth outcome that affects over 900,000 babies in Sub-Saharan Africa, with Ethiopia having one of the highest rates, at 12 per 1,000 pregnancies, according to the 2019 EDHS. Fetal death occurs after 28 weeks of gestation, either during pregnancy or during labor and delivery19,25.

This study aimed to assess the effect of antenatal depression on adverse birth outcomes, which was previously explored in only one study in northern Ethiopia, and more evidence is needed to strengthen the evidence base. Additionally, low APGAR scores were included in this research’s assessment of adverse birth outcomes13. This study is significant as it focused on the effect of antenatal depression on newborn adverse birth outcomes. The outcome of our findings will provide circumstantial evidence to improve maternal and newborn health by promoting early identification and management of depression during antenatal follow-up. Furthermore, the findings can inform clinical practice, guide public health policies, and support the development of targeted interventions in resource-constrained countries like Ethiopia, ultimately reducing both short- and long-term health risks for mothers and their newborns. However, to the best of the investigator’s knowledge, no studies have been conducted on the effect of antenatal depression on adverse birth outcomes among pregnant women, particularly in Southwest Ethiopia. As a result, this study aimed to assess the effect of antenatal depression on adverse birth outcomes among pregnant women in Jimma University Medical Center, Southwest Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study setting, period, and design

A facility-based prospective cohort study was conducted at JUMC from May to September 2023. JUMC, located in Jimma town, Oromia Regional State, southwest Ethiopia, is approximately 352 km from the capital, Addis Ababa. As a teaching and referral hospital with over 1,000 beds, it serves a catchment population of 15–20 million people. Annually, the hospital manages around 15,000 inpatients, 160,000 outpatients, and 11,000 emergency cases26. The ANC ward serves approximately 9,000 women each year, with about 4,500 attending for labor and delivery27.

Population

The source populations were all pregnant women attending antenatal care at JUMC, while the study populations were all third-trimester pregnant women attending antenatal care during the study period.

Eligibility criteria

All third-trimester pregnant women attending antenatal care at JUMC, Southwest Ethiopia, during the study period were included in the study. Pregnant women who were severely ill or in a coma were excluded from responding to the question, and pregnant women who came by referral from other health facilities for ANC follow-up were excluded from the study.

A sample size of 237 was computed, and an additional 10% for expected losses to follow up increased the final sample size to 261. The 261 third-trimester pregnant women at ANC were screened for depression by using EPDS through a convenience sampling method. After screening, the proportion of women with and without antenatal depression was 1:4. So, the women who were exposed and unexposed to antenatal depression were 52 and 209, respectively.

Data collection tool and procedures

Data was collected twice; at the first data collection, screening was done for antenatal depression by using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). The tool is a standard and validated for evaluating antenatal depression. Further, it was modified and approved by a field of experts for usage in the Ethiopian context29,30. The pregnant women’s scores of EPDS, 12 and above out of 30, indicate positive for antenatal depression31. It asks about every symptom of depression throughout the present pregnancy (through the first and second trimester), looking backward. Then, the screened women were followed. The screening process included all third-trimester women attending antenatal care follow-up32 of 261 pregnant women. Then, the Actual data were collected through face-to-face interviews, and the screened women were grouped into women with depression and without depression. So, women exposed and unexposed to antenatal depression were 52:209, respectively. Reliability was tested and checked by the Cronbach alpha value of 0.87. All factors/independent variables were collected at the first contact, through face-to-face interviews. The tool has three parts: the first part is socio-demographic, which has six items, the second part is Obstetric factors, which has nine items, and the third part is chronic medical illness and misuse of substances, which has six items, for a total of 21 items used12,13.

Exposure to tobacco was identified by asking, “Have you been smoking since your current pregnancy, or has there been someone who smokes around you in your home or your workplace?” If the answer was yes to one of the two questions, the woman was considered to have had tobacco exposure33. Obstetrics-related complications, like pregnancy or labor-related complications, were recorded by asking the women, “Do you have any pregnancy-related complications such as pregnancy-induced hypertension, Antenatal hemorrhage, or Premature rupture of membranes?” and the response was yes or no. And labor-related complications were recorded from reviewing their Medical record number if at least one of prolonged labor, obstructed labor, malpresentation, or malposition was recorded12. Chronic medical history was identified as at least having one of hypertension, Diabetes mellitus, known heart diseases, or others; by asking, “Have you been diagnosed with any known chronic medical illness?” and their response was recorded as yes or no12.

Pregnant women were asked whether they participated in moderate-intensity physical activity such as brisk walking, dancing, gardening, or routine housework for two to three hours per week. Their responses were recorded as ‘yes’ for those who were physically active and ‘no’ for those who did not exercise34.

Alcohol use was assessed using the alcohol use disorders identification test-consumption (AUDIT-C), a validated three-item screening tool adapted for pregnancy and contextualized to the study setting. The AUDIT-C evaluates the frequency and quantity of alcohol intake, generating a total score ranging from 0 to 12. Consistent with previous studies, scores were categorized into low1–3, moderate4,5, and high (≥ 6) risk levels; in this study, women scoring ≥ 3 were classified as alcohol users35.

The data were collected by two bachelor’s degrees in Midwifery, and they were unemployed, giving free service at different health institutions, and they had experience in data collection. The master’s degree holder in maternity health nursing was supervised. The period of follow-up was from May 1 to September 20, 2023. To minimize loss to follow-up and ensure timely tracking of participants, the following procedures were implemented1: flexible ANC appointment schedules were arranged to accommodate participants’ availability2; rapport and trust were built through culturally appropriate and respectful communication during each visit3; follow-up reminders and appointment confirmations were made through regular phone calls; and4 reasons for missed visits or withdrawal were carefully documented in the tracking log. Then, the second data collection took place by reviewing their medical records at JUMC. The primary outcome was newborn adverse birth outcomes that were identified by the occurrence of at least one of the four conditions of preterm birth, low Apgar score, Low birth weight, or stillbirth, which were collected from the women’s medical records.

Newborn adverse birth outcomes.

Independent variables

history of chronic medical illness, chat chewing, drinking coffee, alcohol use, and exposure to tobacco.

Exposed to antenatal depression and not exposed to antenatal depression and physical activity during pregnancy.

Operational definition and measurements

Antenatal depression

is depression that develops during pregnancy, characterized by persistent low mood, loss of interest, and emotional or physical symptoms. It was measured according to the EPDS scale, pregnant women who scored 12 and above indicate antenatal depression31,36.

Birth outcome It is a newborn birth outcome that results in either a normal or an adverse birth outcome. A normal outcome is defined as a live, term birth (between 37 and 42 weeks) with an appropriate birth weight (2,500–4,000 g) and an Apgar score above 7 within the first five minutes16.

Newborn adverse birth outcomes

refer to negative health conditions that occur before, during, or after birth, affecting the baby’s health, survival, or development. In this study, newborn adverse birth outcomes were measured by the presence of one or more conditions such as preterm birth, low birth weight, stillbirth, or low Apgar scores13.

Preterm birth

This is when a baby is born before 37 weeks of gestational age37.

Low birth weight

A newborn weighing 2,500 g or less at birth, or when measured within the first hour after birth, regardless of gestational age, is referred to as low birth weight (LBW)22.

Low APGAR scores When the measurement of APGAR is less than 7 at five minutes is considered low, indicating a poor chance of a healthy birth38,39.

Stillbirth

refers to the death of a fetus after 28 weeks of gestation. The fetus may die inside the uterus before labor begins, or it may die during labor and delivery25.

Chronic medical illness Women who have one or more chronic illnesses such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart disease, or other conditions lasting at least one year12.

Data quality control

The quality of data was ensured by providing training for the data collectors and the supervisor. The tool was translated into the local language and back-translated into English by a language expert. The EPDS tool was examined by psychiatrists. Every day after data collection, questionnaires were reviewed and checked for completeness, accuracy, and clarity by the supervisor and principal investigator, and the necessary feedback was offered to data collectors the next morning. The pretest was done for reliability and checked by a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.87. Participants’ permission was asked, and informed consent was taken to precede the data collection process.

Statistical analysis

The data was entered into EpiData version 4.6 and checked for completeness, clarity, and coding. Then the data is exported to the STATA 17 version for analysis. Descriptive statistical analysis was computed to summarize and describe the characteristics of study participants. Bivariate analysis was conducted to identify the differences in the proportion of women with normal birth outcomes and adverse birth outcomes and their risk factors. For the multivariate analysis, the dependent variable was the newborn birth outcome, and the independent factors were. As the GLM Log-binomial analysis is the appropriate model to estimate the incidence of rare outcomes and report relative risk, it was used to identify the predictors of newborn adverse birth outcomes. The variables that yielded a P-value of < 0.25 in bivariable log-binomial analysis were candidates for multivariable analysis, and potential confounders were controlled. From the multivariable analysis, the determinants with a p-value of < 0.05 were reported as predictors of newborn adverse birth outcomes. Overall model fitness was assessed using several diagnostic procedures. The investigators examined observed versus predicted probabilities across quantiles of predicted risk to evaluate goodness-of-fit. Residual diagnostics, including Pearson and deviance residuals, were inspected to identify potential outliers and systematic patterns. Furthermore, overdispersion was assessed using the residual deviance-to-degrees-of-freedom ratio, and no convergence issues were encountered. The estimated Crude relative risk (CRR) and adjusted relative risk (ARR) with a 95% CI were used to estimate the strength of the association between explanatory and response variables. Multi-collinearity was checked, and no collinearity among predictors. A variance inflation factor (VIF) of > 10 or a standard error of > 2 was considered suggestive of multicollinearity. For all independent variables, the multicollinearity effect was checked by co-linearity diagnostic statistics via variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance test with a maximum value of 1.40 and a minimum value of 82.6%, respectively. The incidence of newborn adverse birth outcomes was reported by their proportion. Then, the finding was presented using text, tables, and figures.

Results

In this study, a total of 261 pregnant women were enrolled. Six participants (2.3%) were lost to follow-up and thus excluded from the analysis. The characteristics of participants lost to follow-up (n = 6) were generally comparable to those who completed the study in terms of age, parity, and baseline antenatal depression status. Consequently, data from 255 participants were analyzed, of whom 51 were exposed and 204 were unexposed to antenatal depression. The overall response rate was 97.7%.

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

The mean (± SD) age of the participants was 27.4 ± 4.35 years, and no age difference was observed between women with and without adverse birth outcomes. A total of 180 participants (76.3%) were Muslim. Most participants, 243 (95.2%), were married. Additionally, 113 participants (44.3%) had no formal education. The mean monthly income of the participants was 2,557.12 Ethiopian birr. There were no differences in religion, occupation, educational status, or marital status between women with and without adverse birth outcomes (p-values > 0.05) (Table 1).

Chronic medical illnesses and substance use of the study participants

In this study, 251 (98.4%) participants were not exposed to tobacco, and 162 (63.5%) had no history of khat chewing. A total of 241 (94.5%) participants reported drinking coffee. Among the women, 96 (37.6%) had a history of chronic medical illness, of whom 34 (13.3%) had hypertension, 3(1.17%) had diabetes mellitus, and 59 (23.1%) had other chronic medical conditions. Among participants with chronic medical illness, 64 (25.1%) were receiving treatment. All participants reported no alcohol consumption during pregnancy (0%); therefore, alcohol use was not analyzed further in relation to adverse birth outcomes. Compared to those without a history of chronic medical illness, participants with chronic medical conditions were significantly more likely to experience adverse birth outcomes (p-value < 0.001) (Table 3).

Antenatal depression and incidence of newborn adverse birth outcomes

Among the 255 participants, 20% of women were exposed to antenatal depression. The cumulative incidence of adverse birth outcomes was 27 (53%) among women with antenatal depression and 59 (29%) among those without antenatal depression, with a ratio of approximately 1:4 between exposed and unexposed women (Fig. 1).

In these findings, there was a significant difference in the proportion of preterm births among participants with and without depression (P-value < 0.001). The incidence of preterm birth among participants with and without antenatal depression was 12 (23.5%) and 17 (8.3%), respectively.

The incidence of low birth weight (LBW) was significantly different between participants with and without antenatal depression, being 5 (9.8%) and 13 (6.3%), respectively. The incidence of low Apgar scores among participants with antenatal depression was 6 (11.7%), and among those without depression was 20 (9.8%). Risk factors of cumulative newborn adverse birth outcomes

The significant predictors of newborn adverse birth outcomes in generalized linear model (GLM) log-binomial analysis were physical exercise, chronic medical illness, exposure to tobacco, labor status, and antenatal depression. The adjusted relative risk of developing newborn adverse birth outcomes was 1.54 (95% CI: 1.22, 1.94) times more likely in the women who were not physically active. The risk of developing newborn adverse birth outcomes was 2.64 (95% CI: 1.90, 3.67) times more likely in the women exposed to tobacco. The adjusted relative risk of developing newborn adverse birth outcomes was 2.14 (95% CI: 1.68, 2.72) times more likely among participants with chronic medical illness. The adjusted relative risk of developing newborn adverse birth outcomes was 1.47 (95% CI: 1.17, 1.85) times more likely among participants with induced labor. In this study, women exposed to antenatal depression had a 1.58-fold higher risk of experiencing adverse birth outcomes compared with women who were not exposed to antenatal depression (ARR = 1.58; 95% CI: 1.26, 1.99) (Table 4).

Discussion

The effects of mental health problems were well identified in developed countries40. Even though the prevalence of mental health problems is high in developing countries13, their impact on birth outcomes remains poorly understood. Few studies have attempted to examine the relationship between maternal mental health and adverse birth outcomes. However, there is still no more evidence-based research in our country. Thus, this study assessed the incidence and the risk of newborn adverse birth outcomes among women exposed and unexposed to depression during pregnancy at JUMC.

The third trimester of pregnancy was much riskier for the development of depression compared to the first and second trimesters41. The proportion of depression in this study was 20%, which is higher than findings from other studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries, including a study in Ibadan, Nigeria, where the proportion of depression during pregnancy was lower than in the present study13. The variation may be due to socio-cultural and economic differences, or it may be related to the increased cost of living in the country resulting from instability and expanded conflicts, which have led to poor access to antenatal care services42,43. The evidence, high levels of the stress hormone cortisol are produced during pregnancy, especially during the second and third trimesters, by depressed women, which may have negative effects on fetal development and result in adverse birth outcomes44.

In this study, the cumulative incidence of adverse birth outcomes was 53% and 29% among participants with and without antenatal depression, respectively. This finding is a lower incidence compared to the previous study conducted in Gondar town36 and Bale Zone hospitals12. The variation may be due to a relatively small sample size, the difference in health care facilities, socio-cultural variation, and the study area, as the women who haven’t had antenatal care visits were more vulnerable to adverse birth outcomes28.

In the present study, the significant predictors of cumulative newborn adverse birth outcomes were physical activity, chronic medical illness, exposure to tobacco, labor status, and antenatal depression. The adjusted relative risk of developing newborn adverse birth outcomes was 1.54 (p = 0.00) more likely in the women who weren’t physically active. Physical activity during pregnancy is beneficial and can reduce the risk of adverse birth outcomes. Evidence from observational studies and clinical guidelines indicates that appropriate physical activity has protective effects, whereas physical inactivity increases the risk of various adverse birth outcomes45,46. Furthermore, the risk of developing newborn adverse birth outcomes was 2.64 (p = 0.00) times more likely in the women exposed to tobacco. Evidence shows that tobacco use during pregnancy is associated with adverse birth outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight, stillbirth, and long-term child outcomes47,48. The estimated incidence of preterm birth among women with and without antenatal depression in this study was 23.5% and 8.3%, respectively, which was lower than the study findings reported in Kenya49 and Gondar town28. However, higher than the incidence reported in Nigeria13. The difference may be due to the difference in sample size, study period, and socio-cultural background of the study participants.

In this study, the incidence of LBW among participants exposed to antenatal depression was 9.8%, while it was 6.3% among those unexposed. These figures are relatively higher than the incidences reported in Gondar town28. Additionally, the risk of LBW among women with depression was higher than the results reported in Gondar town28, Nigeria13. The deviation may be due to differences in the study period, setting, and sample size. However, similar to the study done in other parts of our country, shows the relative risk of developing newborn adverse birth outcomes was 2.14 times more likely at (p-value = 0.00) in the participants with chronic medical illness. The adjusted relative risk of developing newborn adverse birth outcomes was 1.47 times more likely among participants with induced labor (p-value = 0.001). The finding indicated that mothers who gave birth through induced labor were associated with adverse birth outcomes, particularly low APGAR scores50.

In this study, the adjusted relative risk of developing newborn adverse birth outcomes was 1.58 times more likely in the women exposed to antenatal depression (p-value = 0.00). Antenatal depression wasn’t a significant predictor of LBW; this may be due to the small sample size used to explore the risks. In this study, the incidence of stillbirth among participants exposed and unexposed to antenatal depression was 8% and 4.6%, respectively, which was higher than the finding reported in Kenya49. The variation may be due to differences in socio-cultural and economic factors, a lack of trained health professionals, and a lack of attention given to antenatal depression.

Implications for practice

The high incidence of adverse newborn outcomes indicates an effect on maternal and child health. The healthcare system should strengthen health education during antenatal care, as this can help women understand how antenatal depression leads to the risk of adverse birth outcomes. Early screening and counseling for antenatal depression should be incorporated into standard maternal healthcare services, like nutritional counseling, family planning, and breastfeeding support. The findings of this study showed that health authorities and policymakers should strengthen the national standards and training programs for healthcare personnel, focusing on early identification and management of antenatal depression and other risk factors to reduce the incidence of adverse newborn outcomes. At the community level, awareness initiatives should strengthen health extension workers to provide education on the importance of knowing early symptoms of depression and reducing cultural barriers to timely antenatal care. The incorporation of antenatal depression screening and management into maternal health policies and routine practices will help improve newborn outcomes and prevent further maternal and child health complications and morbidities.

Limitations of the study

This study used a convenience sampling method, which may limit generalizability. Additionally, the relatively small sample size may lead to either overestimation or underestimation of the incidence of adverse birth outcomes in newborns. Even though we have tried to incorporate all factors, some potential confounders, including nutritional status, BMI, anemia, intimate partner violence, social support, stress level, and antidepressant use, were not measured, which may introduce residual confounding. Some factors in this study were based on self-reported data, which may be influenced by recall bias and social desirability bias, as women might provide more socially acceptable responses. Although efforts were made to minimize these limitations, they should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

The incidence of adverse birth outcomes among women with and without depression at Jimma University Medical Center was 53% and 29%, respectively. Physically inactive women, those exposed to tobacco, those with chronic medical illness, those who underwent induced labor, and particularly those with antenatal depression showed statistically significant associations with newborn adverse birth outcomes. Thus, to minimize the incidence of adverse birth outcomes, early anticipation, screening, and management of antenatal depression are recommended during antenatal care follow-up. Furthermore, depression during pregnancy requires the attention of healthcare providers to identify and provide holistic care that includes mental health care to reduce the incidence of adverse birth outcomes related to antenatal depression.

Recommendations

The following recommendations were given based on the findings to the concerned bodies: Health care providers are encouraged to consider minimizing the incidence of newborn adverse birth outcomes by early anticipation, screening, and management of antenatal depression during antenatal care follow-up, and women who are diagnosed with antenatal depression after screening should be linked to a psychiatrist for further management. Health institutions are encouraged to consider incorporating antenatal depression screening tools into antenatal care services, based on existing literature supporting their usefulness. For researchers, it is encouraged to conduct research on the effects of antenatal depression on birth outcomes at the community level, with an ideal sample size for further investigation, and to identify other risk factors, particularly seasonally varying, of adverse birth outcomes.
