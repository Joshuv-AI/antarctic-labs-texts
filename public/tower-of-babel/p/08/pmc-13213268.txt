The Role of YouTube as an Information Platform for Ovarian Cancer: the Educational Quality and Reliability
Authors: Soo Young Jeong, Jiye Kim, Jisong Hwang, Sung Taek Park, Sung Ho Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeOvarian cancer is the eighth most common malignancy among women worldwide and remains associated with poor survival. Patients and caregivers often experience unmet informational needs and turn to online resources such as YouTube; however, the quality and reliability of ovarian cancer-related content have not been evaluated.Materials and MethodsOn October 4, 2022, the 100 most-viewed YouTube videos retrieved using the keyword “ovarian cancer” were screened, and 86 English-language videos were included. Data collected included uploader type, views, length, likes, dislikes, comments, and content category. Educational quality and informational reliability were assessed using the Global Quality Score (GQS) and modified DISCERN criteria. Three obstetrics and gynecology residents independently rated all videos. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests, and inter-rater reliability was assessed by intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).ResultsOf the 86 videos, 56.98% were uploaded by medical sources and 43.02% by non-medical sources. Informational content comprised 49%, followed by patient experience (43%). The videos collectively accumulated over 16 million views. Non-medical videos were longer and received more likes and comments, with a higher like ratio. The mean GQS was 3.5±1.1 (moderate quality), and the mean modified DISCERN was 2.2±1.1 (low reliability). Modified DISCERN scores were higher in medical than non-medical videos (p=0.001). Inter-rater agreement was excellent (ICC>0.9).ConclusionYouTube provides broad access to ovarian cancer-related information, but a substantial gap exists between educational quality and informational reliability. Professional involvement and platform-level strategies may improve evidence attribution and the educational value of popular videos.

INTRODUCTION

Ovarian cancer is the eighth most common malignancy among women worldwide, ranking high in both incidence and mortality.1 Although global incidence rates have slightly declined in recent decades, survival outcomes remain poor, with a 5-year survival rate of only 49%. In the United States, 19880 new diagnoses and 12810 deaths were estimated in 2022.2 These figures underscore the substantial disease burden and highlight the urgent need for improved strategies to support patients and their families.

Beyond medical treatment, unmet supportive care needs remain significant in oncology. A review of advanced cancer patients and their informal caregivers reported that patients required psychological, physical, and informational support, while caregivers most frequently identified a lack of information—particularly concerning disease processes, treatment options, and caregiving responsibilities.3 In ovarian cancer, newly diagnosed patients often experience considerable anxiety and uncertainty due to limited understanding of their condition. Reliable and comprehensible information is therefore critical to empower patients and caregivers, reduce distress, and promote shared decision-making.4

Despite this recognized need, barriers persist in clinical practice. Time constraints during outpatient visits and the scarcity of standardized educational materials frequently limit the amount of information that can be delivered. Consequently, many patients turn to online platforms to obtain supplementary information. Previous research has emphasized the importance of complementing clinical encounters with alternative modes of communication. For instance, Fitch and Steele5 proposed the use of computer- and video-based interventions to enhance information delivery and provide patients with accessible resources beyond the clinical setting.

Among the available platforms, YouTube has emerged as the most widely used video-sharing service worldwide. With over 2 billion unique monthly users and more than 1 billion hours of content viewed daily, it provides unparalleled opportunities for disseminating health-related information to diverse populations.6 However, the open-access nature of YouTube raises concerns regarding the accuracy, quality, and reliability of the information presented. Misinformation or low-quality educational content may not only fail to meet patients’ needs but also make it easier to increase confusion and distress. This underscores the necessity of systematically evaluating YouTube as a potential source of medical information.

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the educational quality and informational reliability of YouTube videos related to ovarian cancer. It seeks to clarify whether this platform can serve as a credible supplementary resource for patient education, thereby contributing to efforts to improve supportive care and health literacy in ovarian cancer patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Video selection and data collection

A search of YouTube was performed on October 4, 2022, using the keyword “ovarian cancer.” The search was conducted in incognito mode, with cookies cleared and without logging into a user account. The search was performed from the Republic of Korea. After sorting search results by view count, videos that had enabled comments and visible like/dislike counts were considered eligible, and the 100 most-viewed eligible videos were selected. Subsequently, 14 non-English videos were excluded, leaving 86 eligible videos for final analysis (Fig. 1). For each video, the following parameters were collected: number of subscribers of the uploader, total views, days since initial upload, video length, number of likes, number of dislikes, and number of comments.

Video classification

Videos were categorized according to uploader and content. Uploaders were classified as medical (hospital channels, doctors, and health-related channels) or non-medical (patients, news, magazines, talk shows, commercials, and other unspecified sources). Content categories included: 1) disease information related to ovarian cancer, 2) patient experiences, 3) surgical techniques, 4) advertisements, and 5) unclassified.

Indices and scoring systems

The like ratio was defined as the number of likes divided by the sum of likes and dislikes, multiplied by 100. The view ratio was defined as the number of views per day since upload. A video power index was then derived by multiplying the like ratio by the view ratio and dividing the product by 100.7 Educational quality was assessed using the 5-point Global Quality Score (GQS), in which a score of 1 indicates poor quality and a score of 5 indicates excellent quality (Table 1). Informational reliability was assessed using modified DISCERN criteria, consisting of five items scored as 0 (no) or 1 (yes), with higher scores indicating greater reliability (Table 2).8

Evaluation process

Three second-year residents in obstetrics and gynecology independently evaluated all 86 videos using both the GQS and modified DISCERN criteria. The mean score across the three evaluators was used for subsequent analyses.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For each variable, the median values and ranges were calculated. Comparisons between medical and non-medical uploaders were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, while comparisons across video content categories were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. These analyses were intended to be exploratory and descriptive, aiming to identify general patterns in video characteristics, educational quality, and informational reliability. Accordingly, p-values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Inter-rater reliability for the GQS and modified DISCERN scores was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

RESULTS

Video characteristics

Among the 86 videos analyzed, 49 (56.98%) were uploaded by medical sources and 37 (43.02%) by non-medical sources. The largest proportion of uploaders were health channels and news/magazine channels, each accounting for 23.26%, followed by hospital channels (22.09%). By content, informational videos comprised 48.84% (n=42), followed by patient experience videos (43.02%, n=37). Collectively, more than 90% of the videos were categorized as informational or patient experience videos (Table 3). Informational content accounted for 61.22% of medical uploaders, whereas patient experience content accounted for 64.86% of non-medical uploaders, indicating distinct content tendencies across uploader groups.

The 86 videos accumulated a total of 16240535 views (median 52018; range 21361–3456074) and 13433 comments (median 30; range 0–2042). The single most-viewed video (3456074 views), uploaded by a health channel, addressed risk factors, symptoms, diagnosis, and treatment of ovarian cancer, and received the highest numbers of likes (27593) and dislikes (1333).

Comparison by uploader

Non-medical videos were significantly longer in duration (median 474 seconds vs. 225 seconds, p=0.012), received significantly more likes (median 768 vs. 283, p=0.001), and had a higher number of comments (median 41 vs. 20, p=0.046). The like ratio was also significantly higher in non-medical videos (98.06 vs. 96.07, p=0.017). No significant differences were observed in subscribers, view counts, dislike counts, or view ratios (Table 4).

Comparison by content

For content-based comparisons, analyses were restricted to the major content categories—informational videos and patient experience videos—because other categories had extremely small sample sizes. Between these two content types, no significant differences were observed in view counts or comments. However, the like ratio was significantly higher in patient experience compared with informational videos (median 97.63% vs. 96.53%, p=0.015). Other indices, including view ratio and video power index, did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table 5).

Reliability and quality assessment

Inter-rater reliability among the three reviewers was excellent, with an ICC of 0.923 [95% confidence interval (CI), 0.889–0.947; p<0.001] for GQS and 0.990 (95% CI, 0.985–0.993; p<0.001) for modified DISCERN scores (Table 6). The mean GQS across all videos was 3.50±1.06, corresponding to moderate-to-good quality. The mean modified DISCERN score was 2.24±1.12, indicating relatively low informational reliability. Most videos scored highly for clarity of aims (mean 0.97), but much lower for reliable sources (0.47), listing of additional sources (0.19), and mention of uncertainties (0.05).

Group comparisons

The modified DISCERN score was significantly higher in the medical group than in the non-medical group (median 3.00 vs. 1.00, p=0.001), whereas no significant difference in GQS was observed between the two groups (Table 7). Content-based comparisons were limited to informational and patient experience videos, and informational videos achieved significantly higher GQS than patient experience videos (median 4.50 vs. 3.00, p<0.001). Modified DISCERN scores were also significantly higher in informational videos compared with patient experience videos (median 3.00 vs. 1.00, p<0.001) (Table 8).

DISCUSSION

This study systematically evaluated the educational quality and informational reliability of YouTube videos related to ovarian cancer using the GQS and modified DISCERN criteria. Overall quality was moderate-to-good, whereas informational reliability was comparatively low. These findings align with prior analyses of cancer-related content on YouTube, which have reported variable educational quality and frequent deficits in source attribution and evidence citation across disease areas.9101112131415

Uploader type and content category were strongly associated with performance on the reliability metric. Videos from medical sources achieved higher modified DISCERN scores than those from non-medical sources, consistent with the expectation that professionally produced content more often references guideline-based or peer-reviewed information. In contrast, non-medical videos tended to be longer and achieved greater engagement (likes and comments), echoing prior observations that narrative-driven media may attract attention without necessarily improving factual accuracy.91112 Engagement metrics on YouTube do not directly reflect informational quality, as they are influenced by factors such as narrative appeal and uploader popularity. Accordingly, we focused on descriptive group-level patterns rather than correlation analyses between engagement and quality scores.

A notable observation in this study was the popularity of patient-experience videos featuring public figures (e.g., actors, athletes, journalists). Celebrity disclosures are known to shape health information–seeking behavior and screening interest—the so-called “Angelina Jolie effect” in breast cancer is a well-described example.14 Such narratives may provide psychosocial validation and foster community support for patients and caregivers; however, they can also transmit anecdotal recommendations that are not evidence-based. Despite these concerns, lower reliability scores do not necessarily indicate a lack of patient value. Given the absence of effective population-based screening and the high likelihood of advanced-stage diagnosis and recurrence, video content with experiential or narrative elements may offer emotional reassurance, thereby complementing clinic-based education even when formal evidence citation is limited. Therefore, these findings suggest that professional engagement in the creation of educational content may enhance the dissemination of accurate, guideline-concordant information, particularly when videos are supplemented with references to established patient guidelines and clinical trial resources.

Conversely, videos addressing recent therapeutic advances (e.g., immunotherapy, novel fluorescent markers, hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy) demonstrated high scores on both GQS and modified DISCERN scores in our analysis. This pattern supports the premise that professionally curated videos—particularly those produced by academic centers, hospitals, or specialty organizations—can effectively disseminate up-to-date, evidence-based information when structured with clear aims, balanced benefits/harms, and explicit citations.9101112 Surgical procedure videos (robotic, laparoscopic, or open hysterectomy) appeared more clinician-oriented than patient-oriented, underscoring the need to tailor content to its intended audience and to clearly label target users.

Domain-level weaknesses were concentrated in specific DISCERN items: reliable sources, additional resources, and acknowledgement of uncertainty were seldom provided. Similar gaps have been reported across diverse medical topics on YouTube, including spine disorders, rheumatoid arthritis, and the human gut microbiome.67816 Addressing these deficits is feasible with lightweight, standards-based practices for health video production. At minimum, creators should: 1) articulate explicit aims; 2) cite up-to-date, authoritative sources (e.g., NCCN/ESMO guidelines, peer-reviewed studies); 3) present balanced information on benefits, risks, and alternatives; 4) provide links to additional high-quality resources; and 5) identify uncertainties or areas with limited evidence. Adopting such a checklist would directly improve performance on the modified DISCERN domains and enhance trustworthiness.

The implications of these findings extend beyond individual content creators. Platform-level interventions, such as incorporating health-context labels, verified institutional attribution, and standardized reference fields, could improve transparency and reliability. In addition, recommendation algorithms that integrate evidence provenance alongside user engagement metrics may further enhance the visibility of high-quality content. Collaboration with professional societies could enable the development of curated playlists addressing common patient concerns, including diagnosis, staging, treatment options, and survivorship. Furthermore, the use of analytic tools to iteratively refine clarity and accessibility would support continuous improvement. Collectively, these strategies align with previous recommendations to ensure that the broad digital reach of online platforms is matched by improvements in the informational quality of patient education resources.111213

This study has limitations. First, the analysis was limited to English-language YouTube videos and to the most-viewed content identified at a single time point; temporal drift in rankings and metadata is possible. Second, the search strategy was restricted to a single disease-specific keyword (“ovarian cancer”). This strategy was chosen to prioritize relevance and specificity; however, it may have excluded videos with alternative search terms such as tumor or mass. Future studies using multi-keyword strategies may better explore the wider spectrum of patient search behaviors. Third, video quality and informational reliability were evaluated using the GQS and modified DISCERN criteria applied from a clinician-oriented perspective. Although these instruments are validated and widely used to assess transparency, structure, and reliability of health information, they do not capture patient-perceived usefulness, emotional support, or understandability. Consequently, the real-world educational impact of these videos from the patient perspective could not be directly assessed. Finally, although inter-rater reliability among evaluators was excellent, assessments were conducted by obstetrics and gynecology residents rather than subspecialty experts or patients. While residents possess foundational clinical knowledge relevant to ovarian cancer education, their level of experience may differ from that of board-certified specialists. Future research incorporating multidisciplinary expert panels and patient-reported evaluations may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the educational value of online video content.

Future studies should incorporate multilingual sampling, longitudinal surveillance, and mixed-methods designs that link video characteristics to patient-centered outcomes (knowledge gain, decisional conflict, anxiety) and behavioral endpoints (consultation preparedness, adherence). Randomized studies comparing professionally produced videos with narrative content could clarify causal effects on learning and engagement.

In summary, YouTube provides substantial reach for disseminating information on ovarian cancer; however, a gap remains between content quality and reliability. Greater professional involvement and the application of standardized production practices, together with platform-level measures to enhance source transparency, may contribute to improving the overall quality of content while maintaining accessibility. Until such measures are widely implemented, it remains important for healthcare providers to guide patients toward credible, evidence-based video resources and to emphasize the need for critical appraisal when utilizing open-access platforms for health information.
