Spinal mobility and functional limitations in patients with diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis compared to healthy controls and patients with axial spondyloarthritis
Authors: David Kiefer, Benedikt Eisenbach, Imke Redeker, Uta Kiltz, Philipp Sewerin, Anna Kernder, Ioana Andreica, Johanna Mucke, Diana Vossen, Xenofon Baraliakos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) is characterised by ossification of the spine including the anterior longitudinal ligament. While often still considered a pure radiographic finding, recent evidence suggests relevant impairments in physical functioning.ObjectiveTo evaluate spinal mobility and functional limitations in DISH compared with radiographic (r-) and non-radiographic (nr-) axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) and healthy controls (HCs).MethodsConsecutive patients and HCs were assessed regarding mobility, including the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Metrology Index (BASMI), Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), Ankylosing Spondylitis Performance-based Index (ASPI), electronic measures of measuring range of motion (ROM) and range of kinematics (ROK) with the Epionics SPINE.ResultsWe examined 50 patients with DISH, 30 patients with axSpA and 20 HCs. Compared with HC, patients with DISH showed significantly worse BASMI (4.6±1.0 vs 2.8±0.6), SPPB (10.2±2.0 vs 11.9±0.4), ASPI (46.5±26.1 s vs 20.3±5.3) and broad restrictions across ROM and ROK measures (all p<0.001). Compared with axSpA, BASMI (4.6±1 vs 5.1±1.9; p=0.13), SPPB (10.2±2.0 vs 9.5±1.3; p=0.08) and ASPI (46.5±26.1 vs 47.4±23.9; p=0.91) were comparable, but profiles differed: patients with DISH performed better in rotation to both sides and hip-related measures. Age-adjusted analyses confirmed similar group differences independent of age.ConclusionDISH is associated with substantial impairments in spinal mobility and physical performance compared with HC. While limitations were comparable to axSpA, the restriction pattern differed, with DISH showing less deficits in rotational movement. These findings highlight that DISH is not merely a radiographic condition but patients experience significant impairments of mobility, similar to axSpA.

Introduction

Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) is a frequent disease characterised by new bone formation and flowing ossifications along the anterolateral aspects of the spine. The disorder was first clinically described by Forestier et al who differentiated spinal hyperostosis from ankylosing spondylitis based on its radiographic and clinical features.1 In the 1970s, Resnick and Niwayama systematically defined the morphologic features of DISH and established classification criteria that are still used today.2 These criteria require radiographic bridging of at least four contiguous vertebrae with preservation of the sacroiliac joints. Despite being a relatively frequent entity, the pathophysiology and clinical impact of DISH have only partially been clarified. The prevalence increases with age, particularly beyond 60 years, and men are more frequently affected than women.35 Epidemiological studies consistently demonstrate associations with obesity and components of the metabolic syndrome.69 The distribution of new bone formation shows that the thoracic vertebrae are most commonly and severely affected, followed by cervical and lumbar regions.4 10 11 Sacroiliac changes may occur but differ morphologically from the erosive inflammatory sacroiliitis of axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA).1214

Although originally described as a radiographic condition, the disease burden of DISH has become increasingly recognised. Patients frequently report axial stiffness and limitation of spinal mobility.15 The impact of DISH on physical function, mobility and physical performance has not been consistently demonstrated. Early clinical observations documented reduced cervical rotation and restricted thoracic movements compared with controls.15 It has also been demonstrated that DISH was associated with impairment in both objective and self-reported physical function in men and women; men with DISH had significantly more difficulty performing the chair stand test without arm support.16 More recent Japanese studies confirmed reduced lumbar range of motion in DISH measured with electronic devices, though some domains of physical function such as walking speed or grip strength were not consistently impaired.17 In addition, no significant differences in gait speed and chair stands between subjects with and without DISH or in passive hip joint motion compared with controls were found.12 18 In contrast, it has been reported that patients with DISH and back pain had significantly reduced chest expansion and increased occiput-to-wall distance.19 Furthermore, a high prevalence of DISH among patients with hypopnea-hypoventilation syndrome has been found, linking thoracic ossification to respiratory impairment.20

Like DISH, axSpA is a chronic inflammatory disease driven by entheseal inflammation and new bone formation. It leads to pain, functional impairment and progressive reduction of spinal mobility.21 22 Physical functioning is routinely assessed using the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI), spinal mobility is mostly assessed using the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Metrology Index (BASMI) and supplemented by performance-based tests such as the Ankylosing Spondylitis Performance-based Index (ASPI).2325

Novel electronic systems such as Epionics SPINE (ES) have proven to be objective, reproducible methods on range of motion (ROM; movement range) and range of kinematics (ROK; speed of movements) and have been validated in patients with degenerative spine disease, healthy people and recently in axSpA cohorts.2628 These electronic measurements showed that patients with axSpA have marked impairments in spinal mobility and movement velocity, which are closely linked to the extent of structural damage in the axial skeleton.2931

The aim of this study is to investigate measures of spinal mobility and physical performance in patients with DISH and compare the results to healthy controls (HCs), and patients with axSpA.

Methods

In this single centre observational, cross-sectional study with two comparator groups, patients with confirmed DISH according to the Resnick criteria were recruited consecutively. HCs were recruited as community volunteers from the local population through informal advertisement and word-of-mouth. All HCs reported no musculoskeletal symptoms, no history of rheumatic disease and no functional limitations affecting performance-based testing. To ensure the absence of relevant functional impairment in HC, the self-perceived spinal mobility deficit (0–10 scale) was required to be ≤2. Patients with a clinical diagnosis of axSpA were identified from a previously published cohort. For this analysis, an age-restricted sample comparable to the DISH group was used. The classification of radiographic (r-) and non-radiographic (nr-) axial spondyloarthritis was based on the modified New York criteria.30 32

Main exclusions for all participants were prior spinal surgery, body weight >120 kg, as excessive body weight may affect performance-based test outcomes, pregnancy or any condition that precluded standardised performance testing.

Baseline demographics, routine clinical data and patient-reported outcomes including questions about overall pain, spinal pain, nocturnal spinal pain, morning stiffness (Numeric Rating Scale 0–10) as well as the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) and the BASFI were assessed.24 33 Because C reactive protein (CRP) values were not available in the HC group, disease activity was assessed using BASDAI to allow comparability across groups, although the Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score is the recommended measure. To allow comparability across groups, patients with DISH and HC also completed these questionnaires, although they were originally developed as disease-specific instruments for axSpA. Morning stiffness duration and severity were assessed using items 5 and 6 of the BASDAI. Data on comorbidities and laboratory parameters were extracted from clinical records and were available only for patients with DISH.

Mobility assessments

Spinal mobility was measured using the linear version of the BASMI.34 Components comprised modified Schober (lumbar flexion), lateral spinal flexion, cervical rotation, tragus-to-wall distance and intermalleolar distance with higher scores indicating greater impairment of spinal mobility.

Objective spinal mobility was assessed with ES, a certified electronic movement-analysis system consisting of bilateral flexible sensor strips placed paraspinally. The ES is capable of capturing a range of motion (ROM, °) and range of kinematics (ROK, °/s) during a standardised choreography of spinal flexion, extension, rotation and lateral flexion and is validated in patients with axSpA.26

All patients were assessed by performance-based tests. The Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB)—balance stances, 4 m usual gait speed and five-times chair rise—with the 0–12 point scoring and the ASPI, which times three standardised tasks derived from BASFI items (forward bending, rising from floor and putting on socks).35 36

To ensure that no diurnal bias occurred, all assessments of mobility were performed between 08:00 and 10:00 in the morning.30 Furthermore, all assessments of mobility were performed by the same trained assessor.

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics were compared between groups using independent t-tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Mobility and performance measures were analysed using independent t-tests and Wilcoxon rank-sum test with 95% CIs. For group comparisons of mobility outcomes adjusted for covariates, analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) were conducted including age. Left-right differences were examined with paired t-tests in the DISH group. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The sample size calculation was adapted from a previous study on spinal mobility by our team.29 All explorative statistical analyses were performed using the software Bluesky statistics V.10.3.1.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 100 patients were included, 50 patients with DISH, 20 HC and 30 patients with axSpA. Patients with DISH were older (65.1±8.4 years) compared with both HC (56.9±11.0 years, p<0.001) and axSpA (57.6±5.2 years, p<0.001). The sex distribution was similar across groups, with 62% men in the DISH group, 60% in HC and 60% in axSpA (p=0.84 and p=0.66, respectively) (table 1). According to the modified New York criteria, 7/30 (23%) patients were classified as non-radiographic axSpA and 23/30 (77%) as radiographic axSpA.

BMI was higher in patients with DISH (29.9±5.6 kg/m²) than in HC (25.4±3.0 kg/m², p<0.001), while no difference was observed compared with axSpA (29.5±5.0 kg/m², p=0.69) (table 1).

Pain scores were markedly higher in DISH (5.5±2.1) than in HC (1.2±1.7, p<0.0001), but similar to axSpA (6.0±2.1, p=0.29). A comparable pattern was observed for spinal pain (DISH: 5.5±2.3; HC: 1.4±1.9, p<0.001; axSpA: 6.5±2.0, p=0.05). Nocturnal spinal pain was reported higher in DISH (4.1±2.6), was absent in HC (0.0, p<0.001) and was slightly higher in axSpA (5.2±2.7, p=0.07) (table 1).

Morning stiffness was more pronounced in DISH than in HC for both intensity (3.6±2.6 vs 0.5±1.2, p<0.0001) and duration (3.9±2.9 vs 0.7±1.6, p<0.0001). Compared with axSpA, patients with DISH showed lower stiffness intensity (3.6±2.6 vs 5.0±2.7, p=0.03), while duration was comparable (3.9±2.9 vs 3.8±2.7, p=0.97).

Questionnaires specific to axSpA demonstrated higher BASDAI scores in DISH (4.9±1.8) compared with HC (1.4±1.5, p<0.0001), with no difference versus axSpA (5.1±1.7, p=0.60). Self-reported functional limitations were also greater in DISH (BASFI 5.1±2.3) than in HC (0.7±0.8, p<0.0001), but did not differ from axSpA (5.6±2.3, p=0.31) (table 1).

In patients with DISH, hypertension was present in 13 patients (26%), diabetes mellitus in 9 (18%) and hypercholesterolaemia in 7 (14%). In patient with DISH, mean creatinine was 0.9±0.7 mg/dL (median 0.8), and mean uric acid was 5.1±1.5 mg/dL (median 5.0). CRP levels were overall low, with a mean of 1.2±2.9 mg/dL (median 0.2), indicating a right-skewed distribution. HbA1c was available in 24 participants and showed a mean of 6.0±0.67% (median 5.95).

Measures of mobility

The BASMI total score was significantly higher in DISH (4.6±1.0) than in HC (2.8±0.6; p<0.001). Significant differences were observed across all BASMI components as shown in table 2. Performance-based assessments supported these findings: SPPB total was significantly lower in DISH than in HC and ASPI total time was more than doubled. Electronic measurements confirmed significant restrictions in DISH across ROM of flexion (27.7±9.7° vs 36.6±9.5°), extension (12.9±6.6° vs 21.0±10.9°), rotation left (17.6±8.0° vs 26.0±10.3°), rotation right (17.7±8.1° vs 27.6±11.2°), lateral flexion left (12.2±5.1° vs 22.5±6.6°) and lateral flexion right (12.3±4.8° vs 21.1±7.2°)—and across ROK—flexion (32.2±13.1°/s vs 54.5±17.7°/s), extension (18.4±9.9°/s vs 38.6±19.8°/s), rotation left (31.2±17.8°/s vs 60.3±31.4°/s), rotation right (30.8±19.1°/s vs 62.1±31.4°/s), lateral flexion left (25.7±14.8°/s vs 59.8±31.1°/s) and lateral flexion right (24.6±14.0°/s vs 55.6±31.6°/s) (table 2). After adjustment for BMI, patients with DISH showed significantly impaired physical performance and spinal kinematics compared with HCs. Specifically, SPPB (F=12.1, p=0.001) and ASPI (F=15.7, p=0.001) were significantly worse in DISH, and total spinal kinematics (ROK) remained markedly reduced (F=35.24, p<0.001). In contrast, total spinal range of motion did not differ between groups (F=2.82, p=0.11). BMI itself was not independently associated with any of these outcomes (all p≥0.05) (online supplemental table 1).

Comparisons between DISH and axSpA showed a mixed pattern. The BASMI total did not differ significantly (4.6±1.0 vs 5.1±1.9). Among BASMI items, DISH showed significantly greater lateral spinal flexion (10.9±3.5 cm vs 8.0±4.8 cm) and larger intermalleolar distance (90.8±15.6 cm vs 81.1±22.6 cm), while cervical rotation was higher only by trend (44.9±13.4° vs 38.4±18.8°). SPPB total tended to be better in DISH (10.2±2.0 vs 9.5±1.3), with a significant advantage in the chair-rise component (2.7±1.3 vs 1.9±1.1) (table 3). ASPI total time was comparable (46.5±26.1 s vs 47.4±23.9 s), though forward bending was significantly faster in DISH (16.6±9.1 s vs 22.6±13.8 s); putting on socks and rising from the floor did not differ (table 3). ES-ROM values for flexion and extension were similar, only rotation to the left was slightly better in DISH (17.6±8.0° vs 13.3±8.2°). ES-ROK parameters favoured DISH significantly in several planes—extension (18.4±9.9°/s vs 11.0±8.1°/s), rotation left (31.2±17.8°/s vs 17.8±9.9°/s) and rotation right (30.8±19.1°/s vs 18.9±13.4°/s)—with a trend for flexion ROK (32.8±12.2°/s vs 27.0±16.8°/s); only lateral flexion to both sides ROK did not differ (table 3).

Age-adjusted analyses showed similar results between DISH versus HC. Age contributed additional variance to several measures, but for most outcomes, including BASMI, SPPB, ASPI and most ROM/ROK variables, the interaction between group and age was not significant. The only exceptions were flexion and extension ROM, where the interaction terms reached significance (F=5.26, p=0.03 and F=4.18, p=0.045) (table 4). In DISH versus axSpA, BASMI remained nonsignificant after adjustment, SPPB showed a significant diagnosis effect, and the most consistent differences appeared in kinematic outcomes (extension ROK and rotational ROK favouring DISH). Again, age had isolated main effects and interactions were mostly absent, indicating that—apart from a few exceptions—age did not alter the group contrasts observed in the unadjusted analyses (table 5). After adjustment for BMI, no independent association of diagnosis was observed for physical performance or global spinal mobility measures in the comparison between DISH and axSpA. Specifically, SPPB (p>0.05), ASPI (p>0.05) and total spinal ROM (p>0.05) did not differ between groups. BMI itself was not independently associated with these outcomes (all p>0.05). In contrast, total spinal kinematics (ROK) remained significantly different between diagnoses (F=10.56, p=0.002), while BMI showed only a non-significant trend (p=0.067) (online supplemental table 2). In this axSpA cohort, biologic therapy was not associated with differences in spinal mobility, and disease activity was comparable between patients with and without biologics (BASDAI 4.9±1.3 vs 5.2±2.0, p>0.05) as shown in online supplemental table 3.

Discussion

This study shows for the first time that DISH is not only characterised by mobility deficits. Compared with HCs, these are not limited to the pure range of motion but also involve the range of kinematics, similar to those observed in axSpA. However, although both conditions impose mobility restrictions, their pattern may differ. DISH predominantly produces ossification and bony bridging along the anterior longitudinal ligament, especially in the thoracic spine, while the lumbar spine and sacroiliac regions are less frequently involved and the facet joints are often spared joints.37 38 In contrast, axSpA is characterised by inflammatory changes starting from the sacroiliac joints and ascending to the lumbar and thoracic spine, frequently including ankylosis of the facet joints.26 39 As the sacroiliac joints and hip joints are more commonly affected in axSpA than in DISH, the results in restriction of the intermalleolar distance observed in patients with axSpA in this study are explained by involvement of these anatomical sites.14

We also hypothesised an asymmetry of the left and right body sites: because DISH often exhibits right-side predilection of osteophyte formation, postulated to be inhibited on the left by aortic pulsations, one might expect greater restrictions on the right side of the axial skeleton.40 However, paired comparisons revealed no significant differences between left vs right in rotation ROM, rotation ROK, lateral flexion ROM or lateral flexion ROK. This absence of asymmetry suggests that functional mobility is symmetrically constrained, possibly because compensation or soft tissue adaptation attenuates the structural asymmetry in motion.

Another noteworthy observation is that patients with DISH reported substantial back pain and stiffness, at levels similar to patients with axSpA. This underscores that, although DISH is often thought of as an ‘osseous’ rather than inflammatory condition, it can occur with similar clinical symptoms as axSpA. In axSpA, nocturnal pain is generally considered a hallmark of inflammatory activity, whereas DISH has traditionally been regarded as a non-inflammatory, metabolically associated condition. However, recent studies have also suggested inflammatory features in DISH, which might contribute to nocturnal pain symptoms in these patients similar to axSpA.19 41

Our data demonstrate relevant limitations in spinal mobility among patients with DISH. In contrast to axSpA, where the benefits of physical activity and exercise are well established and incorporated into treatment recommendations, there are currently no specific mobility-focused guidelines for DISH.4246 Evidence is scarce, and apart from a single 24-week exercise programme showing only modest improvements—reaching statistical significance only for lumbosacral flexion, though with subjective benefit reported by about half of participants— further robust data are lacking.47

This study has several limitations. We did not apply a radiographic scoring system to quantify structural changes in DISH or axSpA; therefore, we were unable to directly relate the extent of ossification or syndesmophytes to the degree of mobility impairment, limiting conclusions regarding the contribution of structural severity to functional restriction. In addition, due to the cross-sectional design, causal relationships and longitudinal progression cannot be assessed. Some functional assessment tools validated for axial spondyloarthritis were applied to patients with DISH, although they have not been specifically validated for this condition. Finally, detailed clinical data on comorbidities—particularly metabolic syndrome features—were not systematically available and were only complete for the DISH cohort, precluding comparative analyses. Future studies should integrate validated radiographic scoring systems for both conditions, ideally combined with segment-level motion analysis and longitudinal follow-up, to better clarify structure–function relationships.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that DISH is associated with widespread and symmetric impairments in spinal mobility, affecting both the range of motion and the speed of movement across multiple measurement modalities. Compared with HCs, individuals with DISH showed marked and consistent deficits in all performance-based, clinical and electronic mobility assessments. When contrasted with patients with axSpA, the overall impairment was comparable or slightly less limited in magnitude. The pattern differed, with DISH showing relatively preserved hip-related mobility (intermalleolar distance). These findings highlight the need to consider DISH not merely as a disease described by imaging but as a condition with clinically functional impact leading to severe impairments of mobility, and suggest that mobility assessments may have diagnostic value in patients with DISH.
