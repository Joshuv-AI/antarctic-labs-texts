Single Immunoglobulin Interleukin1-Related Receptor-TWIST1 Axis Regulates Barrier Function in Neonatal Intestine
Authors: Aparna Venkatraman, Wei Yu, Heather L Menden, Sherry M Mabry, Joshua L Wheatley, Shahid Umar, Susana Chavez-Bueno, Venkatesh Sampath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsThe genetic basis of impaired intestinal barrier function in preterm infants is poorly understood. Variants in single immunoglobulin interleukin1-related receptor, a negative regulator of Toll-like receptor signaling, have been identified in preterm infants with necrotizing enterocolitis. We hypothesized that single immunoglobulin interleukin1-related receptor variants associated with necrotizing enterocolitis impair gut barrier function to pathobionts. The aim of this study was to determine how single immunoglobulin interleukin1-related receptor genetic variants disrupt neonatal intestinal barrier integrity and promote susceptibility to Gram-negative bacteria implicated in sepsis and necrotizing enterocolitis pathogenesis.MethodsTransgenic mice and preterm infant–derived enteroids expressing SIGIRR variants identified in necrotizing enterocolitis were used to define how single immunoglobulin interleukin1-related receptor regulates epithelial junctional protein assembly. An Escherichia coli strain identified in neonatal sepsis was used for functional evaluation of gut barrier function.ResultsNeonatal mice harboring the SIGIRR p.Y168X mutation (Sigirrmu/mu), exhibited loss of epithelial junctional proteins, impaired barrier function, and activation of Twist family BHLH transcription factor 1 with exaggerated Toll-like receptor signaling. Sigirrmu/mu mice were highly susceptible to bacteremia when enterally challenged with pathogenic E coli. Pharmacologic inhibition of Twist family BHLH transcription factor 1 or genetic deletion of Toll-like receptor 4 restored barrier integrity. Preterm human enteroids expressing the SIGIRR p.Y168X and p.P115R variants recapitulated barrier defects and Twist family BHLH transcription factor 1 activation in a bacteria-free environment. Twist family BHLH transcription factor 1 inhibition reversed these defects in both human and murine enteroids.ConclusionsSIGIRR preserves neonatal intestinal epithelial barrier integrity by repressing TWIST1-mediated disruption of junctional proteins. Loss of SIGIRR function compromises epithelial barrier and promotes bacterial translocation, revealing a genetic mechanism underlying gut barrier dysfunction in necrotizing enterocolitis and neonatal sepsis.Keywords: Neonatal intestine, Gut barrier, Sepsis, SIGIRR, Preterm human enteroids, TLR signaling

Summary

Single immunoglobulin interleukin1-related receptor loss-of-function variants found in preterm infants with necrotizing enterocolitis disrupts intestinal barrier integrity by inducing Twist family BHLH transcription factor 1–mediated loss of junctional proteins. Upon enteral challenge with pathogenic Escherichia coli, neonatal mice with single immunoglobulin interleukin1-related receptor deficiency exhibit increased bacteremia reversible by Twist family BHLH transcription factor 1 inhibition.

What You Need to Know

Background

Aberrant TLR signaling contributes to NEC, with SIGIRR regulating TLR4. SIGIRR variants are enriched in affected infants, and loss of junctional proteins weakens the intestinal barrier, allowing pathobionts to drive inflammation.

Impact

This study shows that a SIGIRR p.Y168X mouse model has impaired barrier function, worsened by pathogenic E coli. TWIST1 activation suppresses junctional proteins, and human enteroids and patient biopsies replicate these defects, confirming clinical relevance.

Future Directions

These findings demonstrate how host genetic factors can disrupt gut barrier function, improving our understanding of NEC pathogenesis. They also highlight the potential for strategies aimed at preserving barrier integrity to prevent neonatal intestinal disease.

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is the most frequent gastrointestinal emergency in premature infants, developing in 5% to 10% of neonates born with a birth weight <1500 g.1, 2, 3 It is believed that the interplay of intestinal dysmaturity, microbial dysbiosis, and genetic predisposition leads to barrier dysfunction, resulting in pathobiont-mediated intestinal inflammation, necrosis, and systemic bacterial translocation.4,5 The intestinal epithelium provides a dynamic barrier that allows selective permeation of nutrients while impeding invasion of pathobionts. The functional properties of the epithelial barrier are regulated by intercellular junctional complexes including adherens junction (AJs) and tight junction (TJs) proteins. Mutations in TJ proteins can disrupt the formation of AJs, whereas TJ formation itself depends on E-cadherin-based adhesion, suggesting a high level of interdependency.6, 7, 8 AJs and TJs work in concert to prevent bacterial invasion.9 Decreased expression of intestinal epithelial junctional proteins and impaired barrier function are associated with NEC and Gram-negative bacterial sepsis in neonates.10, 11, 12 However, the specific mechanisms by which genetic mutations contribute to epithelial barrier dysfunction in the neonatal intestine remain largely unknown.

A central event in the pathogenesis of NEC is the dysregulated activation of Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, particularly TLR4.13, 14, 15 Although high TLR4 expression is a programmed feature of the preterm intestine for in-utero development, acquisition of tolerance to gut microbiota is key feature of postnatal intestinal adaptation. This tolerance is mediated by attenuating TLR4-mediating signaling through several mechanisms, including induction of genes such as single immunoglobulin interleukin1-related receptor (SIGIRR) and tumor necrosis factor α–induced protein 3 (A20). Persistence of heightened TLR4 signaling in the postnatal environment therefore represents a loss of homeostatic control, and excessive activation in response to Gram-negative bacteria (GNB) after birth drives the inflammatory cascade characteristic of the disease.13, 14, 15 SIGIRR is a negative regulator of inflammation mediated by several TLRs. SIGIRR inhibits TLR signaling by competitively binding to myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MYD88), the major TLR adapter, through its Toll/interleukin1 receptor domain, thus blocking TLR–MYD88 signal transduction.16 Increased expression of TLR signaling activators like TLR4 and MYD88, along with downregulation of negative regulators like SIGIRR and A20, are observed in human intestinal tissues from preterm infants with NEC,17 suggesting an imbalance between negative and positive regulators of intestinal TLR signaling in development of NEC. In previous work, our lab conducted a forward genetic screen in infants with NEC to identify a novel stop mutation (p.Y168X) and missense variants (p.P115R, p.S80Y) in SIGIRR.18 Importantly, the SIGIRR mutation studied here was originally identified in premature twins who developed NEC, providing a direct human genetic link to disease susceptibility. Using a novel transgenic mouse that genocopies the SIGIRR p.Y168X mutation, we revealed that SIGIRR regulates postnatal gut adaptation and intestinal TLR responsiveness, suggesting a role for SIGIRR in the compromised barrier function that characterizes NEC pathogenesis.19 Our work builds on these observations by mechanistically defining how SIGIRR deficiency disrupts epithelial barrier function.

We hypothesized that SIGIRR-mediated repression of aberrant TLR signaling in the neonatal intestine is essential for maintaining integrity of AJ/TJ proteins and that SIGIRR mutations will compromise barrier function. In this study, we discovered that the truncating mutation in SIGIRR p.Y168X (Sigirrmu/mu) that we identified in patients with NEC results in a striking functional loss of E-cadherin and junctional proteins in neonatal mice. To define the mechanisms by which Sigirr deficiency programs intestinal epithelial cell (IEC) loss, we examined transcription factors that couple inflammation to junctional disruption and identified TWIST1 as a key mediator. TWIST1 is known for its essential role in fetal development, where it regulates mesodermal differentiation and morphogenesis.20,21 Beyond development, TWIST1 has been extensively studied in cancer, where it integrates inflammatory cues from nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways to drive epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), repress E-cadherin, and promote invasion.22,23 Consistent with these functions, TWIST1 is positioned as a central regulator of AJ and TJ architecture.24

Although TWIST1’s role in developmental and oncogenic is well-defined,20 its function in the neonatal intestine, a developmental window characterized by heightened TLR sensitivity and immature junctional architecture, has not been previously characterized. Importantly we were able to recapitulate the pathogenic effect of the SIGIRR p.Y168X and p.P115R (another SIGIRR variant we identified in infants with NEC)25 on intestinal barrier function in preterm infant–derived enteroids, demonstrating a conserved mechanism across species. Altogether, our findings identify a novel role for SIGIRR in repressing TWIST1 and promoting intestinal epithelial barrier function in the neonate.

Results

Single Immunoglobulin Interleukin1-Related Receptor Regulates Epithelial Junctional Protein Expression in the Neonatal Terminal Ileum, Both In Vivo and Ex Vivo

As SIGIRR mutations are enriched in infants with NEC,18,26 we initially probed developmental regulation of SIGIRR expression in the postnatal intestine of Sigirr+/+ and Sigirrmu/mu mice. SIGIRR expression in the terminal ileum (TI) epithelial cells was less abundant on postnatal day 3 (P3), peaked at P8 and decreased by P14 (Figure 1A). To further localize SIGIRR expression within the TI, we performed immunofluorescence at the indicated timepoints in Sigirr+/+ and Sigirrmu/mu (Figure 1B). At P8, SIGIRR was primarily localized to the villi, but by P14, its expression was also observed in the crypts. In Sigirrmu/mu mice, SIGIRR expression was markedly reduced at P8 and P14, consistent with nonsense-mediated decay of Sigirr messenger RNA resulting from the truncating mutation,19 and no sex-based difference was observed in SIGIRR expression (Figure 1C and D).

Loss of barrier function in neonates allows a Gram-negative bacterial invasion that underlies systemic sepsis and NEC pathogenesis.27,28 The integrity of the intestinal barrier is regulated by the coordinated expression of AJ and TJ proteins. We posited that SIGIRR mutations might exert a pervasive effect on IEC AJ and TJ proteins. To test this, we initially screened for various junctional proteins in IECs isolated from Sigirr+/+ and Sigirrmu/mu mice on P8 and P14. In addition to reduced SIGIRR expression, Sigirrmu/mu mice exhibited significant downregulation of multiple junctional proteins, including Cdh1 (E-cadherin), Cldn1 (claudin1), Cldn3 (claudin3), Cldn4 (claudin4), Cgn (cingulin), and Ocln (occludin) (Figure 1E and F). To further investigate SIGIRR’s role in junctional protein localization and abundance, we performed immunofluorescence staining for E-cadherin (AJ) and claudin1 (TJ). Robust expression of both proteins was observed in enterocytes from P3 onward. However, Sigirrmu/mu mice showed marked reductions in E-cadherin and claudin1 on P8 and P14. Specifically, levels of E-cadherin and claudin1 dropped 12-fold and 3-fold, respectively, at P8, with partial recovery by P14 (2.5-fold and 2-fold decreases, respectively) (Figure 1G and H). No sex-based differences were observed in junctional protein expression loss (Figure 1I). Western blot analysis corroborated both the gene expression and immunofluorescence findings, providing robust quantitative confirmation that loss of SIGIRR leads to reduced expression of intestinal epithelial junctional proteins (Figure 1J and K). To exclude the possibility that Sigirr deficiency causes global epithelial dysfunction, we examined olfactomedin-4, a well-established marker of crypt epithelial identity. Olfactomedin-4 expression remained unchanged in SIGIRR-deficient intestines, indicating preservation of crypt fate and overall epithelial integrity (Figure 1L). These findings demonstrate that SIGIRR loss does not cause global epithelial impairment but instead produces a selective disruption of AJ and TJ components. Together, these findings demonstrate that truncating mutations in SIGIRR selectively disrupt AJ and TJ components, particularly during early postnatal gut adaptation, without inducing widespread epithelial impairment. These complementary approaches support the conclusion that SIGIRR deficiency functionally compromises intestinal epithelial barrier integrity.

To investigate whether the partial recovery of IEC junctional protein expression on P14 was driven by compensatory upregulation of alternative negative regulators of TLR signaling, we examined the expression of A20, TOLLIP, and SOCS1-3. By P14, expression levels of A20, TOLLIP, SOCS2, and SOCS3 were significantly elevated in both Sigirr+/+ and Sigirrmu/mu mice (Figure 1M). The pronounced and time-limited loss of junctional proteins in Sigirrmu/mu mice in the immature gut is reminiscent of the narrow postnatal developmental window in preterm infants during which they are vulnerable to gut-derived bacterial sepsis and NEC.

To further characterize the impact of SIGIRR deficiency, histopathologic examination of intestines of mutant mice on P8 revealed discrete pockets of epithelial cell accumulation at the base of the villus, as shown by epithelial cell adhesion molecule staining, suggesting compromised barrier integrity29 (Figure 2A and B). To assess whether the loss of junctional proteins is evident in the heterozygous state typically seen with human mutations, we crossed Sigirr+/mu and Sigirr+/mu mice to generate littermate Sigirr+/+, Sigirr+/mu and Sigirrmu/mu offspring, confirming genotypes by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (Figure 2C and D). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the presence of even 1 copy of the mutant allele caused impairment of junctional proteins, although the defect was most prominent in Sigirrmu/mu mice (Figure 2E–J). To further validate SIGIRR’s role in regulating junctional protein expression, we examined SIGIRR, E-cadherin, and claudin-1 levels in Sigirr−/− mice. Consistent with findings in Sigirrmu/mu animals, Sigirr−/− mice displayed marked reductions in these proteins at P8 compared with wild-type controls (Figure 2E–J). To test whether loss of SIGIRR can directly induce junctional protein deficiency, we employed a small interfering RNA (siRNA) approach in primary human intestinal epithelial cells (HIECs) isolated from neonatal gut (ATCC; CRL-3266). Two days post-transfection with siSIGIRR, SIGIRR expression was reduced approximately by 60% relative to scrambled siRNA (Figure 2K). Notably, siSIGIRR-treated HIEC cells displayed marked downregulation of multiple junctional protein genes (Figure 2L). Western blot analysis further confirmed these findings at the protein level (Figure 2M and N). Collectively, these results indicate that SIGIRR is a critical regulator of junctional protein expression in both murine IECs and HIECs.

To investigate the functional consequences of SIGIRR loss, we assessed intestinal permeability to 4-kDa fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran (FD4) in Sigirr+/+, Sigirr+/mu, Sigirrmu/mu, and Sigirr−/− mice. Pups were orally gavaged with a dose of FD4 4 hours prior to euthanasia at P8, and blood and TI samples were collected (Figure 3A). FD4 concentrations in the serum were significantly elevated in Sigirr mutant mice in a gene–dosage-dependent manner, with Sigirr−/− mice exhibiting the highest levels. No sex-based differences were found in FD4 permeability (Figure 3B). Immunofluorescence imaging of the TI revealed FD4 accumulation around the villus in Sigirr+/+ mice. In contrast, FD4 penetrated the mucosa and submucosa in Sigirr+/mu, Sigirrmu/mu, and Sigirr−/− mice, with the most extensive permeability observed in Sigirr−/− mice (Figure 3C). Given the heightened susceptibility of the neonatal intestine to bacterial invasion and recent findings implicating the gut as the source of GNB in preterm infant bacteremia,27 we next examined whether SIGIRR modulates GNB translocation. P4 Sigirr+/+ and Sigirrmu/mu mice were enterally challenged with pathogenic Escherichia coli (RS218 strain), originally isolated from a human neonate with sepsis,30 and bacteremia was assessed on P9. Splenic cultures were performed to assess tissue bacterial loads resulting from intestinal translocation (Figure 3D). Sigirrmu/mu mice showed a significantly greater proportion of infected spleen with significantly greater bacterial loads compared with Sigirr+/+ mice, with no sex differences (Figure 3E–G). Mice gavaged with only saline showed no splenic bacteria (data not shown). Together, these findings demonstrate that loss of SIGIRR impairs epithelial barrier integrity during early postnatal development, increasing permeability and susceptibility to Gram-negative bacterial translocation.

Single Immunoglobulin Interleukin1-Related Receptor Inhibits Activation of Twist Family BHLH Transcription Factor 1 in a Toll-Like Receptor 4–Dependent Manner in Neonatal Terminal Ileum

TWIST1 is a well-characterized inflammation-responsive transcription factor induced downstream of NF-κB and MAPK signaling.22 Once activated, TWIST1 regulates transcriptional programs that influence junctional stability and barrier integrity.31 Given the pervasive deficiency in AJ and TJ proteins in Sigirr mutant mice, we queried whether SIGIRR deficiency would activate TWIST1 in the neonatal intestine and could contribute to this altered barrier phenotype. Immunostaining of terminal ileal tissues from Sigirrmu/mu at P8 revealed that TWIST1, barely detectable in Sigirr+/+, was found to be not only upregulated but also translocated to the nucleus in Sigirr+/mu and Sigirrmu/mu mice, indicating TWIST1 activation. Nuclear localization of TWIST1 was evident in both male and female Sigirrmu/mu mice (Figure 4A). TWIST1 stability and protection from proteasomal degradation are enhanced through phosphorylation of serine residues by MAPKs such as p38.32 Consistent with TWIST1 activation, Sigirrmu/mu mice displayed increased nuclear localization of phosphorylated Ser68 TWIST1 (PTWIST1) and its upstream mediator, Pp38MAPK (Figure 4B). To accurately assess protein abundance, we performed Western blotting of isolated IECs, which demonstrated clear baseline TWIST1 expression and robust induction of TWIST1, pTWIST1, and P-p38 MAPK in Sigirrmu/mu mice, with no change in total p38 MAPK (Figure 4C and D). These biochemical data validate TWIST1 activation and complement the localization-focused immunohistochemistry results. Together, these findings demonstrate that loss of SIGIRR leads to activation and stabilization of TWIST1 in the neonatal intestine, positioning TWIST1 as a mechanistic contributor to the AJ/TJ defects observed in Sigirr-deficient epithelium.

Activation of p38MAPK is a feature of canonical TLR signaling.33 Positing that TWIST1 activation in SIGIRR deficiency arises from activation of canonical TLR signaling in IEC, we examined canonical markers of TLR signaling—phosphoprotein 65 (Pp65) and phosphorylated inhibitor of NF-κB kinase subunit beta in IEC. Western blot analysis of IECs isolated from Sigirr+/+ and Sigirrmu/mu revealed a robust increase in both Pp65 and phosphorylated inhibitor of NF-κB kinase subunit beta in Sigirrmu/mu mice, indicating activation of canonical TLR signaling with the truncating SIGIRR mutation. (Figure 4E and F). To further confirm that TWIST1 activation with SIGIRR deficiency is TLR-dependent, we crossed Tlr4−/− mice with Sigirrmu/mu mice to generate Tlr4−/−Sigirrmu/mu double mutants (Figure 4G). The double mutants exhibited negligible expression of SIGIRR (Figure 4J). The expression profiles of SIGIRR, junctional proteins, and TWIST1 in TLR4-deficient mice resembled those in Sigirr+/+ mice (Figure 4J). Strikingly, loss of TLR4 in Sigirrmu/mu mice attenuated the increased expression and nuclear localization of TWIST and rescued both molecular and functional barrier integrity, including FD4 permeability (Figures 4J and 6A). Furthermore, IECs isolated from Tlr4−/− and Tlr4−/−Sigirrmu/mu mice did not reveal elevated expression of Pp65 and inflammatory cytokine marker intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (Figure 4H and I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TLR4-mediated signaling contributes to TWIST1 activation and defective barrier function in Sigirrmu/mu mice, implicating dysregulated SIGIRR–TLR4 signaling as a critical driver of TWIST1-dependent neonatal IEC barrier dysfunction.

Twist Family BHLH Transcription Factor 1 Inhibitor Restores Gut Barrier Integrity in Single Immunoglobulin Interleukin1-Related Receptor Mutant Mice In Vivo

To determine whether TWIST1 activation contributes to the loss of junctional proteins in Sigirrmu/mu mice, we employed Harmine HCl, a TWIST1 inhibitor (i-TWIST1) that induces proteasomal-dependent degradation of TWIST1,34,35 because Twist1−/− mice exhibit embryonic lethality36 (Figure 5A). Dose titration identified 10 mg/kg as the optimal concentration for inhibiting TWIST1 expression and nuclear localization (Figure 5B). Treatment with i-TWIST1 for 3 consecutive days restored claudin1 and E-cadherin expression at P8 (Figure 5C) and normalized intestinal permeability as measured by FD4 flux (Figure 6A).

We employed mouse enteroids cultured under bacteria-free conditions to probe epithelial-intrinsic regulation of TWIST1. Enteroids derived from Sigirr+/+ and Sigirrmu/mu mice recapitulated the in vivo expression patterns of TWIST1 and E-cadherin. Inhibition of TWIST1 in Sigirrmu/mu enteroids prevented TWIST1 nuclear accumulation and restored E-cadherin expression (Figure 5D). Similarly, inhibition of upstream kinase p38MAPK reduced nuclear Pp38MAPK and TWIST1 and partially rescued E-cadherin expression (Figure 5F). To directly confirm the role of TWIST1 in regulating epithelial junctions, Sigirrmu/mu enteroids from P8 mice were transduced with lentiviral short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting TWIST1 (shTwist1).21 shTwist1 transduction effectively prevented TWIST1 nuclear localization and significantly increased e-Cadherin expression compared with controls (Figure 5E).

To assess whether TWIST1 inhibition prevents the translocation of GNB, we administered i-TWIST1 to Sigirrmu/mu daily between P3 and P5, followed by an enteral challenge with a green fluorescent protein (GFP)-tagged pathogenic E coli (RS218 strain) at P5. Bacteremia and splenic bacterial loads were quantified at P7 (Figure 6B). Sigirrmu/mu mice without TWIST1 inhibition exhibited significantly higher rates of blood bacteremia and splenic infection compared with inhibitor-treated Sigirrmu/mu littermates (Figure 6C). Collectively, these results demonstrate that activation of TWIST1 mediates the loss of junctional proteins and barrier integrity to GNB in Sigirrmu/mu mice. Pharmacologic inhibition of TWIST1 restores epithelial junctions and prevents GNB translocation, establishing TWIST1 as a critical downstream effector of deviant intestinal barrier function with SIGIRR deficiency.

Single Immunoglobulin Interleukin1-Related Receptor Deficiency Activates Twist Family BHLH Transcription Factor 1 and Impairs Epithelial Integrity in Human Preterm Enteroids

To determine if SIGIRR repression of TWIST1 is relevant to the human neonatal intestine, we cultured human ileal enteroids derived from surgical samples of premature infants without NEC. Enteroids were transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding either an empty control, the SIGIRR p.Y168X truncating mutation, or the missense variant SIGIRR p.P115R (Figure 6D and E). As shown in Figure 6F, unlike control enteroids, those expressing SIGIRR p.P115R or SIGIRR p.Y168X exhibited marked reductions in E-cadherin and claudin-1 expression, accompanied by nuclear translocation of TWIST1. Treatment with the i-TWIST1 in SIGIRR p.Y168X mutant enteroids restored E-cadherin expression and prevented TWIST1 nuclear translocation (Figure 6F). To determine whether the loss of junctional proteins resulted in impaired barrier function, we assessed epithelial permeability using apical-out neonatal enteroids incubated with FD4 and imaged by differential interference contrast and fluorescence confocal microscopy (Figure 6G). FD4 diffusion assays revealed that only 34% of apical-out SIGIRR mutant enteroids excluded FD4, compared with 80% of control enteroids. Treatment with i-TWIST1 restored FD4 exclusion in SIGIRR p.Y168X enteroids (Figure 6H–J), demonstrating that SIGIRR mutations compromise epithelial barrier function through TWIST1 activation in human neonatal enteroids.

To assess the translational relevance of these findings, we next examined SIGIRR, E-cadherin, and TWIST1 expression in autopsy and surgical samples from patients with and without NEC. Details of the patient cohort are shown in Figure 7A. Visualization of distal ileal samples from premature infants with NEC revealed marked reductions in both SIGIRR and E-cadherin protein expression. In contrast, increased TWIST1 expression and nuclear localization of TWIST1 were observed in sections from both surgical and autopsy samples of patients with NEC, but not in any age-matched control samples (Figure 7B). Together, these findings suggest that decreased SIGIRR expression in the human neonatal intestine is associated with TWIST1 activation and junctional disruption, implicating this pathway in the pathogenesis of NEC.

Discussion

Intestinal epithelial junctional proteins are critical to sustain barrier function against Gram-negative pathobionts implicated in causing NEC and systemic sepsis in the neonatal intestine.2,27,37 Our study identifies SIGIRR as a pivotal regulator of intestinal epithelial junctional integrity during neonatal development, establishing its essential role in maintaining barrier function and preventing bacterial translocation in the immature gut. Using complementary in vivo and ex vivo approaches, we demonstrate that mice that genocopy the SIGIRR p.Y168X mutation, previously identified in human NEC, led to a profound downregulation of both AJ and TJ proteins, increased epithelial permeability, and heightened susceptibility to Gram-negative bacteremia. Preterm infant–derived enteroids encoding the p.Y168X and p.P115R variants recapitulated these findings, demonstrating that loss of SIGIRR is sufficient to compromise gut barrier integrity. These effects were mediated by TLR4-dependent activation of TWIST1, a transcriptional repressor of junctional proteins whose role in neonatal barrier function has not been previously investigated. Although SIGIRR mutant mice do not spontaneously develop NEC, the observed barrier defects, heightened inflammatory tone, and increased vulnerability to bacterial invasion and systemic dissemination represent mechanistic hallmarks of the disease. Together, these findings establish a coherent biological link between SIGIRR signaling, TWIST1 activation, and NEC susceptibility, connecting genetically mediated defects in innate immune regulation to compromised neonatal intestinal barrier function, even in the absence of an induced NEC model.

The functional importance of SIGIRR to the neonatal intestine is further underscored by its dynamic postnatal expression pattern in the TI, peaking around P8 and declining to adult levels by P14. This transient increase coincides with a critical window of neonatal gut adaptation, implying that SIGIRR functions as a protective brake against excessive innate immune activation during microbial colonization. In Sigirr mutant mice, this regulatory checkpoint fails, leading to marked reductions in key junctional proteins including E-cadherin, claudin1, claudin3, claudin4, occludin, and cingulin. The coordinated expression of AJ and TJ proteins is essential for barrier integrity. Their interdependence has previously been demonstrated, as mutations in E-cadherin gene or claudin, specifically in the intestine, disrupt barrier function and intestinal homeostasis.38,39 The observed impairment in junctional protein expression in Sigirrmu/mu and Sigirr–/– mice mirrors the barrier immaturity seen in preterm infants, who are particularly vulnerable to NEC and gut-derived sepsis.27 Notably, even heterozygous Sigirr+/mu animals, genotypically analogous to variants commonly observed in humans, exhibited significant loss of junctional proteins, consistent with the dominant-negative effect of truncating SIGIRR mutations reported in preterm human neonates. siRNA-mediated SIGIRR knockdown in primary HIECs further confirmed that SIGIRR is both necessary and sufficient for maintaining epithelial junctional protein expression.

Although deficient expression of TJ proteins and E-cadherin have been observed in preterm infants with NEC,40 the role of genetic variants in programming these defects have not been previously elucidated. A recent study, using shotgun metagenomic and whole-genomic sequencing, revealed that gut-derived bacterial colonization precedes bloodstream infection in preterm infants.27 Pathogenic bacteria are known to disrupt E-cadherin junctional complexes in polarized epithelia, facilitating barrier dysfunction and bacterial invasion.41 Our findings extend this paradigm by demonstrating that mutations in immune regulatory genes, such as SIGIRR, predispose to junctional protein loss, barrier dysfunction, and bacterial translocation. The physiological consequence of SIGIRR loss was evident in vivo, where permeability assays demonstrated a gene dosage-dependent increase in FD4 flux across the intestinal epithelium. This was accompanied by bacterial translocation and bacteremia following enteral challenge with pathogenic E coli.42 Together, these results provide a mechanistic link between gene variants, barrier disruption, and susceptibility to Gram-negative sepsis.

To elucidate the downstream mechanism underlying SIGIRR-mediated barrier dysfunction, we focused on TWIST1, a basic helix–loop–helix transcription factor known to repress E-cadherin and TJ proteins during EMT.24 Loss of the SIGIRR Toll/interleukin1 receptor domain due to the p.Y168X mutation activated p38MAPK and NF-κB signaling, which mediate post-translational phosphorylation and stabilization of TWIST1. TWIST1 expression and stability are regulated by phosphorylation-dependent control of its proteasomal degradation.32 Specifically, serine phosphorylation of TWIST1 by kinases such as p38MAPK shields it from proteasomal degradation, facilitating its nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity.43 In Sigirrmu/mu mice, IEC TWIST1 expression, nuclear translocation and Ser68 phosphorylation were strongly induced, coinciding with enhanced p38MAPK phosphorylation. Activation of NF-κB (p65 phosphorylation) and p38MAPK in IECs paralleled robust induction of TWIST1, disruption of junctional proteins, and impaired epithelial barrier integrity. Together, these data indicate that deviant activation of canonical TLR signaling in the absence of functional SIGIRR drives p38 MAPK–dependent TWIST1 stabilization, linking innate immune dysregulation to transcriptional repression of junctional proteins and epithelial barrier compromise in the neonatal intestine.

To directly assess TWIST1’s role, we employed both pharmacologic and genetic inhibition strategies in vivo and in vitro. As Twist−/− mice exhibit embryonic lethality and Twist+/- mice display mesodermal defects characteristic of the human Saethre–Chotzen syndrome,36 we employed Harmine HCl, a validated TWIST1 inhibitor,35 for in vivo studies and lentiviral shRNA knockdown (shTwist1) in Sigirrmu/mu enteroids from P8 mice. Twist1 inhibition in vivo, shTwist1-mediated knockdown in mouse enteroids, and pharmacologic inhibition in both mouse and preterm human enteroids expressing the SIGIRR p.Y168X truncating variant effectively suppressed TWIST1 nuclear localization and significantly restored E-cadherin expression. Moreover, TWIST1 inhibition in mice in vivo and human enteroids expressing the SIGIRR p.Y168X mutant significantly reduced FD4 flux, confirming functional recovery of barrier function. These findings establish TWIST1 as a direct mediator of epithelial junctional repression and barrier dysfunction in neonatal intestine. Harmine HCl promotes proteasome-dependent degradation of TWIST1 protein without altering transcript abundance, consistent with a post-translational mechanism of regulation via enhanced ubiquitination and proteasomal turnover of TWIST1, leading to stabilization of epithelial junctional proteins such as E-cadherin.34 As the SIGIRR p. Y168X variant is very rare (mean allele frequency <0.001), we also pursued studies with the p. P115R variant identified in infants with NEC, which has a mean allele frequency of 6 of 1000 (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/). The p.P115R variant identified in infants with NEC produced similar effects, inducing TWIST1 activation and reducing E-cadherin expression. The mean allele frequency of loss of function (eg, stop) or predicted loss of function SIGIRR missense variants in population databases (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) is 3.5%, and our results are representative of each of these variant classes. Our mouse model mirrored surgical and biopsy samples from patients with NEC, where TWIST1 activation was accompanied by decrease in SIGIRR and E-cadherin expression. Collectively, this result provides a unifying explanation for the barrier immaturity observed in SIGIRR-deficient neonatal intestine. Importantly, the restoration of E-cadherin and barrier function following TWIST1 inhibition demonstrates that the phenotype is reversible, highlighting TWIST1 as a potential therapeutic target in at risk neonates.

The discovery of TWIST1 as a modulator of gut barrier function is unexpected, as TWIST1 has primarily been implicated as a transcriptional repressor in cancer and in mesodermal lineage specification. Typically, TWIST1 expression is downregulated after birth and restricted to mesenchymal stem cells in adults, with minimal expression in epithelial cells unless undergoing EMT.43 Although TWIST1 has been linked to endothelial barrier dysfunction in adult lung and cancer models,44, 45, 46 its pathogenic role in the neonatal intestine has not been described. Our study identifies, for the first time, that TWIST1 regulates intestinal barrier function during early life and uncovers a novel SIGIRR–TWIST1 regulatory axis in which SIGIRR modulates TWIST1 expression to link anti-inflammatory signaling with epithelial permeability. TLRs are innate immune sensors that initiate inflammatory responses to microbial invasion. TLR4, a key sensor of GMB, plays a central role in NEC pathogenesis, and Tlr4−/− mice are protected against experimental NEC.47 TLR4 deficiency in Sigirrmu/mu mice inhibited p38MAPK and NF-κB activation, prevented nuclear TWIST1 localization, and restored junctional protein expression and barrier integrity. Notably, even in bacteria-free conditions, SIGIRR-deficient enteroids retained active TLR signaling, indicating an intrinsic epithelial defect. Together, these findings suggest that SIGIRR constrains TLR4-dependent signaling to prevent aberrant TWIST1 activation and preserve epithelial homeostasis. Loss of this regulatory balance may predispose neonates to barrier dysfunction and inflammation, providing a mechanistic link between innate immune dysregulation and early-life intestinal disease. We have previous shown that Sigirrmu/mu mice demonstrate increased intestinal villus injury in response to enteral formula feeding, and therefore, we focused on vulnerability to bacterial invasion through a permissive intestinal barrier.48 Beyond its canonical anti-inflammatory role, SIGIRR emerges as a critical coordinator of epithelial barrier maturation during early life.

Collectively, our data reveal a novel SIGIRR–TWIST1 regulatory axis that maintains epithelial barrier integrity in the neonatal intestine. Disruption of SIGIRR-mediated TWIST1 inhibition in the neonatal intestine arising from deleterious genetic variants can program defects in barrier function leading to Gram-negative sepsis or NEC in preterm infants. Given that TWIST1 inhibition rescues the defect arising from SIGIRR deficiency in both mouse and human enteroids, and TWIST1 is upregulated in intestinal tissue from NEC infants, inhibiting TWIST1 or augmenting SIGIRR function might provide therapeutic options.

Materials and Methods

Sex as Biological Variable

Both male and female mice were used, and similar findings are reported for both sexes. Animals were sex- and age-matched, and whenever possible, littermates were used for each experiment.

Animals

Mouse experiments were performed according to the protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Missouri-Kansas City Laboratory Animal Resource Center and Children’s Mercy Hospital Kansas City. Sigirr mutant (Sigirrmu/mu) mice were generated as described previously.19 B6(Cg)-Tlr4tm1.2Karp/J (TLR4−/−; stock no. 029,015) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratories. B6N.129S%-Sigirrtm1lex (Sigirr−/−) mice were generated commercially from Mutant Mouse Resource and Research Centers. Mice of all strains were housed in an environment with controlled temperature and humidity, on a 12-hour light:dark cycle, and fed regular rodent chow. The numbers of animals used for each experiment are specified in the figure legends as n = x mice per group, per timepoint analyzed.

Human Tissues

Deidentified intestinal biopsy specimens from surgical as well as autopsy samples of patients with NEC and control patients were obtained from the pathology department at Children’s Mercy Kansas City under the auspices of Institutional Review Board–approved data repository (IRB #16020116). Informed consent was obtained from parents/legal guardians for surgical samples. Matched controls were obtained from infants who died of central nervous system disease or respiratory diseases and had a full autopsy. Additional surgical controls were obtained from infants who had surgical resection for noninflammatory intestinal conditions such as atresia. Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Mouse Intestinal Epithelial Cell Isolation

IECs from the TI were isolated at P8 as previously described.19 Briefly, a 5-cm segment from TI was flushed with ice-cold 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), opened longitudinally, and placed in an ice-cold dissociation reagent containing 30 mM EDTA, and 1.5 mM dithiothreitol in 1× PBS. The tissue was incubated on ice for 20 minutes. The intestine was then placed in prewarmed dissociation reagent containing 30 mM EDTA in 1× PBS and incubated at 37 °C on a shaker for 10 minutes. The tube with the intestine was shaken vigorously every 2 minutes during incubation. IECs were collected by passing the supernatant through a 100-μm cell strainer and centrifuging at 300 g for 5 minutes at 4 °C. IECs were used for gene and protein analysis.

Cell Culture

HIEC-6 (ATCC; CRL-3266) cells were grown in OptiMEM 1 Reduced Serum Medium supplemented with 4% fetal bovine serum, 20 mM HEPES, 10 mM GlutaMAX, and 10 ng/mL epidermal growth factor in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Transfection was performed using lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For RNA interference, the specific siRNA duplexes targeting human SIGGIR (sc-61547), fluorescein-conjugated control siRNA (sc-36869), transfection reagent (sc-29528), and reduced-serum transfection medium (sc-36868) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. When cell confluence reached 60% to 80%, the cells were washed with 1× PBS and transiently transfected with transfection complexes prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At 48 or 72 hours after transfection, silencing of SIGGIR at the messenger RNA and protein level was checked by real-time quantitative PCR and Western blot, respectively.

Mouse Enteroids

Two-dimensional mouse small intestinal enteroids were generated from both male and female Sigirr+/+ and Sigirrmu/mu at P8 and cultured as previously described.49 The enteroids were transduced with lentiviral TWIST1 shRNA or control shRNA for 5 days before processing immunostaining.

Human Enteroids

Human enteroids obtained from Baylor College of Medicine (kind gift of Dr Sashirekha Ramani, PhD). Briefly, ileal tissue samples were collected from infants undergoing gastrointestinal surgery following approval by the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston Institutional Review Board and parental consent. Infant tissue samples were transferred to Baylor College of Medicine under a material transfer agreement for establishment of human intestinal enteroids. Human intestinal enteroid cultures were generated at the Texas Medical Center Digestive Diseases Center Gastrointestinal Experimental Model Systems Core from intestinal crypts isolated from the surgical tissues of infant and adult patients as previously described.50 Human enteroids were grown embedded in Matrigel with growth media (Advanced Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium/F12, 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin, 10 mM HEPES, 1× GlutaMAX, 1× N2, 1× B27, 1 mM N-acetyl-cysteine, 10 nM gastrin, 50 ng/mL epidermal growth factor, 10 mM nicotinamide, 10 mM Y27632, 500 nM A83-01, 10 mM SB202190, and 50% L-WRN-conditioned media.51 Human enteroids were transduced with lentiviral particles carrying empty vector of IRES-dTomato or SIGIRR 168X IRES-dTomato to generate stable lines expression empty vector (dTomato only), SIGIRR p.Y168X, and SIGIRR p.P115R. Apical-out enteroids were generated as previously described.52 Briefly, matrigel-embedded enteroids were solubilized in 5 mM EDTA in PBS for 1 hour at 4 °C. Enteroids were centrifuged at 150 g for 3 minutes at 4 °C, and the supernatant was removed. The pellet was resuspended in growth media in ultra-low attachment 24-well tissue culture plates (Corning Costar 3473) and cultured for 48 to 72 hours.

Permeability Assays

In vivo in mice

To assess the effect of Sigirr mutation on intestinal permeability, FD4 with molecular weight of 4KD (46,944; Millipore Sigma) was administered via an orogastric tube. Each group (wild-type and mutant mice pups) at P8 received 44 mg/100 g body weight of FD4, suspended in sterile PBS. Four hours later, pups were euthanized, and TI was collected for visualization of FD4 permeability into the intestinal mucosa. To collect serum, blood was coagulated for 30 minutes at room temperature and 10,000 g for 10 minutes at room temperature. Fluorescence was measured in diluted serum at an excitation wavelength of 480 nm and emission wavelength of 520 nm. The amount of dextran in the plasma was calculated based on standard dilution curves of known dextran concentrations.53

Ex vivo in human enteroids

Epithelial barrier integrity of human enteroids using FD4 was evaluated as previously described.52 Briefly, apical-out enteroids were resuspended in a solution containing 2 mg/mL 4 kDa FD4 and incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature. Enteroids were centrifuged at 150 g for 3 minutes at 4 °C and washed with ice-cold 1× PBS to remove residual FD4. Enteroids were placed on a slide, mounted using fluorogel with tris buffer (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and imaged using 20× objective inverted laser scanning confocal microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope with CSU- W1 scanner unit) by differential interference contrast and fluorescence microscopy. Fluorescence intensity of the area inside the organoids was graphed using Fiji (Image J software). The percentage of enteroids with intact barrier integrity was determined by dividing the number of enteroids that excluded FD4 by the total number of enteroids.

Bacterial Inoculation

For E coli inoculations, we used the archetypal K1-postive RS218 E coli strain, which was isolated from a neonate with bacteremia and meningitis.30,42 RS218 was modified via Tn7 transposon mutagenesis to express both GFP and chloramphenicol resistance as selection markers. This was achieved by conjugation with pMRE-Tn7-132 (Addgene), which produces nonrandom gene insertions that pose no bacterial fitness cost.54 P4 mice were infected by gavage with 2 doses of 1 × 109 colony forming units (CFU) of RS218, 2 hours apart. On P9, mice were euthanized, and spleens were collected to determine spleen bacterial loads in CFU/100 mg tissue. Spleen was weighed, and 1:1 volume of cold 1× PBS/0.1% gelatin was added. Spleen was homogenized using a handheld pellet pestle. Splenic homogenates were cultured in lysogenic broth plates containing chloramphenicol (10 μg/mL) for specific quantification of RS218. Bacterial loads in spleen homogenates and blood (bacteremia) were quantified in square lysogenic broth plates using the track dilution method.55 Additionally, a shorter incubation experimental model was developed, where P5 pups were enterally gavaged with GFP-tagged RS218. On P7, mice were euthanized, and spleens were collected to determine bacterial loads in CFU/100 mg tissue. For inhibitor studies, mice were intraperitoneally injected with either saline or 10 mg/kg body weight Harmine HCl (PTWIST1inhibitor; Millipore Sigma; SMB00461) at P3 to P5, followed by enteral challenge with GFP-tagged RS218 at P5. On P7, mice were euthanized, and spleens were collected to determine bacterial loads in CFU/100 mg tissue. Pups reported missing were likely consumed by mother due to illness.

Gene Expression Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the IECs using Ambion TRIzol Reagent (Life Technologies) and reverse-transcribed using LunaScript RT SuperMix Kit (New England Biolabs). Gene expression analysis was performed using Forget-Me-Not Eva Green qPCR Master Mix (Biotium) using prevalidated primers from Millipore Sigma on a ViiA 7. The housekeeping gene ß-actin were used for normalization.

Western blotting

Isolated IECs were lysed in ice-cold 1× radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo Scientific) and then homogenized using a bullet blender. The lysed cells were centrifuged at 14,500 g for 20 minutes at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and protein was quantified using Pierce BCA protein Assay kit. Equal amounts of total protein lysate were loaded and resolved using Any KD Mini-Protean precast gels (4,569,034; BIO-RAD). Proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membrane and blocked for 1 hour with Pierce Protein-free blocking buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with indicated primary antibodies. Membranes were washed several times with 1× Tris-buffered saline with 0.5% Triton X-100 (TBST) and incubated at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase–coupled secondary antibodies. ß-actin was used as a loading control. Proteins were detected by chemiluminescence using immobilon Forte Western horseradish peroxidase substrate, and the images were captured using Invitrogen iBright 1500 (Thermo Fisher Scientific); ß-actin was used as a loading control. Densitometry was performed using Image J software, and changes were normalized to ß-actin or the corresponding nonphosphorylated antibody. The primary antibodies used were: rabbit anti-p38MAPK (no. 8690) and rabbit anti-phospho-p38 (Thr180/Tyr182, no. 4511) from Cell Signaling (1:500); mouse anti-β-actin from Sigma (A1978, 1:5000); mouse anti-TWIST1 (AB175430, 1:500) and rabbit anti-TWIST phospo S68 (AB187008, 1:500) from Abcam; goat anti E-cadherin (AF748,1:2000) from R&D Systems; rabbit anti-SIGIRR (AbB228077, 1:2000) from Abcam; rabbit-anti occludin (ab216327, 1:2000) from Abcam; and rabbit anti-cingulin (21369-1-AP, 1:2000) from Proteintech.

Inhibitor Studies

In vitro

IECs isolated from P8 mice with the Sigirr mutation were cultured in 8-well glass-chambered slides. Initial screening at different concentrations of inhibitors revealed 10 μM Harmine HCl (PTWIST1 inhibitor) and 10 μM SB202190 (p38MAPK inhibitor) were optimal to inhibit TWIST1 and p38MAPK. The cells were treated in presence and absence of 10 μM Harmine HCl (PTWIST1) and 10 μM SB202190 (p38MAPK inhibitor; 1264/10; Tocris).35,56 After 24 hours of drug treatment, slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and proceeded for immunofluorescence immunostaining.

In vivo

P3 Sigirr+/+ and Sigirrmu/mu mice received 10 mg/kg body weight Harmine HCl (i-PTWIST1inhibitor; Millipore Sigma; SMB00461) or saline via intraperitoneal injection for 2 to 3 consecutive days. An initial screen of different dosages (2, 5, and 10 mg/kg body weight) of Harmine HCl for 3 consecutive days revealed that 10 mg/kg body weight was the optimal concentration for inhibiting expression and nuclear localization of TWIST1. At P8, pups were euthanized, and TI was excised. Tissue was subjected to paraffin embedding and sectioned for immunostaining experiments.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Paraffin section

TI was fixed in 10% buffered formalin and transferred to 70% ethanol for paraffin embedding. Four-micrometer paraffin sections of intestine were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (Sigma Aldrich) for 20 minutes at >95 °C in a steamer. Sections were permeabilized using 1× PBS + 0.05% Tween-20 (1× PBS-T) and blocked with Power Block Universal Blocking Reagent (BioGenex) for 1 hour at room temperature. The primary antibody was diluted in antibody diluent reagent solution (Invitrogen). Sections were then stained overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody. After several washes with 1× PBS-T, the tissue sections were then incubated for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark with secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa fluor 555, Alexa fluor 488, and/or Alexa 647 and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; ThermoFisher). Slides were mounted using Fluorogel with Tris Buffer (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Only the secondary antibody was used as the negative control for theinitial evaluation of all antibodies. Images were acquired using a ×25/oil objective Zeiss Inverted LSM 510 meta laser scanning confocal microscope, and image analysis was performed with Fiji software. Primary antibody used were: rabbit anti-SIGIRR (1:100; AbB228077; Abcam), mouse anti-Twist1 (1:100; AB175430; Abcam), goat anti-E-cadherin (1:200; AF748; R&D Systems), rabbit anti-claudin 1 (1:100; 71-7800; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and anti–zona occludens-1 (1:100; AB221547; Abcam).

Frozen sections

To visualize FD4 permeability in the intestine, mice administered with FD4 were euthanized, and the TI was excised. Freshly isolated intestine tissue was fixed in ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes to 1 hour,57 rinsed several times with ice-cold 1× PBS, and then left overnight at 4 °C in 30% sucrose. For visualization of TWIST phospo S68 and phospho-p38 Thr180/Tyr182, tissues were fixed in ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde for 6 hours, rinsed several times with ice-cold 1× PBS, and then left overnight at 4 °C in 30% sucrose. The tissue was embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound and cryo-sectioned with 10-μm thickness. Samples were permeabilized with TBST and stained for DAPI before being mounted. Images were acquired using ×25/oil objective with a Zeiss Inverted LSM 510 meta laser scanning confocal microscope. The primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-phospho-p38 Thr180/Tyr182 (1:50; 4511; Cell Signaling) and rabbit anti-TWIST phospo S68 (1:50; AB187008; Abcam).

Organoids immunostaining

Patient-derived and mouse-derived organoids were cultured in in 8-well chambered slides (Falcon culture slides; Corning). Organoids were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, permeabilized in 1μ PBS + 0.2% Triton X-100, and blocked with Universal Blocking Reagent for 1 hour. The slides were stained overnight at 4 °C with primary anti-TWIST1, anti-E-cadherin, anti-claudin1 and anti-Pp38MAPK and then labeled with the fluorescent dye-conjugated secondary detection antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. After nuclear staining with DAPI, the slides were mounted with Fluorogel, and the images were captured using 20× objective inverted laser scanning confocal microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope with CSU-W1 scanner unit).

Figures

Figures were created using GraphPad Prism 9, Adobe Illustrator, or Biorender.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean. All animal data were obtained from 4 to 13 animals for each experimental group. Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism 9 with statistical significance set at P < .05. For statistical analysis of data generated from 2 groups, a 2-tailed unpaired Student t test or Fisher exact test was used when a normal distribution could be assumed; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. To compare multiple groups, we initially examined whether distribution of data was Gaussian using the D’Agostino–Pearson omnibus normality test. If data were normally distributed, then analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test with multiple comparisons was used for analysis.
