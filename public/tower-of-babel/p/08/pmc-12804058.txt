Three-Dimensional Echocardiography in Congenital Heart Disease: Comparison with Pathologic Specimens—A Case Series
Authors: Helen M. Stanley, Yan Wang, Michael D. Quartermain, Matthew A. Jolley, Lindsay S. Rogers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Atrioventricular valve (AVV) anatomy in congenital heart disease (CHD) varies widely, and derangements in structure can lead to valve regurgitation.1 Atrioventricular valve regurgitation in this patient population is associated with poor clinical outcomes, including circulatory failure and death.2, 3, 4, 5 Three-dimensional echocardiography (3DE) has informed the understanding of AVV structure in adults6,7 and is increasingly being applied and studied in the pediatric CHD population.8, 9, 10, 11

The use of human heart samples has historically led to advances in cardiovascular research,12,13 and specimens may serve to elucidate the strengths and weaknesses of 3DE to define AVV anatomy. To that end, we present a case series comparing findings by 3DE to pathologic heart specimens among patients with CHD. This work received approval from the institutional review board at our center.

Case Presentations

To compare 3DE findings to pathologic specimens, echocardiograms were reviewed by 3 clinical experts (2 physicians and 1 senior sonographer), and valve anatomy was observed, including number and location of valve leaflets, leaflet abnormalities, chordae tendineae abnormalities, and papillary muscle number and location. Pathology specimens were assessed across the same domains by a congenital cardiac pathologist.

Case 1

Patient 1 was born with a prenatal diagnosis of unbalanced common atrioventricular canal defect, double-outlet right ventricle {S,D,A} with right ventricular hypoplasia, coarctation of the aorta, and moderate-to-severe AVV regurgitation (Figure 1, Videos 1 and 2). An unsedated 3DE was performed at 2 days old. Review of this study showed thickened leaflet tips, restrictive motion of the inferior bridging leaflet, and a single papillary muscle (Figure 1A and B, Videos 1 and 2). The patient was palliated as a neonate with a ventricular assist device (VAD) and stage 1 hybrid procedure, and ultimately underwent heart transplant later in infancy. Case 2

Patient 2 was born at 32 weeks’ gestation with a prenatal diagnosis of restrictive cardiomyopathy and hydrops fetalis (Figure 2, Videos 3 and 4). Postnatal imaging and diagnostic catheterization revealed a diagnosis most consistent with polyvalvular disease (Video 3). The patient underwent unsedated 3DE at 1 month old. Review of this study demonstrated that the tricuspid valve (TV) was bileaflet and the mitral valve was arcade with prolapse of the anterior leaflet (Figure 2A and B, Videos 3 and 4). The patient was palliated with a VAD and ultimately underwent transplant in infancy. Pathologic review confirmed the presence of a bileaflet TV, with a missing posterior-anterior commissure (Figure 2C and D). Case 3

Patient 3 was born with a prenatal diagnosis of hypoplastic left heart syndrome with severe TV regurgitation (Figure 3, Videos 5 and 6). The patient had an unsedated 3DE study on day 8 of life. Study review demonstrated a dysplastic TV with thickened leaflets, shortened chordae to multiple small papillary muscles, and deficiency of the posterior leaflet (Figure 3A and B, Videos 5 and 6). They were palliated with a stage 1 hybrid procedure and placement of a VAD and underwent heart transplant later in infancy. The age at time of 3DE study ranged from 1 day to 16 years old (Table 1). All studies were transthoracic echocardiograms (TTEs), performed using a Philips Epiq machine (Philips Medical Systems) with an X5-1 or X5-1c transducer. Three-dimensional (3D) images were obtained with 3D zoom or full volume, using 4- or 6-beat acquisitions that achieved a frame rate of 20 to 40 Hz in the majority of images (range, 15-60 Hz across all studies). Three of 9 studies were performed under sedation. No transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) 3D studies were available for comparison to pathologic specimens during the period of interest for this series.

There was agreement between echocardiography and pathology interpretations across each domain in the majority of cases (Table 2). Leaflet number correlated to pathology findings in all 10 studies. Leaflet abnormalities were recognized in nearly all patients; in patient 7, the 3DE was interpreted as showing thickened valve leaflets; however, the leaflet tissue was normal on pathology review. In patient 6, 3DE was interpreted as showing shortened chordae to the septal leaflet. On pathology review, the septal leaflet was not restrictive. Chordal abnormalities were otherwise correctly identified. In 2 of 10 valves evaluated, the echocardiography reviewers could not describe papillary muscle anatomy using the available 3DE images.

Discussion

This series demonstrates the potential for 3DE to provide important information about AVV anatomy in patients with CHD, as shown through comparison with pathologic specimens. The number of AVV leaflets and details of leaflet abnormalities, such as leaflet thickening or deficiency, were reliably identified in nearly all available cases. Papillary muscle anatomy findings correlated with pathology in the majority of cases, although at times imaging was insufficient to make a determination. Although this is a small, observational series, this work is both encouraging and relevant as the field increasingly embraces 3DE. We show that this technology can be applied clinically even to our smallest patients, with included studies utilizing a TTE 3D probe on neonates less than 5 kg in size.

In addition, this series showcases the significant heterogeneity that exists within congenitally abnormal AVVs. Valve anatomy can differ significantly between patients labeled with the same diagnosis, which inherently impacts the approach to any potential valve repair. Careful, detailed evaluation using both two-dimensional and 3D imaging is therefore important to direct a patient-specific approach. This is supported by societal guidelines, which emphasize the use of 3DE in presurgical planning.14

No patients with available pathologic specimens within the time window of interest for this series had a 3D TEE. This reflects the prior state of our field, in which 3D TEE transducers did not exist for most pediatric patients. The increasing availability of high-frequency, pediatric-sized TEE probes is expanding the access and utility of 3DE in CHD, a population in which small patient size and high heart rates previously posed a barrier to adequate temporal and spatial resolution.15 Our center began clinical use of a pediatric 3D TEE in summer 2024.

This advance in 3D TEE probe miniaturization goes hand in hand with new rendering technology with enhanced lighting and realistic shadows as shown in Videos 2 and 4, to improve the comprehension of complex anatomy in real time. Greater granularity of detail than what was evaluated here can be gleaned through the use of postprocessing tools, allowing for modeling and quantification using 3DE images.9,16

It has been our center's experience that the use of sedation with paralysis and breath holding, when possible, to limit motion artifact is particularly important to achieve the image quality needed for AVV evaluation in children. In our review of 3DE studies, those with insufficient imaging or inconsistent findings were notably performed without sedation. Although it requires additional resources and coordination with anesthesia or intensivist colleagues, it is now our standard practice to use sedation with breath holds for all 3D studies (TTE or TEE) evaluating AVVs. This format also allows the imager to more easily incorporate TEE, should transthoracic images prove to be suboptimal. In addition, standardized protocols for 3DE studies and use of a core group of sonographers and readers have allowed our center to minimize variability in image acquisition and interpretation, as well as concentrate and grow our team's expertise in 3DE. Regularly scheduled team conferences are vital opportunities for exchange of experience and perspective between our imagers and surgeons.

By its nature, this series includes only those patients with autopsy or explanted heart specimens, representing the most severe forms of CHD. In assessing correlation, we are limited by subjective assessment of both 3DE and pathologic specimens given that normal values for leaflet size, thickness, and chordal length do not exist for common AVVs or valves in most forms of CHD. Further work into establishing normal values in these patient populations may help standardize assessment, as has been done in patients without CHD.17

As the field of congenital cardiology continues to adopt expanding 3DE technology, it will be important to enact rigorous scholarly work to study its application, accuracy, and impact. Although the patients in our series were not considered candidates for surgical valve repair, this technology is used routinely in our practice to assess patients prior to valve surgery. Analysis of 3DE findings correlated with surgical findings is an important next step in validating this tool.

Conclusion

Three-dimensional echocardiography can provide information about AVV anatomy in pediatric CHD that is correlative to pathologic specimens. Further comparisons of 3DE to surgical findings and pathologic specimens as the technology continues to advance will improve understanding of the strengths and limitations of this modality.
