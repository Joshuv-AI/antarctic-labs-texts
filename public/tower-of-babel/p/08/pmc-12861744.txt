Authors: Mingyu Luo, Jimin Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractImage, graphical abstract

Chikungunya virus disease (CHIKVD), a mosquito-borne arboviral disease, is clinically characterized by encompass fever, acute articular pain or arthritis, and a maculopapular rash. By December 2024, autochthonous transmission had been recorded in 119 countries and territories, and persistently high attack rates leave susceptible populations continuously at risk.1

The changing epidemiology of CHIKVD

Since the disease originally occurred in Eastern Coastal Africa (The United Republic of Tanzania [Tanzania]) in 1952,2 sporadic cases and explosive outbreaks were mainly limited in Africa and Southeast Asia. After 2004, CHIKVD re-emerged, and consolidating a sustained eastward expansion of its endemic and epidemic range.3 The epidemic started in the coastal region of the Republic of Kenya (Kenya) and propagated explosively through the Comoros Islands, Reunion Island, and other islands in the southwest Indian Ocean in early 2005, followed by an extensive outbreak in the Indian subcontinent in 2005–2006 (Fig. 1). Within the last five years, incidence of CHIKVD has exhibited a pronounced upsurge, evolving into a significant global public health hazard (Fig. 2). From January through early June 2025, fourteen countries or territories have already recorded roughly 220,000 cases and 80 related deaths. Natural foci persists in the Americas (especially the Federative Republic of Brazil [Brazil]), Africa, Southeast Asia, and Reunion and Mayotte in the Indian Ocean region. Prior to 2004, two major genotypes of chikungunya virus (CHIKV)—West African and East/Central/South African (ECSA)—were originated in Africa, subsequently, the Asian genotype evolved from the ECSA genotype in Southeast Asia; Aedes aegypti constituted the principal vector for CHIKVD, thereby restricting its endemic foci. The emergence of the Indian Ocean Lineage group within ECSA genotype in Kenya triggered explosive outbreaks across Indian Ocean islands and South Asia. Adaptive substitutions (e.g., E1-A226V) facilitated a vector shift toward the more widely distributed Aedes albopictus, extending epidemic reach. Subsequent mutations (e.g., E1-K211E/E2-V264A) have concurrently enhanced CHIKV infectivity for Aedes aegypti in hyper-endemic regions of Africa, South America, and southern India. These adaptive mutations demonstrate a sustained capacity of CHIKV to optimize fitness in both Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus, thereby facilitating continuous geographic expansion.

The epidemiological burden of CHIKVD is escalating. Studies conducted in natural-foci regions of Africa and Americas indicate that the current prevalence or seroprevalence exceeds 30%, while evidence from Asian natural-foci regions demonstrates a progressive increase in prevalence.6 In addition to chronicity and severe infections (may related to virus load), the clinical burden of CHIKVD includes elevated risks of hospitalization and attributable mortality.7 Consequently, the economic burden is substantial, mainly manifesting as direct costs and productivity losses—that is especially pronounced in low- and middle-income countries. Ongoing climate warming can expand the spatial and temporal scope of the environment suitable for the growth and reproduction of mosquitoes, and disease-threatened populations will further increase. Thus, the disease burden and public health risk of CHIKVD remains substantial.8

CHIKVD outbreaks in southern China, 2025

First imported chikungunya case was identified in China in 2008. Since then, sporadic autochthonous outbreaks have been reported in Yunnan Province, Guangdong Province and other southern provinces. However, persistent endemic foci have not been demonstrated. In 2025, several cities in southern China experienced imported events that seeded subsequent autochthonous transmission. From August 10 to August 16, Guangdong Province reported an additional 830 autochthonous cases, elevating the provincial tally to 9,933 cases for the year.9 The intensity and geographic reach of this outbreak indicate a critical epidemiologic situation with a potential risk of onward exportation to neighboring regions.

In response to this risk, China issued the Technical Guidelines for Prevention and Control of Chikungunya Fever (2025 Edition). The guideline divides risk areas into different levels and prescribes targeted interventions accordingly. Throughout outbreak containment, priority is given to integrated vector management of Aedes spp. Vector density thresholds, adapted from dengue-control standards, are strictly enforced, and outbreak termination is declared only after Aedes mosquitoes below these thresholds. With no licensed vaccine or specific antiviral therapy currently available, vector control and prevention remain the demonstrably effective strategy for curbing mosquito-borne diseases such as CHIKVD.

Risk assessment of the CHIKVD endemic in China

The CHIKV emerged in China in 2025 is ECSA genotype,10 which is predominantly transmitted within densely populated urban areas. Moreover, the principal vector for this outbreak is Aedes albopictus. In China, six southern provinces including Guangdong Province have been designated as the highest risk regions for CHIKVD. Compared with northern China, the active period of Aedes albopictus is substantially prolonged in southern provinces, and Aedes aegypti persists in Guangdong Province and several other southern provinces. Southern provinces maintain intensive exchanges with adjacent Southeast Asian countries, within which the Asian and ESCA genotypes coexist.

Although the reported cases from July 27 to August 2 have begun to decline, outbreaks continue in other regions worldwide, the risk of importation persists because of intensive international connectivity. In addition, abundant rainfall in summer generates numerous breeding sites for vector mosquitoes, that brings challenges for sanitary conditions management. Consequently, epidemic prevention and control remain challenging.

Conclusions

Globally, CHIKVD and other mosquito-borne diseases constitute a significant threat to public health and socio-economic stability. Governments should accord these vector-borne diseases much higher priority, and should translate the concept of “One Health” into practical policies and strategies, with vector prevention and control serving as the key implementation. Public health education must emphasize on community engagement in the elimination of breeding sites, and must reinforce environmental sanitation practices that maintain mosquito density below disease-transmission thresholds.

At the same time, a comprehensive, trans-regional disease-surveillance system should be constructed by consolidating extant dispersed datasets, enhancing the sensitivity of case and vector monitoring, and developing intelligent early-warning models capable of forecasting epidemic and geographic diffusion of CHIKVD and similar arboviral diseases, thereby providing technical support for disease control and prevention.
