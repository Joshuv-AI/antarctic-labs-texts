Research Priority Setting Exercises in Neglected Tropical Diseases: A Systematic Mapping Review
Authors: Manish Barik, Sushree Nibedita Panda, Gooty Shirisha, Prabhu Kalyan Das, Bhaskar Tiwary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeglected tropical diseases (NTDs) affect more than 1.7 billion people worldwide, primarily in low- and middle-income countries. Despite their high burden, NTDs remain underfunded and receive fragmented research attention. Research priority-setting provides a systematic approach to align limited resources with the most pressing needs. While several priority setting exercises have been conducted for NTDs, their findings have not been synthesized. This study aimed to systematically map the published research priority-setting exercises for NTDs, synthesize their methodologies, stakeholder involvement, and thematic priorities.MethodologyWe conducted a systematic mapping review of studies published in PubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science up to March 28, 2025. Eligible studies were those that explicitly reported research prioritization for at least one WHO-listed NTD. Data were extracted on study design, geographic scope, prioritization methods, stakeholder engagement, and outcomes, and were analyzed descriptively. Reported priorities were classified into six domains: (1) health systems and service delivery, (2) diagnostics and therapeutics, (3) surveillance and data systems, (4) community engagement and health education, (5) integration and coordination, and (6) equity, policy, and implementation research.ResultsOf the 1,678 records, 15 studies met inclusion criteria. Most were mixed-methods (n = 10) and conducted at the country (n = 5) or regional level (n = 5). Delphi was the most used framework (n = 6). Fourteen (out of 15) studies engaged clinicians, policymakers, and researchers; 12 involved NGOs; 8 included vulnerable populations; 6 included patients. Lymphatic filariasis was the most frequently prioritized NTD (n = 7), followed by soil-transmitted helminths and schistosomiasis (n = 5 each). Research priorities clustered around health system strengthening (6/15 studies), community engagement (7/15 studies), and equity-oriented implementation research (10/15 studies).ConclusionResearch priority-setting for NTDs has expanded in recent years but remains heterogeneous in methodology, stakeholder inclusiveness, and transparency. Findings also call for caution against disease prioritization approaches that focus only on high-prevalence NTDs which risk overlooking less common NTDs and the populations affected by them. Six cross-cutting domains of research priorities were identified across the studies, largely consistent with the WHO NTD Roadmap. The identified domains can guide WHO regional offices and national programs in updating research roadmaps, explicitly linking priorities to policy and implementation pathways to accelerate progress toward global NTD targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44197-026-00614-y.

Introduction

Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTDs) are a diverse group of poverty-associated infections caused by viruses, bacteria, parasites, fungi or toxins [1]. NTDs disproportionately affect more than 1.7 billion people annually, primarily in low- and middle-income countries across tropical and sub-tropical regions [1]. Unlike many infectious diseases, NTDs rarely cause large-scale mortality but are strongly associated with chronic disability and stigma that reduce quality of life and productivity [2, 3]. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 study estimated that NTDs accounted for over 18 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), with conditions such as lymphatic filariasis, leprosy, onchocerciasis, and snakebite envenoming causing long-term impairments and social exclusion. Skin-related NTDs, in particular, contribute to visible disabilities and social discrimination, reinforcing cycles of poverty [4, 5]. Despite this global impact, NTDs historically attracted only a fraction of the global health research funding [6].

The co-endemic nature of NTDs, combined with complex transmission dynamics, further complicates control efforts and highlights the need for coordinated and integrated research agendas [7]. Analyses of the recent GBD assessments have highlighted both high disability-adjusted life years attributable to NTDs and the fragmented coordination of control programs, which are often siloed by disease or region [8, 9]. This fragmentation hampers the development of integrated strategies and leaves key knowledge gaps unaddressed, particularly in diagnostics, therapeutics, and health systems research and strengthening [10, 11]. The WHO’s NTD Roadmap 2021–2030 now sets ambitious targets – including a 90% reduction in people requiring treatment and a 75% reduction in NTD-related DALYs by 2030 underscoring the urgent need for intensified research and programmatic action to achieve these goals [12]. These highlight the essential role of NTD control in achieving health equity and the broader Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) agenda.

Recent WHO report on NTD shows a steep 41% decline in official development assistance (ODA) for NTDs between 2018 and 2023, falling from about US$ 440 million to US$ 260 million. Most of this funding came from three donors – the United States, the United Kingdom, and the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation [13]. Also, there is no pooled financing mechanism for NTDs, unlike malaria or HIV programs. This funding gap makes sustained research and implementation uncertain, especially in low-income countries. The report also warns that such shortfalls threaten elimination progress and calls for stronger national ownership, domestic resource mobilization, and integration of NTD work into primary health care [13].

Health research priority-setting (RPS) is the process of deciding which health research questions or areas should be prioritized. This includes what many organizations describe as “priority-setting exercises,” “strategic planning,” or “agenda setting.” By engaging diverse stakeholders ranging from affected communities and frontline health workers to policymakers and funders, RPS can enhance transparency, equity, and impact [14, 15]. These processes aim to balance scientific feasibility with public health relevance, thereby enhancing transparency and strengthening accountability in decision-making. In global health, RPS has been applied across diverse domains [16–18].

Priority-setting in NTDs is conceptually distinct from other health research fields. Unlike diseases managed through single-pathogen programs, NTDs share ecological overlap, co-endemic distribution, and strong links to poverty and social exclusion. Thus, the priority setting in NTDs must integrate multisectoral inputs spanning health, sanitation, and environment while maintaining a focus on equity and community participation. Several individual priority setting exercises have been conducted for NTDs -at global, national or disease-specific levels, but their findings have not been synthesized [19].This lack of synthesis constrains the ability of researchers, funders, and policymakers to identify consensus priorities, benchmark progress, or assess alignment with global strategies.

This systematic mapping review addresses these gaps by collating and critically appraising existing RPS exercises for NTDs. It aims to synthesize their objectives, methodological approaches, stakeholder involvement, and reported priorities. By doing so, the review seeks to highlight areas of convergence, reveal underexplored research domains, and provide evidence to guide future NTD research agendas in line with global disease burden and elimination targets.

Methods

This systematic mapping review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251021734.) and adopted the PRISMA 2020 guideline [20]. All procedures followed the pre-registered protocol to ensure consistency in the study’s design, methodology, and analysis .

Search Strategy

We performed a comprehensive search in PubMed, EMBASE and Web of Science, covering all records from database inception through 28 March, 2025. Our strategy combined each platform-specific controlled vocabulary with relevant keywords specifically “research priority” AND “NTDs” or “neglected tropical diseases.” All retrieved citations were exported and organized using Zotero and deduplicated. All retrieved records were then screened against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The detailed search strategy is given in the Supplementary file.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This review included studies that applied a structured process to identify, rank, score, or reach consensus on research priorities addressing at least one WHO-listed NTD [1]. To be eligible, studies had to clearly report the the objective of the prioritization exercise. We imposed no date restrictions but excluded publications not written in English. We excluded non-empirical studies (such as commentaries, editorials, discussion papers, and opinions), secondary studies, guidelines, books, and book chapters.

Study Screening and Selection

Four authors (MB, SNP, PKD and GS) independently assessed the titles and abstracts in the first phase followed by full-text screening of potentially relevant studies performed by SNP, PKD, & GS using Rayyan platform. Each article was double screened (in both the phases) to maintain the quality of the review. Disagreements or conflicts that arose during the screening process were resolved through online discussions with the third author (MB) to reach a final consensus.

Data Extraction

We developed a preliminary data extraction form referring to the ten domains in REporting guideline for PRIority SEtting of health research (REPRISE) [21]. We piloted and modified the data extraction form with two included studies. For each document, we extracted its basic characteristics such as publication year, journal or report source, country or setting, geographical focus, targeted NTD(s), type of prioritization exercise, funding sources, and conflict of interest statements, before detailing its methodological process. This included the composition of the leadership team, the specific framework or method employed, the sequential stages of prioritization, and the year the exercise was conducted (defaulting to the publication year if unspecified). We also recorded stakeholder involvement, including stakeholder types and engagement methods (online or in-person); as well as techniques used to identify and compile research priorities. Finally, we captured the outcomes by listing the priority items in each study. All team members (SNP, PKD and GS) participated in the data extraction. Each article was double extracted by two reviewers. Any discrepancies that arose were resolved via discussion with a third author (MB). The included studies describe research priority setting processes and methodologies rather than outcomes or effectiveness. We did not conduct a formal quality appraisal because reporting varied widely across studies. Many studies lacked sufficient methodological detail to support consistent scoring across appraisal criteria. This review therefore focused on mapping methodological approaches, stakeholder engagement, and reported priority themes in NTD research priority setting rather than judging overall study quality.

Data Analysis and Synthesis

We used Microsoft Excel to perform descriptive analysis. Visualizations were developed in Google Looker Studio to present patterns of key characteristics, including year of publication, geographic focus, study design, stakeholder groups, and funding sources. These were summarized using frequencies and proportions to identify patterns and descriptive trends. We assessed the methodological approaches used for research priority setting by summarizing the types of prioritization methods employed, and the modes of stakeholder engagement (e.g., online, in-person, or both). The composition of stakeholder groups involved in each study was also analyzed, capturing the types and diversity of participants, including clinicians, researchers, policymakers, donors, patients, and representatives of vulnerable populations. Additionally, we examined the thematic focus of the included studies by calculating the number and proportion of studies addressing specific NTDs, and distinguishing between those that focused on skin-related versus non-skin-related NTDs.

The research team reviewed the WHO NTD Roadmap 2021–2030 and its strategic pillars, along with related WHO research strategies and monitoring frameworks, to identify broad thematic areas that encompass major dimensions of NTD control and research [12]. The six predefined domains were (1) health systems and service delivery, (2) diagnostics and therapeutics, (3) surveillance and data systems, (4) community engagement and health education, (5) integration and coordination(across diseases and sectors), and (6) equity, policy, and implementation research.

Each extracted priority was mapped to the most relevant domain based on its primary focus. When a priority overlapped with more than one area, classification was guided by consensus among the authors. This approach enabled aggregation of diverse and context-specific priorities into structured domains that are directly linked to the global agenda for NTD research and action.

Results

The initial database search found 1,678 records from PubMed (n = 444), Embase (n = 605), and Web of Science (n = 629). After removing 671 duplicates and seven records for other reasons(books etc.), 1,000 unique records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 819 were excluded, leaving 181 full-text articles sought for retrieval. Twenty reports could not be retrieved, and 161 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. A total of 146 articles were excluded for not fulfilling eligibility criteria. Ultimately, 15 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis [22–36]. Study Design and Geographic Scope

Among the included studies, mixed methods designs were the most frequently adopted design (n = 10) [23, 26–29, 31–35], followed by qualitative approaches (n = 4) [24, 25, 30, 36], and one study employed quantitative methods [22]. The majority of studies were conducted at country-specific (n = 5) or regional levels (n = 5), with only a few having a global focus (n = 4) and one study did not specify the geographical focus (Fig. 3). Countries most frequently represented in the priority setting processes were India, Ghana, Kenya, Ethiopia, Colombia, and South Africa.Fig. 3Distribution of focus areas in NTD research priority-setting studies

Distribution of focus areas in NTD research priority-setting studies

Methods Used for Priority Setting

The most common method employed in the included studies was a “Delphi” strategy (n = 6). A total of 5 studies used a “multi approach” which often involved combinations of surveys, interviews, workshops, or literature reviews. Frameworks such as Child Health and Nutrition Research Initiative (CHNRI) and the James Lind Alliance framework were used in one study each. Stakeholder Engagement

All 15 studies engaged multiple stakeholder groups in their research priority-setting processes. Clinicians or healthcare providers and policymakers were included in 15 of the studies. Non-governmental organizations (NGOs) participated in 11 studies, and researchers in 14 studies, while specific groups such as vulnerable or marginalized populations were involved in 8 studies. Patients were included in 6 studies, while caregivers were represented in 3. Donors or funders participated in 4 studies. Less frequently represented groups included traditional healers (n = 1), community volunteers (n = 1) and industry representatives (n = 1) Fig. 5.Fig. 5Types of stakeholder engagement across studies

Reporting of Funding and Conflict of Interest

Funding support was reported in 10 studies. Key funders included USAID, WHO/TDR, the Carter Center, the Wellcome Trust, UK Aid, the Hamish Ogston Foundation, and national government agencies(example: ICMR, India) (Table 1). Two studies did not declare their funding sources, and three explicitly reported receiving no funding. Declarations of conflict of interest were largely absent; only one study(1/15) reported having no conflicts.

Diversity in Diseases Prioritized

Across the included studies, research priority-setting exercises covered a diverse range of neglected tropical diseases (NTDs). Lymphatic filariasis was the most frequently addressed, appearing in seven studies [22, 24, 27–30, 36]. Four studies each focused on leprosy [24, 26, 32, 36] and soil-transmitted helminths [24, 28, 33, 36]. Schistosomiasis and trachoma were included in three studies each [24, 28, 36], while leishmaniasis was highlighted in three studies [27, 33, 36]. Two studies each examined rabies [23, 31], Chagas disease [27, 33], and snakebite envenoming [25, 35]. Other NTDs such as Buruli ulcer, scabies, yaws, dengue, brucellosis and Guinea worm disease were represented in only one study each. Classifications of Research Priorities Across Thematic Domains

From the 15 included studies, a wide range of research priorities were reported, reflecting variations in methodology and stakeholder perspectives. The priorities clustered into six predefined thematic domains: (1) health systems and service delivery, (2) diagnostics and therapeutics, (3) surveillance and data systems, (4) community engagement and health education, (5) integration and coordination (across diseases and sectors) and (6) equity, policy, and implementation research. These domains span multiple disease areas and represent the main pillars around which research priority-setting in NTDs has been organized in Fig. 6.Fig. 6Mapping of included studies to priority domains

Discussion

This systematic mapping review synthesizes the methodologies, stakeholder engagement strategies, and thematic areas reported in 15 research priority-setting exercises conducted across diverse NTD contexts. While individual exercises have been previously reported, this is, to our knowledge, the first comprehensive synthesis focused exclusively on RPS in NTDs. Our findings reveal significant heterogeneity in how research priorities are identified, the extent of stakeholder inclusion, and the methodological rigor underpinning these processes.

Most included RPS studies employed descriptive or mixed-methods designs and were concentrated in high disease-burden regions, including India, Sub-Saharan Africa, and Latin America. Yet, despite the local relevance, a majority of corresponding authors were affiliated with institutions in high-income countries. This geographic imbalance reflects broader concerns around epistemic inequity in global health research, where LMIC perspectives—particularly those of affected communities—remain underrepresented in agenda-setting processes [37–39]. Global funders allocate an estimated 75% of direct and 70% of indirect NTD funding to leading institutes in nonendemic nations [40]. While such investments modernize laboratory and data infrastructure in HICs, they often perpetuate colonial legacies through sample collection and data extraction from endemic countries without equivalent capacity strengthening [41–43]. Although we found that community members and people with lived experience were involved in over half the studies, their roles were often less explicitly detailed or systematically integrated compared to researchers and policymakers. For example, Pratt et al. (2021) describe how priority-setting processes often include community members only in limited roles rather than as equal partners [44].

Thematically, the most consistent research priorities spanned across service delivery, capacity building, and access to care. This emphasis on strengthening health systems affirms long-standing critiques of vertical disease-specific programming that often neglects the structural determinants of poor NTD outcomes [6]. Studies called for improved training of frontline workers, context-adapted treatment protocols, and enhanced supply chain logistics—challenges echoed in other sectors of global health but made more urgent in the context of chronic underfunding and infrastructural neglect in NTD-affected areas [45–47].

Priority areas in diagnostics and therapeutics also featured prominently, particularly for diseases like rabies, leprosy, and Guinea worm, where existing tools are either unaffordable or suboptimal [48]. The call for point-of-care diagnostics, vaccine development, and alternatives to costly biologicals like rabies immunoglobulin reflects both scientific urgency and health equity concerns [49]. Similarly, the inclusion of community-based strategies and participatory health education highlights a growing recognition that biomedical innovation alone cannot bridge the gaps in NTD care. Evidence from large-scale Visceral Leishmaniasis elimination programs in India that integrated indoor residual spraying, community engagement, and strengthened screening shows that community ownership and behavioural insight are essential for lasting impact [50–54]. This emphasis on community engagement also resonates with the WHO NTD Roadmap’s call for a change in operating culture toward stronger country ownership, defined in part by clearer stakeholder roles and responsibilities in delivering NTD goals [12].

Our findings align with these goals, especially in highlighting operational research needs for integrated service delivery (e.g., joint mass drug administration and MMDP strategies for skin-NTDs), One Health surveillance, and cross-cutting coordination across water, sanitation, and health sectors. However, relatively few studies explicitly adopted or referenced these policy frameworks, suggesting a missed opportunity to align RPS exercises more closely with evolving global strategies. This gap may reflect both the timing of some studies (pre-Roadmap) and the disjointed nature of research governance in the NTD space.

The prevalence of research priorities across the six domains also has implications for future funding direction. The priorities in health systems and service delivery suggests a funding gap in operational research and capacity building at subnational levels, consistent with previous analyses of NTD program implementation [46, 55]. The priorities in equity and implementation research highlight the need for donors to support social science and policy translation work that links research to real-world delivery [56]. Funding for NTDs declined by 41% between 2018 and 2023, increasing dependence on a small number of donors. Transparent, country-led research priority-setting is therefore essential to guide the efficient allocation of limited resources. The WHO report also stresses integrating NTD research into primary health care, adopting rights-based and gender-responsive approaches, and promoting domestic financing for sustainable, equitable research agendas [13].

Our review also highlights methodological inconsistencies across RPS exercises. While some used systematic frameworks like Delphi or CHNRI, others employed less formal or hybrid approaches. Stakeholder engagement methods ranged from multi-stage consultations to single-mode surveys, with limited documentation of processes for consensus-building or conflict resolution. These disparities limit the reproducibility and policy relevance of many RPS outcomes. Use of standardized reporting guidelines such as REPRISE could help to address these gaps by ensuring consistent documentation of processes, stakeholder roles, consensus methods, and conflict-of-interest reporting in RPS exercises [21]. Notably, a few studies disclosed funding sources or conflicts of interest, which raises concerns about transparency and potential bias—particularly in settings where research priorities may influence downstream investments or funding allocations.

Strengths and Limitations of Study

This review studied the domains of NTD research priority-setting efforts, providing a coherent view of what has been prioritized, by whom, and how. However, this review has several limitation. First, our search was limited to English-language publications, which may have excluded relevant exercises reported in non-English sources or grey literature. Second, the absence of detailed reporting in many studies constrained our ability to compare stakeholder dynamics, funding influences, or alignment with national priorities. Third, while we identified convergence in thematic domains, we did not assess the extent to which identified priorities were implemented or evaluated; which is an important area for future research. Finally, the exclusion of grey literature and early programmatic RPS exercises may bias the evidence base toward donor-funded academic studies published in peer-reviewed journals. This could have under-represented the national or WHO-led consultations, where priorities were defined through policy processes rather than formal research frameworks.

Implications for Future Research and Practice

The findings highlight the need for research agendas in NTDs to move beyond disease-specific silos and address systemic barriers that constrain progress. Strengthening health systems, improving access to affordable diagnostics and therapeutics, and embedding equity considerations within implementation research are critical. Future exercises should prioritize inclusive stakeholder engagement, especially of community members and persons with lived experiences, and apply structured, transparent methods for prioritization.

The six domains identified in this review provide a practical framework for policy and program guidance. WHO regional offices could use these domains to align national and regional research roadmaps with measurable targets in the NTD Roadmap 2021–2030. For example, priorities under health systems and service delivery can guide integration of NTD care into essential health packages. Diagnostics and therapeutics priorities can inform pooled investments in point-of-care tools across multiple NTDs. Surveillance and data systems can support digital data harmonization in regional monitoring frameworks. Community engagement and health education can shape social mobilization strategies through participatory models. Integration and coordination can support multi-disease MDA planning. Equity, policy, and implementation research can help countries address gender, stigma, and social determinants in national NTD action plans. These domains together form an adaptable template that WHO and national programs can operationalize during roadmap reviews and strategy updates.

To maximize impact, funders and implementers should support the identification of research priorities and also their translation into funded projects, programmatic innovation, and policy reform. Finally, there is an urgent need to build regional research leadership and infrastructure, particularly in LMICs, to ensure that NTD research is driven by those closest to the burden. Strengthening local capacity in priority-setting and implementation research will be key to closing the persistent gap between global ambition and ground realities in the NTD response.

Conclusion

Despite variations in methodology and scope, six predefined domains—health systems strengthening, access to diagnostics and treatments, data systems, community engagement, integration, and equity; remained central priorities. Future research priority-setting should be transparent, inclusive, and adapted to local contexts. Strengthening local capacity in priority-setting and implementation research is critical to bridging the gap between global goals and on-ground realities, ensuring progress toward the WHO NTD Roadmap targets. The study also calls for caution against disease prioritization approaches that focus only on high-prevalence NTDs and risk overlooking less common NTDs and the populations affected by them.
