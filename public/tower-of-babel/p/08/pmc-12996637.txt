Who Is Really Deciding? AI and Clinical Judgement in Dentistry
Authors: Owais A. Farooqi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The integration of AI in dental practice, primarily through AI-enabled software, influences clinical workflows by promoting rapid decision-making and potentially increasing bias stemming from its automation bias. While these systems demonstrate high accuracy in standardised evaluations, their real-world performance, particularly in atypical cases, remains less validated. This reliance on AI necessitates a broader AI literacy in clinicians, focusing on ethical awareness and critical judgement rather than solely technical proficiency. Ongoing evaluation of AI systems must encompass their impact on clinical reasoning, and dental education should prepare professionals to discern when to slow down their intuitive judgements in response to algorithmic insights to enhance clinical accountability and trust.

Who Is Really Deciding? AI and Clinical Judgement in Dentistry

Daniel Kahneman, the Nobel Prize–winning psychologist whose work reshaped understanding of human judgement and decision-making, observed that people are often ‘content to trust a plausible judgement’ rather than engage in sustained deliberation.1 This tendency toward fast, intuitive thinking is not a weakness of professional practice; it is a necessary feature of clinical work. In contemporary dentistry, however, it increasingly operates under conditions that reward speed, efficiency and cognitive ease.

Artificial intelligence (AI) in dentistry is most often encountered as AI-enabled software embedded within diagnostic and clinical workflows, where its clinical role is now beginning to take shape.

Much of routine dental practice relies on rapid, intuitive judgement, particularly under conditions of time pressure and cognitive load. This mode of thinking is efficient and often appropriate, but it is also more vulnerable to bias and premature closure. Dentistry increasingly operates under sustained productivity pressures—throughput expectations, administrative burden and growing diagnostic complexity—that further constrain the space for slow, reflective reasoning.

AI-enabled software used as a device is increasingly introduced into this ecosystem. Most systems do not issue explicit recommendations; instead, they present visual highlights, probability estimates, alerts or ranked outputs embedded within clinical workflows. These features are designed to streamline interpretation and reduce cognitive effort. In practice, they often shape early impressions, making certain interpretations feel immediately plausible while rendering alternatives less salient. This influence can occur even when clinicians are fully aware that software outputs are imperfect and require clinical judgement.

Fast thinking in this context is not a failure of professionalism; it is a structural feature of modern clinical work. When productivity pressures reward rapid closure and consistency, tools that appear to accelerate interpretation are naturally attractive. Software-based diagnostic systems introduced into this environment do more than support efficiency. They interact directly with the forces that promote fast thinking, potentially further compressing the space for deliberation at precisely the moments when reflection is most needed.

These effects are consistent with what has been described as the ‘automation bias’.2, 3 Such bias does not imply abdication of professional responsibility or blind reliance on technology. Rather, it reflects predictable human responses to information presented with apparent authority in cognitively demanding settings.

It is often argued that such concerns diminish when software-based diagnostic systems achieve high levels of accuracy. Indeed, recent studies report strong performance across a range of dental diagnostic tasks under study conditions, helping to explain the growing interest in their clinical adoption.4, 5 However, accuracy reflects average performance across defined datasets. Clinical judgement is most consequential in the smaller proportion of atypical cases—those involving aberrant anatomy, compromised image quality, uncommon disease presentation or complex clinical contextuality.

Paradoxically, the apparent reliability of these systems may amplify their response. Outputs that are usually correct tend to be trusted, appropriately reducing cognitive friction in routine cases. When errors occur, however, the same trust can delay recognition that something does not fit. This is not a flaw in clinicians’ reasoning; it is a consequence of how human judgement adapts to tools that are generally dependable. Concerns about influence are further compounded by the limited extent of independent validation. Much of the available performance evidence for AI-enabled software used as a device is derived from evaluations conducted using curated datasets or within development-adjacent contexts. Independent studies examining performance across institutions, patient populations, imaging systems and real-world workflows remain relatively uncommon. Because model performance is highly context sensitive, headline accuracy figures may obscure variability or reveal where systems are most likely to falter.

Recent discussions in the dental literature have therefore highlighted the need for a broader conception of AI literacy—one that extends beyond technical proficiency to include critical judgement, ethical awareness and understanding of how software can shape clinical reasoning.6 Without such preparation, clinicians may become proficient users of AI-enabled clinical software without being fully equipped to recognise its cognitive effects or to manage their cognitive efforts.

As these systems mature, several implications follow. Evaluation should extend beyond technical performance to include independent validation across diverse clinical settings, with explicit attention to variability and failure modes.7 Assessment should also examine how software influences clinical reasoning in practice—how it affects attention, confidence, verification behaviour, and error recognition—rather than treating these effects as secondary to accuracy.

In parallel, dental education and continuing professional development should address the cognitive dimensions of AI-enabled software use, equipping clinicians to recognise when rapid, intuitive judgement should be slowed and critically reassessed in the presence of algorithmic input. Transparency around uncertainty and limitations should likewise be normalised to support appropriate scepticism rather than overconfidence.

These considerations are not obstacles to innovation. They are necessary accompaniments to ensure that innovation strengthens clinical judgement rather than subtly redirecting it. Accuracy, influence and validation must be considered together if AI-enabled software used as a device is to be integrated into dental practice in a manner that preserves accountability and trust.

Paying attention to how software used as a device shapes clinical judgement—and equipping clinicians through education to manage that influence deliberately—is now a central responsibility of contemporary dental practice.
