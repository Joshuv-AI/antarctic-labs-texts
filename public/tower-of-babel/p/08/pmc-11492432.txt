Diffuse B-cell lymphoma of the testis presenting as epididymo-orchitis
Authors: Patrick Davol, Callie Davol
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractB-cell lymphoma is the most common type of lymphoma, accounting for 85 percent of all lymphomas in the United States. It is more common in men and older adults, and typically presents with fever, night sweats, fatigue, weight loss, and swollen lymph nodes. Primary testicular lymphoma is rare, representing only 1–2% of non-Hodgkin's lymphomas, and can present a diagnostic challenge with its clinical presentation. We present an unusual presentation of testicular lymphoma in an elderly man, and discuss the challenges in the diagnosis and treatment of this condition.

Introduction

Epididymo-orchitis is the most common cause of intraoperative scrotal swelling in adults, accounting for more than 600,000 medical care visits a year in the United States.1 While most adults presenting with clinical symptoms of epididymo-orchitis will have uncomplicated clinical courses and good response to antibiotics, a high index of suspicion must be maintained in patients with atypical presentations and symptoms refractory to treatment, as rarely malignancy can be a present in this manner. Primary testicular lymphoma is exceedingly rare, but must be entertained in the differential diagnosis, particularly in elderly patients presenting with scrotal pain and swelling, as this potentially lethal condition requires prompt diagnosis and treatment.

We present a case of an elderly male presenting with fairly classic symptoms and ultrasound findings of epididymo-orchitis, who was ultimately diagnosed with primary testicular lymphoma.

Case report

An 84-year-old male presented with left-sided scrotal swelling and pain to the outpatient clinic, on referral from urgent care. He had originally presented with clinical symptoms of epididymo-orchitis, including left scrotal swelling and discomfort. He was treated empirically with Rocephin as well as Bactrim. He initially had improvement in symptoms, but the symptoms subsequently returned. He was then treated with a 10-day course of Levaquin, with no improvement in symptoms. Scrotal ultrasound showed no distinct mass, and findings were consistent with epididymo-orchitis (Fig. 1).

Scrotal ultrasound showing inflammation of the left testis and epididymis with no distinct mass.

On referral to our clinic, he was noted to have a diffusely enlarged left testis, which was firm and indurated, with a firm mass palpable to the level of the external inguinal ring. No erythema or fluctuance of the scrotal wall was noted. A CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis was ordered, which demonstrated a left para-aortic retroperitoneal mass measuring 6 × 5 cm (Fig. 2). CT also showed enlargement and heterogeneous enhancement of the left testicle, with tissue density extending throughout the left inguinal canal (Fig. 3).

Transverse images of the CT of the abdomen showing a 6 cm left para-aortic retroperitoneal mass.

Coronal CT image showing the left testicular mass extending into the left internal inguinal ring.

A PET scan showed a large and markedly FDG avid left testicular mass, suspicious for testicular cancer as well as PET avid 6 cm retroperitoneal mass consistent with conglomerate of metastatic adenopathy. Standard tumor markers including AFP, bHCG, and LDH were negative. Given the findings, the patient was taken to the operating room for a left radical orchiectomy. During surgery, he was found to have a solid mass of the left testis, with mass extending to the internal ring. The tunica was not compromised, and the testicle and the spermatic cord were successfully removed. The patient had an uneventful recovery. Final pathology revealed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma with high ki67 80–90 %. He was referred to Medical Oncology for chemotherapy and completed 6 cycles of R-mini CHOP as well as radiation to the contralateral testicle.

Discussion

Annually an estimated 1 in 1000 men develop epididymitis, and it accounts for more than 6000 medical visits a year in the United States. It is the most common cause of inter-scrotal inflammation and is therefore at the top of differential diagnosis with men presenting with scrotal pain and swelling. However, a high index of suspicion of neoplasm must be entertained if symptoms do not resolve with standard treatment, regardless of imaging findings. Testicular presentation of B-cell lymphoma is rare, accounting for only 1 to 2 percent of non-Hodgkin's lymphomas. It represents only 5 percent of all testicular malignancies.2 As demonstrated in our case, the initial ultrasound did not demonstrate a distinct mass, leading to a delay in diagnosis. Ultrasound may show local or diffuse areas of hypoechogenicity and hypervascularity in an enlarged testis in up to 57 % of testicular lymphoma cases, leaving a significant minority with nonspecific findings, such as in our case. CT imaging as well as PET scan can therefore be helpful in making a definitive diagnosis before proceeding to orchiectomy. The median age at presentation for testicular lymphoma is 62 years, and 80 % present with testicular swelling. Only 13 % have systemic symptoms.3 After orchiectomy, standard therapy includes chemotherapy, and occasionally external beam radiation. However, given the rarity of the condition, there is no standardized regimen. It generally has a poor prognosis, further highlighting the need for timely diagnosis and treatment.
